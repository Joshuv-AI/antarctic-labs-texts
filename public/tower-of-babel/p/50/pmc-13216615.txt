Scene classification in dance art using deep learning with squeeze and excitation networks and the Advanced Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm
Authors: Yude Dong, Linfang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomation of scene classification within artistic domains has become more prevalent due to the advent of deep learning. This study presented a new framework for classifying dance scenes using an adapted Squeeze-and-Excitation Network (SENet) and optimized hyperparameters with Advanced Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA). The SENet architecture allows for redistributing features by adaptively focusing on the most discriminative visual features (e.g., posture, costume), and ABWOA efficiently optimizes hyper-parameters improve convergence and generalization. The framework has been evaluated on the Dance5K dataset that significantly outperformed the state-of-the-art models with an accuracy value of 92.6% and an F1-score value of 96.4%. Comprehensive experiments, including statistical significance tests, and cross validation validates the robustness of the framework. These results illustrate the potential of blending attention-based CNNs and bio-inspired optimization techniques within fine-grained visual categorization of cultural and artistic domains.Keywords: Scene classification, Deep learning, Squeeze-and-excitation network, Advanced beluga whale optimization, Dance art recognition, Attention mechanismSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the development of the field, scene classification in the arts has become a key area of computer vision and artificial intelligence, whereby machines try to understand and classify visual contents according to contextual semantics1.

In particular, the classification of dance scenes across its cultural, stylistic, and choreographic forms becomes a unique challenge given the rapidly changing human motions, complex body posturing, and varied styles of visual texture characteristic of the different dance genres2.

The successful categorization of such scenes would not only prove useful in areas like the digital archiving process and educational tools as well as multimedia indexing, but would also play a significant part in the automated processes of preserving the intangible cultural heritage3–6.

Dance scene analysis constitutes an artistic domain that differs from other classification paradigms for which a digital model exists7. Though several advances have been made applying deep learning algorithms for image classification, with promising results; the techniques are still frail at most times while addressing the complexities of such artistic domains8.

Classic convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are great at modeling spatial hierarchies; however, they may not focus on the most critical discriminative features against high intra-class variability and inter-class similarity9.

For instance, in terms of apparel and light condition, ballet, and contemporary dance present similar features; they only diverge in areas of posture and dynamics of movements10. This demands an advanced mechanism of feature recalibration that can elevate the awareness and sensitivity of the model to cues specific to class membership11.

For remedying the stated drawbacks, this work formulates a new framework, combining the Squeeze-and-Excitation Networks (SENet) with an advanced version of Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA). SENet proposes a lightweight methodology to enable adaptive recalibration of channel-wise feature responses by modeling interdependencies among feature maps, thus allowing the network to highlight informative features while suppressing others deemed irrelevant (see Fig. 1).

However, the performance of SENet, like other CNN architectures, strongly depends on hyperparameter settings such as learning rate, batch size, optimizer type, and regularization coefficients.

For effective optimization of these parameters, therefore, we will deploy a modified version of BWOA that imitates the social behavior and migration patterns of the beluga whale. This approach provides a powerful method with a good rate of convergence and global search capability when put in comparison with many other conventional optimizers such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Genetic Algorithm (GA). Although both Squeeze-and-Excitation Networks (SENet) and metaheuristic optimizers such as Beluga Whale Optimization are developed in isolation, the synergistic combination between the two is driven by the semiotic framework of dance art in a unique way. Dance classification is more object-sparse than object-centric in that it depends on delicate interdependent visual features, including angularity of relationship between limbs, fabric tension during motion, and spatial alignment to the axes of the stage, which are not channel-sparse but semantically rich.

The conventional CNNs do not consider these discriminative, although low-magnitude features in the uniform manner of the other channels. To deal with it, SENet allows channel-wise recalibration, and its performance is very sensitive to hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, weight decay) that control gradient flow via the excitation gate. Generic optimizers (e.g. Adam) frequently settle on suboptimal configurations where one part of the visual (e.g. costume color) is overemphasized and another part is ignored (e.g. kinematic nuances). Enhanced (with Levy flight and chaotic maps) ABWOA gives a stochastic yet structured search that tradeoffs exploration of the various hyperparameter regimes and exploiting the configurations that best maximize attention to postural semantics.

More importantly, the chaotic map prevents the early convergence to costume-biased solutions whereas Levy flight helps to allow large jumps occasionally that uncover learning rates helpful to fine-grained pose discrimination. Therefore, this synthesis is not just an engineering conglomerate but a theoretically based solution to the two-dimensional problem of dance, high intra-class variance of appearance and low inter-class variance of semantic organization.

The relevance of the adaptive capacity of the attention mechanism to an optimizer aimed at traversing the rough loss landscape of artistic subjects in which visual similarity is not a predictor of belonging to a specific class is explicitly specified in the proposed framework.

The current work is to clarify that the research is concerned solely with the static-image-based scene classification of dance arts, excluding medical imaging, video sequence modeling, and hybrid CNN-RNN architectures like CNN-LSTM. While CNN-LSTM models have been used in the realm of medical diagnosis (CT scan analysis, for example, for lung cancer) and action recognition, the proposed framework differs widely from such applications in terms of purpose and design. Among the most important aspects that distinguish this framework from others is the fact that the proposed method does not incorporate an LSTM for temporal modeling and only works with static images.

Even though there may be advantages in sequence analysis for action recognition through video, this paper will deal with the classification of individual dance scenes inside a visual semantic classification framework based on factors such as costume, pose, and composition; thus, it stands apart from both medical image analysis and video-action recognition paradigms.

The novelty of the approach lies in combining a modified version of the Squeeze-and-Excitation Network (SENet) with the Advanced Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA) for the purpose of channel-wise feature recalibration and hyperparameter optimization toward fine-grained artistic scene classification.

Unlike previous works that employ standard CNNs or handcrafted features, we introduce an adaptive attention mechanism capable of emphasizing discriminative visual cues in real time, such as pose, costume texture, and lighting, that are pertinent to discriminating among similar visual elements of different dance genres.

Furthermore, the ABWOA improves Levy flight and chaotic search strategies over other classic optimizers (e.g., PSO, GA) to achieve faster convergence and better generalization in high-dimensional hyperparameter spaces. These contributions, specific to the challenges of cultural and artistic scene classification, as opposed to medical diagnostics, thus carve out a completely distinct and innovative path for AI-centered investigative work in heritage and performance analysis.

Literature review

During the last decade, scene classification has come a long way, especially with the advent of deep learning techniques from traditional computer vision methods. Various hybrid architectures, feature fusion strategies, and optimization techniques have been explored by researchers in order to improve classification accuracy across diverse application domains including remote sensing, sports monitoring, dance art analysis, and so forth.

Several works have focused on combining the classical CNNs with additional modules such as recurrent networks or attention mechanisms, in order to capture better the spatial and temporal dependencies present within visual data.

Dong et al.12 used the Inception- LSTM (long short-term memory) hybrid model. Initially, a hand-crafted feature was coupled with the deep learning feature that was taken from the pretrained Inception-V3 model. After that, the various features were integrated and fed into the batch normalization (BN) layer. Second, the BN layer served as a bridge to integrate the LSTM model, which included a Softmax classifier, with the InceptionV3 model. The many high-resolution remote sensing scene photos were analyzed for characteristics and categorized using the LSTM model. The entire suggested model could be trained consistently. The efficacy of the suggested model was confirmed using three distinct datasets: the SIRI-WHU dataset, the NWPU-RESISC45 dataset, and the UC Merced dataset. The outcomes demonstrated that the suggested Inception-LSTM model performed exceptionally well in the scene classification challenge.

Li13 employed a deep convolutional neural network based on differential evolution to increase the scene categorization accuracy for dance art videos. First, the human silhouette in the video’s key frames was identified using the Canny operator in YCbCr color space. Following that, human target tracking and labeling were implemented using the AdaBoost algorithm based on cascade structure, and the development and maintenance of weak classifiers were examined. The convolutional neural network’s structural parameters were then optimized using a differential evolution method, and the accuracy of the optimization solution was increased by using an adaptive strategy for the differential evolution algorithm’s scaling factor. In order to get consistent scene classification results, the tagged videos were then trained using the enhanced deep convolutional neural network. The experimental findings demonstrated that good scene classification performance could be achieved by appropriately adjusting the convolutional kernel size of the convolutional neural network and the crossover rate of differential evolution. The suggested method was shown to be applicable for classifying dance art scenes due to its high accuracy and low root-mean-square error.

Yang et al.14 suggested a deep learning-based study on real-time scene detection of sports dancing competitions to enhance the real-time detection effect. To achieve the seamless processing of sawtooth and mosaic information produced by panoramic mapping, the gathered scene image was grayed using the weighted average approach, and the optimal image interpolation was determined using the deep learning method. The panorama of the competition scenario was created by projecting the processed scene information onto the visual plane once the cube model was chosen. The moving target was finally obtained by calculating the changes between neighboring image frames in addition to the three-frame difference. According to the test results, professional dancers’ motion detection accuracy could surpass 75.0%, while amateur dancers could surpass 64.2%.

Zakry et al.15 suggested the use of deep learning models to categorize amateur dancers’ movies based on how their motions and actions resemble those of other dance classes. In this study, several frames of actions from multiple dancing films were utilized to train the AlexNet, ResNet, and SqueezeNet models for label prediction. The models’ classification accuracy throughout each training epoch was compared. According to the study, the deep learning models’ average classification accuracy was 94.9669%, which was on par with previous methods for classifying dances.

He et al.16 conducted deep learning model, which focused more on cases that were hard to distinguish across comparable scenarios. The main concept was to guide model learning by using the level of difficulty across categories. The technique used an autoencoder to identify different scene distributions in the samples, and then used the differences between distributions to build a similarity matrix. The scene categorization module then implemented a stacking ensemble framework, with the base layer capturing sample representations from shallow to deep levels using different neural networks. The usefulness and superiority of the suggested method over the state-of-the-art techniques were proven by thorough empirical assessments using benchmarks for remote sensing scenes.

Nevertheless, there are some limitations that prevail in the current approaches. A fixed architecture of the network has been employed commonly without any adaptive recalibration mechanism for dynamically improving the importance of features during the training. The other works mostly discuss using optimization algorithms like differential evolution or genetic algorithm but their global search capability along with convergence efficiency usually doesn’t suffice for high-dimensional parameters of deep learning.

Real-time applications like dance competition monitoring also suffer from computational latency and inconsistency in processing frames. Ensemble methods and autoencoder-based similarity modeling have shown promise in discriminating hard-to-discriminate classes; however, their complexities and training overhead can prove prohibitive to their practical deployment.

Therefore, a need still exists for a lightweight yet powerful architecture that can deliver better sensitivity of features along with an efficient and robust optimization strategy typically tailored for dance scene classification tasks.

Dataset description

The evaluation of the proposed framework occurs on the Dance5K dataset, which is a large-scale multi-modal benchmark for human motion analysis and dance scene understanding. The dataset has been made publicly available as part of the Awesome-WFlow collection17, which provides high-quality dance videos synchronized with motion capture, 2D pose estimations, audio, and metadata annotations. It comprises 5,000 images from RGB dance videos uniformly sampled across ten classes: Ballet, Hip-hop, Bharatanatyam, Contemporary, Salsa, Flamenco, Kathak, Ballroom, Tap dance, and Breakdance. Each of the ten classes holds exactly 500 images, thereby ensuring an ideal class balance that excludes bias due to sample size disparity. Figure 2 portrays the sample images extracted from the dance scene dataset.

Sample images from dance scene dataset.

The images are in high resolution (512 × 512 pixels) and present a single performer with diverse poses, attires, lighting conditions, and backgrounds. The dataset is stratified and split through random sampling such that the class distribution is maintained across all subsets, comprising 70% for training (3,500 images), 15% for validation (750 images), and 15% for testing (750 images). This makes sure there will be no bias in the performance evaluation and allows proper hyperparameter tuning through cross-validation.

In order to exclude the possibility of temporal or identity-based leakage, we split our dataset at the video rather than frame level. In particular, the Dance5K dataset is comprised of 500 different videos of dance performances (50 videos per class), and out of a single video, there were 10 equal timeframes evenly spaced and formed the 5,000-image benchmark. In the process of partitioning, each frame of a particular source video received only one of the three subsets, namely, 350 videos (3,500 frames) to training, 75 videos (750 frames) to validation and 75 videos (750 frames) to testing. Here, it guarantees that training and evaluation sets are separated on the video level, meaning that the model does not exploit the near-duplicate frames across splits.

In addition, we performed another video-independent cross-validation at 5 folds in which in every fold, the videos (not images) were randomly assigned to the other folds and the balance of the classes maintained. Proposed methodology

This section describes the methodology used in this research for solving the problem of dance scenes classification using deep learning techniques. The framework proposed is the incorporation of a modified Squeeze-and-Excitation Network (SENet), along with an advanced variant of the Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA), to improve class identification and generalization performance. The methodology is organized into four major portions: dataset description, network architecture design, optimization strategy, and training protocol.

Network architecture specifications

Based on modified SENet-50 architecture, the proposed framework employs it as its feature-extracting backbone. The network starts with an initial convolution layer of 64 filters with a 7 × 7 size, a stride of 2, and a padding of 3, followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation function. Next, a 3 × 3 max-pooling layer with stride 2 is applied to speed up the reduction of spatial dimensions in the network.

The main portion of the architecture has four residual stages (conv2_x to conv5_x), each containing many bottleneck blocks enhanced with Squeeze and Excitation (SE) modules. The first bottleneck block contains a standard 1 × 1 convolution (64 channels) for input dimensionality reduction, followed by a convolution with a 3 × 3 kernel (64 channels) for spatial filtering, and rounded off with yet another 1 × 1 convolution (256 channels) to restore the dimensions.

ReLU activation is applied after the 3 × 3 convolution. The SE module is inserted immediately following the final convolution of each block; it consists of global average pooling (termed ‘squeeze’), followed by fully connected layers (with a reduction ratio of r = 16), and terminated by sigmoid activation (termed ‘excitation’) that redistributes the scale of channel-wise feature maps. The overall architecture integrates 50 of such SE-residual blocks. Global average pooling follows the last stage, then a fully connected layer with 10 output units (corresponding to the 10 dance classes) and softmax activation for classification.

All hyperparameters, namely learning rate, batch size, optimizer type, weight decay, and dropout rate, were optimized using the Advanced Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA), as detailed in Table 1. This architecture enables adaptive recalibration of channel responses; consequently, the model can become more sensitive to subtle stylistic cues like posture, texture of the costume, and dynamic patterns of movement pertaining to each dance genre.

The ABWOA employed for feature optimization in this research is a hybrid metaheuristic that improves hyperparameter tuning of deep learning models by merging the qualities of the original BWO with two powerful random search-enhancing strategies, Lévy flight and chaotic search strategy, to establish an effective search mechanism.

The ABWOA improves upon the BWO because it circumvents certain disadvantages associated with it, like exploration diversity and susceptibility to premature convergence. The mechanism of Lévy flight is introduced for long random jumps in the search space to escape local optima more efficiently while exploring along distances, imitating the beluga whales foraging pattern in the wild. In addition, a sinusoidal chaotic map to modulate the exploitation phase was introduced so that the search could disturb dynamically, promoting sustained population diversity and preventing stagnation in the region of suboptimal solutions.

The incorporation of a “whale fall” mechanism within the algorithm, inspired by the natural phenomenon of deceased whales sinking onto the ocean floor, reinitializes poorly performing search agents to new positions with a certain probability, thereby keeping the population alive and open to continuous exploration. All of these working together give rise to a balanced and adaptive optimization process that will navigate efficiently through the high-dimensional hyperparameter space, which consists of learning rate, batch size, and regularization parameters, to ensure fast convergence to robust globally optimal configurations. This hybrid design is not just a joint use of some methods without deep consideration; rather, this integration takes advantage of biology and mathematics to theoretically justify it as an improvement to the speed and quality of optimization, producing modally superior performance in dance scene classification.

To affirm the reproducibility of the hyperparameter optimization process, a detailed yet simple methodology and flowgraph of the ABWOA have been given. The optimization process is a population-based iterative search in which each “whale” stands for a candidate hyperparameter setting (e.g., learning rate, batch size, optimizer type). Figure 5 shows the flow diagram of ABWOA-based hyperparameter optimization.

Flowchart of hyperparameter optimization using ABWOA.

It starts with a random setup of its population, then its fitness is assessed on model training and validation. According to a dynamic balance factor, each whale updates its location via Lévy flight for global exploration or uses a chaotic-guided hunting method for local exploitation. Periodically, poor-performing whales are reset by the “whale fall” mechanism to preserve the diversity of the population and avoid early convergence.

The training pipeline consisted of two stages: the first is the initial training of the SENet model over a large sample with default hyperparameters, which is followed by iteration to refinement by the ABWOA. In this phase, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum (0.9), a fixed learning rate of 10E-3, and the same loss function mentioned above were used. When a baseline performance is reached, the loop of optimization based on ABWOA is started. Figure 6 shows the Block diagram of modified SENet architecture.

As shown in Fig. 6, the layout of the modified SENet is shown. As it can be observed, the blocks of Squeeze-and-Excitation were added to the CNN foundation, which highlights how the recalibration of the features assists in enhancing the classification accuracy.

Every generation of the optimization process consists of four major steps; first, creating a population of hyperparameter configurations; second, training the SENet model for each configuration; third, evaluation of the model’s performance on the validation set; and fourth, updating the whale positions according to the fitness score for the search to converge to the good hyperparameters. The stochastic optimization algorithm that will be used to update the trainable parameters of the model during training is the so-called Optimizer Type entry in Table 1. Under this paper, the ABWOA considers optimizer type as an integer hyperparameter that has three choices namely, Adam, SGD, and RMSprop. The optimizer controls the translation of the gradients into parameter updates affecting the convergence rate and stability and the overall generalization.

Adam uses the combination of adaptive learning rates and momentum, and hence, it is effective at various tasks; SGD with momentum makes it simple and effective in terms of generalization, provided it is well-tuned; RMSprop adjusts the learning rate per parameter according to the size of recent gradients. ABWOA independently considers these options when searching through hyperparameters and chooses one that has the best validation performance.

We found in our last set-up that ABWOA with SGD momentum (0.9) performed better and better than Adam or RMSprop in convergence behavior and validation loss on this particular fine-grained dance scene classification task. Simulations and results

This part illustrates the execution of tests and throws light on the results obtained from the proposed architecture to classify dance scenes in dance art. All the experiments in this work have been performed under MATLAB R2019b using Deep Learning Toolbox on a desktop with configuration Intel Core i7-10700 K CPU, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080-based GPU, and 64 GB RAM.

This proposed blending has modification of the Squeeze-and-Excitation Network (SENet) with Advanced Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA) for increasing the feature recalibration power while developing hyperparameter optimization at the same time.

All tests are being held on the Dance5K dataset containing 5,000 RGB images for ten different dance forms: Ballet, Hip-hop, Bharatanatyam, Contemporary, Salsa, Flamenco, Kathak, Ballroom, Tap dance, and Breakdance. Each category contains 500 images. Standard preprocessing steps involved resizing the images to dimensions of 224 × 224 pixels, normalization using ImageNet statistics, and then using data augmentation techniques such as random flipping, rotation, brightness adjustment, and zooming.

Figure 7 shows the block diagram of the proposed methodology. Broadly speaking, the process initiates with the implementation of SENet architecture with Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks; then, the dataset is preprocessed and augmented.

The block diagram of the proposed methodology.

A baseline model is trained by default hyperparameters. After that, the Adaptive Binary Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA) optimizes the hyperparameters of the model.

An initial population of candidate hyperparameter sets was randomly generated such that in one generation after the other, each candidate (whale) was used to train the SENet model. Validation set results were evaluated, and based on accuracy fitness scores were assigned.

Whale positions are updated according to the optimization rules of ABWOA and iterated until the convergence criteria are met or the maximum number of generations is reached. The retrieved optimal hyperparameter set from this process is retrained using the SENet model with both training and validation data. Finally, the fully trained model is evaluated over the test set for generalization performance.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed model, we evaluated it against six state-of-the-art deep learning models, namely, ResNet-507, VGG-1625, Inception-v326, DenseNet-20127, EfficientNet-B028, and MobileNetV210. For fair comparison, all models were trained under the same conditions. The evaluation was done based on a large set of classification metrics: Accuracy (ACC), Specificity (SP), Precision (PR), Sensitivity/Recall (SE), F1-score (FS), Matthew’s Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Area Under the Curve (AUC), and Kappa Score.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel deep learning framework was introduced for scene classification in dance art by integrating a modified Squeeze-and-Excitation Network (SENet) with an Advanced Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm (ABWOA), aiming to enhance feature recalibration and optimize CNN hyperparameters, simultaneously. The methodology encompassed dataset preprocessing using normalization and data augmentation, attention-based architecture design through SENet blocks, and ABWOA-driven hyperparameter tuning to improve convergence and avoid local optima. Evaluated on the Dance5K dataset, which includes five distinct dance styles: ballet, hip-hop, contemporary, jazz, and freestyle. The proposed model outperformed all other existing architectures on different metrics, achieving an average test accuracy of 92.6%, F1-score of 96.4%, and Matthew’s Correlation Coefficient of 0.95. With these numbers, it became clear that SENet is adequate for concentrating on discriminative visual features, whereas ABWOA is superior to training parameter optimization. Following testing of statistical significance, the proposed method provided better performance than the six state-of-the-art models (including ResNet-50 and EfficientNet-B0) with p-values lower than 0.001. The model exhibited excellent generalization abilities across cross-validation folds but its disadvantages were based on static images, reduced interpretability, and an inflexible adjustment to new and culturally different forms of dance. Although the proposed framework performs reasonably well on the Dance5K dataset, its generalization to culturally diverse and thus underrepresented forms of dancing-say maybe African tribal dancing, Southeast Asian classical dances (e.g., Cambodian Apsara or Indonesian Javanese), and Indigenous ceremonial movements-presents a possible limitation. The present dataset largely contains Western and South Asian styles (e.g., ballet, hip hop, Bharatanatyam, Kathak), which likely do not encapsulate the kinematic, gestural, and sartorial diversity present in some other global traditions. Differences in body adornment, rhythmic footwork, facial expressions, and symbolic gestures unique to specific cultures may not have been well represented or learned by the model and thus may introduce a bias or erode the accuracy of models applied to settings outside the trained domain. On top of that, the inputting of static images restricts the model from adequately capturing the temporal dynamics of many traditional dances, which strongly rely on a narrative path and rhythmic cycles. Hence, while the attention mechanism and its optimized architecture do increase feature discrimination for the evaluated classes, crosstalk should be used in cross-cultural application without any further validation and inclusion of ethnically divergent multimodal trained data. Real-time deployment of the proposed framework in dynamic environments such as dance competitions, live performances, and interactive educational applications will be made possible through future works involving model compression and efficient architecture design using lightweight CNN variants. As stated previously, the current ABWOA-optimized SENet is highly accurate but operates on very high resolution (512 × 512) images and, therefore, uses too much computational power to have any suitability for edge devices or low-latency applications in future applications. The next step will be to integrate lightweight backbone networks, such as MobileNetV3, EfficientNet-Lite or GhostNet, that are designed for mobile and embedded visions without significantly sacrificing accuracy. Mobile GPUs can achieve inference speeds of more than 30 FPS due to the parameter and FLOP reduction achieved through depthwise separable convolutions, channel pruning, and compound scaling. In addition, knowledge distillation will be considered, whereby the trained SENet will function as a teacher model to instruct a smaller student network while retaining attention-based feature recalibration in a compact footprint. The latency-aware variant of ABWOA, which incorporates inference speed and memory usage into the fitness function, is going to be employed for hyperparameter optimization pipeline tailoring, to ensure a trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. The second-direction extension of the framework into video sequences will encompass temporal modeling via lightweight 3D CNNs (MobileNetV2 + TDN, for example) or Transformer-based efficient attention (e.g., MobileViT), ensuring timely recognition of choreographic transitions and movement dynamics. This will be tested for deployment across mobile applications (Android/iOS) as well as web platforms using TensorFlow Lite and ONNX Runtime towards cross-platform compatibility. These advances will ensure real-world use in dance pedagogy, automated judging systems, and preservation tools for cultural heritage, where immediacy, accessibility, and low computational overhead are critical.
