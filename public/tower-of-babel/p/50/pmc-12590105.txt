Active Anthocyanin Degradation in Solanum macranthum Flowers Involves Both Peroxidase and β‐Glucosidase Enzymes
Authors: Stalin Nadarajan, Varun Kumar, Rinat Ovadia, Anita Kumari, Adi Doron‐Faigenboim, Bipin Singh, Michal Oren‐Shamir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnthocyanin coloration of fruit, foliage, and flowers is dependent both on pigment synthesis and degradation. Our previous comprehensive study on in planta anthocyanin degradation was conducted on the purple Brunfelsia calycina flowers, whitening due to a one‐step process, involving a single vacuolar peroxidase. Here, we reveal a novel two‐step in planta degradation process in the purple Solanum macranthum flowers, as they whiten. This process involves both vacuolar β‐glucosidases and peroxidases, similar to the in vitro processes described in fruit juices, with β‐glucosidase enzymes stripping the pigments from their sugar moieties, followed by enzymes oxidizing the aglycones. We show that the activities of both β‐glucosidase and peroxidase are crucial for the in planta degradation to occur in S. macranthum flowers. A specific vacuolar β‐glucosidase (SmBGL7) and two peroxidase isozymes (SmPrx01, SmPrx02) increased in their activity parallel to the degradation process. One vacuolar β‐glucosidase gene and two peroxidase genes are induced in the flower tissue just prior to the onset of anthocyanin degradation, with MWs related to those found for the corresponding isozymes of all three enzymes. SmPrx01 has an identical active site proximal heme‐ligand signature sequence to the B. calycina degrading peroxidase gene, BcPrx01, and binds both malvidin (the main aglycone in B. calycina ) and petunidin (the main aglycone in S. macranthum) equally. The second peroxidase, unique to S. macranthum, SmPrx02, has a stronger binding to petunidin than to malvidin, suggesting potential variability and synergistic involvement of peroxidases in anthocyanin degradation.Keywords: anthocyanin degradation, flower pigmentation, Solanum macranthum, vacuolar β‐glucosidase, vacuolar peroxidase

Introduction

Anthocyanins are the largest group of plant pigments, ranging in color from pink to violet and blue. They are phenolic compounds, accumulating in the vacuoles, and comprising two aromatic rings and an oxygen‐containing heterocyclic ring, modified with glycosyl and aromatic or aliphatic acyl moieties to yield more than 1000 different pigment molecules (Andersen and Jordheim 2006). Anthocyanins play major roles in plants as signals for pollinators, seed dispersers, and protectants of vegetative tissues from drastic environmental conditions such as high UV light (Chalker‐Scott 1999; Steyn et al. 2002). The ability of anthocyanins to absorb high‐energy wavelengths helps mitigate photoinhibition, preserving the integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus under harsh conditions.

The biosynthesis of anthocyanins, as part of the phenylpropanoid pathway, is well known and tightly controlled, regulated by a complex interplay of transcription factors and environmental cues (Grotewold 2006; Winkel‐Shirley 2001). However, plant pigmentation is also dependent on the stability of the anthocyanins in the plant vacuoles and on the enzymatic degradation of these pigments. There are several examples in which anthocyanin degradation is clearly controlled and induced when beneficial to the plant. One such example is the degradation occurring in vegetative tissues due to changing light conditions. Since anthocyanins absorb UV light, vegetative tissues, and more specifically leaves, often accumulate anthocyanins at high light intensities and low temperatures, to shield the photosynthetic apparatus from damaging light (Oren‐Shamir 2009). When these conditions change, plants can actively degrade the pigments, enabling penetration of more light into the tissues (Oren‐Shamir and Levi‐Nissim 1997a, 1997b). Similarly, young developing fruit often accumulate anthocyanins to protect their tissue from high light and actively degrade these pigments during growth and maturation, before the accumulation of pigments in the ripe fruit (Borovsky et al. 2004; Oren‐Shamir 2009).

Flower color changes represent a prominent example of regulated anthocyanin degradation, functioning as key visual signals to attract pollinators (Ruxton and Schaefer 2016). This change may be due to a change in the acidity of the vacuoles, causing a visual change in anthocyanin pigmentation, synthesis of new anthocyanins, or induced active degradation of anthocyanins at specific developmental stages. Our previous research on Brunfelsia calycina has shown that flowers change in distinct color transformation from dark purple to white within 3 days postopening, and before the onset of senescence (Bar‐Akiva et al. 2010; Vaknin et al. 2005), as a signal for night moth pollinators (Filipowicz and Renner 2012).

Color loss in flowers as a signal for pollinators is an ideal system for studying active anthocyanin degradation, since the onset of degradation is tightly correlated to specific flower developmental stages. The mechanism of active in planta anthocyanin degradation has been revealed in B. calycina (Solanaceae) flowers (Vaknin et al. 2005; Zipor et al. 2015). B. calycina served as a model for studying the mechanism of active degradation, due to the very specific stage in which flowers begin to degrade their pigments and the fact that this process also occurs in detached flowers dipped in a sucrose solution (Vaknin et al. 2005). We previously reported that a single vacuolar peroxidase, BcPrx01, is the key enzyme responsible for the rapid pigment degradation in these flowers. The gene coding for this peroxidase was induced after flower opening, and the enzyme activity of its product, BcPrx01, was detected in flowers at least 1 day after they opened (Zipor et al. 2015).

Based on these findings, the present study explores the biochemical and transcriptomic analysis underlying anthocyanin degradation in Solanum macranthum flowers, commonly known as the “Giant Potato Tree”—another species within the Solanaceae family. These star‐shaped flowers change color from purple to white within 3–4 days, creating a multicolored effect on the tree (Figure 1A,B). Alongside a slight flower fragrance, the change in flower color serves as a signal to their pollinators, which include carpenter and bumble bees. Here, we present that anthocyanin degradation in S. macranthum follows a coordinated two‐step enzymatic mechanism involving both vacuolar β‐glucosidases and distinct peroxidase isoforms. We identify specific isozymes within these enzyme families that show increased expression and activity parallel to the onset of degradation. A transcriptomic study on the changes in these two enzyme families during the first days after flower opening and subsequent color change complements our biochemical findings. Specific candidate genes encoding for the potential anthocyanin‐degrading isozymes from both β‐glucosidase and peroxidase families are induced prior to degradation. This study shows the first evidence of an in‐planta two‐step anthocyanin degradation process and provides insight into the dynamics between multiple peroxidase isoforms, highlighting differential substrate affinities.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material

S. macranthum plants, propagated via cutting in our greenhouse, were grown in 50 L pots both in a polyethylene greenhouse and outdoors in Rishon LeZion, Israel. Flowers were categorized into distinct developmental stages from the bud stage 2 days before flower opening up to 4 days after opening (D4) (Figure 1A). Four to five flowers from selected stages were collected for the different in vitro analyses.

In Vitro Flower Growth Conditions

Flowers of S. macranthum were detached from the plant at stages D‐4h (4 h before flower opening), D0, and D1, and floated in a 2% sucrose solution (pH 5.5) using a custom‐made Styrofoam support to prevent wetting of the flower petals (Figure 1E). Sodium dichloroisocyanurate (TOG‐6) was added to the sucrose growth solution as an antimicrobial agent. The detached flowers were maintained under controlled conditions with a temperature of 23°C, 70% relative humidity, and an irradiance of 10 μmol m−2 s−1 provided by cool white fluorescent lamps, under a 12‐h photoperiod.

Treatments With Inhibitors

Flowers (D‐4h, D0, D1) were treated with specific inhibitors targeting mRNA synthesis (30 μM actinomycin D), protein synthesis (0.5 mM cycloheximide), and β‐glucosidase (0.5 M d‐gluconic acid). The inhibitors were incorporated into the 2% sucrose solution. Observation of flower color change, morphology, size, and other parameters was recorded for 3 days posttreatment. After photographing, petals were weighed, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Anthocyanin Extraction and Quantification

Anthocyanins were extracted from frozen flower petals (0.2–0.5 g) using a mortar and pestle, followed by the addition of 2 mL of extraction solvent composed of methanol:water:acetic acid (11:5:1, v/v/v) (Markham and Ofman 1993). The anthocyanin content of the fresh and inhibitor‐treated petals was quantified spectrometrically at 530 nm using a UV‐2401 PC UV–VIS recording spectrophotometer (Shimadzu). For hydrolysis, the samples were concentrated to 0.5 mL and then mixed with an equal volume of methanol and 2 N HCl. The samples were hydrolyzed by boiling for 1 h and filtered through a 0.45‐μM polyvinylidene difluoride filter (Nalgene).

Anthocyanin profiles were determined using an HPLC instrument (LC‐2050C 3D Shimadzu) equipped with a photodiode array detector. Extracts were injected into an RP‐18 column (Vydac 201TP54) and separated at 27°C. Solvent A consisted of acidified water (H2O, pH 2.3), and solvent B comprised a mixture of water: acetonitrile:acetic acid (107:50:40, pH 2.3). The flow rate was maintained at 0.5 mL min−1 under a linear gradient consisting of an A:B ratio of 4:1 to 3:7 over 45 min, and holding at 3:7 for an additional 10 min (Oliva et al. 2015). Anthocyanins were eluted and identified based on their retention times and UV–visible absorption spectra at 250–600 nm with those of authentic purified standards, and results were expressed as total peak area per gram fresh weight.

Sample Preparation for Biochemical Assays

Total proteins were extracted from petals by grinding the petals in liquid nitrogen, followed by the addition of sodium acetate extraction buffer (10 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5, 0.025 g mL−1 insoluble polyvinylpolypyrrolidone [PVPP], 10 mM CaCl2, 5 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], 10 mM thiourea, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride [PMSF]). The extracts were centrifuged at 21,000g for 20 min to separate the total soluble protein extracts. Soluble protein extracts were precipitated using 30%–50%, 50%–85% ammonium sulfate, and the precipitates were resuspended and dialyzed against 10 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5, at 4°C (based on Zipor et al. 2015). The 50%–85% precipitated soluble protein fraction was enriched in enzyme activities. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford assay (Bio‐Rad) using bovine serum albumin as a standard.

Anthocyanin Degradation Assay

Anthocyanins extraction from S. macranthum petals was performed according to Markham and Ofman (1993) as mentioned above. The extract was dried in a speedvac at room temperature (RT) (Savant SPD111V; Thermo Scientific) and resuspended in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 4. Degradation assays were performed at RT for 5 min using a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu) as described in Zipor et al. (2015).

β‐Glucosidase Activity

The β‐glucosidase activity assay was performed as described by Zipor et al. (2015), with slight modifications. The assay involved incubating 150 μL of reaction volume containing 10 μg total protein samples in a 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5) with 5 mM 4‐methylumbelliferyl β‐d‐glucopyranoside (MUGlc, Sigma‐Aldrich) as a synthetic substrate and d‐gluconic acid (GlcA) as an inhibitor. The reaction was stopped by adding 150 μL of 0.4 M sodium carbonate after an hour of incubation in the dark at 37°C. Fluorescence emission was recorded at 460 nm after excitation at 360 nm.

Peroxidase and β‐Glucosidase Activities on Seminative PAGE

Peroxidase isozymes were separated using seminative PAGE with 10% polyacrylamide gel with 0.1% SDS, followed by activity assessment through 4‐chloro‐1‐naphthol staining in the presence of 5 mM H2O2. Protein samples for loading on a seminative PAGE were prepared with glycerol and bromophenol blue. MUGlc zymogram analysis was used for in‐gel β‐glucosidase activity as described previously (Li et al. 2016), with some modifications. Samples were analyzed by native PAGE with 10% and 5% polyacrylamide separation and stacking gels, respectively. Upon completion of electrophoresis, the gel was washed twice with 100 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) followed by the addition of 10 mM MUGlc substrate in 100 mM sodium acetate buffer and incubated for 20 min at 45°C. The fluorescence bands in the gel were visualized under a UV gel documentation system (UVIDOC‐HD6 PLUS 26MX).

RNA Isolation

Total RNA was extracted using the Quick‐RNA Plant Miniprep Kit from Zymo Research, following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was measured using the NanoPhotometer NP80 from IMPLEN. To assess RNA integrity, electrophoresis was conducted on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel, and the RNA bands were visualized using ethidium bromide staining.

Illumina Sequencing

RNA sequencing was carried out by Macrogen Europe B.V. (The Netherlands). In brief, cDNA libraries were synthesized from the isolated RNA using the TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit v2 (Illumina). The resulting cDNA underwent paired‐end sequencing, generating 100 bp reads on the Illumina sequencing platform. Base call files (BCL) were transformed into FASTQ format using the bcl2fastq conversion software from Illumina. The reliability of the RNA sequencing data across the 16 samples was analyzed through a Pearson correlation matrix, principal component analysis (PCA), and clustering of all differentially expressed genes (Figure S1).

RNA‐Seq Data Processing

RNA‐Seq Data Processing and De Novo Transcriptome Assembly

Due to the absence of a reference genome for S. macranthum, a de novo transcriptome assembly was performed using the Trinity software (version: v2.9.1) as described by Grabherr et al. (2011). This method allowed the reconstruction of full‐length transcripts from RNA sequencing data, allowing for the identification of novel genes and isoforms. The RNA sequencing reads were first assembled into transcripts, forming the basis for downstream expression analysis. The transcript quantification from the RNA‐Seq data was performed using the Bowtie2 aligner (Langmead et al. 2009) and the expectation maximization method (RSEM) by estimating maximum likelihood expression levels (Li and Dewey 2011). RSEM estimates gene and isoform expression levels using a maximum likelihood framework, providing accurate quantification even in the absence of a reference genome. Following the Trinity protocol (Haas et al. 2013), expression values were normalized using the TMM (trimmed mean of M‐values) method. FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) values were calculated to standardize expression across samples.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (Love et al. 2014). Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if they exhibited a fold change of ≥ 2 and a false discovery rate (FDR)‐adjusted p ≤ 0.05, based on the Benjamini–Hochberg correction method (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). To visualize expression patterns and sample relationships, PCA and heatmaps were generated using tools from the Bioconductor suite in R (Gentleman et al. 2004), enabling a clear representation of the expression patterns and sample clustering.

Sequence Similarity Search and Functional Annotation

To functionally characterize the assembled transcriptome, we conducted sequence similarity searches using the merged transcript dataset as a query. The DIAMOND program (Buchfink et al. 2015) was used to search against the NCBI nonredundant (nr) protein database, enabling rapid identification of homologous protein sequences. In addition, homologous sequences were identified by conducting BLASTx searches against the Swiss‐Prot database (Altschul et al. 1990), using an E‐value threshold of 1e–5 to ensure high‐confidence matches.

To further refine functional predictions, we conducted BLASTx searches against well‐annotated, phylogenetically related species databases, including Solyc (tomato), Petunia, and TAIR (Arabidopsis). This comparative approach helped identify likely coding transcripts and infer their biological roles. The annotated results were imported into Blast2GO version 4.0 (Conesa et al. 2005), which facilitated the assignment of gene ontology (GO) terms, enzyme codes, and KEGG pathway annotations. To further explore the biological significance of differentially expressed genes, enrichment analysis was performed using KOBAS 3.0 (Xie et al. 2011), accessible at http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/kobas3/. This analysis identified significantly enriched GO categories and KEGG pathways, providing insights into the biological processes and molecular functions associated with the observed transcriptional changes.

Physicochemical Properties and Subcellular Location Prediction

Physicochemical properties such as theoretical molecular weight (MW) and isoelectric points (pI) of putative peroxidases and β‐glucosidases were calculated using ExPASY ProtParam tools (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The signal peptide and subcellular localization of both peroxidases and β‐glucosidases protein predictions were performed with SignalP 6.0 and DeepLoc servers (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP‐6.0/; https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc‐2.1/), respectively.

Phylogenetic Analysis

The evolutionary phylogenetic analyses were conducted in MEGA 11 software (Tamura et al. 2021; https://www.megasoftware.net/) using the maximum likelihood method and the Jones–Taylor–Thornton (JTT) matrix‐based model (Jones et al. 1992). The bootstrap consensus tree inferred from 1000 replicates represents the evolutionary history of the taxa analyzed. ClustalW with default parameters was used for multisequence alignments of peroxidases and β‐glucosidases amino acid sequences. Phylogenetic analysis of each peroxidase and β‐glucosidase sequence from S. macranthum was BLAST against Solanum lycopersicum , Petunia auxillaris, and B. calycina peroxidases and β‐glucosidases protein sequences for phylogenetic tree construction. The bootstrap consensus tree was then visualized and formatted by using the iTOL online tool.

Molecular Docking and Modelling

The three‐dimensional structures of SmPRX01, SmPRX02, and BcPRX01 were generated through Swiss‐Model using their respective amino acid sequences. Swiss‐Model predicts the three‐dimensional structure of proteins using the homology modeling approach (Waterhouse et al. 2018). Molecular docking was used to generate bound structures with malvidin and petunidin using the SwissDock platform (Bugnon et al. 2024). All the noncovalent interactions between protein and ligand were profiled using PLIP (Adasme et al. 2021), and Pymol was used for the preparation of molecular interaction figures (Schrödinger LLC, 2025, The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0.5).

Statistical Analysis

All the statistical significance of the data was assessed through one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey–Kramer p < 0.05, utilizing JMP 14.0 (SAS Institute Inc.). The significance is expressed by different letters above the error bar.

Results

The Color Change of S. Macranthum Flowers Is Dependent Both on mRNA Induction and Protein Synthesis

S. macranthum flowers open in a dark purple color that changes to white within 3–4 days, while the flowers grow and increase in weight (Figure 1A). The anthocyanin concentration per flower decreases close to threefold while flower weight increases twofold within the first 2 days after flower opening (Figure 1C). These processes occur both when the flowers are still attached to the S. macranthum plant as well as when they are detached about 4 h before flower opening, and their pedicel is dipped in a sucrose solution (Figure 1E). There was no detectable change in the fragrance as the flowers grew and whitened.

The purple color of S. macranthum flowers is composed mainly of three anthocyanin aglycones, petunidin, delphinidin, and malvidin, all originating from the delphinidin biosynthetic pathway (Figure 1D). Three additional unidentified pigments were detected, possibly anthocyanins composed of these same aglycones that haven't been fully hydrolyzed. All six pigment components decrease in concentration as the flowers develop and whiten (Figure 1D). Furthermore, the nonhydrolyzed anthocyanins, with glycosyl and acyl moieties, also all decrease between D‐4h and D3 (Figure S2). These results suggest that the anthocyanin degradation processes occurring in the flowers are not biased for specific anthocyanins or their aglycones.

Anthocyanin degradation in S. macranthum is dependent on the induction of genes and the synthesis of proteins after D‐4h. When flowers were detached at stage D‐4h and dipped either in an mRNA inhibitor, actinomycin D, or a protein synthesis inhibitor, cycloheximide, they remained darker than control nontreated flowers, after 3 days (Figure 2A). The cycloheximide‐treated flowers remained dark purple apart from the petals' edges, and the actinomycin D flowers remained dark purple near their veins, but did fade a little in the petals. In both cases, the patterns are probably due to the amount of each inhibitor reaching the different petal areas. In contrast, flowers picked at D0, when they were still dark purple (Figure 1A), and treated with either actinomycin D or cycloheximide, faded in color to a light purple within 3 days. When flowers were picked and treated at D1 they became completely white, similar to controls (Figure 2A). Quantification of the anthocyanin concentration in the flowers revealed the same results, with a significant difference in color between controls and treated flowers seen only for flowers treated at D‐4h (Figure 2B,C). In both cases (cycloheximide and actinomycin D treatments), when the treatments were given at D0, anthocyanin concentrations were slightly higher than controls (not significant). This may be due to a variability in the exact stage at which the flowers were picked during D0. Unlike D‐4h, stage D0 lasts several hours.

Cycloheximide also inhibited anthocyanin degradation in S. macranthum flowers while still attached to the plant (Figure 2D). Treatments were performed by spraying flowers at D‐4h on the plant and monitoring their change in color. Here, the pattern due to inhibition was different from that on detached flowers, with dark purple patches on the flower petal. This difference is probably technical due to the different ways of application. The colored patches are due to spraying variations and the amount of inhibitor reaching a specific area on the petals. Application on the attached flowers involved manually opening the buds 4 h before their natural opening and spraying them with the inhibitor. Both results with attached and detached flowers suggest that induction of anthocyanin degradation occurs in flowers after D‐4h and before D1.

Both Peroxidase and β‐Glucosidase Activities Are Involved in Active Anthocyanin Degradation

Peroxidases are probable candidates for the in vivo active degradation of anthocyanins in flowers, since they are the main oxidizing enzymes located in the vacuoles, and are involved in anthocyanin degradation in B. calycina flowers (Zipor et al. 2015). To test their involvement in S. macranthum flowers, total protein extracts from D3 flowers were used as the source for the anthocyanin‐degrading enzymes, and total anthocyanin extracts from the flowers were the substrate. Stage D3 was chosen for total protein extracts based on the anthocyanin degradation curve (Figure 1C) and on the measurement of total peroxidase activity in S. macranthum flowers throughout their development (Figure 3A). Peroxidase activity increases in the flower petal of S. macranthum as they develop, from Days D‐4h through D4, with the highest activity at D3 and D4 (Figure 3A).

Active anthocyanin degradation was measured as the change in absorbance at 539 nm. Anthocyanin concentrations remained stable in the solution, with either preboiled or fresh protein extracts (Figure 3B). Only the combination of D3 protein extract and H2O2 resulted in active degradation of the anthocyanins, pointing to the involvement of peroxidase activity in this process. Furthermore, increasing concentrations of the extract in the presence of H2O2 increased the rate of degradation (Figure 3B).

In an attempt to enrich the crude extract with anthocyanin‐degrading peroxidase activity, total protein extracts were fractionated via ammonium sulfate precipitation, and the 50%–85% fraction was found to have a significantly higher rate of anthocyanin degradation (Figure 3B). This was also detected in a peroxidase activity gel, revealing much higher activity in the 50%–85% fraction (Figure 3C). A comparison between the active peroxidases in D‐4h and D3, both in the crude extract and enriched fraction, revealed a significant increase in two active peroxidases (Figure 3C). Much of the peroxidase activity seen in the gel is at high MW, due either to dimers or trimers of the proteins, or the limitation of the semi native gel to separate all. However, the only clear differences between D‐4h and D3 are the two isozymes above and below the 45 kDa marker (Figure 3C).

To test whether β‐glucosidase activity is also involved in the enzymatic degradation of anthocyanins, d‐gluconic acid, an inhibitor of β‐glucosidase, was added to the sucrose solution with detached flowers at different developmental stages, and their change in color and anthocyanin concentration were monitored 3 days after the treatment (Figure 4A,B). The d‐gluconic acid concentration chosen completely inhibited β‐glucosidase activity (Figure S3). Flowers treated with d‐gluconic acid at stage D‐4h remained dark purple after 3 days, in contrast to controls (Figure 4A), and their anthocyanin concentration was significantly higher (Figure 4B). When flowers were detached and treated with the inhibitor at D0, they had more coloration than controls after 3 days, and their anthocyanin concentration was slightly higher. However, at D1, treated and control flowers were similar, suggesting an involvement of β‐glucosidase activity in the degradation of S. macranthum anthocyanins. A β‐glucosidase activity gel, comparing the activity between D‐4h and D3 revealed one band significantly higher at D3 (Figure 4C). Interestingly, even though experiments with the detached flowers showed a very clear involvement of β‐glucosidase in the active anthocyanin degradation, the addition of d‐gluconic acid to the flowers' protein extract had no inhibitory effect on the degradation, but even increased the process (Figure 4D).

Change in Gene Expression Levels During Active Anthocyanin Degradation

The changes in gene expression levels during active anthocyanin degradation in S. macranthum flowers were followed along four developmental stages of the flowers, D‐4h, D0, D1, and D3 (Figure 5 and Table S1). Heat maps of the differentially expressed peroxidase and β‐glucosidase transcripts (Figure 5A,B), revealed genes from both families with an expression pattern that matches our biochemical results, namely induction between D‐4h and D0 (Figure 5A). Those genes included four peroxidases and three β‐glucosidases (Figure 5C). Among these candidate genes, only two peroxidases and one β‐glucosidase had vacuolar signal peptides, while the rest had secretion signal peptides. In summary, out of the 22 and 26 full‐length amino acid sequences of peroxidases and β‐glucosidases, respectively, three genes were found to be relevant candidates for active anthocyanin degradation in S. macranthum, SmPrx01, SmPrx02, and SmBGL7 (Figure 5C).

Phylogenetic and Molecular Docking Analyses of the S. macranthum Peroxidases

The two peroxidases identified here as potential enzymes involved in active anthocyanin degradation are mapped on separate branches of the phylogenetic tree comparing peroxidases from S. macranthum and three additional Solanaceae plants, including B. calycina (Figure 6A and Table S2). However, one of these two genes, namely SmPrx01, is on the same phylogenetic branch as the anthocyanin‐degrading peroxidase in B. calycina, BcPrx01 (Figure 6A, Zipor et al. 2015). These two genes, SmPrx01 and BrPrx01, have identical active sites and peroxidase proximal heme‐ligand signatures (Figure 6B), strengthening our assumption that SmPrx01 may be involved in the induced active anthocyanin degradation in S. macranthum. SmPrx02 is on a different phylogenetic branch and does not have significant sequence homologies on the active site and heme‐ligand signature.

Phylogenetic Analysis of S. macranthum β‐Glucosidase

Out of 26 full‐length β‐glucosidase DEGs, one vacuolar β‐glucosidase gene (SmBGL7) was identified as a candidate based on the phylogenetic study and sequence homology in comparison with S. macranthum, B. calycina, S. lycopersicum , and Petunia axillaris (Figure S4A). SmBGL7 had a relatively high sequence similarity to a B. calycina vacuolar β‐glucosidase BcBGL8 (Figure S4B).

Discussion

The most detailed enzymatic studies on anthocyanin degradation were performed on fruit extracts, due to the importance of color stability in fresh fruit juices. In these studies, a two‐step degradation process is suggested. The first step is stripping the anthocyanins of their glycosyl moieties via glucosidase enzyme activities, followed by oxidation of the aglycones by either cytoplasmic PPOs or vacuolar peroxidases. This mechanism was also suggested for the browning process of anthocyanin‐pigmented fruit, after harvest, such as litchi and blueberries (Oren‐Shamir 2009). In browning Litchi pericarps, the anthocyanin oxidation was suggested to occur via an epicatechin‐mediated laccase oxidation reaction (Fang et al. 2015). Both in fruit juices and postharvest fruit, decompartmentation of the tissue cells occurs, enabling enzymes from all cell organs to participate in the degradation of anthocyanins.

Several genome‐wide studies of various plants and plant organs reveal induction of peroxidase and β‐glucosidase genes in correlation with a decrease in anthocyanin pigmentation. One example is the induction of two peroxidases in lotus flowers during loss of anthocyanin pigmentation (Liu et al. 2022). Other examples are the induction of both peroxidases and β‐glucosidases during the red color loss in Kale leaves (Ren et al. 2019) and seasonal induced color transition in purple tea leaves (Maritim et al. 2021; Ren et al. 2019), and induction of peroxidase genes in ripening crabapple and grape berries, due to increased temperatures (Movahed et al. 2016; Rehman et al. 2017).

In an in planta system, such as S. macranthum and B. calycina flowers, which change color during their development, the degradation of anthocyanins is expected to occur in the intact vacuoles. Here we show, for the first time, evidence of a two‐step anthocyanin degradation process in planta, including stripping of the glycosyl moieties and then oxidation of the pigments. The change in color of developing S. macranthum flowers occurs after induction of vacuolar β‐glucosidase and peroxidase genes and increases in specific vacuolar‐located isozymes from both families, as they change color from purple to white. Our results suggest that both vacuolar β‐glucosidase and peroxidase enzymes are involved in the controlled pigment degradation process. These results differ from the anthocyanin degradation mechanism identified in our previous study in B. calycina flowers, where only one enzyme (the vacuolar peroxidase enzyme [BcPrx01]) was identified and characterized as the sole candidate responsible for the active in vivo anthocyanin degradation process (Zipor et al. 2015).

As mentioned above, the great advantage of studying active anthocyanin degradation in flowers changing color as a signal for pollinators is that the initiation of degradation correlates very tightly with a specific developmental stage in the flowers. Here, we pinpointed this time to the few hours before flower opening (D‐4h) until sometime during their first day after opening (D0). Inhibition of both mRNA and protein synthesis after this time resulted in degradation (Figures 2 and 3). A more frequent monitoring of the changes in gene expression level during D0 could pinpoint a more accurate timing of degradation initiation. In Brunfelsia flowers, the induction of pigment degradation occurs only after the flowers are fully open (Vaknin et al. 2005; Zipor et al. 2015).

We identified two peroxidase isozymes as probable candidates for the active anthocyanin degradation process occurring in S. macranthum flowers. These two isozymes were the only detected peroxidases that increased significantly in their activity between D‐4h and D3. Furthermore, the RNA‐Seq analysis of peroxidase genes also resulted in two candidate peroxidase genes (SmPrx01, 02) (Figure 5A,C). These two genes are the only peroxidase genes that are induced after stage D‐4h and before D1, and contain a vacuolar signal peptide. The two additional peroxidase genes induced contained an extracellular signal peptide, suggesting that these enzymes are in the cell wall, and therefore not detected in the soluble protein extract loaded on the activity gels. The predicted MWs (Figure 5A) of the two peroxidases based on their sequences were slightly lower than the two bands observed in the peroxidase gel (Figure 3C). These differences may be because the proteins in seminative gels are only partly denatured, migrating at a slower rate and resulting in an apparent increase in MW.

A strong indication of the role of one of the two S. macranthum identified peroxidases, SmPrx01, is its very close phylogenetic relation to the B. calycina anthocyanin degrading peroxidase, BcPrx01, and the homology in both their active sites and heme‐ligand signature (Figure 6). Both of these peroxidase genes (SmPrx01 and BcPrx01), from two different Solanaceae flowering plants, are induced in the flowers, and the activity of their products increased in correlation with the degradation. The second S. macranthum peroxidase induced, SmPrx02, is phylogenetically distant from BcPrx01 and SmPrx01, but molecular docking analyses revealed a stronger affinity of this enzyme to the main aglycone in S. macranthum, petunidin, in comparison to SmPrx01. We suggest that both peroxidases, differing in their binding affinities to the two main aglycones, petunidin and malvidin, are involved synergistically in the anthocyanin degradation process. The differences between the degradation mechanisms in S. macranthum and B. calycina suggest adjustment of different plants to the active degradation, depending on their anthocyanin profile.

The novelties in this study are both the involvement of a β‐glucosidase, in addition to peroxidases, in the in planta anthocyanin degradation in S. macranthum flowers, and the involvement of two different peroxidases in the process. Inhibition of β‐glucosidase activity in the developing flowers prevents their whitening, suggesting that they are crucial for the process. One specific β‐glucosidase increased in activity between D‐4h and D3, correlating with the induction of one vacuolar β‐glucosidase gene, SmBGL7, at the initiation of degradation (Figures 5B and S4). The predicted MW from the gene sequence was close to that seen on the β‐glucosidase activity gel. Here too, as with the peroxidases, the two additional β‐glucosidase genes induced had an extracellular signal peptide (Figure 5B), explaining their absence on the activity gel (Figure 4C). SmBGL7 is closely related, phylogenetically, to a B. calycina β‐glucosidase, BcBGL8, with a high homology between them (Figure S4). This is true, even though β‐glucosidases were not shown to have a role in the active anthocyanin degradation of B. calycina . The high homology found between the S. macranthum anthocyanin‐degrading candidate β‐glucosidase and a similar gene in B. calycina suggests future testing of the role of this β‐glucosidase in Brunfelsia flowers, particularly its potential involvement in anthocyanin degradation.

Interestingly, despite the clear indications of involvement of β‐glucosidase in the degradation process in S. macranthum, the addition of d‐gluconic acid to the in vitro experiments with total protein extracts and anthocyanin extracts from the flowers did not inhibit, and even accelerated, anthocyanin degradation (Figure 4D). One possible explanation for this is that the decompartmentation of the cells in the protein extracts enables access to additional cytoplasmic oxidizing enzymes, such as PPOs, that may not need the initial stripping of the glycosyl groups in order to degrade the anthocyanins.

In conclusion, we present a detailed characterization of an active two‐step anthocyanin degradation mechanism in S. macranthum, involving a vacuolar β‐glucosidase enzyme and two distinct peroxidase isoforms. The two peroxidases vary in their binding affinities to the different aglycones, suggesting potential variability and synergistic involvement in the degradation process, which opens new paths for understanding regulation and selectivity in pigment fading. Future studies with the isolated peroxidases and β‐glucosidases, and the development of a gene knockout‐based transformation system for S. macranthum may clarify the exact molecular mechanism and functional validation of anthocyanin degradation. This study may contribute to understanding the loss of pigmentation in other plant organs and species, leading to ways of controlling this process.
