Minocycline and bone marrow–derived mononuclear cells as potential therapeutics for hereditary retinal degenerations
Authors: Alba Videla‐Ristol, Ana Martínez‐Vacas, David García‐Bernal, Manuel Vidal‐Sanz, María Paz Villegas‐Pérez, Diego García‐Ayuso, Johnny Di Pierdomenico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess in Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rats if the combination of two previously documented neuroprotective strategies: minocycline administration and bone marrow–derived mononuclear cells (BM‐MNCs) intravitreal transplantation, offers enhanced neuroprotection compared with each treatment alone.MethodsForty‐eight homozygous pigmented RCS rats were divided into four groups: control, intravitreal BM‐MNC transplantation, systemic minocycline treatment or combined therapy. Treatments were administered at postnatal day (P) 27–28. Animals were evaluated in vivo by spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD‐OCT) at P28, P35 and P51. Retinal sections were analysed for photoreceptor survival (outer nuclear layer [ONL] cell counts), synaptic preservation (Bassoon immunoreactivity), microglial activation (Iba‐1+ cells) and macroglial reactivity (glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP] expression).ResultsBoth BM‐MNC and minocycline monotherapies significantly preserved total and outer retinal thicknesses and increased ONL nuclei counts compared with controls. Combined treatment yielded superior protection at both P35 and P51. Minocycline alone or in combination significantly reduced microglial cell activation, while BM‐MNCs alone or in combination significantly reduced GFAP expression. Synaptic integrity in the outer plexiform layer, assessed via Bassoon labelling, was significantly better preserved in treated animals, with the highest levels observed in the combined therapy group.ConclusionsMinocycline and BM‐MNCs provide complementary neuroprotective effects in the RCS rat model. While minocycline reduces microglial activation, BM‐MNCs modulate macroglial response. Their combination enhances photoreceptor survival and synaptic preservation more effectively than either treatment alone. These findings support the therapeutic potential of combining pharmacological and cell‐based interventions in hereditary retinal degenerations.Keywords: bone marrow–derived mononuclear cells, glial activation, minocycline, photoreceptor neuroprotection, RCS rat, retinal degeneration

INTRODUCTION

The inherited retinal degenerations are genetic diseases characterised by significant genotypic and phenotypic diversity. However, they are all caused by genetic defects that typically affect genes encoding proteins involved in phototransduction or the visual pigment cycle (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Hartong et al., 2006; Jones et al., 2017; Silverman & Wong, 2018). There are more than 80 disorders with over 200 implicated genes, which can be categorised by their inheritance pattern (Armstrong & Mousavi, 2015; Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Hadj‐Said et al., 2016; Sobrin & Seddon, 2014).

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is the most common inherited retinal dystrophy, characterised by progressive degeneration of photoreceptors (PRs) and retinal pigment epithelium. It affects approximately 1 in 4000 individuals, and thus, it is estimated that there are 50 000–100 000 cases in the United States and 1.5 million globally (Frick et al., 2012; Hartong et al., 2006). This disease is caused by various mutations in rod proteins, leading to PR cell death, initially affecting rods and later cones. Mutations in the same gene can cause different clinical phenotypes, while similar clinical phenotypes can result from mutations in different genes (Bourne et al. 2018; Flaxman et al. 2017). Because RP is an inherited disease characterised by substantial genetic heterogeneity, effective non‐genetic strategies to preserve retinal structure and function are needed. (Chaumet‐Riffaud et al., 2017; Cross et al., 2022).

To facilitate the understanding of the pathophysiology of the inherited retinal degenerations, animal models of RP have been employed. A widely used model is the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2014; LaVail, 1981; LaVail et al., 2018) rat. This animal carries a recessive mutation in the MERTK gene that encodes a transmembrane protein present in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) that regulates the phagocytosis of PR outer segments by the RPE, a crucial process for PR survival (LaVail, 1981; LaVail et al., 2018). The MERTK mutation carried by the RCS rat has been identified in 1–2% of human RP cases (Audo et al., 2018; Poli et al., 2023). PR degeneration in RCS rats begins later than in other RP models, such as the P23H‐1 rat, but it progresses rapidly, resulting in severe loss of both rods and cones within the first 3 months of life (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017). MERTK mutations in human RP cause also a severe disease with early macular involvement, but the phenotypic characteristics are variable, probably due to mutation diversity (Audo et al., 2018; Poli et al., 2023). PR degeneration leads to a decline in scotopic and photopic electroretinographic function and loss of visual acuity (Berson et al., 1991; Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017; Gal et al., 2000; Garcia‐Ayuso, Di Pierdomenico, Agudo‐Barriuso, et al., 2018; Martinez‐Vacas et al., 2022).

As the inherited PR degenerations are caused by gene mutations, the optimal treatment would be the replacement of the damaged gene. However, this is not possible in many of the inherited retinal degenerations at the moment because there are many genes causing the disease, and the genetic treatment is available only for Leber Congenital Amaurosis (Jones et al., 2017). Therefore, research efforts have focused on developing pharmacological or cellular therapies that could slow PR loss and/or replace lost PR s (Dias et al., 2018; Gagliardi et al., 2019; Gasparini et al., 2019; Jayakody et al., 2015; Jones et al., 2017; Trouillet et al., 2018).

Microglial cells have been shown to play a significant role in the progression of retinal degeneration in the RCS rat model (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017, 2018, 2019, Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al. 2020, Martinez‐Vacas et al., 2022; Noailles et al., 2014). In this model, microglial cell activation begins simultaneously with the onset of PR death (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, Martinez‐Vacas et al., 2022), and the prevention of microglial activation with minocycline reduces PR degeneration (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2018; Zhang & Wang, 2010).

Minocycline is a tetracycline analogue that exerts anti‐inflammatory, anti‐apoptotic and neuroprotective effects in various experimental models such as Parkinson's disease (Biscaro et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2002), neuropathic pain (Mika et al., 2007), Alzheimer's disease (Garrido‐Mesa et al., 2013) and retinitis pigmentosa (Peng et al., 2014). In a previous study, we documented that intraperitoneally administered minocycline reduces microglial reactivity and shows neuroprotective effects in the RCS rat retina (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2018).

Other strategies, such as cellular therapy can also prevent PR death in the RCS rat model. Bone marrow stem cells (Park et al., 2017; Tomita et al., 2002) include mesenchymal stem cells (Li et al., 2009; Park et al., 2017), mononuclear cells (Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al., 2020; Park et al., 2017; Zaverucha‐do‐Valle et al., 2014) and CD34+ cells (Park et al., 2017; Yazdanyar et al., 2020) have also shown neuroprotective effects in different animal models of neuronal degeneration, and also in various human and animal models of PR degeneration (Cotrim et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2010; Moisseiev et al., 2016; Otani et al., 2004; Park et al., 2017; Satarian et al., 2017; Tzameret et al., 2014, 2015). We have documented that the intravitreal and/or subretinal transplantation of bone marrow–derived mononuclear cells (BM‐MNCs) modulates macroglial reactivity and has neuroprotective effects in the RCS rat retina (Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al., 2020, Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022; Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022). This action may be to the release of neurotrophic survival‐promoting factors, anti‐inflammatory and anti‐apoptotic factors by these cells (Ding et al., 2017; Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Hermankova et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2020). Although both minocycline and BM‐MNC transplantation have been documented to have neuroprotective effects in retinal RP models, no studies have evaluated the efficacy of the combination of both therapies in RP models. Both therapies, as described above, have individually demonstrated neuroprotective effects in RP models, with BM‐MNCs preserving PR s and modulating macroglial reactivity, and minocycline reducing microglial activation.

In this work, we aim to explore if the combination of these two previously documented neuroprotective strategies could further enhance PR survival in RCS rats and therefore be a promising therapy for the inherited retinal degenerations.

METHODS

Animals

Homozygous pigmented RCS rats from the breeding colony of the University of Murcia were used. Animals were housed in temperature‐ and light‐controlled rooms with a 12‐h light/dark cycle (light from 8 AM to 8 PM and light intensity under 500 lux) and had food and water ad libitum. Animal manipulations were carried out following the Spanish and European Union regulations for the use of animals in research (Council Directive 86/609/EEC), the indications of the University of Murcia ethical committee, and the ARVO statement for the use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research.

A total of 48 RCS rats were divided into a control and 3 experimental groups (Figure 1). The control group was age‐matched with the experimental groups. The first experimental group (n = 12) received an intravitreal injection (IVI) of BM‐MNC at postnatal day 28 (P28). The second experimental group (n = 12) received daily intraperitoneal injections of minocycline hydrochloride (M9511; Sigma‐Aldrich, Alcobendas, Madrid, Spain), from P27 to processing. The third experimental group (n = 12) received combined therapy: an intravitreal injection (IVI) of BM‐MNC at postnatal day 28 (P28) and daily intraperitoneal injections of minocycline hydrochloride (M9511; Sigma‐Aldrich, Alcobendas, Madrid, Spain), from P27 to processing. The animals were treated at P27‐28 because in a previous work (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017) we documented that in the RCS rat PR death starts at P33 and is maximal between P33 and P45. All the animals were examined in vivo with optical coherence tomography at P28, P35 and P51 (see below). Animals of the control and experimental groups were processed half at P35 (n = 6 for each group) and half at P51 (n = 6 for each group).

Administration of treatments

Minocycline (M9511; Merck‐Millipore, Alcobendas, Madrid, Spain) was diluted in distilled water to achieve a concentration of 45 μg/μL and injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 1 μL per gram of rat body weight (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2018).

The BM‐MNCs was obtained from the bone marrow extracted from the femur and tibia of 3 months old RCS rats. The BM‐MNCs were separated from the other cells by density gradient centrifugation and were resuspended in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to obtain a concentration of 100 000 cells/μL (Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al. 2020; Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022) and 2.5 μL of the cell suspension were injected intravitreally in the left eye. For this procedure, a sclerotomy was made in the superotemporal sclera, 2 mm from the sclerocorneal limbus, with a sterile 27G needle, and a Hamilton microsyringe (26G) was introduced through the sclerotomy towards the centre of the vitreous and injected the cell suspension. Observation of the eye fundus was carried out at the end of the procedure to ascertain that the lens or retina had not been damaged by the injection.

Retinal thickness measurement with OCT

Optic coherence tomography of the retina was obtained using a Spectralis SD‐OCT system (Heidelberg, Germany) and a contact lens specifically adapted for use in rat eyes (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2024; Rovere et al., 2015). The rats were sedated with isoflurane inhalation and the pupil was dilated with 1% tropicamide eye drops (Colicursí tropicamide 1%; Alcon‐Cusí, SA., El Masnou, Barcelona). The macular cube scan protocol, covering a field of 20 × 25° centred in the optic disc, was selected. The follow‐up mode of the OCT was also selected to obtain scans of the same region in subsequent explorations. In the scans, the outer retina thickness and the total retinal thickness were measured, following previously described methods (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2024; Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2019; Martínez‐Vacas et al., 2021; Rovere et al., 2015). In brief, in three scan lines of the cube (the upper and lower scans of the cube and the centre one, passing through the optic nerve), we measured in four regions the total (from the nerve fibre layer to the retinal pigment epithelium) and outer (from the inner limit of the ONL to the retinal pigment epithelium) retinal thickness. In the upper and lower scans, these measurements were taken at four equidistant points, while on the central scan, two measurements were taken at either side of the optic disc. We thus had 12 measurements (4 measurements x 3 lines) of total and outer retinal thickness per animal.

Processing

Rats were first sedated with an intraperitoneal injection of diluted sodium pentobarbital (1:1 in saline; Dolethal Vetoquinol, S.A., Lure, France) and then sacrificed with a lethal dose of sodium pentobarbital. Then, rats were perfused transcardially through the ascending aorta, first with saline and then with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The eyes were enucleated maintaining their orientation using a 6/0 silk suture in the superior conjunctiva, and the eyecups were prepared to obtain vertically oriented cryostat cross sections that contained the optic disc and the dorsal and ventral retina following previously described methods (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017; Garcia‐Ayuso, Di Pierdomenico, Hadj‐Said, et al., 2018).

Immunohistofluorescence

Cross sections spanning the centre of the optic disc were selected per animal, and double immunodetection was performed following standard procedures (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017, 2018; Garcia‐Ayuso, Di Pierdomenico, Agudo‐Barriuso, et al., 2018; Martínez‐Vacas et al., 2021, 2022) using different antibody combinations. Briefly, cross sections were washed in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X‐100 (Tx; Sigma‐Aldrich) and incubated overnight at 4°C with a mixture of the primary antibodies (see next paragraph) diluted in blocking buffer (PBS containing 2% Tx and 2% normal donkey serum). After washing with PBS‐0.1% Tx, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a mixture of the secondary antibodies (see next paragraph) diluted in PBS‐2% Tx. Sections were washed again with PBS‐0.1% Tx and, after a rinse in PBS, mounted with a mounting media containing DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole; Vectashield Mounting Medium con DAPI; Vector Atom, Alicante, Spain) to counterstain all retinal nuclei.

The primary antibodies used and their cell targets were: (i) rabbit anti‐Iba1 antibody (1:1000, 019–19 741; Wako Chemichals, Neuss, Germany) to immunodetect microglial cells (ii) goat anti‐GFAP antibody (1:250; C‐19: sc‐6170; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Heidelberg Germany) to immunodetect astrocytes and Müller cells; (iii) rabbit anti‐cone arrestin (C‐arrestin) antibody (1:1000, AB15282, Chemicon‐Millipore Iberica) to immunodetect cones and (iv) mouse monoclonal anti‐bassoon antibody (1:750; ADI‐VAM‐PS003; Enzo life Science, Lausen, Switzerland) to immunodetect synaptic connections. We carried out double immunodetection of the sections with anti‐bassoon and anti‐arrestin or with anti‐Iba‐1 and anti‐GFAP.

The secondary antibodies were: donkey anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor® 488 conjugate (1:500; Invitrogen Inc., Madrid, Spain) and donkey anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor® 594 conjugate (1:500; Invitrogen Inc., Madrid, Spain).

Image acquisition

Three sections doubly immunodetected with the mentioned primary antibodies were selected from each eye were photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6 B microscope, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The images were analysed for manual cell quantification using Adobe® Photoshop® CC (Adobe Systems, Inc., San José, CA, USA), and for automated calculation of signal intensity from macroglia expression and synaptic connections using the Histogram Analysis tool of Image Pro Plus software (IPP 5.1 for Windows; Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA). This software evaluates fluorescence intensity in the photomicrographs selected, generating both a plot of fluorescence intensity per image and calculations for the average intensity and area under the curve, which represents the total fluorescence for each image (Martínez‐Vacas et al., 2021, 2022). To ensure consistent measurements, all photomicrographs used for analysing GFAP or Bassoon immunoreactivity were captured with identical gain and exposure settings.

A confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) was also used to obtain more detailed microphotographs of cone morphology and synaptic connections in the outer plexiform layer.

To evaluate GFAP and bassoon immunofluorescence, images of the same retinal areas used for cell quantification were acquired again using fixed gain and exposure settings, calibrated to match the average automatic exposure time required to image the corresponding regions in control animals. The Relative Fluorescence Units (RFU) for GFAP and bassoon were then measured in these photomicrographs using the ‘Histogram Analysis’ function of the Image Pro Plus software. This analysis yielded a plot for each image showing the distribution of RFU values, as well as the average RFU and the area under the curve (AUC), reflecting the total fluorescence intensity across the eight analysed retinal regions. The RFU per photomicrograph was pooled to obtain the mean RFU per animal.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was carried out using SigmaPlot® software (Version 14.0; Systat Software Inc. (SSI), San José, California, USA). For comparisons between two groups, a t‐test or Mann–Whitney test was applied depending on whether the data were parametric or non‐parametric. To compare more than 2 groups in the same or different survival intervals, a one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post‐hoc test was employed. To compare the influence and the interaction of treatment and time, a two‐way ANOVA was performed including both factors. Differences were considered significant when p ≤ 0.05.

RESULTS

We have assessed total and outer retinal thickness, PR survival, cone and synapse preservation and glial cell reactivity in control animals and also in experimental animals following administration of minocycline, BM‐MNC, or both.

Total and outer retinal thickness

We measured total and outer retinal thickness at P28 (baseline), P35 and P51 (pre‐processing). At P28 (baseline), no significant differences in total or outer retinal thickness were observed between the control and experimental groups (One‐way ANOVA; for total retinal thickness: F(3,36) = 1.03, p = 0.63; for outer retinal thickness: F(3,36) = 0.29, p = 0.83; Figure 2; Table 1).

At P35, total retinal thickness in the control group and in the groups that received BM‐MNCs, minocycline, or the combination therapy, represented 90.2%, 94.6%, 95.3% and 97.5% of the total thickness found in the same experimental groups at P28, respectively (Figure 2; Table 1). Also, at P35 outer retinal thickness in control group and in the groups that received BM‐MNCs, minocycline, or the combination therapy, represented 93.2%, 97.2%, 97.1% and 98.9% of the outer retinal thickness found in the same experimental groups at P28, respectively, (Figure 2; Table 1). At this survival period, the total retinal thickness and also the outer retinal thicknesses were significantly greater in the treated groups than in the control group (one‐way ANOVA; for comparison of total retinal thickness: F(3,36) = 1.62, p = 0.002; and for comparison of outer retinal thickness: F(3,36) = 0.66, p = 0.005; Figure 2; Table 1). There were also significant differences of the total and outer retinal thickness between the experimental groups, because both the total and the outer retinal thickness were significantly greater in the experimental group that received combined therapy with BM‐MNC and minocycline than in the experimental groups that received single therapy with BM‐MNCs or minocycline (for the comparison of total retinal thickness between the group with combined therapy and the groups with single therapy with BM‐MNC or minocycline: t‐test p = 0.025 and p = 0.015, respectively; for the comparison of outer retinal thickness between the group with combined therapy and the groups with single therapy with BM‐MNC or minocycline: t‐test p = 0.052 and p = 0.05, respectively; Figure 2; Table 1).

At P51, total retinal thickness in the control group and in the groups that received BM‐MNCs, minocycline, or the combined therapy, represented 73.1%, 76.1%, 76.4% and 78.7% of the total retinal thickness found in the same experimental groups at P28 respectively. Also, at P51, outer retinal thickness in the control group and in the groups that received BM‐MNCs, minocycline, or the combined therapy, represented 50.6%, 53.5%, 53.2% and 56.6% of the outer retinal thickness found in the same experimental groups at P28, respectively (Figure 2; Table 1). At this survival period, the total retinal thickness and also the outer retinal thickness were significantly greater in the treated groups than in the control group (one‐way ANOVA; for comparison of total retinal thickness: F(3,36) = 1.62, p = 0.002; for comparison of outer retinal thickness: F(3,36) = 2.29, p = 0.049; Figure 2; Table 1). There were also significant differences between the experimental groups, because total and outer retinal thickness were significantly greater in the animals that received combined therapy with BM‐MNC and minocycline than in the experimental groups that received single therapy with BM‐MNCs or minocycline alone (for the comparison of total retinal thickness between the group of combined therapy and the groups that received single therapy with BM‐MNCs or minocycline alone: t‐test: p = 0.012 and p = 0.009, respectively; for the comparison of outer retinal thickness between the group of combined therapy and the groups that received single therapy with BM‐MNCs or minocycline alone: t‐test p = 0.005 and p = 0.002, respectively; Figure 2; Table 1). Finally, a two‐way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the interaction between treatments and time, and the analysis revealed a significant interaction between group and time for total retinal thickness (F(3, 72) = 12.45, p < 0.0001). In contrast, no significant interaction was observed for outer retinal thickness (F(3, 72) = 1.87, p = 0.16).

Photoreceptor survival

Animals were processed for immunodetection at P35 and at P51. We did not process animals at the initial time point, P28, because for this age we have robust baseline data for untreated RCS rats (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022) and we know that at this age there is no significant PR loss (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017), and also to adhere to the 3Rs principles governing the ethical use of animals in research. In a previously published study (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022), the values of the different variables analysed in this study in control 28 days old RCS rats were: number of ONL nuclei rows: 8.1 ± 1.7; RFU of Bassoon expression: 8.1 ± 0.2; mean numbers of Iba1+ cells per picture per animal: 22.4 ± 1.2; and RFU of GFAP expression per picture per animal: 3571.2 ± 247.

We analysed PR morphology in the control and experimental animals and observed that the experimental groups showed qualitatively improved PR outer segment morphology: the PR outer segments appeared less fragmented, straighter and longer in the experimental groups when compared with age‐matched naïve control retinas (Figure 3a–h).

We determined the numbers of nuclei rows in the ONL. In control animals, the mean numbers of nuclei rows were 7.52 ± 1.1 at P35 and 4.13 ± 0.3 at P51 (Figure 4i). In the experimental animals, the mean numbers of nuclei rows were: in the animals that received minocycline alone 8.61 ± 1.30 at P35 and 5.06 ± 0.60; in the animals transplanted with BM‐MNCs alone, 8.77 ± 1.40 at P35 and 4.98 ± 0.80 at P51; and in animals that received the combination of both treatments 10.31 ± 1.20 at P35 and 6.09 ± 0.50 at P51. The number of nuclei rows was significantly lower in the control group than in the experimental groups, both at P35 (One‐way ANOVA F(3,36) = 1.62, p < 0.001) and at P51 (One‐way ANOVA F(3,36) = 17.89 p < 0.001). Also, the number of nuclei rows differed between the experimental groups: it was similar in the groups that received single therapy with BM‐MNC or minocycline alone (t‐test p = 0.87) and significantly higher in the group that received a combination of BM‐MNC and minocycline (t‐test: p < 0.05 for the comparison between combined and single therapy; Figure 4; Table 2). Thus, all the therapies decreased PR loss, but the combined therapy achieved more PR neuroprotection. Finally, a two‐way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the interaction between treatments and time, and the analysis revealed a significant interaction for PR survival (F(3, 72) = 14.23, p < 0.0001).

Microglial activation

To determine the degree of microglial cell activation, the number of Iba‐1+ cells per animal were calculated. The mean numbers of Iba‐1+ cells per picture per animal were in the control group 45.54 ± 6.80 at P35 and 66.06 ± 7.98 at P51 (Figure 5, Table 2). The mean microglial cell numbers were significantly higher at P51 than at P35 (t‐test p < 0.005) and indicate increased microglial cell activation during the course of retinal degeneration (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017, Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al. 2020, Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022).

However, the numbers of microglial cells were similar in the control group and in the group that received BM‐MNCs alone (t‐test: P35 p = 0.1; P51 p = 0.99). These results indicate that microglial cell activation was not reduced with BM‐MNC administration alone, but it was reduced with minocycline administration, alone or in combination. Finally, a two‐way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the interaction between treatments and time, and the analysis revealed a significant interaction for microglial activation (F(3, 72) = 8.92, p = 0.0005).

Macroglial cell activation

Increased GFAP expression was observed in astrocytes and Müller cells, both in control and experimental animals, and increased with age, as observed in previous studies in the RCS rat (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017, 2018, 2020). In control retinas, the mean numbers of GFAP RFU were 13 475 ± 1569 at P35 and 24 465 ± 1864 at P51 (Figure 6, Table 2), and these numbers were significantly different (t‐test p < 0.001), indicating that GFAP expression significantly increases with age in the RCS rat retina.

Treatments effects on synaptic connections in the outer plexiform layer

We also evaluated the RFU of the Bassoon immunoreactivity in the OPL to study the integrity of the PR synaptic connections. In control animals, the mean numbers of Bassoon RFU were 6.81 ± 0.9 at P35 and 4.11 ± 0.7 at P51 (Figure 3a'–h', Table 2), and these values were significantly different (t‐test p < 0.001) indicating degeneration of synaptic connections in the INL with age.

In the experimental retinas, the mean values of Bassoon RFU were: in the animals that received minocycline alone 9.81 ± 1.10 at P35 and 5.46 ± 0.80 at P51, in the animals that received BM‐MNCs 10.27 ± 1.00 at P35 and 5.78 ± 0.80 at P51 and in the combined treatment group 12.24 ± 1.20 at P35 and 6.81 ± 0.90 at P51 (Figure 3a'–h', Table 2).

Both at P35 and at P51, the mean values of Bassoon RFU were significantly higher in the experimental groups than in the control group: (t‐test p < 0.001 at P35 and p < 0.05 at P51; Figure 3, Table 2). Also, the group that received a combination of minocycline and BM‐MNCs showed significantly higher values of Bassoon RFU than the groups that received single (minocycline or BM‐MNC) treatments (t‐test p < 0.001 at P35 and p < 0.05 at P51; Figure 3, Table 2) compared with the other two experimental conditions, indicating increased synaptic ribbon terminal preservation in the treated groups but especially in the group that received a combination of both treatments. Finally, a two‐way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the interaction between treatments and time, and the analysis revealed a significant interaction for synaptic connections in the outer plexiform layer (OPL) (F(3, 72) = 10.04, p = 0.0002).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigate the effects of the systemic administration of minocycline and the intravitreal administration of BM‐MNCs, alone or in combination, on PR degeneration and glial cell activation in homozygous pigmented RCS rats.

We have analysed in vivo with OCT the total and outer retinal thickness in control and experimental animals, and we document that the experimental animals show smaller decreases of total and outer retinal thickness than the control animals. Thus, experimental animals show a preservation of the total and outer retinal thickness, suggesting that the treatments achieved some degree of PR neuroprotection. These data are consistent with results obtained in previous studies in RCS rats which demonstrate the PR neuroprotection with minocycline or BM‐MNC treatments. (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022; Martinez‐Vacas et al., 2022; Nakazawa et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023).

To document PR neuroprotection, in this study we have assessed the number of rows of nuclei in the ONL and we document that these numbers were significantly higher in the groups of treated animals and also that the animals that received a combination of both treatments had significantly higher numbers than the animals that received treatment with minocycline or BM‐MNC alone. Thus, all the treatments, but specially the combined treatment had a neuroprotective effect on PRs. Minocycline, a broad‐spectrum tetracycline antibiotic, has demonstrated antimicrobial, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐apoptotic and neuroprotective properties (Arroba et al., 2011; Di Pierdomenico et al., 2018; Scholz et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2005). Minocycline acts as a microglial cell inhibitor and it is thus widely employed in the study of central nervous system diseases (Cai et al., 2013; Ferretti et al., 2012; Hashemi‐Monfared et al., 2018; Scholz et al., 2015). This substance has a neuroprotective effect in retinal degenerations, that has been attributed to a direct anti‐apoptotic effect on PRs (Gargini et al., 2007) and to an anti‐inflammatory action, reducing microglial cell activation and migration (Bosco et al., 2008; Di Pierdomenico et al., 2018; Scholz et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2007). Transplantation of Bone marrow–derived mononuclear stem cells to the vitreous or retina has been shown to decrease PR degeneration in RCS and P23H rats (Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al., 2020, Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022; Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Weiss & Levy, 2018, 2019). Its mode of action is still unknown, although it may be due to their paracrine effects: the release of trophic substances that may influence multiple retinal cell types simultaneously (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2017; Léveillard & Klipfel, 2019; Lu et al., 2010; Tomita et al., 2002; Weiss & Levy, 2018).

BM‐MNCs contain a heterogeneous population of effector cells, namely haematopoietic stem cells, endothelial progenitor cells, CD34+ progenitors and other progenitor subsets, as well as immune cells, including monocytes, T cells, B cells and natural killer cells (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2023; Vahidy et al., 2016). Evidence suggests that these subsets can contribute to retinal protection through secretion of trophic factors (e.g., BDNF, CNTF, bFGF, VEGF, IGF‐1) and release of extracellular vesicles and miRNAs that modulate recipient cell behaviour (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2023). Although BM‐MNCs may show a transient expression of retinal markers, their differentiation into functional PRs is uncommon, supporting the idea that their neuroprotective effects are due to paracrine and immunomodulatory factors (Tracy et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2017; Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al., 2020, Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022). Indeed, BM‐MNCs secrete trophic factors (e.g., BDNF, CNTF, bFGF, VEGF, IGF‐1) and release of extracellular vesicles and miRNAs that may modulate recipient cell behaviour (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2023).

In this study, we document not only that minocycline administration and BM‐MNC transplantation decrease PR degeneration, but also that the combined treatment with minocycline and BM‐MNC transplantation has a higher PR neuroprotective effect when compared with minocycline administration or BM‐MNC transplantation alone. This is probably due to complementary neuroprotective effects of these two strategies, and it is in agreement with the results from other studies that have shown that the combination of minocycline administration with the intravitreal injection of neurotrophic factors like bFGF has also additive effects on PR neuroprotection, also in the RCS and in the P23H rat (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2018).

In this study we also document that minocycline administration, alone or in combination with BM‐MNC transplantation, reduces the numbers of microglial cells both at P35 and P51, the two survival periods analysed, by half at P35 and by one‐third at P51 when compared with controls. Microglial activation plays a fundamental role in the progression of PR degeneration in inherited retinal degenerations (Armstrong & Mousavi, 2015; Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017, 2019, 2020, Martínez‐Vacas et al., 2021; Noailles et al., 2014). In this study, because the animals that received minocycline showed not only decreased microglial cell activation but also increased PR survival during both survival periods studied, it is likely that the increased survival is due, at least in part, to microglial cell inhibition. Minocycline has been shown previously to provide a neuroprotective effect on PRs due to its anti‐inflammatory and anti‐apoptotic properties during the course of retinal degenerations (Armstrong & Mousavi, 2015; Di Pierdomenico et al., 2018; Scholz et al., 2015).

GFAP expression in macroglial cells is an indicator of retinal stress (de Hoz et al., 2016; Vecino et al., 2016) and begins very early in RCS rats (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2017; Hippert et al., 2015; Martínez‐Vacas et al., 2021). In this study, we document that intravitreal injection of BM‐MNC, alone or in combination, decreases GFAP immunoreactivity. The decreased macroglial cell reaction may be due to the modulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels through paracrine mechanisms produced by BM‐MNCs (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2011; Léveillard & Klipfel, 2019; Li et al., 2009; Mathew et al., 2017). Other studies have also demonstrated that BM‐MNCs transplantation and systemic treatment with taurine reduce GFAP expression and decrease PR degeneration in this animal model (Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al., 2020, Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022; Di Pierdomenico, García‐Ayuso, et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2017; Léveillard & Klipfel, 2019; Martinez‐Vacas et al., 2022).

The retinas suffering from inherited retinal degenerations exhibit a progressive deterioration of the synaptic terminals that may be due to secondary retinal remodelling (Cuenca et al., 2005; Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022; Garcia‐Ayuso, Di Pierdomenico, Agudo‐Barriuso, et al., 2018; 2019; Pinilla et al., 2007). The results of this study reveal that administration of minocycline and BM‐MNCs, alone or in combination, preserve Bassoon expression and reduce PR synaptic degeneration at both survival periods studied, and this preservation is higher when the two therapies are combined. Because preservation of Bassoon immunoreactivity in treated animals is accompanied by increased PR survival, it indicates that the rescued PRs maintain synaptic connections with the other retinal neurons that may allow them to send information towards the afferent sensory pathway. This achievement could limit secondary retinal remodelling, which ultimately results in the involvement and death of retinal ganglion cells (Garcia‐Ayuso et al., 2010, Garcia‐Ayuso, Di Pierdomenico, Agudo‐Barriuso, et al., 2018) consequently limiting the potential success of therapies aimed at replacing or substituting PRs.

Although in this study we document increased PR survival and synaptic preservation, we have not documented functional preservation because no functional assessments, such as electroretinographic (ERG) recordings, were performed. Electroretinographic testing was skipped because RCS rats exhibit early a marked reduction in ERG responses (Machida et al., 2008; Pinilla et al., 2004) and because in previous work we documented that this technique lacked sufficient sensitivity and specificity to detect the subtle neuroprotective effects induced by BM‐MNC treatment in the RCS rat retina (Di Pierdomenico et al., 2022).

In conclusion, our study documents that in RCS rats (1) intravitreal transplantation of BM‐MNCs increases PR survival and decreases GFAP expression; (2) minocycline administration increases PR survival and decreases microglial cell activation; and (3) the combination of intravitreal transplantation of BM‐MNCs and minocycline administration has additive effects promoting PR survival and preserving their synaptic connections. Therefore, we show that combination treatments may have additive effects on PR survival, and we propose the use of combined treatments in retinal degenerations.
