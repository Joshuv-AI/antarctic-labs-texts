Homotypic cell-in-cell structure as a novel prognostic predictor in non-small cell lung cancer and frequently localized at the invasive front
Authors: Xiaona Liu, Rui Guo, Dongxuan Li, Ya’nan Wang, Jingya Ning, Shuanying Yang, Jun Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Homotypic cell-in-cell structures (hoCICs) are associated with tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis and is considered a promising prognostic marker in various cancers. However, the role of hoCICs in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains unclear. Tumor tissue sections were obtained from 411 NSCLC patients. We analyzed the relationship between clinicopathological variables and the number of hoCICs. LASSO and multivariate Cox regression analysis were employed to identify prognostic factors for NSCLC. The impact of hoCICs on overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank test. Prognostic models for OS and DFS were developed and validated using the C-index, time-dependent area under the curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). Among the cohort, 56% of patients had hoCICs while 44% did not. Notably, hoCICs were primarily found at the tumor invasion front. Male gender, smoking, squamous cell carcinoma, low differentiation, tumor size ≥ 3 cm, advanced TNM stage, lymph node metastasis, pleural invasion, vascular invasion, necrosis, P53 mutation, and high expression of Ki-67 were identified as relative risk factors for hoCICs. Furthermore, hoCICs was found to be a significant prognostic factor for both OS and DFS, with higher frequencies of hoCICs correlating with poorer outcomes. We constructed nomograms for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and DFS based on hoCICs, and the calibration curves showed good agreement between the predicted and actual outcomes. The results of the C-index, time-dependent AUC, NRI, IDI, and DCA analyses demonstrated that incorporating hoCICs into the prognostic model significantly enhanced its predictive power and clinical applicability. HoCICs indicated independent perdictive value for OS and DFS in patients with NSCLC. Furthermore, the frequent localization of hoCICs at the tumor invasion front suggested a strong association between hoCICs and tumor invasion as well as metastasis.

Introduction

Cell-in-cell structure (CICs) has been discovered for over a century1. However, it has recently garnered increasing attention. CICs are characterized by the presence of one cell, referred to as the target cell, residing within another cell, known as the host cell2,3. The target cell is typically intact, active, and enclosed within a large vacuole inside the host cell. CICs are frequently observed in cancer tissues, but they can also occur in normal tissues and benign lesions4. Moreover, CICs plays a crucial role in the immune homeostasis of the liver5, viral infection6–8, embryonic development9, and tumor progression10. Based on the type of target cells involved, CICs can be classified into homotypic CICs (hoCICs), where the target cells are sibling tumor cells, and heterotypic CICs (heCICs), where the target cells are immune cells11.

Most target cells die through lysosomal-dependent degradation, while a few cells can actively escape from host cells or undergo mitosis within them12. In the presence of heCICs, tumor cells can engulf and eliminate immune cells, leading to immune escape and the survival of tumor cells13,14. Consequently, heCICs can induce an unfavorable prognosis in various tumors including breast cancer15, buccal mucosa squamous cell carcinoma16, pancreatic cancer17, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)18. However, hoCICs exhibited a dual function. On one hand, it provides nutrients for tumor cells survival19,20, contributes to tumor genomic instability12 and promotes the clonal selection of tumor cells for competition and evolution21,22. On the other hand, it can eliminate tumor cells detached from the matrix and inhibit their metastasis12. Therefore, the dichotomous function of hoCICs has led to divergent perspectives regarding its role in tumor prognosis, particularly in NSCLC. While Mackay et al.23 demonstrated that hoCICs is an independent predictor for the poor outcome of NSCLC; in contrast, Wei et al.18 suggested that hoCICs appears to favor a good prognosis. In summary, the prognostic value of hoCICs in NSCLC remains uncertain.

NSCLC accounts for approximately 85% of lung cancer and is the most prevalent malignancy threatening human life24. Albeit several molecular biomarkers, such as the RMH score25 have been identified for predicting the prognosis of NSCLC, their detection methods are often time-consuming and labor-intensive. Furthermore, significant advancements have been made in the clinical treatment of lung cancer, particularly with the advent of targeted therapy26 and immunotherapy27–29. However, various factors influence the effectiveness of immunotherapy like ECOG performance status30 and albumin levels31, Additionally, immunotherapy is associated with toxicity and side effects32,33. Consequently, the prognosis of NSCLC remains poor, with the survival rate of advanced lung cancer of no more than 15%34. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more sensitive and direct biomarkers to effectively assess the malignancy and prognosis of NSCLC.

Aberrant mitosis has been shown to trigger the formation of CICs35. In breast cancer, Ki-67-positive target cells have been identified in hoCICs, with the number of hoCICs positively correlating with the percentage of the Ki-67 proliferation index36. However, no studies have investigated the relationship between Ki-67 and hoCICs in NSCLC. Additionally, cell stiffness plays a crucial role in the formation of CICs, as stiffer cells are more susceptible to engulfment by softer cells exhibiting enhanced invasiveness21. Furthermore, mutations in the P53 gene can induce cytoskeletal rearrangement, resulting in softer cells and the formation of CICs13,37.

In this study, we investigated the role of hoCICs in NSCLC tissues and observed a significant increase in their abundance at the tumor invasion front. We quantified the frequency of hoCICs and assessed their association with the clinicopathological characteristics of NSCLC patients. Moreover, the impact of hoCICs on overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were explored. Meanwhile, we developed and validated a prognostic model based on hoCICs. Our findings suggest that hoCICs could serve as a novel prognostic biomarker for NSCLC.

Methods

Study cohort selection

Between March 2017 and July 2022, patients with lung cancer who underwent surgical resection at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiao Tong University were retrospectively reviewed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathological confirmation of NSCLC; (2) presence of a single tumor lesion; (3) age ≥ 18 years old; (4) absence of preoperative neoadjuvant therapy; and (5) availability of complete clinical baseline data. The exclusion criteria included: (1) tumor in situ; (2) a history of other malignancies; (3) metastatic or recurrent NSCLC; (4) other serious or life-threatening diseases; (5) unknown TNM stage and histological grade.

Clinicopathological data collection

A total of 18 parameters were collected from the clinicopathological characteristics of the patients, including age, gender (female or male), smoking history (smoking or never), different lung lobe (upper or middle, lower), tumor location (right or left), histologic type (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma or others), differentiation state (well, moderate, or poor), tumor size (≤ 3 cm or > 3 cm), lymph node metastasis (positive or negative), TNM stage according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Staging manual38 (I, II, III, or IV), pleural invasion (yes or no), vascular invasion (positive or negative), necrosis (yes or no), spread through air spaces (STAS) (yes or no), P53 mutation status (aberrant or wild type), EGFR expression levels (negative, weak, moderate or poor) and Ki-67 proliferation index.

Quantification of hoCICs

Tissue sections of the NSCLC patients were obtained from the tissue archive and observed using an optical microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany). The tumor cell enrichment area was identified at 4X magnification, followed by preliminary verification of hoCICs at 20X, with the final hoCIC count conducted at 40X. According to Mackay's criteria23, the identification of hoCICs required the observation of at least four of the following six characteristics: (1) nucleus of the target cell; (2) cytoplasm of the target cell; (3) nucleus of the host cell; (4) cytoplasm of the host cell; (5) crescent-shaped nucleus of the host cell; and (6) vacuole enclosing the target cells. At least four out of the six above items could be identified as hoCICs. Our study quantified the total number of hoCICs across 10 high-power fields (hpf).

Follow-up

Survival data were gathered through telephone follow-up or hospitalization records. The follow-up period extended from the date of surgery until October 2023. Our study focused on two primary endpoints: OS and DFS. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death resulting from any cause, while DFS was defined as the interval from surgery to either tumor recurrence, distant metastasis, or death from any cause. The diagnosis of tumor recurrence and distant metastasis was established based on imaging, cytology, or biopsy analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out by SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) and R Studio version 4.3.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical data are presented as frequencies (%). Continuous data that follow a normal distribution are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with a non-normal distribution are presented as the median [interquartile range (IQR)]. The statistical association between the number of hoCICs and the clinicopathological characteristics of NSCLC patients was analyzed. The relationship between the number of hoCICs and continuous or ordinal variables was assessed using the Spearman's rank correlation test. Differences between the number of hoCICs and categorical clinicopathological features were analyzed by the Mann–Whitney U test for binary variables or the Kruskal–Wallis test for those involving multiple independent subgroups. In the latter case, statistical difference between the groups were further confirmed by the Mann–Whitney U test and Bonferroni's correction. The number of hoCICs in the tumor invasion front and non-tumor invasion front areas was compared pairwise using the Wilcoxon rank sum test.

The survival analyses exclude P53, EGFR, and Ki-67 due to a significant number of missing values that compromised the accuracy of the results. The optimal cut-off value for hoCICs was determined using X-tile software. The independent prognostic factors associated with OS and DFS were identified through the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression analysis. The Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank test were utilized to compare the OS and DFS between patients with high and low number of hoCICs across the overall population and various subgroups. Additionally, the nomograms predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS and DFS based on hoCICs were constructed and evaluated using the calibration curves. The performance of these models was evaluated with the C-index, time-dependent area under the curve (AUC). The net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were utilized to compare the accuracy of the two models, with NRI > 0 and IDI > 0 indicating an improved predictive power. Furthermore, the decision curve analysis (DCA) was applied to compare the clinical benefit of the two models. All internal validations were performed using the bootstrapping method with 1000 resamples. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the biomedical Ethics Committee of faculty of medicine Xi'an Jiaotong University (No.2021.954) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s).

Results

Patient's characteristics

A total of 411 patients with NSCLC who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in this study, yielding a median OS of 33 months (range, 0.25–79 months) and a median DFS of 30 months (range, 0.25–79 months). The baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The majority of patients were male (62.8%) and reported no history of smoking (65.2%). Most patients' lesions were in the upper or lower lobes (93.2%). The predominant histopathological types were adenocarcinoma (66.2%) and squamous cell carcinoma (29.9%), with other histological types comprised 3.9%, including 2 cases of large cell carcinoma, 12 cases of adenosquamous carcinoma and 2 cases of carcinoid. The differentiation states were categorized as well differentiated (101 cases, 24.6%), moderately differentiated (220 cases, 53.5%), and poorly differentiated (90 cases, 21.9%). Additionally, 313 cases (76.1%) were classified as early TNM stage (I-II), while 98 cases (21.9%) were classified as advanced TNM stage (III-IV).

HoCICs events in NSCLC cohort

Sections of tumor tissue from 411 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemical CK-pan to identify hoCICs using optical microscopy. The target tumor cells were completely surrounded by the host tumor cells, which exhibited distinctive crescent-shaped nuclei (Fig. 1A–C). Besides, complex structures formed as a result of multiple internalization events, with each target cell entirely enclosed by large vesicles (Fig. 1D–F). HoCICs frequently localized at the tumor invasive front

An intriguing phenomenon was observed during the quantification of hoCICs. We noted that hoCICs were frequently located at the tumor margin within 1 mm from the interface of the tumor and para-carcinoma tissue, as well as in single infiltrating tumor cells or tumor cell clusters dispersed throughout the tumor stroma (Fig. 3A–C). The tumor board region at the tumor-host interface represents the most active site for tumor cell metastasis and invasion, hence it is referred to as the invasive front39. Our study revealed that hoCICs at the tumor invasive front were found in approximately 80.9% of NSCLC patients with hoCICs (Table S1). A significant difference was observed in the number of hoCICs between the invasive and non-invasive front of tumor tissues (Fig. 3D), demonstrating an increasing trend in hoCICs occurrence from the non-invasive to the invasive fronts. Furthermore, the total number of hoCICs escalated with the increasing count of hoCICs at the invasion front (Fig. 3E). HoCICs associated with OS and DFS of NSCLC patients

To achieve a more precise stratification, patients were divided into two subgroups based on the optimal cut-off value of hoCICs (2 CICs/10 hpf) as determined by the X-tile program (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To prevent overfitting of the variables, LASSO regression analysis was employed to analyze the correlation between sixteen variables and OS (Supplementary Fig. S2A,B). Four variables with non-zero coefficients were selected for subsequent multivariate Cox analysis, demonstrating an association with OS: tumor size (HR = 2.386, P = 0.001), TNM stage (HR = 1.908, P = 0.013), vascular invasion (HR = 5.804, P < 0.001), and hoCICs (HR = 3.104, P < 0.001) (Table 2). Therefore, hoCICs showed independent prognostic value for the OS of NSCLC patients. Further analysis of the relationship between hoCICs and OS was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier curve with the log-rank test, which revealed that a high hoCICs number was notably associated with poor OS (Supplementary Fig. S3A, P < 0.001). LASSO regression was also applied to identify the crucial prognostic factors for DFS (Supplementary Fig. S2C,D). Combining the results from LASSO and multivariate Cox analysis, four variables, including TNM stage, vascular invasion, necrosis and hoCICs, were found to be associated with DFS in NSCLC patients (Table 2). Furthermore, the Kaplan–Meier survival curve was implemented to assess the relationship between hoCICs and DFS. High hoCICs demonstrated significant prognostic value (P < 0.001) and poor DFS across various histologic types, differentiation states and TNM stage, consistent with the findings for OS (Supplementary Fig. S4). Ultimately, hoCICs was confirmed to have independent prognostic significance for DFS in NSCLC patients.

HoCICs as a promising prognostic factor for NSCLC

To further evaluate the independent prognostic value of hoCICs in NSCLC, we constructed and validated nomograms for OS and DFS at 1, 3, and 5 years, based on the results of the aforementioned analyses.

The nomograms for predicting OS and DFS of NSCLC are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S5. The calibration plots for the OS and DFS nomograms, which were based on hoCICs demonstrated a high degree of consistency predicted probabilities for 1-, 3-, and 5-year outcomes and the actual values (Fig. 4A,B). Next, we evaluated the predictive capacity of the model based on hoCICs to determine whether hoCICs could serve as a potent clinical prognostic factor. In the OS group, the C-index of the hoCICs nomogram was 0.784 (95% CI 0.729–0.839), which was larger than that of the nomogram without hoCICs (0.739; 95% CI 0.678–0.800). Similarly, in the DFS group, the hoCICs nomogram (0.723; 95% CI 0.674–0.772) also demonstrated higher C-index compared to the nomogram without hoCICs (0.700; 95% CI 0.651–0.749). At the same time, the time-dependent AUC analysis for both OS and DFS indicated that the hoCICs model exhibited good prognostic accuracy for predicting clinical outcomes in comparison to the model without hoCICs (Fig. 5A,C), which was corroborated by the bootstrap-corrected time-dependent AUC (Fig. 5B,D). Although the NRI and IDI did not reach statistical significance for predicting 1-year OS and DFS between the models with and without hoCICs (P > 0.05), the NRI for 3- and 5-year OS was 0.363 (95% CI 0.086–0.691) and 0.333 (95% CI 0.058–0.691), respectively. The IDI values for 3- and 5-year OS were 0.042 (95% CI 0.007–0.088) and 0.074 (95% CI 0.026–0.156), respectively. For 3- and 5-year DFS, the NRI was 0.254 (95% CI 0.061–0.424) and 0.290 (95% CI 0.084–0.460), respectively, while the IDI values were 0.034 (95% CI 0.005–0.070) and 0.048 (95% CI 0.010–0.100), respectively. Finally, the DCA analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility and net benefit of the predictive models for 1-,3- and 5-year OS and DFS. Discussion

CICs has been identified in various solid tumors, including breast cancer40, endometrial stromal sarcoma41, pancreatic cancer42, skin cancer43, oral cancer44, colorectal cancer45, esophageal cancer46, liver cancer47, and lung cancer23. In this study, we observed the presence of typical hoCICs in the tumor tissue of NSCLC and found that 56% of the samples exhibited hoCICs, while the remaining 44% did not, which aligns with previous research findings18,23. In vitro cell experiment revealed that the complex hoCICs formed from multiple reiterative internalization events were observed12, a phenomenon we also identified in NSCLC tumor tissues(Fig. 1D–F).

Of particular interest for this study was the frequent localization of hoCICs at the tumor invasion front. Tumor cells at this front exhibited higher rate of cell dissociation and poorer differentiation compared to those in other areas within the tumor. The molecular and morphological characteristics of these cells are more accurately reflective of prognosis and predictive of the tumor's biological behavior than those of tumor cells in other regions39. Han et al. revealed that, in comparison to cancer cells located at the tumor organoid core, those at the invasive front were faster, softer, and larger. Meanwhile, the temporal fluctuations indicating cytoplasmic dynamics were more pronounced. The invasiveness of tumor cells was significantly inhibited by modulating the stiffness of peripheral cells48. Consequently, the invasion front emerged as the most active region for tumor invasion and metastasis. CICs was primarily associated with cytoskeletal rearrangement and actomyosin contraction, providing the driving force for forward motion49. Tumor cells at the invasion front displayed enhanced actin dynamics, and their softer phenotype conferred a 'winner' status among these cells21. These characteristics thus created favorable conditions for the occurrence of CICs. Furthermore, tumor invasion and metastasis require increased energy and nutrients to overcome various barriers, which further incentivizes tumor cells to engulf neighboring sibling cells as an additional source of sustenance19, thereby leading to the formation of more hoCICs.

Ki-67, a nuclear antigen associated with cell proliferation, is widely used as a marker to assess cellular proliferation. It is highly expressed in various malignant tumors and reflects the proliferative activity, malignancy, and invasiveness of tumor cells50,51. Consequently, it is commonly applied to evaluate the proportion of mitotic cells across different tumors. Our study found a positive correlation between the number of hoCICs and Ki-67 expression, potentially attributable to the increased demand for materials and energy during tumor cells proliferation. This supports the viewpoint that host cells acquire energy by engulfing target cells. The internalized cells remain within the outer cells, which can interrupt the mitosis of the outer cells, leading to the destruction of the contraction ring and the generation of binuclear tumor cells52. These cellular behaviors ultimately result in genomic instability and the accumulation of mutations, thereby promoting tumor progression and invasion. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed a significant association between hoCICs and histological grade, TNM stage, pleural invasion, and vascular invasion in NSCLC. Additionally, male patients with squamous cell carcinoma patients and a smoking history showed an increased prevalence of hoCICs. Furthermore, compared to wild-type P53, patients with P53 mutations showed a higher number of hoCICs, reinforcing the idea that P53 mutations facilitate the occurrence of CICs23,37. These findings provide valuable insights for the screening of hoCICs in the pathological diagnosis of NSCLC.

There are limited reports on the relationship between the prognosis of NSCLC and hoCICs. Mackay et al.23 found that hoCICs was associated with a poor prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma, while Wei et al.18 suggested that hoCICs seemed to be a favorable prognostic factor for NSCLC. Due to the contradictory findings in these two studies, our research aimed to investigate the relationship between hoCICs and the prognosis of NSCLC. We conducted LASSO and multivariate Cox regression analyses, which revealed a significant association between hoCICs and OS as well as DFS in NSCLC patients. The Kaplan–Meier curve and log-rank test demonstrated that high hoCICs were associated with an unfavorable prognosis, showing significantly worse OS and DFS compared to low hoCICs. According to the above analyses, we constructed prognostic models for OS and DFS incorporating the hoCICs. The calibration curve confirmed the accurate prediction of OS and DFS at 1-, 3-, and 5-year, with the hoCICs models performing well. Compared to the model without hoCICs, the hoCICs model showed higher C-index and time-AUC values, indicating superior discrimination. Although no statistical significance was observed in NRI and IDI values at 1-year OS and DFS between the models with and without hoCICs, NRI and IDI values were greater than 0 at 3- and 5-year OS and DFS, suggesting that the incorporation of hoCICs enhanced the predictive capability of the prognostic model. Furthermore, the DCA further indicated that integrating hoCICs into the prognostic model increased its clinical applicability. In summary, hoCICs plays a pivotal role as an independent prognostic biomarker for predicting the prognosis of NSCLC.

This study has certain limitations that warrant acknowledgement. Firstly, it is essential to recognize that this investigation is retrospective in nature, which may introduce potential bias. Secondly, the manual counting of hoCICs events could introduce subjectivity and impact accuracy. To mitigate these limitations, larger sample sizes should be enrolled to reduce bias, and standardized counting methods and tools should be utilized to enhance the accuracy and reliability of clinical studies on hoCICs.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates a strong association between higher hoCICs and poorer OS and DFS in NSCLC patients. Moreover, we found that the invasive phenotype of tumor cells may facilitate the formation of CICs, which in turn promotes tumor invasion and contributes to tumor progression and metastasis. These findings suggest that hoCICs can serve as an independent prognostic factor for NSCLC and should be included in the pathology reports of patients undergoing NSCLC resection. Further research will focus on identifying specific markers of hoCICs to further support their pathological diagnosis.
