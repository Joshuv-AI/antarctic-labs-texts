Breastfeeding and children’s sleep duration at 1 year of age: A nationwide birth cohort - The Japan Environment and Children’s Study
Authors: Yuri Nakagawa, Kenta Matsumura, Akiko Tsuchida, Hidekuni Inadera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreastfeeding plays a vital role in preventing childhood infections and enhancing many aspects of long-term health. In this study, we investigated the link between feeding and sleep duration and considered the underlying mechanism of the observed effects, particularly the role of nutrients in breast milk and formula as well as that of the brain–gut axis. Few studies have examined the relationship between breastfeeding and infant sleep, and thus there is a need for a prospective study involving a large population.SubjectsThis study analyzed data on 82,918 mother–infant pairs from the Japan Environment and Children’s Study, a nationwide birth cohort study. We examined the association of breastfeeding pattern over the first 6 months of life by comparing sleep duration at 1 year of age between babies exclusively formula-fed for 6 months and those breastfed for <6 months, those breastfed for 6 months with formula, and exclusively breastfed for 6 months.ResultsThe adjusted odds ratio (95% confidence interval) for short sleep duration (<11 h/day) at 1 year of age was 0.84 (0.73–0.97) for breastfed for <6 months, 0.79 (0.69–0.91) for breastfed for 6 months with formula, and 0.77 (0.67–0.89) for exclusively breastfed for 6 months compared with exclusively formula-fed for 6 months (Formula alone).ConclusionsBreastfeeding during the first 6 months of life was associated with a decreased risk of short sleep duration in 1-year-old infants.Subject terms: Epidemiology, Epidemiology, Preclinical research

Introduction

An adequate amount of good sleep is essential for children’s healthy development [1]. Their sleep patterns change as they develop from the neonatal period through infancy and into early childhood. Short sleep duration during infancy has been found to be associated with subsequent obesity [2], poor academic and life skills [3], hyperactivity [4], and problem behavior [5], all of which can adversely affect physical and psychological development [6]. Therefore, it is important to investigate the causes and effects of short sleep duration from the earliest stages of infancy. The WHO has stated that breastfeeding plays a role in childhood infections and malocclusion, improving intelligence, and reducing overweight and diabetes in adulthood [7–12]. In this study, we investigated the link between feeding and sleep duration and considered the underlying mechanism of the observed effects, particularly the role of nutrients in breast milk and formula as well as that of the gut–brain axis. Few studies have examined the relationship between breastfeeding and infant sleep, and thus there is a need for a prospective study involving a large population.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

The Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS) protocol has been described in detail elsewhere [13, 14]. Briefly, JECS participants were women living in 15 regions of Japan who were enrolled in the first trimester of pregnancy between January 2011 and March 2014. Follow-up was conducted in person during the second trimester of pregnancy and during routine in-hospital checkups at delivery. Subsequent follow-up was conducted by mail at 6 and 12 months postpartum. This study analyzed the jecs-qa-20210401 dataset, which was released in April 2021. This dataset covers 103,057 pregnancies, excluding 5647 cases of multiple births and cases that resulted in miscarriage or stillbirth. In addition, 6413 records with incomplete responses to questionnaire items on nutritional practices were excluded, as were 4517 records with no responses to items on sleep duration or child sex. Finally, a total of 82,918 mother–infant pairs were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of participant recruitment and exclusion.

Exposure

To assess the duration of breastfeeding and formula feeding, the following question was included in a self-administered questionnaire to be completed by the mothers 6 months after delivery: “Please indicate the duration of breastfeeding and formula feeding.” Mothers marked the duration of breast milk and formula feeding by drawing a line in the box indicating the 1-month interval from the first month to before the 6th month.

Based on these answers, and taking into account the categories used in previous studies [15, 16], we divided the mothers into four groups according to the following patterns: exclusively formula-fed for 6 months, exclusively breastfed for <6 months, exclusively breastfed for 6 months, and breastfed and formula-fed for 6 months.

Outcome

To measure the infant’s sleep duration at 1 year of age, parents marked the time the infant slept by drawing a line in the box indicating a 30-min interval from 12:00 a.m. to 12:00 a.m. the following day. We calculated infant sleep duration by multiplying the number of checked boxes by 0.5 h. Because the National Sleep Foundation in the United States recommends that 1-year-olds sleep 11–14 h in a 24-h period, we defined cases of short sleep duration in 1-year-old infants as <11 h of sleep time [17].

Confounding factors in multiple logistic regression analysis

The following potential confounding factors, defined as variables likely to affect both the exposure and outcomes, were selected and used in the multiple logistic regression analysis: maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (<18.5, 18.5–25, >25 kg/m2), number of deliveries (0, 1, 2 or more), marital status (married, never married, divorced, widowed) at 6 months after delivery, educational background (middle or high school; technical college, vocational, or associate degree; bachelor’s degree or graduate degree), household income (<4 million yen, 4–6 million yen, >6 million yen), employment status during pregnancy (employed, unemployed), exercise habits during pregnancy [18, 19] (no, yes), social support during pregnancy [20] (lower, lower-middle, upper-middle, upper), negative attitude toward pregnancy (no, yes), history of any physical or mental illness (no, yes), pregnancy complications (none, yes), cesarean section (none, yes), weeks of gestation (<34 weeks, 34- < 37 weeks, 37– < 42 weeks, ≥42 weeks), birth weight (≥2500, <2500 g), infant sex (male or female), major congenital anomalies [21] (no, yes), infant’s sleep duration at 1 month (≥16, <16 h), location where the baby sleeps at night (in the parent’s bed, in a baby bed located in the parent’s room, other), first breastfeeding (within 1 h postpartum, after 1 h postpartum, no breastfeeding), frequency of looking at or talking to the baby when feeding [15] (always, sometimes, seldom, never), alcohol drinking status [22] (never, before pregnancy, during pregnancy), smoking status (never, quit before pregnancy, quit after pregnancy, smoking during pregnancy), passive smoking status (never, in the absence of child, in the presence of child), support from the father after birth (always, sometimes, rarely, never), postpartum depression defined as an Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale [23] score of ≥9 [24, 25] at 1 month postpartum (no, yes), infant maltreatment [26] (no, yes), daycare attendance at 6 months of age (never, yes). All the variables were categorized in accordance with the categories used in previous studies [15, 26].

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise noted, data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or percentages. Multivariable logistic analyses were performed to estimate the association of short sleep duration (<11 h per day) at 1 year of age in association with exclusively formula-fed for 6 months, exclusively breastfed for <6 months, exclusively breastfed for 6 months, and breastfed and formula-fed for 6 months. Logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate the unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Adjusted ORs were calculated using the covariates described in the previous section with the force entry method, and crude ORs were calculated without these covariates. Missing data were also included in the model as dummy coded variables. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) and R ver. 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 82,918 mother–infant pairs were analyzed in this study. Of these, 26.6% of mothers breastfed for <6 months, 37.5% breastfed for 6 months with formula, and 35.9% exclusively breastfed for 6 months. The detailed demographic and obstetric characteristics are shown in Table 1. Compared with the exclusively formula-fed for 6 months group, the other 3 groups tended to have higher education, higher household income, more social support, no negative attitude toward pregnancy, no history of physical or mental illness, no pregnancy complications, no cesarean section, term delivery, no congenital anomalies in the baby, breastfeeding soon after birth, no history of alcohol consumption, no history of smoking or passive smoking, and no daycare attendance. Compared with mothers who were included in the analysis (n = 82,918), those who were excluded (n = 10,930) due to a lack of responses or missing data tended to be current smokers, to be younger, and to have a shorter educational background.

In the exclusively breastfed for 6 months group, the unadjusted OR (95% CI) for infant’s short sleep duration at 1 year of age was 0.69 (0.61, 0.79; p < 0.001) and the adjusted OR (95% CI) was 0.77 (0.67, 0.89; p < 0.001) compared with the exclusively formula-fed for 6 months group, while the breastfed for 6 months with formula group had an unadjusted OR (95% CI) of 0.77 (0.67, 0.88; p < 0.001) and an adjusted OR (95% CI) of 0.79 (0.69, 0.91; p = 0.001), while the breastfed for <6 months group had an unadjusted OR (95%CI) of 0.82 (0.72, 0.94; p = 0.004) and an adjusted OR (95% CI) of 0.84 (0.73, 0.97; p = 0.014).

Discussion

In this study, we hypothesized that breastfed and formula-fed infants would differ in sleep duration at 1 year of age and examined the association between feeding status up to 6 months of age and sleep duration at 1 year of age, using JECS data of 82,918 mother–infant pairs. The results showed that breastfeeding during the first 6 months of life was associated with increased infant sleep duration at 1 year of age, independent of formula use. The exact mechanism underlying the association of breastfeeding in the first 6 months of life with reduced risk of short sleep duration at 1 year of age is unknown, but there are several possibilities.

In this study, we considered the differences in nutrient content between formula and breast milk. First, a major difference is the presence or absence of melatonin [27, 28]. Melatonin regulates circadian rhythms [29], improves sleep quality, promotes sleep onset [30], and supports neural development and growth in infants [31]. Formula does not contain melatonin but breast milk does. Melatonin is produced in the pineal gland, but newborns cannot produce much melatonin on their own because their pineal gland is still developing. Autonomous nocturnal secretion of melatonin begins at 2–3 months of age, with concentrations and rhythms becoming similar to those of adults by around 6 months of age [32]. Thus, the primary pathway for melatonin in infants up to 6 months is melatonin of maternal origin. It is quite possible that exposure to melatonin in children up to 6 months may affect sleep duration at 1 year of age.

Second, the major difference between formula and breast milk is the presence or absence of a diurnal variation in tryptophan concentration [27]. Tryptophan is converted to melatonin via serotonin in the pineal gland and is involved in the formation of circadian rhythms. Tryptophan is an essential amino acid, but given that it cannot be synthesized by the body, the main route of intake for infants is through breast milk or formula. The concentration of tryptophan increases in breast milk at night, whereas the concentration in formula is always the same. Therefore, it is likely that infants receiving tryptophan from breastmilk develop circadian rhythms earlier than those receiving tryptophan from formula, affecting sleep duration at 1 year of age.

Third, breastfeeding has been reported to affect the intestinal microflora, creating a favorable bacterial flora. The human body is equipped with a system called the gut–brain axis, which is a two-way exchange of information between the gut and the brain. The gut microbiota has been reported to influence neurodevelopment and sleep in children [33, 34]. It is possible that children’s exposure to breast milk or formula up to 6 months of age might build up their individual gut microbiota and affect their sleep duration at 1 year of age.

This study has several strengths. First, we recruited participants from 15 Regional Centers throughout Japan, so our population is considered to be largely representative of Japanese mothers. Second, we used a large dataset, which allowed for highly accurate estimation. Third, we followed the mother–infant pairs until the child reached 1 year of age and a relatively high response rate of 88.3% was maintained. Consequently, selection bias due to a lack of responses was kept to a minimum.

However, there are also several limitations that should be mentioned. The first is that, as in previous studies [35, 36], we did not directly examine hormonal changes or changes in intestinal bacteria. A second limitation is that recall bias might have occurred because the participating mothers were asked about the infant’s sleep duration and breastfeeding duration, using a questionnaire. A third limitation is the possibility of time-dependent bias in the exclusively formula-fed for 6 months group and the breastfed for <6 months with formula group, given that the duration of breastfeeding might have been as little as 1 week or as much as 5 months. A fourth limitation is that the effect size is not so large, with a maximum difference of 3.4% among the four groups. A fifth limitation is that there might have been unmeasured variables that confounded factors regarding the infant’s sleep environment, including the brightness of the infant’s sleeping surroundings or the presence of lullabies. The sixth limitation is that, although we adjusted for sleep duration at 1 month of age as a confounder to control for the potential influence of early weaning, this adjustment alone might not have been sufficient. Future studies should improve on these limitations and examine the relationship between breastfeeding and children’s sleep duration over the long term.

In conclusion, breastfeeding during the first 6 months of life was associated with a decreased risk of short sleep duration in 1-year-old infants.
