Effects of dim-evening lighting optimised for geographical orientation versus standard lighting on mental health: protocol paper for a quasiexperimental study in a psychiatric hospital
Authors: Carlo Volf, Dennis Dan Corell, Torben Skov Hansen, Julie Margrethe Dubois, Xinxi Zeng, Lone Baandrup, Paul Michael Petersen, Klaus Martiny
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionResearch has provided novel insights into how light stimulates circadian rhythms through specialised retinal ganglion cells to the suprachiasmatic nucleus. In addition, there has been a revolution in light-emitting diode (LED) technology, leading to tunable LED light sources and lighting systems, enabling 24-hour dynamic light scenarios with bright blue-enriched short wavelength light during the day and dim evening light, stimulating the circadian system. These dynamic LED lighting systems are now being implemented at hospitals without adequate understanding of how it may affect the health and well-being of patients and staff.Methods and analysisAn optimised dynamic LED lighting scenario is investigated at a newly built psychiatric hospital in Copenhagen. In the 12 months baseline period, a standard lighting scenario with dynamic colour temperature and fixed light intensity is investigated. In the following 12-month intervention period, a new DEL scenario is investigated, having dynamic colour temperature as well as dynamic light intensity with a higher daytime and lower evening-time melanopic daylight equivalent illuminance. This setting is furthermore adjusted for geographical orientation to compensate for differences in sunlight access in wintertime. The study uses a quasiexperimental design comparing patients admitted in the two study periods. Prior to each of the study periods, daylight and the contribution from the LED-lighting scenarios was measured. Patient sociodemographic and mental health data will be retrieved retrospectively from electronic medical records and by questionnaires administered in the two periods, evaluating lighting, noise, sleep quality and quality of life. Primary outcome is the proportion of patients receiving pro re nata medications. Secondary outcomes are the length of stay, sleep onset latency, sleep quality and quality of life.Ethics and disseminationNo ethical issues are expected. The results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed international journal, lectures, posters and interviews.Trial registration numberNCT05868291.Keywords: psychiatry, inpatients, hospitals, sleep medicine

Introduction

Historically, architectural planning of daylight has played a central role in the health and recovery planning at hospitals. Dating way back to Hippocrates1 2 and Vitruvius.3 Later, Nightingale investigated the impact of sunlight on the health, mortality and recovery of British soldiers during wartime.4 5 Her work inspired to a close collaboration between different medical and architectural professions, aiming at creating a healthier environment at hospitals. This resulted in the so-called Nightingale Wards, laid in a strict grid, with facades facing towards the south-east to make the most of the sunlight during the wintertime.2 At this dark time of year, mortality rates were peaking, according to Nightingales own empirical studies. Based on her personal experiences during the Crimean War, Nightingale insisted on bringing the forces of nature to work. According to Nightingale, ‘Medicine should assist nature and do nothing more’. What nursing had to do was to ‘put the patient in the best condition for nature to act on him’. Her work and publications from 1859 and onwards spawned a new era in the architectural planning of hospital architecture. During the following decades, hospitals were constructed and optimised for natural ventilation and natural daylight, especially sunlight, with facades faced to maximise the entry of sunlight during the dark winter period. Today, most of the Nightingale Pavilions remain in service.

Originally, lighting at hospitals was based on daylight autonomy, with possibilities for artificial lighting, limited to wax candles and oil lamps. This is clearly visible in the architectural planning of the old hospitals in 19th and early 20th century, including the original Bispebjerg Hospital. During evenings and night, all wards were often laid in complete darkness, no coincidence that Nightingale often was referred to as the female with the lamp, since she insisted on attending the patients in the dark wards during the evening and nighttime. At that time, artificial lighting was inefficient and often very expensive and making the most of daylight was crucial in the planning of hospitals.6 However, later electrification fundamentally changed the architectural planning during the 20th century most clearly observed in postwar hospitals, where daylight and solar exposure no longer was crucial to health or high visual performance at hospitals. Thus, not all patient rooms had access to sunlight. A larger and deeper footprint of buildings enabled more square metres, however, at the cost of incident daylight, which ultimately became secondary since it could easily and cheaply be replaced by artificial lighting. The buildings thus passed from constructions built for daylight, oriented according to the sun, to closed envelopes with facades facing disparate orientations including the north, typically with monolithic structures like Beaujon Hospital in France or the State Hospital in Copenhagen, Denmark.7

New Psychiatry Bispebjerg (NPB) is constructed along these new lines, built in 2022, as part of a new extension of the existing hospital Bispebjerg Hospital. In contrast to the original Bispebjerg Hospital, built as Nightingale Pavilions in 1913, the NPB has a compact building envelope, with its dimensions and room layouts making the architectural planning highly dependent on electrical lighting. Hence, the NPB is optimal for studying the impact of different lighting scenarios on the mental health of patients and staff using the building.8 9

Depression and chronotherapy

Chronotherapeutic methods, using light as treatment such as in bright light therapy (BLT), have shown promising results on depression and sleep disorders.10,12 Light is a powerful zeitgeber,13 giving the human brain information of time of day, allowing us to adjust circadian rhythms to the light/dark cycle.14 15 This information is mediated by certain cells of the retina, the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGC)16 17 and signalled to the brain through two pathways. One via the retinohypothalamic tract to the central clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and one independent of the SCN, consisting in pathways modulating the homeostatic sleep system, the enhancement of alertness and regulation of mood and emotions.18 19

Dynamic light-emitting diode (LED) lighting systems are integrated into the building, enabling changes in both light intensity and spectral distribution throughout the 24-hour day.20 These systems can be adjusted to stimulate and entrain the 24-hour circadian cycle of the SCN through the ipRGC system. Specifically, the spectral composition and intensity of LED lighting can be adjusted throughout the day to either advance or delay the sleep–wake cycle, based on the phase response curve for light.14 Short-wavelength (blue-enriched) light during the morning will advance sleep and increase alertness, while short-wavelength light administered during the evening conversely phase delays sleep onset. There is an association between depression and late chronotypes21 and late chronotypes have been found to be associated with non-remission of depression.22 When administered correctly, dynamic LED lighting with enriched short-wavelength morning light (enriched in the blue portion of the spectrum) and low content of short wavelength (blue-depleted) light during evening can prevent patients from drifting and even phase advance their sleep–wake cycle, thus entraining their circadian rhythm and augmenting their antidepressant treatment response.23 24 Depressed patients often exhibit a circadian disruption of their cortisol rhythm, with higher levels in the morning acting as a barrier to the effects of light therapy.25,27 Correctly administered 24-hour light is expected to normalise this disruption and augment the antidepressant effect of light. Patients with depression often also exhibit a large day-to-day instability of sleep and mood,28 and often with diurnal mood patterns, with low morning mood and an evening improvement. This diurnal mood pattern may be part of the explanation why patients with depression stay up late at night, delaying their sleep timing. Late bedtime carries a risk of light exposure in the delay phase of the phase response curve of light, further delaying sleep timing14 and the drift of the sleep–wake cycle has been shown to worsen depression.29

In psychiatry, a variety of treatments aiming to stabilise the circadian system are gaining impact such as Circadian Reinforcement Therapy (CRT) aiming to strengthen zeitgebers including daylight,28 30 cognitive–behavioural therapy for insomnia and BLT.15 31 32 All aiming at promoting and stabilise circadian rhythms and sleep. In this respect, dynamic lighting works as a chronotherapeutic treatment method, built into the hospital, thus working for 24 hours a day as a passive, fully integrated treatment element.

Daylight and dynamic LED lighting

The technical development has brought us a long way from the use of the daylight and sunlight only, to state-of-the-art LED lighting mimicking the characteristics of daylight with high-intensity light sources and efficient dynamic control of intensity and spectral distribution. Today, we now have the technology at our disposal to enable specific LED-lighting scenarios.33 34 However, we need to study the optimal intensity and spectral composition of light, including how to dose the right light at the right time of day to achieve maximal beneficial impact in the chronobiological system. Given the technical state-of-the-art LED lighting today, we also have the possibility to compensate for geographical differences in daylight availability in rooms, which according to several studies represent a health challenge at hospitals,1135,37 by improving the darker north-facing rooms, through the setting of the LED system in each individual room.

The precise amount of light necessary to entrain and stabilise an individual’s circadian remains to be standardised.38 However, we know that light has a proven effect on different mental health conditions. BLT has an established effect on patients suffering from seasonal depression and non-seasonal depression and bipolar depression, and BLT is a first line treatment for patients suffering from delayed sleep disorder. The antidepressant effect of BLT works within days compared with most medical antidepressants, taking 4–6 weeks.39 40 However, standard BLT light boxes do not provide any dynamics in intensity and spectral distribution during the day. Light boxes are expensive and carry with them a host of managerial problems. Hence, built-in, architectural light therapy, replacing the standard lighting systems, makes sense if the effect can be proven to be just as good as standard light therapy or helpful for patients unfit for BLT. Furthermore, a built-in dynamic lighting system may deliver bright light during the day, together with a dim light wake-up period and a dim light sleep preparation period, that cannot be achieved with traditional light boxes.

Very few studies have been conducted using built-in, architectural dynamic lighting system at hospitals so far.41 A study, at the neurorehabilitation unit of the Glostrup Hospital,42 administered dynamic lighting to patients suffering from a stroke. Patients were exposed to dynamic LED lighting, while having treatment as usual. The results showed that patients had significantly less depression and anxiety and a better quality of life compared with patients exposed to the static lighting. In other health centres in Denmark, dynamic lighting systems with a circadian rhythm has been implemented but so far postoccupancy effects on patients and staff have not been fully documented.

The aim of the study is to gain insight in how to promote better mental health with optimal dynamic light systems and with lower energy consumption. This protocol paper describes the design of the study with detailed characteristics of the daylight and LED lighting in NPB in the two scenarios, thus enabling others to test the scenarios in other settings.

Methods and analysis

Sample selection

The study will evaluate the effects of two dynamic lighting scenarios: a standard dynamic lighting scenario (SL) and a newly developed dynamic dim-evening lighting (DEL) scenario. The study is based on prior measurements in a 1:1 mock-up of the patient room in the NPB and a feasibility study.20 43 This study will investigate the effect of two different lighting scenarios:

Data will be collected for admitted patients to the NPB closed wards containing 72 single patient rooms (figure 1). All patients admitted at NPB during the two period will be included. These patients will have diagnoses covering a variety of psychiatric conditions that currently requires intensive care treatment in a closed ward. The study is expected to retrospectively retrieve case file data from approximately 1500 adult patients who will be admitted to these wards during the two study periods. In addition, questionnaire-based data from a total of 80 patients and 40 staff members, from each period, will be collected in the winter period of each of the 2 study periods.

Interventions

Design

This is a quasiexperimental study investigating all patients admitted at the four wards in the NPB during the two study periods. Thus, the patient population exposed to SL is to some degree different from the patient population exposed to the DEL, as length of stay is maximally 30 days. The SL scenarios is a continuation of the light dynamic scenario delivered from the contractors. The DEL scenario was implemented following a 1-year service inspection at the new hospital on 1 November 2023, where it replaced the SL scenario. The DEL scenario will be active until the study is concluded on 1 November 2024.

Data is collected during a baseline period from 1 November 2022 to 31 October 2023, and during an intervention period from 1 November 2023 to 31 October 2024.

Within the intervention period, the DEL scenario was adjusted from winter setting to summer setting on 1 March 2024. The summer DEL scenario differed from the winter DEL scenario by extending the illuminance levels by 1 hour in both morning and evening.

General description of LED luminaires and the dynamic lighting scenarios

Patient bedrooms, common areas and corridors are all included in the study. All physical luminaires remain the same during the study, only the dynamic spectral range and illuminance of the lighting is changed between baseline and intervention period (figure 1). The dynamic lighting at NPB is controlled by Digital Addressable Lighting Interface type 8 (https://www.dali-alliance.org/tech-notes/device-types.html) and all luminaires have their separate IP addresses.

Patient rooms are each equipped with two dynamic LED ceiling luminaires, specified to a maximum of 27 W, 2370 lumen (lm) and correlated colour temperature (CCT) from 2700 to 6500 K. Additionally, there is an LED dynamic spotlight above the TV, specified to a maximum of 19W, 1100 lm and CCT from 2700 to 6500 K.

Corridors (hallways outside patient rooms) have lighting consisting of a row of the 19 W spotlights, placed asymmetrical, adjacent to walls near patient rooms and opposite windows in the corridors. These areas are also adjusted in the DEL scenario and share the same lighting profile as southeast (SE)-facing patient rooms.

Common areas are equipped with similar 19 W spotlights mounted in the ceiling above tables and dining areas. These areas are included in the DEL having the same SE-facing lighting profile as the patient corridors, although with higher light intensity during the evening.

Offices and staff-only areas are not included in the study design.

In the SL scenario, all luminaires have dynamic colour temperature and fixed light intensity. This is the standard light scenario at NPB, as delivered by contractors.

In the DEL scenario, luminaires use dynamic colour temperatures and dynamic light intensity and have a markedly lower evening light intensity. The DEL scenario is furthermore adjusted for the geographical orientation during daytime to compensate for differences in access to sunlight.

Light measurements in patient rooms

To document incident daylight in geographically different locations in the building and to estimate the performance of the dynamic lighting conditions in patient rooms, a comprehensive light measurement protocol was carried out both before the baseline study period and before the start of the intervention period. The aim was to facilitate creation of the DEL scenario in NPB.

Daylight measurements in patient rooms

Daylight was measured on-site in four patient rooms, each representing different geographical orientations: northeast (NE), southeast (SE), southwest (SW) and northwest (NW). Daylight was measured using a Gigahertz-Optik BTS256 spectrometer mounted in the window of the patient room facing outwards (yellow dot in figure 1). A 7-day average 24-hour profile of the measured daylight is shown in online supplemental figure 1, with results from all four geographical directions given as melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (M-EDI) values.38 Results show that SE and SW received most daylight (>2200 lux M-EDI) in the morning, while NE and NW received very little daylight (<500 lux M-EDI) later in the day, as we documented in an earlier study.11

The M-EDI is found by applying the measured spectral power distribution (SPD) of the investigated light condition to the toolbox of CIE S 026:2018. The M-EDI results location point is shown in figure 1 (red dot).

The logged data in the windows are transferred to a vertical point 1.2 m above ground. Conversion factors are calculated in the simulation software Dialux to be 0.08 for NE and NW and 0.06 for SE and SW. The difference in conversion factors is because SE-facing and SW-facing windows receive a component of direct sunlight, whereas NW-facing and NE-facing windows receive no sunlight at the simulated time, date and location. These light exposures are then accumulated into doses of M-EDI hours as visualised in figure 2 as relative dose contributions during the morning (06:00–14:00) and evening (14:00–22:00) part of the day, defined in online supplemental table 1. Furthermore, the different exposures are shown in online supplemental file 2, using a single-lens reflex camera with fixed shutter speed placed 1.2 m above the floor near the door, as shown in figure 1. The results confirm the data from the prior measurements, revealing huge differences and variations between time of day and between different orientation of rooms.

Artificial lighting measurements in patient rooms

The artificial lighting was measured in a calibrated laboratory at the Technical University of Denmark (DTU) photonics, where the two applied luminaires were characterised regarding luminous flux, SPD and power consumption at all possible luminaire settings, with 10% interval in intensity and 100 K in CCT. That enabled us to fully document the performance of the artificial lighting in both baseline and intervention settings and to combine it with the daylight measurements. Based on this laboratory characterisation of the applied luminaires at DTU Photonics, it was possible to create the desired dynamic lighting profile for DEL and represent it as M-EDI at the spot measurement point at 0.8 m horizontal, as shown in figure 1. Furthermore, by measuring the illuminance level at the same spot in laboratory lighting conditions with daylight excluded, it was possible to compare both photopic and melanopic lighting conditions in the room at baseline and intervention, as shown in figure 3.

The created DEL lighting scenario

The hypothesis of the study is that the DEL scenario with bright light during the day facilitates energy and mood and the dim light in the evening functions as a preparation for sleep,11 12 20 compared with the SL scenario.

Based on data collection and from prior studies, including the study of a 1:1 mock-up of the NPB bedroom,41 and the daylight and dynamic light measurements at the NPB, the DEL scenario was created to favour higher light intensities in the shortwave spectral range of light in the morning (6.00–14.00) with a gradual decrease in M-EDI during the evening (14.00–22.00) and with a low M-EDI during night-time (22.00–6.00). In this way, rooms in ‘the dark facades’ of the building, NE and NW receive higher levels of artificial lighting during the daytime, when compared with ‘the sunny facades’, SE and SW. This balances the differences in average daylight exposure, caused by the orientation of the wards, ranging from 1404 M-EDI hours in NE to 5982 M-EDI hours in SE.

Sample size calculation

If we expect a mean duration of stay of 10 days (with a range 3–30 days, depending on the ward) and an observation time of 5*2 months, a total of more than 1500 patients will go through the hospital in the two study periods together. Of these, a few might appear in both periods due to readmissions.

The primary outcome was chosen as the proportion of patients given any pro re nata (PRN) psychotropic during their stay. With an expected proportion in the SL period of 60% and of 50% in the DEL period, a power of 80 %, and an alfa value of 0.05, a total number of 294 patients are required.

The number of patients was calculated from known length of stay in similar hospital wards. The number of questionnaires was estimated based on the known willingness and capability of patients and staff to fill in questionnaires.

Patients staying at the NPB will have a variety of psychiatric conditions, some of which might be less influenced by lighting conditions than affective disorders. This can be investigated from diagnostic categories that can be retrieved from Epic, allowing subanalyses.

Ranking of outcomes

The primary outcome was chosen as the proportion of patients given any PRN psychotropic during their stay retrieved from the Epic system.

Secondary outcomes are the length of stay retrieved from the Epic system as well as sleep onset latency (SOL), sleep quality and WHO-5 quality of life data from the questionnaires.

Exploratory outcomes are the proportion of patients experiencing acute coercive medication use, mean Brøset violence score, proportion of patients with an increased suicidal risk, all retrieved from Epic, and all remaining items from the questionnaires. Seasonal effects will be analysed in the Epic data.

All comments from patients and staff in the two study periods will be collected and categorised according to the good clinical practice (GCP) reporting of adverse events.

Description of outcome measures

Data from case report files (Epic)

These data will be sampled from the Epic files: sociodemographics, patients’ room and ward number, admission date and duration of patient’s stay, number of hours sleep, Brøset violence checklist score, prescription of psychotropic medication including PRN medication, acute sedating medication gives as coercive measure, number of coercive measures, Hamilton depression rating scores,44 suicidality assessments, observation levels, length of stay. Data from Epic will be retrieved after the end of the intervention period in 2024.

Data from questionnaires from patients and staff

The questionnaires contain questions regarding: tolerance of dynamic lighting (morning, afternoon and evening), SOL, sleep quality, duration of time in the ward gardens, noise perception, temperature tolerance at bedtime, quality of life from the WHO-5 scale (only patients).45 The questionnaires for the patients and the staff were identical apart from the SOL question and WHO-5 Scale that were chosen not to be included in the questionnaire to the staff for reasons of privacy. The questionnaire for patients is shown in online supplemental table 2 and for staff in online supplemental table 3.

Data monitoring

According to Danish law, only drug studies are required to comply with GCP guidelines. We will, however, adhere to the GCP rules to secure study quality. Data analyses, performed by the primary investigators, will be supervised by a statistician. Patient data from the Epic system will be collected retrospectively for the standard and intervention period. The investigators will be in continuous contact with the staff at the wards and will follow the flow of admissions to secure questionnaire collection.

Data analysis plan

Data from Epic and from questionnaires will be entered into the RedCap system, where data are stored on secure servers within the Mental Health Services. Data collected from light sensors during the preparation period is stored on hard drives at the technical University of Denmark, and these do not contain any patient data. Sociodemographic data will be analysed with parametric or non-parametric methods according to distribution of data, proportions with a χ2 test and numerical data with be analysed with parametric or non-parametric methods according to distribution of data. The primary outcome will be analysed in a generalised linear model with month, geographical location of room, and diagnosis as covariates. The significance level is set at 5% two tailed. No interim analyses are planned. As all patient present in the study periods are included for data analysis, no inclusion or exclusion criteria are relevant.

Patient and public involvement

The DEL lighting scenario was tested in the ward before start of the intervention period where staff and patients could comment on the intensity and color of the light and the possibility of overriding the setting.

Ethics and dissemination

The DEL lighting scenario was tested on selected patients and staff prior to the study start, with a favourable response. We do not expect that the study will expose participants to any serious hazard, as their usual clinical management is maintained and the known side effect profile of light is beneficial.12 46 Furthermore, the lighting intensity, including the melanopic stimulation, is within normal hospital settings. The dimming of light at evening might cause some inconvenience, but light in the bedrooms can be overridden to increase light intensity and will then return to the dynamic setting within half an hour. All adverse events will be recorded until the end of the follow-up period, and regulatory rules for reporting of Serious Adverse Events (SAEs) and Serious Adverse Reactions (SARs) will be adhered to. Any harm due to the study procedures is covered by the Danish Patient Compensation Association. The study will be stopped if there is a clinical suspicion of harm in the intervention group, or if new evidence emerges that participants can come to harm due to the intervention. Staff and patients has been explained of the change of lighting including that they can override the setting. Written and oral information about the study was given to all the patients that provided a written informed consent before filling in the questionnaires.

Information has been given to the head of the psychiatric hospital and to the ward staff during the planning of the project and will be given regularly during the project period. We do not expect the intervention lighting to induce manic states or to disturb sleep.

Patients will not be asked for informed consent regarding the change of light settings as this is considered a postoccupancy quality study with the aim of securing the best use of the dynamic lighting system.

Results will be published in peer-reviewed international journals, posters and oral presentations at international symposia and reporting of light will adhere to current standards.47 All data whether negative, positive or inconclusive will be reported in full. All members of the study group are coauthors with a prearranged order. All data will be available to all authors.

No professional writers will be used. Depending on the journal of publication, part of the protocol, statistical code and dataset will be publicly available.

The light settings will only be altered if serious adverse events occur that can be ascribed to the light setting, and if substantial problems with light tolerability/feasibility occurs.

Regarding the questionnaire data, we provide written information and oral information to all participants prior to obtaining an informed consent.

The regional ethical committee of the Capital Region of Denmark was consulted and responded that the study did not meet the requirements for an application (Jr. number 22006662).
