Allopurinol Versus Febuxostat Use and the Risk of Cardiovascular Disease in People With Chronic Kidney Disease: A New‐User Active Comparator Cohort Study
Authors: Reiko Inoue, Satoko Yamaguchi, Akira Okada, Toshimasa Yamauchi, Takashi Kadowaki, Masaomi Nangaku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimHyperuricaemia, a common comorbidity among people with chronic kidney disease, is widely treated with uric acid‐lowering agents such as allopurinol and febuxostat. Cardiovascular outcomes of people with chronic kidney disease receiving allopurinol or febuxostat have been controversial. The present study evaluated the risk of cardiovascular events associated with allopurinol or febuxostat treatment in people with chronic kidney disease.MethodsWe conducted a new‐user active comparator cohort study using a nationwide insurance claims database in Japan. Individuals with an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 who were newly prescribed allopurinol or febuxostat were included. The primary outcome was a composite of cardiovascular events, including fatal and non‐fatal acute myocardial infarction, fatal and non‐fatal stroke, and all‐cause death. Hazard ratios were estimated using a multivariate Cox regression model. A sensitivity analysis was performed using an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) Cox regression model.ResultsA total of 1673 and 7805 individuals were included in the allopurinol and febuxostat treatment groups, respectively. The febuxostat group had a similar incidence of the composite outcome as the allopurinol group (hazard ratio 0.93, 95% confidence interval: 0.79–1.08, p = 0.33). The hazard ratio for febuxostat compared with allopurinol treatment did not vary across different estimated glomerular filtration rate levels. The sensitivity analysis using IPTW showed similar results.ConclusionIn conclusion, no statistically significant difference in the risk of cardiovascular events was observed in people with chronic kidney disease who were newly prescribed febuxostat compared with those newly treated with allopurinol.Keywords: cardiovascular risk, chronic kidney disease, cohort study, database, uric acid

Introduction

Hyperuricaemia is a common comorbidity among people with chronic kidney disease (CKD), and the prevalence of hyperuricaemia increases with CKD progression. Hyperuricaemia is associated with an increased risk of gout, cardiovascular events, mortality, and CKD progression [1, 2, 3]. Experimental studies have identified several potential pathways that may explain the mechanisms of uric acid‐induced kidney damage. These include activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system, induction of oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, and tubular precipitation of urate crystals [4, 5]. However, applying uric acid‐lowering therapy to individuals with hyperuricaemia who do not have gout for the purpose of preventing cardiovascular events or CKD progression remains controversial [4, 6]. Several randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and meta‐analyses have indicated the positive effect of uric acid‐lowering therapy [7, 8], whereas other studies have shown no significant benefit [9, 10]. This situation has led to varying approaches to managing asymptomatic hyperuricaemia in people with CKD across regions and countries. The most recent global guideline recommends not offering uric acid‐lowering therapy to people with CKD and asymptomatic hyperuricaemia for the purpose of delaying CKD progression [11]. In contrast, the guidelines from East Asian countries including Japan have suggested the use of uric acid‐lowering drugs in patients with CKD for renal protection [12, 13].

Among the various uric acid‐lowering drugs, xanthine oxidase inhibitors are recommended over uricosurics for patients with CKD stage 3 or higher [13], and allopurinol and febuxostat are the most frequently prescribed xanthine oxidase inhibitors in the CKD population in Japan. Several RCTs have been conducted to evaluate the effects of these two xanthine oxidase inhibitors on the risk of cardiovascular events. The Cardiovascular Safety of Febuxostat and Allopurinol in Patients with Gout and Cardiovascular Morbidities (CARES) trial, reported in 2018, showed that all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality rates were higher in patients treated with febuxostat compared with those administered allopurinol [14]. Conversely, the subsequently published Febuxostat versus Allopurinol Streamlined Trial (FAST) reported that febuxostat was not associated with an increased risk of overall or cardiovascular mortality compared with allopurinol [15]. However, neither trial primarily focused on people with CKD, and both excluded those with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2. Moreover, both trials focused on patients with gout and did not include individuals with asymptomatic hyperuricaemia. The effects of these drugs on cardiovascular risk in people with CKD, including those with moderate to severe CKD or asymptomatic hyperuricaemia, have been less documented in previous studies, and have shown conflicting results [16, 17, 18, 19]. Although some studies observed no significant difference in cardiovascular risk between the two drugs in patients with CKD stages 3 to 5 [16, 17], other studies suggested that febuxostat may offer cardiovascular benefits in patients with CKD stage 5 [18], whereas allopurinol was associated with better outcomes among those undergoing haemodialysis [19].

Given the high cardiovascular risk in people with CKD, selecting drugs with lower cardiovascular risks is important. However, previous RCTs comparing the major uric acid‐lowering drugs used in people with CKD, allopurinol and febuxostat, had limitations in that they excluded people with advanced CKD and focused only on those with gout. Here, we conducted an observational cohort study using real‐world data from Japan to evaluate the hypothesis that treatment with febuxostat, compared with treatment with allopurinol, is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events in people with CKD.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using a Japanese health insurance claims database, the DeSC database (DeSC Healthcare, Inc. Tokyo, Japan). The detailed characteristics of the database have been described elsewhere [20]. Briefly, the database encompasses nationwide insurance claims data provided by three types of insurers: the Advanced Elderly Medical Service System for all individuals aged 75 years or older, the National Health Insurance for proprietors, pensioners, or irregular employers, and the association/union‐administered health insurance for salaried employees of large corporations. The database contains information on annual health check‐ups and insurance claims records submitted by clinics, hospitals, and pharmacies. Annual check‐up data include information on body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, blood pressure measurement, questionnaires on smoking habits and alcohol consumption, and laboratory biomarkers. Claims data include disease and medication names documented using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10), and the World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System (WHO‐ATC) codes, respectively. The database also contains information on deaths provided by the insurers, which is derived from the reported reasons for the loss of insurance eligibility.

Study Population

A new‐user active comparator cohort design was employed to minimise confounding by indication [21]. Both allopurinol and febuxostat are indicated for patients with CKD and hyperuricaemia, regardless of the presence or absence of gout, and neither is specifically preferred over the other in Japan. All individuals aged 18 years or older who were newly prescribed allopurinol (WHO‐ATC: M04AA01) or febuxostat (WHO‐ATC: M04AA03) between 1 April 2014, and 31 July 2023, with at least a 6‐month look‐back period before the first prescription and with available data from an annual health check‐up, were identified. The date of the first prescription was defined as the index date. New users were defined as those with no prescription for allopurinol or febuxostat during the look‐back period. In addition, the individuals included in this study met the following criteria: (i) data on BMI, smoking habit, serum creatinine and uric acid levels, and urinary protein were available from annual check‐ups performed before the index date; (ii) eGFR values based on serum creatinine levels were < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2; and (iii) information on death and on the type of facility (e.g., university hospital, general hospital, or clinic) where allopurinol or febuxostat was first prescribed was available. Individuals with a history of cancer, acute myocardial infarction, stroke, or kidney transplantation, those undergoing dialysis, or those prescribed uric acid‐lowering agents, comprising agents other than allopurinol and febuxostat, during the look‐back period were excluded. The 6‐month look‐back period was adopted to ensure a new‐user design, as the maximum prescription period in Japan is limited to 90 days, and continuous administration of prescribed agents requires a new prescription at least every 3 months. The study size was determined by the availability of eligible patients in the database, as all individuals who met the inclusion criteria were included. Therefore, a formal sample size calculation was not performed.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Graduate School of Medicine at the University of Tokyo (Approval Number: 2018030NI) and was performed according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [22]. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the anonymity of the data.

Study Outcomes and Variables

The primary outcome was defined as a composite of cardiovascular events, including fatal and non‐fatal acute myocardial infarction (ICD‐10 codes I21, I22, I23, I24, and I25), fatal and non‐fatal stroke (ICD‐10 codes I60, I61, I62, I63, and I64), or all‐cause death. All‐cause death was employed because reliably classified information on cardiovascular death was not available in the database. Secondary outcomes focused on each component of the primary outcome.

The following information was obtained from the check‐up performed before the index date: sex, age, BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, laboratory results for haemoglobin A1c, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, liver enzymes, serum creatinine, uric acid, and dipstick tests for urinary protein and glucose. Additionally, data on smoking status and alcohol consumption frequency were obtained. Information on the amount of alcohol consumption was not obtained due to a large proportion of missing values.

Information was obtained from the claims data during the look‐back period on comorbidities of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, angina pectoris, heart failure, arrhythmia, cerebrovascular disease other than stroke, peripheral arterial disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchial asthma, autoimmune disease, human immunodeficiency virus infection, psychiatric disease, urolithiasis, hepatic disease, and gout. Medication histories for insulin, sodium‐glucose co‐transporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, other hypoglycaemic agents, calcium channel blockers, beta blockers, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors, diuretics, statins, other anti‐dyslipidaemia agents, oral corticosteroids, and antithrombotic agents prescribed during the look‐back period were also obtained. The disease and drug codes used to define the comorbidities and medication histories are provided in Table S1. In addition, information on the type of facility (e.g., university hospital, general hospital, or clinic) in which allopurinol or febuxostat was first prescribed was collected.

Statistical Analysis

The follow‐up time was calculated from the index date to the date of an outcome event, or the date of the last record available in the database, whichever came first. The baseline characteristics were summarised by using mean and standard deviation (SD) values for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student's t‐test for continuous variables and the chi‐squared test for categorical variables.

A multivariate Cox regression model was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs). Adjusted factors were as follows: age, sex, eGFR, urine protein dipstick, BMI, smoking habit, type of facility for the first prescription, presence of comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, angina pectoris, cerebrovascular disease other than stroke, and heart failure), and drug prescriptions (SGLT2 inhibitors, angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors, statins, and antithrombotics). Age, eGFR, and BMI were analysed as continuous variables. The Fine and Gray regression model was used to analyse acute myocardial infarction and stroke outcomes considering death as a competing risk.

To evaluate the impact of febuxostat compared with allopurinol treatment on the association between eGFR and adjusted HR values of the primary outcome, we employed a restricted cubic spline model. The eGFR was treated as a nonlinear continuous variable with knots placed at 15, 30, and 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. The results were adjusted for covariates utilised in the multivariate Cox regression analysis.

Four sensitivity analyses for the primary and secondary outcomes were conducted to ensure the robustness of the study. First, we employed an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) Cox regression model instead of multivariate Cox regression to estimate HRs. Propensity scores, calculated by logistic regression with 45 variables (Table S2), including age, sex, eGFR, and uric acid level at the annual check‐up performed before the index date, as well as comorbidities and medications during the look‐back period, were used to calculate the stabilised average treatment effect for IPTW. Two individuals with missing low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol data and 67 with missing HbA1c data were omitted from the propensity score calculation and subsequent analysis. Standardised differences were calculated to compare the characteristics between the allopurinol and febuxostat treatment groups. Absolute standardised differences of < 0.10 were considered balanced. In the second sensitivity analysis, a per‐protocol analysis instead of an intention‐to‐treat analysis was applied. Individuals were followed from the index date until the date of occurrence of an outcome event, or the end of the prescription period, defined as the number of days covered by the last prescription. In the third sensitivity analysis, individuals included were restricted to those with two or more prescriptions and a total prescription duration of 180 days or longer. In the fourth sensitivity analysis, the look‐back period of 6 months was extended to 12 months.

Subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age (< 75 vs. ≥ 75 years), eGFR (< 45 vs. ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2), and history of gout were conducted.

A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study Population

A total of 14 362 individuals with new prescriptions for allopurinol or febuxostat met the inclusion criteria for this study. After the application of the exclusion criteria, a total of 1673 and 7805 individuals were included in the allopurinol and febuxostat treatment groups, respectively (Figure 1). The mean follow‐up period was 954.57 days (SD: 635.72) and 824.96 days (SD: 591.50) for the allopurinol and febuxostat groups, respectively (Table S3). Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of individuals by treatment group. Individuals prescribed febuxostat had a significantly lower eGFR, and more severe proteinuria, were more likely to receive their initial prescription at general or university hospitals, had more comorbidities (e.g., diabetes and heart failure), and received more medications compared with those prescribed allopurinol.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

In the allopurinol treatment group, 204 individuals experienced the composite outcome, with an incidence rate of 46.66 per 1000 person‐years, whereas 909 individuals experienced the composite outcome, with an incidence rate of 51.56 per 1000 person‐years in the febuxostat treatment group (Table S3). Our multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that the febuxostat group had a similar incidence of the composite outcome compared with the allopurinol group, with an adjusted HR of 0.93 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.79–1.08, p = 0.33) (Table 2).

Sensitivity Analyses

The results of our sensitivity analyses for the primary and secondary outcomes with respect to acute myocardial infarction and stroke were comparable to those of the main analysis (Table S4). After IPTW, all the confounders were balanced between the groups with absolute standardised differences of < 0.10. For the analysis based on the IPTW Cox regression model, the HR for the composite outcome was 0.95 (95% CI: 0.81–1.12, p = 0.54). Employing a per‐protocol instead of an intention‐to‐treat analysis, the adjusted HR for the composite outcome was 1.03 (95% CI: 0.80–1.32, p = 0.83). When the analysis was restricted to individuals with two or more prescriptions and a total prescription duration of 180 days or longer, the adjusted HR for the composite outcome was 0.96 (95% CI: 0.80–1.15, p = 0.64). When the look‐back period was extended from 6 to 12 months, the adjusted HR for the composite outcome was 0.97 (95% CI: 0.81–1.15, p = 0.70).

For the secondary outcome of all‐cause death, the sensitivity analysis using the IPTW Cox regression model showed results consistent with the main analysis. However, the per‐protocol analysis, the analysis restricted to individuals with two or more prescriptions and a total prescription duration of 180 days or longer, and the analysis with a 12‐month look‐back period showed no statistically significant difference between the groups.

Subgroup Analyses

In the subgroup analyses, the results were broadly consistent across the categories of sex, age, eGFR, and history of gout for the primary and secondary outcomes, particularly acute myocardial infarction and stroke. However, in males, in individuals aged ≥ 75, in those with eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, and in those with a history of gout, the association was attenuated and did not reach statistical significance for the secondary outcome of all‐cause death. Tests for interaction were not statistically significant (Figure S1).

Discussion

In the present study that employed a Japanese nationwide health insurance claims database, no statistically significant difference was observed in the risk of cardiovascular events between people with CKD treated with febuxostat and those treated with allopurinol. Baseline eGFR levels did not modify the differences in risk between allopurinol and febuxostat treatment.

Few previous studies comparing the cardiovascular risks of allopurinol and febuxostat therapy have focused on people with CKD. A recently published retrospective cohort study using a Japanese database assessed the incidence of cardiovascular events among a CKD cohort identified based on diagnoses recorded in insurance claims [17], and concluded that no differences in cardiovascular risks were observed between allopurinol and febuxostat treatments, which is consistent with our current results. Our present study used serum creatinine data from medical check‐ups to identify the CKD population. Since the sensitivity of CKD diagnoses recorded in claims data is generally low [23], and CKD‐related diagnoses are more likely to be recorded in more severe CKD which requires specific treatment such as medication for renal anaemia or hyperkalaemia [24], the CKD population in this study differs from that in the previous study [17]. The results of the present study are likely to be more generalisable to the broader CKD population. Another study conducted a network meta‐analysis of 12 RCTs and four cohort studies to compare the renal and safety outcomes of allopurinol and febuxostat treatments in patients with CKD stages 3 to 5 and asymptomatic hyperuricaemia [16], and also reported no difference in cardiovascular risk between allopurinol and febuxostat therapies. This result was based on an analysis involving indirect comparisons, using data from RCTs comparing either allopurinol or febuxostat treatment with placebo. Moreover, the two cohort studies directly comparing allopurinol and febuxostat treatments that were included in the meta‐analysis were both small and single‐centre studies [25, 26].

Focusing on patients with CKD in more severe stages, cohort studies reported conflicting results. Tsai et al. concluded that febuxostat treatment was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular events compared with allopurinol therapy in patients with CKD stage 5 requiring erythropoiesis‐stimulating agents [18]. Conversely, Ishii et al. reported that allopurinol was associated with fewer cardiovascular outcomes among patients on haemodialysis [19]. Allopurinol and febuxostat are both xanthine oxidase inhibitors but differ in several pharmacological properties. Febuxostat, more recently introduced into clinical settings, is a non‐purine selective inhibitor of xanthine oxidase and has minimal effects on other enzymes involved in purine and pyrimidine metabolism [27]. Febuxostat is reported to have more pronounced antioxidant and anti‐atherogenic effects than allopurinol [28]. Further, febuxostat is reported to be a strong inhibitor of the ATP‐binding cassette transporter G2 (ABCG2), whereas allopurinol has little effect on this transporter [29]. ABCG2 is a urate efflux transporter expressed in various tissues, including the luminal membranes of the kidneys and the intestinal tract. ABCG2 dysfunction is reported to be associated with eGFR decline, possibly by reducing the excretion and aggravating the accumulation of indoxyl sulphate, one of the major uraemic toxins elevated in patients with CKD [30]. Thus, the different pharmacological properties of allopurinol and febuxostat may have varying effects on patients with CKD depending on their kidney function. However, in the present study, no differences in the adjusted HRs for the various cardiovascular outcomes were observed in relation to eGFR. This may be attributable to the heterogeneity in genetic activation levels of urate transporters, pathogenesis of hyperuricaemia, and utilised dosage of allopurinol or febuxostat among the individuals with CKD included in the present study.

In the present study, the risk of all‐cause death was significantly lower among individuals prescribed febuxostat compared with those prescribed allopurinol based on both multivariate and IPTW Cox regression models. The FAST study found that treatment with febuxostat was not associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular or all‐cause death compared with allopurinol, and indeed, fewer deaths were observed in the febuxostat group than in the allopurinol group [15], which is consistent with our findings. However, our results were not robust across sensitivity analyses and should, therefore, be interpreted with caution. The reason for this inconsistency may be that the individuals in the allopurinol group tended to discontinue treatment earlier. This may be because some individuals who were initially prescribed allopurinol may have subsequently switched to febuxostat or discontinued treatment owing to adverse drug reactions. As a result, the number of death outcomes was markedly reduced in the per‐protocol analysis (Table S5). Additionally, the number of eligible individuals for sensitivity analyses was decreased, as one analysis was restricted to individuals with two or more prescriptions and a total prescription duration of 180 days or longer, and another had an extended 12‐month look‐back period. In addition, all‐cause death included deaths from causes other than cardiovascular diseases, such as cancer or infectious diseases. Although individuals with a history of cancer were excluded from the present study, non‐cardiovascular deaths may have influenced the results.

The key strengths of this study are as follows. First, our study applied the new‐user active comparator design to reduce indication bias and improve baseline comparability between the treatment groups. This was achieved by the utilisation of the claims‐based database which contains all claims information regardless of which hospitals or clinics the individual visited, making it possible to accurately identify the first‐time prescriptions of the drugs (allopurinol and febuxostat). Second, although the study focused on individuals with CKD, a relatively large sample size was achievable by using a nationwide claims database. Third, we focused on individuals with CKD including those without a history of gout. Asymptomatic hyperuricaemia is treated in only a few countries including Japan, and the majority of clinical studies conducted outside East Asia do not include this population. Although the generalisability of this study may be limited to the populations of these countries, such evidence is required in clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, owing to the retrospective nature of the study using a database, information on socio‐economic status, amount of alcohol consumption, physical activity, dietary patterns (e.g., fruit, vegetable, and salt intake), and the extent of drug adherence after the prescription was not available, and therefore the possibility of residual confounding may remain. The imbalance in baseline characteristics, such as more advanced CKD and a greater burden of comorbidities in the febuxostat group, raises the possibility of unmeasured confounding, despite adjustment for measurable variables. For example, individuals with more comorbidities may have been treated by more highly specialised physicians. In such cases, the cardiovascular risk in the febuxostat group may have been underestimated. Residual confounding may therefore involve physician‐level factors such as clinical expertise or board certification in nephrology. Second, the dosage of allopurinol or febuxostat was not considered, and the uric acid levels after the administration of uric acid‐lowering therapy were not monitored, both of which could potentially influence cardiovascular outcomes. Third, since the present study compared allopurinol and febuxostat without a placebo or non‐user comparator, we were not able to evaluate the benefits or adverse effects of the uric acid‐lowering therapy itself compared with individuals who did not receive medications for hyperuricaemia. Fourth, the CKD population in the present study was identified based on a single serum creatinine measurement preceding the first prescription of allopurinol or febuxostat. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that some individuals included in the study did not meet the precise diagnostic criteria for CKD at the index date.

In conclusion, no statistically significant difference in the risk of cardiovascular events was observed in people with CKD treated with febuxostat compared with those treated with allopurinol. Further studies are required to establish a medication strategy for treating hyperuricaemia in people with CKD.
