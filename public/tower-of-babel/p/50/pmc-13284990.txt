Why has continuity of general practitioner care been neglected?
Authors: Denis Pereira Gray, Kate Sidaway-Lee, Nada Khan, Philip Evans
Published: 2026

Imagine that a health innovation was shown in multiple studies to be associated with reduced hospital admissions [1, 2], less urgent and emergency care use, reduced healthcare costs [3], and reduced primary care workload [4] with better overall patient outcomes, including reduced mortality [2, 5]. We might expect such an innovation to be lauded by policymakers and widely adopted in healthcare systems. Surprisingly, this ‘innovation’ is not a new app or wonder-drug but one of the foundational features of primary care and general practice, namely continuity of care.

Relational continuity, meaning a patient seeing the same family physician repeatedly, is low in many countries. In some, such as the UK, where it used to be the norm, continuity has fallen spectacularly [6] in most practices. Governments and health authorities appear to ignore the research and underestimate how intrinsic continuity is to primary care. The reasons for this are complex, multifactorial, and interlinked (Fig. 1). For example, there has been a historic bias in favour of secondary over primary care. Continuity of care is a form of human behaviour, as are some of its primary outcomes, and policymakers have long undervalued behavioural science research in medical care. The importance of the doctor-patient relationship is devalued in an increasingly transactional doctor-patient context.

We hypothesize that these might form a vicious circle which could lead to a progressive reduction in continuity unless there is consideration of continuity as part of a more holistic approach to healthcare planning.

There has also been a failure to increase family physician numbers, despite increasing demand. There is more GP turnover with retirement and a more mobile workforce, disrupting continuity. Practices are serving greater numbers of patients, and more GPs are working part-time. With deliberate planning, these factors do not necessarily reduce continuity, but in practices with no system to promote continuity, continuity falls as seeing one particular GP becomes less likely by chance.

In many countries policymakers have repeatedly ignored continuity, while fostering and financially incentivizing initiatives that prioritize access above continuity. While a focus on continuity does not reduce access, as shown in practices with good levels of both, prioritizing fast access often means an ‘any doctor will do’ approach and the policies also can reduce the possibility of booking with a specific doctor further in advance [7]. Once politicians have committed themselves to policies which turn out to be detrimental to continuity, it becomes psychologically and politically more difficult for them to change focus.

In addition, a consequence of falling continuity is that newly qualified family physicians are less likely to work in a clinical environment that values continuity and therefore they undervalue it. Patients with no experience and appreciation of GP continuity are less likely to want or expect it. This adds to the disincentive for politicians to prioritize continuity above fast access to any doctor. Another key factor is the lack of routine measurement of relational continuity to allow practices and clinicians to benchmark and improve levels of continuity, while access can be easily measured in the time from first contact to appointment.

At one level this lack of prioritization must mean that findings from the extensive international research literature on relational continuity in primary care have not been fully acknowledged nor have they affected policy. Some people express methodological concerns about the quality of evidence and possible confounding factors linking continuity with positive outcomes. The great majority of continuity studies remain observational and there have only been two, very limited, randomized trials in medical care [8], although there have been several RCTs and a Cochrane review of continuity within midwifery [9]. Outcomes of the RCTs in midwifery, such as increased patient satisfaction and better outcomes, match those of the observational studies in medicine. A randomized trial of GP continuity is needed. In the meanwhile, national policymakers and primary care providers must decide if GP continuity should now be provided on the basis of the long-established common law test of benefit based on the balance of probabilities.

Comprehensively measuring GP continuity within practices is the next step for the future as, without measurement, improvement or deterioration in continuity levels cannot be tracked. An increasing number of countries are now doing this.

Despite the current progressive fall in continuity there is now new hope that the tide is turning. Some countries, including Norway [2], Finland and Canada have introduced policies aimed at increasing continuity and are reversing the downward trend in relational continuity. Research is needed into the reasons behind and the effects of the different policies which influence continuity, for better or for worse. This could identify key avenues for influence and improvement.

It has taken time to understand that GP continuity drives higher quality of care [10], including clinical alertness and better prescribing. However, with clear advocacy from GP leaders, better research and effective measurement of continuity there is a possibility that we have reached the turning point for intentionally building relational continuity into primary care systems worldwide. We hope that the neglect of continuity of GP/family physician continuity by policymakers will finally end.
