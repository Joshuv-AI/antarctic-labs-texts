Intelligent risk assessment and early warning for human–machine–environment coupling in coal preparation plants
Authors: Yuechuan Zhao, Yuxi Hu, Qinghui Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoal washing and preparation is a critical process for cleaner and more efficient coal utilization, while safety performance in coal preparation plants is often challenged by harsh operating conditions, tightly coupled equipment, and limited effectiveness of manual supervision. In this study, a coal preparation plant was used as an engineering scenario to investigate equipment safety risk grading and a deployable workflow for risk-based monitoring and on-site warning. Hazardous factors in key processes were identified through accident analysis and field investigation, and a multi-dimensional risk assessment model was established by integrating Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) and the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). To enhance real-time supervision in high-risk areas, an optimized YOLOv10-based object detection model was developed, improving robustness under heavy dust, low illumination, and complex backgrounds. On this basis, a personnel safety monitoring system integrating monocular vision-based ranging and audible–visual alarms was implemented to enable real-time warning and traceable logging. Experimental and field results show that, under the reported deployment conditions, the proposed system achieved. These results suggest that risk grading can be combined with vision-based monitoring to support risk-informed warning and traceable logging in coal preparation plants.Keywords: Coal preparation plant, Safety risk grading, Fault tree analysis, YOLOv10, Risk-based warning, Intelligent monitoring and early warningSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Safety production has long been positioned by the Chinese government as a central component of modernizing the national governance system and capacity. With the continuous refinement of the Work Safety Law of the People’s Republic of China and the sustained implementation of the “dual prevention mechanism”—namely, safety risk classification and control alongside hazard identification and remediation—the coal industry faces intensified regulatory scrutiny while also encountering strategic opportunities driven by digital and intelligent transformation1–3. Since 2024, a series of national-level policies on mine intelligence have explicitly called for accelerating intelligent development of mines (including coal mines and associated surface systems) and for integrating next-generation information technologies—such as the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT), big data, and artificial intelligence—into safety governance. These initiatives aim to restructure risk-tiered control and hazard management systems, thereby improving intrinsic safety in a systematic manner4–6.

Coal preparation plants serve as critical hubs for coal processing and conversion. Their production systems involve multiple continuous and tightly coupled unit operations, including crushing, screening, dense-medium separation, flotation, thickening, and filtration7. The plant environment is typically characterized by dense equipment deployment, complex belt-conveyor networks, and persistently adverse conditions (e.g., high noise, heavy dust, and high humidity), giving rise to multi-source, dynamically evolving, and interactively amplified risks. Although coal preparation technologies in China have advanced substantially in recent years, safety governance under complex operating conditions still exhibits notable weaknesses8,9. An analysis based on publicly available MSHA fatality reporting has suggested that a substantial share of mining fatalities in 2024 occurred in surface facilities (including processing plants), underscoring the likelihood that hazards in surface processing systems are systematically underestimated10.

In long-term industrial practice, coal preparation plants in large coal enterprises commonly encounter three major challenges. (1) Large-scale and aging equipment fleets: a portion of washing and preparation equipment has been in service for extended periods, leading to increasing failure rates; the coexistence of legacy and upgraded units further elevates coupling and cascading-failure risks. (2) Frequent human–machine interaction: maintenance, inspection, and cleanup tasks often require workers to operate in close proximity to high-speed machinery, while unsafe behaviors (e.g., maintenance without proper power isolation or crossing protective barriers) are difficult to detect and intervene in a timely manner. (3) Predominantly post-event supervision: conventional “person-to-person” monitoring cannot provide comprehensive coverage across distributed, multi-point, and high-frequency scenarios; existing CCTV systems are largely used for post-incident tracing, lacking proactive warning and in-process interruption capabilities. Consequently, data-driven risk assessment and intelligent closed-loop warning-and-logging workflow tailored to coal preparation plants are of substantial practical significance for strengthening safety governance at both enterprise and industry levels11,12. Safety risk assessment constitutes a core component of modern safety management. Internationally, widely adopted approaches include Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)13 and Hazard and Operability Analysis (HAZOP)14. In the coal industry, semi-quantitative tools such as the LEC method have also been commonly used for risk identification and prioritization15. In recent years, driven by advances in system safety engineering and computational capability, probabilistic risk assessment methods based on Fault Tree Analysis (FTA), Event Tree Analysis (ETA), and Bayesian Networks (BN) have increasingly become key pathways for modeling risks in complex industrial systems16,17. Recent studies indicate that semantic mining techniques—such as large language models (LLMs) applied to large-scale accident investigation reports—combined with association-rule mining can enable more granular identification of latent causal factors and managerial deficiencies. In China, disaster-governance frameworks such as “3LA” further highlight the coupled effects of psychological factors and execution-level deviations on risk evolution. For coal preparation plant scenarios, the combined use of the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)18,19, and fuzzy comprehensive evaluation provides a practical methodological foundation for interpretable weighting and integrated decision-making under multi-factor and inherently fuzzy indicators, thereby supporting risk grading20,21. Recent progress in deep learning has enabled wider use of computer-vision methods in industrial safety monitoring. Object detection algorithms built upon convolutional neural networks (CNNs)-particularly the YOLO family-have been widely deployed in industry due to their end-to-end detection pipelines and strong real-time performance. In parallel, open-source ecosystems have continuously advanced the YOLO lineage, further expanding the applicability of real-time visual intelligence in challenging environments22–24. Nevertheless, existing studies have largely focused on tasks such as underground personnel localization, helmet-wearing detection, and conveyor-belt anomaly recognition, while systematic research on plant-wide human–machine distance–aware collaborative warning and real-time intervention in coal preparation plants remains limited. The concept of “closed-loop warning-and-logging workflow,” originally rooted in automated feedback mechanisms25–28 has been introduced into safety governance to emphasize a “perception–decision–execution–feedback” loop29,30. With the development of mine automation and monitoring systems, practical applications are evolving from isolated protections toward system-level interlocking; however, real-time recognition and strong intervention capabilities targeting dynamic human behaviors are still insufficiently addressed.

Motivated by the above context and industrial demands, this paper conducts a systematic investigation for safety governance in large-scale coal preparation plants from four dimensions-management standardization, risk assessment, algorithmic design, and system implementation. First, hazardous factors and compliant behaviors across key unit operations are analyzed, and a multi-dimensional risk grading model integrating FTA and AHP is established to formulate risk-tier criteria. Second, to address harsh conditions such as heavy dust, low illumination, and cluttered backgrounds, an improved YOLOv10-based detection approach is developed to enhance the recognition accuracy of personnel, critical equipment, and unsafe behaviors. Finally, a vision-assisted monitoring and warning system was implemented to integrate risk perception, real-time inference, warning, and traceable data logging, thereby providing an engineering reference for deployment of risk-based warning and the dual-prevention mechanism in coal preparation plants.

Identification of hazardous factors in the production environment of coal preparation plants

Coal preparation plants are characterized by energy-intensive equipment, persistently humid operating conditions, and frequent multi-level cross-operations, leading to widely distributed and often hidden hazards that may be amplified through multi-source coupling. Following the 4M1E framework (Man, Machine, Material, Method, and Environment), the major hazardous factors are identified as follows.

Mechanical exposure risks in crushing and screening

Crushers and screening units are typical sources of high noise and strong vibration in coal preparation plants, and their operating areas present pronounced mechanical exposure risks.

Accident severity analysis based on fault tree analysis (FTA)

To quantify the risks identified above, this study applies Fault Tree Analysis (FTA). FTA is a top–down deductive method that uses Boolean logic and logic-gate structures to represent causal relationships between a top event (i.e., an accident) and underlying basic events. By deriving minimal cut sets, FTA identifies the critical combinations of basic events that can trigger the top event, thereby pinpointing dominant accident pathways and vulnerable links in the safety control chain. FTA is a qualitative structured use in this paper, and no probability quantitative calculation is performed.

Construction of the fault tree model

A fault tree is constructed by defining the top event (T) as “injury caused by unintended start-up of a scraper conveyor.” Top event (T): personnel casualty. Intermediate events: A1: a worker is located in a hazardous zone (inside the equipment or in close proximity to moving parts); A2: unintended start-up of the equipment. Basic events: X1: lockout/tagout not implemented (management or procedural failure); X2: accidental energization due to an electrician’s operational error (human error); X3: contactor sticking or short circuit in the control circuit (equipment/electrical failure); X4: absence or negligence of on-site supervision/spotter; X5: lack of physical guarding or infrared interlocking devices (insufficient engineered safeguards). By connecting events through logic gates (AND/OR), the top event can be expressed as T = A1A2, indicating that casualty occurs when a worker is exposed to the hazardous zone while the equipment starts unexpectedly. Solving for minimal cut sets shows that {X1, X2} and {X3, A1} constitute the dominant minimal pathways leading to the top event. These results indicate that failures in management and work control (X1 and X2) and electrical/control hazards (X3) should be prioritized for prevention and mitigation. Meanwhile, engineered safeguards (X5) act as a critical barrier to reduce exposure and break the accident chain, serving as the last line of defense.

In the present study, FTA was used as a qualitative structuring method to organize accident causation pathways and to support factor prioritization for subsequent risk grading.

Basis and results of safety risk classification

Following the ISO 31000 risk management framework and the Chinese national standard GB/T 13861-2020, this study integrates the FTA findings with a risk matrix approach (R = L×S) to establish a risk grading model for equipment safety in coal preparation plants.

Criteria and parameter settings

Likelihood (L): defined on a five-level scale from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high), determined using historical failure/incident frequencies and expert scoring. Severity (S): defined on a five-level scale from 1 (minor) to 5 (catastrophic, potentially involving multiple fatalities), specified according to potential consequences. Risk score (R): calculated as R = L×S.

Risk grading results

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) is used to determine the weights of three major factors—equipment, environment, and personnel—with weights of 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2, respectively. A composite Total Risk Index (TRI) is then constructed accordingly. The resulting risk grading is summarized in Table 1.

Design of a YOLOv10-based real-time object detection and distance-alarm system

To satisfy the requirement for real-time monitoring and timely intervention associated with Level I (high-risk) equipment, a vision-based perception and warning module was constructed using YOLOv10. Compared with two-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN), YOLOv10 was selected because competitive detection accuracy can be maintained while substantially higher inference efficiency is achieved, which is particularly suitable for deployment on resource-constrained industrial PCs and edge-computing devices in on-site applications.

Principles of object detection

Object detection is defined as the simultaneous localization and classification of targets of interest in an input image. In the YOLO (You Only Look Once) family, a detection-as-regression paradigm is adopted, in which the input image is divided into an × grid. For each grid cell, bounding boxes and class probabilities are predicted for objects whose centers fall within that cell, such that real-time detection can be performed in a single forward pass.

The YOLOv10 architecture is generally decomposed into three components: a Backbone, a Neck, and a Head. Performance improvements have been enabled by design choices including Mosaic data augmentation, AutoAnchor and the Cross Stage Partial (CSP) structure, through which feature representation efficiency is enhanced under limited computation and small-object detection capability is improved.

Optimization of the YOLOv10 model

In coal preparation plants, harsh conditions-such as low illumination, heavy dust, and strong background clutter (e.g., dense pipelines and compact equipment layouts)-are frequently encountered, by which missed detections and false positives are likely to be induced when the vanilla YOLOv10s model is directly applied. To improve robustness and reliability under real operating conditions, targeted optimizations were performed from both the network architecture and the training objective.

Network architecture enhancement via attention

To strengthen discriminative feature learning, the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) was introduced near the end of the CSPDarknet53 backbone. CBAM is composed of channel attention and spatial attention.

With channel attention (focusing on “what”), spatial information is aggregated so that informative feature channels are emphasized and interference from coal dust and water mist is suppressed. With spatial attention (focusing on “where”), channel-wise information is aggregated so that salient spatial regions of the target are highlighted, thereby improving localization stability in cluttered scenes.

It was demonstrated experimentally that feature extraction and recognition for helmet-wearing personnel and critical equipment components were enhanced after CBAM was incorporated.

Loss function design: CIoU loss

For bounding-box regression, the conventional IoU (Intersection over Union) loss is known to suffer from insufficient gradients when no overlap exists between predicted and ground-truth boxes, by which regression optimization may be hindered. Therefore, the Complete IoU (CIoU) loss was adopted as the bounding-box regression objective. In addition to IoU overlap, center-point distance and aspect-ratio consistency are explicitly considered in CIoU, such that regression stability and convergence behavior can be improved. Feature fusion and post-processing

Neck optimization: Multi-scale feature fusion was implemented using the Path Aggregation Network (PANet). Through PANet-based aggregation, the perception capability for targets at different distances was enhanced, and the pronounced scale discrepancy between near-field and far-field objects within the camera’s field of view was mitigated, particularly in scenes where target sizes vary substantially with depth.

Soft-NMS: Soft Non-Maximum Suppression (Soft-NMS) was adopted to replace conventional NMS. In personnel-dense operating scenarios (e.g., multi-worker maintenance tasks), true positive bounding boxes with high overlap were prone to be incorrectly suppressed by standard NMS. With Soft-NMS, confidence scores were attenuated rather than hard-discarded, by which the missed-detection rate was effectively reduced.

Video acquisition and preprocessing

Video streams were read via the cv2.VideoCapture interface in OpenCV. To match the model input requirements, raw frames were resized to 640 × 640 and were normalized accordingly. Calibration sensitivity: Ranging accuracy depends on calibration quality. Uncertainty in the reference-object width (Wreal), imprecision in selecting the reference region (Wpixel), and changes in installation geometry (camera height/pitch or vibration) may introduce systematic bias. Re-calibration is recommended after camera repositioning, and lens-distortion correction may be considered to reduce edge-region bias under wide-angle optics.

Failure modes. Typical failure modes in industrial scenes include partial occlusion, dust/smoke, strong illumination variation (backlight/low-light), and motion blur, which may destabilize bounding boxes and cause distance fluctuations near the alarm threshold. These non-ideal conditions may increase false alarms or missed events and should be considered when interpreting on-site performance.

Alarm mechanism and closed-loop warning-and-logging workflow logic

In this manuscript, “closed-loop” was used to denote a technical feedback workflow (detection → ranging → alarm → logging), while organizational learning loops (e.g., procedure updates, training, staffing, governance changes informed by alarm statistics) were not demonstrated and were considered beyond the current scope. The emergency-stop interface was described as an optional extension subject to site authorization and interlock configuration. A multi-level alarm mechanism was configured to form a closed-loop response.

Level-1 alarm (distance < 2 m): A yellow warning box was displayed with flashing behavior on the interface, and a message indicating “maintain a safe distance” was prompted.

Software implementation workflow

The system was developed in Python 3.10. Major dependencies were used, including PyTorch (deep-learning inference), OpenCV (image processing), Pandas (data logging), and PyQt6 (GUI development). A multithreaded design was implemented: the main thread was assigned to GUI refreshing and user interaction, whereas worker threads were used for video streaming and computationally intensive model inference, such that a responsive interface was maintained without noticeable lag.

Field deployment outcomes and representative cases

Pilot operation was conducted in the belt-conveyor corridor and the filter-press workshop of the Wanglou coal preparation plant.

Approximately 15 valid alarms per day were triggered on average, by which multiple unsafe behaviors-such as approaching running equipment too closely or failing to wear safety helmets—were promptly interrupted. Alarm snapshots were automatically stored, and violation time, location, and behavioral details were clearly documented, providing actionable materials for safety training and addressing the previous management difficulty where violations were hard to substantiate. By analyzing Excel-based alarm logs, the shift handover period (15:00–16:00) was identified as a high-incidence window for violations. Accordingly, inspection schedules were adjusted, and supervision during this period was strengthened, after which the violation rate was reduced by 40% within one month.

Conclusions

An engineering-oriented workflow for risk assessment and on-site early warning in coal preparation plants was established by integrating FTA–AHP risk grading with a vision-based proximity-warning module. Major hazards were structured from accident cases and field investigation, and risk levels were derived to support graded control and threshold setting. A YOLOv10-based “detection–ranging–warning–logging” pipeline was implemented, under which an mAP (@0.5:0.95) ≥ 94.3%, a mean ranging error on the order of ~ 0.05 m, and an alarm response time of ~ 0.3 s were obtained in the reported deployment setting. Monocular ranging in this work is validated within 0.5 ~ 2.0 m under controlled conditions (mean absolute error ≈ 0.057 m) and relies on a fixed camera pose and an approximately planar floor; thus, its accuracy may degrade outside the validated envelope or under camera vibration, non-planar ground, severe occlusion, dust, or adverse lighting.

This study adopts FTA/AHP and a risk matrix as pragmatic structuring tools for risk communication and prioritization; however, safety performance in coal preparation plants is a complex socio-technical phenomenon in which outcomes can be emergent, path-dependent, and not fully decomposable into stable linear causal chains. Therefore, the proposed risk model should not be interpreted as a complete representation of underlying risk dynamics, but as an engineering-oriented framework to support site-level grading and attention allocation. In addition, the implemented loop in this work is limited to a technical workflow (detection → ranging → alarm → logging) and does not constitute a demonstrated multi-level organizational learning loop (e.g., procedure/training/staffing/governance updates based on alarm analytics). The dataset and validation are site-specific (single plant and camera layout), and generalization to other plants or domains likely requires re-calibration of the ranging module and re-training or fine-tuning of the detection model under domain shifts (layout, dust, illumination, and camera pose). Moreover, we did not perform like-for-like baseline comparisons or module-wise ablation studies under identical experimental settings, and audited field metrics such as nuisance-alarm rate per hour were not available due to the absence of continuous logging with independent human verification. Accordingly, the results (mAP, ranging error, and alarm latency) should be interpreted as feasibility evidence under the stated deployment conditions rather than claims of universal robustness or benchmark-level superiority.
