The impact of Kommerell diverticulum resection on patient outcomes: A 21-year comparative analysis of surgical techniques for vascular rings
Authors: Julian C Marsh, Ashok Muralidaran, Irving Shen, John P Iguidbashian, Yoshio Ootaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveKommerell diverticulum is an embryologic remnant of the dorsal fourth aortic arch. It often presents as part of vascular rings with a right dominant arch. Historically, these have been treated by dividing the ligamentum arteriosum. In recent years, some centers have adopted Kommerell diverticulum resection with subclavian translocation as a primary approach. However, no long-term studies have directly compared outcomes with simple ligamentum division. To address this, we conducted a 21-year dual-institution review comparing short- and long-term outcomes across surgical strategies.MethodsA retrospective review of 131 patients who underwent vascular ring repair at 2 institutions was performed. Of these, 93 underwent simple ligamentum division and 38 underwent Kommerell diverticulum resection with subclavian artery translocation. Anatomic subtypes, symptoms, perioperative outcomes, and follow-up data were compared.ResultsThere were no significant differences in intraoperative or postoperative complication rates between groups. Central Message

Our findings suggest that KD resection is a safe approach and may offer advantages in reducing long-term symptom recurrence without increasing perioperative morbidity.

Perspective

Currently, there is a lack of long-term comparative data evaluating KD resection versus simple ligamentum division for vascular rings. This study addresses that gap by providing a dual-institutional study over a 21-year period. Our findings suggest that KD resection is a safe approach and may offer advantages in reducing long-term symptom recurrence.

Kommerell diverticulum (KD), an embryologic remnant of the dorsal fourth aortic arch, was first described in 1936 by B. Kommerell. This remnant is often located at the origin of the aberrant left subclavian artery (LSA) in vascular rings with a right-dominant aortic arch. Historically, and still commonly today, vascular rings have been treated by simple division of the ligamentum arteriosum. In 2002, Backer and colleagues1 described resection of the KD and retroesophageal LSA translocation as their institution's preferred approach for children with a right aortic arch (RAA) and a significant KD.

This more aggressive approach has gained traction at other centers, both for treating persistent symptoms and preventing long-term complications such as aneurysmal degeneration or rupture. Despite this trend, surgical practice remains heterogeneous.2 At our institution, vascular rings have traditionally been repaired via ligamentum arteriosum transection and posterior pexy of the KD to the prevertebral fascia, favoring a less-invasive approach that alleviates compressive symptoms without complex vascular reconstruction.

Currently, no standardized guidelines exist for surgical management of asymptomatic KD, with most recommendations based on small observational studies. Although earlier reports cited rupture risks as high as 19%, one cohort found a rupture rate of only 1.1% among 88 patients managed nonoperatively, raising questions about the true natural history of KD and the necessity of routine prophylactic resection.3,4 These findings underscore the ongoing uncertainty surrounding optimal management, particularly in minimally symptomatic or asymptomatic patients.

Despite the increasing adoption of more aggressive surgical strategies (eg, resection of the diverticulum with subclavian artery transfer), there are no studies directly comparing short- and long-term outcomes of this approach versus simple ligamentum division. To address this gap in the literature, the present study undertakes a 21-year review from 2 institutions, incorporating data from a center that routinely performs Kommerell resection and subclavian translocation. This study aims to provide a clearer understanding of the risks and benefits of routine KD resection by comparing short- and long-term outcomes across surgical strategies to help inform best practices in surgical management.

Material and Methods

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained from Oregon Health & Science University (IRB #MOD26550, 02/06/2024) and Randall Children's Hospital at Legacy Emanuel (IRB #MOD2165, 12/12/2024). This study retrospectively collected data on all patients who underwent vascular ring repair at either institution. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the minimal-risk nature of the study and use of deidentified data.

Between 2003 and 2024, a total of 131 patients underwent a primary operation for a vascular ring. Of these, 38 patients underwent resection of a KD. In all cases, the ligamentum arteriosum was ligated and divided. Patients with pulmonary sling anatomy, those requiring cardiopulmonary bypass, or those needing additional thoracic or cardiac procedures (eg, tracheal reconstruction, aortic arch repair, or correction of associated congenital heart defects) were excluded from this cohort.

All patients received a preoperative echocardiogram to rule out significant associated congenital cardiac defects. Cross-sectional imaging of the aortic arch was performed in most patients using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. Patients were classified based on aortic arch anatomy into the RAA with an aberrant LSA, a double aortic arch (DAA), or a left aortic arch (LAA) with aberrant right subclavian artery (RSA).

At both institutions, operations were performed via a left thoracotomy. The descending aorta, LSA, esophagus, and recurrent laryngeal nerve were visualized. After identification, the ligamentum arteriosum was doubly ligated and transected in all cases.

In patients with a KD that was not resected, posterior aortopexy was performed by securing the tip of the diverticulum to the prevertebral fascia using a 5-0 polypropylene horizontal mattress suture. This maneuver created a minimum 2-cm separation between the divided ends of the ligamentum arteriosum, allowing ample space for esophageal and airway expansion.

For patients who underwent KD resection, the procedure began with division of the ligamentum arteriosum. The LSA was snared with tourniquets, typically at or near its bifurcation. Then a partial occlusion clamp was applied to the base of the diverticulum. The diverticulum was resected at its junction with the LSA. The residual stump was then oversewn in layers with running 5-0 polypropylene, and the clamp was released. The LSA was fashioned with a beveled opening. After a left carotid artery was controlled proximally and distally, an anterior arteriotomy was created. The LSA was transferred and anastomosed to the carotid artery using continuous polypropylene sutures. Standard de-airing maneuvers were performed before completion of the anastomosis.

For patients with a DAA, the patent ductus arteriosus was doubly ligated and divided. A clamp was placed at the base of the left arch at its junction with the right arch. Tourniquets were used to control the LSA at its junction with the left arch. A segment of the left arch was then transected, and its junction with the right arch was oversewn in 2 layers using polypropylene. The end adjacent to the LSA was similarly oversewn.

Among patients undergoing simple division, 61.3% had an RAA with an aberrant LSA, 34.4% had a DAA, and 4.3% had an LAA with an aberrant RSA. In contrast, 81.6% of patients who underwent KD resection had RAA/LSA anatomy, and 18.4% had a DAA. No patients in the KD resection group had an LAA/RSA configuration.

For patients who underwent simple ligamentum division only, mean age at operation was 4.8 ± 6.2 years (range, 2 days to 31.3 years). For patients who underwent KD resection, the mean age was 4.9 ± 6.6 years (range, 5 days to 23.8 years). The KD resection group included 18 female patients (47.4%) and 20 male patients (52.6%) compared with 47 female patients (50.5%) and 46 male patients (49.5%) in the simple division group. Carotid artery clamp time ranged from 13 to 29 minutes, with a mean of 20 ± 5 minutes. In both groups, the median length of hospital stay was 3 days. Short-term symptom outcomes were collected at their first postoperative visit within 1 year, and long-term symptom outcomes were collected at their last documented clinic visit 1 year or more after surgery. This excluded 5 patients from the KD resection cohort and 9 patients from the simple division cohort, leaving n = 33 and n = 84, respectively, for long-term follow-up data analysis. The mean duration of long-term follow-up in the KD group was 3.8 ± 2.3 years (range, 1.1-9.2 years).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD and were compared using independent 2-tailed t tests as they were approximately normally distributed. Categorical variables are reported as number (percentage) and compared using Fisher exact or chi-square tests, as appropriate. Freedom from reoperation was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method to account for variable follow-up durations, with patients grouped by surgical strategy (simple division vs KD resection).

Results

All 131 patients across both institutions received preoperative echocardiography to evaluate for associated congenital cardiac defects. A vascular ring was detected by fetal ultrasound in 53 patients. Cross-sectional imaging of the aortic arch was obtained using computed tomography in 79 patients, magnetic resonance imaging in 32 patients, and barium swallow study in 6 patients. The mean long-term follow-up for the simple division group was 9.6 ± 4.9 years (range, 1.1-19.2 years).

At the time of presentation, the most common preoperative symptom in both groups was dysphagia, occurring in 47.3% of patients undergoing simple ligamentum division and 50.0% of patients undergoing KD resection (P = .85). Noisy breathing was significantly more common in the KD resection group (15.8% vs 4.3%, P = .03). There were no significant differences between the simple ligamentum division group and KD resection group in the prevalence of cough (14.0% vs 13.2%, P = .57), recurrent respiratory infections (10.8% vs 10.5%, P = .62), choking (15.1% vs 13.2%, P = .51), cyanosis (2.6% vs 0%, P = .50), or dyspnea (22.6% vs 26.3%, P = .65) (Table 1).

Of the 131 patients who underwent surgical repair of a vascular ring, 93 underwent simple ligamentum division, and 38 underwent KD resection. There were no operative or late mortalities in either group. No complications were associated with the subclavian artery transfer, and all subclavian artery anastomoses remained patent on follow-up imaging. A palpable left radial pulse was present in all patients.

There were no statistically significant differences in intraoperative or postoperative complication rates between the 2 groups (Table 2). Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury occurred in 6 patients overall—5 in the simple division group (5.6%) and 1 in the KD resection group (2.6%) (P = .67). Chylothorax was observed in 11 patients—10 in the simple division group (10.6%) and 1 in the KD resection group (2.6%) (P = .18). Cardiac-specific postoperative complications, including arrhythmia, pericardial effusion, and transient hypertension, were observed in 2 patients (2.2%), both in the simple division group (P = .50). No patient in either group developed a stroke postoperatively. Pneumothorax occurred without a significant difference between groups (P = .56). Postoperative infections occurred in 10 patients (10.8%) in the simple division group and 4 patients (7.9%) in the KD resection group (P = .62). Readmission within 30 days was reported in 3 patients (3.2%) in the simple division group and 2 patients (5.3%) in the KD resection group (P = .63). The length of hospital stay did not differ significantly between groups (P = .49).

Four patients required reoperation. Two underwent delayed KD resection with LSA translocation, one 5 years and one 11 years after the initial procedure. A third patient underwent reoperation 2 years after the index surgery of simple ligamentum division to excise a dense fibrous band, more than 1 cm in diameter, that was causing mid-esophageal compression. The fourth patient required reintervention for recurrent narrowing at the site of the previous aortic arch repair. The difference in reoperation rates between groups was not statistically significant (P = .32). Freedom from reoperation was 95.7% at 2 years, 94.6% at 5 years, and 93.5% at 11 years in the simple division group. No patients in the KD resection group required reoperation during the follow-up period, yielding 100% freedom from reoperation at a mean follow-up of 3.3 years.

Postoperative symptom resolution was favorable across both groups (Table 3). Symptom analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between the simple division and KD resection groups in the incidence of stridor (P = .32), cough (P = .72), dysphagia (P = .55), raspy voice (P = .50), dyspnea (P = .56), or reflux (P = .71) at short-term follow-up. Likewise, at long-term follow-up, there were no significant differences between groups in the incidence of stridor (P = .28), cough (P = .67), dysphagia (P = .15), raspy voice (P = .68), dyspnea (P = .18), or reflux (P = .54). No late mortality was observed in either group (Table 4).

Discussion

The standard surgical management for a vascular ring involving an RAA, an aberrant LSA, and a diverticulum of Kommerell traditionally has been division of the ligamentum arteriosum via a left thoracotomy. It had been believed that this therapy would fully resolve the preoperative symptoms caused by the compressive ring. In 2002, Backer and colleagues1 reported that patients with an RAA and aberrant LSA can continue to have residual symptoms from a residual KD. In 2012, they published that a cohort of their patients demonstrated complete resolution of preoperative swallowing and respiratory symptoms from primary resection of the KD.5 At the time, their cohort experienced no perioperative or postoperative mortality, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, or chylous effusion. Thus, they began recommending resection of the associated KD and transfer of the retroesophageal LSA to the left carotid artery as their primary procedure.

Persistent or recurrent symptoms have been reported in the literature when the KD is left in place during vascular ring repair, likely due to continued tracheoesophageal compression. These findings have led some centers to advocate for routine resection of the diverticulum to ensure complete symptom resolution and mitigate long-term complications.6 This surgical approach also eliminates potential future complications of aneurysmal rupture or the development of an aortoesophageal fistula.2,7

The theoretical disadvantage of this surgical approach is the risk of injury to the thoracic duct, recurrent laryngeal nerve, or dissection from manipulation of the left subclavian and carotid artery due to the more complex nature of the procedure. Currently, there is no published study examining the short- and long-term postoperative outcomes of KD resection compared with simple ligamentum division. Obtaining these data through a multi-institutional review will better inform surgical decision-making by providing comparative evidence on the safety and efficacy of KD resection to help guide best practices in vascular ring management.

Given the absence of increased perioperative morbidity, the decision to perform KD resection with LSA transfer may offer meaningful long-term advantages. In our series, symptom recurrence was notably lower in the KD resection group (24.2%) compared with patients who underwent simple ligamentum division (33.3%), and no patients in the resection group required reoperation. In contrast, 2 patients initially treated with simple ligamentum division later returned with recurrent symptoms requiring secondary KD resection and LSA translocation. These findings, consistent with previously published data, suggest that a more definitive initial operation may reduce the likelihood of symptom persistence and eliminate the need for future surgical intervention.5,8

Although the overall reoperation rate did not differ significantly between groups, the timing and nature of reoperation are noteworthy. Two patients initially treated with simple ligamentum division required delayed KD resection with LSA translocation 4 and 11 years after their index operations, highlighting the potential for delayed symptom recurrence. These cases demonstrate the importance of definitive anatomic decompression during the initial operation and support the consideration of routine KD resection in select patients to minimize the risk of late reintervention.

Although our institution traditionally has favored simple ligamentum division for its low invasiveness and reliable symptom relief, we are increasingly leaning toward routine KD resection in patients with an RAA and retroesophageal LSA. Currently, this decision is made on a case-by-case basis during multidisciplinary surgical conferences, and further longitudinal data may help formalize a stratified approach.

Neither institution defined a strict morphologic threshold for when a KD should be resected, which remains an important area for future investigation. Some institutions have suggested resection for diverticula measuring greater than 1.5 times the LSA diameter or exceeding 1 cm in width, although consensus guidelines are lacking.9

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective review, there is risk of selection bias, particularly given that surgical approach was determined by surgeon and institutional preference rather than randomized assignment. We also acknowledge that the KD resection group had a shorter mean follow-up duration compared with the simple division group (3.8 vs 9.6 years). This discrepancy limits our ability to assess long-term outcomes, such as late symptom recurrence or need for reoperation, in the KD cohort. As a result, the true durability of KD resection over time remains uncertain. Additionally, the study population spanned a wide age range (2 days to 31.3 years in the simple division group; 5 days to 23.8 years in the KD resection group), reflecting the heterogeneous nature of vascular rings as they present across infancy, childhood, and young adulthood. Given the limited sample size and low event rates, particularly in the KD resection cohort, subgroup analysis by age was not feasible without compromising statistical power. Future studies with larger multicenter datasets may be better powered to explore age-based differences in surgical outcomes or recurrence risk. Another limitation is the potential for an era effect, because KD resections were more frequently performed in recent years. Improvements in imaging, perioperative care, and surgical technique over time may have contributed to the favorable outcomes observed in this group, independent of the surgical approach itself. Finally, these operations were performed at centers with specific experience in vascular ring and aortic arch surgery, and the technical aspects of KD resection and subclavian translocation may not be replicated easily at all institutions. For these reasons, the generalizability of our results may be limited.

Conclusions

Although both surgical approaches offer excellent outcomes, our data suggest a modest rate of symptom recurrence when KD is left unresected. These findings suggest resection of the KD with translocation of the LSA is a safe and effective strategy. This approach may become the preferred option in select patients. Future prospective studies should aim to define morphologic criteria for resection, evaluate long-term symptom relief across standardized follow-up intervals, and include broader, multicenter datasets encompassing a wider range of surgeons to account for inter-institutional variability.
