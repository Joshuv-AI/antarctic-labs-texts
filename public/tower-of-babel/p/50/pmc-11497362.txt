Medial talonavicular-calcaneal fracture-dislocation in an adolescent: A challenging traumatic association (case report)

Introduction

Fractures of the talus are rare in childhood, with an estimated prevalence of 0.008 % of all pediatric fractures, compared to 0.3 % in adult's ones [2]. These fractures typically result from an axial force applied to the talus against the anterior tibia with the foot in dorsiflexion. Subtalar dislocation accounts for only 1 % of all dislocations in trauma cases [3]. Therefore, the combination of a subtalar fracture-dislocation is extremely rare, especially in the pediatric population. The pediatric skeleton is less fragile than adult's one, with higher elastic resistance, allowing it to withstand greater forces before fractures occur [4,5].

Case presentation

A 14-year-old patient with no prior medical history had an accident and fell from an estimated height about 1.5 to 2 m, landing on his right ankle. The foot was in inversion, resulting in a closed and uncomplicated trauma to the right ankle without any associated injuries. The patient was brought to our emergency department and the clinical examination revealed: a complete functional impairment of the lower right member, deformity of the ankle with irreducible inversion of the foot associated with a varus of the hindfoot, and no skin lesions. There was pain upon palpation around the tibiotalar and talocalcaneal joints, no sensory or motor deficits, and palpable pedal and posterior tibial pulses.

The X-rays showed a medial subtalar dislocation associated with a fracture of the neck of the talus classified as Hawkins stage III (Fig. 1).

Initial X-rays revealing a medial subtalar dislocation associated with a Hawkins stage III fracture of the talar neck.

The patient was rushed to the operating room, where reduction under general anesthesia using Bootjack Technique at one-hour post-trauma, by putting an arm on the inside of the tibia and stabilizes the tibia between the arm and side and grasps onto the patient's calcaneus with thumb and PIP joint of one of hand. A fluoroscopy was done after reduction in operating room by performing normal views of the ankle and the Canale-Kelly view. After testing the stability following reduction, an immobilization with a plaster cast boot was done.

Radiological control validated the reduction of the dislocation as well as the fracture (Fig. 2).

Postoperative X-Ray control confirming the reduction of the dislocation and the fracture.

A joint CT scan (Fig. 3) was performed to better analyze the anterior and posterior subtalar surfaces and to propose an appropriate treatment plan. It showed: a non-displaced comminuted fracture of the right talus with fracture lines at:

-The neck of the talus, with the line extending subchondrally to the medial talar dome, with detachment of a millimetric bone fragment at the tibiotalar joint.

The neck of the talus, with the line extending subchondrally to the medial talar dome, with detachment of a millimetric bone fragment at the tibiotalar joint.

-The medial cortex of the talar trochlea, with detachment of a fragment, which remains at the distance from the posterior tibial tendon and the flexor digitorum longus.

The medial cortex of the talar trochlea, with detachment of a fragment, which remains at the distance from the posterior tibial tendon and the flexor digitorum longus.

CT-Scan of the patient showing a non-displaced comminuted fracture of the right talus with fracture lines at the neck of the talus, extending subchondrally to the internal talar dome.

In view of the very satisfactory anatomical reduction observed radiologically, as well as the preservation of the congruence of the subtalar joint, we decided to adopt an orthopedic treatment for our patient, with immobilization using a plaster cast boot without any secondary dislocation and non-weight-bearing during six weeks.

The follow-up at six weeks of immobilization, physiotherapy sessions were initiated with partial weight bearing to total weight bearing above 3 months. Sporting activities were resumed, by returning to his swimming club, three months after the injury, with bone consolidation. At the Last follow up, about four years later, the patient has no functional impairment without any functional symptoms and the X-ray shows no avascular necrosis of the talus (Fig. 4).

X-Ray at last follow-up.

Discussion

Fractures of the talus are rare, due to the high elastic resistance of this bone, which is mainly composed of cartilage during childhood [6]. Studies describe two common accident circumstances that are most frequently responsible for this type of fracture: a road accident or a fall from a height [4].

According to Baumgartner and Huguier [7], the mechanism of medial subtalar dislocations is explained by the tearing of the peroneo-calcaneal ligament of the lateral collateral ligament (LCL), then of the hedge ligament, leading to inward displacement of the foot and eventual rupture of the astragalo-scaphoid ligament.

However, Allieu [8] and his team conducted a study based on clinical, anatomical, experimental and biomechanical arguments and concluded that the mechanism of this type of dislocation involves trauma to the foot which is in a weakened position, specifically inversion and equinus, rather than at a right angle as suggested by Baumgartner and Huguier.

Clinically, the diagnosis is generally easy. Major deformity is present, and in our case, the foot is fixed in inversion. Swelling and oedema appear quickly and can sometimes mask the deformity. Diagnosis is confirmed by standard X-rays, especially the anteroposterior view showing the talus in place within the tibiofibular mortise while the foot is deviated inward. However, talus fractures can sometimes go unnoticed and may require more advanced imaging such as oblique X-rays, as reported by Ann Maria [9]. Follow-up X-rays are essential to ensure that the reduction is anatomically correct, a critical factor for achieving good functional outcomes. CT scan can confirm the diagnosis and assess the extent of associated osteochondral lesions [10].

Fractures of the talar neck are the most common, similar to those in adults as classified by Hawkins [11]. Peripheral and central fractures have been uniformly classified into:

2.Central fractures without displacement (neck, head).

3.Central fractures with displacement or dislocation of a central joint (primarily subtalar).

4.Central comminuted fractures or fracture-dislocations with displacement of at least two joints.

The treatment consists of an urgent reduction using the “boot-strap” maneuver, with the knee flexed to relax the triceps surae. Malgaigne [12] recommends exerting force on the head of the talus to push it towards the articular sphere. Irreducibility should raise suspicion of interposition and necessitates an open reduction. The prognosis is good if the reduction is performed within hours following the accident.

In isolated subtalar dislocations without fracture, if the reduction is stable, no osteosynthesis is justified [13]. Aharram [14] reports a case of isolated internal talonavicular-calcaneal dislocation without associated fractures, reduced in the operating room under general anesthesia, then immobilized for six weeks with non-weight bearing, followed by rehabilitation. The outcome was marked by good functional results six months post-treatment.

Non-surgical treatment is recommended only for non-displaced or minimally displaced talar fractures [6]. Surgery remains the treatment of choice for displaced talar fractures to achieve a stable and anatomical reduction. Orthopedic treatment is sufficient for non-displaced fractures, but this is not the case for displaced fractures, which require surgery to reduce the risk of talar necrosis due to disruption of the vascular supply from the talar neck.

Pereles reported a case of an open Hawkins type III fracture-dislocation of the talar neck in a 10-year-old girl, treated by osteosynthesis, which was later complicated by the development of avascular necrosis of the talus. This case illustrates the guarded prognosis of this type of injury in children [15]. Jensen [5] reported an excellent prognosis for non-displaced or minimally displaced talus fractures in the pediatric population.

Avascular necrosis of the talus has been reported in children even with little displacement [16] but occurs mainly after talus dislocation fractures [15]. In contrast, isolated dislocations have a low risk of necrosis because the vascular supply is preserved [17]. in Smith's series [18] of 29 children, 7 % of cases of talar necrosis and 17 % of signs of arthritis were reported on average two years after later a talus fracture.

Osteoarthritis, which is very common in certain series, affects the subtalar joint, the Chopard joint, and even the subtalar joint [17,19]. The presence of osteoarthritis can also be explained by cartilaginous contusion, subchondral ischemia, and injury to the hedge ligament [20]. Merier et al. observed a long-term need for ankle arthrodesis in 25 % of cases, with an average of 11 years post-injury [21].

The originality of our case lies in the type of closed subtalar fracture-dislocation, which is not described in the pediatric population in the literature. Additionally, our decision to adopt orthopedic treatment, after clinically and radiologically verifying that the reduction was stable and anatomical, is noteworthy.

Conclusion

The management of subtalar dislocation fractures in children has yet to be elucidated. However, given the rarity of this type of injury, and the considerable risk of complications, it is important to be able to identify it, in order to speed up management and, above all, avoid the risk of talus necrosis.
