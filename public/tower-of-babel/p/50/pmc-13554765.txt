Transthyretin Tetramer as a Prognostic Biomarker in Heart Failure
Authors: Xinxin Zhang, Yunlong Zhang, Yanli Zhang, Ning Wang, Meng You, Rui Gao, Di Shen, Jinrui Wei, Yunfeng Cao, Yu Liu, Ying Liu
Published: 2026

Heart failure (HF) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 Although N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) is the standard biomarker for HF diagnosis and prognosis, its accuracy may be enhanced by novel biomarkers. Transthyretin (TTR) is a homotetrameric protein, and reduced TTR tetramer levels commonly occur in malnutrition, inflammation, and TTR amyloidosis (ATTR).2 Low plasma TTR has been associated with HF risk in the general population, but whether circulating TTR tetramer levels are associated with outcomes in patients with HF is unknown (Figure 1A).3 Using a validated ultraperformance liquid chromatography assay that specifically quantifies stable TTR tetramer,2,4 we investigated the prognostic value of TTR tetramer in HF.

Methods

Study population

From December 2021 to August 2022, we enrolled 462 patients with acute decompensated HF classified as NYHA functional class II-IV and 1,716 healthy controls from the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. Controls were community-based volunteers with no cardiovascular disease and normal echocardiography. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (PJ-KS-KY-2021-229) and conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki.

TTR quantification

Serum TTR tetramer levels were measured using an ultraperformance liquid chromatography–based fluorescent labeling method as previously described.2,4 Quality control samples confirmed assay consistency.

Outcomes

The adverse endpoints included all-cause death, all-cause rehospitalization, cardiovascular death, and cardiovascular rehospitalization. Time-to-event was calculated from the date of TTR tetramer measurement to the date of first event occurrence or December 31, 2024, whichever came first.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test (2 groups) or analysis of variance (≥3 groups), with non-normally distributed data analyzed by the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables were compared by the chi square test. Patients were stratified by TTR tertiles. Propensity score matching (1:1 nearest neighbor, caliper 0.2 SD) using age and sex balanced covariates (standardized mean difference <0.1); conditional Cox regression accounted for paired structure. Multivariable Cox models adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, coronary artery disease, diabetes, left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction, early diastolic mitral inflow velocity/early diastolic mitral annular velocity, NT-proBNP, and creatinine. The proportional hazards assumption was verified by Log-Minus-Log plots. Discrimination was assessed using Harrell's C-statistics and time-dependent areas under the curve (at 12, 24, and 36 months). The incremental value of TTR over NT-proBNP was evaluated by comparing C-statistics and likelihood ratio tests. The optimal TTR cutoff was determined by maximizing the log-rank statistic. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team) and SPSS Version 26.0 (IBM Corp), and a 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

HF patients were older (66.5 ± 14.0 vs 51.8 ± 7.7 years) and had lower TTR than controls (3.35 ± 1.19 vs 4.59 ± 1.05 μmol/L; P < 0.001). After propensity score matching (age and sex), the difference persisted (3.68 ± 1.21 vs 4.81 ± 1.11 μmol/L; P < 0.001). HF causes included ischemic heart disease (24.0%), dilated cardiomyopathy (18.0%), valvular heart disease (17.7%), arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (15.4%), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (10.2%), hypertensive heart disease (8.6%), and others (6.1%).

TTR and HF severity

TTR decreased with worsening NYHA functional class (II: 4.13 ± 0.95; III: 3.19 ± 1.11; IV: 2.67 ± 1.13 μmol/L; P < 0.001) and was lower in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) than in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) (3.03 ± 1.16 vs 3.61 ± 1.16 μmol/L; P < 0.001). Patients with low TTR exhibited more ATTR-cardiomyopathy “warning signs” (eg, HF with EF ≥ 40% for those aged 65 years and above, no LV enlargement, and unexplained LV hypertrophy, carpal tunnel syndrome, spinal stenosis, etc.).5

Prognostic value

Over a median follow-up of 34.0 months (Q1-Q3: 32.0-36.0 months), patients in the lowest TTR tertile (<2.75 μmol/L) had higher all-cause mortality (log-rank P < 0.001) and rehospitalization (log-rank P < 0.001) (Figure 1B). After full adjustment, low TTR remained independently associated with all-cause mortality (HR: 2.19; 95% CI: 1.05-4.55; P = 0.036) and all-cause rehospitalization (HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.12-2.18; P = 0.008). Associations with cardiovascular-specific outcomes were not significant. The optimal cutoff value derived from maximizing the log-rank statistic was 2.28 μmol/L for all-cause mortality.

Incremental value

Time-dependent areas under the curve for NT-proBNP alone vs NT-proBNP + TTR at 12, 24, and 36 months were 77.9%/83.9%, 75.6%/78.6%, and 72.6%/74.5% for all-cause mortality, respectively. For all-cause mortality, Harrel's C-statistics improved from 0.741 (95% CI: 0.6724-0.8096) for NT-proBNP alone to 0.765 (95% CI: 0.6964-0.8336) after adding TTR tetramer. Likelihood ratio tests confirmed the incremental value (P < 0.001).

Discussion

This is the first study to demonstrate that serum TTR tetramer is independently associated with adverse outcomes in HF, extending prior work linking low total TTR to HF risk through a specific assay for the stable tetrameric form. Notably, TTR tetramer levels were lower in HFrEF than in HFpEF. Although ATTR-CM is classically associated with HFpEF, recent evidence indicates that it frequently presents with reduced ejection fraction and even LV dilatation;6 thus, lower TTR levels in HFrEF may reflect undiagnosed ATTR-CM or other contributing factors such as malnutrition and inflammation. The association between low TTR tetramer and adverse outcomes is likely multifactorial: although ATTR-CM remains an important consideration—supported by the higher prevalence of ATTR warning signs in patients with low TTR—other common conditions in HF are also well-established causes of low TTR and independently linked to poor prognosis.7 Thus, TTR tetramer may serve as a broader marker of disease severity and physiological reserve rather than a specific indicator of ATTR-CM and may function as an adjunctive biomarker to identify high-risk individuals warranting further evaluation with echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance, pyrophosphate scintigraphy, and/or biopsy.8 Although we lacked C-reactive protein data, the persistence of the association after adjusting for comorbidities supports an independent prognostic role.

Study limitations

Limitations include the single-center design, lack of inflammatory markers, and absence of systematic ATTR-CM screening, underscoring the need for external validation in future multicenter studies with comprehensive phenotyping. In addition, although we observed improvements in Harrel's C-statistics and likelihood ratio, decision curve analysis was not performed to evaluate net clinical benefit, which should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

Serum TTR tetramer is an independent prognostic biomarker in HF, providing incremental value over NT-proBNP. Its measurement may aid in risk stratification and identify patients warranting further evaluation for ATTR-CM.
