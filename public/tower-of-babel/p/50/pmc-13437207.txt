Response to “Standardizing Obesity Imaging: From Confirmation of Excess Adiposity to Integrated Body Composition Phenotyping”
Authors: Sang Eun Won, So Yeon Kim, Hyo Jung Park
Published: 2026

We appreciate the insightful letter by Nguyen and Yi [1] on our review article [2]. Their letter extends our discussion by emphasizing that the next step in obesity imaging is not merely the broader adoption of imaging in obesity assessment, but the standardization of imaging acquisition, analysis, and interpretation, together with the integration of body composition phenotyping into clinical trials and practice.

Our review primarily focused on the role of imaging in the context of clinical trials and regulatory frameworks. Nguyen and Yi [1] broadened this perspective by highlighting the expanding role of imaging in obesity diagnosis. Consistent with the 2025 Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology Commission statement [3], a paradigm shift in obesity diagnosis is underway, moving away from reliance on body weight or body mass index toward the direct demonstration of excess adiposity, which can be accurately assessed using imaging. At the same time, we emphasize that cost-effectiveness and accessibility must be carefully considered when applying this approach to the general population.

The concept of integrated body composition phenotyping (i.e., assessing not only fat burden but also muscle quantity and quality) aligns closely with the perspective advanced in our review. Indeed, growing evidence supports sarcopenic obesity and myosteatosis as independent prognostic biomarkers across a wide range of conditions, with demonstrated negative impacts on overall survival and disease-related complications [45]. Given concerns about weight-loss–associated muscle loss with anti-obesity medications [6], we propose that future clinical trials include not only fat mass reduction but also the preservation of muscle mass and quality as clinical endpoints, all of which can be reliably assessed using imaging.

We also agree on the importance of ethnic and racial variation in body composition and the consequent need for population-specific imaging thresholds [7]. We strongly support the goals proposed: standardized imaging protocols, multiethnic normative databases, and consensus standards for automated body composition tools. Regarding the feasibility of these efforts, a previous study of 11,845 Korean health screening participants established CT-based reference data and T-scores for lumbar skeletal muscle area using semiautomated segmentation methods [8]. This approach, originally developed for sarcopenia, could also serve as a model for establishing imaging-based diagnostic criteria for obesity in the Korean population [910]. With the advent of deep-learning–based automated segmentation tools [111213], large-scale analyses can now be performed with greater efficiency, rendering the construction of large databases increasingly feasible. To translate imaging-based body composition assessment from research settings into clinical and policy practice, further studies drawn from representative populations are essential to establish reliable, population-specific reference values and diagnostic thresholds. Achieving this goal will require rigorous validation of reproducibility across multicenter, multi-vendor environments, and interdisciplinary collaboration among radiologists, obesity medicine specialists, and regulatory authorities.

In conclusion, Nguyen and Yi [1] have proposed a valuable roadmap for advancing the role of imaging in obesity assessment, from adiposity quantification toward standardized, reproducible, and clinically integrated body composition phenotyping. We look forward to interdisciplinary collaboration to realize this vision and strengthen the contribution of imaging to both clinical care and the regulatory evaluation of anti-obesity therapies.
