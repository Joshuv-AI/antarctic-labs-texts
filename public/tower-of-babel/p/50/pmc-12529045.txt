Three‐Dimensional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of Median Nerve Volume for Assessing Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Severity
Authors: Kazuki Hayakawa, Taku Suzuki, Katsuhiko Hayakawa, Yusuke Kawano, Takuji Iwamoto, Nobuyuki Fujita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/Aims3D magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a tool for visualizing and quantifying the volume of the median nerve; however, the diagnostic value of the volume of the median nerve for assessing CTS severity is unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the utility of 3T MRI combined with three‐dimensional (3D) imaging to assess the cross‐sectional volume (CSV) of the median nerve for diagnosing carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) and determining its severity.MethodsWe used MRI to measure the CSV of the median nerve in 95 patients with CTS and 26 healthy controls. CTS severity was graded according to the Padua classification, and differences in CSV according to severity were analyzed.ResultsThe mean CSVs for the severity groups were as follows: control, 22.4 mm3; minimal, 28.8 mm3; moderate, 34.4 mm3; severe, 53.6 mm3; and extreme, 48.3 mm3. CSV increased with CTS severity, with significantly higher values in the severe and extreme groups compared with the moderate, minimal, and control groups.DiscussionThis study showed that the CSV of the median nerve increased with disease severity, both visually and in three dimensions. This helps to visually understand the severity of CTS, and the association between morphology and disease severity may contribute to a better understanding of the pathophysiology of CTS.Keywords: carpal tunnel syndrome, cross‐sectional volume, magnetic resonance imaging, median nerve, severity, three‐dimensional MRI

Introduction

The standard method for carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) diagnosis and severity evaluation is electrophysiological testing [1]. An alternative approach is the use of ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to measure the cross‐sectional area (CSA) of the median nerve at the carpal tunnel, serving as a qualitative evaluation method [2, 3]. Imaging‐based assessments are advantageous because they reveal critical pathological information that electrophysiological testing cannot, particularly in patients with severe conditions [2], and several studies have reported that the CSA is a reliable indicator of CTS severity [3, 4].

Advances in MRI technology have enabled the generation of three‐dimensional (3D) images of the median nerve and carpal tunnel [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Although some exploratory studies have measured the volume of the carpal tunnel [4, 10], they used 1.5T MRI scanners, which provide a low resolution that may compromise measurement accuracy. Furthermore, they did not measure the volume of the median nerve. Due to the limited number of studies using 3D measurements, the diagnostic value of the volume of the median nerve for assessing CTS severity is unclear.

A recent study using 3T MRI with a T2*‐enhanced 3D sequence and a novel workstation demonstrated high‐resolution, clear 3D imaging of the median nerve morphology [11, 12]. This advanced imaging technique enables the accurate measurement of the cross‐sectional volume (CSV) of the median nerve, capturing pathological changes, such as swelling and narrowing [11]. Thus, the objective of this study was to evaluate the utility of 3D MRI in assessing the CSV of the median nerve and its association with CTS severity.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively reviewed patients aged > 18 years who were diagnosed with CTS and underwent 3D MRI between April 2020 and March 2024 at a medical institution specializing in hand surgery. At our institution, 3D MRI is routinely performed for patients undergoing surgery for CTS as part of their preoperative evaluation. All patients provided informed consent for the study via an opt‐out approach. CTS was diagnosed based on clinical examination findings according to the American Academy of Orthopedic Surgeons standards, including Tinel sign at the carpal tunnel, a positive reverse Phalen test, paresthesia or numbness in the median nerve distribution, and pinch grip weakness [13]. Electrophysiological testing was performed to confirm the CTS diagnosis. Patients with negative nerve conduction test results, as defined by the Padua classification [14], were excluded from the CTS group. Patients with polyneuropathy or cervical radiculopathy were excluded. These conditions were identified based on clinical examination findings or medical history interviews. Cases involving diabetes, dialysis, a history of chemotherapy, or a history of radial fracture were also excluded. At our institution, the majority of CTS patients are female. To avoid statistically misleading results due to the small number of male patients, only female patients were included. All CTS diagnoses were confirmed by a hand specialist.

For the control group, healthy volunteers provided verbal informed consent to participate in the study. MRI scans and electrophysiological tests were performed on all of these healthy volunteers. We recorded the complete medical history of healthy volunteers. From this information, we excluded those with a history of diabetes, dialysis, or radial fractures and included those with no history of CTS. Those showing abnormal electrophysiological testing results were excluded from the study cohort. Our study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of our institution (approval number: HM2016‐001).

MRI Examination and Measurement of CSV

MRI was part of the standard care protocol for CTS patients at our institution. T2*‐weighted axial phase‐contrast MR images were obtained from the distal radioulnar joint to the distal carpal tunnel. All MRI examinations were performed using a 3D gradient echo sequence on a TRILLIUM OVAL 3T MRI scanner (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time, 32.6 ms; echo time, 15 ms; field of view, 100 mm; flip angle, 20°; slice thickness, 2.2 mm; number of slices, 80; voxel size, 0.12 × 0.19 × 2.2 mm3; parallel imaging factor, 1.5; and scan time, 20 min.

All images were acquired using a 10‐channel wrist coil (MR‐RWC‐300; Hitachi Ltd.). The patient was placed in the supine position, keeping the arm straight alongside the body, the forearm supinated, and the wrist in a neutral position (0° extension). For 40 of the 80 slices spanning from the pisiform to the hook of the hamate, a radiologist experienced in carpal tunnel anatomy and blinded to clinical information manually delineated the regions of interest (ROIs) for the median nerve, carpal bones (pisiform, scaphoid, and lunate), and the carpal tunnel. These images were used to create a 3D image of the median nerve from the inlet to the outlet of the carpal tunnel. The CSA of the median nerve was calculated at the proximal edge of the pisiform level. Because previous studies have shown almost perfect inter‐reader agreement for CSA values, only one radiologist was employed [15, 16].

We reconstructed 3D images using the SYNAPSE VINCENT workstation (Fujifilm Medical Co. Ltd., Toyoko, Japan), thereby enhancing the clarity of the median nerve and carpal bones. We measured the CSV of the median nerve at the same level as the CSA. The CSV reflects the volume of the median nerve in a single slice (2 mm). We also measured the total volume of the median nerve from the proximal level of the pisiform to the distal level of the hook of the hamate. The workstation automatically calculated the CSV based on the ROI delineation and 3D image reconstruction. The average reconstruction time was 17.2 min (range: 15–20 min).

CTS Disease Severity

CTS severity was graded based on the Padua classification [14] as negative (normal nerve conduction study), minimal (normal sensory nerve conduction velocity with abnormal comparative studies), mild (prolonged distal sensory latency [DSL] and normal distal motor latency [DML]), moderate (prolonged DSL and DML), severe (unobtainable median sensory nerve action potential and abnormal median DML), or extreme (unobtainable sensory nerve action potential and compound muscle action potential).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the comparison of the median nerve CSA and CSV between the control and CTS groups. We also analyzed differences in CSA and CSV according to severity classifications. The association between the total CSV and the CSV at the proximal level of the pisiform was evaluated. The secondary outcome was the evaluation of the association between CSA and CSV to determine the reliability of CSV measurement. The CSA was selected because previous studies demonstrated that its measurement was highly reliable [17].

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted by a physician who was blinded to all clinical information to minimize bias. Statistical analysis was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test to identify significant differences in the clinical characteristics between the CTS and control groups. Differences in CSV by severity classification were evaluated using the Steel–Dwass test. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to evaluate the association between CSA and CSV, and between the total CSV volume and the CSV at the proximal level of the pisiform.

A post hoc power analysis (α = 0.05) was performed to assess whether the sample size was sufficient to detect a significant difference. The mean and standard deviation of the CSV values were used to calculate the effect size. The power analysis results indicated a statistical power of 100% for the Kruskal–Wallis test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Participants

We excluded two patients in the CTS group who had only clinical symptoms of CTS but negative nerve conduction study results. We also excluded one participant in the control group who had a history of wrist trauma. There were no CTS patients with polyneuropathy or cervical radiculopathy in this study. Therefore, the final study cohort comprised 95 patients in the CTS group and 26 healthy volunteers in the control group. Table 1 presents the clinical characteristics of the study cohort. There were no patients classified as “mild” in our cohort. There were significant differences in BMI between the control and moderate groups (p = 0.04), as well as between the control and severe groups (p = 0.01).

CSA and CSV of the Median Nerve in Each Severity Group

The mean CSA and CSV for each severity group were as follows: control, 10.3 ± 2.4 mm2 and 22.4 ± 5.4 mm3; minimal, 13.3 ± 3.8 mm2 and 28.8 ± 8.0 mm3; moderate, 16.1 ± 5.2 mm2 and 34.4 ± 10.9 mm3; severe, 24.4 ± 6.4 mm2 and 53.6 ± 14.1 mm3; and extreme, 22.5 ± 10.1 mm2 and 48.3 ± 22.0 mm3 (Figure 1A,B). The CSA and CSV progressively increased with CTS severity, except when comparing the severe to the extreme group. The CSA and CSV values in the severe and extreme groups were significantly higher compared with those in the moderate (p < 0.01), minimal (p < 0.001), and control (p < 0.001) groups. Although there were no significant differences between the severe and extreme groups (CSA: p = 0.71; CSV: p = 0.57), there was a significant difference between the moderate and control groups (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the minimal and control groups (CSA: p = 0.12; CSV: p = 0.12). Similarly, the total volume of the median nerve significantly increased with increasing severity of CTS, except when comparing the severe to the extreme group (Figure 1C, p < 0.001). In addition, there was a strong correlation between the total volume of the median nerve and the CSV at the pisiform level (Spearman's correlation coefficient = 0.84, p < 0.001). Association Between the CSA and CSV

All severity groups showed a strong association between the CSA and CSV values of the median nerve (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.99 for all groups). All correlations were significant (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the CSV of the median nerve increased with disease severity, both visually and three‐dimensionally. In the most advanced cases, with a Padua classification of extreme (for which conduction velocity cannot be measured), the median nerve was not more swollen than in those cases with a Padua classification of severe. This might have been because of nerve atrophy. The CSV at the pisiform level can serve as a reliable indicator of the total median nerve volume. There was no significant difference between control and minimal groups, suggesting that the volume may not be particularly useful for detecting mild CTS.

The association between CTS severity and median nerve volume using MRI is unclear. Instead of analyzing the CSV, Öten et al. [18] investigated the relationship between carpal tunnel volume and disease severity. They found a decrease in carpal tunnel volume as CTS severity increased, with a mean volume of 5.5, 5.3, 4.9, and 4.4 cm3 in the normal, mild, moderate and severe groups, respectively [18]. A previous study using MRI to investigate the relationship between CSA and CTS by severity classification reported CSA values of 9.0, 14.1, 17.7, and 19.5 mm2 in the control, mild, moderate, and severe groups, respectively [4]. These measurements demonstrated that the CSA of the median nerve enlarged with increasing CTS severity, which is consistent with our study.

A strength of 3D MRI is its ability to easily visualize median nerve swelling through 3D images. Furthermore, 3D imaging provides clarity, showing that the larger the median nerve volume, the more severe the condition [11]. We believe that CSV measurement has limited clinical benefit because it strongly correlates with CSA. However, 3D information may offer a more intuitive visual understanding compared to conventional 2D data. On the other hand, compared to ultrasound, there are several disadvantages. The cost of MRI is high, and the need for a 3T MRI with a dedicated workstation limits its accessibility. The use of 3D MRI is also restricted in patients with claustrophobia or metallic implants. Ultrasound, on the other hand, is more cost‐effective, faster, widely available, and generally better tolerated. The majority of CTS cases can be quickly and easily diagnosed using a combination of clinical and electrodiagnostic testing, along with ultrasound. We consider that the clinical utility of 3D MRI remains limited, except for its advantage in providing a visually intuitive 3D understanding of pathological conditions.

There are several limitations to our study. First, this study only included female participants. Sex impacts median nerve size, which may introduce sex‐related bias. Second, clinical diagnosis was not purely systematic because this is a retrospective study. Third, the control group was younger than the CTS group. Since the volume of the median nerve may vary with age, this age difference could have influenced the results. Fourth, although significant differences in CSA and CSV were observed among the severity groups, there was considerable overlap in values between the control and severe/extreme groups. Therefore, this technique may have limitations in distinguishing normal from abnormal findings at the individual patient level. Fifth, there were significant differences in BMI among the groups, which might have potentially influenced the CSA and CSV measurements. Consequently, this research should be considered a pilot study for future investigations.

Despite these limitations, our findings highlight the utility of 3D MRI in visualizing and quantitatively evaluating median nerve volume. We demonstrated that the CSV of the median nerve increased with disease severity, providing visual insight into the pathophysiology of CTS. Although the clinical application remains limited, our results may contribute to a better visual understanding of CTS severity.
