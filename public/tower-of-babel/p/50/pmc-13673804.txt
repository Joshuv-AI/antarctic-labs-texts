Adaptation and psychometric properties of the Coping Flexibility Scale-Revised in Chinese parents of children with cancer
Authors: Ruijie Shan, Shujing Wang, Nadya Golfenshtein, Jian Ge, Hong Zhang, Yu Zhang, Yinghong Zhu, Naixue Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA psychometrically suitable instrument is a prerequisite to explore the role of coping flexibility in vulnerable populations, such as parents of children with cancer. The Coping Flexibility Scale-Revised (CFS-R) is a 12-item measure of coping flexibility. Although increasingly used in research, the instrument’s use is limited to just Japanese and English languages.MethodsThe Chinese Version of Coping Flexibility Scale-Revised (C-CFS-R) was obtained through translation and cross-cultural adaptation. A sample of 318 Chinese parents of children with cancer, recruited from four tertiary hospitals via convenience sampling, completed the C-CFS-R and validated measures of cognitive flexibility (for convergent validity) and social desirability (for discriminant validity). Item analyses, confirmatory factor analysis, Pearson correlation analysis, Cronbach’s alpha and intraclass correlation coefficients were used to determine item quality, construct validity, convergent and discriminant validity, internal consistency and test-retest reliability, respectively.ResultsItem analyses provided evidence of good item quality. Both the first- and second-order three-factor models showed acceptable fit indices. The C-CFS-R showed a small to medium correlation with cognitive flexibility, and its correlation with social desirability was not statistically significant, except abandonment with a small effect size. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency and good test-retest reliability.ConclusionsThis study provides preliminary evidence for the psychometric properties of a coping flexibility scale under severe adversity in the Chinese context. Practically, the C-CFS-R can be used to identify mothers of children with cancer in the initial diagnosis and treatment phase who demonstrate low coping flexibility, enabling early and targeted psychosocial support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05160-7.Keywords: Coping flexibility, Reliability, Validity, Cancer, Parents

Introduction

Cancer is one of the major causes of death in children [1]. Undoubtedly, childhood cancer and its associated burden, such as frequent hospital visits [2], financial constraints [3] and fear of death or recurrence [4] seriously threatens parents’ mental and physical health [5]. A meta-analysis showed that the pooled prevalence of anxiety, depression and posttraumatic stress disorder among parents of children with cancer were 21%, 28% and 26%, respectively, higher than parent controls of noncancer children [6]. Evidence also suggested that parents of children with cancer suffered from poor social functioning, sleep quality and immunological functioning [7, 8]. Given these profound physical and psychological tolls, identifying modifiable factors related to health outcomes has been one of the key focuses in childhood cancer research.

The famous Transactional Theory of Stress and Coping, developed by Lazarus and Folkman, proposes that coping is a key process that determines an individual’s health outcomes under stressful situations [9, 10]. Coping is defined as “constantly ongoing cognitive and behavioral efforts to manage specific external and/or internal demands that are appraised as taxing or exceeding individual’s resources” [9]. Previous research involving coping of parents of children with cancer has typically focused on either a single coping strategy, such as support-seeking [11], or on coping style, such as positive coping and negative coping [12]. However, the diverse and fluid nature of coping strategies has been overlooked [13]. The dualistic perspective on coping strategies as either beneficial or harmful was labeled as the “fallacy of uniform efficacy”, which means that no particular coping behavior is universally effective or ineffective in all situations [14]. Consequently, the paradigm is shifting, suggesting that the efficiency of coping is embedded within coping flexibility [15, 16]. Consistently, the coping flexibility hypothesis (CFH) presumes higher coping flexibility tends to produce better health outcomes.

There is currently no consensus on the definition of coping flexibility. One of the most widely used definitions was proposed by Kato and defined as the ability to relinquish a coping strategy regarded as ineffective and devise and implement an alternative strategy [15, 17]. Corresponding with the definition, Kato developed the 12-item Coping Flexibility Scale-Revised (CFS-R) based on the Dual-Process Theory of Coping Flexibility, in which coping consists of two continuous processes, namely evaluation and adaptive coping [17]. Evaluation coping includes monitoring and evaluating coping outcomes, as well as abandoning an ineffective coping strategy; adaptive coping refers to devising and implementing alternative coping strategies [15]. In a sample of Japanese adults, a confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the appropriateness of the three-factor construct for CFS-R, namely abandonment, re-coping and meta-coping [17]. CFS-R scores also showed adequate convergent and discriminant validity, reliability and incremental validity for predicting depressive symptoms in Japanese college students [17]. The scale has been used among Japanese and UK populations [18, 19].

With the second heaviest childhood cancer burden in the world [20], China presents a critical context for understanding parental coping flexibility with the challenges presented by situation. An approach focused on coping flexibility can be particularly important for designing tailored interventions to promote parental well-being. However, to our best knowledge, there is still a lack of a valid tool to measure coping flexibility in this population. Given that Kato’s CFS-R has demonstrated good validity and reliability and was developed within a similar Asian cultural context, it poses significant potential for use among Chinese parents. Therefore, the objective of this study was to translate and adapt the CFS-R into Chinese and examine its psychometric properties among parents of children with cancer.

Methods

Participants

Convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants from four tertiary hospitals in Shandong, China from March 2023 to June 2023. Inclusion criteria included being a father or mother of a child aged 14 years or younger who had been diagnosed with cancer but without comorbid critical diseases such as cardiovascular and cerebrovascular, lung, liver diseases and accompanying the child during hospitalization. The father or mother was excluded if he/she had a history of psychiatric or neurological illness, or could not read and write in Chinese. When both the father and mother in a family were eligible, one of them was selected by themselves to participate.

Confirmatory factor analysis requirs at least 10 observations per estimated parameter according to the N: q rule suggested by Jackson [21]. In view of this, the minimum sample size for this study was 270. Accounting for 15% invalid data, the sample size was estimated to be 318. We obtained informed consent from 329 parents and all of them returned their questionnaires. After removing 7 questionnaires with missing data in the Coping Flexibility Scale-Revised for Chinese (C-CFS-R) and 4 questionnaires with patterned answers, a total of 318 valid responses were obtained.

Procedure

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong University School of Nursing and Rehabilitation (Reference Number: 2022-R-141) in October 2022, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Measures for the Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. Permission to enter the pediatric cancer wards of the four hospitals was first obtained from the head nurses, and then the informed consent was obtained from the parents before distributing the questionnaires. Trained data collectors collected the completed questionnaires on site or within two days. A total of 26 participants were conveniently selected to re-complete the scale after 2–8 weeks to assess the test-retest reliability of the scale.

Measures

General information

The authors developed a general information questionnaire to obtain sociodemographic characteristics of the parents and children, as well as information about the children’s conditions. These included parental age, sex, ethnicity, religion, place of residence, and education, children’s age, sex, cancer type, duration after cancer diagnosis, and phase of cancer treatment.

Coping flexibility

The CFS-R was used to evaluate coping flexibility; the original version, which was offered in both Japanese and English languages, consisted of 12 items that sought to evaluate abandonment, re-coping and meta-coping, using a 4-point scale from 0 (not applicable) to 3 (very applicable). The higher the score was, the better the coping flexibility. After obtaining permission from Kato, translation and cross-cultural adaptation were carried out according to the principles of good practice [22], and the procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Procedure of the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the Coping Flexibility Scale-Revised for Chinese. Two forward translators and the proof reader were all native speakers of Chinese and fluent in English. One back translator was an assistant professor and the other was a professional translator

We purposively selected parents varying in educational background for the cognitive debriefing. The first three parents interviewed had difficulty understanding some terms and the rating options. The problems may be due to the fact that parents of children with cancer had lower cognitive capability compared to college students [17] among whom the CFS-R was developed/validated. We made some changes to increase understandability of the tool. First, “coping strategy” was replaced with “ways of dealing (处理方法 chu li fang fa)”, and explanations and examples of coping were added to the scale instruction section. In Chinese, “ways of dealing” and “coping strategy” were similar and the former was more used in daily language and easier to understand whereas the latter was more used in academic area and too abstruse for non-specialists. Second, adding the sentence “What do you usually do after implementing a coping strategy?” in the instructions to some items as a cue. Last, the rating options were changed to “0 (not like me) to 3 (very like me)”.

We further recruited five parents, who suggested that the revised one was easier to understand and preferred. After obtaining persion on the revision from Professor Kato, the revised scale was used for further data collection.

Cognitive flexibility

Research has found that cognitive flexibility has a positive correlation with coping flexibility [23]. Cognitive flexibility refers to awareness of alternatives in any given situation, willingness to adapt to the situation, and self-efficacy in being flexible [24]. All participants completed the Cognitive Flexibility Scale (developed by Martin & Rubin [24]), which was used for convergent validity analysis. The 12-item measurement was rated on a 6-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 6 = strongly agree) and higher scores reflected greater cognitive flexibility. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was 0.71, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Social desirability

In line with Kato’s studies [15, 17], we used social desirability for discriminant validity analysis. Social desirability is a personality trait that results in reporting bias for self-report scales, i.e., the tendency to respond in a way that matched with expected social norms [25]. The Lie scale of Eysenck Personality Questionnaire-Revised Short Scale for Chinese, revised by Qian et al. [26], was used to assess social desirability. The scale consisted of 12 true-or-false questions, each scored 0 or 1, and six of them were randomly selected and used for this study considering the need to avoid participants being annoyed by too many lie detection questions and reduce burden, with higher scores indicating higher social desirability. The Kuder–Richardson Formula for the six items was 0.63, which was acceptable for subscales [27].

Statistical analysis

The absolute values of skewness of continuous variables (range 0.01–0.53) were under one and the absolute values of kurtosis were below three (range 0.03–0.78), indicating that the distribution of the variables was approximate to normal [28], and parametric statistics can be used. Data were described using mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 24 and SPSS AMOS 28 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Analysis of individual items was a reasonable approach to discerning questions relating to total instrument performance because individual items were the foundation of the composite instrument [29]. We used item analysis to test the quality of each item of C-CFS-R, including the critical ratio (CR) method and item-total correlation method in this study. We categorized the parents with the top 27% score as the high score group and those with the bottom 27% score as the low score group [27], and carried out independent t tests to compare the difference in item scores between groups. Items with non-significant differences between groups were removed due to low discrimination. The correlation of each item and the total score was evaluated using the Pearson correlation analysis, and the item with a correlation coefficient < 0.30 was considered to be removed as the low correlation.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to examine the construct validity of C-CFS-R [30, 31]. Criteria for adequate model fit were χ2/df < 5 (3 as good), comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) > 0.90 (0.95 as good), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08 (0.06 as good), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) < 0.08 [32, 33]. Items that share substantial semantic overlap tend to exhibit additional covariation beyond what is explained by the common factor, as reflected in correlated residuals [34]. Therefore, we allowed residuals of items within the same dimension to correlate, with reference to the modification indices [34, 35].

Cognitive flexibility and social desirability were separately used for the evaluation of convergent and discriminant validity using Pearson correlation coefficients. A correlation coefficient of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 demarcated small, medium, and large effects, respectively [36]. Discriminant validity between dimensions was assessed using the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT), which below 0.85 indicate adequate discriminant validity [37].

For the total scale, the internal consistency reliability was described as acceptable and good depending on the Cronbach’s α greater than 0.70 and 0.80, respectively; for the subscales, the cutoff values for acceptable and good were 0.60 and 0.70, respectively [27]. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated to assess the test-retest reliability based on a single measurement, absolute-agreement, 2-way mixed-effects model [38]. Test-retest reliability was deemed poor for ICC below 0.40, moderate for 0.40 ≤ ICC < 0.60, good for 0.60 ≤ ICC < 0.75 and excellent for ICC ≥ 0.75 [39]. Research has shown that a minimum sample size of 20 can provide stable estimates of test-retest reliability [40]. We set the test-retest intervals at 2 to 8 weeks primarily out of consideration of study feasibility. We followed up with the participants when they returned to the hospital for cyclic chemotherapy or routine follow-ups, which usually occurred 2 to 8 weeks after they were discharged.

Results

Participant characteristics

The mean age of parents was 36.11 (SD 5.26; range 23–53) years and the mean age of children was 5.87 (SD 3.45) years. The majority of parents were female (n = 229, 72.0%), Han ethnicity (n = 309, 97.2%), and not religious (n = 270, 84.9%). About one third of the parents hold a degree of advanced education. About two fifths (n = 131, 41.2%) of the families resided in the countryside. About half of the children were male (n = 200, 62.9%). The majority of the children were diagnosed with cancer within the recent year (217/318, 68.2%) and in the initial diagnosis and the phase of treatment (253/318, 79.6%), with more children diagnosed with leukemia or lymphoma (175/318, 55.0%) than solid tumor (143/318, 45.0%). Construct validity

The fit indices of the initial CFA for C-CFS-R without correlation among items were not satisfying (χ2/df = 3.645, CFI = 0.896, TLI = 0.866, RMSEA = 0.091, SRMR = 0.063). The initial model was modified by allowing correlation among the items of the same construct with reference to the modification indices (Fig. 2), resulting acceptable model fit (χ2/df = 2.789, CFI = 0.934, TLI = 0.909, RMSEA = 0.075, SRMR = 0.063). The correlation coefficients between the three factors were high, ranging from 0.66 to 0.78 (all P<0.001; Fig. 2), suggesting a potential presence of a common high-order factor. Therefore, a second-order CFA was conducted, which showed acceptable model fit (χ2/df = 2.789, CFI = 0.934, TLI = 0.909, RMSEA = 0.075, SRMR = 0.063; Fig. 3). The second-order CFA showed that the standardized path coefficients of coping flexibility with re-coping, abandonment, and meta-coping were as high as 0.87, 0.76, and 0.89 respectively.

Internal consistency and test-retest reliability

The Cronbach’α coefficients were 0.87, 0.80, 0.72 and 0.74 for the total scale, abandonment subscale, re-coping subscale, and meta-coping subscale, respectively, indicating good internal consistency reliability. A total of 26 participants re-completed the C-CFS-R with a median lag of 19 days (Q1-Q4: 16–26 days; range 14–46 days). The test-retest reliability of the total score was good (ICC = 0.64, P < 0.001). For abandonment, the ICC was 0.75 (P < 0.001), which was considered excellent. The ICC of re-coping was 0.45 (P = 0.01), reflecting moderate test-retest reliability. However, the test-retest reliability of meta-coping was poor (ICC = 0.33, P = 0.049). Considering the variability in the retest intervals, we examined the correlation between the test-retest intervals and the absolute difference between the two measurements. The results showed that the correlation coefficients were not statistically significant, ranging from 0.06 to 0.19 (all P > 0.05). We further conducted test-retest reliability analyses excluding the two participants with the intervals over 4 weeks, which showed that the ICC values for the total score, abandonment, re-coping, and meta-coping were 0.63, 0.74, 0.43, and 0.35, respectively (all P < 0.05).

Discussion

This study successfully translated the Coping Flexibility Scale-Revised (CFS-R) into Chinese and evaluated its psychometric properties among the uniquely vulnerable population of parents of children with cancer. Our findings provide preliminary evidence that the C-CFS-R is a valid and reliable instrument, offering a much-needed tool for researchers and clinicians.

Consistent with the original scale, the C-CFS-R confirmed the appropriateness of the three-factor construct for CFS-R. This factor structure also aligns with the Dual-Process Theory of Coping Flexibility [17]. Abandonment refers to “individuals discontinue coping strategies evaluated as ineffective in the previous process” [17]. For parents of children with cancer, “Abandonment” may involve letting go of the attempts to preserve “normal” parenting practices and/or job functioning. Relinquishing an ineffective strategy prevents one from continuing to use it and from experiencing repeated failures that can lead to negative emotions and harm to one’s health [41, 42]. A meta-analysis reported that higher levels of abandonment were significantly associated with lower psychological stress [43]. “Re-coping is the process of devising and implementing alternative coping strategies that differ from those relinquished in the previous process” [17]. For parents of children with cancer, “Re-coping” may be a constant need to develop new strategies for managing treatment side-effects, financial strains, and emotional turmoil. Studies show that limited flexibility in this process may contribute to psychopathology [42]. Similarly, poor re-coping ability has been shown to negatively correlate with psychological distress [43]. Meta-coping refers to “one’s ability to monitor and provide feedback on the effects of each coping flexibility process” [17]. The dual-process theory highlights that the meta-coping process serves to monitor the whole cycle of a coping flexibility process, i.e., whether one should repeat the abandonment-re-coping cycle. Although crucial, the “Meta-coping” may be an exhausting process for parents who must assess whether their coping methods are truly helping them support their child and family or merely contributing to burnout. Notably, HTMT results indicated that the three factors are empirically distinct, while the three first-order factors showed strong correlations which was also in line with Kato’s study [17]. Taken together, these findings suggest the presence of more general structures in secondary-order hierarchies [44]. Moreover, this was confirmed by the second-order CFA, in which the standardized path coefficients of coping flexibility with three dimensions were approximately 0.8, suggesting that the three dimensions reflect facets of an overarching construct of coping flexibility and supporting the use of the total score [31, 45].

C-CFS-R scores were moderately associated with cognitive flexibility, supporting its convergent validity. Cognitive flexibility refers to “one’s ability to realize which options are available, willingness to be flexible within a given situation, and confidence in the ability to behave effectively” [17], and is regarded as the cognitive foundation for coping flexibility [46]. Previous studies have demonstrated that individuals with higher cognitive flexibility tend to evaluate stressful situations more adaptively, and adjust their coping strategies more effectively [47]. Nevertheless, the moderate correlation indicates that the two constructs are not redundant and the C-CFS-R measures a distinct aspect of functioning beyond general cognitive flexibility. Furthermore, the discriminant validity of the C-CFS-R is evidenced by its non-significant or weak associations with social desirability. A possible explanation for the weak negative correlation between abandonment and social desirability may be that parents with lower social desirability are more comfortable acknowledging personal shortcomings, and thus more willing to accept and abandon ineffective coping strategies when they fail.

Our results also indicated that the C-CFS-R demonstrated good internal consistency for both the full scale and its subscales, and acceptable test–retest reliability. However, the test-retest reliability of the re-coping and meta-coping subscales was particularly unsatisfactory, as reflected by their lower ICC values. Within a more restricted test-retest interval (2 to 4 weeks), the ICC values for these two subscales remained low. The relatively small sample size for test-retest reliability estimates may contribute to the low ICC values for re-coping and meta-coping subscales. On the other hand, not all subscales showed low test-retest reliability. Specifically, the test-retest reliability of the abandonment subscale was high and similar to that of the original scale [17]. Therefore, the wide retest range is not the sole reasons for the low ICC values. Furthermore, the overall test-retest reliability of the C-CFS-R was lower than that of the original scale study among college students, which used a longer time-lag (8 weeks) and reported test-retest reliability coefficients ≥ 0.75 for all subscales [17]. One possible explanation may be attributable to the differences in situational characteristics. Hertzog & Nesselroade proposed that psychological variables may contain both state-like and trait-like components [48]. Research has shown that low socioeconomic status is associated with higher coping flexibility [49]. It is possible that when individuals experience socioeconomic disadvantage, they often cannot rely on primary coping strategies to handle diverse stressors, which in turn forces them to cope more flexibly [49]. Parents of children with cancer experience more uncertain and challenging circumstances (e.g., treatment outcomes, alterations in child conditions, economic constraints) than college students. These overwhelming and often uncontrollable circumstances may similarly force parents to cope more flexibly in order to adapt effectively. Re-coping and meta-coping may be more unstable over time under fluctuating stress. This finding, therefore, provides a potentially insightful suggesting that in populations under fluctuating stress, coping flexibility may function less like a ‘trait’ and more like a dynamic, context-sensitive ‘state’, especially for re-coping and meta-coping subscales. Finally and most importantly, this is merely a possible explanation proposed based on our findings and relevant literature. It suggests that future research is warranted to focus on exploring potential dynamics of coping flexibility and how the dynamics relate to situational characteristics, which would help researchers better understand the nature of coping flexibility and provide guidance for subsequent intervention studies.

Limitations

The study has limitations. The majority of the participants were mothers, and their children were in the initial diagnosis and the phase of treatment, which limits our understanding on the psychometric properties of the scale among fathers and parents whose children were in other oncological treatment phases. Convenience sampling further limits the generalizability of the findings; however, given that probability sampling was not feasible in this population, our multicenter study design helped to enhance sample diversity and improve sample representativeness to a certain degree. It should be cautious to use this scale in fathers, and parents of children with cancer who are in the recurrence or regular review phase. Furthermore, we call for further investigating the psychometric properties of C-CFS-R in more representative samples. The examination of convergent and discriminant validity was preliminary. Convergent validity relied on a single measure. The use of a non-standard, six-item version of the social desirability scale, while pragmatic, yielded only marginally acceptable reliability, which may have attenuated weak but genuine associations to non-significance. Future work could extend our findings by examining associations with more criterion variables, such as accommodative coping, goal reengagement, goal disengagement and metacognition [17] and using the full 12-item Lie scale. The relatively small sample size for test-retest reliability estimates may contribute to the low ICC values for re-coping and meta-coping subscales. We further conducted test-retest reliability analyses excluding the two participants with intervals exceeding 4 weeks, and found that the results were largely consistent with those from the original analysis. However, for this specific population of parents of children with cancer, a retest interval of 2 to 4 weeks may still be relatively broad. We call for future research to assess the test-retest reliability of the C-CFS-R using a more fixed and shorter interval in larger samples. This study did not assess the measurement invariance, which is essential for a cross-cultural adaptation study. Future research is needed to further establish the measurement invariance of the C-CFS-R. Until measurement invariance is assessed, any direct comparisons between findings obtained using the C-CFS-R and those using the original version should be interpreted with caution.

Practical implications

The study has important theoretical and clinical practical implications. Theoretically, the study confirms the three-factor structure of the CFS-R within a new cultural and situational context, supporting the robustness of the Dual-Process Theory of Coping Flexibility [17]. Practically, following the validation of the C-CFS-R in pediatric oncology settings, healthcare providers can now use this brief, reliable tool to screen mothers of children with cancer in the initial diagnosis and treatment phase for potential difficulties in coping. Identifying parents with low coping flexibility profiles can trigger early, targeted psychosocial support, such as problem-solving therapies and resource navigation support to effectively redirect coping [16]. Ultimately, using the C-CFS-R can shift the paradigm from reactive to proactive mental health support for this highly stressed population. Considering the high factor correlations in CFA and the second-order CFA, we recommend using the total scale score in clinical screening and for predictive analyses to avoid multicollinearity of subscale scores. If a study focuses specifically on a particular dimension—for example, developing targeted interventions to improve the re-coping—then using the subscale score is appropriate. Furthermore, we recommend reporting both total and subscale scores to facilitate cross-study comparisons and a deeper understanding of the construct.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence for the psychometric properties of a coping flexibility scale under severe adversity in the Chinese context, which has good construct validity and internal consistency reliability, and its convergent validity, discriminant validity, and test-retest reliability have been preliminarily assessed. Practically, the C-CFS-R can be used to identify mothers of children with cancer in the initial diagnosis and treatment phase who demonstrate low coping flexibility, enabling early and targeted psychosocial support. In light of the limitations of this study, we recommend that future research further examine the psychometric properties of the C-CFS-R in more representative samples, including testing its measurement invariance and enriching the assessment of convergent and discriminant validity.
