Preoperative detection of perforating vessels in autologous abdominal flap-based breast reconstruction using a smartphone-based thermography device
Authors: Andrea SM Alberti, Davide Brucato, Corrado Parodi, Daniel Schmauss, Yves Harder, Riccardo Schweizer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominal-based microvascular perforator flaps remain the technique of choice in autologous breast reconstruction. Preoperative identification of the morphology, localization, and course of perforating vessels is of utmost importance. Various imaging modalities are currently used, including duplex sonography, computed tomography angiography, and magnetic resonance angiography. Thermography displays temperature variations on the skin surface and may help identify hotspots using a smartphone-based, handheld, low-cost device. This cross-sectional observational study evaluated such a thermography device and compared its accuracy with computed tomography and magnetic resonance angiography. Sixty patients undergoing abdominal tissue-based breast reconstruction between 2019 and 2022 were included. Preoperative skin perfusion was assessed using the device after cooling the abdominal skin with soaked towels at approximately 17 °C for five minutes, followed by spontaneous rewarming to room temperature. Thermographic images were taken at defined intervals. Hotspots were defined as the hottest 1 cm diameter areas on the skin surface. In patients with subcutaneous tissue thickness below 30 mm, hotspots at 3 min after cooling correlated with radiologically identified perforators in 42 percent of cases. In patients with thickness above 30 mm, the highest correlation (43 percent) was observed at 30 min. Across all timepoints, correlation ranged from 0 to 75 percent. Smartphone-based thermography provides a quick, non-invasive method to identify hotspots on the skin surface. However, correlation with computed tomography or magnetic resonance angiography was below 50 percent and highly dependent on subcutaneous thickness. As it does not show vessel caliber or intramuscular course, thermography cannot replace standard imaging but may serve as an adjunct.Keywords: Breast cancer, Computed tomography angiography, Deep inferior epigastric artery perforator flap, Magnetic resonance angiography, Non-invasive imaging, Perforator flaps, Thermography

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women, with more than two million new cases per year worldwide.1,2 Breast reconstruction after mastectomy offers women the opportunity to restore anatomical integrity after oncological surgery, thus improving quality of life and self-confidence. Autologous breast reconstruction, if indicated, remains the technique of choice with high patient satisfaction and finds its fore runner in the “deep inferior epigastric artery perforator” (DIEP) flap, currently considered the gold standard in autologous breast reconstruction.1,3,4

Blood supply to the flap is granted connecting the flap’s pedicle to the internal mammary vessels with the selected perforator(s) serving as the sole source of blood for the flap. Choosing the best perforating vessel(s) is a crucial step to shorten operative time, to lower complication rates and to ensure a better overall outcome.1,5

There are several methods for preoperative identification of perforating vessels: Doppler ultrasound (DU), color Doppler ultrasound (cDU), computed tomography angiography (CTA), and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA).2,3,6,7 Each of these methods has its own pros and cons that make them more or less appealing for this purpose. Hand-held Doppler may lead to the identification of false positives or miss perforators (perforating vessels). All other mentioned techniques require usually a specific competence and/or a radiology department and eventually specialist for their evaluation and are therefore less accessible, more time consuming and more expensive. MRA and CTA expose the patient to the risks of intravenous contrast medium and/or ionizing radiation.8

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) offers the advantage of avoiding ionizing radiation and iodinated contrast, and provides good soft-tissue contrast. However, its use is limited by longer acquisition times, susceptibility to motion artifacts, and reduced spatial resolution compared to CTA, which may impair the accurate identification of smaller perforators.5

CTA, in turn, remains the current gold standard for perforator evaluation and mapping in most institutions, by which location, anatomy, caliber and the three-dimensional course of the perforators can be revealed. CTA is non-invasive and has a high spatial resolution with visualization of the intramuscular and subcutaneous course of the vessels. However, despite its value in preoperative planning, its use in the intraoperative or postoperative setting is limited by logistical constraints, the need for radiologic facilities, radiation exposure, and lack of real-time perfusion data.1,6

In order to be considered ideal in clinical conditions, a method should be non-invasive, radiation-free, easy to use, practical, investigator-independent and useful for repetitive intraoperative assessment at an accessible cost.1,9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Thermography, or thermal imaging, detects infrared radiation from an object to produce an image based on the local surface temperature. Clinically it reveals areas of relatively increased skin temperature corresponding to local blood supply. Due to its safety, ease of use, and the absence of direct contact or radiation exposure, thermography has also been employed across a wide range of medical and surgical disciplines, including wound care, oncology, and vascular diagnostics.14,15 More recently, smartphone-based thermography has emerged as a low-cost and portable tool for intraoperative flap monitoring and burn wound evaluation,16 expanding its accessibility beyond traditional settings. Itoh et al. first described this process more than 30 years ago, demonstrating what we now refer to as the “hotspots” corresponding to perforators.17 As technology has advanced, thermal imaging’s potential has increased: New-generation cameras are smaller, cheaper, discriminate smaller temperature differences (i.e. more sensitive), and have improved software and compatibility with modern smartphones and tablets, resulting more accurate and user-friendly.1,9,10,17

The aim of this study was to evaluate thermography for the localization of skin perforators in abdominal-based autologous BR and to compare it with already established methods such as CTA and MRA.

Materials and methods

Patients

Patients equal or older than 18 years of age that underwent mastectomy and autologous breast reconstruction with a microsurgical DIEP or muscle sparing transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap (msTRAM) were included in this study. All patients underwent preoperative evaluation with thermographic mapping of the abdomen and CTA or MRA mapping of the perforator vessels (with 2 or more perforators identified by one of these two techniques) between January 2019 and December 2022, and their charts were reviewed. All available charts were analyzed for following criteria: age at admission, body mass index (BMI), number of pregnancies and history of prior bariatric surgery.

The patient cohort was divided into two distinct groups. The first group consisted of patients who had undergone preoperative assessment with CTA and thermographic mapping. The second group comprised patients who had undergone MRA and thermographic mapping.

Patients were excluded if they had prior non-minimally invasive abdominal surgery (laparotomic/mini-laparotomic surgery) or extensive abdominal scarring secondary to prior trauma and if a total of 2 or less perforators were identified in preoperative CTA/MRA. To reduce selection bias, all eligible patients consecutively meeting these criteria during the study period were included. The sample size was based on available cases during the study period.

Thermography

Thermography was performed using a commercially available smartphone-compatible device (FLIR One, FLIR Systems, Inc., Wilsonville, Oregon, USA) to assess skin perfusion of the donor site. The regions of interest, specifically the hottest points at the donor site along with the adjacent surrounding hot area (1 cm in diameter), were defined as hotspots. A threshold of 1 cm was applied to define correlation between thermographic hotspots and CTA/MRA perforators, in order to minimize false-positive matches and ensure clinically relevant precision for surgical planning.

Prior to surgery, abdominal skin cooling was performed by placing towels soaked with tap water at a +- temperature of 17 °C on the abdominal skin for 5 min. This was followed by spontaneous rewarming to room temperature (∼22 °C).

Thermographic images were acquired at a standardized distance of 50 cm at regular time intervals: baseline (prior to cooling), immediately post-cooling (0 min), and during the rewarming phase at 3 min respectively 30 min (Figure 1). The thermographic findings were subsequently compared with preoperative CTA and/or MRA to evaluate perfusion patterns.

An exemplary thermographic acquisition over time. (A) A thermography at baseline (prior to cooling of the abdominal skin), (B) at 0 min. after cooling, (C) at 3 min. and (D) 30 min. of spontaneous rewarming. The arrowheads indicate the hotspots sorted by increasing temperature.

It was observed that, after the first 30 min of spontaneous rewarming, the skin showed more or less the same temperature patterns measured before cooling to again reach baseline conditions. Therefore, a 30-minute has been established as the maximum time frame for collecting data originating from thermographic mappings during the phase of spontaneous rewarming. To specify the locations of the hotspots, coordinates in centimeters were determined, based on a Cartesian axis outlined on the abdominal skin during the thermographic assessment, with the origin established at the level of the umbilicus (Figure 2). The umbilical region (corresponding to coordinates (0|0) was systematically excluded from hotspot detection. This step was necessary because the thermographic camera consistently identified the umbilical introflexion as a hotspot to varying degrees, depending on the patient's characteristics, in the totality of cases. Thermographic acquisitions were recorded and stored in JPG format. Thermography and drawings of the cartesian axis were always performed by the same investigator (A. A.).

The systematic misalignment between thermographic image and the underlying photograph due to the distance between the photo camera and the thermal camera was corrected manually at the beginning of each acquisition.

CTA and MRA imaging

Mapping of the DIE-artery and its perforators was performed by means of CTA and MRA by the Radiology Department of our institution, following a standardized acquisition protocol.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed with the patient in supine position, covering the region from 5 cm cranial to the umbilicus down to the pubic symphysis. No bolus tracking was used. Arterial phase images were acquired with a fixed delay of 30 s after contrast injection, followed by venous phase images acquired 20 s later. Contrast administration included 10 ml of saline, followed by iodinated contrast medium adjusted to body weight, and a final 40 ml saline flush, injected at 3.5 ml/s. Axial images were reconstructed with a slice thickness/increment of 2 mm/1 mm. Multiplanar reformats (MPR) and maximum intensity projections (MIP) were generated in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes (3 mm thickness with 2 mm increment), along with MIP triplanar series (10 mm slabs with 5 mm overlap) and thin-slice MPRs (0.6 mm) for 3D evaluation.

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) examinations were conducted on a 3 Tesla system with the patient in supine position. To reduce vasoconstriction, pre-warmed towels were applied over the abdomen before and during the scan. The imaging field extended from the pubic symphysis to the upper abdomen. A gadolinium-based contrast agent (Dotarem, 0.5 mmol/ml) was injected at 0.2 ml/kg body weight and 5 ml/s using a care bolus technique for timing. Sequences included axial, sagittal, and coronal T2-haste (5 mm slice thickness) and T1-weighted VIBE or Dixon sequences with 1.0–1.5 mm slice thickness, acquired before and after contrast administration. All sequences were performed during breath-hold when feasible.

Preoperative radiological examinations were conducted using the medical image analysis software Philips Vue Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) (Koninklijke Philips N.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands), and included the measurement of subcutaneous panniculus thickness at the level of the umbilicus in the axial plane, expressed in millimeters.

The location where the perforating vessels penetrate the superficial fascia of the rectus abdominis muscle was recorded in centimeters using the Cartesian coordinate system, with the umbilicus serving as the origin of the axes. These coordinates were then compared with thermographic hotspots identified via thermal imaging. A thermographic hotspot correlated with a perforator when the perforator's coordinates fell within a circular region of 1 cm in diameter, centered on the location of the hotspot.

Statistics

Discrete values were expressed as counts with percentages, while continuous variables were presented as median with first and third quartile as appropriate since non-gaussian distribution was observed. No missing data were recorded. Non-parametric tests were used for subgroup comparisons: Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to compare correlation percentages between CTA and MRA, and between patients with subcutaneous thickness ≤30 mm and >30 mm. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). No additional subgroup or sensitivity analyses were performed.

Results

Demographics and flap data

A total of 61 DIEP and 2 msTRAM flap procedures were performed in 60 women who underwent mastectomy due to breast cancer. The patients had a mean age of 56.9 ± 9.7 years and a mean Body Mass Index (BMI) of 24.6 ± 3.5 kg/m². Of these patients, 57 underwent unilateral breast reconstruction, while 3 underwent bilateral reconstruction. In each flap a single arterial anastomosis was performed at the internal mammary artery. A single venous anastomosis was performed in 58 cases, while in 3 cases, two veins were anastomosed for each flap using a coupler device.

The patients' reconstructive procedures can be categorized as follows: seven were immediate breast reconstructions, 43 were secondary reconstructions (comprising 41 cases following expander reconstruction and 2 following radical mastectomy), and ten were tertiary reconstructions (following failed implant-based reconstructions). Correlation of thermography with CTA and MRA

A total of 43 preoperative thermographic examinations were performed, corresponding to the 43 included patients. At baseline, the number of thermographic hotspots that correlated with perforators identified by CTA and MRA were 57/160 and 60/96, respectively. At the 0-minute interval (post-cooling), hotspots decreased to 48 for CTA and 54 for MRA. During the rewarming phase, hotspots increased to 96 for CTA and 98 for MRA at 3 min and reached 94 for CTA and 100 for MRA at 30 min.

Correlation analysis (CTA)

At baseline, the median correlation was 37.5 %, ranging from 0 to 100 %. This decreased to 35.4 % immediately after cooling (0 min), peaked at 46.4 % at 3 min, and again decreased to 41.7 % by the 30-minute mark. Comparison of CTA and MRA correlations

Correlations of thermographic mapping with CTA and MRA showed distinct patterns influenced by subcutaneous tissue thickness and rewarming intervals. At baseline (BL), the median correlation was higher for MRA (41.7 %) compared to CTA (37.5 %). During the rewarming phase, the highest correlation for CTA was observed at 3 min (46.4 %), whereas for MRA, it peaked at the same time point with a higher value (47.9 %). Stratification by subcutaneous thickness revealed differences in performance. For patients with ≤30 mm tissue thickness, MRA demonstrated a superior correlation at 30 min (66.7 %) compared to CTA (33.3 %). In contrast, for patients with >30 mm thickness, CTA and MRA correlations were comparable, with CTA showing slightly better values at 30 min (50 %) compared to MRA (11.1 %). Overall, MRA performed rather better than CTA, in terms of correlation in thermographic mapping, in patients with thinner subcutaneous tissue, particularly during later intervals, while CTA showed a more consistent performance across different thicknesses. While there are some overlaps in trends (e.g., both modalities peaking at 3 min), the trends are not consistent when stratifying by subcutaneous thickness or time intervals.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the smartphone-based thermography device represents an easy, fast and non-invasive method to identify hotspots on the abdominal skin. However, the correlation between the hotspots identified by thermography and the perforators, as identified in the CTA and the MRA demonstrated a significant variability and lack of consistency. This suggests that the smartphone based and thus hand-held thermography device is not yet a valid “stand-alone” tool for preoperative detection of perforator vessels in autologous breast reconstruction with abdominal based flaps such as DIEP or msTRAM.

Thermography has gained popularity in the medical field in recent years due to its availability, improved accuracy, and eventually affordability.1,9,11 Several studies have shown its potential for identifying perforating vessels in BR since Itoh et al. first described its use in 1993.17

Thermography cannot visualize the three-dimensional course of perforating vessels in muscle and subcutaneous tissue; it only identifies superficial infrared radiation indirectly emitted by blood perfusion in the skin or differences in temperature, producing a two-dimensional image.12,18 Moreover, it neither can determine where the perforating vessel pierces the superficial sheet of the rectus abdominis muscle fascia. Nor can thermography determine anything about the course of the perforating vessels within the subcutaneous tissue. This limitation is consistent with previous studies, emphasizing that IRT provides only a superficial, two-dimensional map of the vascular anatomy, lacking detailed morphological and physiological insights compared to imaging methods such as CTA and MRA.12,18, 19, 20

To improve perforator localization, several studies have introduced a ``cold challenge.'' After cooling the abdominal skin, thermographic images are acquired at pre-determined intervals. The first hotspots appearing after cooling represent the location of the perforating vessels.9,12 In our study, soaked towels at approximately 17 °C for five minutes were applied, chosen for reproducibility and practical feasibility in the clinical setting. This approach produced clear relative temperature differences during rewarming but, as also visible in Figure 1B, did not always result in perfectly uniform cooling. Previous reports have indicated that fan cooling may achieve a more homogeneous surface effect, whereas our experience suggests it yields a less pronounced thermal contrast. Importantly, as emphasized in the literature, no universally accepted cooling protocol currently exists, and further comparative studies are required to determine the optimal approach.1,10,12,21,22

The imaging reference standards themselves also presents some limitations. CTA, performed with a slice thickness of 1 mm in our protocol, remains the gold standard for preoperative perforator mapping owing to its high spatial resolution. MRA, acquired with 3 mm slices at 3 Tesla, represents a valuable alternative with improved technology, but still offers lower spatial resolution and is more susceptible to motion artifacts. These technical differences may partly account for the discrepancy in mean perforator counts observed in our cohort. Given the relatively small sample size, this comparison should be interpreted with caution and not taken as evidence of true anatomical variation. In addition, duplex ultrasound provides dynamic flow assessment without radiation exposure, and its potential combination with thermography could represent a complementary approach deserving further investigation.

De Weerd et al. had noted inconsistencies between the Doppler signal location of a perforating vessel and the hotspot identified by thermography.9 However, no comparison with the precise location identified by CTA or MRA had been performed so far.21 This study addressed this gap of knowledge and revealed a high degree of correlation variability. Additionally, the larger sample size, compared to previous studies often limited to 25 subjects, strengthens the herein presented findings.23 Despite this, more favorable results in other studies may be attributed to stricter patient selection, controlled environmental conditions, and higher-resolution imaging devices minimizing artifacts.8,21,24,25

While our study attempted to overcome some of the limitations of prior literature, it still presents relevant constraints. Most importantly, the lack of intraoperative verification of perforator location remains a major limitation, as direct surgical assessment would have provided the most accurate gold standard. Although we employed both CTA and MRA—using a 3 Tesla protocol for the latter—to ensure optimal imaging resolution, these remain surrogate, non-invasive imaging techniques and may not fully replicate the intraoperative reality. Future prospective investigations including direct intraoperative validation are warranted to confirm the clinical reliability of thermography.

Another important methodological consideration concerns the definition of correlation between hotspots and perforators. In our study we applied a strict 1 cm threshold to minimize false positives and ensure clinically meaningful precision. While this may underestimate the true correspondence, as larger distances have been reported in the literature, those findings were based on comparison with color Doppler ultrasound rather than intraoperative verification, and referred to ALT rather than DIEP flaps without stratification for subcutaneous thickness.26 Moreover, “correlation areas” extending up to several centimeters, rather than spots, as described in that context, would markedly reduce the clinical value of thermography in preoperative planning. Since our aim was to determine whether smartphone-based thermography could provide a sufficiently precise, stand-alone guide in comparison with CTA/MRA, a stricter threshold was necessary to maintain surgical relevance, even at the cost of possible underestimation.

In addition to this methodological limitation, thermography itself is inherently constrained by anatomical and physiological variability. The perforating vessels exhibit varying degrees of arborization and a tortuous course extending several centimeters in all directions as they become more superficial. Thermography identifies a hotspot on the skin corresponding to a point along this course (Figure 5). Hotspots also tend to vary in size and migrate during progressive heating. This suggests that thermal imaging currently lacks the precision needed to identify the exact point of fascia perforation.

We also analyzed the correlation between subcutaneous tissue thickness and the ability of thermography to identify perforators. The highest correlation was observed in subjects with a subcutaneous thickness around the umbilicus of less than 30 mm, particularly during later intervals. Conversely, in subjects with a thicker panniculus adiposus (>30 mm), correlation was higher at 30 min for CTA, whereas MRA showed lower performance in these cases. These findings align with previous reports suggesting that thermography becomes less effective in detecting perforators deeper than 2 cm, highlighting the critical role of subcutaneous thickness for the interpretation of results.12 Nevertheless, correlation variability remains significant even after stratification, likely due to the vessel's three-dimensional course and branching variability.

In conclusion, while studies suggest potential utility in identifying perforating vessels in BR, the herein shown data indicate significant limitations of the smartphone-based and therefore hand-held thermography device. The observed variability in hotspot-to-perforator correlation, as identified by CTA and MRI, raises concerns about its current validity. Notably, the thermography's inability to visualize the three-dimensional course of vessels and pinpoint the fascia penetration point is a major drawback. Despite these limitations, IRT remains promising due to its cost-effectiveness and ease of use, which may support its adoption in clinical settings once limitations are better understood and addressed.13,27, 28, 29 Furthermore, the lack of standardization in thermographic protocols across studies complicates result comparison, highlighting the need for uniform methodologies.12,21,30

Despite efforts to improve precision through techniques like the ``cold challenge'' and stratification by subcutaneous thickness, variability persisted, underscoring the challenges of achieving consistent results with thermography. The unpredictable vessel course and arborization limit its accuracy in identifying fascial penetration points. Moreover, the absence of intraoperative validation as a ground truth and the reliance on fixed imaging timeframes may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future prospective studies including real-time intraoperative verification and standardized acquisition protocols will be essential to better define the potential and limits of thermography in this setting. Therefore, thermography is not yet a reliable stand-alone alternative for preoperative visualization and mapping of vascular pedicles of fasciocutaneous flaps.

Conclusion

This study assessed the accuracy of a smartphone-based thermography device in the preoperative identification of perforator vessels for abdominal flap-based breast reconstruction. While the technique proved simple, fast, and non-invasive, the correlation with perforators identified by CTA and MRA was inconsistent and generally low, particularly in patients with greater subcutaneous thickness. Importantly, the device lacks the ability to visualize vessel caliber, intramuscular course, and the precise point at which perforators penetrate the fascia. These limitations significantly affect its diagnostic reliability.

Based on the current data, this device cannot replace established imaging techniques such as CTA or MRA. Its role should be clearly understood as an adjunctive tool, potentially useful in selected cases or as a complement to standard imaging, but not as a standalone method.
