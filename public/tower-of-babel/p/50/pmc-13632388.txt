Psychometric evaluation of the Korean version of the Child Oral Health Impact Profile administered online among schoolchildren: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ji-Soo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Child Oral Health Impact Profile (COHIP-34) is a validated instrument used to assess oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) in children and adolescents. However, evidence on its reliability and validity is limited when administered online, particularly among school-aged children. This study aimed to assess the reliability and construct validity of the Korean version of the COHIP-34 when administered online to elementary school children participating in a national oral health program.MethodsA total of 980 fourth-grade students completed the Korean COHIP-34 using a smartphone-based Google Form. Internal consistency and test–retest reliability were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and the intraclass correlation coefficient, respectively. Convergent and discriminant validity were analysed using self-rated oral health and treatment needs questions, and oral examination results. Exploratory factor analysis (principal component analysis with Promax rotation) and confirmatory factor analyses were conducted, to evaluate the factor structure and overall model fit.ResultsThe COHIP-34 demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.828) and excellent test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.903). Convergent validity was supported by significant correlations with self-rated oral health (r = 0.399, P < 0.01) and treatment needs (r = -0.263, P < 0.01). Discriminant validity was demonstrated by significantly lower COHIP scores among children with caries experience or malocclusion. Exploratory factor analysis yielded eight factors with eigenvalues ≥ 1.0, explaining 56.1% of the total variance. Examination of the scree plot and parallel analysis indicated that a five-factor solution was more appropriate, which was subsequently confirmed by confirmatory factor analysis showing acceptable fit indices.ConclusionThe Korean COHIP-34, administered online, showed acceptable psychometric properties, suggesting that it may be a practical tool for assessing OHRQoL in large-scale epidemiological studies and school-based oral health programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09102-y.Keywords: Oral health-related quality of life, COHIP-34, Schoolchildren, Online survey, Psychometric validation

Background

Oral diseases are closely linked to systemic diseases, sharing multiple risk factors and thus constituting an essential component of overall health [1]. Their high prevalence and incidence make them a major global public health concern, especially among individuals with special healthcare needs and socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds [2]. Poor oral health negatively impacts the physical, social and emotional well-being of children and adolescents [3], and managing oral diseases imposes a substantial financial burden on both families and healthcare systems [4]. Therefore, comprehensive national oral health policies are essential to enhance access to services and resources necessary for preventing oral diseases and promoting oral health at the individual level [5].

To ensure effectively implemented and continuously improved oral health policies, their outcomes should be evaluated systematically and objectively. Various domains have been used to evaluate policy impact, including access to care, dental visit frequency, quality and cost of care, changes in oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours, equity in service utilization by income, changes in oral health status, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL). Among these, change in the oral health status is the most direct indicator of policy effectiveness [6]. However, long-term follow-up is required for the accurate assessment of such changes, while most existing studies have focused primarily on short-term clinical outcomes [7].

OHRQoL is a multidimensional concept that encompasses individuals’ subjective evaluations of their oral health status, functional well-being, social and emotional well-being and self-image [8]. Because it reflects the patient’s own perspective and experiences, OHRQoL plays a critical role in understanding perceived oral health needs and outcomes and has been widely used to assess the impact of specific oral conditions and dental treatments [9]. Moreover, oral health policies not only aim to improve clinical oral status but also influence behaviors, attitudes and values related to oral health. Therefore, OHRQoL is a valuable measure for evaluating their effectiveness [10].

Given that children and adolescents face oral health challenges distinct from those of adults, age-appropriate instruments are essential for evaluating their OHRQoL [11]. Among them, the Child Oral Health Impact Profile (COHIP) is one of the most widely used instruments for paediatric populations [12]. The original COHIP consists of 34 items across five subscales (COHIP-34) and is designed to evaluate both the negative and positive impacts of oral health in a diverse pediatric population, thereby minimizing acquiescence response bias [11, 13]. The Korean version of the COHIP-34 has demonstrated internal consistency, test–retest reliability, face, discriminant, and convergent validity, and an appropriate factor structure through a face-to-face survey of Korean students aged 8–15 years in general school settings [13, 14].

To ensure data accuracy and response reliability, most OHRQoL studies have traditionally employed face-to-face surveys. However, in-person surveys are often limited by time, cost, sample size constraints, and potential interviewer bias, which may affect representativeness [15]. With digital technology advancements, online surveys have emerged as a more efficient method for data collection, and several studies have used them to assess OHRQoL [16]. Nonetheless, most studies using online surveys have focused on adult populations, and when applied to children or adolescents, responses are often collected from caregivers rather than the children themselves [17]. Although online surveys enhance accessibility while minimizing logistical and administrative limitations, concerns remain regarding the validity and reliability of data collected from children compared to traditional face-to-face methods. Because changes in the mode of administration may influence children’s response behavior through factors such as device type, attention span, and environmental distractions, it is important to re-evaluate the psychometric properties when transitioning from paper-based to online formats. International guidelines, including those from the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) on electronic patient-reported outcomes (ePRO) also recommend assessing mode equivalence and measurement invariance to ensure validity and reliability [18]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the reliability and construct validity of the Korean COHIP-34 when administered online to participants of the Student Dental Home Program (SDHP), a major national oral health program in South Korea.

Methods

Student Dental Home Program

The SDHP provides preventive dental services to fourth-grade elementary school students (aged 10 years) in Seoul, South Korea [19]. Parents register their children through a mobile application, and participants visit designated dental clinics at scheduled times. The program is delivered by multiple licensed dentists who have completed a standardized training program covering the program protocol, oral examination procedures, diagnostic criteria, and the content of oral health education. It involves medical and dental history reviews and oral examinations, followed by preventive education on oral hygiene, diet, and fluoride use. Finally, clinical preventive procedures include professional tooth cleaning, fluoride application, sealants, and scaling. The Seoul Metropolitan Government fully funded all services, with no cost to participants.

Participants and survey procedure

The present study was conducted as part of a broader evaluation of OHRQoL among participants in the SDHP. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University School of Dentistry (IRB No. S-D20190018). The 2023 SDHP was implemented from May to November, and a recruitment banner for this study was displayed on the mobile application between July and September. By clicking the banner, guardians of eligible participants were directed to a Google Form questionnaire. Eligible participants were fourth-grade students of Korean ethnicity who were enrolled in the 2023 SDHP and residing in South Korea. Children were included if they and their guardians provided informed consent and the child completed the questionnaire independently, whereas responses were excluded if they were duplicated, incomplete, or submitted by children not participating in the SDHP. Data collected from the same participant cohort were previously used in an online validation study of the Korean version of the abbreviated Child Oral Health Impact Profile (COHIP-SF 19) [20].

The first screen of the form provided detailed information about the study. Only after the guardian and the child had read and agreed to the study description and signed the consent form could the child proceed to complete the questionnaire. The survey was designed to be filled out by the student, not the guardian, and the responses reflected the child’s oral health experiences over the past three months. To assess test–retest reliability, participants were asked to complete the same questionnaire twice, with an interval of one week. A mobile gift certificate worth approximately 8 USD was provided as an incentive to those who completed both survey rounds.

A minimum sample size of 5–10 participants per item and at least 300 participants overall is generally recommended for factor analyses of psychometric instruments [21, 22]. Accordingly, the required sample size for the 34-item COHIP-34 was estimated to be approximately 340 participants, which was considered sufficient to ensure stable factor solutions and precise estimates of reliability and validity.

COHIP-34 questionnaire

The Korean COHIP-34 questionnaire, previously validated through face-to-face administration among a general school population, was used in this study [14, 23]. As the original COHIP-34 had already undergone translation, cultural adaptation, and validation for face and content validity in earlier study, these procedures were not repeated in the present study.

Each item was rated using a five-point Likert scale. Twenty-eight items across four subscales—Oral Health Well-being (OH), Functional Well-being (FW), Social-Emotional Well-being (SW), and School Environment (SE)—evaluate the negative impacts, with responses from ‘never’ (4) to ‘almost all the time’ (0). The remaining six Self-Image (SI) items evaluated positive impacts and were reverse-scored, so that higher scores reflect better OHRQoL. Subscale scores were calculated by summing item scores, and the total COHIP score by summing subscale scores [24]. Responses of ‘almost all the time’ or ‘fairly often’ to negatively worded items and ‘somewhat disagree’ or ‘strongly disagree’ to positively worded items were considered indicative of a negative impact on OHRQoL.

Furthermore, participants self-rated their overall oral health status and perceived need for restorative dental treatment using a six-point Likert scale, where 0 represented ‘very poor’ or ‘no need at all’ and 5 represented ‘very good’ or ‘very necessary’.

Oral examination

The oral examination assessed dental caries, malocclusion, gingivitis, and oral hygiene. Dental caries was assessed based on the International Caries Detection and Assessment System, with codes ≥ 4 classified as carious lesions [25]. The number of decayed, filled, and missing teeth was recorded separately for the primary and permanent dentitions. Malocclusion was assessed using the Dental Health Component of the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need, with grades ≥ 3 indicating malocclusion [26]. Gingivitis was recorded in the presence of gingival bleeding, enlargement, or calculus formation. Oral hygiene was evaluated using the Patient Hygiene Performance Index, with scores 0–10 indicating good hygiene, 11–20 moderate, and 21–30 poor [27].

Statistical methods

Reliability

The internal consistency of the COHIP-34 was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for both the total score and each subscale score, as well as Cronbach’s alpha if each item was deleted. To evaluate scale homogeneity, Spearman’s correlations between the corrected item-total scores and the corrected item-subscale scores were also evaluated. For these analyses, the total and subscale scores were calculated by excluding the item being assessed. A Cronbach’s alpha value of ≥ 0.70 was considered indicative of satisfactory internal consistency, and corrected correlations of ≥ 0.20 were regarded as acceptable for retaining items within a scale. The test–retest reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Validity

Convergent validity was assessed by examining Spearman’s correlations between the COHIP scores and participants’ self-rated overall oral health status and treatment need. Discriminant validity was evaluated by comparing the COHIP-34 total and subscale scores based on oral examination results. Independent t-tests and analyses of variance were used for group comparisons, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni method.

Factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed to examine the underlying structure of the COHIP-34. Principal component analysis with Promax rotation (Kaiser normalization) was applied. The number of factors to retain was determined based on eigenvalues ≥ 1.0, inspection of the scree plot, and results of a parallel analysis (1,000 random data sets, PCA criterion). Loadings < 0.40 were suppressed for clarity. The extracted factor structure was assessed for consistency with the original five-subscale model.

Based on the EFA results, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was subsequently conducted to validate the model fit and examine the relationships among the latent variables and their observed indicators. In the initial model, correlations among the latent variables and factor loadings for each item were examined. When high modification indices (MIs) were observed between error terms within the same latent variable, covariances were added only between conceptually justifiable item pairs, and the modified model was reanalysed. This process was repeated until no item pairs within the same latent variable had an MI > 30, with model fit indices assessed after each modification. Due to the potential for inflated type I error in large samples, the model fit was primarily evaluated using the Chi-square Minimum Discrepancy divided by Degrees of Freedom (CMIN/DF) instead of the raw CMIN. Fit indices less sensitive to sample size—Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR)—were used instead of the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) and Adjusted GFI. In addition, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was used to compare the relative model fit [13]. Acceptable model fit was defined as CMIN/DF of ≤ 3, CFI and TLI of ≥ 0.90, RMSEA of ≤ 0.08 and SRMR of ≤ 0.10. For AIC, lower values indicated better fit among the models [28]. Factor loadings and residual variances were assessed in the final model.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 31.0, and AMOS version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the participants

Among the 23,937 fourth-grade students eligible for the 2023 SDHP, 20,495 students participated in the program. Between July and September 2023, 1,068 students completed both rounds of the online survey before receiving any dental service. Among them, 42 students who participated in the survey twice were included only once using their initial response, and 46 students who completed the survey but did not ultimately participate in the program were excluded from the analysis.

As a result, data from 980 students were included in the final analysis. These students were enrolled in 166 schools across all 25 districts of Seoul, representing 4.78% of all program participants. Of the 980 respondents, 508 (51.8%) were boys and 472 (48.2%) were girls. The participants had a mean age of 10.1 ± 0.4 years, with no significant difference in age between boys and girls.

Reliability

The Cronbach’s alpha for the COHIP-34 total score was 0.828, indicating satisfactory internal consistency (Table 2). When each item was deleted, Cronbach’s alpha either remained unchanged or slightly decreased for all items. When calculated based on subscale, all items had decreased Cronbach’s alpha when deleted, except for ‘Been teased (SW7)’, which showed only a minimal increase. All items demonstrated significant corrected item-total score correlations. However, all items in the SE subscale and SW7 had relatively low correlations, with coefficients of 0.096, 0.159, 0.138, 0.092 and 0.169, respectively. Similar patterns were observed in the corrected item-subscale score correlations, where all items exceeded 0.2, except for item SE1, which had a correlation of 0.198. The test–retest reliability, assessed using ICC, was 0.903 for the total score and 0.854, 0.871, 0.881, 0.740 and 0.876 for the OH, FW, SW, SE and SI subscales, respectively (all Ps < 0.001).

Validity

Results of the convergent validity are shown in Table 3. The COHIP-34 total score showed a moderate positive correlation with self-rated oral health (r = 0.399, P < 0.01) and a moderate negative correlation with treatment needs (r = − 0.263, P < 0.01). Subscale correlations were generally weaker but followed the same theoretical direction, with higher perceived oral health and lower treatment needs associated with better OHRQoL scores.

Table 4 depicts the discriminant validity based on the oral examination results. Children with caries experience had significantly lower total COHIP-34 scores than those without caries (P = 0.002), with a marked difference in the SI subscale (P < 0.001). Participants with malocclusion also showed lower scores, with a significant difference in the SW subscale (P = 0.011). No significant differences were found by gingivitis status or oral hygiene, except for a lower SI subscale score among children with poorer oral hygiene (P = 0.017). These results support the discriminant validity of the Korean COHIP-34, indicating that poorer clinical oral conditions are associated with lower OHRQoL.

Factor analysis

Results of the EFA are presented in Supplementary Tables S1–S4 and Figure S1. Eight factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were initially extracted, accounting for 56.1% of the total variance. However, considering the scree plot and the results of the parallel analysis (Supplementary Table S4 and Figure S1), the number of factors was limited to five, consistent with the original COHIP-34 subscale structure. Most items loaded onto factors generally corresponding to their intended subscales. Items belonging to the SW, SE, and SI subscales each formed a single, well-defined factor, demonstrating clear domain separation and strong internal coherence. Meanwhile, OH items were distributed across three components, but each group formed a distinct cluster, indicating a coherent and conceptually consistent structure. In contrast, FW items were also dispersed across three components but showed overlapping loadings with items from other domains, suggesting less distinct factor separation. Nevertheless, both OH and FW items were considered to retain conceptual homogeneity, and the original COHIP-34 subscale groupings were maintained accordingly. Moderate inter-factor correlations further supported the adequacy of retaining the original subscale structure.

Results of the CFA are presented in Table 5. In the initial model, the RMSEA and SRMR were acceptable; however, the CMIN/DF, CFI, and TLI indicated an unacceptable fit. Correlations among latent variables ranged from 0.023 to 0.608, and all factor loadings were above 0.33. Therefore, retaining the five latent variables, covariances were added between the error terms with an MI > 100 within the same latent variable to construct a second model. Although this modification improved all fit indices, CMIN/DF, CFI, and TLI were still below the recommended thresholds.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the reliability and construct validity of the Korean version of the COHIP-34 when administered online to elementary school children participating in the SDHP. The instrument showed satisfactory internal consistency and test–retest reliability. Convergent validity was supported by significant associations with self-rated oral health and perceived treatment need, while discriminant validity was demonstrated by differences in COHIP scores according to dental caries experience and malocclusion. In addition, both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses generally supported the original five-factor structure, providing further evidence for the construct validity of the instrument in a smartphone-based online format.

Evaluating prevention-focused programs is particularly challenging, as their effects may take longer to emerge compared with treatment-oriented programs, and few observable clinical changes may be present immediately post-implementation [29]. Consequently, more comprehensive assessment methods are required to evaluate the effectiveness of preventive oral health programs, and OHRQoL measures can serve as useful tools in this context [8, 10]. Although previous studies have reported comparable responses across different survey modes, limited evidence is available regarding smartphone-based administration among children, highlighting the importance of evaluating the psychometric performance of OHRQoL instruments in online formats [18, 30].

The distribution of the COHIP-34 total and subscale scores in this study was similar to those reported in a 2010 study conducted among Korean children and adolescents aged 8–15 years [14]. In particular, both studies reported significantly low proportion of children reporting any impact on the SE subscale. This may because neither study recruited children seeking dental treatment but instead focused on general community samples [23]. The COHIP scores in this study were slightly higher than those in previous community-based studies using the same questionnaire [14, 23, 31]. This difference may be explained by the homogeneity of our participants, all of whom were Korean students residing in Seoul—a capital city with a relatively high socioeconomic status. Conversely, previous community samples frequently included individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds or ethnically diverse populations.

The Cronbach’s alpha for the COHIP-34 total score was slightly lower than those in studies conducted in the USA, Canada and South Korea, but higher than that of a Dutch sample, indicating favourable internal consistency overall [14, 23, 32]. However, the SE subscale exhibited relatively low internal consistency, consistent with findings from previous studies in South Korea and the Netherlands [14, 33]. This may be due to the SE subscale containing only four items, fewer than other subscales [34]. In Broder’s original development study of the COHIP, the Cronbach’s alpha for the SE subscale was also lower than those of other subscales in a community sample [23]. This finding suggests that community-based populations may have more varied perceptions of the impact of oral health on school life compared with clinical samples.

The analysis of Cronbach’s alpha values when each item was excluded showed that all items contributed positively to the overall internal consistency of the scale. In the corrected item-subscale score correlations, all items demonstrated correlation coefficients of > 0.2, apart from SE1, showing a coefficient of 0.198. This finding indicates that all items could be retained within their respective subscales [14]. However, in the corrected item-total score correlations, SW7 and all items within the SE subscale reported coefficients of < 0.2. This may be due to the absence of negative impacts for those items, suggesting they could be excluded in the development of a Korean short form of the COHIP-34. The test–retest reliability was consistent with previous studies [14, 23], revealing the satisfactory reliability of the instrument.

The results of convergent validity indicated that children with higher COHIP scores—reflecting better OHRQoL—tended to rate their own oral health more positively and reported less perceived need for dental treatment. This finding is consistent with that of previous studies [14, 31] and supports the Korean COHIP-34 as a reliable tool for reflecting self-perceived oral health status and treatment needs. Conversely, the SE subscale was not significantly correlated, demonstrating that the perceived impact of oral health on school life may be relatively limited in this population.

Children with dental caries experience had significantly lower COHIP-34 scores compared with those without, and similarly, children with malocclusion tended to have lower scores, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.053). As COHIP-34 is a generic OHRQoL instrument designed to assess a broad range of oral health impacts, condition-specific aspects of malocclusion may not be fully reflected. At the subscale level, significant differences were observed in the SI subscale for caries experience and in the SW subscale for malocclusion. This may indicate that even in the absence of pain or functional impairment, children are particularly sensitive to the aesthetic and social aspects of oral health [35].

Conversely, no significant differences were found based on the gingivitis status. This aligns with previous studies revealing that only severe forms of gingivitis significantly affect OHRQoL and that children may be less aware of gingival health compared with caries or malocclusion [36]. Regarding oral hygiene, the SI subscale scores in children with moderate or poor oral hygiene were significantly lower than those with good oral hygiene. Although only few studies have examined the association between oral hygiene and OHRQoL in the paediatric population, this finding may indicate that oral hygiene has a great influence on self-perception of appearance in school-aged children.

The EFA results supported the overall construct validity of the Korean COHIP-34, with eight extracted components initially extracted. However, based on the eigenvalue criterion, inspection of the scree plot, and the results of the parallel analysis, the number of factors was limited to five, consistent with the original subscale structure [11]. Items within the OH subscale were distributed across three components but did not overlap with other domains, suggesting that children distinguish between different types of oral symptoms while maintaining conceptual consistency within the oral health construct. In contrast, FW items exhibited overlapping loadings with other domains, indicating a less distinct structure. This may reflect the heterogeneous nature of functional limitations, which often intersect with perceptions of pain, emotional impact, and social well-being. Nonetheless, the original FW subscales were retained based on theoretical consistency and content homogeneity, consistent with prior validation studies favoring conceptual coherence over strict statistical separation [37]. Meanwhile, the SW, SE, and SI items each loaded clearly onto single, coherent factors, confirming their conceptual independence in this population.

The CFA further confirmed the adequacy of this five-factor structure. The correlations between latent variables were low enough to suggest sufficient independence, justifying the retention of all subscales. In addition, the factor loadings and residual variances of the observed variables supported the inclusion of all items, unlike previous Korean study [13]. A total of nine inter-item covariances were added between items with conceptually related content and similar response tendencies, based on the MIs. This led to improvements in all fit indices in the final model.

Although few studies have reported full inter-subscale correlation matrices to allow direct comparisons, the correlation patterns between latent variables in the final model suggest that functional limitations are tightly coupled with the school environment and are also closely linked to socio-emotional well-being and oral symptoms. In practical terms, difficulties related to oral discomfort, chewing, and speaking tend to extend beyond physical symptoms, influencing classroom participation and social–emotional experiences. By contrast, self-image shows weaker ties to the other domains, implying a relatively distinct construct in this age group. Taken together, the correlations support a multidimensional yet interrelated structure, with FW serving as a central hub connecting symptomatic, school, and socio-emotional aspects of OHRQoL. The final model showed good explanatory power, indicating that the Korean COHIP-34 maintains its five-subscale structure with acceptable construct validity through limited inter-item covariances.

This study was conducted among elementary school students in Seoul using a convenience sampling approach, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to children from other geographic regions, age groups, socioeconomic backgrounds, or cultural settings. While the online survey improved accessibility and efficiency, limited researcher involvement may have affected response accuracy. As the data were self-reported, a response bias may have occurred, especially considering the participants’ age. In addition, oral examinations were conducted by multiple dentists, and inter-examiner variability cannot be excluded because formal examiner calibration was not performed. Finally, although the five-factor structure of the COHIP-34 was supported through EFA and CFA, the need for multiple model modifications suggests that further refinement of the instrument may be warranted. Future validation studies in more diverse populations and settings are needed to strengthen the evidence supporting the Korean version of the COHIP-34. Additional evaluation of the proposed factor structure in independent samples is also warranted and may inform the development of a revised or shortened version.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the Korean COHIP-34, when administered online, possesses acceptable reliability and validity in a national oral health program targeting schoolchildren. Its five-factor structure was retained, and the tool effectively assessed OHRQoL in a community setting. These findings support the potential applicability of the COHIP-34 for assessing quality of life in large-scale public oral health programs involving children and adolescents.
