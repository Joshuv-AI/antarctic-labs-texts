Virtual reality‐based cognitive‐motor training in middle‐aged adults at high Alzheimer's disease risk improves frontal cortex cerebral blood flow: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Maayan Harel, Anat Leibovici, Nofar Itzhaki, Ramit Ravona‐Springer, Erin Moshier, Glen M. Doniger, Amihai Gottlieb, Yotam Bahat, Barbara B. Bendlin, Gabi Zeilig, Meir Plotnik, Michal Schneider Beeri, Abigail Livny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease (AD) pathological processes begin decades before symptom onset. Early intervention in high‐risk populations may be crucial for prevention. We investigated the effect of an intervention utilizing virtual reality (VR) cognitive‐motor training on cerebral blood flow (CBF) and cognitive functioning in middle‐aged adults at high AD risk due to parental history.METHODS:In this randomized controlled trial, participants (n = 79) were randomly assigned to: VR cognitive–motor training while walking on a treadmill (VR+T, n = 24, treatment); VR cognitive training without treadmill (VR‐T, n = 21, active control); treadmill walking while watching documentaries (TV+T, n = 20, active control); or no intervention (n = 14, passive control). Training consisted of 45‐min sessions, twice weekly, for 12 weeks. CBF was measured at resting state using arterial spin labeling (ASL) at baseline, 3‐month, and 6‐month follow‐up. Cognition was assessed using a comprehensive neuropsychological battery. We applied the intent‐to‐treat approach.RESULTS:All groups improved in executive functions and memory over time (all p‐values < 0.05), with no consistent between‐group differences at follow‐up. CBF of the VR+T group significantly increased at 3 months in the superior (p = 0.013, middle (p = 0.014), and inferior (p = 0.003) frontal gyri compared to the passive control group, which showed a decline in CBF over the same period. No significant differences in frontal CBF change were observed between VR+T and the TV+T active control group. This increase was sustained for 6 months in the superior (p = 0.035) and middle (p = 0.028) frontal gyri. In contrast, in the middle temporal gyrus, the VR+T group had lower CBF at 3 months, compared to the VR‐T (p = 0.033) and to the passive control groups (p = 0.004).DISCUSSIONCognitive‐motor VR training increased CBF in frontal regions susceptible to early AD‐related changes in middle‐aged adults at high AD risk. This intervention shows promise as a preventive approach and may be suitable for implementation as a home‐based program for individuals at high risk.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD), while typically manifesting clinically in late life through memory deterioration and additional cognitive deficits, begins its pathological processes decades before symptom onset. Current interventions in diagnosed AD patients demonstrate moderate benefits in global cognition 1 , 2 , 3 and can meaningfully delay disease progression. However, the recognition that AD neuropathology emerges decades before clinical symptoms has prompted increased interest in preventive approaches, particularly targeting high‐risk individuals during middle age when pathological changes are developing but cognitive function remains largely intact. While most intervention studies have focused on older adults (≥60 years), 4 targeting middle‐aged individuals (40‐65 years) with AD family history represents an opportunity to intervene before significant neurobiological changes occur.

The advantage of maintaining a mentally active lifestyle is supported by extensive evidence of neuroplasticity in animal and human studies. Animal research demonstrates that enriched environments increase brain weight, cortical thickness, and neurotrophic factors, indicating that environmental stimulation can promote beneficial neural adaptations throughout the lifespan. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 In humans, cognitive interventions have demonstrated significant benefits in both preventing cognitive decline 10 , 11 and enhancing brain function. The randomized controlled ACTIVE trial showed that participants receiving speed of processing training were substantially less likely to develop dementia over 10 years, with protection occurring in a dose‐dependent manner. 12 , 13 Critically, behavioral interventions are free from adverse effects common to pharmacological interventions, and emerging evidence suggests cognitive stimulation may be particularly effective when administered before significant neuronal damage occurs. 14 , 15 Furthermore, cognitive training can increase cerebral blood flow (CBF) in prefrontal and middle/posterior cingulate cortices in healthy older adults. 10 However, traditional computerized cognitive training programs show limited transfer of gains to everyday activities, 13 highlighting the need for more ecologically valid approaches that bridge the gap between experimentally based improvements and real‐world functional benefits.

The combination of cognitive tasks with simultaneous walking mirrors complex activities of daily living (e.g., shopping), improving ecological validity and real‐life transfer potential. Evidence suggests more engaging, cognitively demanding training is more effective 16 ; combining cognitive training with motor tasks may improve efficacy compared to single‐modality interventions through greater cognitive demands, 17 particularly in dual‐tasking contexts requiring increased attentional allocation and executive control. 18 The biological basis for this combined approach is rooted in evolutionary development, as complex motor activities inherently rely on cognitive processes, with attention systems evolving primarily to serve motor function. 19 Virtual reality (VR) technology offers an ideal platform for such combined cognitive‐motor training, particularly for middle‐aged adults still actively engaged in complex daily activities. VR creates multisensory, dynamic, interactive environments with greater similarity to real life. VR‐based training has demonstrated feasibility in both healthy older individuals and those with cognitive impairment, with participants often preferring VR over traditional methods. 20 , 21

Reduction in CBF has been associated with cognitive decline, with AD patients demonstrating decreased CBF and increased brain vascular resistance. 22 Importantly, CBF changes are detectable in both mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and high‐risk populations before cognitive symptoms emerge, 23 , 24 , 25 with pronounced alterations primarily affecting the frontal, temporal, and parietal regions 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ‐ areas crucial for memory and executive functions. 30 , 31 While most studies have examined CBF changes in older populations, CBF alterations begin in midlife, particularly in individuals with genetic predisposition to AD. 32 , 33 In healthy older adults, cognitive training specifically benefits executive function and increases CBF in prefrontal and middle/posterior cingulate cortices. 10 , 34 This dual pattern, vulnerability of brain regions involved in memory and executive functions to CBF reduction and the demonstrated responsiveness of these same regions to targeted interventions, 34 suggests that early cognitive–motor training interventions could provide comprehensive protection against impaired CBF and subsequent cognitive decline.

The present randomized controlled trial examined whether VR‐based cognitive‐motor training could enhance CBF and cognitive functioning in middle‐aged adults at high risk for AD due to parental family history. The study focused on a priori regions of interest ‐ the middle temporal gyrus, inferior frontal, middle frontal gyrus and superior frontal gyrus ‐ which were previously shown to be associated with reduced CBF in AD patients. 23 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 35 , 36 By comparing a 12‐week VR cognitive training program combined with a motor task to each training type alone (VR task or motor task) and to a passive control group, we hypothesize that the combined VR and motor training will lead to greater improvements in CBF and cognitive performance than any of the other groups, both at 3 months (immediate post‐intervention) and at 6 months (long‐term effect).

METHODS

Study design, participants, and intervention

Participants were recruited from the community via a research database of potentially eligible participants at the Joseph Sagol Neuroscience Center at the Sheba Medical Center, Israel, advertisements on the website of Israel's major healthcare organizations, and email campaigns. The study design has been previously described in detail. 37 In short, participants’ inclusion criteria were having at least one parent (alive or deceased) with AD, age at enrollment 40–65 years, fluent in Hebrew, having an informant and living in a 50 km radius from the Sheba Medical Center. Participants were excluded if they had a major health condition that may affect cognition or treadmill walking (e.g. Parkinson's disease, traumatic brain injury), unstable medical condition in the previous 6 months or if they were unable to comply with the training regimen. Before participating, all individuals gave their informed consent, and the study received ethical approval from the Helsinki Committee at the Sheba Medical Center (protocol #2988‐16‐SMC) and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02832921).

The current study is a randomized controlled trial with blind assessors, employing a parallel‐group design. Training sessions lasted 45 minutes, twice/week for 12 weeks, resulting in 24 sessions. The VR+T group was trained with virtual reality “real world” tasks in a virtual supermarket setting, while walking on a treadmill. All tasks included different groceries shopping instructions, derived from specific executive functions (e.g., planning, task switching, see more 37 ) and participants walked the treadmill while simultaneously picking up virtual groceries using their hands, session to session progression in training was achieved by increasing the complexities of the cognitive tasks, and increasing T speed. The VR‐T was trained in the same virtual tasks as group 1 but stood on the treadmill instead of walking. This group served as active control but without the motor component of treadmill walking. Task difficulty was adaptively increased as participants improved. For cognitive complexity, tasks were made progressively more demanding by increasing the cognitive load (e.g., lengthening the product list in the working memory task, introducing more complex rules in the rule‐deduction task). For motor complexity, treadmill speed was progressively increased from 0.4 to 0.8 m/s to maintain an appropriate challenge while minimizing physical exertion and keeping the intervention sub‐aerobic, consistent with our goal of accentuating cognitive rather than cardiovascular demands The TV+T group walked the treadmill while watching a documentary movie. During that time, participants were asked three questions about the movie. The movie was paused while the question appeared on the screen and the participants responded on a response sheet. Progression in training was achieved only by increasing the treadmill speed. Just as participants in the VR groups (VR+T, VR‐T) received periodic questions from the experimenter during their training to ensure engagement with the virtual tasks, these movie‐related questions served to maintain cognitive engagement in TV+T group. Heart rate was monitored throughout each session and maintained below 65% of maximum capacity (calculated as 220 minus age, 38 ) to ensure the motor component remained sub‐aerobic and did not constitute cardiovascular exercise. This group served as active control, including the motor component but providing non‐specific cognitive stimulation instead of targeted cognitive training. The passive control group served as passive control, as participants did not engage in any kind of intervention during the 12‐week period. The training sessions took place in the VR laboratories of the Center of Advanced Technologies in Rehabilitation at the Sheba Medical Center. The CONSORT flow diagram detailing participant screening, enrollment, randomization, and group allocation is presented in the study design paper 37 (see Figure 1 therein).

Assessments

Participants provided written informed consent prior to completing baseline evaluations, which were administered across two separate sessions‐neuroimaging procedures on one day and all remaining assessments on another day (for detailed descriptions refer to Ref.37). Outcomes were measured at baseline, at 3 months (immediately after the intervention), and again at 6 months (3 months post‐intervention) to assess its long‐term effects.

Cognitive assessment

Participants completed a neurocognitive battery composed of conventional neuropsychological tests focusing on memory and executive functions, which are well known cognitive domains impacted by AD. 39 , 40 The battery included the Mini‐Mental Status Examination to assess global cognition. Episodic memory was assessed with the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) immediate and delayed recall and the Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS) Logical Memory I and II. Executive functions were measured with the Clock Drawing Test, Color Trails Test A and B, Block Design, and digit symbol test. Language was assessed with the Boston Naming Test (15‐item), Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) vocabulary subtest, and category and letter Fluency tests. Finally, attention/working memory was assessed with the digit span forwards and backwards tests and the WAIS working memory subtest. The primary cognitive outcome measure was a composite global cognitive score, calculated by averaging the z‐scores derived from all neuropsychological tests. Secondary cognitive outcomes were the four domain‐specific scores, computed by averaging the z‐scores from the neuropsychological tests within each cognitive domain. 37

Image acquisition

Imaging was performed on a 3T Philips Ingenia scanner, using a 32‐channel head coil, at Sheba Medical Center. T1‐weighted anatomical images were acquired using 3D turbo field echo (TFE) sequence with 1 mm isotropic voxels, repetition time (TR) = 7.92 s, echo time (TE) = 3.5 s, field of view (FOV) 256 × 180 mm, 150 axial slices.

Resting state pseudo‐continuous arterial spin labeing (pcASL) was scanned with the following parameters: TR = 4500 ms; TE = 13 ms; FOV = 240 × 240 × 155 mm; slice thickness = 5 mm no gap; matrix size = 128 × 128; with a post‐labeling delay of 1800 ms. 3D T1‐weighted imaging was acquired with the following parameters: inversion‐ recovery prepared fast spin echo, TR 7.92, TE 3.5, FOV 256 × 180 × 155 mm, acquired over 155 axial slices with isotropic 1mm voxel resolution and no gap.

MRI data preprocessing and analysis

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) images underwent standard preprocessing using SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The pre‐processing of ASL images included reorienting to the anterior commissure, co‐registration to the individual's 3D T1 images, normalization to the standard stereotactic space of the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template using a 12‐parameter linear affine transformation. Finally, in order to minimize anatomical differences and increase signal to noise ratio a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm for smoothing was applied. The PcASL sequence used for this study was not acquired using a proton‐density scan and thus precise quantification using ml per volume units was not calculated. In order to allow examination of the changes in CBF maps, we regressed out each voxel by the mean of the global signal per participant using an in‐house developed MATLAB (v2019b) script.

ROI analysis

We implemented an a‐priori region of interest (ROI) analysis focusing on brain regions previously shown to be associated with reduced CBF in AD patients: the middle temporal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, and superior frontal gyrus. 23 , 27 , 35 , 36 ROIs were defined using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas. 41 Mean CBF values for each ROI were extracted from individual CBF maps after correcting for global signal. For each participant, the mean CBF value within each ROI was calculated by averaging the CBF values across all voxels within that region. This process was repeated for each time point (baseline, 3‐month, and 6‐month follow‐up) to enable analysis of CBF changes over time.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline differences across the four intervention groups (VR+T, VR−T, TV+T, Passive control) were evaluated for age and years of education using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and for sex using the chi‐square test. All analyses followed an intent‐to‐treat (ITT) principle, whereby participants were analyzed in their originally assigned groups regardless of adherence, protocol deviations, or early withdrawal.

For cognition (five prespecified domains: executive function, memory, attention, language, and an overall composite) and CBF (four a priori regions: middle frontal, inferior frontal, superior frontal, and middle temporal gyri), outcomes were analyzed using a constrained longitudinal mixed‐effects analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), as described by Cao et al. 42 The constrained approach provides unbiased estimates of treatment effects under the missing‐at‐random (MAR) assumption and generally yields a smaller estimated variance of the treatment effect compared with traditional longitudinal ANCOVA models. Time was modeled as a categorical factor (baseline, 3 months, 6 months). The constraint was implemented by omitting the main effect of group and including group‐by‐time indicators, which forces equality of adjusted baseline means across groups, so that between‐group effects represent differences in change from baseline. Fixed covariates were age, years of education, and sex. Models included a random intercept for subjects to account for within‐person correlation and used an unstructured covariance to model correlation among repeated measures. Estimation used restricted maximum likelihood with the Kenward−Roger denominator degrees‐of‐freedom adjustment. Adjusted (least‐squares) means and changes from baseline were reported at age = 56 years, education = 16 years, and sex = female.

Planned contrasts estimated within‐group changes from baseline to 3 months and from baseline to 6 months for each group, as well as between‐group differences in change comparing VR+T, VR−T, and TV+T each versus Passive control at 3 and 6 months. The overall group‐by‐time interaction tested whether all constrained group‐specific differences in change were equal to zero.

All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Two‐sided tests used a significance level of 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 80 participants were enrolled in the study. One withdrew prior to randomization leaving a final sample size of 79 subjects. DISCUSSION

This trial assessed the effectiveness of combining VR‐based cognitive training with treadmill walking (dual‐task) on cognition and CBF in middle‐aged individuals at high AD risk due to parental family history. Although all groups showed cognitive improvements over time, there were no differences between groups in global cognition except at six months for executive functions, which was a secondary cognitive outcome. In contrast, the VR+T group demonstrated increased CBF relative to the passive control group immediately post‐intervention in all three frontal gyri, with sustained improvement at 6 months in the superior and middle frontal gyri. Interestingly, the middle temporal gyrus showed greater CBF improvements in the passive control and VR−T groups compared with VR+T at 3‐month follow‐up.

Targeting CBF, one of the earliest modifiable markers in AD's pathological cascade, 22 , 28 represents a unique opportunity to influence disease trajectory before clinical symptoms emerge. The study examined whether the combination of everyday‐life‐task‐based VR training combined with treadmill walking would be superior to single interventions or no intervention in modifying early, pre‐clinical cerebrovascular changes. By focusing on middle‐aged adults with family history of AD, this approach addresses the critical window when neuropathological changes begin but cognitive function remains intact.

The observed increase in frontal CBF following our cognitive‐motor dual‐task intervention is promising. This outcome contrasts with the typical AD pattern where CBF reduction is an early biological marker of neurodegeneration 22 . In AD, decreased CBF, particularly in frontal, temporal, and parietal regions crucial for memory and executive functions, is commonly observed. 26 , 27 , 43 This positive impact aligns with evidence showing CBF sensitivity to cognitive interventions in healthy older adults without significant neuronal damage. 10 , 34 This increase in frontal CBF may indicate improved neural efficiency or enhanced cognitive reserve, potentially contributing to prevention or delay of cognitive decline.

The different CBF patterns between VR+T and passive control can be understood in the context of AD's complex pathophysiology. While aging influences CBF decrease across the lifespan, 36 AD pathogenesis and genetic predisposition create a dynamic pathological process of CBF changes. 22 , 23 , 35 Consistent with patterns observed in early AD pathology, hyperperfusion has been observed in cognitively intact or MCI patients who are apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4) carriers in several brain regions, including hippocampus, parahippocampus, amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, and temporal regions. 23 , 35 , 44 However, as disease progresses into AD, this pattern changes and hypoperfusion is observed. 22 This compensatory trajectory aligns with AD's spatiotemporal progression, beginning in medial temporal lobe structures before spreading to frontal and parietal regions. 45 Previous studies have consistently shown that MCI patients exhibit increased CBF predominantly in frontal regions, interpreted as a compensatory mechanism. 28 , 46 The observed increase in temporal CBF in our high‐risk sample without intervention could represent a compensatory response similar to that seen in APOE4 carriers in early AD pathology. 47

This multimodal approach of a virtual supermarket task while performing walking represents a significant step toward enhancing ecological validity in non‐pharmaceutical interventions. It provides an engaging context for cognitive stimulation, potentially increasing participant motivation and adherence, as demonstrated by the high adherence rates in our study. The similarity to everyday activities enhances skill transfer likelihood, bridging the gap between laboratory‐based training and real‐life application. 48 , 49 The virtual supermarket environment allows simultaneous engagement of multiple cognitive domains, including memory, attention, decision‐making, and spatial navigation, resembling their integrated use in daily life.

All groups showed cognitive improvements over time, including passive control, suggesting a learning effect. However, there were no beneficial cognitive effects for VR+T compared to other groups in this healthy middle‐aged sample. While dual‐task interventions show consistent improvements in attention, executive function, and memory in older adults and clinical populations, 50 , 51 , 52 evidence supporting such benefits in healthy middle‐aged adults remains limited. Several factors might explain this discrepancy between biological and cognitive outcomes. First, the relatively young age of participants compared to previous studies' older populations may have affected observed effects, as inducing and detecting changes in younger asymptomatic individuals is particularly challenging. Second, current cognitive assessment tools may not capture subtle cognitive changes. Finally, CBF alterations may precede detectable cognitive decline in the natural progression of AD. 22 , 28

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The small sample size limits generalizability, underscoring the need for replication in larger, multi‐site, and more diverse cohorts. Although randomization should have minimized systematic differences between the passive and active groups, we did not measure exposures outside the intervention, such as physical activity, that might nonetheless have affected the results. We used the 220‐age formula to calculate maximum heart rate, which is now considered less accurate than more recent age‐prediction equations and may have resulted in a lower target heart rate threshold. However, our primary goal was to maintain sub‐aerobic intensity to minimize physical exertion rather than induce cardiovascular effects, so this is unlikely to have substantially affected our results. The time‐intensive intervention may hinder feasibility, particularly among younger, working‐age populations. Additionally, MRI‐based CBF measurement costs preclude large‐scale applications, suggesting future studies should explore more accessible methods such as Doppler ultrasound. 53 Advances in portable and user‐friendly digital platforms allow for the integration of cognitive tasks into motor exercises outside of laboratory or clinical settings. For example, VR goggles and smartphone‐based cognitive applications may offer engaging home‐training options that can adapt task difficulty in real time. Since walking with VR goggles may pose safety concerns, other simple motor activities, such as seated or standing arm flexion synchronized with cognitive tasks, can effectively increase cognitive demands, 54 similar to our study. These approaches could make dual‐task training both accessible and scalable for community‐based or home interventions.

Strengths of the study include the use of a “real‐life” style VR training paradigm enhancing relevance to daily lives of individuals at high AD risk; focus on a middle‐aged, high‐risk population representing an optimal window for preventive interventions; inclusion of a passive control group; and incorporation of follow‐up assessments immediately post‐intervention and at 6 months to evaluate longer‐term effects.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the potential benefits of cognitive‐motor VR training in middle‐aged adults with AD family history. By targeting this population when neural plasticity may be greater and neuropathological changes have not yet accumulated, we identified promising CBF changes that contrast with typical early AD patterns of CBF decline. Our study suggests that a multimodal cognitive‐motor approach may be more effective than each modality alone. Future larger‐scale and longer trials will determine whether observed CBF improvements translate into long‐term cognitive and functional benefits in at‐risk middle‐aged populations.
