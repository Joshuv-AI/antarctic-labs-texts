The impact of the primary surgeon’s routine surgical experience on the clinical efficacy of robot-assisted knee arthroplasty
Authors: Hongping Wang, Mingyou Wang, Xiaoqin Yang, Zhuodong Tang, Zhu Mei, Xunzhou Song, Heng Xiao, Shaojiang Liu, Yuping Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impact of a surgeon’s routine operative experience on outcomes in robot-assisted total knee arthroplasty (RATKA) remains unclear. This retrospective study investigated whether the volume of a surgeon’s primary conventional TKA experience influences the clinical efficacy of subsequent RATKA procedures. Based on the primary surgeon’s level of experience, a total of 167 patients undergoing RATKA were enrolled and allocated into two cohorts: Group A (surgeons with fewer than 100 prior cases, n = 64) and Group B (surgeons with more than 500 prior cases, n = 103). Perioperative data, radiographic outcomes, and clinical functional scores were compared. The results showed that while surgeons in Group B had significantly shorter intraoperative registration, osteotomy, and total operative times (P < 0.001), Incision length, blood loss, and postoperative complication rates did not differ significantly between Group A and Group B. Crucially, at both 3 and 180 days post-surgery, functional outcomes as measured by VAS, KSS, ROM, and WOMAC scores were comparable between the two groups (P > 0.05), as was the accuracy of postoperative lower limb alignment and component positioning (P > 0.05). Our findings suggest that although a surgeon’s extensive routine experience enhances procedural efficiency in RATKA, the robotic system’s precision and standardization effectively mitigate the impact of experience variability, enabling surgeons with differing backgrounds to achieve equivalent and excellent short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes. This supports the role of robotic assistance in promoting outcome homogeneity in knee arthroplasty.Keywords: Robot-assisted surgery, Total knee arthroplasty, Surgical experience, Clinical efficacy, Outcome homogenization

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is the primary surgical treatment for end-stage knee diseases. Its efficacy is closely associated with the accurate placement of prosthetic components, satisfactory restoration of lower limb alignment, and optimal soft tissue balance [1]. In conventional TKA, surgical outcomes are highly dependent on the primary surgeon’s operative experience. Substantial evidence-based medical research indicates a direct correlation between the surgeon’s experience and surgical results [2–4]. Consequently, the surgeon’s experience is a significant factor contributing to variability in clinical outcomes. Literature reports indicate that surgeons with extensive routine surgical experience—namely, a higher volume of primary procedures—demonstrate superior outcomes compared to lower-volume surgeons in terms of surgical precision and alignment reconstruction, complication and reoperation rates, as well as functional results and patient-reported outcomes [5, 6]. This heterogeneity in efficacy, based on the surgeon’s procedural volume, underscores the high dependence of conventional techniques on the surgeon’s individual experience. It also represents a core challenge in achieving consistent clinical outcomes across surgical practice.

In recent years, the adoption of robot-assisted knee arthroplasty has offered a new technological pathway to address the challenge of clinical outcome variability caused by reliance on surgical experience [7]. Robotic-assisted systems transform key surgical steps from reliance on the surgeon’s ‘feel’ into quantifiable, reproducible, and standardized processes. Their core theoretical advantage lies in enhancing surgical precision and predictability, thereby reducing outcome variability attributable to differences in surgeon experience and promoting the ‘standardisation’ of TKA outcomes [8]. This is particularly significant for ensuring surgical quality for surgeons with less extensive operative experience. However, current robot-assisted surgery essentially operates in a “human-machine collaboration” mode, not as fully autonomous robotic procedures. The primary surgeon remains responsible for critical tasks such as preoperative planning, assessment of soft tissue tension and dynamic balance adjustment, as well as executing steps like osteotomy [9]. Nonetheless, these actions by the surgeon are all supported by the artificial intelligence functionalities of the robotic system, thereby significantly reducing reliance on the surgeon’s personal experience. Consequently, with the adoption of this high-precision robotic tool to assist TKA, the question arises: has the ‘traditional experience’ accumulated through a high volume of routine surgeries become less important or devalued? Currently, there are few reports in this area, so this study will evaluate the clinical outcomes of RA-TKA performed by orthopedic surgeons with varying levels of experience through a retrospective study.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients with end-stage knee disease. (2) All scheduled to undergo robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty. (3) All underwent unilateral knee arthroplasty. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients undergoing unicompartmental knee arthroplasty or revision knee arthroplasty. (2) Patients receiving bilateral knee arthroplasty. (3) patients with incomplete clinical and imaging data.

Clinical data

The study cohort comprised 167 patients who underwent RA-TKA at our department from June 2023 to September 2025. Based on the primary surgeon’s routine surgical experience (quantified by the number of primary procedures performed), patients were divided into Group A (< 100 cases) and Group B (> 500 cases), comprising 64 and 103 patients, respectively. The two groups were comparable, as no statistically significant differences were found in their baseline characteristics (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Surgical technique

A posterior-stabilised (PS) type knee prosthesis was used in all cases. Intraoperatively, following routine exposure of the knee joint, Following tracker placement on the femur and tibia, all reference points were sequentially registered as directed by the robotic system. Subsequent verification and adjustment of the surgical plan were performed. Once the plan met with approval, osteotomy was performed with robotic assistance. Following osteotomy, the stability of the knee joint was assessed with trial components. After satisfactory verification, the joint cavity was irrigated and the prosthesis was implanted.

Postoperative management

Postoperative care involved a comprehensive perioperative management protocol including anti-inflammatory, anticoagulant, and analgesic therapies. After operation, the knee joint anteroposterior and lateral views and the full length X-ray of both lower limbs were taken, and the angles were measured. Knee function rehabilitation exercises were conducted under the guidance of a professional physiotherapist.

Outcome measures

For both groups, we recorded intraoperative parameters (registration time, osteotomy time, total operative time, incision length, blood loss, and complications), radiographic outcomes (postoperative HKA angle, LTC, FTC, LFC, and FFC), and clinical assessments (KSS, VAS pain score, ROM, and WOMAC score at postoperative days 3 and 180).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables (normally distributed) were expressed as mean ± SD (x̄±s) and compared between groups using independent t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as rates and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (α = 0.05).

Results

Comparison of complications

Intraoperative Complications: In both groups, there were no intraoperative complications involving major neurovascular injuries or damage to ligaments and other soft tissues. However, conversion to the conventional technique occurred in 4 cases (6.25%) in Group A: one exclusion occurred due to intraoperative tracker mount loosening, and three cases where the osteotomy deviated from the planned amount due to insufficient intraoperative adjustments. In Group B, conversion occurred in 5 cases (4.85%): one exclusion occurred due to intraoperative tracker mount loosening, and four cases where failure resulted from excessive modification of the robotic plan based on the surgeon’s personal experience. Intraoperative complication rates were similar across both groups(P > 0.05). No severe postoperative complications, including acute periprosthetic joint infection or wound infection, occurred in either group. Regarding postoperative complications, early wound exudate occurred in 1 patient from Group A and 2 from Group B, while muscular calf vein thrombosis was observed in 1 patient in each group. Overall complication rates were similar between Group A (3.13%) and Group B (2.91%), with no significant intergroup difference detected (P > 0.05).

Comparison of intraoperative indicators

Group A exhibited significantly longer intraoperative registration, osteotomy, and total operative times relative to Group B (P < 0.05). Conversely, no significant differences were found between the groups regarding incision length or intraoperative blood loss (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Discussion

In conventional TKA, the primary surgeon’s operative experience is a critical variable contributing to variability in clinical outcomes. This experience is largely derived from the volume of primary procedures performed. A direct association between surgeon experience and surgical outcomes has been demonstrated in multiple studies [10]. Research indicates that high-volume surgeons achieve superior TKA outcomes, including lower rates of alignment outliers, early revision, and perioperative complications, relative to low-volume surgeons [11]. Another study indicated that surgeries performed by experienced surgeons resulted in lower postoperative pain scores and superior KSS and WOMAC scores [12]. The underlying reasons for these disparities may include the ability of experienced surgeons to more precisely control osteotomy, more adeptly achieve soft tissue balance, and make superior decisions when encountering complex intraoperative situations [13]. Consequently, within the conventional surgical paradigm, the surgeon’s routine operative experience is one of the core determinants of therapeutic efficacy. However, with the progressive maturation of robotic-assisted knee arthroplasty technology, its advantages lie in achieving precision and reproducibility surpassing that of conventional surgery, shortening the surgical learning curve, accelerating surgeon proficiency, and promoting standardisation of the surgical workflow [14–16]. The introduction of robotic-assisted surgical systems aims to reduce reliance on the surgeon’s individual experience through digitalised and standardised operative procedures, thereby enhancing the homogeneity of outcomes [17]. Thus, the implementation of robotic assistance represents a significant impact on, and a potential dilution of the influence of, traditional surgical experience on clinical efficacy.

Although robotic-assisted TKA can achieve homogenised clinical outcomes across surgeons with varying levels of experience, this does not imply that the surgeon’s routine surgical experience has become unimportant in the robotic-assisted setting. On one hand, this study confirms that the value of the surgeon’s routine experience in robotic-assisted TKA is primarily reflected in enhancing procedural proficiency and efficiency. Our results show that surgeons with extensive routine experience had significantly shorter intraoperative registration time, osteotomy time, and total operative time compared to their less-experienced counterparts, with these differences being statistically significant. This finding aligns with conclusions from some existing studies. Literature reports indicate that even for experienced arthroplasty surgeons, mastering a robotic system requires an adaptation period. However, their extensive routine surgical experience facilitates a quicker understanding of the system’s logic, completion of spatial registration, and manipulation of instruments, thereby shortening the learning curve and manifesting as higher surgical efficiency [18]. On the other hand, this study identified an interesting pattern: the reasons for intraoperative conversion to the conventional technique differed between highly experienced surgeons and those with relatively less experience. In this study, the primary reason for conversion in Group A was excessive reliance on the robotic plan coupled with insufficient intraoperative adjustment based on the patient’s specific anatomy, leading to failure. In contrast, the primary reason in Group B was excessive trust in their own routine surgical experience, leading them to modify the robotic plan based on that experience, which consequently resulted in failure. This reveals a differential manifestation of experience disparity within the human-machine interaction paradigm: less-experienced surgeons may exhibit over-reliance on the system’s plan coupled with insufficient confidence in autonomous adjustment, while highly experienced surgeons might experience cognitive conflict between their ingrained experience and the robotic plan [19]. This suggests that optimised robotic training should extend beyond system operation to specifically address the effective integration of the surgeon’s clinical expertise with artificial intelligence-driven planning [20].

However, despite differences in intraoperative process-related metrics between surgeons of varying experience when performing robotic-assisted TKA, the ultimate surgical precision and clinical outcomes achieved a high degree of consistency. On one hand, in this study, the postoperative HKA angle, along with LFC, FFC, LTC, and FTC angles, were all accurately controlled within the ideal range for both groups, no significant differences were detected. This robustly supports the core value of robotic-assisted TKA: by utilising preoperative 3D planning, intraoperative real-time navigation, and precise robotic-arm-guided osteotomy, it transforms key surgical steps from reliance on ‘surgical feel’ into quantifiable, reproducible, and standardised operations. This substantially mitigates potential errors in limb alignment and component positioning that might otherwise arise from differences in surgeon experience [21]. This finding aligns with existing literature reporting that robotic assistance significantly improves the accuracy and reproducibility of alignment restoration in TKA, and that this advantage is consistently demonstrated across surgeons of different experience levels [22, 23]. In contrast, no significant intergroup differences were found in VAS scores, KSS scores, joint range of motion, or WOMAC scores at any postoperative time point. This indicates that although experienced surgeons may operate with greater efficiency, the ‘standardising’ capability of the robotic system empowers surgeons with relatively less experience to achieve comparable levels of excellent postoperative pain relief and functional recovery for their patients [24]. This provides a powerful technological solution for achieving homogeneity in surgical care quality and ensuring that patients receive consistently reliable surgical outcomes regardless of the specific surgeon [25–27].

Limitations: First, the single-centre retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Second, surgeon experience was quantified solely by procedural volume, without consideration of surgical quality metrics. Third, this study’s short follow-up period limited evaluation of long-term prosthesis survival and complications. Therefore, multicentre prospective studies with extended follow-up are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, the impact of the primary surgeon’s routine surgical experience on robot-assisted knee arthroplasty is primarily manifested in the intraoperative process, specifically in reducing operative time and enhancing the efficiency of human-robot collaboration. However, empowered by the high precision and standardisation of the robotic system, surgeons with differing levels of experience achieved comparable results in terms of accurate postoperative lower limb alignment reconstruction, prosthesis positioning, and favourable short- to mid-term clinical functional outcomes. This indicates that robotic-assisted TKA can substantially mitigate the reliance of knee arthroplasty on the surgeon’s routine surgical experience, providing a reliable technological pathway towards promoting the ‘standardisation’ of outcomes in joint replacement surgery.
