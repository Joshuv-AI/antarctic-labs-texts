Selective membrane wetting of phase-separated giant unilamellar vesicles by coacervate droplets
Authors: Emmanuel Joseph, Etienne Ducrot, B. V. V. S. Pavan Kumar, Nicolas Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) has emerged as a key biological paradigm, playing a critical role in cellular compartmentalization and potentially contributing to the origins of life. Lipid membranes are equally important in these processes, serving as selective barriers that define and protect cellular environments. Recent studies have revealed a dynamic interaction between LLPS and lipid membranes, both in cellulo and in biomimetic systems. In vitro studies typically use simplified model membranes, while biological membranes are far more complex, often exhibiting phase-separated domains. Despite growing interest, the relationship between lipid phase separation within membranes and LLPS remains largely unexplored. Here, we explore how coacervates interact with membranes composed of liquid-ordered or liquid-disordered lipid phases using giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs). We show that coacervate-membrane interactions are modulated by coacervate composition and lipid domain properties. Notably, we report the selective wetting of lipid domains in phase separated GUVs. Different coacervates exhibit distinct wetting behaviors, with variations in their composition influencing whether they preferentially wet liquid-disordered or liquid-ordered lipid domains, highlighting the coacervate-specific nature of these interactions. These findings highlight how lipid phase separation can mediate selective membrane wetting, offering insights into condensate-membrane interactions in cells and in simplified cell-like model systems.

Introduction

Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is emerging as a central paradigm in biology1, and is hypothesized to have played a role in the emergence of protocells2. In living cells, phase-separated droplets, known as biomolecular condensates, perform critical biological functions, such as metabolic regulation, signal transduction and stress management3. In vitro, droplets produced by associative LLPS between polymers or surfactants, known as coacervates, capture key physicochemical properties of biomolecular condensates4,5, including increased viscosity and reduced surface tension, but also serve as plausible protocell models by concentrating chemical species6,7, enabling reactions that would otherwise be unfavorable in aqueous environments8–11.

Lipid membranes are also fundamental to both cellular and protocellular compartmentalization and evolution12,13. Modern cells, but also a number of intracellular organelles, are delineated by complex proteolipidic membranes that form selectively permeable barriers capable of regulating the exchange of solutes. This structural role is equally important when considering the origins of life, as simple membranes must have been essential to define and separate the internal protocell environment from the surrounding medium14.

These two forms of compartmentalization—phase-separated droplets and membrane-bounded structures—are not mutually exclusive. Recent studies have highlighted the interactions between LLPS and lipid membranes, revealing a dynamic interplay that influences both cellular function and protocell models. In living cells, biomolecular condensates often interact closely with lipid membranes15. These interactions can drive processes, such as membrane remodeling16 and protein recruitment17, playing a key role in cellular dynamics and signaling pathways. In vitro, early experimental studies using PEG/dextran aqueous two-phase systems (ATPS) encapsulated within giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) provided foundational insights into membrane wetting and the role of interfacial tension balance in determining wetting morphology18,19. More recent studies have delved deeper into the mechanisms by which coacervates interact with lipid membranes19–21. On the one hand, the interfacial assembly of lipids around coacervates creates hybrid protocells, combining a crowded interior able to accumulate chemicals with a physical barrier that prevents droplet coalescence and limits material exchange22–25. On the other hand, pioneering studies have started investigating interactions between biomolecular condensates or coacervates and model lipid membranes using GUVs. In a seminal example, electrostatic interactions and hydrophobic lipid bilayer anchoring were shown to promote coacervate binding to membranes26. More recently, charge-dependent wetting of GUVs by coacervates was reported to modulate lipid packing27 and induce membrane remodeling28, with examples showing that strong coacervate-vesicle interactions favored partial or complete endocytosis of the droplets within the GUVs29,30.

These observations represent the first steps towards elucidating coacervate-membrane interactions based on non-specific interactions. However, cellular membranes are composed of complex lipid mixtures, where lipid phase separation can give rise to domains enriched in specific lipid species. This complexity, driven by the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of biological membranes, raises fundamental questions about the role of lipid composition, membrane fluidity and domain formation in modulating coacervate-membrane interactions. In this context, the interplay between lipid phase separation within bilayers and LLPS remains largely unexplored. Recent work has shown a dynamic interplay between domain formation in ternary lipid membranes and LLPS using PEG/dextran systems31. In parallel, a study revealed that condensates preferentially interact with liquid-disordered domains in phase-separated membranes, highlighting the need to understand how both lipid composition and the chemical nature of the condensate control this selectivity32.

Here, we assemble GUVs using high- and low-melting temperature lipids, forming either liquid-disordered or liquid-ordered bilayers. We investigate their interactions with polyelectrolyte-based coacervates prepared at varying positive-to-negative charge ratios. We find that electrostatic interactions are not the primary driver of coacervate-vesicle interactions; rather, the lipid composition dictates the preferential affinity of coacervates for more fluid membranes. We identify conditions where coacervates selectively wet one type of GUVs in a binary vesicle population. Additionally, using phase-separated vesicles with micron-sized lipid domains, we demonstrate that coacervates selectively wet fluid domains, irrespective of net surface charges. We further demonstrate that such wetting is reversibly modulated by temperature, reflecting changes in lipid mixing. Importantly, we find that selective domain wetting can be inverted depending on the chemical nature of the coacervate, revealing that coacervate chemistry provides an additional lever, complementary to membrane composition, for tuning membrane affinity. Altogether, these insights underscore the potential of lipid phase separation to mediate selective membrane wetting by coacervates, offering a compelling framework for understanding the role of condensate-membrane interactions in cells and in simplified cell-like model systems.

Results

Surface charge does not dictate selective coacervate-membrane interactions

Our model coacervate droplets were assembled by mixing poly(diallyldimethylammonium chloride) (PDDA, Mw = 8300 g mol-1, 5–20 mM expressed in charge concentration) and poly(sodium acrylate) (PAA, Mw = 5100 g mol-1) close to charge stoichiometry in water at pH 8 (Fig. 1a). Optical microscopy images of the obtained turbid suspension confirmed the formation of liquid-like micrometer-sized coacervate droplets (Fig. 1b). Turbidity screening revealed that optimal coacervation conditions were met at charge neutrality, although some turbidity persisted for a PDDA/PAA molar charge ratio comprised between 0.6 and 2 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The coacervate surface charge changed from negative to positive by tuning the polyelectrolyte charge ratio within this range (Fig. 2a). Model GUVs were prepared using the lipid film hydration method in a 200 mM sucrose buffer, first employing either DOPC (Tm = −20 °C) or DPPC (Tm = +41.3 °C). Due to their low melting temperature, DOPC phospholipids formed membranes in a liquid disordered phase at room temperature33,34 (ℓd, Fig. 1c). In contrast, the high rigidity of DPPC membranes at room temperature (gel phase33) hindered proper GUV formation, even when lipid film hydration was performed at 60 °C (Supplementary Fig. 2). To address this, 30 mol% cholesterol was added during DPPC lipid film formation to increase membrane fluidity and produce a liquid-ordered lipid phase35,36 (ℓo, Fig. 1d), allowing for a higher GUV production yield. The DOPC- and DPPC-based GUVs were labeled with red fluorescent (Rho-PE, 0.1 mol%, Fig. 1e, coloring to magenta was used) and green fluorescent (NBD-PE, 1 mol%, Fig. 1f) lipids, respectively. Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching confirmed the higher lateral mobility of the lipids within DOPC membranes compared to DPPC ones (Fig. 2c). Both types of vesicles exhibited a negative net charge (Fig. 2b), in line with previous reports37,38, with a slightly less negative ζ-potential observed in DPPC GUVs likely due to the addition of the fluorescently labeled NBD-PE lipid (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We next mixed GUVs and coacervates in an imaging chamber to investigate their interactions using confocal fluorescence microscopy. We noted that a small fraction (approximately 20%) of the GUVs broke during this mixing step, possibly due to osmotic stress or shearing by pipetting. Nevertheless, a sufficient fraction of GUVs remained intact and was used to characterize interactions with coacervates. As an example, coacervates prepared at a PDDA/PAA molar charge ratio of 0.7 (negative surface charge) appeared to partially wet DOPC vesicles (Fig. 1g) but did not interact with DPPC/cholesterol vesicles (Fig. 1h). Since both types of GUVs carried a net negative ζ-potential (Fig. 2b), these preliminary observations suggested that differences in electrostatic attraction could not account for the selective interaction of coacervates with DOPC vesicles.

To test this hypothesis further, we then systematically varied the PDDA/PAA molar charge ratio, thereby altering the net surface charge of the coacervates (Fig. 2a), and monitored their interactions with GUVs. We found that coacervates prepared with a large excess of PAA (8 mM; ζ < 0 mV) did not interact with any of the GUVs (ζ < 0 mV), as indicated by the coexistence of non-adhering and non-deformed droplets and vesicles (Fig. 2d(i,iii)). This suggested that electrostatic repulsion between same-charge coacervates and GUVs suppressed adhesion. In contrast, coacervates prepared at equimolar or lower amounts of PAA ( ≤ 5 mM; ζ > 0 mV) interacted with both types of GUVs (ζ < 0 mV), consistent with electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged compartments. Under these conditions, we only observed partial wetting, which primarily caused deformations to the lipid membranes while maintaining the overall spherical shape of the droplets. Most importantly, selective wetting of DOPC vesicles occurred only for a specific polyelectrolyte ratio (PDDA/PAA = 0.7) where coacervates exhibited a net negative surface charge (same as for GUVs). This observation suggests that interaction selectivity is driven by non-electrostatic factors, such as short-range interactions or differences in membrane physical properties.

Taken together, these results show that electrostatic interactions influence coacervate-GUV adhesion at the extremes of coacervate charge (highly negative coacervates repel GUVs, while positive coacervates adhere to both types of vesicles). However, the selective wetting of DOPC vesicles observed only within a narrow range of mildly negative coacervates cannot be explained by electrostatics. This indicates that additional factors govern the selective interaction between coacervates and GUVs.

To investigate further the role of membrane fluidity on coacervate wetting, we prepared DOPC GUVs containing 30 mol% cholesterol, which produced membranes with a slightly reduced fluidity compared to pure DOPC vesicles, as confirmed by FRAP (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2), while still exhibiting a net negative surface charge (Fig. 2b). Similar to all other GUVs, confocal fluorescence imaging revealed that coacervates did not interact with these vesicles at low PDDA/PAA molar charge ratio but partially wetted them above a ratio of 0.8 (Fig. 2d(ii)). Across this range of PDDA/PAA ratios, the geometric factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varPhi$$\end{document}Φ plateaued at ~0.5–0.6 and θin at ~50–60°, indicating an intermediate wetting propensity that more closely resembled DPPC/cholesterol vesicles than pure DOPC. Notably, unlike pure DOPC vesicles, DOPC/cholesterol GUVs did not interact with coacervates prepared at a PDDA/PAA ratio of 0.7 (Fig. 3b, c). These findings indicated that addition of cholesterol to DOPC membranes, which produced more ordered lipid bilayers, reduced the propensity of coacervates to interact with GUVs.

Coacervates selectively wet fluid lipid domains

Initially, we simply mixed a binary population of DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles with coacervate droplets prepared at a PDDA/PAA molar charge ratio of 0.7. In these conditions, we observed that coacervates interacted selectively with DOPC GUVs (Fig. 4a), mirroring results in individual GUV populations. Building upon the well-established ternary DOPC/DPPC/cholesterol phase diagram33,40, we then moved to preparing phase-separated GUVs exhibiting DOPC- and DPPC-rich domains at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 6). We mixed these phase-separated GUVs with coacervate droplets prepared at a PDDA/PAA molar charge ratio of 0.7. Coacervates appeared to selectively wet the DOPC-rich domains on GUVs (Fig. 4b). We repeated these observations at varying PDDA/PAA molar charge ratio (Fig. 4c). Similar to DOPC, DOPC/cholesterol and DPPC/cholesterol, excess PAA (8 mM) resulted in non-wetting of GUVs. Partial wetting, associated with a geometric factor lower than 1 and an intrinsic contact angle of ca. 60° (Fig. 3b, c), was observed for PDDA/PAA molar charge ratios higher than 0.7, similar to pure DOPC vesicles. Specifically, for lower PAA concentrations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varPhi$$\end{document}Φ decreased toward ~0.2 and θin increased to ~70-80° on DOPC-rich domains, indicating a higher wetting propensity than for DOPC/cholesterol domains but lower than for pure DOPC vesicles. This likely reflects the partitioning of cholesterol into DPPC-rich domains40, making DOPC-rich domains more fluid than DOPC/cholesterol but less fluid than pure DOPC. Importantly, for all these ratios, we observed that coacervates selectively wetted DOPC-rich domains (Fig. 4c), despite the affinity of coacervates for both individual DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles. Statistical analyses confirmed these observations, showing a > 95% preference for DOPC domains at a PDDA/PAA ratio of 0.7 (Fig. 4d). When this ratio was increased, the selective wetting of DOPC domains persisted, although with a slightly decreased preference ( ~ 75%). Time-lapse imaging at a PDDA/PAA ratio of 1 showed that coacervate wetting reached a stable configuration within 30 s–3 min after initial contact and remained unchanged for at least 20 min (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Movies 1-2). During this brief transient period, some coacervates initially adhered at the boundary between the two lipid phases, followed by lipid rearrangement that relocated the droplets onto the DOPC-rich regions, indicating that selective wetting was dynamically established through short-lived membrane reorganization.

We further explored the effect of temperature on selective wetting using PDDA/PAA coacervates at a fixed molar charge ratio of 1. Phase-separated GUVs were heated up to 50 °C and subsequently cooled down to room temperature while monitoring coacervate-membrane interactions by confocal microscopy (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Movie 3). Upon heating, mixing of the two lipid domains was detected at 34 °C–35 °C in the coacervate-free membrane segment (Supplementary Fig. 8). This lipid mixing was accompanied by a gradual decrease in coacervate wetting propensity, reflected by a smooth two-fold increase in the geometric factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varPhi$$\end{document}Φ (Fig. 4f) and ca. 1.2-fold decrease in the intrinsic contact angle (Fig. 4g). Upon cooling, lipid domains re-emerged at around 34 °C–35 °C (Supplementary Fig. 8), concomitant with the restoration of the initial wetting configuration and a sharp decrease and increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varPhi$$\end{document}Φ and θin, respectively, toward their initial value below 34°C. We note a hysteresis between heating and cooling, which may reflect the coupling between lipid phase behavior and droplet adhesion; however, additional experiments would be required to establish the origin of this effect. Importantly, reversibility indicates that selective wetting of DOPC domains is an equilibrium property and not a kinetically trapped state. Statistical analyses on six different GUVs confirmed a heterogeneous yet significant two-fold increase of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varPhi$$\end{document}Φ (Fig. 4h) and ca. 1.2-fold decrease of θin (Fig. 4i) at 50 °C compared to room temperature. In comparison, control experiments using homogeneous DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles showed no significant temperature dependence of wetting (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Fig. 4h, i). These observations indicate that temperature modulates wetting primarily through changes in membrane organization due to lipid mixing rather than through membrane fluidization or an effect on coacervate properties. In particular, the reduced wetting observed at elevated temperature for Janus-like GUVs is consistent with lipid mixing diminishing the contrast between fluid and ordered membrane environments that underlies selective wetting.

Overall, these observations indicate that PDDA/PAA coacervates preferentially wetted more fluid lipid domains on phase-separated GUVs. This behavior aligns with a recent study showing that lipid packing primarily determines protein- and peptide-based coacervates affinity for fluid membranes32. Our results further show that this preference persists across PDDA/PAA ratios, although the extent of wetting increases at lower PAA concentrations. Temperature-controlled experiments reveal that mixing of lipid domains above the miscibility transition reversibly decreased coacervate wetting. Taken together, these findings highlight that coacervate-membrane interactions are strongly influenced by membrane fluidity, with the strength of wetting modulated by coacervate composition and temperature, while the preference for fluid domains remains consistent.

Chemical composition of coacervates modulates wetting selectivity

Last, to assess the generality of this selective wetting behavior, we tested several other coacervates which ζ-potential was also assessed (Supplementary Fig. 10). First, we replaced PAA with carboxymethyl-dextran (CM-dex, Mw = 20 kg mol−1) to produce PDDA/CM-dextran coacervates. These droplets exhibited a wetting behavior similar to that of PDDA/PAA coacervates (Fig. 5a, e), namely, no wetting of GUVs at high polyanion concentrations (ζ < 0 mV) and selective wetting of DOPC domains at lower concentrations (ζ > 0 mV, Fig. 5b, f). Next, we tested coacervates assembled from PDDA and a low molecular weight anion, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which ζ-potential remained slightly negative across ratios (Supplementary Fig. 10). At low ATP concentrations, the coacervates selectively wetted the DOPC domain, similar to PDDA/PAA droplets. However, when ATP was used in excess, we observed that coacervates wetted preferentially DPPC domains (Fig. 5c), with a selectivity higher than 90% (Fig. 5g). Finally, we substituted PDDA with diethylaminoethyl-dextran (DEAE-dex Mw = 500 kg mol−1) to generate DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates. These droplets displayed a markedly different wetting behavior: while no wetting of GUVs occurred at low polycation concentration (ζ < 0 mV), higher concentrations (ζ > 0 mV) led to the preferential wetting of less fluid DPPC domain (Fig. 5d, h), together with complete wetting at the highest DEAE-dex concentration (Supplementary Fig. 11). These results highlight that the chemical nature of the coacervating species influences selectivity towards specific lipid domains. Three distinct behaviors emerged: (i) no wetting at low polycation/polyanion ratios and selective wetting of DOPC domains at higher ratios (PDDA/PAA and PDDA/CM-dextran); (ii) selective wetting of DPPC domains at low polycation/polyanion ratios and selective wetting of DOPC domains at higher ratios (PDDA/ATP); (iii) no wetting at low polycation/polyanion ratios and selective wetting of DPPC domains at higher ratios (DEAE-dextran/PAA).

Control experiments on pure DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles were performed for all conditions to assess whether the domain selectivity observed on phase-separated GUVs reflected an intrinsic preference for one membrane composition (Fig. 5a–d). PDDA/CM-dex coacervates behaved identically to PDDA/PAA: selective wetting of the DOPC-rich domain on phase-separated GUVs was accompanied by wetting of both pure DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles, whereas conditions showing no wetting on Janus-like GUVs also showed no wetting of either pure vesicle type. PDDA/ATP coacervates exhibited two regimes: under conditions where they selectively wetted the DOPC-rich domain on phase-separated GUVs, they wetted both pure vesicle types, similar to PDDA/PAA and PDDA/CM-dex; in contrast, when they selectively wetted the DPPC-rich domain on Janus-like GUVs, they wetted DPPC/cholesterol but not DOPC vesicles, revealing a genuine preference for DPPC-rich membranes. DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates showed a similarly strong DPPC selectivity, wetting DPPC/cholesterol but not DOPC vesicles whenever wetting occurred on Janus-like GUVs.

These interactions were quantified via the geometric factor and intrinsic contact angle analyses for one select polyion ratio (Supplementary Fig. 12). Specifically, we fixed the polycation/polyanion ratio to explore different wetting scenarios, including selective wetting of DOPC domains (PDDA/PAA: 5 mM/5 mM; PDDA/CM-dex: 5 mM/2.5 mg mL−1; and PDDA/ATP: 5 mM/2.5 mM), and selective wetting of DPPC domains (DEAE-dex/PAA: 2.5 mg mL−1/5 mM). Interestingly, even when no clear preference for either pure DOPC or DPPC/cholesterol vesicles was apparent from confocal images, geometric factor and intrinsic contact angle analyses revealed a consistent trend: the domain selectively wetted on phase-separated GUVs corresponded to the membrane composition that exhibited slightly lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varPhi$$\end{document}Φ and highest θin on individual vesicles. While these differences were modest, they suggest that the domain selected on phase-separated membranes is always the more-wetted of the two individual membrane types. Together, these controls demonstrate that, depending on the chemical nature of the coacervating species, selective wetting on phase-separated GUVs can reflect either a weak relative bias between two otherwise wettable membranes or, in some cases, an exclusive affinity for one membrane composition.

To rationalize these observations further, we characterized different coacervate properties, including their critical salt concentration (CSC)—conventionally used as an indicator of the coacervate robustness –, interfacial tension, and inverse capillary velocity—the ratio of viscosity to surface tension (see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Tables 3-5).

CSC values showed no clear correlation with wetting behavior (Fig. 5i). For instance, PDDA/PAA (CSC = 356 ± 42 mM), PDDA/CM-dex (CSC = 118 ± 6 mM) and PDDA/ATP (CSC = 48 ± 2 mM) had significantly different CSC values, despite both selectively wetting DOPC domains. In contrast, interfacial tension displayed a pronounced difference between coacervates with distinct wetting selectivity. DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates, which wetted DPPC/cholesterol-rich domains, exhibited an ultra-low interfacial tension ( ~ 2.5 μN m−1), markedly lower than that of coacervates selectively wetting DOPC-rich domains ( ~ 35–90 μN m-1) (Fig. 5j). In parallel, inverse capillary velocity measurements showed that DEAE-dex/PAA also exhibited an order-of-magnitude higher η/γ compared to PDDA/PAA, PDDA/CM-dex and PDDA/ATP (Fig. 5k), resulting in a viscosity intermediate between that of PDDA/ATP or PDDA/CM-dextran and PDDA/PAA systems (Fig. 5l and Supplementary Table 6). Hence, coacervate viscosity did not show any correlation with wetting selectivity. Viscosity is indeed expected to primarily affect wetting dynamics rather than equilibrium wetting configurations.

Last, using the measured coacervate interfacial tensions, we calculated the coacervate-membrane (Σcm) and membrane-dilute phase (Σmd) segment tensions associated with wetting on pure DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 14). For PDDA/PAA and PDDA/ATP coacervates, which selectively wet DOPC-rich domains on phase-separated GUVs, Σcm (resp. Σmd) was slightly lower (resp. higher) on DOPC compared to DPPC/cholesterol vesicles, suggesting a slight preference for coacervates to wet DOPC membranes, although the differences were modest. For PDDA/CM-dex coacervates, both Σcm and Σmd were slightly lower on DPPC/cholesterol vesicles than on DOPC vesicles, despite preferential wetting of DOPC-rich domains on phase-separated membranes. In contrast, DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates, which selectively wet DPPC-rich domains, displayed very small absolute values of both Σcm and Σmd. Wetting by DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates was observed on DPPC/cholesterol vesicles but not on DOPC vesicles, precluding a direct quantitative comparison of segment tensions between the two membrane compositions in this case. Overall, across the different coacervate systems investigated, segment tensions measured on pure DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles differed only modestly and did not exhibit a consistent correspondence with the domain selectivity observed on phase-separated GUVs when considered independently of other membrane or coacervate properties.

Taken together, these results reveal a clear empirical distinction between coacervates displaying different wetting behaviors. DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates, which are characterized by an ultra-low coacervate interfacial tension, are the only system observed (i) to selectively wet DPPC-rich domains and to fail to wet DOPC domains in phase-separated GUVs, (ii) to completely wet GUVs at a given polyion ratio. In contrast, coacervates with substantially higher interfacial tensions systematically wet preferentially DOPC-rich domains in phase-separated GUVs. Segment tension differences measured on individual DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol vesicles are small and do not systematically correlate with domain selectivity, indicating that they do not, by themselves, account for the observed selective wetting.

Discussion

Early studies using polymer-based ATPS, such as PEG/dextran established the physical principles of droplet-membrane wetting18,19,31, yet they lacked the chemical specificity inherent to biomolecular condensates and coacervates. Recent work has begun to elucidate our understanding of how condensates interact with lipid membranes, emphasizing the role of membrane properties, such as net charge27 and cholesterol content32, in governing the wetting behavior of condensates.

Building upon this membrane-centric perspective, our study adds a complementary view by revealing that coacervate stoichiometry and chemical composition strongly modulate selective membrane wetting. We observe that at low polycation/polyanion molar ratios, coacervates do not exhibit any GUV wetting. As the molar ratio increases, wetting of GUVs emerges, though the threshold ratio at which this occurs varied between different coacervates systems. Importantly, different coacervates display distinct wetting selectivities toward liquid-disordered (DOPC-rich) or liquid-ordered (DPPC-rich) membrane domains in phase-separated GUVs, highlighting that membrane composition alone is insufficient to predict wetting behavior.

By quantifying geometric factors, intrinsic contact angles, and segment tensions on homogeneous vesicles, we find that differences between DOPC and DPPC/cholesterol membranes are modest and do not systematically correlate with the domain selectivity observed on phase-separated GUVs. These observations indicate that selective wetting cannot be explained solely by local interfacial energetics extracted from homogeneous membranes. In particular, the membrane-coacervate and membrane-dilute phase segment tensions measured on individual vesicles differ only weakly between the two membrane compositions and therefore cannot, on their own, account for the pronounced selective wetting observed in ternary, phase-separated GUVs.

Membrane mechanics provides an additional contribution that influences wetting selectivity by modulating the energetic cost of membrane deformation during droplet adhesion. Wetting requires membrane deformation, which incurs a bending energy cost governed by the membrane bending rigidity and the curvature imposed by the droplet. For coacervates with higher interfacial tension (in the tens of µN m-1), selective wetting is consistent with an elastocapillary balance in which wetting is favored on more deformable DOPC-rich domains, where the bending energy penalty associated with membrane deformation is lower compared to stiffer DPPC/cholesterol-rich domains, which resist deformation.

Consistent with our findings, recent work has shown that lipid packing, as opposed to membrane phase identity per se, governs the wetting affinity of biomolecular condensates on model membranes32. In that study, protein- and peptide-based condensates exhibited progressively reduced wetting as lipid packing increased, and in phase-separated vesicles preferentially interacted with more fluid, less tightly packed liquid-disordered domains over more ordered or gel domains. This behavior was attributed to the increased accessibility of lipid headgroups in these less packed domains, facilitating interactions with condensates, as well as the reduced bending rigidity of these domains, which lowers the energetic cost of membrane deformation and promotes condensate adhesion. Although our study focuses on synthetic polyelectrolyte coacervates, the qualitative agreement with these protein-based systems suggests that selective wetting of membrane domains may be a general feature of phase-separated droplets interacting with heterogeneous membranes.

Yet, in our work, DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates constitute a distinct case. Their ultra-low interfacial tension places the system close to the complete-wetting threshold41, consistent with our observation of complete wetting at specific polyion ratios. In this ultra-low-interfacial-tension regime, the energetic differences between alternative wetting configurations are small, rendering wetting behavior particularly sensitive to variations in local membrane properties. Under these conditions, membrane elasticity may no longer simply penalize deformation but instead bias which wetting geometries are mechanically stable. One preliminary hypothesis is that the higher bending rigidity of DPPC/cholesterol-rich domains may constrain membrane deformation in a way that stabilizes specific droplet-membrane contact geometries, leading to an apparent preference for these domains. In this framework, domain selectivity arises from the interplay between coacervate interfacial energetics and membrane mechanical properties, with their relative importance depending on the magnitude of the coacervate interfacial tension. Direct measurements of membrane bending energies, local curvature, and droplet-induced deformation would be required to test this mechanism quantitatively.

The behavior of PDDA/ATP coacervates further highlights the importance of chemical specificity in selective membrane wetting. Depending on stoichiometry, these coacervates exhibit either DOPC- or DPPC-domain wetting, despite interfacial tensions comparable to other DOPC-wetting systems. One possible explanation for this behavior lies in the specific interaction of ATP with lipid membranes. ATP has been reported to interact with phosphatidylcholine bilayers through the hydrophobic effect, influencing membrane organization and fluidity42. In particular, at concentrations above 5 mM ATP has been shown to increase lipid fluidity in DMPC bilayers42. The switch in wetting selectivity observed for ATP-rich coacervates may therefore reflect ATP-induced changes in the local membrane environment and thus membrane-coacervate interfacial energies. While our data do not allow us to isolate the molecular origin of this effect, they demonstrate that coacervate composition can qualitatively alter wetting selectivity even within a similar interfacial tension regime.

An additional consideration is that coacervate suspensions necessarily contain coacervate components in the dilute phase. Varying the polycation/polyanion ratio not only changes the properties of the droplets, but also alters the concentration of free polyions in the surrounding solution. These species may interact with lipid membranes independently of droplet contact and could influence membrane properties or interfacial tensions, as in the case of ATP. While the present experiments do not disentangle contributions from condensed and dilute phases, this observation highlights an additional layer of complexity inherent to coacervate-membrane systems.

Together, these results show that selective wetting of phase-separated membranes by coacervates cannot be attributed to membrane composition, surface charge, or coacervate properties in isolation. Instead, wetting selectivity emerges from the specific combination of coacervate chemistry and membrane phase context, which determines whether stable wetting occurs on a given domain. Our analysis indicates that differences in local interfacial tensions measured on homogeneous membranes are insufficient to account for the observed domain selectivity, pointing to additional contributions that arise specifically in phase-separated membranes. This work therefore highlights the importance of considering both coacervate composition and membrane heterogeneity when interpreting or designing coacervate-membrane wetting phenomena in synthetic cells. Extending these experiments to condensates formed from biologically relevant polyelectrolytes, such as RNA or intrinsically disordered proteins, and to physiological ionic conditions, will be essential to assess the functional implications of selective wetting in cellular contexts.

To conclude, our findings advance the understanding of coacervate-membrane interactions by demonstrating how lipid phase separation within bilayers can mediate selective wetting behaviors. Using GUVs composed of liquid-ordered and liquid-disordered phases, we show that lipid domain properties, including fluidity, modulate coacervate affinity, beyond net surface charge or purely electrostatic interactions. This selective wetting behavior depends on the chemical nature of the coacervates, as different coacervate compositions exhibit distinct preferences for liquid-ordered or liquid-disordered domains. Our findings underscore the complex interplay between coacervate properties and lipid membrane organization, pointing to the role of interfacial energetics and membrane mechanics in shaping wetting selectivity, while also highlighting potential contributions from specific molecular interactions, such as those involving ATP. By revealing how lipid composition and phase separation modulate coacervate-vesicle interactions, this work highlights the intricate interplay between LLPS and lipid membranes. These insights may have implications for understanding the functional roles of biomolecular condensates in cellular environments, where complex lipid mixtures and domain formation are ubiquitous. Furthermore, the observed tunability of coacervate wetting behaviors by coacervate composition offers a perspective on protocell-inspired model systems, where the interplay between lipid phase separation and LLPS could help shape compartmental organization. This study lays the groundwork for future investigations into how the coacervate chemistry and membrane heterogeneity jointly influence the interfacial organization in cellular and minimal synthetic systems.

Methods

Materials

The phospholipids, 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC), 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(lissamine rhodamine B sulfonyl) (ammonium salt) (Rho-PE), 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(7-nitro-2-1,3-benzoxadiazol-4-yl) (ammonium salt) (NBD-PE) were purchased from Avanti polar lipids (IL, USA). Hydrochloric acid (CAS: 7647-01-0), sodium hydroxide (CAS: 1310-73-2), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (CAS: 9002-89-5), cholesterol (CAS: 57-88-5), adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) disodium salt hydrate (CAS: 34369-07-8) and poly(acrylic acid sodium salt) [PAA, Mw = 5100 g mol-1] (CAS: 9003-04-7), DEAE-dextran hydrochloride [DEAE-dex, Mw = 500,000 g mol-1] (CAS:9064-91-9) and CM-dextran sodium salt [CM-dex, Mw = 10,000–20,000 g mol-1] (CAS:39422-83-8) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. PDMS (Sylgard 184) was purchased from Dow Corning. Poly(diallyldimethylammonium chloride) [PDDA, Mw = 8500 g mol-1, 28 wt. % in H2O] (CAS: 26062-79-3) was purchased from Polysciences. Cyanine 5-labeled single stranded DNA (Cy5-ssDNA) was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (the sequence is provided in Supplementary Table 1).

Giant vesicle preparation

GUVs were prepared by the modified lipid film hydration method as reported elsewhere43. Briefly, 50 μL of the desired lipid or lipid mixture solution at 4 mM in chloroform were spin coated onto a glass slide at 1000 rpm for 120 s at room temperature. The lipid compositions were as follow: 99.9 mol% DOPC + 0.1 mol% Rho-PE (DOPC), 69.9 mol% DOPC + 30 mol% cholesterol + 0.1 mol% Rho-PE (DOPC/cholesterol), 99 mol% DPPC + 1 mol% NBD-PE (DPPC), 69 mol% DPPC + 30 mol% cholesterol + 1 mol% NBD-PE (DPPC/cholesterol), 34.9 mol% DOPC + 34 mol% DPPC + 30 mol% cholesterol + 0.1 mol% Rho-PE + 1 mol% NBD-PE (DOPC/DPPC/cholesterol). The spin coated lipid film on the glass slide was dried under vacuum for 1 h to remove any traces of chloroform. A rectangular chamber was assembled using a PTFE spacer (25 ×35 x 5 mm), filled with 500 μL of a 200 mM sucrose solution (swelling solution), and closed with a second glass slide held with two binder clips. The dried lipid film was hydrated with this swelling solution for 30 min at 25 °C for DOPC and DOPC/cholesterol lipid films and 60 °C for DPPC/cholesterol and DOPC/DPPC/cholesterol lipid films using a heating plate equipped with a temperature controller (IKA RCT standard 2.0). The resulting suspension of GUVs was transferred to Eppendorf tubes shielded from light to avoid photobleaching and stored at room temperature for a maximum duration of one week.

Preparation of stock solutions for coacervate formulation

Stock solutions of PDDA, PAA and ATP were prepared at 100 mM or 1 M (expressed in monomer concentrations) by diluting the 28 wt% PDDA solution or dissolving the appropriate amount of PAA or ATP powder using Milli-Q water and adjusting the pH to 8.0 with NaOH (1 M) or HCl (1 M). The concentration of ATP was checked via UV-Vis spectroscopy (PerkinElmer) at 260 nm using an excitation co-efficient of 15.4 × 103 L mol-1 cm-1. Carboxymethyl (CM)-dextran and diethylaminoethyl (DEAE)-dextran stock solutions were made at 50–100 mg mL-1 in Milli-Q water and the pH adjusted to 8.0. Given that the fraction of charge units on these polysaccharides is not defined, we do not provide a charge concentration for these two polymers but keep the concentration as mg mL-1. All stock solutions were stored at 4 °C, expect for ATP where aliquots were stored at −20 °C.

Coacervate characterization

Turbidity screening

The optimal polymer ratio for PDDA/PAA coacervate formation was determined by measuring the turbidity at 700 nm of 20 µL solutions prepared at fixed (5 mM) PDDA and variable PAA concentrations (0.5–50 mM) in sucrose/glucose medium (90 mM each, final concentration) using a 384-well plate (Corning, flat bottom) and a microplate spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices). For critical salt concentration (CSC) determination, 20 µL coacervates suspensions were prepared at 10 mM PDDA and 10 mM, 5 mM or 5 mg mL-1 PAA, ATP, CM-dextran, respectively; or 10 mM PAA and 4 mg mL-1 DEAE-dextran in sucrose/glucose medium (90 mM each, final concentration) and varying NaCl concentrations and the turbidity determined at 700 nm. The data was fitted with a sigmoidal logistic function. The CSC was determined by extrapolating the first-order derivative at the inflection point to zero turbidity.

Interfacial tension measurements

The interfacial tension of the different coacervates were estimated by the interfacial profile between the dense coacervate phase and dilute supernatant after macroscopic phase separation in a cell with parallel walls at room temperature, as described in ref. 44. Coacervate suspensions were here prepared at high concentration to produce sufficient amount of bulk coacervate phase, specifically, for PDDA/PAA: 200 mM PDDA and 200 mM PAA; for PDDA/ATP: 60 mM PDDA and 30 mM ATP; for PDDA/CM-dextran: 50 mM PDDA and 25 mg mL-1 CM-dextran; for DEAE-dextran/PAA: 40 mg mL-1 DEAE-dextran and 100 mM PAA. All systems except PDDA/ATP were formulated in sucrose/glucose medium (90 mM each, final concentration). These suspensions were placed into a 1 mm thick quartz cell (Hellma), then centrifuged at 1400 ×g for 5 minutes to favor complete macroscopic phase separation, resulting in a net interface between a denser polymer-rich (coacervate) phase and a polymer-dilute (supernatant) upper phase. Zeta potential measurements

The ζ-potential of coacervate suspensions and of lipid vesicles (with and without dyes) were quantified at room temperature using a Malvern Zetasizer (Nano ZS) device. Dispersions of coacervates were prepared immediately prior to the experiments at 5 mM PDDA final concentration and varying PAA (2.5, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 mM), CM-dextran (2.5, 5, 10 mg mL-1) and ATP (2.5, 5, 8 mM) concentrations, or at 5 mM PAA final concentration and varying DEAE-dextran concentrations (1.5, 2, 2.5 mg mL-1), in sucrose/glucose medium (90 mM each, final concentration). Five measurements were performed for each sample, and the average value and standard deviation reported. For GUVs, measurements were conducted following the approach of Dimova et al47. adapted from Carvalho et al48. Briefly, the voltage for electrophoretic movement was set to 150 V in the instrument settings to limit vesicle disruption, and only measurements on populations fitting to the instruments quality criteria were considered. Five measurements were performed for each sample, and the average value and standard deviation reported.

Preparation and surface functionalization of imaging chambers

Custom-made PDMS-based chambers with a glass coverslip at the bottom were prepared for imaging, and glass coverslips were functionalized using polyvinylalcohol (PVA) to prevent coacervate spreading onto the glass surface. Briefly, PDMS was prepared by thoroughly mixing Part A and Part B in 10:1 ratio using a Sylgard 184 silicone elastomer kit, followed by crosslinking at 55 °C for 12 h. The cross-linked PDMS was cut into rectangular blocks (40 × 20 mm), in which a 8 mm-diameter hole was punched. These blocks were subjected to oxygen activation using a plasma cleaner (Pico, Diener Electronic) at 80% power for 2 min. Glass coverslips (24 × 36 mm) were also plasma activated using oxygen. The plasma treated PDMS blocks and glass coverslips were sealed and cross-linked at 55 °C for 1 h, resulting in homemade imaging chambers (Supplementary Fig. 15).

A 2 wt% PVA solution was prepared by dissolving the desired amount of powder in Milli-Q water and stirred for 6 h using a magnetic stirrer. The assembled imaging chambers underwent plasma treatment for 2 min at 80% power, then 200 µL of the PVA solution were added to the chambers for 15 min to create a thin PVA coating. The solution was removed, then the chambers were dried at 110 °C for 15 min. This PVA coating process was repeated three times to achieve stable functionalization.

Imaging of coacervate-vesicle interactions

Vesicles suspensions prepared in 200 mM sucrose were diluted with an isotonic 200 mM glucose solution (1:1 v/v) to facilitate density mismatch-based sedimentation of GUVs on the glass coverslip in the imaging chamber. PDDA/polyanion coacervates were prepared in an Eppendorf tube by adding varying volumes of polyanion stock solution (100 mM PAA or ATP, and 50 mg mL-1 CM-dextran) to 10 µL of PDDA stock solution (100 mM), then added to the vesicle suspension at a 10% v/v (final total volume of 200 µL) to reach a final PDDA concentration of 5 mM within the chamber and varying polyanion concentration. Similarly, DEAE-dextran/PAA coacervates were prepared in a test tube by adding varying volumes of DEAE-dextran stock solution (50 mg mL-1) to 10 µL of PAA stock solution (100 mM), then added to the vesicle suspension at a 10% v/v (final total volume of 200 µL) to reach a final PAA concentration of 5 mM within the chamber and varying DEAE-dextran concentration. In all conditions, the final sucrose and glucose concentrations were 90 mM each. Coacervate droplets were fluorescently labeled by adding Cy5-ssDNA (10 μM final concentration) to the droplet suspension. After gentle mixing the vesicle–coacervate suspension by pipetting, the sample was allowed to sediment at the bottom of the passivated imaging chamber for 10 min before imaging.

Epifluorescence and confocal fluorescence microscopy images were acquired on a Zeiss LSM 980 microscope using a ×63 oil immersion lens (HCX PL APO, 1.4 NA). Rho-PE, NBD-PE, and Cy5-ssDNA were excited using the 561 nm, 488 nm and 633 nm laser lines, respectively.

Wetting geometries quantification

All wetting geometries involve three aqueous solutions: the interior solution of the GUV, the exterior solution of the GUV and coacervates droplets. At the contact point, the apparent contact angles α, β, and γ sum up to 360° (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Geometric factor

A detailed explanation of the contact analysis and geometric parameters used in this study is reported elsewhere28,50,51. Briefly, the contact angle between the surface segments from the microscopy images requires the rotational axis of symmetry of the vesicle-coacervate system to lie on the 2D projected plane of the image. Any improper orientation can cause artifacts in the contact angle measurements. Because of this, we focused our analysis on vesicle-coacervate pairs that preserve the rotational axis of symmetry of the system by choosing systems where coacervates and GUVs have the same size (and therefore whose equatorial plane lies in the same z-plane).

Selective wetting quantification

Selective wetting behavior was assessed using confocal fluorescence imaging at the central plane of interacting coacervate–GUV systems. To ensure adequate sampling of binding events, image tiles of 20×20 confocal fields were acquired.

Selective wetting was defined as the preferential adhesion of a coacervate droplet to either the DOPC-rich or DPPC/cholesterol-rich membrane domain. Events in which coacervates exhibited complete wetting of the vesicle were not assigned to a specific membrane domain, as domain identity cannot be resolved in this configuration. These complete-wetting events are reported separately where relevant (e.g., for DEAE-dex/PAA coacervates).

Temperature-controlled wetting experiments

Rectangular glass capillaries (200 µm × 2 mm × 5 cm; VitroCom) were PEGylated to prevent nonspecific adhesion via incubation in a 5 vol% PEG-silane (3-[methoxy(polyethyleneoxy)propyl]tri-methoxysilane, 90%, 6-9 PE units, abcr GmbH) solution in toluene for 48 h, then rinsing with ethanol and Milli-Q water.

Vesicles-coacervate mixtures were prepared as described above for imaging directly into a PDMS-based imaging chamber, where samples were allowed to settle for 15 min. A PEGylated glass capillary was then gently inserted into the chamber, positioned at the bottom, and filled by capillary action. The capillary was transferred onto a glass coverslip, and both ends were sealed using UV-curing adhesive (NOA63, Norland) to prevent evaporation and sample drift.

The sealed capillary was mounted on a custom-built temperature-controlled microscope stage. Samples were heated from 25 °C to 50 °C at a rate of 1 °C min-1, held at 50 °C for 10 min, and subsequently cooled back to 25 °C at the same rate. Confocal fluorescence microscopy images were acquired continuously during the thermal cycle using a Zeiss LSM980 microscope equipped with the Definite Focus system (Zeiss) to minimize focal drift during temperature changes, and using a long-working distance 63× air objective (Plan-Neofluor, 0.75 NA).
