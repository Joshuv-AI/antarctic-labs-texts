Bone Health Assessment and Management among Survivors of Non-metastatic Breast Cancer: A Narrative Review
Authors: Rebecca John, Kripa E. Cherian, Patricia Sebastian, Rajesh Balakrishnan, Selvamani Backianathan, Thomas V. Paul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer, the most common hormone responsive cancer, is on a rise both globally and in India. With increased longevity of breast cancer survivors, addressing long-term complications such as osteoporosis is crucial. Several mechanisms contribute to the bone loss seen in breast cancer patients, including age, post-menopausal status, chemotherapy, and other adjuvant therapies received. Appropriate evaluation and counselling on the risks of therapy with aromatase inhibitors on bone health are much needed. Timely initiation of anti-resorptive medication can help improve bone mass, reduce the incidence of fractures, and improve quality of life.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, the incidence of hormone-responsive cancers is increasing, contributing significantly to cancer-related morbidity and mortality.[1] The term “hormone-responsive cancers” typically refers to tumours which are hormone-dependent, and the hormone receptors present within the tumour tissue are targeted as part of therapeutic interventions.[2] It includes prostate carcinoma in men and breast, endometrial, and ovarian carcinoma in women. This narrative will focus on bone health among survivors of breast cancer, the most prevalent among hormone responsive malignancies and with the highest mortality rate.[1]

Cancer constitutes a significant risk factor for both generalised and localised bone loss, as well as for fractures.[34] This may result from the direct impact of cancer cells on bone or as a secondary consequence of the deleterious effects of chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and adjuvant endocrine therapy on bone.[3] Various combinations of chemotherapeutic agents are employed to target occult tumour cells, reduce disease burden, and enhance the likelihood of cure. With advancements in diagnostics and targeted treatment, a rise in longevity is noted, underscoring the importance of appropriately addressing bone health.

Data suggest that the prevalence of osteoporosis among post-menopausal women may be as high as 40–50%.[5] The annual rate of pre-menopausal bone loss has been estimated at 0.7–1.3% at the lumbar spine and 0.3% at the femoral neck.[6] Among post-menopausal women, the annual bone loss has been approximated around 1.3–1.5% at the lumbar spine and 1.4% at the femoral neck.[67] Around 80% of women diagnosed with breast cancer are post-menopausal.[8] In women who are pre-menopausal at the time of the diagnosis and receive chemotherapy, between 60% and 90% are likely to encounter premature menopause due to ovarian failure within the subsequent year.[9] Hence, survivors of breast cancer are at a significantly elevated risk of developing osteoporosis. In this review, we focus on the bone health of treated survivors of non-metastatic breast cancer.

METHODOLOGY

A detailed literature search was conducted utilising Pubmed and the Google search engines using the following MeSH “Breast Cancer,” AND “mechanisms of bone loss”, AND “Chemotherapy induced bone loss”, “Radiotherapy induced bone loss”, “Fractures”. Review articles and original studies in English language describing the bone loss, its mechanisms, and treatment in hormone responsive cancers were included in preparing this review.

Mechanism of bone loss in breast cancer

Besides the direct effect of cancer, low oestrogen levels due to hormone therapy and loss of ovarian function are contributory to bone loss in breast cancer treatment. The hypoestrogenic state may result from one or more of the following: (i) anti-oestrogen therapy, (ii) use of aromatase inhibitors, and (iii) chemotherapy-induced ovarian failure (CIOF) and bone loss.[101516] Radiation therapy is also identified to be a cause for bone depletion. Other detrimental mechanisms of chemotherapy are toxic effects of the drugs on the bone cells and lack of vitamin D, calcium, and other nutrients following nausea and vomiting.[17]

Epidemiology

Studies on the assessment of bone health in women undergoing breast cancer suggest an increased prevalence of osteoporosis and fractures. A study was conducted in Baltimore, USA, aimed at detection of bone loss by assessing bone mineral density (BMD) in breast cancer survivors compared to cancer-free women with a familial risk of cancer. The incidence of osteoporosis and osteopenia was significantly higher among young breast cancer survivors, with risk varied with the type of cancer treatment received.[18] Data from the Women’s Health Initiative among other studies have suggested the incidence rates of falls and fragility fractures increased after the diagnosis of breast cancer.[1920] A study conducted in Korea in breast cancer survivors showed lower hip BMD in the breast cancer group relative to controls with no difference in lumbar BMD or incidence of fractures.[21] Conde et al.[22] from Brazil reported a combined prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis at the femoral neck of 37.2% and lumbar spine of 56.9% of population. Artese AL et al.[23] from USA assessed body composition and BMD changes in 10 breast cancer survivors and 10 age-matched women over a 12- to 15-month period via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). The forearm BMD was noted to be lower among the breast cancer survivors.

Despite this, there is a lack of data from the Indian subcontinent. A prospective study conducted in South India recruited 109 post-menopausal individuals newly diagnosed with early and locally advanced breast cancer, who were scheduled for anthracycline and taxane-based chemotherapy. A significant association between cytotoxic chemotherapy and worsening of bone health, as assessed by BMD and FRAX (Fracture Risk Assessment Tool) scores, was demonstrated.[13]

Role of cancer chemotherapy: Conventional chemotherapeutic regimens, including Cyclophosphamide, Etoposide, and 5-Fluorouracil cycles, are associated with an intermediate to high risk of premature ovarian failure.[24] In patients with high-risk disease, cytotoxic therapy typically involves the use of both an anthracycline and a taxane. Conversely, in cases of low-risk disease, anthracyclines are more frequently omitted.[25]

The mechanism of chemotherapy-induced bone loss is age-related decreases in ovarian reserve related to reductions in the number and quality of ovarian follicles. Cyclophosphamide reduces secondary ovarian follicles resulting in total follicle loss with ovarian fibrosis, inducing premature menopause. CIOF is influenced by age and treatment parameters with low oestrogen levels during chemotherapy-induced menopause promoting osteoclast formation and increasing bone resorption.[26] Studies have shown that 5-FU induces significant trabecular bone loss, thereby enhancing bone resorption. Additionally, it markedly suppresses hematopoietic cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis in chondrocytes and osteoblasts. Epirubicin exhibits genotoxic effects on mouse bone marrow cells.[1327] The impact of regimens including taxanes and trastuzumab, a recombinant human monoclonal antibody targeting the Her-2 protein, on bone remains unclear at present.[17] Shapiro et al. have shown that in the context of CIOF, the rate of lumbar spine BMD loss can reach approximately 6.7% within 1 year, compared to the 1–2% annual loss observed following natural menopause.[2829]

Role of adjuvant therapy

Adjuvant endocrine therapy constitutes a fundamental aspect of the treatment regimen for oestrogen receptor positive (ER+) breast cancer. By reducing the availability of oestrogen to tumour cells, this therapy aims to inhibit further tumour growth. In pre-menopausal women, therapeutic options include oestrogen receptor blockade with tamoxifen, temporary suppression of ovarian oestrogen synthesis via gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists, or permanent cessation of ovarian oestrogen synthesis through oophorectomy or radiotherapy. AIs block the aromatisation of androgens to oestrogens and result in a decline in circulating peripheral oestrogen levels. Three generations of AI have developed over the years, and these can broadly be categorised as steroidal (exemestane) and non-steroidal inhibitors (letrozole and anastrozole).[31] At our institute, pre-menopausal women with oestrogen receptor positivity are given tamoxifen for at least 5 years. Treatment beyond 5 years can be with either tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor depending upon menopausal status.[32]

a. Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERM) and Aromatase Inhibitors (AIs): In a chemoprevention study conducted by Powles et al.[33] among healthy women, it was found that among pre-menopausal women treated with tamoxifen, BMD decreased progressively at the lumbar spine (P < 0.001) and at the hip (P < 0.05), but not in those on placebo. The mean annual loss in lumbar BMD per year over the 3-year study period in the tamoxifen-treated arm was 1.44% compared with a small gain of 0.24% per annum in the placebo arm. Tamoxifen had the opposite effect in post-menopausal women with a mean annual increase in BMD for women on tamoxifen of 1.17% in the spine and 1.71% in the hip (P < .005) compared with an insignificant loss for women on placebo.

The TEXT (Tamoxifen and Exemestane Trial) and SOFT trials (Suppression of Ovarian Function Trial) assessed the effects of tamoxifen and exemestane in pre-menopausal women. In the SOFT trial, participants were randomly allocated to receive either 5 years of tamoxifen, tamoxifen combined with ovarian suppression, or exemestane combined with ovarian suppression.[34] The TEXT trial allotted pre-menopausal women with HR+ early breast cancer to the receive ovarian suppression with either aromatase inhibitor exemestane or tamoxifen for a period of 5 years.[35] Osteoporosis was observed in 13.2% of patients in the exemestane arm compared to 6.4% of those receiving tamoxifen. Additionally, fractures were more frequently reported among patients treated with exemestane with ovarian suppression.[35]

The ATAC (Arimidex, Tamoxifen, Alone or in Combination) trial recruited 9366 patients with invasive operable breast cancer who had completed primary treatment. The subjects were allotted to receive anastrozole, tamoxifen, and combination therapy. The 10-year follow-up data suggest that while the Anastrozole-treated group showed a better disease-free survival, higher incidence of vertebral fracture was noted during the active treatment phase which became comparable to Tamoxifen in the post-treatment follow-up period.[36] AI leads to a 2–4-fold increased bone loss (2.6% per year at lumbar spine) compared to post-menopausal BMD loss, leading to an increased risk of fractures as well as morbidity and mortality.[37]

b. GnRH agonists: Leuprolide and Goserelin inhibit the pulsatile secretion of gonadotropins, luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) from the anterior pituitary gland, inducing ovarian suppression, commonly called chemical castration. While GnRH agonists’ effects on ovarian function are reversible, limited data exist regarding bone mass changes after treatment cessation. A Swedish study of 89 women with early-stage breast cancer showed a 5% decline in BMD after 2 years of Goserelin therapy. One year after stopping treatment, partial bone loss recovery occurred, with a 1.5% increase in BMD.[38] It is interesting to note that higher FSH levels in post-menopausal women have also been suggested to be linked to increased hip fractures independent of estradiol levels.[3940]

Radiation therapy: Radiation therapy is frequently used in breast cancer management for local tumour control in early-stage disease and palliation in advanced stages with bone metastases. By damaging the DNA of cancer cells, it destroys mitotic cells.[41] Despite advances in planning and techniques of focal radiation therapy, challenges with beam scatter persist and can affect local bone health. Radiation therapy by damaging the microvasculature of the bone results in stasis of osteoclasts and osteoblasts. It reduces osteoblast number and increases osteoblast apoptosis, leading to a decrease in bone formation.[42] A higher dose of radiation increases rib fracture risk. A higher fracture incidence occurred in women receiving radiation doses less than 50 Gy (1.4%) compared to those receiving doses above 50 Gy (5.7%).[43]

Addressing bone health in survivors of breast cancer

Despite the aforementioned reasons for bone loss, screening and treatment of osteoporosis among breast cancer survivors remain suboptimal. By fostering awareness, lifestyle modifications such as regular weight-bearing exercise, smoking cessation, calcium and vitamin D supplementation (to achieve a total intake of 1200 mg/day and 600–1000 IU/day, respectively), and fall prevention strategies can be integrated into daily routines.[44] A comprehensive history, including fracture history, comorbidities, medication history, dietary calcium intake, physical examination, fall risk assessment, and the use of the FRAX, will aid in evaluating the survivors’ overall bone health status.

Evaluation

Current guidelines advocate for an initial DXA measurement for all women diagnosed with early invasive breast cancer who are undergoing tamoxifen therapy, those initiating treatment with an aromatase inhibitor, prior to the commencement of ovarian ablation or suppression therapy, or cases of treatment-induced menopause. Additionally, it is recommended that all post-menopausal breast cancer survivors undergo routine evaluation by DXA.[3245]

Osteoporosis is characterised by low bone mass as well as micro-architectural deterioration of bone tissue.[46] The determination of bone mass alone may not be clearly representative of the fracture risk, and hence, BMD alone may not accurately predict fracture risk. Trabecular bone score (TBS), an indirect indicator of bone microarchitecture, has now been incorporated into FRAX (FRAX plus), and its use demonstrated to be superior to FRAX without TBS in the assessment of fracture risk in post-menopausal osteoporosis.[47]

There is insufficient information on the use of TBS in patients with breast cancer.[48] In a study conducted in the United States, 100 women with early hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer undergoing treatment with AI were recruited. At baseline, BMD alone identified 4% of patients with elevated fracture risk. The inclusion of the FRAX increased detection to 13%, while the combination of BMD, FRAX, and TBS identified 20% of patients with elevated fracture risk. Aromatase inhibitor treatment resulted in change in TBS which was independent of BMD.[49] Siddiqui et al.[50] also suggested that integrating TBS into FRAX would be of additional value to assess bone quality in patients with breast cancer and can enhance fracture prediction. Phalangeal quantitative ultrasound, a radiation-free and inexpensive tool, may also have a role in the follow-up of AI-treated patients.[51] High-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) is a 3-dimensional technology that enables separate analysis of trabecular bone, cortical bone, and microarchitecture. It is measured at the distal radius and tibia. Hopson et al.[52] studied DXA and HR-pQCT prospectively among 18 individuals with pre-menopausal breast cancer undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy. At 1 year, changes noted on HR-pQCT included a decrease in cortical thickness and area at the tibia, decreased total and cortical volumetric BMD at the radius and tibia, and an increase in tibial trabecular area. A single-centre cross-sectional study examined TBS, bone strain index (BSI), and DXA parameters in 610 post-menopausal women with early breast cancer to determine whether TBS or BSI better predicts fractures. In the AI-naive group, vertebral fractures correlated with lower total hip BMD and T-score, and higher femoral BSI. In AI-treated groups, with and without prophylactic Denosumab, vertebral fractures showed no associations with lumbar BSI and TBS. Bone geometry parameters varied across the three groups: AI-naive, AI-treated with prophylactic Denosumab, and AI-treated without prophylactic Denosumab.[53] Further studies are needed to ascertain the role of BSI in breast cancer survivors.

Bone turnover parameters, such as serum pro-peptide of type 1 collagen (P1NP) and serum betacrosslaps (CTX), may be useful in identifying distant metastasis. Lumachi F et al.[54] in their study in Italy reviewed 297 records of women with early breast cancer who were managed with breast conserving surgery. They compared 26 patients who developed bone metastasis at follow-up and 24 randomly selected controls with breast cancer without metastasis (ruled out with FDG-PET) with the aim of looking at diagnostic accuracy of various bone turnover markers in detection of bone metastasis and suggested a combination of bone specific alkaline phosphatase (BSALP), PINP, and TRACP5b (Tartarate-resistant acid phosphatase 5b) would be helpful in identifying skeletal metastasis [AUC: 0.889 (95% CI: 0.798–0.981)]. Data from AZURE trial also suggested that Serum P1NP, CTX, and 1-CTP may have a role in prognostication and identifying those with high risk of bone metastasis. It also has a role in follow-up of patients on treatment with anti-resorptives similar to those with post-menopausal osteoporosis.[55]

TREATMENT

Prevention of bone loss and fractures

Various guidelines suggest different treatment thresholds for the treatment of bone loss associated with breast cancer.[50] Several international cancer and bone organisations in a joint position statement have recommended pharmacological treatment be advised for those with a T-score below − 2.0 or for those with two or more of the following risk factors present:

Age > 65 years

BMI < 20 kg/m2

T-score < -1.5

Family history of hip fracture

Previous fragility fracture at age > 50 years

Current smoking

Glucocorticoid used for more than 6 months.[37]

Due to the absence of safety data for breast cancer survivors, the use of routine anabolic treatments like teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab is not currently advised. Hence, in patients with breast cancer, prevention of bone loss relies solely on the use of the anti-resorptives, namely, bisphosphonates and denosumab.[44] The concern arises from pre-clinical rat studies which showed a dose-dependent increase in risk of osteosarcoma incidence with teriparatide. However, these risks in humans have not been proven.[56] In pre-menopausal women, intravenous zoledronate was previously the only pharmacological agent documented to prevent bone loss, with the added advantage of potential anticancer benefits.[57] However, recent research has indicated that initiating denosumab therapy at the onset of estradiol suppression therapy effectively preserves BMD.[58]

Among post-menopausal women, several oral bisphosphonates such as Alendronate, Risedronate, and Ibandronate have shown beneficial effects in prevention of AI-induced bone loss. IV Zoledronic 4 mg has been studied at 3 monthly and 6 monthly intervals in post-menopausal women on AI in several trials.[41] The ZO-FAST trial (Zoledronic acid for post-menopausal women with early breast cancer receiving adjuvant letrozole) assigned 1065 women on Letrozole to immediate treatment with Zoledronic acid or delayed treatment group (initiated upon fracture or on-study BMD). After 5 years, mean lumbar spine BMD increased by 4.3% with immediate zoledronic acid treatment, versus a decrease by 5.4% with delayed intervention (P < 0.01), indicating benefits of immediate co-administration.[59] Among the anti-resorptive agents, it is noteworthy that Denosumab is the sole agent for which a clinical outcome of fracture reduction trial has been evaluated while the outcomes of other trials have primarily focused on the changes in BMD. The ABCSG-18 (study 18 from the Austrian Breast and Colorectal Cancer Study Group) was conducted at 58 centres over Austria and Sweden among post-menopausal women with hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. The women receiving Denosumab had a significantly delayed time to first clinical fracture, and results suggested a role for adjuvant denosumab 60 mg twice per year in post-menopausal women with breast cancer receiving aromatase inhibitors.[60] While using these therapeutics, it remains crucial to be vigilant for side effects such as osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures which may be seen with both Denosumab and bisphosphonate therapy.[41]

Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs), despite being effective anti-resorptives, are not preferred among women on treatment of breast cancer, particularly on treatment with AI. This stems from the results of the Arimidex, Tamoxifen, Alone or in Combination trial which revealed a reduction in the effect of AI on breast cancer recurrence when combined with SERM.[61] However, pre-clinical studies have suggested a role for combination of Raloxifene and Letrozole.[62]

Prevention of breast cancer re-occurrence

Several trials have suggested a possible role for bisphosphonate therapy in increasing disease-free survival in women with breast cancer. Initial evidence supporting this hypothesis emerged from a study assessing the effects of oral clodronate in reducing bone metastasis and increasing survival in primary operable breast cancer.[63] Gnant et al.[64] examined the effect of adding Zoledronic acid to pre-menopausal women with HR+ breast cancer on Goserelin with either tamoxifen or anastrazole. A 36% relative risk reduction in the risk of disease progression was demonstrated in the group receiving Zoledronate. However, these have also been refuted in trials. The Adjuvant Zoledronic Acid to Reduce Recurrence (AZURE) trial assigned 3360 pre-menopausal women with early breast cancer after primary surgery to adjuvant systemic therapy with or without placebo. Zoledronic acid was administered every 3 to 4 weeks for six doses, followed by administration every 3 to 6 months for a total duration of 5 years. At a 59-month follow-up, no significant difference in disease-free survival was observed between the groups.[65] In 2021, Friedl et al.[66] published the results of SUCCESS A trial, which was a 2 × 2 design, open-label multicentre randomised controlled trial among node-positive or high-risk node-negative primary invasive breast cancer. Data from the study suggest that extending the duration of treatment with Zoledronic acid beyond 2 years may not improve prognosis. With regards to Denosumab, these benefits for improving survival extending beyond bone health have not been proven.[67] The American Society of Clinical Oncology clinical guidelines have recommended that adjuvant bisphosphonate therapy (IV Zoledronate 4 mg IV every 6 months, oral Ibandronate 50 mg/day or oral Clodronate 1600 mg/day) must be discussed among all post-menopausal women with breast cancer planned for adjuvant systemic therapy.[68]

In summary, the strongest evidence for fracture prevention among breast cancer survivors is with Denosumab and this may be considered as the first-line agent for those with very high risk for fractures. Head-to-head trials comparing bisphosphonates and Denosumab are lacking. However, clinical characteristics of the patient, expected side effects, and financial concerns should be addressed in the decision-making process. The fact that several studies have suggested a reduced risk of recurrence and better survival outcomes with adjuvant bisphosphonate therapy in non-metastatic breast cancer may shift decision making in favour of bisphosphonates.[41]

CONCLUSION

With the rising incidence and effective treatment of early breast cancer, coupled with increased longevity, bone health of breast cancer survivors has become a considerable public health concern. Enhanced awareness of the detrimental effects of both the cancer itself and hormone therapy-related bone loss should prompt clinicians to routinely monitor and assess bone mineral density. Furthermore, the timely initiation of bone-directed therapy is essential to mitigate skeletal-related events and reduce overall morbidity and mortality.
