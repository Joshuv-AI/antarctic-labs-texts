HIPEC with cisplatin desensitization at the time of secondary debulking in a patient with a prior carboplatin hypersensitivity reaction
Authors: Michelle Greenman, Yasmin Abozenah, Yifang Eva Pan, Elena S Ratner, Gary Altwerger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First reported case of hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) with platinum desensitization.•Intravenous desensitization is established, but no standardized protocol exists for desensitization in HIPEC therapy.•A modified stepwise cisplatin perfusion allowed safe HIPEC, offering a strategy for platinum allergy in recurrence.Keywords: Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy, HIPEC, Recurrent ovarian cancer, Platinum therapy, Platinum hypersensitivity, Desensitization protocol, Secondary cytoreductive surgery

Introduction

Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) has gained increasing adoption in gynecologic oncology over the past decade, driven by recent multicenter phase III randomized controlled trials. The trials are supported by biologic rationale, as chemotherapy delivered directly into the peritoneal cavity achieves high local drug concentrations, while hyperthermia enhances penetration at the peritoneal surface (van Driel et al., 2018). Hyperthermia increases chemosensitivity by impairing DNA repair, altering membrane permeability, and activating immune pathways (van Driel et al., 2018).

HIPEC demonstrates improved recurrence-free and overall survival (OS) when added to interval cytoreductive surgery (CRS). In OVIHIPEC-1, patients with stage III epithelial ovarian cancer were treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, with a reported median recurrence-free survival benefit of 3.5 months and OS of 11.8 months compared with surgery alone (van Driel et al., 2018).

The publication of the OVIHIPEC-1 trial reinforced the benefit of HIPEC in ovarian cancer by demonstrating an overall survival advantage (HR 0.63, p = 0.006). Similarly, the KOVIHIPEC-1 trial reported a significant improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) among patients undergoing interval debulking with HIPEC (22.9 vs. 14.2 months PFS, p = 0.005), while the CHIPPI-1808 French trial further confirmed safety and survival benefit (El Hajj et al., 2021, Lee et al., 2023). Meta-analyses concluded improved outcomes without increased grade ≥ 3 toxicities, validating these results. Collectively, this evidence has made HIPEC a reasonable strategy in the management of advanced epithelial ovarian cancer.

More recently, the phase III open-label, randomized CHIPOR study established the role of HIPEC in platinum sensitive recurrent ovarian cancer (Classe et al., 2024). The addition of HIPEC at the time of secondary CRS in platinum-sensitive patients significantly improved survival. Median overall survival was 54.3-months compared to 45.8-months with surgery alone (HR = 0.73, p = 0.024) (Classe et al., 2024). These findings strengthen the rationale for treating recurrent platinum-sensitive disease with HIPEC.

Platinum Hypersensitivity.

Platinum hypersensitivity has been a clinical challenge in managing platinum-sensitive recurrent ovarian cancer, where platinum agents remain the mainstay of therapy. Throughout the years, intravenous desensitization protocols have been developed, allowing patients with platinum-sensitive disease to safely continue effective treatment despite hypersensitivity reactions. These reactions are most frequently encountered after a ≥ 6-month platinum-free interval, typically after the seventh cycle of intravenous therapy. In many institutions, however, rechallenge is still avoided, and patients may be transitioned to non-platinum regimens despite guideline recommendations, leading to undertreatment. While NCCN guidelines specifically endorse intravenous desensitization, no protocols exist for intraperitoneal administration. As HIPEC becomes more widely adopted in the recurrent setting, platinum hypersensitivity may become a barrier in this scenario as the absence of desensitization strategies for intraperitoneal use leaves a gap in management.

We present a patient with platinum-sensitive oligometastatic disease who, after developing carboplatin hypersensitivity at her 12th cycle of platinum, successfully underwent HIPEC with cisplatin desensitization at the time of secondary CRS. As HIPEC use expands in the recurrent setting, platinum hypersensitivity will increasingly represent a barrier for patients otherwise eligible for this therapy. Strategies to safely continue platinum in the HIPEC setting, including defining cross-reactivity risks between platinum agents and establishing desensitization protocols for intraperitoneal administration, will become increasingly important. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating the feasibility of HIPEC with cisplatin desensitization in a patient with recurrent high-grade serous ovarian cancer treated in accordance with the OVIHIPEC-1 protocol approach. To date, one other case report addresses a strategy for HIPEC with cisplatin desensitization in a granulosa cell tumor at primary debulking. In comparison to our approach, their protocol required a much slower and gradual perfusion with a lesser emphasis on premedications (Villarreal-González et al., 2025). Outside of gynecologic oncology there is sparse literature to extrapolate from, however, one prior case report of a patient with gastric adenocarcinoma and oxaliplatin hypersensitivity who was able to tolerate cisplatin HIPEC following oxaliplatin hypersensitivity (Morgan et al., 2020). In this case report, no desensitization protocol was implemented and no adverse effects were experienced. At current, the best strategy to mitigate the risk of reaction during HIPEC in a patient with a history of platinum hypersensitivity has not yet been elucidated. Our case implements a fast and safe method for HIPEC with cisplatin desensitization in a patient with platinum sensitive recurrent high grade serous ovarian cancer.

Case Presentation

Patient Background and Initial Diagnosis.

A 45-year-old woman presented in March 2023 with a complex right adnexal mass and was diagnosed with high-grade serous carcinoma of the fallopian tube. She was initially scheduled for a robotic total laparoscopic hysterectomy with mass removal but intraoperatively was found to have extensive malignant disease. Therefore, the procedure was converted to an exploratory laparotomy with an en bloc type B radical hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, low anterior resection with side to end anastomosis, resection of the terminal ileum and cecum with a side-to-side anastomosis of the terminal ileum to the ascending colon, and total omentectomy, with a protective diverting loop ileostomy for complete cytoreduction.

Adjuvant Therapy and Maintenance.

The patient received six cycles of adjuvant combined carboplatin and paclitaxel without complications and was subsequently started on PARP inhibitor maintenance. She also underwent genetic testing with no known detectable mutations. Tumor sequencing through next generation sequencing (Foundation Medicine) demonstrated microsatellite stability, tumor mutation burden of 2 mut/Mb, with a loss of heterozygosity score of 16.3 %, and homologous repair deficiency positive.

Disease Recurrence.

In December 2024, a CT of the abdomen and pelvis identified a questionable soft tissue mass along the vaginal cuff. A subsequent PET-CT confirmed hypermetabolism along the vaginal apex, in close proximity to the bowel. An MRI further characterized the lesion to be 3.3 x 2.3-cm on the right and 3.2 x 1.6-cm on the left, inseparable from the adjacent rectum and sigmoid colon. This corresponded with a deviation from her baseline CA-125 value of 7–9 U/mL, increased to 39.0 U/mL.

The patient was offered secondary CRS for oligometastatic disease based on DESKTOP III trial criteria. However, she elected to defer surgery and proceed with systemic therapy. In February 2025, she reinitiated treatment with carboplatin and paclitaxel.

Carboplatin Hypersensitivity Event.

At the completion of cycle six, 12th overall, the patient experienced hypersensitivity to carboplatin including hypotension, diffuse itching, facial swelling, and a sensation of throat tightness. We administered diphenhydramine, dexamethasone, and two doses of epinephrine. She was monitored for several hours and until the reaction resolved.

Secondary Cytoreductive Surgery with Cisplatin HIPEC.

In July 2025, following her second regimen of carboplatin and paclitaxel (12 cycles overall), the patient underwent secondary CRS surgery with intraoperative cisplatin HIPEC, with modification of the CHIPOR protocol due to her hypersensitivity. Prior to the procedure, the case was reviewed by a multidisciplinary team including pharmacy, allergy and immunology, anesthesiology, and critical care. Shared decision-making with the patient and team supported use of a modified desensitization protocol, incorporating our institution’s standard premedications and a stepwise cisplatin perfusion with increasing concentrations over 90-minutes. The protocol included home premedication with dexamethasone (20 mg) and fexofenadine (60 mg BID), followed by intravenous dexamethasone (20 mg), diphenhydramine (50 mg), and famotidine (20 mg) 30-minutes before perfusion (Table 1). After perfusion, intravenous diphenhydramine (25 mg) was re-dosed every four hours until postoperative day one to reduce the risk of hypersensitivity.

Modified desensitization premedication regimen.

For the HIPEC perfusion, cisplatin was administered at a dose of 100 mg/m2 using a graduated schedule over 90-minutes (1:1000 initially, 1:100 at 30-minutes, and 1:20 at 60-minutes), with complete dosage delivery by the end. This perfusion differed from established regimens in the literature, including the OVHIPEC-1 protocol, which delivered 50 % of the dose initially, followed by 25 % at 30-minutes and final 25 % at-60 min (van Driel et al., 2018) or the CHIPOR single one-hour perfusion of cisplatin (Classe et al., 2024). Given the patient’s previous hypersensitivity, we mirrored our institutional intravenous desensitization protocol, employing stepwise increases in cisplatin concentration. Intraoperatively, she was closely monitored for dermatologic, respiratory, and cardiovascular signs of hypersensitivity, and none were observed. In the immediate postoperative period, she was monitored in the ICU with physical exams, hourly vital signs, urine output assessments, and continued diphenhydramine every four hours for twenty-four hours without evidence of hypersensitivity reaction. The patient was discharged on post-operative day six.

Discussion

Platinum-based DNA-alkylating agents remain the cornerstone of gynecologic malignancies. For patients that develop platinum sensitive recurrent disease after at least a six-month disease-free interval, platinum-based chemotherapy persists as the most effective treatment. Although hypersensitivity reactions to carboplatin are uncommon during the initial course, subsequent re-treatment for platinum sensitive recurrent disease increases the incidence of reaction. At retreatment, it is estimated that the incidence of a hypersensitivity reaction increases to 27 % among patients that have received more than seven cycles of carboplatin (Markman MD et al., 1999). Carboplatin hypersensitivities are believed to be caused by a type I IgE-mediated reaction or a type IV delayed hypersensitivity which is facilitated through T-cell response and can cause flushing, chest pain, cardiovascular compromise, and death. (Navo et al., 2006).

Avoiding intravenous platinum therapy in the setting of platinum-sensitive recurrent disease would risk worsened patient outcomes due to the use of inferior regimens (Bergamini et al., 2017). Intravenous desensitization protocols have allowed patients to continue the preferred agent and avoid resorting to second or third line treatments (Tsao et al., 2022). Management of carboplatin hypersensitivity reactions involve premedication with corticosteroids and antihistamines followed by administration of the agent using an intravenous desensitization protocol (Navo et al., 2006). Several intravenous desensitization protocols have been proposed to allow for continued carboplatin use of (Altwerger et al., 2017, Li et al., 2014, O’Malley et al., 2017). Although there is no standardized intravenous desensitization protocol, a 4-step, 12-step, and 16-step regimens exist, each with variation in infusion rate, titration schedule, and time to complete infusion (Altwerger et al., 2017, Lee et al., 2004, Li et al., 2014).

At our institution, we historically use a four-step intravenous dilution protocol over 3.5-hours, with approximately 75 % of patients with prior hypersensitivity reactions completing intravenous desensitization without recurrence. Among the remainder, most experience only grade 1–2 reactions during desensitization, which were managed, allowing for completion of the intravenous desensitization protocol (Altwerger et al., 2017). Intravenous platinum desensitization has generally been safe and effective. Nevertheless, retrospective data did report a fatality in a patient with preexisting pulmonary hypertension, where the increased cardiac workload from elevated pulmonary pressures, combined with impaired ventricular filling during anaphylaxis precipitated rapid cardiovascular collapse (Altwerger et al., 2018, 2017). While intravenous desensitization protocols are safe in the vast majority of cases, careful patient selection and risk–benefit assessment remain essential.

In this report, we describe a patient with a prior hypersensitivity reaction to carboplatin that tolerated HIPEC with cisplatin successfully. To mitigate the risk of hypersensitivity, we implemented a modified desensitization protocol based on our intravenous platinum desensitization protocol. This included home premedication with fexofenadine and dexamethasone, followed by intravenous dexamethasone, famotidine, and diphenhydramine 30-minutes before perfusion along with sodium thiosulfate infusion (Table 1). We then continued diphenhydramine every four hours until 24-hours following the completion of cisplatin infusion.

On the cisplatin perfusion step, we opted to implement aspects of the institute's intravenous desensitization protocol into the HIPEC perfusion procedure by utilizing a stepwise dilution and gradual increase in cisplatin perfusion concentration. This approach mirrors the multi-step intravenous desensitization schedules but intraperitoneally and differs from the concentration regimens employed in OVHIPEC-1 and CHIPOR. We implemented this given the patient’s significant hypersensitivity reaction requiring two doses of epinephrine. Whether dilution and titration of cisplatin with premedications during HIPEC reduces the risk of hypersensitivity remains unknown, but this case suggests it to be feasible and enhancing safety.

Another strategy to decrease hypersensitivity reactions is by substituting the platinum-based agent. Cross-reactivity in carboplatin allergy is estimated at approximately 7 % and 37 % to cisplatin and oxaliplatin, respectively (Pasteur et al., 2019). Rates of hypersensitivity in patients challenged with cisplatin after a carboplatin-related hypersensitivity range between 13.1–25 % (Bergamini et al., 2017, Callahan et al., 2007). These relatively low rates suggest that cisplatin can reasonably be given in patients with prior carboplatin hypersensitivity, however, risk of hypersensitivity remains present.

There is limited data on the cross-reactivity to platinum agents with intraperitoneal administration. The use of HPEC in recurrent disease is recent, and available data remain limited, with platinum hypersensitivity not yet described. However, the historical use of intraperitoneal platinum therapy provides important context, with reports of anaphylaxis during infusion underscoring the potential risk of hypersensitivity with this route (Özgüroglu et al., 1999). One proposed method to identify this potential risk is the use of skin testing to stratify patients following a hypersensitivity reaction. Although a positive skin test can stratify patients at increased risk for further hypersensitivity reactions, negative tests must be taken with caution as both false negatives and seroconversion can occur (Lax et al., 2015). Given the variability and high false-negative rate and risk of seroconversion, we did not rely on skin testing results to guide our clinical decision to proceed with HIPEC.

The utility of skin testing and need for implementing desensitization including premedication and increasing cisplatin concentrations in the perfusate are not well established. Further studies are indicated to understand the cross-reactivity between systemic and intraperitoneal chemotherapy, and to elucidate best practices for preventing hypersensitivity reactions during HIPEC. We anticipate this scenario will become more common with the increasing use of HIPEC in platinum-sensitive recurrent disease, and our experience may help guide other groups in managing this complex situation.
