Metabolic syndrome-related gene signature for prognosis and immune microenvironment prediction in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Liuyan Chen, Yaobin Wang, Shuai Li, Shan Lu, Yiling Luo, Yanping Ning, Jifei Chen, Sufang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic syndrome is a major risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression, yet the role of metabolic syndrome-related genes in HCC remains incompletely understood. Using bioinformatics approaches, we investigated the influence of these genes on HCC prognosis and tumor biology. Two distinct patient clusters (C1 and C2) were identified based on metabolic gene expression, with C2 exhibiting poorer survival, increased stemness, and higher risk scores. A risk model further revealed that high-risk patients had worse outcomes, elevated immunosuppressive cell infiltration, and distinct phenotypic features. Experimental validation via qRT-PCR confirmed upregulation of risk model genes (particularly KIAA1841 and TUBA1B) in HCC cell lines and patient blood samples. Notably, post-surgical declines in KIAA1841 and TUBA1B levels were observed. Our findings highlight the clinical relevance of metabolic syndrome-related genes in HCC, linking them to prognosis, tumor microenvironment remodeling, cancer stemness, and immunotherapy response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26334-0.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Metabolic syndrome, Tumor microenvironment, Stemness index, ImmunotherapySubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for 90% of primary liver cancers and is the sixth most common cancer globally, ranking as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths as of 20221. Traditionally, chronic viral hepatitis was the primary cause of HCC2; however, effective treatments and prevention strategies for hepatitis B virus (HBV) have led to a decline in the prevalence of viral hepatitis in recent decades3. In contrast, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has seen a significant increase. A study using a U.S. healthcare database identified MASLD as the most prevalent form of chronic liver disease, present in 59% of reported HCC cases4. As MASLD rates continue to rise, so does the incidence of MASLD-related HCC, which is now the fastest-growing cause of HCC and a leading indication for liver transplantation5. MASLD is characterized by fat accumulation in the liver, leading to inflammation and an increased risk of cancer, predominantly affecting individuals who are overweight, obese, or have type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)4.

Numerous studies have established metabolic syndrome (MetS) as a known risk factor for HCC6. MetS is a global health issue defined by the presence of at least three of the following five risk factors: hyperglycemia, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, low levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and increased waist circumference7. It is often associated with age, obesity, and insulin resistance, all of which can significantly impact liver function8. In the context of insulin resistance, adipocytes release free fatty acids (FFAs) into the bloodstream9,10. Visceral obesity, common in sedentary lifestyles, further elevates FFA levels and disrupts adipokine balance, leading to decreased adiponectin and altered liver metabolism11. This metabolic imbalance results in increased FFAs in the liver, contributing to hypertriglyceridemia and decreased HDL cholesterol, which can exacerbate hyperglycemia by enhancing gluconeogenesis12. Additionally, this condition involves heightened production of angiotensinogen and inflammatory cytokines, adversely affecting liver and kidney metabolism, and leading to endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress10. Over time, individuals with MetS may experience pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory states that can contribute to cancer progression9,13. Thus, the association between HCC and MetS presents a “causal” relationship in the disease’s progression. Despite strong clinical and epidemiological evidence linking HCC and MetS14,15, little is known about their gene signatures based on regulatory mechanisms.

Recent advances in sequencing technology and bioinformatics have facilitated the exploration of common pathogenesis in disease interactions at the genetic level16, potentially identifying effective therapeutic targets and enhancing our understanding of liver cancer while advancing treatments for metabolism-related liver diseases17,18. In this study, we first examined the differential expression of metabolic syndrome-related genes in HCC. Next, we analyzed the association between the differential expression of these genes and the tumor microenvironment (TME), as well as various clinical characteristics of HCC. We then constructed a risk score model based on the differential expression of metabolic syndrome-related genes to predict patient prognosis and response to immunotherapy. Finally, the rationality and reliability of hub genes of the risk score model were verified by quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis on HCC cell lines and whole blood of HCC patients. Therefore, an in-depth exploration of the relationship between metabolic syndrome and HCC is essential for developing new prevention and treatment strategies, including early screening and personalized interventions aimed at reducing the incidence of HCC and improving patient outcomes, while also investigating the expression patterns of metabolic syndrome-related genes to enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying HCC and contribute to the development of more personalized and precise therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data sources and processing

A cohort of 420 samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (LIHC) project was utilized for model development. This cohort included 370 tumor tissues and 50 adjacent normal liver tissues. From this cohort, we collected RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) transcriptome data, somatic mutation profiles, copy number variation (CNV) data, and comprehensive clinical annotations, including gender, age, TNM stage (T: tumor size, N: lymph node involvement, M: distant metastasis), clinical stage, histological grade, survival data, and etiologies. The etiological distribution was as follows: hepatitis B (26%), hepatitis C (13%), dual HBV and HCV infection (2%), non-B non-C hepatitis (54%), and unspecified (5%). Additional clinical and transcriptomic data were integrated from the LIHC Vis repository (LIHC Vis - Visualization Tools for Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma Datasets (cnio.es)).

We further acquired two categories of datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): one metabolic syndrome dataset (GSE98895) and one HCC cohort (GSE14520). The GSE98895 dataset contained FPKM-normalized expression profiles from 40 samples (20 metabolic syndrome patients and 20 healthy controls), spanning 25,125 genes. Differential expression analysis was performed on GSE98895 using the limma R package (thresholds: |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05), identifying 4,115 metabolism-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs)19. These DEGs were subsequently intersected with protein-coding genes from the TCGA-LIHC cohort. After filtering the TCGA-LIHC expression matrix to retain 19,433 reliably detected genes (expressed in ≥ 50 samples), 2,441 overlapping genes were obtained. Re-evaluation within the TCGA-LIHC cohort confirmed 189 of these as significantly differentially expressed in HCC. Univariate Cox regression analysis applied to these 189 genes identified 39 that were significantly associated with overall survival (p < 0.05), establishing the core set of metabolism-related prognostic genes in HCC20.

All analyses—including somatic mutation and CNV profiling, consensus clustering, and the construction and validation of the prognostic risk model—were conducted exclusively within the TCGA-LIHC cohort, which was randomly divided into training and testing sets for internal validation. The merged LIHC Vis dataset was specifically employed for weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to assess module–trait correlations. The GEO HCC cohorts (GSE14520) were used solely for external survival association analyses and were not involved in model training or validation21.

Characteristics of metabolic syndrome-related genes

We first characterized the 39 metabolic syndrome (MS)-related prognostic genes identified from the TCGA-LIHC cohort. A correlation heatmap was generated to visualize their interrelationships. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated from the normalized gene expression matrix (statistical significance: p < 0.0001). Genes were annotated according to their risk profiles, as determined by univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis: genes with hazard ratios (HR) > 1 were categorized as high-risk, and those with HR ≤ 1 as low-risk. Hierarchical clustering was applied to both rows and columns. Somatic mutation and copy number variation (CNV) analyses were performed on the same 39 MS-related genes using data from the TCGA-LIHC project. Somatic mutation profiles were analyzed across all 370 HCC cases. Among them, 68 cases carrying at least one nonsynonymous mutation in the gene set were included in the mutation prevalence analysis20,22. CNV alterations—including amplifications and deletions—were visualized across these 68 mutated cases and mapped to genomic coordinates using the R package maftools21. Furthermore, we evaluated the expression profiles of these 39 genes across different HCC subtypes and performed univariate Cox regression analysis by integrating the GSE14520 dataset, with the results presented in a forest plot23.

Consensus clustering

Molecular subtyping of the TCGA-LIHC cohort (n = 370) was conducted via consensus clustering (CC), an unsupervised algorithm that assesses cluster stability based on a resampling algorithm24. Using the ConsensusClusterPlus R package25 and the expression profiles of the 39 metabolic syndrome-related genes, we identified two stable patient clusters, designated C1 and C2.

Characteristic differences between C1 and C2

We employed Single-Sample GSEA (ssGSEA), ESTIMATE, and CIBERSORT to quantify the TME. To better understand the differences between the C1 and C2, we assessed the HCC tumor stemness index for each cluster26. Additionally, we analyzed variations in immune checkpoints, immune inhibitors, and immune stimulators across the different patient27.

Construction of the risk score model

To construct a prognostic model, we first identified DEGs between clusters C1 and C2 using the “limma” R package. These DEGs were then subjected to WGCNA to identify key modules significantly correlated with TME features, stemness, and prognosis28. Genes from the most relevant modules were used for subsequent feature selection. Subsequently, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to select metabolic syndrome-related genes for predicting survival and prognosis in HCC. The final risk score for each patient was calculated using the formula: Risk Score = Σ (Gene Expressioni × LASSO Coefficienti). The TCGA-LIHC cohort was randomly split into training and test sets in a 1:1 ratio for model validation. The prognostic performance of the risk score was evaluated by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis in both sets, and its predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves29. Finally, a forest plot was generated to display the hazard ratios of the risk score and other integrated clinical prognostic factors22.

Nomogram construction and characteristics of the risk score model

By integrating prognostic and clinical characteristics, we constructed a nomogram using the R package “RMS.” To evaluate the performance of both the risk score model and the nomogram, we employed calibration curves and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves30. Additionally, we investigated the differences in TME and stemness between the high- and low-risk groups, as well as explored potential associations with various clinical characteristics of HCC27.

Immune function and immune checkpoint analyses

We conducted immune function analysis using the ‘limma’, ‘GSVA’, ‘GSEABase’, ‘pheatmap’, and ‘reshape2’ R packages. The expression levels of immune checkpoints were determined using the ‘limma’, ‘ggplot2’, ‘ggpubr’, and ‘reshape2’ R packages31. In addition, potential immunotherapy responsiveness was evaluated using the Immunophenoscore (IPS), obtained from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA, https://tcia.at). IPS is a computational framework that integrates expression levels of MHC molecules, immune checkpoints, effector cells, and immunomodulators to predict the likelihood of response to immune checkpoint blockade32. IPS values for anti–PD-1, anti–CTLA-4, and their combination were retrieved for TCGA-LIHC patients and compared between high- and low-risk subgroups defined by our model33.

Cell lines and patient samples

Human normal liver cell (L02) and human HCC cell lines (HepG2, SK-Hep-1, Huh7, SMMC-7721, Hep3B, HCCLM3, and MHCC-97 H) were kept in our laboratory and cultured according to standard protocols. These cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Biological Industries, Israel), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biological Industries, Israel) and 100 units/mL penicillin-streptomycin solution (Biological Industries, Israel) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment.

Blood and tissue samples were collected from HCC patients and healthy individuals at the Department of Clinical Laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Nanning, China) between June and August 2024. A total of 10 whole blood samples were obtained from healthy individuals, alongside samples from 31 HCC patients with metabolic syndrome. Additionally, blood samples were collected from 23 patients with HCC both before and after surgery, along with tissue samples from 6 patients. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (20,200,035). All patients provided informed consent. All experiments of this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University and conformed to the ethical guidelines and regulations of Guangxi Medical University.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Dalian, China) and cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using StarScript III All-in-one RT Mix with gDNA Remover (GenStar, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed on the ABI StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the 2× RealStar Fast SYBR qPCR Mix (High ROX) (GenStar, Beijing, China). The qRT-PCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 2 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 30 seconds. The following primers (Gensysbio, Nanning, China) were used: human CAD, 5’-GTACAGCAGCCTTTGAACCCA-3’ (sense) and 5’-GCCCACACAGTTCTCATCCAC-3’ (antisense); FAM83D, 5’-TCGGCGCGAGAGGTGATTG-3’ (sense) and 5’-ATTGAGAGAGGAGAGCCTGGT-3’ (antisense); IQGAP3, 5’-GAACATGGAGAGGAGAGCAGC-3’ (sense) and 5’-TGATAGGCAACATTCTGCCGC-3’ (antisense); KIAA1841, 5’-TTGCGACGTTCACATTTTCAAC-3’ (sense) and 5’- CTCTAAAGTGGGCATCTCACAAT-3’ (antisense); PSRC1, 5’-GAAGTGGACACAGGCTCAGAT-3’ (sense) and 5’-CCCGTCGAAGTGGTTTCTCTG-3’ (antisense); TUBA1B, 5’-CGCCTCCTAATCCCTAGCCAC-3’ (sense) and 5’- TCCCCCAATGGTCTTGTCACT-3’ (antisense); and β-actin, 5’- AGATCAAGATCATTGCTCCTCCTG − 3’ (sense) and 5’- AGTCATAGTCCGCCTAGAAGCAT − 3’ (antisense). Relative mRNA levels were quantified using the comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCt) method with β-actin as the reference gene.

Immunohistochemical staining

First, deparaffinize the tissue sections in xylene, followed by washing in a graded ethanol series. Heat the citrate buffer solution to boiling, add the tissue sections, and incubate at low power for 20 min. Allow the solution to cool to room temperature. Next, block the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Add the anti- KIAA1841 antibody (1:300, PS11700; abmart, Shanghai, China) and incubate at 37 °C for 60 min. Then, apply the rabbit anti-horseradish peroxidase reagent from the MaxVision™ kit (MAX, Fujian, China) and incubate at 37 °C for 20 min. Perform DAB staining for 3–8 min, monitoring the color development. Terminate the reaction with water, then stain with hematoxylin for 1 min, followed by differentiation and bluing. Finally, dehydrate the sections through a graded ethanol series and mount with neutral resin.

Western blotting

Protein extracts were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were then blocked with 5% skimmed milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were anti-KIAA1841 (1:1000, PS11700; abmart, Shanghai, China) and anti-actin (1:1000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The next day, the membranes were washed three times and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, SA00001-2, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 2 h. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (Meilun, Dalian, China). Finally, protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MA, USA), with actin serving as the internal control.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 and SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Wilcoxon test and the Kruskal-Wallis test were applied for comparisons between two independent samples and among multiple samples, respectively, for non-parametric data. For parametric data, t-tests and one-way ANOVA were utilized. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genetic variations and expression of metabolic syndrome-related genes in HCC

Through an integrated bioinformatic approach, we identified a core set of 39 metabolic syndrome-related genes that are significantly dysregulated and prognostically relevant in HCC (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for the study flowchart). A heatmap visualization of these genes revealed coordinated expression patterns across HCC samples, with all members exhibiting uniformly positive correlations and association with unfavorable prognosis (Fig. 1A). Analysis of genetic alterations in the TCGA-LIHC cohort (n = 370) revealed somatic mutations or copy number alterations (CNAs) in these genes in 68 patients (18.4%). Among these, MKI67 exhibited the highest alteration frequency (16%, Fig. 1B), which represents the combined burden of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and copy number alterations (CNAs), rather than SNVs alone. CNV profiling demonstrated a high prevalence of CNV alterations, which were mapped across chromosomes (Fig. 1C). Specifically, 25 genes including RUSC1, SMG5, CBX2, CCNE2, CHML, ECT2, PLXNA3, G6PD, DNMT3A, PTTG1, SMC4, TRIM59, HMGA1, GRM4, MCM3, TACC3, TMEM164, BRSK1, RBP2, KCTD7, PHF19, SRXN1, KIF18A, CCDC34, and RACGAP1 exhibited widespread CNV amplification. In contrast, the remaining 14 genes were IL17D, CDC20, MMP1, STIL, STMN1, MCM6, MAPT, LMNB2, MCM2, MKI67, RAD51, CDCA5, CENPH, and CDCA2 showed CNV deletions (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, the expression levels of the 39 metabolic syndrome-related genes were significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1E). The heatmap in Fig. 1F provides a detailed visualization of the expression of these genes in HCC tissues versus normal tissues. To assess the prognostic impact of the 39 metabolic syndrome-related genes, we analyzed overall survival (OS) in an integrated GEO dataset. Elevated expression of 14 genes was significantly correlated with adverse OS outcomes (Fig. 1G). The full list is as follows: DNMT3A, CHML, G6PD, ECT2, PTTG1, TACC3, MCM6, MCM2, RACGAP1, STMN1, CDC20, MKI67, CCNE2, and MMP1.

Correlation of metabolic syndrome pattern with TME, stemness, and clinical traits

Consensus clustering was performed using gene expression data from LIHC-TCGA to identify metabolic syndrome-related clusters in HCC. According to the cumulative distribution function, k = 2 demonstrated strong clustering resilience, characterized by relatively high intracluster correlation and low inter-cluster correlation (Fig. 2A-B). Consequently, we classified 370 patients with HCC into two clusters, designated C1 and C2 (Fig. 2C). A heatmap was generated to visualize the expression of metabolic syndrome-related genes across the C1 and C2 clusters in relation to T-, M-, and N-stages, clinical stage, grade, gender, and age (Fig. 2D). This resulted in the separation of patients into two groups: 228 patients in C1 and 142 patients in C2. Notably, patients in C1 exhibited prolonged overall survival (P < 0.001, log-rank) (Fig. 2E). To further explore the TME, we employed the CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE algorithms. CIBERSORT analysis demonstrated a significant enrichment of various immune cell infiltrates in C1, including Type 1 T helper cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils. In C2, there was a notable enrichment of different immune cell types, such as activated CD4 T cells, central memory CD4 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, T follicular helper cells, Type 2 T helper cells, activated dendritic cells, macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and natural killer T cells (Fig. 2F). ESTIMATE analysis further indicated that C2 had lower stromal scores and higher immune scores compared to C1 (Fig. 2G). In the stemness index analysis, both mRNA stemness index (mRNAsi) and EREG-mRNAsi values for C2 were significantly closer to 1, suggesting a high similarity to stem cells in this cluster (Fig. 2H). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that several pathways were significantly activated in C2, including glycolytic gluconeogenesis, ABC transporters, and starch and sucrose metabolism, among others34–36. Conversely, pathways such as oocyte meiosis, spliceosome, and mismatch repair were more active in C1 (Fig. 2I). Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) biopathway analysis showed that metabolic syndrome-related genes in C2 were significantly enriched in functional sets associated with cortisol response, G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathways, and ventricular cardiac muscle cell membrane repolarization, among others (Fig. 2J).

Although there is partial overlap between the unsupervised C1/C2 clusters and the supervised high/low-risk groups, they are not identical. This is expected, as the two analyses capture different biological axes: C1/C2 clustering reflects global metabolic-gene expression patterns, whereas the risk score was optimized to stratify prognosis. Notably, some high-risk patients fall into C1, underscoring the heterogeneity of HCC. Regarding the ImmuneScore, the higher values observed in C2 are mainly associated with immunosuppressive or immature immune subsets, while the low-risk group exhibits higher ImmuneScore accompanied by stronger HLA and immune-function signatures, consistent with more effective anti-tumor immunity. Therefore, the clustering analysis is retained as an exploratory step to reveal tumor heterogeneity, while the risk model provides an independent and clinically oriented prognostic tool.

Construction and validation of the risk score

To identify the key module most implicated in clinical characteristics, we conducted WGCNA on the merged HCC datasets (Fig. 3A). This analysis yielded a total of 1,465 differential genes associated with metabolic syndrome, from which we identified several co-expression modules. The heatmap of module-trait relationships indicated that the MEblue and MEturquoise modules exhibited the strongest correlations with clinical traits (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, we applied a univariate Cox regression algorithm to preliminarily identify 169 genes associated with HCC prognosis, calculating the hazard ratios (HR) and P-values for the metabolic syndrome-related genes (Fig. 3C). We then utilized the LASSO algorithm to pinpoint prognostic gene sets for HCC, ultimately identifying six significant gene sets (Fig. 3D, E). From this analysis, we constructed a 6-gene risk signature comprising CAD, FAM83D, KIAA1841, IQGAP3, PSRC1, and TUBA1B to predict the survival and prognosis of HCC patients (Fig. 3F). We systematically randomized the cohort of TCGA-LIHC patients to establish a training set (n = 185) and a test set (n = 185). Kaplan-Meier analyses revealed that higher risk scores in both the training and test sets were associated with poorer survival outcomes (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3G, H). To evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of our prognostic model, we employed time-dependent ROC analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA). The model’s performance was assessed based on the area under the ROC curve (AUC). For the training set, the AUCs at 1, 3, and 5 years were 0.763, 0.741, and 0.684, respectively, while for the test set, the AUCs were 0.756, 0.684, and 0.650, respectively (Fig. 3I, J).

Nomogram based on risk score in HCC

We next performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to assess whether the risk score models were an independent prognostic indicator for predicting the survival of HCC patients. In the univariate analysis, we found that clinical stage (HR: 1.865, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.456–2.388, P < 0.001), T-stage (HR: 1.804, 95% CI 1.434–2.270, P < 0.001), M-stage (HR: 3.850, 95% CI 1.207–12.281, P = 0.023), and risk score (HR: 1.151, 95% CI 1.092–1.213, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with HCC prognosis (Fig. 4A). After adjusting for potential confounding factors using multivariate regression analysis, the risk score (HR: 1.124, 95% CI 1.064–1.188, P < 0.001) remained an independent predictor of survival (Fig. 4B). Based on these independent prognostic factors, we developed a comprehensive nomogram that allows for quantitative predictions of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities in HCC patients. The nomogram incorporated gender, age, grade, clinical stage, and the risk score as parameters (Fig. 4C). The clinical applicability of the nomogram was further evaluated using DCA for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival periods. Compared to the model that included clinical characteristics alone, we found that this comprehensive nomogram provided greater net benefits, potentially improving clinical management (Fig. 4D). We also constructed a risk score based on the integrated TCGA cohort and analyzed its correlation with clinical characteristics. We observed statistically significant associations between the risk score and various clinical factors, including vital status, age, grade, clinical stage, cluster, mRNAsi, stromal score, immune score, and estimate score (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, we discovered that the low-risk score group exhibited higher levels of EstimateScore, ImmuneScore, and StromalScore compared to the high-risk score group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4F).

Association between risk score and the tumor microenvironment

To investigate the immune microenvironment, we assessed the infiltration levels of 28 immune cell types in high- and low-risk score groups. The low-risk score group exhibited higher infiltration levels of activated B cells, activated CD8 T cells, central memory CD4 T cells, effector memory CD8 T cells, gamma delta T cells, immature B cells, regulatory T cells, T follicular helper cells, type 1 helper cells, CD56bright natural killer cells, CD56dim natural killer cells, eosinophils, immature dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, MDSC, natural killer cells, and neutrophils, indicating a more robust immune response. Conversely, activated CD4 T cells, memory B cells, and type 2 helper cells were more prevalent in the high-risk score group (Fig. 5A). This finding suggests that patients with low-risk scores have enhanced tumor immune activation, which correlates with improved OS compared to those with high-risk scores. Furthermore, the low-risk score group demonstrated significant enrichment in functional pathways related to immunity, including antigen presenting cells (APC) co-inhibition, CCR, checkpoint pathways, cytolytic activity, HLA, inflammation promotion, parainflammation, T-cell co-stimulation, T-cell co-inhibition, type I interferon response, and type II interferon response (Fig. 5B). The low-risk group also exhibited higher expression levels of HLA-related genes, reflecting a significant concentration of immune cell infiltration (Fig. 5C). To explore potential immunotherapy outcomes for patients with HCC, we analyzed immune checkpoint expression differences between the two risk groups. Eleven common immune checkpoint genes showed statistically significant differences. The high-risk score group had elevated expression of seven immune checkpoint genes, including CD276, TNFSF15, ADORA2A, TNFRSF14, TNFSF4, TNFRSF4, and HHLA2. In contrast, KIR3DL1, IDO2, TMIGD2, and PDCD1LG2 were predominantly expressed in the low-risk score group. The distinct immune checkpoint expression profiles between risk groups suggest potential strategies for tailoring immunotherapy in HCC (Fig. 5D). It should be noted that Fig. 5E does not represent actual clinical treatment outcomes but rather depicts computationally inferred IPS values derived from TCGA data. Higher IPS in the low-risk group indicates stronger predicted immunogenicity and a greater likelihood of response to immune checkpoint blockade, consistent with their enriched immune activation signatures. Immune score prediction (IPS) was employed to assess patient responses to immunotherapy. The results indicated that the low-risk group experienced significant therapeutic benefits from checkpoint inhibitor treatments (Fig. 5E). The mRNAsi in patients with higher risk scores was closer to 1, suggesting that these patients were less differentiated compared to those with lower risk scores (Fig. 5F). When examining the relationship between the risk score model and various patient subgroups in HCC, the Wilcoxon test revealed a significant difference in risk scores between subtypes C1 and C2 (Fig. 5G). However, some studies suggest that higher indices were directly correlated with the progression of different cancer types and were associated with poorer prognoses37,38, emphasizing the need for further investigation. The Sankey diagram illustrates the interrelationship between the C1 and C2 subtypes, revealing that associations with risk and OS were predominantly concentrated in the C2 subtype (Fig. 5H). This result aligned with previous findings suggesting that the poor prognosis of C2 was linked to its high-risk score.

Expression of 6-Gene risk signature in HCC cells and patient samples

To validate the expression of the 6-gene risk signature (CAD, FAM83D, KIAA1841, IQGAP3, PSRC1, and TUBA1B) in HCC cell lines, we conducted qRT-PCR to assess their expression levels in various HCC cell lines, including SK-HEP-1, HepG2, SMMC-7721, Huh7, Hep3B, HCCLM3, and MHCC-97 H. Our results demonstrated that all six genes in the risk signature were upregulated in HCC cell lines compared to normal liver cells (L02), with KIAA1841 and TUBA1B exhibiting the highest expression levels (Fig. 6A). Consequently, we selected these two genes for further analysis of their expression in HCC patients. We found that the expression levels of KIAA1841 and TUBA1B in the blood of HCC patients were significantly elevated compared to those in healthy individuals (Fig. 6B). Moreover, following surgical treatment, the expression levels of KIAA1841 and TUBA1B in the blood of HCC patients significantly decreased (Fig. 6C). To further validate the protein expression of KIAA1841, a gene that has not been previously studied in this context, we conducted immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Western blotting analyses. The IHC results indicated that KIAA1841 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues from HCC patients compared to their matched adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 6D). A similar conclusion was drawn from the Western blotting analysis, where KIAA1841 protein levels were elevated in tumor tissues relative to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, the Western blotting analysis revealed that KIAA1841 protein expression was significantly higher in all the tested HCC cell lines compared to L02 cells, further confirming its potential role in HCC progression (Fig. 6F). The presence of high expression in blood samples is consistent with the release of tumor-derived nucleic acids and vesicles into circulation, suggesting that these genes may serve as minimally invasive blood-based biomarkers in HCC.

Discussion

HCC is the most common primary liver malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide39. Its development is often influenced by multiple factors, including viral infections, alcohol consumption, and steatosis40. Importantly, metabolic syndrome, especially through conditions like MASLD, plays a crucial role in the progression of HCC41. MetS is characterized by a cluster of risk factors, including abdominal obesity, impaired glucose tolerance, hypertriglyceridemia, low levels of HDL, and hypertension42. It presents a significant public health challenge due to its rapidly increasing prevalence, reaching near-pandemic levels in recent years43. Furthermore, MASLD exhibits similar pathophysiological changes, with obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia contributing to hepatic inflammation and damage44,45. These factors lead to increased lipid accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, T2DM, and inflammation, which collectively exacerbate liver injury and promote carcinogenesis46,47. These conditions lead to increased lipid accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation, which collectively heighten HCC risk46. Thus, understanding how metabolic syndrome-related genes impact HCC immune responses and their potential role in predicting HCC prognosis is crucial.

In our study, we identified a core set of 39 metabolic syndrome-related genes that are consistently dysregulated and prognostically significant in HCC. These genes not only reflect metabolic dysfunction but also exhibit strong ties to cell-cycle regulation, with several—including MKI6748, STMN149, and CDC2050—being established proliferative drivers. This dual identity is biologically coherent, as metabolic syndrome fuels hyperinsulinemia and growth-factor signaling, which in turn activate oncogenic pathways48,50, thereby linking metabolic reprogramming to uncontrolled proliferation in HCC.

Beyond transcriptional upregulation, we observed widespread genomic alterations within this gene set. While MKI67 showed the highest mutation frequency, CNV was a major mode of genetic dysregulation. Intriguingly, the relationship between copy number and expression was complex. Although amplifications often corresponded to elevated mRNA levels through a gene-dosage effect51, key regulators such as STMN1 and CDCA2 remained highly expressed even in the context of copy-number loss. This apparent discordance is biologically plausible and points to powerful transcriptional and epigenetic overrides. First, as core cell-cycle regulators, STMN1 and CDCA2 are strongly induced by proliferative programs (e.g., E2F/MYC)52,53, meaning their expression is driven more by pathway activation than by gene dosage. Second, mechanisms such as promoter hypomethylation, enhancer hijacking, and alterations in chromatin architecture can sustain high transcription from a reduced allele count54. Third, tumor heterogeneity and clonal architecture may lead to segment-level “loss” calls in bulk analyses, while the transcriptome captures strong expression from proliferative subclones55,56. Therefore, we interpret the “loss-but-high-expression” pattern of STMN1/CDCA2 not as a data contradiction, but as evidence of dosage-independent transcriptional activation within a highly proliferative context. This, together with the observation that the poorer-prognosis C2 cluster exhibits higher stemness and metabolic pathway activation, indicates that the widespread overexpression of these genes is primarily driven by pathway-level transcriptional activation, with somatic point mutations playing a comparatively lesser role.

These molecular features informed a clinically relevant classification of HCC. Consensus clustering of the 39 metabolic syndrome-related genes robustly classified HCC patients into two distinct subgroups, C1 and C2, which exhibited pronounced differences in clinical outcome and biological characteristics57. Patients in the C1 cluster experienced significantly longer overall survival, coupled with a TME marked by higher stromal scores. In contrast, the C2 cluster was associated with poorer prognosis, an elevated stemness index, and significant activation of metabolic pathways—a finding consistent with the metabolic origins of this gene signature.

To translate this categorical classification into a continuous and clinically applicable metric, we constructed a prognostic risk model. Utilizing WGCNA and machine learning algorithms (univariate Cox followed by LASSO regression), we refined the initial gene set into a parsimonious 6-gene signature (CAD, FAM83D, KIAA1841, IQGAP3, PSRC1, and TUBA1B). The resulting risk score proved to be an independent prognostic factor, effectively stratifying patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly different survival outcomes in both training and test sets. Notably, the risk score was intrinsically linked to the immunosuppressive landscape of the TME. The high-risk group exhibited significantly lower stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores, indicating an immune-depleted “cold” tumor phenotype. Conversely, the low-risk group was characterized by a more robust anti-tumor immune response, evidenced by a greater diversity of infiltrating immune cells, enrichment of immune-related pathways, and elevated expression of HLA-related genes. This immunologically active state in low-risk patients was further corroborated by a higher IPS, suggesting a greater potential benefit from immune checkpoint blockade therapy. Collectively, these findings position our risk score not only as a robust prognostic indicator but also as a valuable predictor of immunotherapy response, highlighting its dual utility in guiding clinical management for HCC patients.

Furthermore, we initially assessed the mRNA expression of six risk genes in HCC cell lines using qRT-PCR and found that these genes were generally upregulated. Notably, KIAA1841 and TUBA1B exhibited prominent expression in HCC cell lines. Therefore, we measured the expression levels of KIAA1841 and TUBA1B in the blood of HCC patients using qRT-PCR. The results demonstrated that both KIAA1841 and TUBA1B were upregulated in HCC cell lines and patient blood but showed a decrease following surgical treatment. These findings further confirm the significant roles of KIAA1841 and TUBA1B in the development of HCC. KIAA1841 is a novel SANT and BTB domain-containing protein SANBR that inhibits Class switch recombination (CSR)58. It has garnered attention due to its relationship with impaired peroxisome production and its role in regulating the transcription of genes critical for immune cell development and function58,59. Tubulin alpha 1b (TUBA1B) is an important microtubule isoform involved in the formation of the cytoskeleton60. It has been shown to be upregulated in various cancers, including liver cancer61. Specifically, consistent with our results, TUBA1B expression was elevated in HCC tumor tissues and proliferating HCC cells, and increased expression of TUBA1B has been associated with poor overall survival in HCC patients60,61. To further validate the protein expression of KIAA1841, a gene not previously studied in this context, we conducted IHC and Western blotting analyses. Consistent results were obtained from both IHC and Western blotting analyses, which demonstrated that KIAA1841 expression was significantly higher in HCC tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues. In agreement with the earlier qRT-PCR results, KIAA1841 protein levels were also elevated in HCC cells, further supporting its potential role in HCC pathogenesis. Although KIAA1841 and TUBA1B are primarily upregulated in tumor tissues and cell lines, their detection in blood samples is biologically plausible. Prior studies have shown that circulating tumor cells and tumor-derived exosomes can transport RNA transcripts into the bloodstream. Our findings therefore indicate that the blood-based detection of these genes reflects tumor activity and may provide a minimally invasive approach for prognosis and monitoring. Importantly, our assay does not measure cell-free DNA but rather RNA transcripts, further supporting the interpretation that these signals originate from tumor-derived cellular components. However, there are few studies on the expression of these two genes in liver cancer and metabolic syndrome, particularly concerning KIAA1841, and their specific mechanisms warrant further investigation. At present, we conclude that KIAA1841 and TUAB1B have great potential as prognostic and immunotherapy markers for HCC.

Conclusion

In this research, we constructed a risk score model for HCC prognosis based on the expression differences of 39 metabolic syndrome-related genes identified in this experiment. We examined the value of the risk score in predicting the response of HCC to immunotherapy and analyzed the differences in the expression of immune-related cells in tumors with high and low risk scores. Our findings revealed that patients with lower risk scores exhibited a more active immune response and significantly prolonged OS. Notably, the identification of two hub genes, KIAA1841 and TUBA1B, in both HCC and MetS within validation cohorts may serve as predictive biomarkers for detecting covert HCC and MetS patients. A systematic assessment of the risk score could enhance our understanding of cancer and contribute to the development of more personalized and precise therapeutic strategies.
