Application of cephalad approach laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal mesh repair in mid-lower abdominal incisional hernias
Authors: Haibo Wang, Shoulian Wang, Chunpeng Pan, Xiaochun Ni, Chihao Zhang, Binggen Li, Jiwei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbdominal incisional hernia is a common complication following abdominal surgery. With disease progression, it can severely compromise patients’ quality of life and even threaten their lives, Among these, the surgical treatment of large incisional hernias remains both challenging and uncertain. As a standard surgical procedure for inguinal hernia, transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair has been adopted worldwide and achieved satisfactory results. Based on this experience, we applied the TAPP procedure to small- and medium-sized incisional hernias in the middle and lower abdomen and achieved promising outcomes.MethodsA retrospective statistical analysis was conducted on all patients with median incisional hernias treated with TAPP repair between January 2022 and December 2024. Demographic data, hernia classification, operation time, postoperative complications (such as seroma, hematoma, and incision site infection), readmission rate, and recurrence rate were analyzed.ResultsA total of 75 patients with incisional hernias underwent TAPP surgery. The operative time ranged from 92 to 141 min, with an average of 112.5 min. The visual analog scale score ranged from 2 to 5 points, with an average of 3.3 points. The postoperative hospital stay ranged from 2 to 4 days, averaging 2.7 days. Seromas occurred in eight patients but had no adverse effect on the final outcome; all cases resolved spontaneously during the 2-month follow-up. No hematoma, incision site infection, or chronic pain was reported. None of the patients required readmission within a short period, and no recurrence was observed during 4–30 months of follow-up.ConclusionCephalad approach TAPP repair is a safe and effective technique for the treatment of small- and medium-sized incisional hernias in the lower abdomen and warrants further investigation.

Background

An abdominal incisional hernia refers to an abdominal wall hernia caused by poor healing of the myofascial layer that plays a major supportive role in the abdominal wall after abdominal surgery. It is associated with both local and systemic factors. The former includes incisional infection, suturing techniques, and wound dehiscence; the latter includes obesity, chronic diseases that increase intra-abdominal pressure, diabetes mellitus, and malnutrition. Relevant research has shown that the long-term outcomes of incisional hernia are far inferior to those of inguinal hernia [1]. Among these, the surgical treatment of large incisional hernias remains both challenging and uncertain. With the advancement and refinement of laparoscopic technology, the surgical treatment of incisional hernias is now primarily laparoscopic. The commonly used laparoscopic intraperitoneal onlay mesh repair (IPOM) has become the first choice for the surgical treatment of incisional hernia because of its relatively simple and safe operation [2, 3]. However, IPOM remains associated with abdominal adhesions and mesh contact-related complications and is therefore still considered controversial [4]. In recent years, some researchers have sought to explore new abdominal wall planes and approaches for hernia mesh repair under endoscopy, such as total endoscopic sublay repair [5]and the enhanced-view totally extraperitoneal repair (TEP) approach [6]. However, these procedures often need to be performed by experienced hernia surgeons and are too difficult for routine beginners.

As a standard surgical procedure for inguinal hernia, transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair has been adopted worldwide and achieved satisfactory results [7]. It is generally believed that TAPP repair is easier to learn than TEP repair. In the field of ventral hernia repair, a similar principle should apply: the TAPP approach is likely easier than the aforementioned TEP technique. Based on this experience, we applied the TAPP procedure to small- and medium-sized incisional hernias in the middle and lower abdomen and achieved promising outcomes. Here, we report our research practice.

Methods

Study population

Between January 2022 and December 2024, 88 patients with incisional hernias were admitted to the Second Department of General Surgery at the Ninth People’s Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine. Patients with defect lengths greater than 10 cm were excluded, and 75 patients met the criteria for small- and medium-sized incisional hernias. All patients successfully underwent laparoscopic TAPP repair without any changes to the surgical plan. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon. The clinical data were retrospectively analyzed.

Surgical method

After pneumoperitoneum was established using a Veress needle, a 12-mm trocar was inserted for the visual port. After the primary adhesions were lysed, the additional working ports were inserted in sequence, following the principle of triangular port arrangement. Pneumoperitoneum pressure was maintained at 13 mmHg (Figs. 1a and 2a). Results

Of the 75 patients, 35 were male and 40 were female. A total of 58 patients had an incisional hernia secondary to laparoscopic radical resection of rectal cancer (Dixon), 7 secondary to laparoscopic radical resection of the right hemicolectomy, 8 secondary to preventive ileostomy closure, and 2 secondary to laparoscopic radical resection of the left hemicolectomy. According to the European Hernia Society incisional hernia classification, there were 18 cases of M3, 40 cases of M4, 10 cases of M5, and 7 cases of L3. All surgeries were successfully completed by the same surgeon without any intraoperative surgical conversion. No severe intraoperative complications occurred.

The data showed that the mean operation time was 112.5 ± 21.2 min (range, 92–141 min), the mean defect size was 6.5 ± 1.3 cm (range, 3.5–8.0 cm), the mean patch area was 216.4 ± 45.8 cm2 (range, 120–300 cm2), and the mean visual analog scale score on the first day after surgery was 3.3 ± 1.4 points (range, 2–5 points). After surgery, the patients were encouraged to ambulate as early as possible to promote postoperative functional recovery. The mean hospital stay was 2.7 ± 1.4 days (range, 2–5 days).

All patients returned to the hospital for follow-up 4 weeks after surgery, after careful physical examination, we will further complete abdominal CT examination if postoperative recurrence is suspected. The patients were followed up by phone every 2–3 months. As of April 2025, 75 patients had been followed up for a mean of 19.5 ± 1.6 months (range, 4–30 months). Eight patients developed seromas, which were treated during outpatient follow-up and resolved spontaneously within 2 months. No hematoma, surgical site infection, or chronic pain was observed. None of the patients required readmission during the short-term follow-up period. None of the patients experienced recurrence after follow-up for 4–30 months (Table 1).

Discussion

Incisional hernia of the abdominal wall usually occurs after abdominal surgery and is a common long-term complication with a high incidence. If not treated in time, the condition may progress into a large incisional hernia, forming a “second abdominal cavity” that is no longer controlled by the abdominal muscles and diaphragm. When the volume ratio of this second abdominal cavity to the original abdominal cavity exceeds a certain threshold, it can lead to functional decompensation of the abdominal muscles and diaphragm. This condition is known as a huge incisional hernia with abdominal wall insufficiency (loss of domain) [8] and significantly compromises patient safety. Therefore, once an abdominal incisional hernia is diagnosed, active treatment measures should be taken [9, 10].However, the treatment of incisional hernia is usually accompanied by certain difficulties and challenges. In this article, we applied the laparoscopic TAPP technique for the treatment of mid-lower abdominal incisional hernias and achieved promising clinical outcomes.

The earliest application of laparoscopic treatment for abdominal incisional hernia can be traced back to IPOM repair in 1993 [11]. Owing to its many advantages, such as avoiding abdominal wall tissue separation, the simple surgical operation, the greatly reduced incidence of wound complications, and rapid minimally invasive recovery, it has been rapidly adopted and popularized worldwide, becoming the preferred treatment for abdominal incisional hernias. In the past two decades of development, however, problems associated with IPOM have gradually emerged, including complications related to intracavitary patches, postoperative pain caused by tack fixation, and the high cost of surgical consumables [12]. As a result, some researchers have advocated for placing the mesh in the retromuscular or extraperitoneal space for incisional hernia repair. Over the past decade, various endoscopic retromuscular or extraperitoneal patch repair techniques have been developed, including total endoscopic sublay repair and enhanced-view TEP approaches. The principles of these techniques are similar to those of TEP repair for inguinal hernia; however, they require a tedious process of cavity construction and space expansion, involve a longer learning curve, and are difficult for beginners to master. Moreover, these procedures carry a higher risk of secondary injuries. By contrast, the TAPP approach is simpler and more intuitive. Most hernia surgeons are more likely to master the TAPP approach during inguinal hernia surgery, making it reasonable to apply similar principles to the repair of ventral incisional hernias [13].

As well known, TAPP repair, together with TEP repair, are the two standard surgical procedures for inguinal hernia surgery [14–16]. The TAPP approach is more popular in clinical practice because of its simplicity, wide surgical field of view, clear anatomical structure, and ease of mastery. However, with a deeper understanding of the TAPP, some researchers have begun to expand its application to the treatment of other types of abdominal wall hernias. Marzena et al. [17] introduced the lateral TAPP approach for the treatment of primary umbilical hernias and reported relatively good results. Compared with the anterior approach, it allows for a larger mesh coverage, which is especially beneficial for obese patients, making the procedure safer and more reliable. However, the operation time is longer than that of the open sublay mesh repair approach.

The application of TAPP repair in the treatment of abdominal incisional hernias is mainly reflected in the recent rapid development of robot-assisted TAPP repair, which is primarily seen in European and American countries. Johannes et al. [18] conducted a cohort study comparing 118 patients with umbilical and incisional hernias using robot-assisted TAPP repair and retromuscular space repair. No surgical plan was changed during surgery, and no recurrence was observed during postoperative follow-up. However, the number of incisional hernia cases in the TAPP group in the study was relatively small (10 cases). Another study [19] compared robot-assisted TAPP with open sublay repair for incisional hernias and found that the robot-assisted TAPP group had clear advantages, including shorter hospital stays, fewer intraoperative complications, and higher patient satisfaction. Regarding postoperative complications, there were no surgical site infections, and only two cases of asymptomatic seromas were reported, significantly fewer than in the sublay group. However, the limitation of a small sample size remained. We believe that robotic TAPP repair can greatly simplify intraperitoneal operations, largely depending on improvements and refinements in surgical equipment to meet the technical demands of various positions in the abdominal cavity. By contrast, in China, robot-assisted hernia surgery has not yet been widely adopted; therefore, classic laparoscopy is still relied upon for the surgical repair of abdominal wall incisional hernias, and there are very few related studies.

Many guidelines emphasize that laparoscopic extraperitoneal repair for incisional hernias is safe when performed by experienced surgeons, which also highlights the technical difficulty of using the TAPP approach for treating incisional hernias. Based on our experience, we summarize several technical challenges and solutions. First, the port layout should be designed flexibly according to the hernia’s location, selecting a high position on the opposite side of the hernia to establish the visual port. For example, if the hernia is on the left side of the middle lower abdomen, the visual port should be started in the right upper abdomen. Second, intraperitoneal adhesiolysis is essential because all patients with abdominal wall incisional hernias have a history of abdominal surgery, making adhesions inevitable. Effective adhesiolysis and safe reduction of hernia contents are crucial, and apart from meticulous intraoperative dissection, taking a detailed preoperative history—especially concerning prior abdominal infections—helps estimate adhesion severity. Third, dissecting the preperitoneal or retromuscular space is often challenging because creating a purely extraperitoneal space is difficult, particularly when separating the peritoneum from the rectus abdominis area around the umbilicus. At this stage, it is necessary to actively incise the posterior sheath of the rectus abdominis to enter the retromuscular space and connect it with the retromuscular preperitoneal space. This posterior layer, consisting of the posterior sheath and peritoneum, is known as the “visceral sac,” so TAPP incisional hernia repair also follows the principle of total visceral sac separation [20]. Fourth, hernia sac management in abdominal incisional hernia differs completely from inguinal hernia: complete dissection of the hernia sac is often difficult, so transection of the hernia sac with continued forward dissection of the preperitoneal space is more feasible, while protecting key structures such as the linea alba and inferior epigastric vessels. Fifth, closure of the hernia defect is required at both the “ceiling” (anterior abdominal defect) and the “floor” (peritoneal opening after hernia sac transection), demanding extensive intra-abdominal suturing skills. To close the anterior defect, we recommend using the reverse needle suturing technique with a barbed suture. Although technically more challenging than traditional passing needle suturing, this method effectively eliminates dead spaces, closes the defect precisely, and prevents skin bacteria from being introduced into the surgical field, thus reducing infection risk. Mastery of this technique requires a long learning curve and repeated intra-abdominal suturing practice.

In the early stage of our clinical practice of performing TAPP approach for patients with incisional hernias, we encountered certain technical challenges. Due to the difficulty in managing peritoneal tears intraoperatively, we had to convert the surgical approach to IPOM approach to complete the hernia repair. However, with the accumulation of clinical experience and the continuous refinement of surgical techniques, we are now able to perform the TAPP procedure proficiently in all patients, with no serious postoperative complications. Except for three cases of asymptomatic seroma, no patient required readmission in the short term, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up, which is an encouraging result. However, several limitations remain, including the small sample size, narrow inclusion criteria for incisional hernia, and the need for longer-term follow-up to obtain more reliable outcomes. Notably, in some of our cases with larger hernia defects, we further performed TAR technique to establish communication between the midline region and the lateral spaces, thus ensuring a broader coverage area for the mesh.

Conclusion

In summary, the TAPP approach for incisional hernia repair effectively avoids the intracavitary mesh-related complications associated with IPOM, requires less traumatic fixation with tackers, and does not necessitate the use of anti-adhesion mesh, making the technique more cost-effective. Additionally, it reduces incision-related complications seen with open sublay repair. Moreover, TAPP repair is simple and practical, aligns well with the learning curve of hernia surgeons, and is more beginner-friendly, safe, and feasible. With the continuous expansion of indications and ongoing technical refinement, starting from relatively simple abdominal wall incisional hernias, this approach can be gradually extended to the repair of larger or more complex incisional hernias as experience accumulates.
