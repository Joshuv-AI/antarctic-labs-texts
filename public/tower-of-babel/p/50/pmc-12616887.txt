Nonlinear integration of sensory inputs and behavioral state by a single neuron in C. elegans
Authors: Amanda Ray, Andrew Gordus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYContext is important for sensory integration; however, explicitly modeling this property as a function of precise presynaptic inputs is not trivial. In Caenorhabditis elegans, the paired interneurons AIBL and AIBR (AIB) integrate both sensory and motor information and strongly drive reversal behavior. Through a series of experiments to monitor reversal behavior and manipulate sensory input onto AIB, we find that while AIB activity is primarily a convolution of behavioral state, its sensory responses are not integrated independently. Instead, the gain in sensory input increases during the transition to the reversal state. Sensory information therefore reinforces the decision to reverse. Context-dependent behavioral responses to sensory input are well-documented. Here, we show this property can be localized to single neurons in the nematode nervous system. This integration property likely plays an important role in context-dependent decision-making, as well as the highly variable dynamics of the C. elegans nervous system.

INTRODUCTION

Brains evolved to perform complex nonlinear tasks. Behavioral responses to sensory cues are often context-dependent. Rather than treat sensory information as independent inputs, neuronal networks often integrate sensory information synergistically, which has the potential to encode more information.1 Nonlinear integration by retinal ganglion cells allows for the encoding of finer spatial detail and chromatic contrast.2–5 In mammalian neurons, dendritic branches serve as individual computational units. Previous reports have proposed a two-layer model of integration where each dendrite processes inputs separately and is then summed collectively to the cell as a whole.6,7 Whether or not a neuron will perform nonlinear integration is strongly influenced by the spatiotemporal pattern of presynaptic input at the dendrites. For example, in layer V and CA1 pyramidal neurons, asynchronous inputs delivered further apart across dendritic branches are integrated linearly, while synchronized synaptic inputs clustered closely along dendrites lead to supralinear integration.6–9

With a stereotyped connectome that was recently updated, the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans offers the opportunity to consistently stimulate and record from the same pre- and postsynaptic pair of neurons reliably across different individuals.10,11 Most nematode neurons have graded potentials that lack dendritic arbors and instead have processes in which pre- and postsynaptic contacts occur en passant.10,11 While many of these synapses appear randomly distributed, several interneurons possess synapses along their processes that are clustered. Previous computational analysis by Ruach et al. proposed these clusters act as local integration hubs in lieu of dendritic branches, which may enable nonlinear computation by the postsynaptic neuron.12

Clustering analysis of synaptic density in the C. elegans connectome revealed two of the top-scoring interneurons with high presynaptic clustering were the AIB pair (the paired interneurons AIBL and AIBR) (Table S1, see STAR Methods). AIB is an interneuron that integrates sensory and motor-driven inputs and drives reversal behavior.10,13–16 Along the AIB process, there is a unique bias between these sensory-related and behavior-related synapses, unlike other nematode neurons, which tend to have more randomly arranged presynaptic partners along the process.10,11 Chemosensory neurons and sensory integration interneurons tend to synapse along the proximal side of AIB’s process closest to the cell body, while neurons associated with motor behaviors lie along the opposite distal end of AIB’s process (Figure 1A; Table S2).10,11

Despite integrating both sensory and motor information, AIB activity most closely correlates with the reverse-motor program.14,16–19 Most of AIB’s postsynaptic partners are motor-related neurons, and when optogenetically activated, AIB increases the likelihood of reversal behavior.20,21 Conversely, optogenetic activation of reversal-state neurons presynaptic to AIB also drives AIB activity,16 which indicates AIB participates in a reinforcing feedback loop for reversals. However, the strength of sensory drive onto AIB is strongly influenced by competing input from behavioral state via RIM, and in the absence of reversal activity, AIB is more likely to produce activity that correlates with sensory input.16 How AIB integrates these competing inputs is not well established.

To investigate the integration properties of AIB, we chose 3 synaptic partners of AIB: AWC, AIA, and RIM (Figure 1B). This combination of candidate neurons provides a simple sensorimotor network to study integration, where AWC and AIA provide sensory information onto AIB, and RIM represents the behavioral state of the animal.

The simplest model of AIB integration is that it integrates presynaptic inputs linearly and independently. For example, when AVA is optogenetically stimulated, it can induce a graded potential in AIB through RIM that appears to be a convolution of AVA activity.16 However, AIB responses to sensory input are highly variable.16,17 This variability is observed behaviorally as well. Even though nematodes may have a preference for a certain odor, individual behavioral responses to odor are highly inconsistent between individuals and within an individual over multiple trials.16,17 Behavioral responses to sensory stimuli can also be highly contextual. How reliably a nematode responds to touch stimuli is strongly dependent on its behavioral state.22,23 This implies that presynaptic inputs may not necessarily be independent, and postsynaptic processing may rely on cross-dependencies between presynaptic or neuromodulatory partners. Here, we examine the integration properties of AIB and how well nonlinear integration assumptions reliably explain AIB responses to presynaptic activity. We find that sensory gain increases during periods of reversal initiation and provide a simple model to reflect this dependency. We find this model works well not only for our experiments but also for other AIB presynaptic sensory inputs from whole-brain datasets. Given AIB’s role in driving reversal behavior, this model reveals that sensory inputs are more salient during reversal initiation, and this in turn reinforces reversals.

RESULTS

A combination of sensory and motor-related synaptic partners drives AIB activity

AIB is a first-layer amphid interneuron that processes information between many sensory and motor-related neurons and other interneurons.10,11 A detailed look at the composition of all the synaptic partners of AIB and their locations along its process11 reveals discrete patterning of presynaptic neuron identities (Table S2). Most of the proximal presynaptic inputs are from amphid sensory neurons, and most of the distal synapses are comprised of presynaptic and postsynaptic motor-related neurons (Figure 1A). Among the interneuron synaptic partners, many of them are like AIB and integrate sensorimotor information, including other first-layer amphid interneurons (AIA, AIY, and AIZ). This polarized separation of presynaptic identities showcases AIB’s role in synthesizing sensory information to drive motor behavior such as reversals.

We chose a subset of neurons to investigate the sensorimotor integration properties of AIB (Figure 1B). AWC is an amphid chemosensory neuron that detects odorants and can variably drive activity in AIB based on odor stimulation.15,16,24 AIA is AIB’s primary presynaptic partner on the proximal process and accounts for roughly 12% of the presynaptic inputs out of a total of 289 inputs from 50 neurons presynaptic to AIB (input numbers based on electron microscopy [EM] synapse sections from https://www.wormwiring.org).11 AIA is a sensory integration interneuron that receives many direct inputs from chemosensory neurons, including AWC.24,25 AIA has several inhibitory synapses onto AIB and AWC, and its activity is typically anti-correlated with AIB and AWC.13,25 AIB shares several pre- and postsynaptic connections with the RIM interneuron, which in turn synapses onto the AVA locomotion interneuron to drive reversal behaviors and is tightly coupled to AVA activity during reversals.14,16,18,19 RIM is the primary postsynaptic partner of AIB, with ∼36% of the synapses out of a total of 158 outputs from 43 neurons postsynaptic to AIB.11

To monitor the activity of AIB in the context of sensorimotor inputs, we expressed the genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP7s26 in AIB, AIA, and AVA together in one strain (Figure 1C). Since RIM is in close proximity to AIB, making it difficult to record from both simultaneously, we recorded from AVA instead as a proxy for RIM activity. RIM and AVA have very closely correlated activity and encode the behavioral state of the animal since they are both active during reversals.16,18,19 The soma from AWC is more lateral than the somata from AVA and AIB and was recorded separately in a different GCaMP strain. AWC has robust and consistent hyperpolarization in response to the odorant isoamyl alcohol, IAA, and repolarizes upon removal of odor,15,16,27 while the responses of AIB, AVA, and AIA to odor were more variable (Figure 1D). We recorded AWCOFF responses using the str-2 promoter (referred to as simply AWC for brevity), but both AWCOFF and AWCON respond to IAA and therefore should both contribute to AIB activity.28,29 We also expressed the red-shifted channelrhodopsin, Chrimson,30 in AIA in our triple-GCaMP strain to optogenetically stimulate AIA in the presence or absence of odor pulses. During our recordings, AVA is frequently sporadically active, allowing us to monitor spontaneous reversal activity without a need for direct stimulation. Thus, we were able to monitor and stimulate AWC and AIA with odor and optogenetics, respectively, to deliver sensory inputs to AIB, while simultaneously monitoring the reversal state of the animal via AVA. Together, this allowed us to observe AIB activity in the context of varying sensory inputs and behavioral states.

To stimulate AWC and AIA individually and simultaneously, the delivery of sensory inputs (odor and red light) was separated into three different phases for each animal—IAA odor only, red light only, and odor + red light—to stimulate AWC only, AIA only, and AWC and AIA together, respectively (Figure 1D). AIA has variable responses to odor,25 which is why the usage of optogenetics with Chrimson and red light was necessary to facilitate consistent AIA stimulation, so the odor-only phase was sometimes redundant with odor + red light in AIA if AIA was also responsive to odor.

Altogether, these three sources of inputs (AWC, AIA, and RIM/AVA) are integrated by AIB and are often represented in AIB’s activity (Figure 1D). AIB is primarily influenced by inputs from RIM and AVA, which corroborates previous findings,16,18,19 with sensory inputs having a mild effect on AIB activity.

AIB dynamics can be modeled as a convolution of AVA activity

Due to the dynamic nature of AIB’s activity in response to presynaptic input, we sought to find an appropriate method to model the contributions of each neuron to AIB’s activity. The convolution of kernels with neuronal inputs is a common method to model the response properties of neurons, either as a convolution of sensory inputs31 or motor behavior.19 The kernel itself represents the response kinetics of the neuron. Since AIB activity is tightly coupled to reversal activity,16–19 we first modeled AIB as a convolution of AVA activity (Figure 2):

The kernel characterizes the response kinetics of AIB, where the difference in the two exponential functions (defined by rate constants α1 and α2) produces the rise and decay kinetics of AIB’s response to input, and A weights the amplitude of AIB’s response to an input.

Kernel parameters (α1 and α2) and A were fit individually for each experiment to find the optimal solution for each parameter that would produce a model of AIB with the smallest residual between modeled and real AIB activity (see STAR Methods). Convolving with AVA alone captured most AIB dynamics (Figures 2A–2C and S1), while convolving with AWC or AIA alone captured very little (Figure S2). The distributions of alpha values for the AVA convolution were fairly narrow (median values ±SD: α1:0.16s−1±0.001, α2:0.64s−1±0.003), while the amplitudes (A) had greater variance, consistent with the response kinetics of AIB being fairly robust across experiments and animals, but the relative gain of AVA varying across trials (Figure S1). Due to their narrow variance, we chose to fix α1 and α2 to their median values for subsequent modeling but allowed the amplitudes to float to reflect the variable gain of each neuron across animals and trials.

The decay t1/2 for this kernel from peak amplitude was ∼6 s, which is comparable to the timescale of AIB activity observed in freely moving animals (1/k = 8.69 s, t1/2 = 6.02 s).19 It is important to note that even though the timescale of reversals in restrained animals is much longer than in freely moving animals,18,19,32 the timescale of AIB’s response to input remains consistent, indicating that the integration properties of AIB are not strongly influenced by the timescale of reversal behavior. The measured kernel was not deconvolved with a GCaMP kernel since the t1/2 decay of GCaMP7s26 is 0.24 s, which is 25x faster than the observed AIB kernel.

AIB integration of sensory inputs is dependent on reversal state

The simplest approach to include sensory input in the model was to model AIB as a sum of convolutions (Figure 2D), where the same kernel was used throughout (i.e., same α1 and α2), but each neuron’s amplitude was fit individually to reflect their differing contributions to AIB:

In most cases, AVA had the largest amplitude, reflecting the observation that AVA has the greatest influence on AIB activity (Figures 2E and 2N). Following AVA, AWC usually had the second highest contribution, reflecting the frequent sensory dynamics provided by AWC. The contribution from AIA was typically small and sometimes negative, which is consistent with AIA having a positive response to odor, which is anti-correlated with AWC’s and AIB’s typical response.16,25,33 The small contribution of AIA was surprising given its numerous synapses onto AIB (Figure 1B). When comparing the AVA-only convolution versus the AVA+AWC+AIA summation convolution, the summation model is a significant improvement and better captures the nuances of AIB activity in response to the pulses of sensory stimuli (Figures 2F and 2M).

Since the amplitudes are constants within each trace, the model assumes the contribution of each neuron is static. The residual of this model relative to the experimental AIB data was greatly improved (Figures 2I and 2M), but also expected, since this model was overfit for each time point. With this approach, the amplitudes of AWC and AIA relative to AVA were small (<10%), but occasionally these amplitudes increased (Figures 2H and S3). However, we noticed that these sudden spikes in AWC and AIA amplitudes frequently coincided with when AVA activity increased. This seemed to indicate that the fluctuations in sensory amplitudes were not arbitrary but tied to reversal state.

With this approach, the sensory amplitudes are time dependent but not arbitrary. Instead, they are tied to the timing of changes in reversal activity via AVA (Figures 2J and 2K). The resulting model (Figure 2L) performs similarly to the overfit time-dependent sensory model, albeit slightly worse, yet still significantly better than the summation model (Figure 2M). The improved performance is not simply due to the structure of the model. Swapping the dependencies (i.e., tying AVA gain to changes in sensory derivatives) or modeling AIB as a sum of AVA and AVA time derivatives did not improve performance (Figure S4). Tying the sensory amplitudes to the derivative of AVA activity was sufficient to produce comparable performance to the overfit model. This implies that sensory gain increases while the animal initiates reversals.

Response to sensory input remains low in the absence of reversal-state input

The prior models were based on experiments where sensory and reversal-state neurons were active. To investigate individual contributions of each neuron, we modeled AIB activity in experiments where AWC, AIA, or RIM/AVA were chemogenetically silenced by cell-specific expression of a histamine-gated chloride channel (HisCl)34,35 in these neurons (Figures 3A–3C).

We used two different methods to compare the contributions of individual neurons, which we dubbed “ignore” versus “absent.” In our “full” models, all three presynaptic neurons were experimentally active, and all were included in the models (Figures 3D–3F). These data are the same as shown in Figure 2M. In our ignore models, these same full experiments were modeled but with one neuron ignored to compare how well the model performed if one neuron’s contribution was computationally excluded, despite the neuron still being experimentally active in this scenario. By contrast, in our absent models, a neuron was both experimentally silenced via HisCl, and its contribution excluded in the model. If a presynaptic neuron’s activity contributes significantly to AIB’s activity, then the ignore models should perform poorly compared with the full model. If AIB’s integration properties are dependent on another neuron, then the silencing of that neuron in the absent models should perform worse than the full models. However, if a neuron is ignored or absent and the effect is comparable to the full counterpart, then this indicates relatively low contribution from the neuron because the remaining neurons are sufficient to capture AIB integration.

As expected, ignoring the contribution of RIM/AVA to AIB’s activity led to poor model performance, which now solely relied on sensory input (Figures 3A, 3D, 3G, and 3J). Interestingly, a small subset (n = 5 animals) retained low residuals (<0.2) in the ignored dataset (Figure 3D) due to low AVA activity. In one animal, AVA activity was dynamic, but AIB only integrated sensory inputs. However, if RIM/AVA activity was silenced in the absent model, this sensory-only model performed as well as the full model, confirming that in the absence of reversal activity, AIB activity can be modeled as a sensory-only interneuron. However, it should be noted that the relative fluorescence amplitude of AIB activity was diminished when RIM/AVA was silenced compared with the fluorescence changes observed when RIM/AVA activity was present, which is consistent with the relatively small contribution sensory input provides to AIB activity in the absence of RIM/AVA input (Figure S5A).

The relative amplitude of AWC when RIM/AVA was silenced was comparable to the baseline offsets of the reversal-dependent model when RIM/AVA was active (Figure S5B), which is expected if there is no motor contribution. In the absence of RIM/AVA input, the reversal-dependent model essentially becomes a summation model where the offsets are the amplitudes. However, in the absence of RIM/AVA input, the AIA amplitude appears to increase, which is consistent with the mixed low-amplitude response AIB has to odor in the absence of RIM/AVA. In the example trace shown in Figure 3J, AIB dynamics appear to correlate with AIA early in the trace but then correlate with AWC in the last pulse.

If AWC is ignored, the reversal-dependent model still performs better than the summation model, but if AWC is absent, both models perform comparably (Figures 3B, 3E, 3H, and 3K). A similar observation was made for silencing AIA, but if AIA is ignored, the reversal-dependent model did not perform significantly better than the summation model (Figures 3C, 3F, 3I, and 3L). However, in both AWC- and AIA-silenced backgrounds, sensory responses in AIB were diminished, decreasing the effective difference between the two models. For both models, silencing AWC or AIA led to the amplitude of the other neuron’s contribution being closer to zero (Figure S5F). The AIA amplitude also shifted toward positive values when AWC was silenced since AIA activity is anti-correlated with AWC, which led to a diminished response to AWC sensory input. If sensory inputs are diminished, then AIB activity becomes solely reliant on behavioral inputs.

Overall, ignoring RIM/AVA input dramatically decreases model performance. However, silencing RIM/AVA input does not necessarily lead to worse model performance since AIB activity is now largely tied to sensory input. AIB amplitude also decreases due to the lack of behavioral input, which removes the sensory gain from reversal dependence. Silencing AWC or AIA decreased sensory input and led to a diminished response of AIB to the sensory input from the remaining neuron.

The reversal-dependent model outperforms the summation model for whole-brain NeuroPAL data

While the activity of sensory and reversal-state neurons explored here is sufficient to explain most AIB activity, there are numerous other AIB presynaptic partners. To explore AIB integration in the context of the whole brain, we turned to whole-brain C. elegans recordings with consistent sensory stimulation.36 This dataset encompasses the calcium activity of 231 neurons (189 in the head, 42 in the tail) in 21 animals in response to chemosensory stimuli. In these recordings, 10-s pulses of three chemicals were delivered in a randomized order: 2,3-pentanedione (an attractive odor), 2-butanone (an attractive odor), and sodium chloride (a repulsive taste stimulus). We chose this dataset over other whole-brain C. elegans datasets19,32 for two reasons. One, this particular dataset had consistent pulses of sensory stimulation, similar to our dataset, so sensory neuron dynamics were well-represented for several neurons (Figure 4A). Two, AIA is often under-represented in datasets due to weak calcium dynamics in the soma, but this dataset had several AIA recordings. A different GCaMP was used (GCaMP6s), but the kinetic differences between GCaMP6s and GCaMP7s are small compared with the response dynamics of AIB.26,37 Due to the sensory-driven nature of this dataset, we investigated whether AIB exhibited the same reversal-dependent integration for sensory neurons other than AWC. We selected the most relevant presynaptic sensory inputs based on general correlation to AIB activity—AWC, ADF, ASG, ASH, and AFD (see STAR Methods for how neurons were selected).

We first compared the summation and reversal-dependent models for AWC, AIA, and AVA neuronal activities from the NeuroPAL dataset to confirm model performance. We initially convolved AVA with our kernel to model AIB activity (Figures 4C–4E). We found the kinetic parameters for the AIB kernel from the whole-brain data were similar to our own (our median values α1:0.16s−1±0.001, α2:0.64s−1±0.003; NeuroPAL α1:0.15s−1±0.0003, α2:0.72s−1±0.002), which suggests the observed response kinetics from both experiments is an innate property of AIB (Figures S6A–S6C). Note there is an outlier for the NeuroPAL alpha values, and the standard deviation range was calculated excluding this outlier (with the outlier included: α1:±0.005, α2:±0.61; Figure S6D). As observed in our experiments, AIB activity is largely driven by AVA activity in the whole-brain dataset (Figures 4C–4E). We next applied the same summation model with AIA and AWC, this time incorporating both AWCOFF and AWCON activity from the whole-brain dataset (Figures 4F–4H). Although the NeuroPAL experiments did not use IAA, AWCOFF responds to 2,3-pentanedione, and AWCON responds to 2-butanone,28,29 which were both delivered during whole-brain imaging, mirroring a similar pattern of AWC stimulation in our experiments such that both are activated, albeit at different times. As observed in our experiments, incorporating sensory information improved model performance, consistent with AIB’s role in integrating sensory information (Figures 4F–4H). Additionally, consistent with our observations, the reversal-dependent model outperformed the summation model for the whole-brain data (Figures 4I–4K and 4L).

We extended our modeling approach by incorporating more sensory inputs (AWC, ADF, ASG, ASH, and AFD) to investigate how AIB integrates information from other sensory neurons. Sensory neuron activities were highly variable since they responded differently to the three chemicals used in the whole-brain experiments. To assess how reversal behavior may influence the gain of these sensory inputs for AIB, we tested each neuron individually as a sum of RIM activity (instead of AVA) and the sensory neuron’s activity, or as a function of the derivative of RIM activity, as used previously for AWC. We used RIM instead of AVA since it is present in the dataset and is the true synaptic partner of AIB, and since AVA and RIM activities are similar, modeling with RIM-only had identical kinetic properties and residuals as the AVA-only model in the NeuroPAL dataset (Figure S6). Because some sensory neuron left-right pairs had similar activities, these were combined, and their averaged trace was used for modeling to minimize parameter overfitting (indicated in Figure 5 as “NeuronL/R”). Those with differing L/R activities were modeled individually (indicated as “NeuronL” and “NeuronR”).

Consistent with our prior observations with AWC, models that tied the gain of sensory neuron activity to changes in RIM activity in the reversal-dependent model were significantly better than the RIM-only model and their summation counterparts (with the exception of AFD, which was only significantly better than the summation model but not RIM-only; Figures 5A–5T). This was true even for sensory neurons that responded to different combinations of stimuli than AWC, such as ASH (which responded to the NaCl pulse) and ADF (which responded to pentanedione and NaCl pulses). For comparison, we also included a presynaptic input of AIB that had low correlation to its activity, FLPL (only the left side of FLP synapsed onto both AIB left and right, so only FLPL was used; Figures 5U–5X). The reversal-dependent model with FLPL performed similarly to AFD, which only performed better than the summation model. Both neurons have fewer synapses to AIB than the other neurons tested here, and both do not participate in the feedforward circuit with AIA, so their influence on AIB activity is likely limited (Figure 4A). Overall, modeling with other presynaptic inputs demonstrates AIB integration potentially extends to other sensory inputs, and sensory gain enhancement during reversals is not limited to AWC.

The reversal-dependent model reinforces decision to reverse for prior sensory experiences

To compare the sensorimotor implications of the summation versus reversal-state models, we computationally slid a single AWC pulse relative to the onset of an AVA ON-state and modeled AIB’s response (Figure 6). In the summation model, AIB’s reinforcement of the reversal state is maximal when the peak of the AWC pulse occurs after the initiation of the reversal (Figure 6C). This is not ideal, because AWC activity indicates the removal of an attractive cue, so if an animal performs a reversal that is followed by a signal that an attractive cue was removed, it is maladaptive to reinforce a behavior that triggered a decrease in environmental quality. However, in the reversal-dependent model, AIB’s reinforcement of the reversal state occurs when the AWC pulse precedes the initiation of the reversal (Figure 6D). This is due to the slow integration time of AIB, as well as the increased sensory gain that occurs at the onset of reversal behavior. Reinforcement of the reversal state after an attractive cue was removed is preferable, because if the environmental quality has decreased, AIB reinforces the decision to perform a reversal.

DISCUSSION

AIB is an important interneuron that integrates both behavioral and sensory information and is critical to behavioral responses to sensory stimuli. AIB drives reversal behavior but is also activated by reversal interneurons, creating a positive feedback loop. While much of its activity is dominated by the reversal circuit, sensory-triggered responses can also be observed; however, these responses are more variable and appear to be tied to the reversal state.16,17 The nexus of sensory and behavioral input onto AIB, and its role in reversal behavior, make it an ideal candidate for sensory reinforcement. The removal of attractive stimuli (sensed by AWC and inversely by AIA) increases the probability of reversals as the animal tries to reorient and find the attractive cue. This is consistent with driving reversal behavior when the environment decreases in quality. Since AIB is also activated by the reversal circuit, which it contributes to, it can serve as a positive reinforcer of reversal behavior when it has detected a past decrease in sensory quality.

The separate computations performed by AIB are reflected in its polarized anatomy. AWC and AIA presynapses both fall on the proximal side of AIB’s neuronal process that is dominated by presynaptic input from sensory neurons, whereas RIM presynapses are on the distal process that is dominated by both pre- and postsynaptic partners tied to motor and behavioral state. Since AIB activity is tightly coupled to reversals, presynaptic inputs in the distal process appear to drive the calcium dynamics observed in the soma. Nearly all of AIB’s postsynaptic partners are also on the distal process, so this region may serve as a local unit of computation. The conditional model proposed here implies that the sensory input from the proximal process is only salient for the distal computation if a decision to reverse has been made.

Local computation of presynaptic input to influence local postsynaptic output is not unique to C. elegans. Drosophila Kenyon cells (KCs) have reciprocal synapses with dopamine neurons (DANs) within the same neurite compartment that provides a reinforcing positive feedback loop to enhance learning.38 In the retina, A17 amacrine cells form inhibitory reciprocal local synapses with the retinal bipolar cells (RBCs) that excite them in order to curtail their responses to light.39 A similar phenomenon occurs in the olfactory bulb, where mitral cells form local reciprocal synapses with inhibitory granule cells that shape the odor-induced activity of the mitral cells.40 In all three cases, local computation enables fast tuning of sensory perception.

Other C. elegans interneurons, such as RIA, not only have segregated presynaptic inputs but also produce compartmentalized calcium dynamics along its process, despite an absence of a membrane barrier between these compartments.41 This property has also been observed in the RIS neuron.42 Other C. elegans neurons besides these have clustered synapses along their processes and may also be sources of nonlinear computation.12 In mice, thalamocortical synapses, which convey sensory information from whisker sensing, exhibit segregated compartmentalization as well and densely cluster basally and near the apical trunk of layer 5 pyramidal neurons. Pairing sensory inputs from thalamocortical synapses (basally clustered) with non-sensory prestimulus depolarization from other cortical regions (clustered at apical tufts) drives increased action potential bursts in L5PT pyramidal neurons in barrel cortex.43 Thus, non-sensory prestimulus depolarization at distal dendrites facilitates the response of L5PT neurons to thalamic sensory input. This is fundamentally a nonlinear computation: the integration of thalamic input is dependent on the integration of non-sensory input at the apical tufts. This is similar to the topology and responses of AIB: non-sensory input from RIM at the distal process facilitates increased sensory gain from the proximal process.

The molecular mechanisms underlying AIB’s nonlinear integration remain to be further explored. In mammalian neurons, strengthened superlinear activity is proposed to be mediated by NMDA glutamate receptors7,44; however, AIB does not express NMDA receptors.45 It should be noted that since C. elegans neurons lack action potentials due to a lack of voltage-gated sodium channels,46 the molecular basis of nonlinear integration between mammalian and C. elegans neurons may operate through different mechanisms. In C. elegans, AVA expresses different excitatory and inhibitory glutamate receptors in a compartmentalized manner throughout its process, which in turn mediate different nociceptive responses in the animal depending on the area of stimulation (head versus tail).47 AIB expresses both inhibitory (AVR-14) and excitatory (GLR-1) glutamate receptors.48,49 Future experiments could probe if glutamatergic signaling from RIM is necessary for AIB sensory integration by repeating experiments in animals with RIM-specific loss of the vesicular glutamate transporter, eat-4.50 In addition to this, AIB shares gap junctions with RIM. AVA has been observed to share rectifying gap junctions with motor neurons, which amplify chemical transmission from AVA.51 A similar mechanism could modulate the nonlinear contribution of RIM motor input to sensory gain in AIB. Neuropeptides also play a significant role in altering functional connectivity in worms.32,52,53 Testing the integration properties of neuropeptide-deficient mutants might reveal their potential role in this process.

Sensory input influences AIB activity, though to a lesser extent than AVA. Silencing AWC and AIA did not have a strong impact on AIB activity, but silencing AWC affected the amplitudes of AIB’s integration of AIA. AIA also tended to have the lowest amplitude compared with AVA and AWC, suggesting AIA has a minor role in AIB integration in our experiments, despite the numerous synapses it shares with AIB. The role of AIA in the circuit may be more context-dependent. Prior work has shown that AIA can operate on different timescales and can promote roaming or dwelling states depending on sensory context.33 Our analysis of the NeuroPAL dataset in Figure 5 demonstrates AIB is more likely to nonlinearly integrate sensory input from neurons that participate in a feedforward circuit with AIA. Further work is needed to clarify the role AIA plays in shaping sensory responses in AIB.

While AIB activity is largely driven by the reversal circuit, we found the onset of reversal behavior increased the gain in sensory input. This is a useful feature for behavioral reinforcement when the animal alters its behavior. For AIB, the saliency of sensory input is highest when a reversal is initiated and helps reinforce the decision to reverse. Sensorimotor feedback between behavioral states and thermotaxis has been previously observed as well,54 suggesting this approach can be extended to other sensory modalities in C. elegans. Behavior-dependent sensory responses have also been observed in the nematode’s touch response, where mechanosensory responses are dependent on the locomotion state of the animal.22,23 Associating changes in sensory perception to changes in behavioral activity is also a critical feature of corollary discharge, or the need to cancel sensory input generated by self-motion.55 Corollary discharge is observed in most sensorimotor systems, as the need to differentiate changes in sensory perception due to egocentric motion and the environment is critical to navigation.56 Therefore, it is advantageous to consider behavioral context when assessing the saliency of sensory input.

Context-dependent sensory responses are common in nervous systems besides C. elegans as well. In the mouse visual cortex, visual gain is enhanced during locomotion compared with remaining stationary.56–58 Whisker sensing is also nonlinearly enhanced in layer 5 pyramidal cells in the barrel cortex in mice trained to move their whiskers during active sensing versus passive whisker sensing of air puffs.59 In Drosophila larvae, animals adjust navigational strategies by executing a different series of runs, turns, and head sweeps during positive or negative thermotaxis.60 Thus, sensorimotor feedback is important for enhancing perception to the environment, and animals constantly assess behavioral contexts with sensory information to promote appropriate behavioral decisions.

Computationally, how does a neuron integrate this context-dependence? Here, we model context-dependence of sensory gain as a nonlinear function that is simply the product of sensory input with the derivative of the behavioral response. In other words, the gain in sensory input increases when there is a change in reversal activity. While neuron polarization is an additive result of presynaptic excitation and inhibition, nonlinear summation has also been observed. Two mammalian examples are superlinear and sublinear visual-auditory integration in the mouse superior colliculus61 and orientation selectivity of layer 2/3 neurons in the visual cortex of ferrets.62 In these two systems, the inclusion of nonlinear summation allows for greater tuning to their respective stimuli. Our results indicate nonlinear computation may also occur in the C. elegans brain. This observation aligns with previous reports stating that global switching between behavioral states is accomplished through nonlinear rather than linear dynamics.63 Whole-brain imaging in C. elegans has shown a considerable degree of state-switching that is tied to behavioral dynamics that seem to show signatures of deterministic chaos.18 Such a system cannot emerge from a solely linear system. Amplifying nonlinearities is necessary to destabilize the system, which is exemplified by the nonlinear gain modeled in this paper. If such computations are coupled to feedback in the system, they could be a source of deterministic chaos and drive the highly variable behavioral switching observed in C. elegans.18

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

C. elegans maintenance

Hermaphrodite animals were grown and maintained under standard conditions using 6 cm nematode growth medium (NGM) plates and fed Escherichia coli OP50 bacteria.64

METHOD DETAILS

Transgenic strains

All transgenic strains generated in this study are listed in the key resources table. AGG0118 and AGG0041 strains were generated by injecting respective constructs below into wild-type N2 nematodes using standard microinjection protocol into the gonads of young adult hermaphrodites.65 CX17432 animals were obtained from,25 and expressed two co-injection markers – Pelt-2::mCherry (intestine) and Punc-122::dsRed (coelomocyte) – to indicate expression of AIA::GCaMP5A and AIA::Chrimson, respectively. To generate AGG0116, we selected CX17432 animals that only expressed the intestinal marker to allow us to select for AIA:: Chrimson expression to cross into our AVA, AIB, AIA::GCaMP7s line (AGG0118) which already had GCaMP7s expression in AIA. We used different combinations of two AIA promoters, Pins-1(s) and Pgcy-28d, in our strains. Pins-1(s) is more specific to AIA but less bright, which was useful for histamine silencing and targeting optogenetically with Chrimson. Pgcy-28d has more non-specific labeling of other neurons other than AIA but has higher expression, which was useful for calcium imaging with GCaMP7s.

Presynaptic clustering

The coordinates of presynapses onto postsynaptic neurons were acquired from Ruach et al.,12 and for each postsynaptic neuron, an ad hoc cluster score was calculated:

where:

N = the total number of presynapses on to the postsynaptic neuron.

m = the total number of presynaptic partners that have n>3 presynapses onto the postsynaptic neuron.

ni = the total number of presynapses of neuron i onto the postsynaptic neuron.

<di> = the average inter-synaptic distance of the ni presynapses.

This form was used so that presynapses from the same neuron that were in close proximity (low <di>) produced high scores. These scores were weighted toward presynaptic neurons with several presynapses (ni) onto the postsynaptic neuron. Postsynaptic neurons with several presynaptic partners (m) also produced higher scores to bias the score toward neurons with better sampling. However, the score was normalized for total presynapse number (N) to bias against neurons that simply had more synapses in total. As expected, command neurons and RIAL/R topped the list. The AIBL/R neurons were the first pair of highest scoring neurons that were not command neurons or RIAL/R (Table S1).

Recording neural activity

Animal preparation for imaging

Animals were picked at the L4 stage and kept at 20°C 16–24 hours before all imaging sessions so that they would be young adults during imaging. L4 nematodes were placed onto either OP50 lawns, or onto retinal plates if the animal expressed Chrimson.30 To make retinal plates, all-trans retinal (Sigma-Aldrich) was mixed with OP50 (50 μM) and was seeded a few hours in advance before picking L4 nematodes expressing Chrimson. All retinal plate handling was performed in the dark to minimize photooxidation.

Live calcium imaging using microfluidic chips

GCaMP animals were imaged using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microfluidic chips66 to monitor calcium activity. Animals were restrained in the chip during imaging and were also paralyzed with 10 mM tetramisole hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in S-basal buffer to minimize movement during imaging. Young adult animals were submerged in a tetramisole bath 10–15 minutes before imaging to ensure paralysis. Each animal was imaged for the duration of all 3 phases (odor, red light, and odor + red light; 2300 frames per phase) of an experiment, up to 2 times. Odor provided was 100 μM isoamyl alcohol, and odor delivery was controlled using LEE valves and AutoMate Scientific Valve Link 8.2. Each phase and set of experiments were imaged within a minute of each other to readjust the focal plane as needed. To record from neurons, we focused on the cell body of AWC in strain AGG0041, and in any strain expressing GCaMP in AVA-AIB-AIA we focused on the cell bodies of AVA and AIB and the process of AIA all in the same focal plane. We focused on AIA’s process as it has greater calcium dynamics compared to its soma.67 Only animals in which AIB and AVA were active during a full set of experiments (i.e. at least during one of the 3 phases), and AWC and AIA were active during appropriate stimulation phases were chosen for analysis.

Microscopy with light strobing

Microscopy was performed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted microscope. Videos were recorded at 10 frames per second using a 40x water objective (Nikon N Apo LWD 40x / 1.15 WI λS). Strobing was performed using Nikon NIS Elements software using a custom illumination sequence protocol to minimize photobleaching and oversaturation of GCaMP7s signals, with a duty cycle of 5 ms of blue light (470 nm, offset by 2 ms beforehand) per 100 ms frame exposure.

Optogenetic stimulation

To stimulate AIA::Chrimson, we used an external red LED (Mightex 617 nm, controlled by Universal LED controller). We employed a strobing approach to minimize cross-activation of Chrimson during GCaMP imaging due to spectral overlap between GCaMP and Chrimson. Acquisition was at 10 Hz with the following illumination protocol: 5 ms blue light to excite GCaMP, 1 ms darkness, 93 ms red light to activate Chrimson, 1 ms darkness. For periods when Chrimson was not activated, illumination was 5 ms of blue light, followed by 95 ms of darkness. This allowed for any blue-light photoactivation of Chrimson to decay during non-stimulation periods.

Histamine-gated chloride channel silencing and imaging

A histamine-gated chloride channel (HisCl) was expressed in either AIA under the Pins-1(s) promoter, in AWCOFF under the Pstr-2 promoter, or in several locomotion interneurons (RIM, AVA, AVB, AVD, AVE, PVC, AVG, AVF)45,68 under the Pnmr-1 promoter. Neurons were silenced with the addition of 10 mM histamine dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich) in loading buffer and all liquids used during microfluidic chip imaging. Animals were silenced for 20 minutes prior to imaging to ensure complete inactivation of neuron activity.

Data processing

We individually analyzed each neuron using custom Python scripts as listed in the key resources table. Regions of interest were hand-annotated to quantify neuron fluorescence and background. Neuron traces were first background subtracted, and then the first 350 frames (35 seconds) of each phase per video were cut off to remove initial photodecay artifacts (seen in Figure 1D). Then, we normalized all 3 phase recordings for each animal across a set of experiments. We did this to ensure that we captured the full dynamic range per set of experiments for each neuron, since some phases were designed to not activate specific neurons. Normalization was performed by taking the Fmax and Fmin of each set of experiments per animal, not for individual phases, and then calculating F−FminFmax−Fmin so that each neuron trace is normalized from 0 to 1. These normalized traces were used for all convolution modeling experiments.

Modeling

Convolution modeling was done using custom Python scripts. To find the model of best fit for each analysis we used the SciPy minimize function with the Nelder-Mead method, which utilizes a simplex approach. AVA-only model

Kernel parameters were optimized by gradient descent. Initially the parameters were optimized separately for each kernel, but since the α1, α2 parameters were stable across experiments, all models afterwards used the median α1, α2 values. Amplitudes (A) were fit separately for each recording.

Summation model

The amplitudes for all kernels were optimized individually for each recording. The same kinetic parameters (α1, α2) from the AVA-only modeling were used for all modeling. The resulting AVA amplitude from this summation model was used for AVA as fixed amplitudes in the time-dependent sensory model and the reversal-dependent model, since these subsequent models were focused on probing sensory contributions, and since AVA was sufficient to explain the majority of AIB dynamics.

Time-dependent sensory inputs model

For the sliding window used in Figure 2G – 2I, a sliding window of convolutions over 50 second intervals was passed over the entire trace. During this short convolution window, the amplitudes of both AWC’s and AIA’s kernel were fitted Aneuron(t) and normalized to AAVA for each window. Note that AAVA was a fixed value (from the summation model); only AAWC and AAIA were allowed to float over time. This process generated a series of short, convolved model fragments of the entire trace, which was then averaged to provide the models in Figure 2G – 2I, 2M. See Figure S3 for illustration of the method.

Reversal-dependent model

The amplitudes of AWC and AIA were a function of a convolved derivative of AVA activity. An offset term (o) was added to account for sensory input in the absence of AVA activity.

Modeling with NeuroPAL data

Data provided courtesy of the Yemini lab.36 For the sake of comparative analysis and consistency, we applied the same metrics of modeling our data to the NeuroPAL dataset. This included normalizing traces from 0 to 1, cutting off the beginning of the traces (50 frames) to exclude initial photodecay artifacts, using a 25 second (100 frames) kernel length (4 fps rate for the head neurons), and using fixed median α1 and α2 values based on the AVA-only model.

AVA-AWC-AIA network

We combined AIB L/R, AVA L/R, and AIA L/R into their own averaged traces due to similarity in activity, and AWC ON/OFF were used separately.

Selecting and consolidating relevant AIB inputs in NeuroPAL

We first documented all of AIB’s presynaptic inputs and gap junction partners present in the NeuroPAL dataset based on the updated C. elegans connectome,11 which ended up totaling 39 inputs to AIBL and 37 inputs to AIBR. Because not all presynaptic inputs necessarily influence AIB activity, and trying to apply our modeling methods to a large dataset risks overfitting parameters, we first narrowed down this list to what would be the most relevant inputs of AIB activity. We compared the activity of each presynaptic neuron and ranked how well they correlated to AIB L-R activity using Pearson’s correlation (ρ). We then applied an arbitrary cutoff of ρ=±0.3 to rule out the least correlative neurons. There were also some trials out of the total of 21 animals where some neurons had very few recordings, so those with less than 10 recordings were excluded as well. We then further ruled out neurons that only had inputs onto one of the AIB pairs but not both left and right.

Individual sensory neurons and motor activity

We considered presynaptic inputs to AIB present in the NeuroPAL dataset, and based on correlated activity to AIB (detailed above), we chose 5 highly correlated sensory neurons (ADF, AFD, ASH, ASG, and AWC), and one low correlated sensory neuron, FLPL, for comparison (only the left side of FLP synapses onto both AIBL and AIBR). Because many of the left-right sensory neuron pairs had similar activity, we compared left-right correlations such that those that were highly similar (ρ=≥0.75) were averaged into one combined trace, and those that were dissimilar (ρ=≤0.75) were left separate. We used these for summation and reversal-dependent modeling using individual sensory neurons with RIM. Since RIM directly synapses onto AIB and was present in the NeuroPAL dataset, we used RIM-only activity instead of AVA after first confirming similarities between RIM- and AVA-only parameters. We used the resulting RIM amplitudes from the summation models as fixed parameters in the corresponding reversal-dependent models. This was to be consistent with our own data where we used the same AVA amplitude from the AVA+AWC+AIA models and fixed it in the reversal-dependent model.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Analysis and modeling of data is described in relevant methods sections, and analysis was performed with custom python scripts detailed in the key resources table. Significance and statistical methods used are described in figure legends throughout the paper. Statistics and p-values between paired datasets stemming from the same groups of experiments and animals (such as between models of the same dataset, or between “Full” and “Ignore” models) were analyzed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For any unpaired datasets (such as between separate silenced animal strains for “Absent” model comparisons), the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Significance for all statistics between relevant groups were corrected using Holm-Bonferroni. Significance was defined based on the following p-values: not significant (N.S.): >0.05, *: <0.05, **: <0.01, ***: <0.001.
