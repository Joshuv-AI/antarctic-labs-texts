Core elements of national policy for digital health technology evidence and access
Authors: Megan Coder, Lacey McBride, Samantha McClenahan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital health technologies (DHT) offer the ability to deliver personalized care, lower barriers to access, and positively impact health outcomes. However, DHT utilization is impacted by insufficient market access pathways. A policy “full-stack”—including regulatory authorization, product value assessment, pricing and reimbursement, and patient access infrastructure—offers a framework for DHT integration into national healthcare ecosystems. Consistent clinical evidence requirements across national jurisdictions will further increase DHT scalability.Subject terms: Health care, Health policy

Personalized care at scale

Digital health technologies (DHTs) regulated as medical devices – often referred to as Digital Medical Devices (DMDs) in Europe1 – represent a new category of medical products that will shift how healthcare is delivered globally. DHTs provide national and local healthcare systems with the opportunity to deliver at scale personalized care in patient- and clinician-determined settings. These technologies may enhance care independently or work in conjunction with pharmaceuticals, clinician-delivered therapies, and other medical devices.

Evidence-backed DHTs have the ability to positively impact the outcomes of a specific disease or disorder, in addition to providing benefits such as optimizing patient overall health status, improving clinician effectiveness and efficiency, lowering overall healthcare system costs, and providing public health and data science benefits2. DHTs that are regulated as, or function alongside, a medical device typically require a form of clinician, health system, or purchaser authorization to reach patients. Patient access to these technologies is, therefore, greatly limited without broad, scalable DHT market access pathways. This is particularly evident in predominantly public payer healthcare systems, where national and local purchaser determinations can enable or prevent access to safe, effective DHTs3.

Public payer market access pathways

Countries are taking a wide variety of approaches to integrate DHTs into healthcare systems. National jurisdictions such as France4, Germany5, Japan6, and South Korea7 are developing comprehensive pathways that enable safe, effective DHTs to achieve patient and clinician access and utilization. Jurisdictions such as England are taking a modular approach that blends national and local determinations8. Whereas, other jurisdictions are only beginning to or are not yet considering the development of DHT market access pathways.

Jurisdictions with comprehensive approaches typically implement four critical components: regulatory authorization, product value assessment, pricing and reimbursement, and patient access infrastructure. These four components form a “full-stack” of policy elements that, when effectively implemented, maximize the appropriate and beneficial integration of safe, effective DHTs into national and local healthcare ecosystems.

The term full-stack is used in software development to refer to a product’s user-facing components, plus all backend components. Each element of a software full-stack plays an important role in the product’s ability to achieve its intended use. Numerous parallels exist between the software full-stack and the policy “full-stack.”

Policy “full-stack” to support DHT integration into healthcare

The first element of a policy “full-stack” is regulatory authorization. A regulatory system involves policy aimed at protecting public health outcomes and innovations through guidance, oversight, and enforcement9. Regulatory systems are critical to assessing therapeutic and medical device products’ safety and efficacy. National jurisdictions such as Australia10, South Korea11, and the United States12 that regulate DHTs qualifying as a medical device tend to do so within the ‘software as a medical device’ (SaMD) framework13 or as part of a traditional medical device pathway.

While regulatory oversight is often required for DHTs that qualify as a medical device to achieve market and patient access, this authorization alone is not sufficient. In Europe, for example, a DHT that receives CE marking recognition at the European Union (EU) level typically needs to meet additional country-specific requirements to achieve patient access14. Similarly, in the United States, a DHT that receives FDA clearance does not necessarily achieve automatic patient access15.

The second element of the policy “full-stack” is product value assessment16. DHT value assessments can take many different forms, including Health Technology Assessment (HTA) processes. HTA involves evaluating health technologies for economic, social, clinical, and ethical issues to inform policy and/or reimbursement plans17. Agencies that conduct HTAs support evidence generation and collaboration between stakeholders to facilitate guidance on potential product pricing and reimbursement. European countries are preparing for the launch of the HTA Regulation in 202518, which will encompass a wide range of therapeutic modalities, including medicinal products, medical devices, and in vitro diagnostic medical devices. While this regulation creates a foundation for DHT-inclusive HTAs, a limited number of HTA pathways currently exist in Europe and globally that specifically account for DHTs.

The third element of the policy “full-stack” includes pricing and reimbursement19. These processes facilitate the access and affordability of health technologies. Where available for DHTs, pricing and reimbursement processes involve a complex system that seeks to determine the value of a product by balancing the new benefit received by society with the benefit the manufacturer ought to receive for providing the product. Pricing models include cost-based pricing, cost-effectiveness thresholds, external price referencing, and value-based pricing20, and consider a variety of factors such as patient and health system needs, clinical guidelines, DHT clinical outcomes and economic impact, and market dynamics.

Country pricing and reimbursement models vary greatly21. While numerous national jurisdictions such as England, Finland, France, and Germany have DHT regulatory assessment and product authorization pathways in place, jurisdictions like England and Finland do not currently pair these DHT evaluation pathways with national public pricing and reimbursement processes8. Therefore, DHTs that receive regulatory authorization, a favorable product assessment, and pricing and reimbursement designations are well-primed for market access. Yet, market access potential alone does not translate into direct patient uptake and utilization.

The fourth element of the policy “full-stack” is patient access infrastructure, which directly addresses patient uptake and utilization. This component involves a range of factors including technical, educational, product usability, equity, and post-market surveillance elements that jurisdictions consider to enable the accessibility and scalability of reliable DHTs within healthcare ecosystems. The implementation of Germany’s digital health applications (Digitale Gesundheitsanwendungen, DiGA) provides an example of how important patient access infrastructure is to enabling DHT patient uptake and utilization. Following the program’s launch, many clinicians believed that DiGA could positively influence health care. However, numerous product access barriers prevented immediate DiGA implementation into clinical routines, such as low initial clinician awareness of available software solutions and the requirement for documented educational conversations in advance of the prescription being authorized22.

Few countries across the global landscape have all four elements of the DHT policy “full-stack” in place. With no single, unified framework or method for classification, evaluation, and financing of DHTs, multiple coalitions are working to shape common clinical evidence standards and technical quality criteria8,23–25.

Clinical evidence requirements provide the backbone of the policy “full-stack”

Clinical evidence requirements represent a common element in and across elements of the policy “full-stack.” To demonstrate DHT safety and efficacy during regulatory and product assessment processes, product manufacturers generate varying levels and types of clinical evidence based on the product and its claims. Yet, instead of serving as a unifying element across pathways, clinical evidence requirements often lack clarity and consistency.

Whereas DHT technical requirements, such as the implementation and use of a quality management system26, are clearly detailed in publications such as ISO standards and EU regulations, manufacturers continue to seek greater clarity on the clinical evidence requirements necessary to meet agency evidentiary requirements.

Regulatory and product value assessment agencies are increasingly taking into consideration the unique features and impacts DHTs offer at the patient, population, and system levels. This is reflected by agencies in countries like Germany and South Korea incorporating greater flexibility in DHT evidence requirements. Germany, for example, enables DHT developers to demonstrate medical, structural, and/or procedural effects that may extend beyond clinical outcomes into patient literacy, improved interactions between physicians and patients, or an improved sense of control among patients in self-management of their disease. While this evidence is typically generated through RCTs, real-world data (RWD) and real-world evidence (RWE) are expected to play a role in the ongoing evaluation of DHTs in practice27.

Since various HTA methods may not be well suited for evaluating DHTs because of their complexity and wide-ranging impact on the health system23, efforts outside of government agencies are beginning to propose value frameworks to provide further guidance to policymakers, purchasers, and developers. Examples include value assessment frameworks for assessing provider-facing DHTs23 and patient-facing DHTs focused on chronic disease management24.

A single DHT can encounter different assessment requirements and/or receive different determinations from regulatory agencies, HTA bodies, and purchasers within and across national jurisdictions17. While case-by-case and fit-for-purpose evidence requirements are useful in reflecting a product’s value within a single system, these pathway-specific requirements reduce the ability to use DHT-generated evidence across multiple agencies. As a result, the scalability of a DHT product is reduced.

Increasing the consistency of clinical evidence requirements within and across national pathways can establish common levels of evidence sufficiency, enhance the efficiency of evidence generation plans, and enable increased market scalability to improve products’ ability to serve patients across multiple clinical settings and locations.

Next steps: scaling DHTs across markets

To better understand the landscape of national evidentiary requirements and assist DHT manufacturers in scaling across multiple jurisdictions, the Digital Medicine Society (DiMe) is undertaking the International Digital Health Regulatory Pathways project. This effort will assess clinical evidence requirements within and across target agencies in Asia Pacific, Europe, and North America28. The project team is using a targeted methodology to conduct thorough evaluations of clinical evidence requirements in regulatory and product assessment pathways. Target outcomes of this work will include clinical evidence requirement comparisons across existing elements of the policy “full-stack” at the national and global levels.

Delivering high-quality, fully comprehensive healthcare that is specifically tailored to patient needs at scale and within budget has long been out of reach. Jurisdictions using a “full-stack” policy approach—regulatory authorization, product value assessment, pricing and reimbursement, and patient access infrastructure—to deliver DHTs to patients and end users will bring this aim within closer reach.
