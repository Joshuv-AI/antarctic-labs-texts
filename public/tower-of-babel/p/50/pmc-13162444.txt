The experience of empathy fatigue among emergency department nurses: a qualitative meta-synthesis
Authors: Jingyi Zou, Xiaojia Zong, Shuangshuang Xu, Jiani Zheng, Yuxuan Nie, Xue Fu, Jing Tang, Jinxia Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCompassion fatigue is a significant occupational psychological issue among emergency department nurses, with profound implications for both their well-being and organizational performance. This review aims to synthesize qualitative evidence on emergency nurses’ experiences of compassion fatigue, identify its contributing factors, enhance understanding of its manifestations and consequences, and provide an evidence base to inform the development of prevention and intervention strategies by healthcare managers.DesignA qualitative meta-synthesis.Review methodsThis review employed a meta-aggregative approach. A systematic search was conducted for qualitative studies in The Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, Ovid, PubMed, and Medline with no restriction on the start date (i.e., all available years were included) and the search was updated to June 20, 2025. The review adhered to the PRISMA reporting guidelines. The methodological quality of included studies was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute criteria, and the confidence in the qualitative findings was evaluated using the ConQual system.ResultsA Meta-phenomenological approach was used to synthesize ten qualitative studies, resulting in 48 key findings. These findings were organized into nine categories and further consolidated into three overarching themes: facilitating conditions, the double-edged effect, and response strategies. These themes encompassed eight sub-themes: insufficient basic resources, negative feedback catalysis, negative effects, positive effects, social security, avoidance strategies, positive attitude, and problem- and transcendence-oriented coping.ConclusionCompassion fatigue among emergency department nurses is a multifactorial psychological phenomenon arising from their specific work environment. Its impact is characterized by both the multidimensional depletion of nurses’ cognitive, physical, behavioral, and emotional resources, and growth-promoting effects. Effective prevention and mitigation require not only strategies to enhance nurses’ individual coping skills but also organizational-level interventions that provide adequate support.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04554-7.

Introduction

As global population aging accelerates and medical technology continues to advance, demands for nursing services are becoming increasingly diverse [1]. As the backbone of the healthcare system, nurses fulfill patients’ medical needs through professional care and health education while also providing support through the practice of humanistic care [2]. Empathy, a core component of high-quality nursing [3], is pivotal to the delivery of humanistic care. The International Council of Nurses (ICN) Code of Ethics explicitly identifies empathy as a core professional value in nurse–patient interactions [4]. However, this professional requirement also continuously exposes nurses to empathic stress, that is, the cumulative psychological burden arising from sustained and deep emotional resonance with patients’ suffering [5], thereby placing them at high risk for compassion fatigue [6].

The concept of compassion fatigue was first introduced by Joinson [7] to describe a distinct form of occupational burnout among nursing professionals. Figley [8] later expanded the definition, characterizing it as a state of physical and emotional exhaustion resulting from excessive empathic engagement or prolonged exposure to the suffering and distress of patients. Stamm further conceptualized it as the confluence of the adverse effects of burnout and secondary traumatic stress [9]. Today, compassion fatigue is widely recognized as a significant concern within global healthcare systems. Evidence from a systematic review encompassing 71 countries indicates that the prevalence of compassion fatigue among nurses increased between 2010 and 2019, with notable regional disparities; nurses in Asia reported higher levels than their counterparts in Europe and North America [10]. Furthermore, while compassion fatigue can affect nurses across various specialties, those working in emergency departments are particularly vulnerable. A cross-sectional survey of 114 nurses by Hooper et al. [11] found that emergency department nurses were at a significantly higher risk for compassion fatigue compared to nurses in intensive care, nephrology, and oncology units, with 86% of emergency nurses reporting moderate to high levels.

The emergency department is a high-acuity medical environment characterized by a diverse patient population, unpredictable clinical presentations, complex disease conditions, and the ongoing need for resuscitation. Frequent exposure to patient death, unpredictable emergencies, and traumatic events further increases emergency nurses’ risk of work-related stress responses. Prolonged exposure to these conditions not only results in emotional exhaustion but also diminishes empathic capacity, rendering nurses highly susceptible to compassion fatigue [12]. Evidence indicates that emergency nurses experiencing compassion fatigue manifest a broad spectrum of symptoms across physical, emotional, psychological, cognitive, and behavioural domains, which tend to accumulate over time [13, 14]. These manifestations pose serious threats to nurses’ physical, psychological, emotional well-being and occupational functioning [15]. Furthermore, they can undermine team resilience and risk tolerance, thereby weakening the department’s overall treatment capacity [13]. Ultimately, such effects jeopardize health outcomes for critically ill patients [13, 16] and impose a substantial burden on families, healthcare systems, and society, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Current research on compassion fatigue among emergency department nurses primarily employs quantitative methods, with a predominant focus on determining its prevalence and identifying predictive factors within this population [17, 18]. Although some scholars have used qualitative research to explore emergency nurses’ personal experiences with compassion fatigue, findings from individual qualitative studies are insufficient to comprehensively represent this population’s diverse experiences. To address this gap, this study applies a meta-synthesis approach to integrate existing qualitative findings on compassion fatigue among emergency nurses. This methodology enables an in-depth exploration of their lived experiences and underlying needs, thereby generating valuable evidence to support nursing administrators in developing strategies to prevent and mitigate compassion fatigue in this population. The research questions guiding this study are: Why do emergency nurses experience compassion fatigue? How do they perceive its impact, and what coping strategies do they employ?

Methods

Study design

This study was reported in accordance with the standardized guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [19]. A meta-synthesis approach was employed to integrate qualitative studies examining compassion fatigue among emergency department nurses. This methodology requires a thorough understanding of the philosophical foundations and methodologies inherent to qualitative research [20]. The included studies underwent iterative review to identify and interpret core themes related to the phenomenon. These findings were then systematically analyzed and synthesized to generate new, integrated insights. This process facilitated a deeper comprehension of compassion fatigue and contributed meaningfully to its subsequent empirical exploration and theoretical development in the field.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in The Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, Ovid, PubMed, and Medline databases with no restriction on the start date (i.e., all available years were included), and the search was updated to June 20, 2025. The search strategy combined both subject headings and free-text terms using Boolean operators. Manual searches of reference lists from included studies were also performed to identify additional relevant publications. Qualitative studies investigating compassion fatigue among emergency nurses were collected and systematically organized. Search terms included: “Emergency Nursing,” “Emergency Service, Hospital,” “Emergency Department,” “Emergency Nurse,” “Nurses,” “Compassion Fatigue,” “Sympathetic Fatigue,” “Empathy Fatigue,” “Experience,” “Perception,” “Feeling,” “Qualitative Research,” and “Research, Qualitative.”

Screening and data extraction

The study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria were established according to the PICoS framework [21], as detailed in Table 1. Two systematically trained researchers independently performed the study screening. The search results were first imported into Zotero software, and duplicate records were eliminated. The remaining studies were then screened against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria; a detailed flow diagram of this process is provided in Fig. 2. Following the identification of eligible studies, two researchers independently extracted the relevant data by creating a data extraction form using Excel 2019, with the final extracted data presented in Table 2. Any disagreements arising during the study selection or data extraction phases were resolved through discussion, or when consensus could not be reached, by consultation with a third researcher.

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two researchers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research [22]. This checklist consists of ten items. Each item was rated as “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” or “not applicable.” The overall methodological quality for each study was then classified as follows: Grade A (all criteria met, indicating low risk of bias), Grade B (partial criteria met, indicating moderate risk of bias), or Grade C (criteria not met, indicating high risk of bias). Any disagreements between the researchers were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third researcher. Following this appraisal, all ten included studies were rated as Grade B or higher. Detailed quality appraisal results are provided in Table 3.

Data synthesis and analysis

The qualitative findings were integrated using the thematic synthesis method [23]. This structured, iterative process involved two independent researchers, with a third researcher available for adjudication when necessary. The synthesis proceeded through three concrete phases: (1) Key findings and interpretations from each study were imported into a shared Excel workbook, where both researchers independently performed line-by-line coding; (2) Building on an understanding of the philosophical and methodological foundations of qualitative research, the two researchers repeatedly reviewed the included literature and independently grouped codes with shared meanings, relevance, and frequency into descriptive themes. Consistency between researchers was maintained, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or arbitration by the third researcher; (3) Through ongoing comparison and discussion within the research team, the intrinsic connections among different descriptive themes were analyzed based on commonality, relevance, and similarity. Key points suitable for integration were identified, ultimately leading to the formation of synthesized results.

ConQual-assessment of evidence confidence

The ConQual system, developed by the JBI Evidence-Based Healthcare Center in 2014 [24], was used to evaluate the confidence in the meta-synthesized findings. This system assesses two key dimensions: dependability and credibility. Dependability, reflecting the methodological quality of the primary studies, was evaluated using five core criteria from the JBI appraisal tool. The dependability grade was downgraded based on the number of unmet criteria: no downgrade for 4–5 met criteria, a one-level downgrade for 2–3 met criteria, and a two-level downgrade for 0–1 met criteria. Credibility, indicating the congruence between the synthesized findings and the supporting primary data, was downgraded as follows: no downgrade for unequivocal supporting evidence, a two-level downgrade for equivocal evidence, and a four-level downgrade for unsupported findings. The final ConQual score, which integrates the assessments of dependability and credibility, classifies the confidence of evidence into one of four levels: high, moderate, low, or very low. Results

The initial search identified 813 studies. Following a rigorous screening process based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, ten studies were included for final analysis. The included studies originated from the United States (n = 5), the United Kingdom (n = 2), China (n = 1), Italy (n = 1), and Canada (n = 1), with the majority published within the last five years. Using the thematic synthesis method and an iterative review process, a total of 48 key research findings were extracted. These findings were organized into nine categories and subsequently synthesized into three overarching themes, as illustrated in Fig. 3. All participant quotes included in the text are followed by a superscript number in square brackets, which corresponds to the reference number of the included primary study from which the quote was extracted.

Themes and subthemes structure

Synthesized finding 1: facilitating conditions

This theme reveals the factors that drive or exacerbate compassion fatigue and comprises two interrelated sub-dimensions: Insufficient basic resources [25–28]and Negative feedback catalyses [26, 29]. The former represents a fundamental barrier to compassion, while the latter serves as an amplifying catalyst. This theme is primarily supported by data from five studies [25–28].

Insufficient basic resources

As the frontline setting for the management of critically ill and emergency patients, emergency departments are characterized by high patient volumes and diverse case types, which frequently result in shortages of material resources such as equipment and beds. Consequently, emergency nurses often experience feelings of powerlessness when they are unable to provide timely and effective care to all patients. As one nurse described: “I have real sick patients out in the waiting room, and now I have to give a bed or a spot to somebody who chose to get high.” [27]. In addition, the fast-paced work environment and the high level of clinical uncertainty further intensify nursing workload and compress available care time, continuously undermining the quality of care. As one nurse noted: “In the ER, they come in unstable, so it is a lot of pressure to be able to play your role, do it quickly, think fast.” [26]. Prolonged exposure to such high-intensity working conditions leads to physical and psychological exhaustion among emergency nurses, reflecting a substantial depletion of energy resources. As described by one nurse: “As an experience from a professional point of view, it is heavy because all those hours and all these PPE take away energy because you sweat so much, you get so tired, you suffer from thirst, you suffer hunger, and you lose lucidity.” [28]. Moreover, insufficient managerial support further exacerbates emotional exhaustion among emergency nurses. Some nurses reported that managerial support was largely superficial: “Management pays only lip service.” [25]. More importantly, deficits in personal resources, such as emotional regulation skills, hinder emergency nurses’ ability to cope with negative emotions, ultimately leading to a decline in empathic capacity: “In the ER, they come in unstable, so it is a lot of pressure to be able to play your role, do it quickly, think fast.” [26]。The intertwined shortages of material, environmental, personal, and energy-related resources collectively place emergency nurses in a state of persistent emotional exhaustion, ultimately contributing to the development of compassion fatigue.

Negative feedback catalyses

When emergency department nurses face multiple resource shortages, their capacity to provide compassionate care becomes compromised, increasing their vulnerability to compassion fatigue. In this context, some nurses experience self-blame and guilt for failing to meet their own professional standards for empathy. This negative internal feedback creates a self-reinforcing cycle that intensifies compassion fatigue’s persistence and severity. As one nurse described: “.you try to be compassionate to everyone that you take care of, but then at a certain point, you don’t have any compassion left to give.At some point it’s all gone.That’s what I’ve always interpreted compassion fatigue as. It’s where, at the end of the day, you just have nothing left to give. You shouldn’t be going home like that. You shouldn’t be going home emotionless.” [26]. Furthermore, the lack of constructive feedback from management leaves nurses disoriented, depriving them of both practical solutions and the psychological support that could be derived from organizational recognition. As one nurse noted: “There’s no positive feedback and no negative feedback, so you’re kind of stuck, you don’t really know what you’re doing.” [29].

Synthesized finding 2: double-edged effect

This theme reveals the dual nature of compassion fatigue experienced by emergency nurses—it can trigger negative changes across multiple dimensions such as cognition, physical and mental health, behavior, and emotions, yet it can also catalyze personal transformation and growth. The theme encompasses two opposing secondary dimensions: Negative effects [13, 26, 30–32]and Positive effects [13, 30, 32]. This theme is primarily supported by data from five studies [13, 26, 30–32].

Negative effect

Compassion fatigue has been shown to exert a range of negative effects on emergency nurses across cognitive, physical, behavioral, and emotional domains, primarily manifesting in impaired individual health, altered professional attitudes, and disrupted family life. With respect to individual health, emergency nurses frequently experience secondary traumatic stress, characterized by persistent intrusive memories and emotional rumination. One nurse described the enduring nature of such intrusive recollections: “That wife screaming for her husband. I just hear it in my mind all the time.” [26]. Another nurse similarly reported difficulty disengaging from traumatic memories: “I could not shake it…you thought about it all the time. You could see it all the time.” [30]. These symptoms are often accompanied by sleep disturbances [13, 33], fatigue [13, 26], and impaired psychological closure [33]. Newly recruited nurses are particularly prone to experiencing existential anxiety and self-doubt: “having flashbacks was awful and obviously I was quite new to ED, so everyone was like it’s fine, it’s normal to be like this, like it’s not a normal situation, it is really traumatic. I was like, am I just not coping with ED? It is quite scary…” [32].With prolonged exposure to traumatic stressors, many nurses gradually develop emotional detachment and cognitive numbing. This is reflected in a self-isolating stance, as one nurse stated: “I don’t bother others, and I don’t want to be bothered.” [13].

At the professional level, compassion fatigue is associated with growing doubt about one’s career choice and a gradual loss of professional enthusiasm among emergency nurses. One nurse articulated how repeated negative encounters eroded professional motivation: “But then you walk away, and you are sad, and you are angry, and those emotions do affect the rest of your shift. You spend the whole 12 h looking after complete strangers to the best of my ability and that person has come in and completely disregarded that and come and called me names or thrown a punch at me, swore at me, it does affect you, to the point of you think, what’s the point in coming back tomorrow.” [32].

In terms of family life, emergency nurses often report a reduced willingness to engage in communication with family members. One nurse described emotional withdrawal after work: “I go home and people wanna talk to me and I find that I’m just zoned out. Like I just don’t care; I don’t or I’m so exhausted and they’re like, I say, ‘Tomorrow; tomorrow we can talk.’ ‘I wanna tell you right now.’ ‘What? Okay,’ and I finally just sit there and they do talk, but I hear nothing.” [26]. Irregular and unpredictable work schedules further disrupt work–life balance: “I get called to come in early, asked to stay late. I don’t have a normal schedule. I don’t get enough sleep. My house, wow, I can’t do anything at home because I’m always working. There are dishes in the sink. But when I am home, I sleep.” [31]. This persistent tension between work and family domains may result in the spillover of negative emotions into the family context [13].

Positive effect

Among emergency nurses experiencing compassion fatigue, a subset demonstrates adaptive coping strategies that facilitate post-traumatic growth. Following emotional exhaustion, some nurses engage in active cognitive reappraisal, reframing occupational challenges as opportunities for self-improvement. One nurse explicitly articulated a strong commitment to professional growth: “The medical profession still has a long way to go in healing the wounded and saving the dying, and we must continue to make efforts on our journey of professional development. I am committed to constantly improving myself.” [13].This growth-oriented mindset further promotes the development of broader adaptive capacities. As reflected by one nurse: “I think it makes me very adaptable. It gives me the mentality that I am able to handle several situations at once, and I can take a step back and look at the bigger picture.” [32].Meanwhile, prolonged exposure to occupational adversity appears to cultivate deeper empathic capacity and enhanced inner strength. One nurse described this transformation as an expansion of empathic depth and personal resilience: “It’s made me a more compassionate, empathetic person…more sensitive to what they are going through personally…a lot more compassionate to people’s pain and suffering, and what they go through. And I think it’s made me strong. I think it has given me some strength at the same time, to be supportive to people, to be able to give people support through tough times.” [30]. These empathic regulatory strategies, forged under sustained stress, enable nurses to deliver care with heightened empathy and understanding. Furthermore, such growth manifests as an enhancement in professional well-being. As one nurse emphasized, a positive mindset plays a central role in sustaining this sense of fulfillment: “The mindset is super important; eveugh it can be tough sometimes. But overall, I can lend a hand to others and it feels amazing when vulnerable groups rely on me (smiling).” [13].

Synthesized finding 3: response strategy

This theme reveals the diverse strategies employed by emergency nurses to cope with compassion fatigue, encompassing four distinct secondary themes: Social security [13, 26, 32], Avoidance strategy [26, 30, 33], Positive attitude [13, 29], and Problem- and transcendence-oriented coping [13, 26, 29, 31]. This theme is primarily supported by data from seven studies [13], 33– [29–32].

Social security

Social support serves as an effective strategy for mitigating compassion fatigue among emergency nurses. Adequate support facilitates the active sharing of emotional experiences, reduces emotional suppression, and alleviates psychological burden. Support from friends provides social resonance and offers understanding and comfort. As one nurse stated: “I prefer not to talk to my family too much, as I worry about how it might affect them and they may not fully understand my work environment. My main intention is to share my emotions with friends who can relate, as we speak the same language and support each other through hard work and relaxation. I really enjoy this dynamic.” [13]. Equally important is the bond-based support from colleagues who share similar experiences; this support, grounded in a deep understanding of compassion fatigue, provides timely and accessible emotional consolation. Another nurse mentioned: “Everyone’s had stuff they’ve seen that’s like affected them a bit… so it’s nice to talk to people who understand.” [32]. Furthermore, systematic, link-oriented support from organizational management helps restore empathic capacity in emergency nurses. As articulated by one nurse: “It would be amazing if someone would even acknowledge it, to begin with, and then give us something to help deal with it. Support groups, talking, education, something. If anything, at least show that they care about our wellbeing, not the patients’ wellbeing and the HCAHPS and patient satisfaction, but our wellbeing is just as important.” [26].(HCAHPS: Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems).

Avoidance strategy

Emergency nurses frequently employ avoidance strategies as self-protective mechanisms when experiencing compassion fatigue. Emotional disengagement was frequently reported, as illustrated by one nurse: “Shut my brain down.go to my happy place. Then 13 hour later, I can turn it back on and come back to the real world because I’ve survived the beat-down, so to speak.” [26]. Task-focused coping was also employed, whereby attention was redirected to technical duties to suppress emotional distress, as described by one participant: “Focusing on tasks allows you to kind of push the sadness and the trauma to the side so that you can complete your tasks successfully and give the best chance at living, or keeping their arm, or anything like that.” [33]. In addition, dark humor was used by some nurses as an emotional buffering strategy: “The dark humor that everybody uses to cover up and there’s so muchthey use humor to cover up all of their hurt.” [26]. Although these avoidance strategies may temporarily distance nurses from stressors and reduce emotional arousal, excessive reliance on such strategies can lead to adverse consequences. One nurse described the delayed emotional rebound associated with prolonged avoidance:“You just kind of put everything in the back of your head. And then maybe a week later, so you are at home and you drop something. And then you cry because you held it off for a week. So it just kind of bubbles up at random times and it might not even be related to what you are doing, because you did not get to properly grieve when it happened.” [30].

Positive attitude

Emergency nurses adopt a positive attitude as an active and constructive approach to managing workplace emotional exhaustion, primarily through cognitive restructuring. Some emergency nurses reported accepting reality and acknowledging the presence of compassion fatigue as part of their professional experience. As one nurse stated, “I don’t dwell on what’s happened.” [29]. Other nurses engaged in positive reappraisal by deliberately focusing on favorable aspects of their work, thereby reconstructing the meaning of their professional experiences. As one participant explained, “I also need to reflect on myself and look at the positive side of things, hoping to realize my value in the work.” [13]. In addition, some participants emphasized self-motivation and self-affirmation as important internal coping resources. One nurse stated, “Although the help of others is important, the key is to rely on yourself, believe in yourself, please yourself and surpass yourself (smiling).” [13].

Problem- and transcendence-oriented coping

Problem-focused and transcendence-oriented strategies represent two key approaches emergency nurses use to manage compassion fatigue. Problem-focused coping involves proactively implementing direct measures to reduce emotional exhaustion.

Some nurses enhanced their professional competence and psychological adjustment skills through systematic learning and experience-based reflection. As one nurse reported, “Why is Teacher Wang (referring to the emergency professional tutor) able to handle many complex problems well? Because she’s experienced, she knows how to navigate complex clinical issues, so I’m actually learning all the time.” [13]. In addition, they actively engaged in self-regulation through diverse media platforms. One participant explained, “To recharge my batteries more, I often go to Bilibili to learn some mind-body relaxation techniques in psychosomatic medicine, and I also go to Xiaohongshu and Douyin to release my mood (smiling).” [13]. Furthermore, timely debriefing and reflective practices were regarded as important means of alleviating psychological burden. As one nurse noted, “It doesn’t matter how long you’ve been a nurse.you deal with such intense moments there and you go home and your family a lot of times doesn’t understand what you’re going through and if there’s not debriefings, you do; you just hold onto that.” [26]. These proactive behavioral strategies contributed to the development of sustainable coping mechanisms among nurses. As one participant summarized, “‘I’ve developed coping mechanisms right throughout. if I hadn’t or if I weren’t aware of how I feel at times or how I behave, I would have probably left.” [29]. Transcendence-oriented coping, often shaped by individual cultural backgrounds, enables some nurses to derive work-related meaning and build resilience through practices such as prayer. One participant shared, “I pray on my way in to work. I do this so I will be prepared when I get there because you never know what you are walking into.” [31].

Discussion

The findings indicate that emergency nurses’ experiences of compassion fatigue can be conceptualized through three core dimensions: precipitating conditions, dual effects, and coping strategies. Precipitating conditions include deficiencies in material, contextual, personal, and energy-related resources, which are further exacerbated by negative feedback cycles. Systemic resource shortages create conditions conducive to the development of compassion fatigue, while negative feedback from multiple sources amplifies and sustains its progression. Nurses experiencing compassion fatigue may demonstrate adverse changes across cognitive, physical, behavioral, and emotional domains; however, such experiences can also create opportunities for post-traumatic growth. In response to compassion fatigue, emergency nurses employ various coping strategies, including social support, avoidance, positive cognitive restructuring, problem-focused approaches, and transcendence-oriented techniques, which may facilitate post-traumatic growth. However, prolonged reliance on avoidance strategies alone may lead to adverse professional and personal outcomes.

Development of compassion fatigue in emergency department nurses is driven by resource scarcity and negative feedback loops

This study indicates that compassion fatigue among emergency nurses is associated with deficiencies across multiple resource domains, including material, contextual, personal, and energy-related resources, with its severity further exacerbated by negative feedback from various sources. These findings are consistent with the Compassion Fatigue Model developed by Coetzee and colleagues [34]. This model, building on earlier compassion fatigue frameworks and the social neuroscience of empathy, integrates Conservation of Resources theory with social neuroscience to elucidate the roles of resource balance, empathy, and stress appraisal in the development of compassion fatigue, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

As the primary setting for treating critically ill patients, the emergency department imposes high demands in terms of workload and environmental stressors [35]. When material, contextual, emotion-regulation, and energy resources are inadequate, emergency nurses, after evaluating patient needs, often prioritize the preservation of their own remaining resources [36]. In such resource-depleted states, nurses adopt one of two distinct approaches to managing empathy, differentiated primarily by the presence or absence of self-reflective capacity [34]. Evidence suggests that reflecting on daily stressors and deriving adaptive insights from such reflection can strengthen psychological resilience [37]. Nurses with higher resilience can regulate emotions more effectively, thereby reducing the risk of compassion fatigue [38]. Conversely, those lacking self-reflective capacity are more likely to become immersed in patients’ suffering, which compromises their ability to sustain compassionate care [39]. It is important to note, however, that self-reflection does not invariably lead to positive outcomes [40]. Even when attempted, self-reflection may fail to help nurses disengage from suffering due to limited external resources, internal resource deficits, or maladaptive rumination triggered by negative emotions during reflection [41], ultimately still resulting in compassion fatigue.

Furthermore, this study found that suboptimal care outcomes resulting from resource deficiencies, when combined with negative emotions such as guilt and helplessness and reinforced by adverse workplace feedback, can trap emergency nurses in a negative cognitive cycle that exacerbates compassion fatigue. Similarly, Fukumori and colleagues [42] reported an association between nurses’ sense of professional inadequacy, characterized by feelings of ineffectiveness and failure in care delivery, and the development of compassion fatigue.

Therefore, systematic intervention strategies are needed to disrupt this vicious cycle of negative cognition and to maximize the potential of self-reflection in mitigating compassion fatigue. First, emergency nurses should be encouraged to engage in adaptive self-reflection, guided by the Systematic Self-Reflection Model proposed by Crane MF and colleagues [37], which outlines five reflective practices designed to strengthen resilience, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Second, adequate resource support, including equitable workload distribution, psychological regulation training, and appropriate scheduling, should be provided alongside structured reflective guidance, such as mindfulness-based cognitive training and reflective journaling frameworks. These combined measures can help emergency nurses transform self-reflection into effective resilience resources, thereby preventing and alleviating compassion fatigue.

The impact of compassion fatigue among emergency department nurses exhibits a dual nature of disruption and growth

This study’s findings indicate that compassion fatigue not only adversely affects emergency nurses’ health, professional attitudes, and family life but may also, in some cases, stimulate professional growth and competency development. From a negative perspective, the primary impact of compassion fatigue manifests as secondary traumatic stress, characterized by symptoms such as sleep disturbances, intrusive memories, emotional dysregulation, and cognitive numbing. These individual-level adverse effects extend into both professional and personal domains.

In the workplace, compassion fatigue was found to diminish professional enthusiasm and foster doubt regarding career commitment. Peters and colleagues [43] reported that nurses experiencing compassion fatigue often question their core professional values and express increased intention to leave their positions. In their personal lives, negative emotions frequently spill over into family relationships, leading to reduced communication and work-life imbalance. Gustafsson and colleagues [44] observed that affected nurses devote most of their energy to work and subsequently withdraw due to exhaustion after their shifts, resulting in diminished overall quality of life.

Given the threats compassion fatigue poses to individual health, occupational functioning, and quality of life, coupled with its rapid onset, strong treatability, and potential for swift recovery [45], early recognition, timely diagnosis, and prompt intervention are critically important. Early screening and diagnosis can be facilitated by existing tools, such as the predictive model for compassion fatigue in emergency nurses developed by Xie and colleagues [46]. With the advancement of global digitalization, digital health technologies have been widely applied in mental health services, delivering interventions and support through electronic devices, internet platforms, and related digital channels [47]. These technologies are characterized by low cost, ease of implementation, and high accessibility [48]. Studies have shown that digital health tools can effectively alleviate negative emotions, reduce perceived stress, and improve sleep quality among nurses [49, 50]. Looking forward, the application of digital health technologies should not be limited to remedial interventions. Instead, they should be further integrated with human factors reliability design principles to develop feedforward intervention systems specifically for managing compassion fatigue in emergency department nurses. Such systems could incorporate wearable devices with intelligent sleep monitoring and heart rate variability detection, thereby enhancing the proactivity, precision, and systematic nature of mental health support while providing more effective psychological resources for these nurses. Additionally, education on compassion fatigue should be strengthened to improve nurses’ awareness of their own symptoms and recognition of professional emotional boundaries [43]. Specialized training in emotional management under high-stakes clinical conditions should also be provided [51].

A key finding of this study is that compassion fatigue may also facilitate positive growth among emergency nurses, suggesting it is not solely a psychological crisis but can also serve as a transformative experience and potential turning point for professional development. Wentzel et al. [52] similarly observed that nurses exposed to compassion fatigue may develop increased psychological strength over time. A cross-sectional study by Zeng and colleagues [53] revealed that while burnout negatively affects post-traumatic growth, secondary traumatic stress can positively influence it by transforming distressing experiences into sustained coping motivation. These findings indicate that psychological distress and growth can coexist and may undergo mutual transformation. This growth typically unfolds through a sequential psychological process comprising three distinct phases: stress, adjustment, and growth [54], as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Individual resilience plays a critical role in this growth process [55], enabling emergency department nurses to mobilize internal psychological resources, adapt to high-pressure environments, and achieve psychological reconstruction and cognitive transformation following periods of emotional exhaustion. Through this process, traumatic experiences can be transformed into opportunities for personal development. Simultaneously, team resilience serves as an important protective factor for individual resilience [56]. Highly resilient teams, characterized by shared beliefs, emotions, and motivations among members, possess a collective capacity to recover from adversity [57]. This not only strengthens team cohesion but also buffers the negative impact of burnout on individuals, thereby creating an organizational environment conducive to post-traumatic growth following compassion fatigue. Therefore, healthcare managers should not only promote diverse educational approaches and skill training to cultivate individual psychological resilience among emergency department nurses [58], but also systematically foster team resilience through strategies such as information sharing, collaborative learning, positive team climate development, and flexible leadership practices [59]. Through these combined efforts, the protective role of resilience in facilitating post-traumatic growth can be fully realized [58].

Coping strategies: a synergistic individual and organizational approach

Coping is defined as the cognitive and behavioral strategies individuals employ to manage stressful events [60]. The effective implementation of such strategies has been demonstrated to protect mental health [61]. In this study, the primary coping strategies used by emergency nurses to manage compassion fatigue were categorized into five domains: social support, avoidance strategies, positive attitude, problem-focused strategies, and transcendence-oriented strategies, as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Coping strategies for empathetic fatigue among emergency department nurses

Social support from friends, colleagues, and supervisors has been identified as a critical factor in mitigating compassion fatigue [44]. Positive interpersonal interactions not only facilitate the regulation of negative emotions but also enhance individual resilience at the team level, thereby contributing to a systemic protective framework against compassion fatigue [59]. To optimize these benefits, healthcare organizations should implement structured communication channels and foster psychologically safe workplace environments that encourage nurses to openly share emotional experiences.

Avoidance strategies, including emotional detachment, task shifting, and the use of dark humor, may provide effective short-term adjustment when stressors are unavoidable or beyond an individual’s control [62]. However, while these approaches enable rapid temporary adaptation, they do not resolve underlying stressors and may lead to adverse long-term consequences [62]. Therefore, the selection of coping strategies should be based on careful assessment of stressor controllability. Targeted training in stress management is recommended to enhance nurses’ ability to identify different types of stressors and select appropriately matched coping strategies, thereby improving overall coping efficacy.

Positive attitude strategies, including acceptance, cognitive reappraisal, and self-motivation, represent emotion-focused approaches through which nurses reinterpret stressors and regulate emotional responses [63]. These approaches rank among the most frequently employed and effective methods for mitigating the stress impacts. This underscores the value of providing nurses with training in cognitive reappraisal techniques and supporting the development of their psychological capital.

Problem-focused strategies involve proactive measures that directly address stressors. Nurses in this study engaged in self-directed learning of psychological adjustment skills through consultation with senior colleagues and use of digital platforms, demonstrating proactive engagement and resilience potential. Managerial support and guidance are essential to transform these individual initiatives into structured organizational psychological support systems. Additionally, both formal and informal post-event debriefings have been shown to mitigate work-related stress and facilitate emotional processing, thereby enhancing psychological resilience and mental health [64]. Cultural and spiritual practices, including prayer, were also observed as potential contributors to alleviating compassion fatigue.

It is important to note that the effectiveness of coping strategies may evolve over time, underscoring the need for flexible, ongoing adjustment of approaches to meet changing situational demands [62].

Limitations

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to examine emergency nurses’ experiences of compassion fatigue; however, some relevant studies may have been overlooked. The ten included studies were conducted across five countries, with most representing Western cultural contexts (United States, United Kingdom, Italy, and Canada), and only one study originating from China. This uneven geographic distribution may limit the findings’ cross-cultural generalizability, given variations in cultural norms, healthcare systems, and professional environments across regions. Additionally, the exclusive focus on qualitative evidence, while providing depth of understanding, inherently restricts the breadth of analysis regarding the developmental trajectories and outcomes of compassion fatigue.

Conclusion

This study explored emergency nurses’ experiences of compassion fatigue, identifying three synthesized themes: facilitating conditions, double-edged effects, and response strategies. Compassion fatigue emerges as a process-oriented psychological experience that develops under conditions of multidimensional resource insufficiency, while exhibiting both detrimental and growth-promoting potential. Effective management requires integrated approaches that combine individual-level coping skills with organizational-level support systems, providing practical guidance for healthcare leaders aiming to enhance psychological well-being in this workforce. Future research should investigate cross-cultural variations in emergency nurses’ experiences of compassion fatigue to better understand culturally specific manifestations, contributing factors, and protective mechanisms. Such understanding will be crucial for developing culturally adapted interventions and support strategies.
