Rapid detection of Chlamydia Psittaci by isothermal recombinase polymerase amplification assays
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhang, Jinfeng Wang, Minna Chen, Yixiang Gao, Bolong Liang, Jianchang Wang, Xiangdong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChlamydia psittaci (Cps) is a zoonotic pathogen widely distributed in nature that strictly parasitizes eukaryotic cells. Cps is typically carried by parrots and other birds, possesses high virulence and toxicity, and can cause psittacosis in humans and animals. Therefore, monitoring and preventing the spread of psittacosis holds significant economic and public health importance. In this study, the specific primers and probes targeting the CPSIT_0429 gene of Cps were designed and synthesized, and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) assays were developed. Utilizing a real-time fluorescence detection platform (qRPA) and a lateral flow strip combined with RPA (LFS RPA), the rapid and effective detection of Cps was achieved. The limit of detection for the qRPA assay and the LFS RPA assay with Cps DNA standards was 120 copies/uL and 13 copies/uL, respectively. The developed qRPA and LFS RPA could specifically detect Cps without cross-reactings with other common respiratory pathogens tested in this study. The qRPA assay and the LFS RPA assay were further evaluated on 334 clinical oropharyngeal-cloacal swabs, feces, and lung samples, and the detection rates of CPs were 4.80% (16/334) and 5.70% (19/334) for the qRPA and the LFS RPA, respectively. Compared with qPCR detection results, the concordance rates were 98.80% and 99.70%, respectively. Furthermore, A, B and E genotype of Cps were identified in the above positive samples. These results indicated that the qRPA assay and the LFS RPA assay developed in this study could serve as effective diagnostic tools of Cps, especially in the field detection and in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Chlamydia psittaci, qRPA, LFS RPA, CPSIT_0429 gene, Rapid detection

Introduction

Chlamydia psittaci (Cps) belongs to the order Chlamydiales, family Chlamydiaceae, genus Chlamydia. It lies between viruses and bacteria and shares similarities with Gram-negative bacteria (Cui and Meng, 2023). Its genome comprises a single circular chromosome of about 1.17 Mb and a plasmid of 7,553 bp, with approximately 1,000 coding sequences (CDS) (Read et al., 2003; Voigt et al., 2012). Cps has an extremely broad host range, with approximately 467 animal species capable of carrying psittacosis. The most common host animals include parrots, pigeons, turkeys, ducks, and other waterfowl (Kaleta and Taday, 2003; Bommana and Polkinghorne, 2019; Hogerwerf et al., 2020). Based on the ompA gene sequences, Cps can be divided into 18 genotypes, including the classical genotypes A-G, E/B, WC, and M56, as well as eight atypical genotypes: 1 V, 6 N, Mat116, R54, YP84, CPX0308, I, and J (Sachse et al., 2008; Heddema et al., 2015).

Cps is a naturally widespread zoonotic pathogen that strictly parasitizes eukaryotic cells (Vanrompay et al., 2007). It is primarily carried by parrots and other birds (De Meyst et al., 2022), and exhibits high virulence and toxicity, leading to psittacosis in humans, birds, and other animals. Infected birds and other animals primarily exhibit systemic diseases such as conjunctivitis, airsacculitis, pneumonia, and hepatosplenomegaly (Knittler and Sachse, 2015). Humans are primarily infected through inhalation of pathogen-containing aerosols or contact with contaminated bird droppings and feathers (Balsamo et al., 2017; Liang et al., 2022), Clinical symptoms include high fever, chills, headache, muscle aches, and other flu-like symptoms. In severe cases, it can lead to lower respiratory tract atypical pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, septic shock, or multiple organ dysfunction, posing a significant threat to human health (Read et al., 2013; Li et al., 2023).

Current Cps detection assays include pathogen culture, serological testing, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), and targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) (Soon et al., 2025). Traditional pathogen culture isolation is relatively difficult and time-consuming, making it challenging to meet the clinical demand for rapid diagnosis. Serological detection methods are relatively simple but struggle to distinguish between different chlamydia species, resulting in lower specificity (Kaleta and Taday, 2003). Although molecular biology techniques such as PCR and real-time fluorescent PCR (qPCR) are widely used, they rely on high-precision, expensive instruments, specialized technical personnel, and well-equipped laboratory environments. This makes them difficult to implement in grassroots laboratories with limited equipment or for on-site testing (Heddema et al., 2015; Wolff et al., 2018; McGovern et al., 2021). mNGS and tNGS technologies are suitable for detecting difficult-to-culture or rare pathogens, but they have limitations such as lengthy testing cycles, complex data processing and high costs, making them unsuitable for large-scale screening of clinical samples (Deng et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025).

In recent years, the development of isothermal nucleic acid amplification technology has provided new solutions for Cps detection, effectively addressing the shortcomings of the aforementioned assays. Isothermal nucleic acid amplification techniques commonly used for pathogen detection include loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) (Ayaz Kök et al., 2023), crossing priming isothermal amplification (CPA) (Deng et al., 2024), rolling circle amplification (RCA) (Chi et al., 2024) and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) (Pang et al., 2021). Although LAMP-based assay for Cps detection have been reported, LAMP assay require 6-8 primer pairs, making primer design relatively complex and increasing the risk of primer mismatches and non-specific amplification products, such as primer dimers, which may affect assay performance (Wang et al., 2023). In contrast, RPA primarily relies on recombinases, single-stranded DNA-binding proteins (SSBs), and strand-displacing DNA polymerases that can bind to single-stranded nuclreic acids (oligonucleotide primers) (Tan et al., 2022). Without the need for expensive thermal cycling equipment, the target fragment can be amplified through a 15–30 min reaction at a constant temperature of 37–42°C, using only a pair of specific primers. These characteristics make RPA a rapid, sensitive, and user-friendly amplification method with considerable potential for point-of-care and field-based pathogen detection (Zingg et al., 2023). Indeed, several RPA or RAA assays for Cps detection have been reported previously; however, most studies focused on a single detection format or different target genes(Pang et al., 2021; Jiao et al., 2022).

In addition to the amplification platform, the choice of target gene is a key determinant of assay specificity and applicability. Several molecular assays for Cps detection have been developed based on targets such as 16S rRNA and ompA (Sachse et al., 2009; Soon et al., 2025). However, the high sequence conservation of 16S rRNA among Chlamydia species may limit species-level discrimination, whereas genetic variability within ompA has been reported among different Cps genotypes (Sachse et al., 2023). Comparative genomic analyses have identified CPSIT_0429 as a Cps-specific gene that is absent from other closely related Chlamydia species (Voigt et al., 2012). Sequence comparison of representative Cps genotypes further indicated a high degree of conservation within the CPSIT_0429 gene (Fig. 1). In addition, CPSIT_0429 has been employed as a target in several previously reported molecular detection assays for Cps (Wang et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025, 2026). Therefore, CPSIT_0429 was selected as the target gene for assay development in this study.

Sequence alignment of CPSIT_0429 gene showing the locations of primers and probes used in this study.

Based on the CPSIT_0429 gene, specific RPA primers, exo probes, and nfo probes were designed to develop two rapid detection assays, including a real-time fluorescence RPA assay (qRPA) and a lateral flow strip RPA assay (LFS RPA). The developed qRPA and LFS RPA assays were validated using 334 clinical samples to provide technical support for rapid field identification of Cps, enabling swift diagnosis of Cps infection.

Materials and methods

Viral, bacteria strains and clinical samples

Chlamydia psittaci (Cps), Streptococcus pneumoniae (SP), Klebsiella pneumoniae (KPN), Staphylococcus aureus (SAU), Salmonella, Chlamydia trachomatis (CT), Chlamydia pneumoniae (Cpn), Chlamydia abortus (CA), Avian influenza virus subtype H9 (AIV-H9), and Fowl adenovirus serotype 4 (FAdV-4), nucleic acids were stored in −80°C freezers at the Shijiazhuang Customs Technical Center Laboratory.

This study collected 334 samples of oropharyngeal-cloacal swabs, feces, and lung samples from pigeons, parrots, ducks, and geese across various regions of Hebei Province, China, between February and December 2025. The samples comprised 273 oropharyngeal-cloacal swabs, 54 fecal samples, and 7 lung samples.

Sample processing and DNA extraction

For feces and lung samples, 50 mg was clipped into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, 1 mL of PBS buffer and 4 sterile grinding steel beads were added, and the sample was placed in a tissue grinder and shaken at 30 Hz for 5 min, and then mixed thoroughly. The grinding solution from feces and lung samples were centrifuged at 4°C and 10000 g for 5 min, discarded the supernatant and retained the pellet for subsequent use. For oropharyngeal-cloacal swabs, 1 mL of PBS buffer was added and was vortexed thoroughly. 300 μL of the sample was centrifuged at 4°C and 10,000 g for 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was aspirated and the pellet was retained for subsequent use. All pellets were processed using a bacterial DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) to extract DNA. Nucleic acids were stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis.

Design and synthesis of primers and probes

Based on the Cps genomic sequences published on GenBank (CP002549.1, CP002744.1, CP002805.1, CP002807.1, HF545614.1, CP002806.1, CP003796.1, CP003798.1, CP024453.1), the CPSIT_0429 genome sequence were analyzed by gene sequence comparison using Megalign software, and Primer5.0 software was used to design the basic RPA primers for the CPSIT_0429 gene.

Screening candidate RPA primers used the GenDx Basic Nucleic Acid Amplification Kit (GenDx Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) to obtain the optimal RPA primers. The basic RPA products were purified by a common DNA product purification kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) and then were analyzed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The best primer pairs with bright bands and no non-specific amplification primer combinations were selected, and exo probe and nfo probe were then designed based on this optimal primer pair. DNA standard preparation

Nucleic acids were extracted from samples using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). A portion of the CPSIT_0429 gene fragment was amplified using PCR primers (Table 1). The amplified products were subjected to 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The target linear gel band was excised and purified using an agarose gel DNA recovery kit (Sanshibio, Hebei, China) for purification and recovery. The product was ligated into the pGEM@-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, US), transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells (Tiangen, Beijing, China), and amplified for 15 hours. Colonies were picked and the bacterial cultures were verified. Positive clonal strains were sent to Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing China) for sequencing. Correctly sequenced strains were cultured in a 37°C shaking incubator for 15 hours. Recombinant plasmids were extracted using the TIANprep Mini Plasmid Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Finally, the DNA concentration of the pGEM@-T Easy-CPSIT_0429 plasmid was measured using NanoDrop 2000c ((Thermo, Waltham, US). The number of DNA copies was calculated using the following formula: 6.02 × 1023 × [X (ng/μL)] × 10-9/ (DNA length × 660) = Y (copies/μL).

In this study, the DNA copy number of the pGEM@-T Easy-CPSIT_0429 plasmid was 1.0 × 1010 copies/μL.

qPCR assay

A previously validated TaqMan real-time PCR assay targeting the CPSIT_0429 gene of Cps, as described by Wolff et al., was used as the reference assay in this study (Wolff et al., 2018). The qPCR assay was performed in a final reaction volume of 25.0 μL, containing 12.5 μL of 2 × PerfectStart® II Probe qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), 2.0 μL of template DNA, 1.0 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 0.5 μL of probe (10 μM), and 8.0 μL of nuclease-free water. Amplification was carried out using a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) under the following cycling conditions: 94°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Fluorescence signals were collected during the 60°C annealing/extension step.

qRPA assay

A plasmid standard at a concentration of 1.0 × 105 copies/uL was used as the template. qRPA amplification was performed with a fluorescent nucleic acid amplification kit (GenDx Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). The total reaction mixture was 50.0 μL: 20.0 μL of rehydration buffer, 2.1 μL of each primer (10 μM), 0.6 μL of exo probe (10 μM), 2.0 μL of magnesium acetate, 2.0 μL of template DNA or 5.0 μL extracted nucleic acid, and ddH₂O added to a final volume of 50.0 μL. The rehydration buffer, primers, exo probe, and ddH₂O were added sequentially to a 0.2 mL reaction tube containing lyophilized enzyme. The template or sample DNA was then added, followed by magnesium acetate added to the tube cap and seal. The reaction tubes were immediately mixed and spun down. The tube was then immediately placed into a Genie III isothermal amplification fluorescence detector (OptiGene Limited, West Sussex, UK), where it was incubated at 39°C for 20 min, with fluorescence signals collected every 30 seconds. The results were reported as threshold time (tt, mm: ss).

LFS RPA assay

The LFS RPA assay was performed in a 50-µL volume using the GenDxTM LFS kit (GenDx Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). The reaction mixture contained 20 μL of rehydration buffer, 2.0 μL of magnesium acetate, 2.1 μL of each primer (10 μM), 0.6 μL of nfo probe (10 μM), 2.0 μL of template DNA or 5.0 μL extracted nucleic acid, and ddH₂O was added to a final volume of 50.0 μL. The reaction tubes were immediately mixed and spun down. Then the reaction mixture was immediately incubated in a metal bath. After the reaction, 5 μL of RPA products was mixed with 200 μL of ddH2O followed by the lateral flow strip analysis (GenDx Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). The results were considered positive when both the test line and the control line were visible, negative when only the control line was visible, and invalid when the control line was invisible.

To determine the optimal amplification temperature, the LFS RPA reactions were performed on a metal bath incubator set at 37-42°C for 20 min. Thereafter, the reactions were performed at the optimal temperature for 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min to determine the optimal incubation time. The optimal incubation time was determined by comparing the brightness of the detection lines. Each reaction was conducted in parallel and repeated three times.

Analytical specificity analysis

The specificity of the developed Cps-specific qRPA and LFS RPA assays was examined with 1.0 × 105 copies/uL of Cps standard DNA and DNA or RNA of other respiratory pathogens including SP, KPN, SAU, Salmonella, AIV-H9, FAdV-4, CT, Cpn, and CA. During the analysis, ddH2O was served as the negative control. This was assessed whether the developed qRPA and LFS RPA assays exhibited cross-reactings with common pathogens. All the samples were tested in triplicates to evaluate the specificity of qRPA and LFS RPA assays.

Analytical sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis of the qRPA and LFS RPA assays was performed using 10-fold serial dilutions of standard Cps standard DNA ranging between 1.0 × 105 and 1.0 × 10° copies/uL. And 2 μL of each was taken to prepare mixed templates for qRPA sensitivity assay. During the analysis, ddH2O was served as the negative control. All the samples were tested in triplicates to evaluate the specificity of the qRPA and LFS RPA assays. Meanwhile, 1.0 × 102 copies/uL of Cps standard DNA was serially diluted in twofold (one in one to one in four) and tested for the qRPA assays in eight replicates, whereas 1.0 × 101 copies/uL of Cps standard DNA was serially diluted in twofold (one in one to one in four) and tested for LFS RPA in eight replicates. A Probit (predicted proportion positive) analysis (SPSS v22.0, Armonk, USA) with data of the positive samples from each of the eight replications was performed to establish the 95% limit of detection (LOD).

Evaluation of the dual qRPA and LFS RPA assays on the clinical samples

The extracted DNA from 334 of oropharyngeal-cloacal swabs, feces, and lung samples were used as templates for detection of Cps in clinical samples using the developed qRPA and LFS RPA assays. The results were compared with those obtained using the qPCR assay (Wolff et al., 2018) to evaluate the practical value of the developed assays in real-world applications.

Sequencing typing of positive samples

OmpA gene typing was performed on the Cps-positive samples detected in this study. Initially, partial segments of the ompA gene were amplified by PCR (Heddema et al., 2015) and complete sequences were obtained by excising the clear positive bands from agarose gel electrophoresis. Due to the short length of the amplified sequences, the bright bands were purified using an agarose gel DNA recovery kit and the purified products were ligated into the pGEM@-T Easy vector and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells for cloning to obtain full-length sequences. After incubation for 15 hours, colonies were selected and cultured for verification. Then, positive clones were sent to Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for sequencing. Finally, the obtained sequences were compared with those published in the NCBI database to determine the genotype of the positive samples.

Statistical analysis and agreement assessment

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Diagnostic agreement between assays was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Differences in paired categorical results between the qPCR assay and the qRPA or LFS RPA assays were assessed using McNemar’s test. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening of the optimal primer–probe combinations

Ten pairs of RPA primers were designed for the CPSIT_0429 gene specific to Cps, amplified using a basic RPA nucleic acid amplification kit. The results demonstrated that all ten primer pairs successfully amplified the target band (Fig. 2). Among these, the band produced by primer pair 7 (RPA-F3/RPA-R4) exhibited greater intensity and showed no evidence of non-specific amplification. Consequently, this pair was selected as the optimal primers for designing the exo probe for the qRPA and the nfo probe for the LFS RPA. The exo probe and the nfo probe had the same sequence but were differently modified. Both possessed a simulated tetrahydrofuran (THF) base site, positioned at least 30 bases from the 5′ end and at least 15 bases from the 3′ end, with a polymerase module added at the 3′ terminus. For the exo probe, the two adjacent thymine (T) bases flanking the THF moiety were substituted with a fluorescent group and a quencher group. The nfo probe was labelled with FAM at the 5′ end, while the corresponding reverse primer was labelled with biotin at the 5′ end. Performance of The qRPA Assay

The specificity of the developed qRPA assay was evaluated using the Cps plasmid standard and nucleic acids from other pathogens. The results showed that only the Cps plasmid standard produced a significant amplification curve, while no nonspecific amplification was observed for the other tested respiratory pathogens, including SP, KPN, SAU, Salmonella, AIV-H9, FAdV-4, CT, Cpn, and CA. These results were consistent across three replicates, demonstrating that the developed qRPA assay exhibited excellent specificity (Fig. 3A).

Performance of RPA assays on clinical samples

Utilizing the assays developed by this study to test 334 clinical samples, the results showed that the detection rates for the qRPA assay and the LFS RPA assay were 4.80% (16/334) and 5.70% (19/334) respectively. These results were validated against a reference qPCR assay (Wolff et al., 2018), which yielded a detection rate of 6.00% (20/334) (Table 2). The concordance between the qRPA assay and the qPCR assay was 98.80% with a kappa value of 0.883; the concordance between the LFS RPA assay and the qPCR assay was 99.70% with a kappa value of 0.974 (Table 3). McNemar’s test showed no statistically significant difference between the qPCR assay and the qRPA assay (P = 0.13), or between the qPCR assay and the LFS RPA assay (P = 1.00). These results indicate that both RPA-based assays exhibited performance comparable to that of the qPCR assay in clinical sample testing. The TT values from the qRPA assay were exhibited a strong linear relationship with the Ct values from the qPCR assay, with an R² value of 0.9730 (Fig. 6). The above results indicated that the qRPA assay and LFS RPA assay developed in this study had detection performance comparable to the qPCR assay and could be effectively applied to real clinical samples.

Sequencing typing of positive samples

Genotyping was performed on CPs-positive samples detected by this study. Using the PCR assay to amplify a partial fragment of the ompA gene, seven positive samples were observed bright bands on agarose gel electrophoresis, while the remaining thirteen positive samples showed faint bands and failed to meet the quality requirements for sequencing. Ultimately, the seven positive samples with bright bands were submitted for sequencing. The sequencing results of Cps-positive samples were compared with Cps strains sequences available in GenBank. For two Cps-positive samples collected from cockatiels and one Cps-positive sample collected from budgerigars, the sequences showed 99.45-100% identity with Cps strain sequence OQ361542.1, confirmed genotype A. For three Cps-positive samples collected from pigeons, the sequences exhibited 100% identity with Cps strain sequence MW679011.1, confirmed genotype B. For one Cps-positive sample collected from a pigeon, the sequence showed 99.45% identity with Cps strain sequence AY762611.1, confirmed genotype E.

Discussion

Rapid and accurate detection of Cps is essential for the prevention and control of psittacosis. In this study, qRPA and LFS RPA assays were successfully developed, both of which enabled the detection of Cps within 20 min. Due to the low temperature requirements and minimal dependence on specialized instrumentation, RPA-based assays are well suited for point-of-care and field-based applications (Daher et al., 2016; Weidmann et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2022). Furthermore, coupling RPA with lateral flow strips enables visual result interpretation without additional instrumentation, enhancing its applicability for on-site testing (Feng et al., 2023).

In addition to the detection platform, target gene selection is a critical for the specificity and applicability of molecular diagnostic assays. In this study, CPSIT_0429 was selected because it has been reported as a species-specific marker of Cps with no homologs in other Chlamydia species (Voigt et al., 2012). Both the qRPA and LFS RPA assays developed in this study exhibited excellent specificity, with no cross-reactivity observed against any of the non-target pathogens tested, while all Cps samples were successfully detected.

The qRPA and LFS RPA assays developed in this study achieved limits of detection of 120 copies/μL and 13 copies/μL, respectively. The higher analytical sensitivity of LFS RPA is consistent with previous reports (Liu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020) and be primarily attributed to endpoint detection, which allows greater accumulation of amplification products prior to visual readout (Lobato and O'Sullivan, 2018; Zhao et al., 2024). In contrast, weak fluorescence signals generated during qRPA may not always reach the predefined detection threshold when target concentrations are low (Feng et al., 2023). Furthermore, qRPA and LFS RPA employ different probe systems, namely exo and nfo probes, respectively. Differences in probe recognition, cleavage efficiency, and signal conversion may also affect assay performance.

Several isothermal amplification assays targeting different Cps genes have been reported previously. A qRPA assay targeting the ompA gene was reported to achieve a detection limit of 100 copies/μL (Pang et al., 2021), comparable to that of the qRPA assay developed in this study. More recently, CPSIT_0429-based CRISPR/Cas assays have demonstrated promising performance for Cps detection (Wang et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025, 2026). Although CRISPR-assisted assays can achieve high analytical sensitivity, they generally require a longer workflow, typically ranging from 1 to 1.5 h, due to the additional target recognition and cleavage steps (Zhou et al., 2025). In addition, most CRISPR-based detection systems involve multiple reaction components, such as Cas proteins, crRNAs, and reporter probes, which increase assay complexity. Furthermore, the relatively high cost of CRISPR-related reagents may limit their application in resource-limited settings (van Dongen et al., 2020; Ji et al., 2025). In contrast, the qRPA and LFS RPA assays developed in this study achieved high sensitivity and specificity without the need for an additional CRISPR detection system and could be completed within 20 min, making them attractive options for field screening and use in primary diagnostic laboratories.

In addition to evaluating assay performance, positive samples were subjected to ompA-based genotyping to characterize the circulating Cps strains (Sachse et al., 2008; Heddema et al., 2015). Genotypes A, B, and E were identified among the positive samples. Genotype A was detected in cockatiels and budgerigars, whereas genotypes B and E were identified in pigeons. The observed association between host species and genotype was consistent with previous epidemiological reports, suggesting that the circulating strains detected in this study followed the host preference patterns commonly reported for Cps (Pannekoek et al., 2010; Tolba et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2023).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small number of positive samples limited the number of strains available for genotyping. In addition, successful genotyping was achieved for only a subset of positive samples, likely due to low pathogen loads that yielded insufficient PCR products for sequencing. Furthermore, assay performance was evaluated primarily using avian samples and was not systematically assessed across different Cps genotypes. Further studies using larger and more diverse sample sets are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the performance and applicability of the developed assays.

Conclusions

The qRPA assay and the LFS RPA assay capable of rapid detection of Cps within 20 min were successfully developed in this study. The developed the qRPA assay and the LFS RPA assay demonstrated high specificity, sensitivity, and operational simplicity with short reaction times, making them suitable as routine laboratory assays for Cps detection. Owing to the portability of fluorescence analyser, the visually observable LFS, and the rapid availability of results, the assays were also deemed well-suited for field deployment during outbreaks, particularly under resource-constrained conditions, for the rapid identification and detection of Cps.
