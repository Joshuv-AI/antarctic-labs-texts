An environmentally relevant phthalate mixture impairs ovulatory prostaglandin and progesterone receptor pathways in human granulosa cells in vitro
Authors: Katie L. Land, Hong Xu, James W. Akin, Patrick R. Hannon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovulatory defects are the leading cause of female infertility. Widespread exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals, such as phthalates, may contribute to the high prevalence of failed ovulation among infertile women. Using primary ovarian granulosa cells obtained from women, we tested the hypothesis that exposure to an environmentally relevant mixture of phthalate metabolites (MPTmix) impairs essential mediators of the ovulatory process, including progesterone (P4), progesterone receptor (PGR), and prostaglandins (PGs). The composition of the MPTmix was derived from urinary phthalate levels in women. Ovarian granulosa cells, obtained from in vitro fertilization patients, were acclimated in culture to regain responsiveness to hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin, clinical luteinizing hormone analogue). Following acclimation, cells were treated for 0.5–36hr with media containing DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide, vehicle control), ± hCG (to initiate the ovulatory cascade), and ± MPTmix (1–500 μg/ml). Compared to hCG controls, treatment with hCG + MPTmix reduced active ovulatory PG levels by up to 77%, likely via decreased synthesis (lower PTGS2 and PTGES levels/activity), enhanced catabolism of PGE2 to PGF2α (elevated AKR1C1 and AKR1C3 levels), and increased metabolism (elevated HPGD levels/activity). MPTmix exposure further impaired PG function by altering the levels of PG transporters (ABCC4 and SLCO2A1) and receptors (PTGER1-4 and PTGFR). These MPTmix-induced disruptions were accompanied by upstream defects in LH/hCG receptor signaling (cAMP/PKA, ERK1/2), P4 steroidogenesis, and PGR expression. Together, these findings demonstrate that exposure to phthalates impairs P4/PGR-driven PG production/function in human ovarian cells and advances our mechanistic understanding of how phthalate exposure may contribute to ovulatory dysfunction in women.

Introduction

Phthalates are synthetic chemicals widely used as plasticizers and solvents in consumer products ranging from food packaging and building materials to medical devices and cosmetics. As a result, humans are exposed to a mixture of phthalate compounds daily through ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact (Hannon and Flaws, 2015). Upon absorption, these phthalates are rapidly metabolized into bioactive monoesters that reach the ovary (Land et al., 2025). Constant and unavoidable exposure to phthalates is concerning, as they are well-established endocrine disruptors that are increasingly being linked to adverse reproductive outcomes, including female infertility (Land et al., 2025; 2022). Additionally, adult women face especially high exposure due to the prevalence of phthalates in personal care products and cosmetics (Land et al., 2025; 2022). Despite the identification of phthalates as top ovarian contaminants and that ovulatory defects are the leading cause of infertility in women, the direct effects of phthalates on the ovulatory process remain poorly understood. This critical gap in knowledge limits our ability to fully evaluate and address environmental contributions to infertility and overall reproductive health.

Ovulation is an endocrine-driven process initiated by the luteinizing hormone (LH) surge or human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) stimulation. Among the most well-characterized downstream mediators of LH/hCG action across species are progesterone (P4), progesterone receptor (PGR), and prostaglandins (PGs)—which are the central focus of the present study (Duffy et al., 2019). Although studies using rodent models have long-established that PGR and PGs serve independent roles in the ovulatory process (Russell and Robker, 2007), recent studies using human models have shown that PGR is upstream of PG synthesis and transport, thereby directly coordinating PG production and function during ovulation in women (Duffy et al., 2019; Choi et al., 2017). Binding of LH/hCG to its receptor (LHCGR) on granulosa cells activates a complex signaling network that includes cAMP/PKA (cyclic adenosine monophosphate/protein kinase A) and AKT (protein kinase B) pathways, in addition to indirect activation of ERK1/2 (extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2) signaling following LH/hCG-induced transactivation of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR/ERBB family) (Duffy et al., 2019). Activation of the cAMP/PKA pathway induces expression of PGR, and subsequent activation of PGR by P4 stimulates expression of PG synthases and transporters, leading to elevated levels of PGs (PGE2 and PGF2α) within the human ovulatory follicle (Choi et al., 2017). Additionally, ERK1/2 signaling downstream of EGFR transactivation has also been shown to directly regulate transcription of PG synthases and promote PGE2 production in human granulosa-lutein cells (Tsai et al., 2008). Functionally, P4/PGR and PGs mediate key ovulatory events within the follicle, including cumulus-oocyte complex (COC) expansion/stability, oocyte maturation, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling, which are all required for the release of a fertilizable oocyte from an ovarian follicle and formation of the corpus luteum (CL; remnant follicular structure that supports pregnancy) (Duffy et al., 2019).

As illustrated in Fig. 1, ovulatory PG production and action involves coordinated expression of synthases, metabolic enzymes, transporters, and receptors, along with upstream LHCGR- and PGR-mediated signaling events. In granulosa cells, LH/hCG stimulation promotes PG production and signaling by inducing multiple PG synthases/reductases (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGES, and AKR1C1), transporters (ABCC4 and SLCO2A1), and receptors (PTGER2, PTGER3, and PTGFR), while simultaneously suppressing metabolic enzymes (HPGD) to maintain elevated levels of active PGs (Duffy et al., 2019; Hannon et al., 2025). In contrast, other PG synthases (PLA2G4A, PTGES2, PTGES3), catabolic enzymes (AKR1C3), and receptors (PTGER1, PTGER4) are expressed constitutively during the ovulatory period (Duffy et al., 2019; Hannon et al., 2025). In a stepwise fashion, ovulatory PG production begins when arachidonic acid is released from membrane phospholipids by PLA2G4A. Arachidonic acid is converted into a short-lived PG inter-mediate (PGH2) primarily by PTGS2 and, to a lesser extent, PTGS1. PGH2 is then converted to active PGE2 primarily by PTGES, with PTGES2 and PTGES3 contributing to a lesser degree (Hannon et al., 2025). PGE2 can be further converted to PGF2α by AKR1C1, while AKR1C3 can generate PGF2α directly from PGH2. PGs are inactivated by the metabolic enzyme, HPGD. Importantly, PGE2 is consistently recognized in the literature as the key PG required for ovulation and is essential for fertility (Duffy, 2015; Duffy et al., 2010). The specific roles of PGF2α during the ovulatory process remain unclear, and the ovulatory increases in PGF2α may, in part, be by-products of PGE2 catabolism (Duffy et al., 2019).

Given the importance of PGR signaling and PGs in ovulation and subsequent fertility, the present study exposed primary human granulosa cells to a phthalate metabolite mixture (MPTmix), derived from urinary levels in women, to characterize phthalate-induced changes in ovulatory PGR levels and production of P4 and PGs following LH/hCG stimulation. Areas of investigation included P4 steroidogenesis, PGR expression, and the regulation of PG synthesis, metabolism, transport, and receptor expression. Unlike prior studies limited to individual phthalate compound exposures in rodent models, this work employs a primary human granulosa cell model and a human-derived phthalate mixture that includes ovarian-relevant concentrations, human-relevant exposure levels, and elevated concentrations that were used to identify ovulatory pathways susceptible to phthalate-induced disruption (Du et al., 2019; 2016; Kavlock et al., 2002; Weuve et al., 2006; Hernández-Díaz et al., 2009; 2013). Collectively, the present study advances the field of reproductive toxicology by elucidating molecular mechanisms through which endocrine-disrupting chemicals compromise key ovulatory mediators in women.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The MPTmix was formulated with the following composition: 36% monoethyl phthalate (MEP), 19% mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (MEHP), 15% monobutyl phthalate (MBP), 10% monoisononyl phthalate (MiNP), 10% monoisobutyl phthalate (MiBP), and 8% monobenzyl phthalate (MBzP). These proportions were based on the percentages of urinary phthalate metabolite levels measured in pregnant women participating in the Children’s Environmental Health Research Center at the University of Illinois (Hannon et al., 2023; Zhou and Flaws, 2017; Zhou et al., 2017). All compounds were obtained from Toronto Research Chemicals (Ontario, Canada) or Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) at >95% purity. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) served as the vehicle to prepare stock solutions of the mixture, and equal volumes of each stock were added to cultures to maintain a constant vehicle concentration (0.75 μg/ml). The resulting final concentrations of the MPTmix in culture were 1, 10, 100, and 500 μg/ml. These concentrations were informed by prior studies demonstrating that similar phthalate mixtures disrupt ovarian function and impair processes critical for ovulation and fertility in both human and mouse models. Specifically, previous work from our laboratory showed that comparable doses of a parent-compound phthalate mixture inhibited ovulatory P4/PGR signaling and PG production in primary human granulosa cells in vitro (Hannon et al., 2025; 2023), and reduced ovulation rates in mouse follicles via decreased PG levels (Land et al., 2021). Additional studies using similar metabolite mixtures and concentrations have reported inhibition of antral follicle growth, reductions in steroid hormone production, and induction of oocyte fragmentation in mouse follicles in vitro (Zhou and Flaws, 2017; Meling et al., 2020; Mourikes and Flaws, 2021).

When considering the individual phthalate compounds as a percentage of the total mixture, the lowest MPTmix dose (1 μg/ml) contains 190 ng/ml MEHP and 150 ng/ml MBP—levels below the highest reported concentrations of these metabolites in human follicular fluid (239 ng/ml MEHP and 415 ng/ml MBP) (Du et al., 2016). Higher MPTmix doses were selected to be representative of populations with extensive phthalate exposure, such as individuals with occupational exposure, those taking daily medications formulated in phthalate-containing capsules, or patients undergoing medical procedures involving the use of phthalate-containing medical tubing and devices (Kavlock et al., 2002; Weuve et al., 2006; Hernández-Díaz et al., 2009; 2013). Therefore, the lowest MPTmix dose (1 μg/ml) was selected to approximate exposure levels measured in women’s ovaries, whereas the higher doses were included to characterize dose-dependent effects, elucidate potential mechanisms of disruption, and identify ovulatory pathways vulnerable to phthalate toxicity under elevated exposure conditions.

Human granulosa cell collection and in vitro culture

This study utilized primary human granulosa cells obtained from sixty-two IVF (in vitro fertilization) patients and an in vitro culture system that has been consistently shown to recapitulate periovulatory events observed in women in vivo, including increased P4, PGR, and PG production (Choi et al., 2017; Hannon et al., 2025; 2023; 2018; Al-Alem et al., 2015; Rosewell et al., 2015). This work was conducted under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Kentucky Office of Research Integrity. Women undergoing IVF at the Bluegrass Fertility Center (Lexington, KY) underwent standardized ovarian stimulation, consisting of recombinant human follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) treatment for 9–11 days, followed by administration of hCG (10,000U) to initiate the ovulatory cascade. The FSH and hCG medications administered to patients were prescribed through external pharmacies based on patient insurance coverage and access. Just prior to oocyte release (36hr post-hCG), follicular contents were collected via aspiration. COCs were isolated at the IVF clinic, and the remaining follicular aspirates were processed using a Percoll gradient to separate granulosa cells from red and white blood cells, yielding a purified granulosa cell population for culture. Granulosa cells were then plated at 2–2.5×105cells/ml in Gibco™ Opti-MEM™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) and 1% Gibco™ antibiotic–antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and were maintained in culture for 6 days with daily media changes. During this acclimation period, granulosa cells revert to a preovulatory phenotype, re-express functional LH/hCG receptors, regain responsiveness to hCG, and recapitulate periovulatory outcomes (Choi et al., 2017; Al-Alem et al., 2015). Of particular relevance to the present study, these revitalized cells upregulate PG synthases/transporters, PGR, and P4 steroidogenic genes in response to hCG, resulting in increased PG production and P4/PGR signaling (Choi et al., 2017; Hannon et al., 2025; 2023). These hCG-induced changes closely mimic in vivo ovulatory responses, thereby providing a robust model for investigating ovulatory mechanisms disrupted by exposure to environmental toxicants in vitro (Land et al., 2025). To assess the impact of phthalates, cells were exposed to DMSO (vehicle control) or the MPTmix (1, 10, 100, or 500 μg/ml) for 48hr prior to hCG stimulation. After pre-treatment, cells were cultured in serum-free media for 1hr, then treated with or without hCG (1 IU/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and with or without MPTmix. Cell and media samples were collected at 0, 0.5, 6, 12, 24, and 36hr post-hCG treatment for the assays described below. For each analysis, data are expressed as a percent change relative to each patient’s hCG control group at the corresponding time point in order to account for inter-patient biological variability.

PG (prostaglandin) measurements

At 24 and 36hr following hCG treatment, media were collected for PG measurements. Concentrations of active PGs, PGE2 and PGF2α, and their inactive metabolites, PGE2 metabolite and 13,14-dihydro-15-keto PGF2α (hereafter referred to as PGF2α metabolite), were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI). The PGE2 metabolite assay measures a single, stable derivative formed after conversion of both 13,14-dihydro-15-keto PGA2 and 13,14-dihydro-15-keto PGE2. Performance characteristics for PGE2, PGF2α, PGE2 metabolite, and PGF2α metabolite ELISAs, respectively, were as follows: Sensitivity: 15 pg/ml, 10 pg/ml, 2 pg/ml, and 15 pg/ml; Intraassay coefficient of variation: 5.7%, 13.8%, 12.5%, and 16.9%; Interassay coefficient of variation: 12.8%, 11%, 15.1%, and 16%.

PTGS2 (prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2) activity assay

COX2 (cyclooxygenase 2)/PTGS2 activity was measured using the COX Activity Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein was extracted from cells 24 and 36hr after hCG treatment using RIPA lysis buffer (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride solution (PMSF; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and samples were normalized by dilution so that 0.8 μg of protein per sample was loaded per well. COX peroxidase activity was quantified colorimetrically at 590 nm using a Synergy LX multimode plate reader (Agilent BioTek), with isozyme-specific inhibitors included to distinguish COX2 activity from COX1 activity. The assay sensitivity was 13 nmol/min/ml, and intraassay and interassay coefficients of variation were 2.6% and 5.4%, respectively.

15-PGDH (15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase) activity assay

HPGD (15-PGDH) activity was measured using the 15-PGDH Activity Assay Fluorometric Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein was extracted from cells 24 and 36hr after hCG treatment using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail, PMSF solution, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and samples were normalized by dilution so that 13.3 μg of protein per sample was loaded per well. In this assay, HPGD oxidizes a substrate to generate intermediates and NADH, which in turn reduces a probe to produce fluorescence (excitation/emission = 535/587 nm). Fluorescence was measured on a Synergy LX multimode plate reader (Agilent BioTek), with the assay sensitivity being 1μU and activity being proportional to the fluorescent signal.

P4 (progesterone) measurements

Media were collected immediately before (0hr) and after (6, 12, 24, and 36hr) hCG treatment for P4 measurements. P4 concentrations were quantified using an IMMULITE kit (Siemens Medical Solutions, Malvern, PA). The assay sensitivity was 0.02 ng/ml, with intraassay and interassay coefficients of variation of 7% and 12%, respectively.

Intracellular signaling molecule assays

Protein was extracted from cells 0.5hr after hCG treatment using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail, PMSF solution, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and all samples were normalized by dilution to ensure equal protein loading for each assay. Normalized lysates were then used to quantify levels of cAMP (24 μg protein/well), PKA activity (0.13 μg protein/well), p-AKT (40 μg protein/well), and p-ERK1/2 (8 μg protein/well). More specifically, cellular cAMP levels were quantified using the cyclic AMP ELISA Kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI), a competitive ELISA in which bound acetylcholinesterase (AChE) reflects cAMP concentration. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm on a Synergy LX multimode plate reader (Agilent BioTek); assay sensitivity was 0.078 pmol/ml. PKA activity was measured using the PKA Colorimetric Activity Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), which detects phosphorylation of an immobilized substrate by PKA in the presence of ATP. Phosphorylated substrate was detected using a two-antibody system, and relative kinase activity was determined by absorbance read at 450 nm; assay sensitivity was 0.037U/ml. Phosphorylated AKT levels were quantified using the AKT1 [pS473] Ultrasensitive ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) with a sandwich ELISA format to specifically detect AKT protein phosphorylated at serine 473. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm; assay sensitivity was < 0.2U/ml, with intraassay and interassay coefficients of variation below 6%. Phosphorylated ERK1/2 levels were quantified using the phospho-ERK 1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204, Thr185/Tyr187) InstantOne ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) with a modified sandwich ELISA format, and bound phospho-ERK 1/2 was detected colorimetrically at 450 nm.

Gene expression analysis

Following collection at each time point, cells were lysed for gene expression analysis via quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Total RNA (200 ng) was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) and reverse transcribed to cDNA with the iScript RT Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA), according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Nuclease-free water was used to further dilute cDNA samples, and qPCR was performed using the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System and accompanying QuantStudio 3 qPCR Data Analysis Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific-Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA). Specifically, qPCR was conducted using either SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) with laboratory-designed primers (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA) or TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Waltham, MA) with TaqMan primers. Primer specificity was conducted by using previously published primer sequences, confirmation of a single product of the appropriate size via agarose gel electrophoresis, and the NCBI Primer-BLAST database (Choi et al., 2017; Hannon et al., 2025; 2023; Al-Alem et al., 2015; Rosewell et al., 2015; Ye et al., 2012; Sayers et al., 2025). Primer sequences and details are provided in Table 1. GNPDA2 (SYBR) and GAPDH (TaqMan) were used as reference genes, as their expression did not significantly vary across time points or treatment groups. Per the manufacturer’s protocol, the SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix program consisted of enzyme activation (95°C, 30 s), amplification and quantification (45 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 10 s) with single fluorescence reading, and a melt curve (65–95°C, 0.5°C/s) with continuous fluorescence readings. The TaqMan program included AmpErase uracil-N-glycosylase activation (50°C, 2 min), denaturation (95°C, 10 min), amplification (50 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min), and ramp dissociation (95°C for 1 min, 58°C for 30 s, 95°C for 30 s). Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method. ΔCt values were obtained by subtracting the reference gene Ct from the gene of interest Ct. ΔΔCt values were calculated relative to the 0hr DMSO control group (immediately prior to hCG treatment), and fold change was calculated as 2−ΔΔCt (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and graph generation were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA), respectively. Results are presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean) across all patients (n = 3–19 patients/group/individual experiment), with each patient representing an independent replicate. To account for inter-patient biological variability, data were normalized and expressed as percent change or percent fold change relative to each patient’s hCG control group at each time point, with hCG set at 100%, meaning each patient’s cells served as their own control. Therefore, multiple comparisons between experimental groups were made using Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn-Bonferroni pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance relative to the hCG control group was assigned at p ≤ 0.05 and was denoted as asterisks in the graphs.

Results

Effect of MPTmix on PG production

The critical rise in PG levels during ovulation is mediated by the coordinated activity of PLA2G4A, PTGS1 (COX1), PTGS2 (COX2), and PGE2-specific (PTGES, PTGES2, PTGES3) synthases. Among these, PTGS2 functions as the rate-limiting enzyme, and its inhibition profoundly disrupts ovulation, fertilization, and implantation in both women and animal models, underscoring its essential role in female fertility (Killick and Elstein, 1987; Pall et al., 2001; Bata et al., 2006; Hester et al., 2010; McCann et al., 2013). In the present study, we first evaluated whether cytotoxicity contributed to MPTmix-induced effects and found no alterations in cellular metabolism or viability across treatment groups prior to hCG stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 1). We next assessed active PG levels and PTGS2 protein activity during the late ovulatory period (24 and 36hr) following hCG + MPTmix treatment, along with the temporal expression of the above-mentioned PG synthases across the ovulatory window (6, 12, 24, and 36hr). Treatment with hCG increased PGE2 (24 and 36hr) and PGF2α (24hr) levels when compared to the DMSO group that did not receive hCG (Fig. 2A and B). However, treatment with hCG + MPTmix decreased PGE2 levels at the 24 (all doses) and 36hr (1, 10, and 100 μg/ml) time points when compared to hCG controls (Fig. 2A). Similarly, PGF2α levels were decreased at the 24 (1, 10, and 500 μg/ml) and 36hr (1, 10, and 100 μg/ml) time points with hCG + MPTmix treatment relative to hCG (Fig. 2B). As anticipated, hCG treatment increased PTGS2 protein activity (24 and 36hr) and gene expression (6 and 12hr) compared to DMSO (Fig. 2C and D). However, depending on the time point measured (24 or 36hr), all tested doses of hCG + MPTmix decreased PTGS2 protein activity relative to hCG ovulatory controls (Fig. 2C). Similarly, PTGS2 expression was reduced with hCG + MPTmix treatment at 6 (500 μg/ml), 12 (1 and 500 μg/ml), 24 (all doses), and 36hr (1, 100, and 500 μg/ml) time points compared to hCG controls (Fig. 2D).

Since all hCG + MPTmix doses reduced the levels of both active PGs, we examined expression of all PG synthases under each treatment condition. Despite only select synthases being known to increase following LH/hCG stimulation, phthalate-induced alterations in the expression or activity of constitutively expressed synthases or synthases not induced by LH/hCG may also impair PG-mediated ovulatory processes. Treatment with hCG increased PLA2G4A (12hr) and PTGES (all time points) expression compared to DMSO (Fig. 3A, C). However, hCG treatment also decreased PLA2G4A (6 and 36hr), PTGS1 (36hr), and PTGES2 (all time points) expression relative to DMSO (Fig. 3A, B, D). In contrast, PTGES3 expression was unaffected by hCG treatment at all tested time points (Fig. 3E). Compared to hCG controls, MPTmix exposure increased PLA2G4A expression at 6hr (500 μg/ml), while 36hr expression was both increased (10 and 100 μg/ml) and decreased (500 μg/ml) (Fig. 3A). Treatment with hCG + MPTmix increased (100 μg/ml) and decreased (500 μg/ml) 6hr PTGS1 expression compared to hCG, but only decreased expression at the 12 (500 μg/ml), 24 (100 μg/ml), and 36hr (500 μg/ml) time points (Fig. 3B). PTGES expression was decreased with MPTmix exposure relative to hCG controls at 6 (10 and 500 μg/ml), 24 (10, 100, and 500 μg/ml), and 36hr (1, 100, and 500 μg/ml) time points (Fig. 3C). Treatment with hCG + MPTmix increased 6 (100 and 500 μg/ml), 12 (1 and 500 μg/ml), and 24hr (500 μg/ml) PTGES2 expression, but decreased expression at 36hr (500 μg/ml) compared to hCG controls (Fig. 3D). PTGES3 expression was increased at 24hr (500 μg/ml) with MPTmix exposure, while 36hr expression was both increased (100 μg/ml) and decreased (500 μg/ml) relative to hCG (Fig. 3E).

Effect of MPTmix on PG metabolism

In addition to LH/hCG-driven increases in PGE2 and PGF2α production, PG metabolism is concurrently downregulated to maintain elevated levels of active PGs. In this study, we measured inactive PG metabolites and HPGD (PG metabolic enzyme) protein activity during the late ovulatory period (24 and 36hr) following hCG + MPTmix treatment. We also assessed the expression of HPGD and AKR (aldo–keto reductase) family members across the ovulatory window (6, 12, 24, and 36hr). AKR enzymes may modulate active PGE2 levels or activity, either by converting PG precursors (PGH2) directly into PGF2α (AKR1C3) or by interconverting PGE2 and PGF2α (AKR1C1). This is particularly significant because, as noted in Section 1, PGE2 is consistently recognized as the critical PG for ovulation. Treatment with hCG increased inactive PGE2 metabolite levels, likely reflecting higher levels of PGE2 available for metabolism, but did not alter inactive PGF2α metabolite levels at either time point compared to DMSO (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast, hCG + MPTmix reduced PGE2 metabolite levels (100 and 500 μg/ml) at both time points and decreased PGF2α metabolite levels (1 μg/ml) at 24hr, but also increased PGF2α metabolite levels at 24 (500 μg/ml) and 36hr (100 and 500 μg/ml) time points relative to hCG (Fig. 4A and B). As expected, hCG treatment reduced HPGD protein activity (24 and 36hr) and gene expression (12, 24, and 36hr) compared to DMSO (Fig. 4C and D). However, hCG + MPTmix increased HPGD activity at 24 (all doses) and 36hr (1 and 500 μg/ml), and increased gene expression at 6 (100 and 500 μg/ml), 12 (500 μg/ml), 24 (500 μg/ml), and 36hr (100 and 500 μg/ml) relative to hCG (Fig. 4C and D). For AKR family members, hCG treatment induced a dynamic response. AKR1C1 (which interconverts PGE2 and PGF2α) expression increased at 6, 12, and 24hr but decreased at 36hr, while AKR1C3 (which directly synthesizes PGF2α) expression was consistently reduced compared to DMSO (Fig. 4E and F). With hCG + MPTmix, AKR1C1 expression was increased at 6 (100 μg/ml), 12 (10, 100, and 500 μg/ml), 24 (10, 100, and 500 μg/ml), and 36hr (10, 100, and 500 μg/ml), despite a single dose (1 μg/ml) that was decreased at 24hr relative to hCG (Fig. 4E). Similarly, AKR1C3 expression was upregulated with hCG + MPTmix at 6 (all doses), 12 (10 and 500 μg/ml), 24 (1 and 500 μg/ml), and 36hr (10 and 100 μg/ml) compared to hCG controls (Fig. 4F).

Effect of MPTmix on PG transporters

PG transporters are also regulated by LH/hCG and modulate the influx/efflux of PGs, which act in both an autocrine and paracrine manner. To assess the impact of phthalates on PG transport to/from granulosa cells, we measured the expression of the two key transporters (ABCC4 and SLCO2A1) across the ovulatory period. Treatment with hCG increased ABCC4 (6 and 12hr) and SLCO2A1 (6, 12, and 24hr) expression, but decreased 36hr SLCO2A1 expression compared to DMSO (Fig. 5A and B). ABCC4 expression was decreased with hCG + MPTmix treatment at 6 (10 μg/ml), 24 (1 μg/ml), and 36hr (500 μg/ml) time points compared to hCG controls, whereas SLCO2A1 expression was increased at 6 (100 and 500 μg/ml), 12 (1, 10, and 100 μg/ml), and 36hr (10 and 100 μg/ml) time points (Fig. 5A and B). However, a single MPTmix dose (500 μg/ml) decreased 36hr SLCO2A1 expression relative to hCG (Fig. 5B).

Effect of MPTmix on PG receptors

PGs mediate their ovulatory effects by binding to receptors expressed on multiple cell types within the ovarian follicle, including granulosa cells. To investigate how phthalates may influence PG function, we measured the expression of the four PGE2 receptors (PTGER1-4) and the singular PGF2α receptor (PTGFR) across the ovulatory period. Treatment with hCG decreased PTGER1 (6, 12, and 36hr) and PTGER4 (all time points) expression compared to DMSO, but increased expression of PTGER2 (6 and 12hr), PTGER3 (6hr), and PTGFR (24 and 36hr) (Fig. 6A–E). PTGER1 expression was elevated at all time points when treated with the hCG + MPTmix 500 μg/ml dose, but was reduced at 6hr with the 10 μg/ml dose (Fig. 6A). PTGER2 expression was largely suppressed by hCG + MPTmix at 6 (10, 100, and 500 μg/ml), 12 (500 μg/ml), 24 (1 and 500 μg/ml), and 36hr (500 μg/ml) time points, though a single MPTmix dose (10 μg/ml) increased 36hr expression (Fig. 6B). For PTGER3, hCG + MPTmix 1 μg/ml decreased 6hr expression, and the 500 μg/ml dose decreased expression at 24 and 36hr relative to hCG (Fig. 6C). PTGER4 expression was also reduced by hCG + MPTmix 500 μg/ml treatment at 6 and 36hr relative to hCG (Fig. 6D). Expression of PTGFR was decreased by hCG + MPTmix treatment at 6 (100 μg/ml), 24 (100 and 500 μg/ml), and 36hr (500 μg/ml) time points, although 12hr expression was increased (500 μg/ml) when compared to hCG controls (Fig. 6E).

Effect of MPTmix on P4 steroidogenesis and PGR

Previous studies from our group using the same IVF granulosa cell model have shown that PGR signaling is required for the hCG-induced increases in PGE2 and PGF2α, certain PG synthases/AKRs (PTGS2, PTGES, AKR1C1), and transporters (ABCC4, SLCO2A1) (Choi, 2017). Across various species, increases in P4 and PGR following LH/hCG stimulation are well-established, and disruption or loss of P4/PGR signaling during the ovulatory period results in anovulation (Choi et al., 2017; Robker et al., 2000; Loutradis et al., 1991; Espey et al., 1990). To investigate whether phthalates disrupt P4 steroidogenesis and PGR signaling—and thereby potentially impair downstream ovulatory PG production/function—we measured P4 levels and expression of PGR and P4 steroidogenic genes (STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD3B2) across the ovulatory period following hCG + MPTmix treatment. As expected, hCG treatment increased P4 levels (all time points) and PGR expression (6, 12, and 36hr) compared to DMSO (Fig. 7A and B). Expression of STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD3B2 was also upregulated by hCG at all time points (Fig. 7C–E). In contrast, hCG + MPTmix reduced P4 levels at 6 (100 and 500 μg/ml), 12 (100 and 500 μg/ml), 24 (500 μg/ml), and 36hr (500 μg/ml) relative to hCG, though a single MPTmix dose (10 μg/ml) increased 36hr levels (Fig. 7A). The mRNA levels of PGR were consistently decreased at all time points with the 500 μg/ml MPTmix dose compared to hCG (Fig. 7B). For steroidogenic proteins and enzymes, hCG + MPTmix 500 μg/ml treatment consistently suppressed STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD3B2 expression at 6hr. At this same time point, CYP11A1 expression was also reduced with a lower MPTmix dose (100 μg/ml), whereas STAR (1 μg/ml) and HSD3B2 (10 and 100 μg/ml) expression was increased relative to hCG (Fig. 7C–E). At 12hr, STAR (1, 10, and 500 μg/ml) and HSD3B2 (100 and 500 μg/ml) expression was reduced with hCG + MPTmix treatment relative to hCG (Fig. 7C–E). At 24hr, hCG + MPTmix treatment decreased STAR (1 and 500 μg/ml), CYP11A1 (1, 10, and 500 μg/ml), and HSD3B2 (500 μg/ml) expression compared to hCG (Fig. 7C–E). At 36hr, hCG + MPTmix treatment reduced expression of STAR (500 μg/ml), CYP11A1 (100 and 500 μg/ml), and HSD3B2 (500 μg/ml), although STAR expression was increased with a single MPTmix dose (100 μg/ml) compared to hCG control groups. (Fig. 7C–E).

Effect of MPTmix on LH/hCG-activated intracellular signaling cascades

Increased PG levels during ovulation are facilitated via complex signaling cascades. Activation of LHCGR promptly elevates cAMP and activates PKA signaling, leading to increased PGR levels. As noted in Section 1, PGR signaling directly regulates the induction of PG synthases, PG transporters, and PG levels in human granulosa cells. LH/hCG stimulation rapidly activates additional intracellular signaling pathways, including AKT/PKB and ERK1/2, with ERK1/2 proposed to drive PG production through direct induction of PTGS2 transcription (Tsai et al., 2008). To determine whether MPTmix exposure disrupts these essential signaling cascades, we measured cAMP, PKA activity, p-AKT, and p-ERK1/2 levels shortly after treatment with hCG + MPTmix (0.5hr). As anticipated, hCG treatment rapidly increased cAMP, PKA activity, p-AKT, and p-ERK1/2 levels compared to DMSO (Fig. 8A–D). In contrast, hCG + MPTmix 500 μg/ml reduced cAMP, PKA activity, and p-ERK1/2 levels relative to hCG controls (Fig. 8A–D).

Discussion

This study provides the first evidence that exposure to an environmentally relevant phthalate mixture, formulated from urinary concentrations in women and representative of in vivo ovarian exposure, disrupts ovulatory PG function through multiple mechanisms. Fig. 9 summarizes these main findings and mechanisms. Specifically, MPTmix exposure reduces active PG levels (PGE2 and PGF2α) and impairs PG function by altering their synthesis, metabolism, transport, and receptor availability. We propose that these effects are mediated through upstream suppression of P4 production and PGR signaling, with higher MPTmix doses also disrupting intracellular signaling cascades that are promptly activated following LHCGR binding. Importantly, cytotoxicity is unlikely to account for the observed effects, as MTS assays assessing cell viability and metabolism revealed no statistically significant differences across all treatment groups prior to hCG stimulation, indicating no detectable differences in the number or viability of these primary human granulosa cells in response to MPTmix exposure. Therefore, any MPTmix-induced changes observed following hCG treatment are unlikely due to cell death and instead reflect disruptions downstream of LHCGR activation. Considering the indispensable roles of PGs and P4/PGR in oocyte release and subsequent CL formation, our findings highlight the potential for phthalate exposure to disrupt ovulation and adversely impact fertility in women (Duffy et al., 2019; 2015; Killick and Elstein, 1987; Pall et al., 2001; Bata et al., 2006; Hester et al., 2010; McCann et al., 2013; Duffy and Stouffer, 2002; Lim et al., 1997; Kim and Duffy, 2016).

At the level of PG synthesis, MPTmix exposure consistently decreased active PGE2 and PGF2α levels that coincided with reduced expression and activity of PTGS2, the rate-limiting PG synthase that has proven requisite for ovulation and fertility (Duffy, 2015; Hester et al., 2010; Tilley et al., 1999). Although increased expression of PLA2G4A potentially reflects a compensatory attempt to liberate more arachidonic acid available for conversion into active PGs, this response appeared ineffective. The ineffectiveness likely stems from MPTmix-induced suppression of the downstream key PG synthase (PTGS2) and the PGE2-specific synthase (PTGES), both of which are abundantly upregulated by LH/hCG stimulation (Hannon et al., 2025). MPTmix exposure also affected additional PG-related synthases, including PTGS1 and the PGE2-specific synthases PTGES2 and PTGES3. Specifically, PTGS1 expression was further suppressed by MPTmix relative to hCG controls, whereas PTGES2 and PTGES3 were generally upregulated, potentially reflecting compensatory increases in response to PTGES suppression. Together, these findings demonstrate that phthalate exposure broadly disrupts the production of ovulatory PGs. The resulting reduction in PGE2 levels at each tested dose of the MPTmix is particularly concerning given the essential roles of PGE2 in facilitating successful oocyte release. Specifically, well-timed treatment with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (PTGS2 inhibitors) block ovulation in women, and PGE2 receptor knockout mouse models exhibit sterility due to anovulation (Duffy et al., 2019; Smith et al., 1996).

Metabolic assessments revealed additional disruption. Inactive PGE2 metabolite levels were also decreased by MPTmix exposure, despite increased expression and activity of the metabolic enzyme HPGD. This is most likely explained by diminished levels of active PGE2 available for metabolism. Conversely, inactive PGF2α metabolite levels were elevated, suggesting a phthalate-driven metabolic shift away from PGE2 and toward PGF2α production and terminal metabolism. This catabolic/metabolic shift is reinforced by MPTmix-induced upregulation of AKR1C1 and AKR1C3, members of the aldo–keto reductase (AKR) enzyme family that either interconvert PGE2 and PGF2α or directly synthesize PGF2α from the short-lived PGH2 intermediate. Notably, a study using human granulosa cells reported that AKR1C1 exhibits unidirectional isomerase activity and exclusively converts PGE2 to PGF2α, which further highlights the enhanced catabolic influence of the MPTmix (Dozier et al., 2008). This redirection of PG metabolism is concerning as PGE2 is indispensable for ovulation and fertility, whereas the physiological role of PGF2α during oocyte release is unclear, and mice lacking PGF2α receptor expression ovulate normally (Duffy et al., 2019; Sugimoto et al., 1997).

Phthalates may also disrupt PG transport. At select doses, MPTmix exposure decreased expression of ABCC4, an efflux transporter that actively pumps PGs out of cells (Yerushalmi et al., 2023), while increasing expression of SLCO2A1, a transporter that primarily mediates PG uptake into cells (Xia et al., 2025). These changes, when considered alongside MPTmix-induced disruptions in PG production and the resulting reduction in active PG levels, may indicate a cellular mechanism aimed at sustaining intracellular PG signaling by limiting PG efflux and conserving newly synthesized PGs, albeit at a diminished capacity evident by decreased PGE2 synthesis detailed above.

At the receptor level, MPTmix exposure altered expression of multiple PGE2 receptors (PTGER1–4), which regulate distinct ovulatory processes depending on their location within the follicle (Duffy et al., 2019; Kim and Duffy, 2016; Harris et al., 2011). Under normal ovulatory conditions, PTGER1 signaling in mural granulosa cells stabilizes the follicle wall to prevent premature rupture, while PTGER2 and PTGER4 signaling at the follicle apex promotes protease expression required for COC expansion and follicle rupture (Kim and Duffy, 2016). PTGER3 provides additional regulation depending on receptor isoform. In mural granulosa cells away from the apex, PTGER3 (isoform 5) increases protease inhibitors to strengthen the follicle wall; whereas PTGER3 (isoform 5) expression is low at the apex, allowing PTGER2 and PTGER4-mediated protease activity to dominate and enable rupture (Kim and Duffy, 2016; Kim et al., 2013). Exposure to the MPTmix disrupted this balance. PTGER1 levels were consistently increased even at late ovulatory time points, which could lead to excessive follicle wall stabilization and the entrapment of oocytes—a hallmark of luteinized unruptured follicle syndrome (LUFS). Simultaneously, PTGER2 and PTGER4 levels were decreased, potentially suggesting impaired protease-driven follicle wall breakdown at the apex and a reinforcement of wall integrity where rupture should occur. In contrast, similar MPTmix doses and time points that increased PTGER1 expression decreased mRNA levels of PTGER3 isoform 5 (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these receptor-level alterations suggest that phthalate exposure disrupts the coordinated signaling required for successful follicle rupture and provides a mechanistic link to LUFS-like phenotypes (Killick and Elstein, 1987; Niringiyumukiza et al., 2018). While epidemiological associations between phthalate exposure and LUFS have not yet been investigated in the literature, various studies have reported associations between elevated phthalate levels and female reproductive disorders that involve impaired follicle rupture, including anovulation, PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome), and POI (premature ovarian insufficiency) (Land et al., 2025; 2022; Yao et al., 2023; Tzouma et al., 2025; Hlisníková et al., 2020).

Finally, upstream regulators of PG synthesis were markedly affected by phthalates. MPTmix treatment generally decreased P4 levels (with the exception of one dose at a single time point), and the highest MPTmix dose (500 μg/ml) consistently suppressed PGR expression across the ovulatory period. These effects were accompanied by alterations in key proteins and enzymes required for P4 steroidogenesis. Notably, the 500 μg/ml MPTmix dose also reduced the levels of cAMP, PKA activity, and p-ERK1/2, suggesting that initial LHCGR signaling may be impaired only with the highest MPTmix dose. In contrast, lower MPTmix concentrations did not appear to disrupt initial LHCGR signaling events, indicating that distinct molecular mechanisms may underline the observed effects across doses, and these warrant further investigation. One plausible mechanism involves phthalate-mediated modulation of nuclear receptor signaling. Phthalates and their metabolites have been reported to interact with multiple nuclear receptors, including estrogen and androgen receptors, as well as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) subtypes, potentially influencing transcriptional networks that regulate steroidogenesis and reproductive function in endocrine tissues (Hlisníková et al., 2020; Begum and Carpenter, 2022; Craig et al., 2011). Activation or antagonism of these receptors could contribute to reduced P4 biosynthesis and altered expression of enzymes and receptors within the PG synthesis and signaling pathways. However, direct ligand-receptor interactions in the human ovary remain uncharacterized, and available evidence suggests that such effects on estrogen receptors, androgen receptors, and PPARs are likely receptor- and context-dependent (Begum and Carpenter, 2022; Craig et al., 2011). In addition to transcriptional regulation, phthalates have been shown to disrupt intracellular signaling pathways independent of classical nuclear receptor mechanisms (Hlisníková et al., 2020; Brassea-Pérez et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2012a;b; Fletcher et al., 2022). Consistent with previous findings demonstrating that parent phthalates impair PGR signaling through the cAMP-PKA pathway (Choi et al., 2017; Hannon et al., 2023), our findings from the present study extend this mechanism to include disruption of ERK1/2 signaling by phthalate metabolites. Given that both cAMP-PKA-PGR and EGFR-ERK1/2 pathways regulate transcription of key PG synthases such as PTGS2 (Choi et al., 2017; Tsai et al., 2008), these impairments likely compound the observed deficits in PG synthesis and function following exposure to phthalates. To further define the contribution of EGFR-dependent pathways, future studies in our laboratory will assess EGFR and its EGF-like peptide ligands to determine how MPTmix exposure alters additional ovulatory signaling mechanisms. These future studies will utilize the cumulus cells that undergo expansion in response to EGFR signaling and are also collected during the IVF retrieval process. In addition to impaired intracellular signaling, phthalate-induced ovulatory dysfunction may also arise indirectly through mechanisms such as oxidative stress or mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which have been implicated in phthalate-induced toxicity of reproductive tissues (Hannon and Flaws, 2015; Brassea-Pérez et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2012a; b; Fletcher et al., 2022). Ultimately, the observed decreases in P4 and PGR are particularly concerning given the indispensable role of P4/PGR signaling in ovulation, with PGR deficiency leading to ovulatory failure, LUFS, and infertility across species (Duffy et al., 2019; Lydon et al., 1996; Lydon et al., 1995; Richards and Ascoli, 2018). Collectively, these findings support a multifaceted model in which phthalates disrupt ovulatory function through coordinated effects on intracellular signaling, steroidogenesis, and PG pathways.

Alongside the observed decreases in PG synthesis (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGES) and increases in catabolism/metabolism (AKR1C1, AKR1C3, HPGD), additional MPTmix-induced alterations were identified that conflict with the typical temporal gene expression profile during the ovulatory period. In this study, hCG treatment generally upregulated PTGS2, PTGES, AKR1C1, ABCC4, SLCO2A1, PTGER2, PTGER3, PTGFR, PGR, STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD3B2 expression, while downregulating PLA2G4A, PTGS1, PTGES2, HPGD, AKR1C3, PTGER1, and PTGER4 expression. These patterns are consistent with the well-documented temporal regulation of PG and P4/PGR production and action during ovulation (Choi et al., 2017; Duffy, 2015; Robker et al., 2000; Loutradis et al., 1991; Espey et al., 1990; Kim and Duffy, 2016). However, treatment with hCG + MPTmix broadly suppressed upregulation of hCG-induced genes. Notable exceptions included AKR1C1 and SLCO2A1, where MPTmix further increased these hCG-stimulated genes. This aligns with a shift toward PGF2α production and a potential compensatory attempt to increase intracellular PG levels following phthalate exposure. Conversely, for genes typically downregulated by hCG, MPTmix exposure produced expression patterns more similar to DMSO vehicle controls than hCG-treated cells, although this pattern was less consistent than the suppression observed among hCG-induced genes. Overall, these changes indicate a disruption of the normal ovulatory environment by favoring production of PGF2α over PGE2 (via increased AKR1C3), promoting excessive PG inactivation (via increased HPGD mRNA and activity), and enhancing follicle wall stabilization (via increased PTGER1 and decreased PTGER2/4). Furthermore, the molecular profile induced by phthalate exposure closely parallels the non-ovulatory (DMSO-vehicle) phenotype. This disruption extends beyond gene expression to protein activity, as seen with the key PG synthase (PTGS2) and metabolic enzyme (HPGD). In both cases, the expected hCG-induced increase in PTGS2 and decrease in HPGD were negated by MPTmix exposure, producing transcript and protein activity levels similar to those of the non-ovulatory, DMSO vehicle control groups.

The present findings reveal that exposure to a mixture of phthalate metabolites, designed to mimic human ovarian exposure, alters ovulatory PG production and PG-related pathways through changes in synthesis, metabolism, transport, and receptor expression. These effects occur alongside disrupted P4/PGR signaling and, at the highest MPTmix dose, impairment of upstream intracellular signaling pathways. Although the overall pattern of responses in this study was largely directionally consistent across doses, some non-monotonic dose responses were observed depending on the endpoint and time point measured (Figs. 3A, 3B, 3D, 3E, 4B, 4E, 5B, 6A, 6B, 6E, 7A, 7C, and 7E). Non-monotonic dose–response relationships are a well-recognized feature of endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) and have been attributed to several molecular mechanisms, including opposing actions mediated by multiple receptors with differing affinities, receptor desensitization, engagement of negative feedback mechanisms at higher doses, and dose-dependent modulation of chemical metabolism (Lagarde et al., 2015; Vandenberg et al., 2012). In the context of the present study, the directionally coherent responses and the non-monotonic responses suggest that disruption of ovulatory pathways occurs across a range of phthalate exposures. Taken together, these findings identify potential mechanisms through which environmentally relevant phthalate exposure may impact ovulatory function and fertility in women. While this study identifies phthalate-associated alterations in PG production and signaling under hCG-stimulated conditions, future studies employing pharmacologic or genetic manipulation of these pathways will be essential to elucidate causal mechanisms of toxicity.

Major strengths of this study include the use of primary human ovarian granulosa cells with a mixture-based exposure paradigm composed of bioactive phthalate metabolites, designed to more closely reflect true human ovarian exposure. Whereas many prior studies rely on rodent models or parent phthalate compounds, our primary human granulosa cell model is innovative in its ability to recapitulate key ovulatory signaling and PG-dependent outcomes observed in vivo (Choi et al., 2017; Hannon et al., 2025; 2023; Al-Alem et al., 2015). Moreover, the use of a phthalate metabolite-containing mixture derived from urinary concentrations measured in women enhances both environmental and translational relevance, given that the parent phthalate compounds ubiquitous in consumer products are rapidly metabolized following absorption. Importantly, this metabolite-based exposure paradigm addresses a central limitation of previous toxicological studies, including our earlier work, by modeling the chemical forms most relevant to human ovarian exposure. Specifically, it is the bioactive metabolites, not the parent compounds, that are measured in women’s ovarian follicular fluid, and the 1 μg/ml dose used in the present study contains levels of individual phthalate metabolites that are within the range measured in women’s ovaries (Du et al., 2019; 2016). Under these more relevant exposure conditions, this study introduces new mechanistic insight, implicating additional LH/hCG signaling cascades and altered PG enzymatic protein activity, alongside disruptions in cAMP-PKA pathways, P4 steroidogenesis, PGR signaling, and ovulatory PG production and function in human granulosa cells. These findings are the first to demonstrate that a mixture of bioactive phthalate metabolites act directly on human ovarian cells to impair essential ovulatory signaling networks. By employing an environmentally and biologically relevant metabolite mixture, this study overcomes limitations inherent to exposures involving individual or parent phthalate compounds and advances the mechanistic framework for understanding phthalate-induced ovulatory dysfunction in women. Furthermore, by identifying previously uncharacterized alterations in LHCGR signaling and PG-related enzyme activity, these results expand our understanding of how relevant phthalate exposures disrupt multiple biochemical pathways critical for ovulation. Together, this work establishes a physiologically relevant paradigm for evaluating ovarian toxicity and advances mechanistic understanding of how environmental exposure to phthalates may contribute to impaired ovulatory function and reduced fertility in women.

A limitation of the present study includes the inability to isolate specific mural granulosa cell subpopulations from follicular aspirates (apex vs non-apex regions), which restricts our ability to fully characterize the effect of phthalates on PG receptors. As mentioned above, the PG receptors function in a spatially dependent manner within the follicle to stabilize or promote rupture at the apex during ovulation. Although the use of primary human granulosa cells represents a major strength, our in vitro culture system focuses on a single ovarian cell type and does not capture the contribution of other components of the ovulatory follicle, including cumulus cells, theca cells, the ovarian stroma, or the neuroendocrine axis—all of which may respond to phthalate exposure in an intact human. Accordingly, this model cannot directly assess the systemic effects of phthalates. However, due to ethical constraints, experimental studies evaluating systemic phthalate exposure in humans are, understandably, limited to epidemiological investigations. Importantly, these epidemiological studies have consistently linked elevated phthalate exposure with adverse ovarian- and ovulation-related outcomes, including decreased pregnancy rates, reduced fecundity, poorer IVF outcomes (fewer oocytes retrieved and lower live birth rates), altered sex steroid hormone levels, and disrupted menstrual cycle length (Land et al., 2025; 2022). Such associations underscore the critical need for direct mechanistic studies, and the present findings offer toxicological evidence and potential cellular mechanisms by which phthalates may contribute to the ovarian and ovulatory dysfunction reported in human populations. Another potential limitation relates to the source of granulosa cells, which were collected from 62 patients undergoing IVF. Of these patients, 52% had no documented infertility diagnosis specific to ovarian function and 44% had no reported infertility diagnosis at all (Supplementary Table 1). We are actively expanding this cohort to evaluate whether non-reproductive pathologies or infertility diagnoses are associated with adverse outcomes in this model. Notably, we did not observe major differences in the hCG response among patient samples in the current dataset, regardless of fertility status or potential pathologies linked to altered PG and P4/PGR-mediated signaling. In parallel, phthalate concentrations are being quantified in follicular fluid aspirates collected at oocyte retrieval to determine whether elevated exposure correlates with infertility diagnoses and/or poor IVF outcomes. These analyses will further allow us to test whether baseline phthalate exposure reduces granulosa cell responsiveness to hCG and/or predisposes cells to heightened phthalate toxicity in our in vitro models. It is also important to note that urinary phthalate metabolite levels do not consistently or robustly correlate with follicular fluid concentrations (Du et al., 2016), suggesting that urinary measurements may only partially capture ovarian-relevant exposures. To address this limitation, future studies will also leverage follicular fluid phthalate measurements to guide both exposure concentrations and potentially metabolite mixture composition in our experimental models to better approximate in vivo follicular conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the effects of an environmentally relevant mixture of phthalate metabolites, derived from urinary levels in women, on P4/PGR signaling and downstream PG production and function in primary human granulosa cells. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that phthalate exposure disrupts intracellular signaling pathways involved in P4 steroidogenesis and PGR signaling, and also impairs PG production. Moreover, phthalates altered PG metabolic enzymes, transporters, and receptors, further compromising PG function. P4/PGR and PGs mediate ovulatory processes such as follicle wall breakdown, tissue remodeling, COC expansion and stability, oocyte maturation, and angiogenesis (Duffy et al., 2019), and phthalate mixture-induced defects in these specific ovulatory events are being explored in our ongoing studies using human and mouse models. Collectively, these results advance mechanistic understanding of how common, unavoidable environmental exposures impair women’s reproductive health, which can potentially inform more effective infertility treatments and improve the overall approach to women’s healthcare.
