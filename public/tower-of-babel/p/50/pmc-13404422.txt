Prospective cohort study identifies barriers and facilitators to point-of-care ultrasound use in an academic emergency department
Authors: Alyssa Platt, Timothy Peterson, Erica Peethumnongsin, Maragatha Kuchibhatla, Rebecca G Theophanous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoint-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) improves patient care by expedited diagnosis and safer procedures. Despite POCUS benefits, some clinicians, including emergency physicians, do not readily use POCUS.ObjectivesThe study objective identified barriers and facilitators to clinical POCUS use and performed an intervention to address these barriers.MethodsA prospective cohort study at a single academic hospital included emergency department attendings, residents and advanced practice providers (APPs). Participants were surveyed on perceived POCUS use barriers and facilitators (primary outcome). A multifaceted intervention from December 2023 to January 2024 addressed identified barriers and involved: in-person POCUS education during shift by ultrasound faculty, clinical POCUS workflow demonstration during resident conference/faculty meetings and QR code reference files on machines.Results42/99 participants (42.4%) responded to surveys preintervention and 28 postintervention (28.3%). 56 physicians and APPs participated in the in-person POCUS intervention (17 attendings, 34 residents, 5 APPs). Perceived POCUS barriers were time constraints on shift; internet/connectivity problems and losing saved images; forgetting to finish exam worksheets online; images not uploading into Butterfly cloud by the end of shift and residents performing ‘phantom scans’. Perceived POCUS facilitators included clear documentation protocols, hands-on teaching sessions and incentives for completing scans. Comfort in teaching diagnostic and procedural POCUS improved pre to postintervention but did not change for performing POCUS.ConclusionIdentified barriers and facilitators were incorporated into a multifaceted intervention to improve clinical POCUS workflow processes. Future individualised interventions for low POCUS users and institutional initiatives with POCUS champions can be studied for improved patient care.

Introduction

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a valuable diagnostic bedside tool for clinical patient care.1 2 Emergency ultrasound advances clinical diagnostics at the bedside, allows for repeat assessments during resuscitation of acutely ill patients and guides high-risk procedures and treatment for improved patient safety.3 4 POCUS expedites patient care workflows by reducing emergency department (ED) radiology study orders and transport times, thereby reducing patient disposition times.1 5 This yields cost efficiencies and improved ED and hospital throughput. Performing POCUS also improves patient-centred outcomes and satisfaction, through increased time at the bedside and direct patient interaction.1 5

The American College of Emergency Physicians and American College of Graduate Medical Education guidelines define the knowledge and performance of 12 core POCUS diagnostic exams and ultrasound-guided procedures as a critical aspect of emergency physician training.3 4 Despite increasing POCUS utilisation at medical centres nationwide, there persists a significant gap between POCUS training and actual clinical use, including in emergency medicine (EM).6–9 Per studies, only 50% of emergency physicians perform POCUS daily, with 30% of physicians using POCUS in community EDs in California, despite adequate ultrasound equipment.7–11 Studies in the Veterans Affairs (VA) healthcare system and rural EDs have cited multiple factors contributing to the gap in POCUS use, including lack of POCUS training (84% of sites in 2020), lack of a systemwide POCUS documentation and archiving programme (35%), lack of hospital resources or funding (28% lack funding for support staff and 50% lack funding for training) and for some sites a lack of equipment (29%).7 8 12–17 Additional studies have investigated whether creation of an institution-specific POCUS programme can increase POCUS utilisation in EDs or inpatient services, although generalisability of the model and sustainability are difficult to achieve.18–22 These studies report teaching bottlenecks with limited ultrasound faculty time, chaotic ED workflows with time constraints while on shift, a need for widespread system and practice integration, and skill degradation over time as barriers to POCUS programme sustainability.18–22

Objective

Our aim for this study was to identify barriers and facilitators to POCUS use by physicians (EM attendings and residents) and advanced practice providers (APPs) in a single academic hospital. We sought to better understand the problems with our clinical ED POCUS system to help overcome these barriers for POCUS use, documentation, archiving and billing processes. By performing this study, our goal was to increase clinical POCUS use in the department and disseminate the data collected as a model for other EDs or hospital systems.

Methods

Study design

We performed a prospective cohort study from July 2023 to June 2024 to identify barriers and facilitators to clinical POCUS use by EM attending physicians, EM resident physicians and APPs working at a single academic hospital ED during the study period. All APPs working at our hospital during the study period were physician assistants. We also conducted a retrospective analysis of electronic health data extracted from ED POCUS Butterfly units, the results of which are described in a separate paper.23 The study was performed according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (online supplemental file 1).24

Study setting

The study was conducted at a single tertiary care, academic, level 1 trauma centre with 1048 inpatient hospital beds, almost 85 000 annual ED visits and 29.8% admissions (percent of ED patients that were admitted to the hospital). The ED has seven diagnostic ultrasound machines, which include two General Electric Healthcare Venue cart-based systems, three Sonosite M-Turbos and two Sonosite Edges. The ED also has three vascular Sonosite Nanomaxx machines for procedural use and four Butterfly IQ handheld ultrasounds only used for educational purposes. Ultrasound machines connect to the hospital 5G Wi-Fi with fast network speeds. The clinical engineering department performs machine maintenance with support from ultrasound company representatives. Clinicians input electronic repair requests when a machine malfunctions. The study site has one Emergency Ultrasound Director and six emergency ultrasound fellowship-trained faculty. The EM programme has 12 residents per academic year and 1–2 emergency ultrasound fellows. Epic Maestro Care is the electronic health record, and Butterfly Network cloud is the POCUS manager for image storage and archival. Quality assurance image review is performed by ultrasound faculty on a weekly basis for all clinical POCUS scans performed and archived.

Selection of participants

All clinicians working in the ED during the study period were invited to participate. The target study population included 48 attending physicians, 19 APPs and 36 residents (PGY1-3). Four attendings were ineligible for the study due to being on the study team. Thus, 99 ED clinicians were eligible. All ED physicians, residents and APPs were invited to participate in both the surveys and training sessions with participation advertised via email, at weekly EM resident conference and at monthly EM faculty meetings. ED nurses, technicians and residents working in the ED from other medical services were excluded as this study focused on physicians and APPs trained in ED POCUS use.

Data collection

All ED attendings, residents and APPs were emailed a secured REDCap survey (with a website link and QR code) pre- and post-intervention to assess their understanding of the clinical ED POCUS workflow and their opinions on barriers or potential facilitators for the process (online supplemental files 2 and 3). The pre and postsurvey questions overlapped on demographics, questions regarding comfort in performing and teaching POCUS, and how often participants used POCUS clinically. The post-survey included additional questions to assess the training intervention impact and an 11-question multiple choice knowledge assessment. The surveys were created by ultrasound content experts and based on validated surveys from other POCUS studies.18 25

Based on the preintervention survey results, we modified an educational POCUS workflow intervention on best practice, systematic clinical ultrasound performance, documentation and archival workflow practice (online supplemental files 4–6).23 ED ultrasound faculty then re-educated participants on the clinical POCUS workflow via an intervention. First, we created an online presentation and reference document outlining the standardised POCUS image acquisition and archival process (online supplemental files 4–6). This was emailed to providers, uploaded to the secured ED clinical folder, and accessible via new QR codes attached to the ED ultrasound machines. Second, ED ultrasound faculty demonstrated the clinical ED POCUS process in-person during ED shift changes over a 4-week period for EM physicians and APPs. Finally, we reviewed monthly ED clinical POCUS billing data and collected data on the number and type of POCUS scans done in the department to assess our intervention’s impact, the results of which are described in a separate paper. We invited ED attendings, residents and APPs to participate in a 6-month postintervention survey that included an 11-question multiple-choice knowledge assessment for POCUS intervention feedback and to assess workflow process improvements (online supplemental file 4). Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the identified barriers and perceived facilitators to POCUS use at our site. Secondary outcomes included participant self-perceived POCUS use and comfort in performing and teaching ultrasound scans.

Data sources

We used Epic Maestro Care for patient chart review and Butterfly Network cloud software to review POCUS scans done in the ED. Surveys built using the institution’s secured REDCap server were emailed to all ED providers’ preintervention in November 2023 and 6 months postintervention in June–August 2024, with reminders sent weekly for 2 weeks. ED clinical POCUS billing data were retrospectively obtained from administrative and support staff for review after 6-month postintervention, for a total period of 18 months preintervention clinical data and 12 months postintervention data, the results of which are described in a separate paper.

Bias

The survey questionnaire was written by the study principal investigator(PI) and edited by the ED ultrasound faculty group prior to distribution. This was to ensure that the survey would be institution-specific and to reduce bias with review by ultrasound experts who were not study team members. The survey was modelled after ultrasound surveys used and tested in other studies.16–18 24 The survey was pilot tested with an ED physician at another local institution with research training who was not eligible for the study.

Participants were enrolled with implied consent after reading the study information sheet with the REDCap survey link emailed by the study PI. All participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time from the study. All survey data were secured in an institutional database per standard research protocols, and only study personnel had access to data. Participant names and dates were necessary to link pre/postintervention survey data to assess for improvement after the POCUS intervention, and for analysis of responses to understand barriers, for scientific integrity and to maximise process improvements. Survey data were deidentified by the study PI and coded with a key for biostatistician review and analysis. POCUS studies are saved within the institution’s approved and secured Butterfly cloud system and written reports in Epic Maestro Care as done in standard clinical care. Only deidentified data were presented to the ED group for feedback and process improvements.

We used the Kirkpatrick framework’s second and third levels of learning to evaluate our POCUS intervention, assessing participants’ reaction to the POCUS intervention through REDCAP survey questionnaires.26 We evaluated behaviour through self-perceived comfort in performing and teaching POCUS and via in-person observed demonstrations of faculty completing a clinical POCUS scan during an on-shift teaching.26

Statistical methods

Patient characteristics and descriptive outcomes were summarised using means (with SD) and medians (within IQR) for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Survey data collected both preintervention and postintervention were summarised by time period. All secondary outcomes were adjusted for age (years) and training level (attending, resident or APP) of survey respondents, with adjusted models considered primary.

Secondary outcomes of preintervention versus postintervention comfort with POCUS use were analysed on a 5-point scale from −2 (very uncomfortable) to 2 (very comfortable) using linear regression analysis with cluster robust SEs to account for repeated measures within person.27 Given the open cohort nature of the data collection, regression analysis allowed us to use all available data, regardless of whether survey respondents participated in one or both surveys. We used Q-Q plots to confirm normality of residuals, an assumption of the linear regression model.28 The secondary outcome of frequency of POCUS use took an ordered categorical form; however, sparsity in lower frequency responses necessitated collapsing of categories into a binary response of often (>1 scan per week) versus all others. For stability of estimation, we used modified Poisson regressions, which allow estimation of risk ratios (RR) (less prone to misinterpretation than ORs) while avoiding issues of model convergence that are common for standard binomial models with log and identity links.29 All secondary outcomes were adjusted for age (years) and training level (attending, resident or APP) of survey respondents, with adjusted models considered primary.

Missing data from blank responses on questionnaires are reported in results tables. Characteristics of respondents were compared by survey response pattern (ie, responded to both pre and post vs responding to one or the other) to add context to pre/post results and potential impact of missing data via non-response. P values were calculated using an alpha of 0.05 for statistical significance. With only one primary outcome, no adjustments were made for multiple testing. All analyses were performed using Stata V.19 Software.

Sample size: as this was a pilot descriptive study identifying barriers and perceived facilitators to POCUS use, sample size and power are not reported.

Results

Characteristics of the study subjects

In the 18-month preintervention to 12-month postintervention period from 1 July 2022 to 30 December 2024, a total of 81 649 patients were seen in the ED and 9968 POCUS were performed. Of 103 total ED providers, 4 attendings were ineligible for the survey study due to being on the study team. A total of 42 eligible providers (42.4%) responded to the questionnaire prior to the intervention, including 26 EM attendings (59.1% of eligible), 14 EM residents (38.8% of eligible), 1 APP (5.3% of eligible) and 1 unidentified user (figure 2). The 4 EM attendings acting as study researchers did not complete REDCap surveys to prevent bias and are not included. The postintervention survey had 28 responses, with six respondents inadvertently answering the survey two times (in these cases, only the first response was included in analysis) for a total of 22 analyzable responses (22.2% of eligible) to the questionnaire postintervention. Across both preintervention and postintervention surveys, a total of 53 individuals responded to at least one survey, with 42 responding to one survey only and 11 responding to both. Comparing characteristics between those responding to one survey versus both, more male providers, fellowship trained providers, and providers with higher numbers of POCUS performed during residency answered both surveys compared with those only responding to one survey. Attendance at the in-person training was 60 total (17 attendings, 35 residents, 6 APPs) among 54 individual participants and 17 sessions, with attendance ranging from 1 to 12 individuals per session (online supplemental file 7).

Eleven participants (26.8%) reported fellowship training. Barriers to POCUS use

Barriers to POCUS use can be categorised into four dimensions: tools and technology, tasks and progress, people and environment/equipment and are displayed in figure 3A.

Preintervention survey responses for (A) barriers, (B) facilitators and (C) usefulness and importance of POCUS. APPs, advance practice providers; ED, emergency department; POCUS, point-of-care ultrasound. US-IV: ultrasound-guided intravenous procedure. RTLS: Real-Time Location Systems.

The most common perceived POCUS barriers were time constraints on shift (n=29, 69.1% respondents reported moderate/significant barrier), forgetting to complete the Butterfly cloud worksheet after scan completion (n=23, 57.5%) and internet or connectivity problems and losing saved images (n=25, 59.5% respondents), with the highest number of respondents reported that images not uploaded in Butterfly cloud by end of shift as a significant barrier (n=17, 65.8%). Additional identified barriers included: difficulty in locating the ultrasound machines (n=20, 46.6% respondents); not knowing how to use the machine tracker system (n=18, 41.8% respondents); residents are performing ‘phantom scans’ and not saving their images (n=19, 46.3% respondents); and problems with the machine malfunctioning (n=21, 48.9% respondents). Fewer respondents felt that residents or APPs not completing worksheets in Butterfly was a barrier (n=15, 36.6% respondents) and that faculty were not signing the completed studies (14% or 34.2% respondents). The most common items to be reported ‘Not a barrier’ included: hospital credentialing not including all necessary specific POCUS exam types (n=24, 70.6%), difficulties with using Butterfly cloud software (n=23, 54.8%), and not knowing how to save images on the ultrasound machine (n=22, 52.5%) (figure 3A).

Facilitators of POCUS use

Perceived POCUS facilitators were clear documentation protocols (n=29, 69.0%), hands-on teaching sessions (n=23, 54.8%) and incentives for completing scans (n=23, 54.8%) (figure 3B). Few participants thought that additional online training and penalties for not completing a certain number of POCUS scans would also be facilitators (both had <10 respondents). Most respondents felt that POCUS was a useful medical tool (n=41, 95.2% agree/strongly agree) and that it is important for residents and EM attendings to learn POCUS (n=42, 100% agree/strongly agree) (figure 3C).

Comfort and self-perceived frequency of POCUS use

Most participants felt comfortable or very comfortable with diagnostic POCUS both preintervention (76.2% performing, 71.5% teaching) and postintervention (90.9% performing, 81.8% teaching). Similarly, survey respondents felt comfortable or very comfortable with procedural POCUS both preintervention (88.1% performing, 83.4% teaching) and postintervention (90.9% performing and 95.5% teaching) (table 2). Evidence of an increase in comfort performing diagnostic and procedural scans was weak, however there was evidence that comfort in teaching diagnostic and procedural POCUS improved preintervention to postintervention (adjusted mean difference=0.35, 95% CI 0.01, 0.69; p value=0.046 for teaching of diagnostic POCUS, adjusted mean difference=0.38, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.73; p value 0.035 for teaching procedural guidance POCUS).

Discussion

Identifying clinical POCUS use barriers and facilitators is important to implement programme changes that are feasible, acceptable and sustainable. Our study found that POCUS use barriers were mixed, with the most significant barriers including time constraints on shift, forgetting to complete the worksheet on the Butterfly archiving system and internet connectivity problems with losing saved images. Perceived POCUS facilitators included clear documentation protocols, hands-on teaching sessions and incentives for completing scans (all with >20 (48%) respondents agreed or strongly agreed).

As a large academic medical and level 1 trauma centre, ED providers at our site felt more comfortable teaching diagnostic and procedural POCUS scans in the postintervention period compared with the preintervention period, citing a lack of POCUS knowledge in performing or interpreting exams as an insignificant barrier. It is not surprising that faculty employed at an academic medical centre, who are credentialed in ultrasound through residency training, do not perceive their POCUS knowledge as a barrier to use. In contrast, POCUS knowledge is still identified as a barrier at other community hospitals/EDs employing non-EM trained physicians or APPs, who typically lack POCUS training and are not as facile with its clinical use.6 9 13 14 17

Interestingly, many of the POCUS barriers identified in both EM attendings and residents are like those identified in a mixed-methods study we performed at a local VA hospital ED. At the VA, a separate EM attending group with mixed training backgrounds (emergency, internal and family medicine training) also identified time constraints on shift and a need for POCUS training as significant barriers.17 Creation of a standardised ED POCUS documentation and archiving system at the VA site, which is reported as a perceived POCUS facilitator in our study, increased VA ED clinical POCUS scans fourfold from 72 to 267 scans in 6 months.17 Some of these same POCUS barriers are also identified in the national VA healthcare system, as reported by Boyd and Remskar et al, with the biggest barriers being a lack of POCUS training (84% of sites in 2020), system infrastructure (65%) and equipment (29%).13 15 A national VA POCUS survey identified that only 61/115 VA sites were using POCUS in 2020, despite an increase in POCUS technology and utilisation nationwide over the past 30 years.12 Furthermore, many rural or community EDs still lack POCUS equipment and training, with only about 50% of emergency physicians performing POCUS daily and only 30% using POCUS in community EDs in California per other studies.6–11 Thus, expansion of this protocol to additional sites to identify and address POCUS barriers and facilitators is needed.

In our study, EM attendings and residents believed POCUS documentation was already standardised and straightforward. In contrast, Schnittke et al’s study in EM residents noted that documenting POCUS for medical decision-making was a significant barrier to performing POCUS clinically.18 Thus, they incorporated resident in-person education on the POCUS documentation and archiving system.18 They similarly identified a need for addressing time constraints, streamlining documentation and strengthening faculty support for resident-performed POCUS.18

Regarding perceived POCUS facilitators, hands-on teaching remains the mainstay as an effective training method for POCUS education.19 21 It employs the medical education framework of Ericsson’s Deliberate Practice, which is learning by doing and repeating a task until it becomes familiar and second nature.20 30 31 Learners cycle through a process of planning, concentration/dedication, repetition/revision and self-reflection, building knowledge from an existing foundation.30 31 With hands-on teaching, the learners actively participate by scanning standardised or clinical patients, under direct supervision from an ultrasound-trained faculty.21 Hayward et al built an EM POCUS curriculum in a Canadian hospital using Ericsson’s Deliberate Practice educational framework, increasing resident POCUS competency from 33% to 100% of PGY2 residents qualifying for the Canadian Emergency Ultrasound Society practitioner examination.20 The Deliberate Practice model yields excellent results but is very time-consuming for ultrasound faculty and difficult to feasibly achieve at sites that may lack additional POCUS training opportunities.20 30 Several VA studies also identified having POCUS champions and positive peer feedback as facilitators to increase clinical POCUS use because people felt more supported and confident in performing POCUS when others were involved or guiding them with the process.16 32

Studies have addressed the problem of time constraints on shift, with examples including finding the ultrasound machine at the beginning of the shift and maintaining it nearby, plugged in and ready to be used, plus bringing the machine into the patient’s room during initial assessment.19 20 22 33 Our institution has marked ultrasound machine locations within each section of the ED and a machine tracking system to save time with finding equipment in a large, busy ED. Also, residents can perform the POCUS scans independently then show the images to attendings afterwards to expedite care while maintaining patient safety standards. Incentives for performing POCUS were favoured as a facilitator, while some respondents recommended penalties for clinical POCUS non-performance as a quality metric standard. Other studies have proposed reward versus penalty systems for POCUS as well as a tiered credentialing system for hospitalists and EM physicians.33–35 Melton et al found an increase in POCUS studies being performed during a 6-month ‘reward’ or financial incentive period compared with baseline. This increase remained stable during a 6-month washout period and increased further during a ‘penalty’ or negative financial period.33 34 Similarly, Adhikari et al implemented a new ED POCUS billing protocol as a financial incentive. They achieved a 45% increase in ED faculty participation, increasing facility fees revenue sevenfold and professional fees revenue 6.34-fold postintervention, gaining net profits of approximately $350 000.35 Since our site was still in preliminary stages of reinforcing POCUS use, we tracked revenue data but did not implement a reward and penalty system per ED leadership preference.

Finally, middleware software such as Butterfly cloud for POCUS archiving is an essential part of ED and hospital workflow. Although this can create problems such as connectivity issues, added time to complete POCUS worksheets and required knowledge to operate another system, the utilisation of middleware has great benefits. These benefits include a storage domain for educational scans, a safety mechanism to prevent incomplete or poor images from entering the patient’s permanent medical chart and the ability to perform quality review and faculty credentialing. None of these tasks can be done with EHR or radiology software in a single, secure location. Thus, creation of a simple, standardised process to save time and properly document POCUS scans and interpretations is important for a successful POCUS programme, as noted in the literature and by our survey results.19 21 22 35 36

Limitations

The single centre study setting limits generalisability but improves barriers specific to our site. The self-selected sample method may introduce bias but maximises potential study participation. Additional multicentre studies with larger sample sizes are needed for generalisability, power and scalability. The low survey response rate and different survey samples preintervention to postintervention may have skewed results and limit the ability to analyse within person change, respectively. It may miss people who are not using POCUS or not reflect improvement of barriers. APPs had the lowest participation, perhaps because this group does not participate in resident education conference days where POCUS training occurs monthly. Also, APPs work in the ED Clinical Observation Unit and fast track areas without a supervising attending physician, thus lack the opportunity to perform POCUS.

Temporal confounding is possible in the preintervention and postintervention periods since new trainees begin working in July. This study timeline captures realistic ED environment results. To mitigate this, the postintervention sustainability period (July to December 2024) matches the preintervention period temporality. Also, we report monthly POCUS counts to detect temporal changes in a subsequent manuscript. Extrinsic factors such as ED and hospital boarding and variability in ED schedule staffing models can affect study data results and ED workflow, which may contribute to decisions on whether to perform POCUS. Finally, the questionnaires were deidentified by non-clinical research staff assistants to prevent unintended work influence and bias, thus ED providers did not receive direct feedback to improve their POCUS use unless they participated during in-person clinical shift education.

Future directions

Future studies could incorporate qualitative data collection through semistructured interviews and focus groups to further improve POCUS processes. The postintervention study timeline can be extended to 12+ months to measure sustainability. Regression models adjusting for training level, hospital privileging, and POCUS reimbursement data will be reported in a future manuscript since this paper focuses on identifying and addressing POCUS intervention barriers and perceived facilitators. Next, we will focus on individually reviewing POCUS use and system processes with ED physicians with infrequent POCUS scans and who work the highest number of clinical hours to further improve clinical ED POCUS use and documentation. Finally, protocol dissemination to additional local, community or VA EDs can identify common or differing POCUS use barriers and facilitators and tailor-specific quality improvement initiatives based on findings.

Conclusions

We identified barriers to clinical POCUS use including time constraints and lack of POCUS training and incorporated perceived facilitators such as a clear standardised POCUS documentation and archival protocol at a single academic ED. Our findings crafted a multifaceted POCUS intervention involving in-person teaching, an accessible reference document and image-based learning tool to increase clinical ED POCUS use. Future studies with individualised interventions for low POCUS utilisers and institutional-wide initiatives with POCUS champions are needed to determine effects on improving patient care.32
