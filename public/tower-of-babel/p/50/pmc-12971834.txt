Long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond head and neck cancer: a scoping review
Authors: Liana Fillo, Hanna Ginther, Joseph C Dort, Benny Rana, T Wayne Matthews, Diane Lorenzetti, Khara M Sauro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals living with and beyond head and neck cancer (HNC) often experience many consequences related to the cancer and its treatment which can persist long-term and have detrimental effects on overall quality of life. Despite the potentially profound impact of these consequences of cancer, the evidence around long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC is varied. The objective of this study is to describe the evidence on long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC.MethodsThis scoping review followed Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and was reported according to the PRISMA-ScR checklist. Structured language, keywords, and synonyms were used for each concept (patients diagnosed with HNC, outcomes, long-term) were used to search six databases. Eligible studies were those that reported outcomes beyond survival and recurrence at least 5-years after diagnosis of HNC. Two independent reviewers conducted screening (title and abstract then full-text screening), and data abstraction using a standardized form. The abstracted data from the evidence sources was synthesized using descriptive statistics, and qualitative data was categorized into themes.ResultsThe search identified 6072 potential evidence sources, of which 2339 full-texts were screened and 166 met eligibility criteria and were included. Most commonly, evidence sources were studies conducted in the USA (35%) between 2020 and 2023 (30%) and were cohort studies (70%). The included evidence sources represent 82,008 patients who were mostly males (69%). The most frequently reported outcomes were treatment-related effects, followed by disease progression and control, psychosocial impact, and physical symptoms/functional status. Quality-of-life was also reported (33%) with heterogeneous measures and reporting.ConclusionsThis study found a large body of evidence exploring long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC; however, there were gaps in evidence exploring the relationship between psychosocial outcomes and a rich understanding of the patient experience. Additionally, the considerable variability in methods, outcome measurement and reporting suggests a more standardized approach to long-term cancer outcomes research, and adherence to reporting checklists.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00520-026-10443-1.Keywords: Cancer, Head and neck cancer, Outcomes, Long term, Survivorship

Introduction

Head and neck cancers (HNC) are a large, heterogenous group of malignant tumors most commonly affecting the oral cavity, pharynx (including the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx), larynx, paranasal sinus and nasal cavity, and salivary glands [1]. Combined, HNC are the 7th most common cancers globally, with an incidence greater than 900, 000 in 2022 and are estimated to increase by 30% by 2030 [1]. HNC disproportionally affects males over the age of 50 and those with several lifestyle risk factors such as alcohol consumption, tobacco use and the presence of human papillomavirus (HPV) [2, 3]. The management of HNC is multimodal and complex, commonly using some combination of surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [4]. Due to the location and nature of HNC treatment, patients often experience chronic or life-long changes to crucial functioning [5], like eating, speaking, and breathing [6]. As many as 56–80% of patients report disturbances in oral function [7], such as persistent dry mouth (xerostomia), difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), trouble articulating speech (dysarthria), poor voice quality (dysphonia), difficulty opening the mouth (trismus), breathing problems, pain, and fatigue [5, 8, 9]. These consequences of HNC can significantly impair quality of life (QoL) [10–12], and often persist for some time [13]. Evidence shows that xerostomia, dysphagia, and dysarthria usually worsen after radiation therapy, with survivors reporting significant impairments in speech, eating, and trismus up to 10 years after treatment [5, 8, 14–16]. These long-term effects highlight the enduring challenges faced by individuals living with and beyond HNC.

Recent advances in treatment have significantly improved 5-year survival rates which leads to a greater proportion of individuals living with HNC long-term [17, 18]. Furthermore, the increasing incidence of HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer, which occurs in younger patients who have a better prognosis [19], has increased the proportion of individuals living beyond HNC long-term. Survival and recurrence remain the principal indicators of treatment effectiveness; however, other dimensions of well-being, such as physical status, functional abilities, psychological health, social interactions, and economic status are increasingly important. These outcomes may not be assessed long-term because most HNC guidelines recommend oncologic follow-up for 5-years after treatment [20, 21]. There is limited awareness and understanding of the importance of outcomes assessment beyond 5 years, which may also contribute to the under-reporting of chronic post-treatment morbidity [22]. Published data in the existing literature often inconsistently report timing of follow-up and evaluations of early versus late consequences of cancer [23–26]. A synthesis of the existing evidence is needed to understand the long-term consequences (e.g., nature, severity, impact) of HNC and its treatment. Such knowledge could then be used to improve the quality of care and quality of life for individuals living with and beyond HNC.

This scoping review aims to systematically characterize the evidence exploring long-term clinical, quality of life, and functional outcomes for individuals living with and beyond HNC.

Methods

Study design

Given the objective of this study is to describe, characterize and describe the literature, scoping review methodology was chosen. A strength of scoping review methodology is that it provides a systematic approach to describing the literature and identifying potential knowledge gaps about long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC. This review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and was reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews – Scoping Review Extension (PRISMA-ScR) [27, 28].

Ethical approval was not required because all included data has been published.

Search strategy

A research librarian (DL) with expertise in evidence synthesis assisted in developing the search strategy. The database searches were conducted using structured language, keywords, and synonyms for the two concepts: 1) patients with HNC and 2) long-term outcomes. The search strategy was adapted to and run in MEDLINE, EMBASE, APA PsycINFO, CINAHL, Cochrane CENTRAL, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (Supplemental File 1). Boolean operators were used to connect each term within a concept with “OR”, and the two concepts were connected with “AND”. Grey literature, which included conference proceedings, viewpoints, editorials, and organizational reports were also searched using similar keywords. The reference lists of eligible evidence sources were screened (akin to title screening) to identify any potential evidence sources that were missed in our search strategy. The search included evidence sources published from database inception until May 9, 2023.

Eligibility criteria

Evidence sources were eligible if they met the following criteria:

Population. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with HNC (including thyroid cancer). Evidence sources with populations that only included pediatric patients were excluded; however, studies that included pediatric and adult patients and stratified the findings based on age (data for adults and data for children) were included.

Outcome(s). Evidence sources that described patient-level or healthcare system-level outcomes beyond survival and recurrence with at least 5-year follow-up were included. If an evidence source included participants who had more than a 5-year follow-up, but also some with less than a 5-year follow-up, the evidence source was included, but only the results from the participants with at least 5-years of follow-up were included. Evidence sources that reported less than 5-years of follow-up, had an unclear follow-up period, or statistically projected 5-year outcomes were excluded. Evidence sources were not limited based on the type or number of outcomes reported; outcomes could include but were not limited to, physical (e.g., mobility, function), psychological (e.g., quality of life, anxiety, depression), or treatment-related (e.g., toxicity, complications). Evidence sources that only included estimates of recurrence and survival were excluded, but if additional variables were reported, the evidence source was included.

Design. All study designs were included. Reviews (systematic, scoping, narrative, literature) were included, but the primary evidence sources were prioritized for inclusion; if the studies included in the eligible reviews were included in our study the review was excluded because of duplicate data.

There were no restrictions based on year or language of publication. If an evidence source was written in a language other than English, there were two approaches to translating the article: 1) a reviewer fluent in the language of the eligible evidence source reviewed the article and conducted the data abstraction, 2) Google Translate was used to translate the article.

Selection of evidence sources

Selection of evidence sources occurred in two phases: 1) title and abstract screening followed by 2) full-text screening. Both phases were performed in duplicate by two independent reviewers (HG, LF, BR, KMS) using Covidence for data management [29]. Disagreements were not resolved during title and abstract screening; evidence sources deemed eligible by at least one reviewer were moved forward for full-text review. Disagreements among reviewers at the full-text screening stage were resolved through consensus or by consulting a third reviewer if needed.

Before beginning screening for both phases, reliability between reviewers was established by testing eligibility screening on a sample of evidence sources by all reviewers (n = 20 for title abstract screening and n = 20 for full-text screening). Testing continued until 80% agreement between reviewers was achieved.

Data charting

A standardized data charting form was used by the first reviewer to chart the data (LF) and was reviewed by a second reviewer (HG) independently. The reviewers pilot-tested the data abstraction form using a sample of five evidence sources and the form was then modified based on pilot testing until reviewers were satisfied that the charting form was able to capture all relevant data using an additional sample of five evidence sources. Data items abstracted from the evidence sources include bibliometric data, information about the study design and methods, population and setting, and outcomes including any qualitative analysis. Any disagreements in the abstracted data were resolved through consensus or if needed, a third reviewer.

As is common in scoping review methodology, data charting is iterative and was revised to accommodate a preponderance of studies reporting on Quality of Life (QoL). Therefore, additional information relating to QoL questionnaires was abstracted. Specifically, the questionnaires used to guide the data charting were the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) quality of life questionnaire (QLQ-C30), which is a 30-item questionnaire, consisting of a global QoL scale, five functional scales (physical, role, cognitive, emotional, and social), three symptom scales (fatigue, pain, and nausea and vomiting), five additional symptom single-items (dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, and diarrhea), and one single item assessing financial difficulty resulting from cancer [30]. All dimensions of the questionnaire are scored from 0 to 100, with higher response levels indicated by a higher score [30]. Therefore, a high score for global QoL represents a high QoL, a high score for a functional scale represents a greater level of functioning, and a high score for a symptom scale/item represents an increased symptom burden[30]. If evidence sources reported the use of the EORTC QLQ-C30 questionnaire, the scales used from the instrument were also reported. The minimum and maximum reported mean scores were also reported for each item from the survey.

Data analysis and presentation of results

All quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics; frequencies (proportions, percentages), means (with standard deviations), medians (with interquartile ranges, and ranges). Qualitative data were synthesized by creating themes through consensus between reviewers and the principal investigator (KMS).

The type of cancer was categorized based on the tumor location and for evidence sources reporting multiple HNC cancers, all tumor sites included were listed. If no specific tumor type was noted, cancer type was categorized as “not specified”. Similarly, the specific outcome reported was abstracted and was categorized (through consensus between reviewers and KMS if consensus could not be reached) into four categories: disease progression and control, treatment-related effects, physical symptoms and functional status, and psychosocial outcomes. Disease progression and control included recurrence, failure, survival, and control. Treatment-related effects included complications, toxicity, implant status, and flap preservation rate. Physical symptoms and functional status included swallowing, dry mouth, fatigue, pain, dental status, HNC specific-symptoms, and speech. Psychosocial outcomes included psychological measures, quality of life, socio-economic factors, and general health. If an evidence source reported multiple outcomes or multiple categories, the outcomes were then categorized as “multiple” then listed each category.

Results

Search results

The search identified 6072 evidence sources and 2339 full texts were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 154 met the eligibility criteria. An additional 12 evidence sources were identified through handsearching and grey literature sources for a total of 166 included evidence sources. Of the 2185 studies excluded, the most common reason for exclusion was a follow-up periods of less than 5-years (n = 754), recurrence and survival as the only outcomes (n = 572), and survival as the only outcome (n = 408) (Fig. 1). Study characteristics

The included studies were primarily conducted in the USA (n = 58, 35%) [31–88], but collectively 48% (n = 79) of studies were from European countries and 49% (n = 81) were from countries with predominantly publicly funded healthcare systems (Fig. 2.a). Almost all evidence sources were published in English (n = 162, 98%), with four published in Japanese (2%) [89–92]. Publication dates ranged from 1988–2023, with 30% being conducted between 2020 to 2023 (n = 50) [31, 32, 38, 39, 42, 47, 55, 60–62, 66, 76, 80, 82, 90, 93–127]. All included evidence sources were journal articles (except for one dissertation source [80]). There were two sources with a qualitative study design [76, 128] and two with a mixed-methods studies [54, 129] with the rest being quantitative designs. The quantitative study designs varied but were most commonly retrospective cohort studies (n = 71, 44%) [31–33, 36, 40, 41, 46, 48–50, 52, 55, 58, 60, 63, 66–68, 70, 75, 78, 86, 101–106, 112–114, 117, 120–122, 126, 127, 130–162] followed by prospective cohorts (n = 33, 20%) [53, 65, 71, 77, 79, 89, 90, 92–97, 100, 111, 118, 124, 125, 163–177] (Fig. 2.b).

Participant characteristics

The included evidence sources represent 82,008 participants, with a median value of 100 participants per study (Table 2). Nearly three-quarters of participants with HNC were male (69%), with nine sources not reporting sex [39, 48, 49, 53, 77, 92, 159, 178, 179]. The mean and median age ranged from 52 to 62 years (n = 90, 54%) and 16 evidence sources did not report a mean or median participant age [35, 36, 59, 61, 62, 74, 88, 92, 98, 99, 104, 109, 117, 128, 129, 136, 138, 159, 179, 180]. Most commonly, sources reported more than one HNC tumor site (n = 113, 68%)[33–35, 37, 39, 41–46, 48–58, 60–64, 66–70, 73–78, 80, 81, 83, 85–88, 90, 92–97, 99–101, 103–106, 108–110, 112, 113, 117–119, 122–127, 129, 130, 133, 135, 137, 139, 142, 143, 146, 149, 152, 154–157, 159, 162, 163, 166, 167, 170, 172–174, 176, 178, 179, 181–193]; the most commonly reported tumor site was the larynx (n = 100, 60%) [32–37, 41–46, 48–54, 57, 58, 60–62, 64–71, 73–76, 78, 80, 81, 83, 85–88, 91, 92, 100–104, 106, 108–112, 115, 122–126, 131–133, 138, 139, 141, 143–147, 149, 150, 152–160, 163, 167, 173, 174, 176, 179, 182–186, 188–190, 192], followed by the oral cavity (n = 99, 60%; Table 2) [33–35, 37, 39, 41, 43–46, 48–57, 60–63, 66–70, 72–76, 78, 80, 83, 84, 86–88, 90, 92–97, 99–101, 103–105, 107, 109, 110, 112–114, 117, 122–127, 130, 133, 135, 136, 139, 140, 142, 143, 146, 149, 151, 152, 155, 159, 163, 166, 168, 170–172, 177, 179, 181–187, 191, 193, 194]. The majority of evidence sources reported that many patients received more than one type of treatment (n = 114, 69%) [31, 32, 34, 35, 38, 39, 41, 43, 45–47, 49–54, 57–61, 63–68, 70–76, 78, 80, 81, 84, 88, 91, 93–97, 99–104, 106, 109, 110, 113, 115–127, 129, 131, 133, 136, 139–141, 144, 145, 147, 149, 151–154, 156, 157, 159, 160, 162–165, 167–171, 173, 175, 177, 181–187, 190, 192–197], with radiation being the most common treatment, whether it was multimodal or unimodal. Most (n = 131, 79%) evidence sources reported cancer staging, but the classification system varied between studies (AJCC n = 66, 40%; Union for International Cancer Control n = 21, 13%; unspecified n = 69, 42%). Follow-up period

All evidence sources reported the length of the follow-up period; however, the definition of the follow-up period varied, with the majority of evidence sources starting time zero as the end of treatment (n = 93, 56%) [31, 33, 36, 39, 41, 43, 44, 48–51, 54, 55, 58–60, 64, 67, 68, 70, 73, 76, 77, 79, 81, 84, 86–88, 91, 95–98, 101–103, 106, 110–112, 114, 116–122, 125, 127–129, 131–134, 136–138, 141–143, 145, 146, 152, 154, 155, 157, 158, 160–162, 164, 165, 167, 172, 173, 175, 177, 179, 181, 183–185, 187, 189, 191, 193–197], while others used time zero as the start of treatment (n = 32, 19%) [32, 34, 37, 42, 45, 56, 57, 65, 69, 78, 82, 83, 85, 92–94, 105, 108, 115, 130, 140, 144, 148, 150, 168, 169, 174, 176, 182, 186, 188, 190] or time at diagnosis (n = 26, 16%)[35, 38, 46, 47, 52, 53, 61–63, 66, 71, 72, 74, 75, 80, 99, 100, 104, 107, 109, 123, 124, 149, 163, 178, 192]. Most evidence sources reported a range of follow-up times (n = 129, 78%), with an overall range from 0 to 23.4 years; only those patients with at least 5-year follow-up were included in the analysis. The mean follow-up time, reported by 43 (26%) evidence sources ranged from 2.8 to 15.9 years [41, 54, 60, 65, 67, 70, 73, 79, 80, 84, 101, 102, 106, 110–113, 117, 122–124, 126, 133, 134, 137, 141–143, 145, 146, 154, 156, 158, 160, 162, 166, 171, 172, 175, 176, 181, 192], while the range of median follow-up time, reported by 70 (42%) of evidence sources was 2.2 to 14.1 years [32–34, 37, 38, 40, 42, 45–49, 52, 55, 56, 58, 61, 68, 69, 82, 83, 86, 89, 90, 92, 93, 101–103, 105, 108, 109, 114–116, 118–121, 130–132, 135, 139, 140, 143, 145, 147, 148, 150–153, 156, 157, 160, 161, 164, 165, 168, 169, 174, 178, 182, 186, 188–192, 196].

Outcome characteristics

The specific terms used to describe the outcomes were varied; a detailed table provides the exact outcome and how it was categorized (Supplemental File 3). The most commonly reported outcome category was treatment-related effects (n = 106, 64%), followed by disease progression and control (n = 104, 63%), psychological impacts (n = 64, 39%), and physical symptoms/function status (n = 57, 34%; Fig. 3). A detailed description for each outcome category follows.

The majority of studies reported on treatment effects, followed by disease prognosis, psychosocial impacts, and physical symptoms and functional status. N represents the number of studies

Treatment-Related Effects

This was the most commonly reported category, with 106 evidence sources reporting treatment-related effects [31–38, 40–42, 45, 48–52, 55, 56, 58, 59, 61, 62, 64, 68, 69, 73, 77–79, 81, 83, 86, 88–92, 95, 96, 99, 101–103, 105–108, 111, 113–119, 121, 122, 125–128, 131, 132, 134–136, 138–144, 146–148, 150–157, 160–162, 164, 165, 168–171, 173–176, 179, 182, 186–188, 190, 195]. Most evidence sources that reported treatment-related effects focused on either larynx or organ preservation (maintain function) rate after cancer treatment (n = 27, 16%) [36, 49, 55, 61, 89, 91, 92, 102, 111, 115, 121, 131, 132, 141, 144, 147, 148, 150, 153, 156, 157, 168, 169, 173–175, 190]. Toxicity was reported in 12 evidence sources (7%) and encompassed 13 different terms [37, 45, 49, 78, 108, 131, 139, 164, 165, 174, 186, 188]. Complications were reported in 76 evidence sources (46%), with 35% of the evidence sources reporting more than one complication [31–38, 41, 42, 48–52, 55, 56, 58–62, 64, 68, 69, 73, 77–79, 81, 83, 86, 88, 90, 95, 96, 99, 101, 103, 105–107, 114, 116, 118, 119, 121, 125, 126, 128, 132, 134, 138, 140, 142, 143, 146, 150–152, 154, 155, 160–162, 164, 165, 173, 174, 176, 179, 182, 186, 187, 195]. Reporting of complications was the most broad and heterogeneous treatment-related effects subcategory, as it encompassed 23 different terms within one outcome. Implant outcomes including survival, failure, and function were reported in nine evidence sources (5%) [40, 113, 117, 122, 126, 135, 136, 170, 171] with populations who had received teeth implants as a result of treatment.

Disease progression and control

This category was the second most frequently reported category (n = 104, 63%) [31–35, 37, 38, 42, 45, 53, 55, 56, 60, 63, 65, 68, 69, 71, 72, 74, 75, 77, 78, 82–84, 88–97, 100–102, 104, 105, 107, 108, 111, 113, 115, 116, 120, 121, 123, 125, 127, 130–132, 134–137, 139–141, 143, 144, 146–165, 168–171, 173–177, 179, 181, 182, 184, 186–188, 190, 194, 195, 197], but because of the eligibility criteria, all of the evidence sources that reported on disease progression and control also reported outcomes that fell into the other three categories. Of the evidence sources that reported disease progression and control, 48 evidence sources reported recurrence (29%) using 18 different terms to describe recurrence [32, 34, 37, 45, 53, 55, 63, 68, 74, 78, 84, 89, 90, 92, 95, 96, 102, 104, 105, 108, 111, 115, 116, 123, 127, 130, 136, 137, 141, 144, 146–148, 150, 152, 153, 155, 156, 160, 161, 175, 179, 181, 182, 184, 186, 188, 197], while 28 evidence sources reported on disease control (17%) using five different terms [32, 37, 90, 92, 102, 108, 116, 121, 131, 132, 139–141, 144, 146, 147, 153, 155–157, 160, 161, 164, 165, 175, 179, 187, 190], and 29 evidence sources reported on treatment failure (17%) using 13 different terms to represent this concept [32, 37, 42, 45, 56, 69, 83, 92, 104, 105, 108, 113, 116, 120, 131, 132, 140, 150, 152, 156, 161, 162, 164, 165, 175, 182, 186, 187, 190]. Finally, 93 evidence sources reported survival (56%) using 11 different terms to represent this concept [31–35, 37, 38, 42, 45, 56, 60, 63, 65, 69, 71, 72, 75, 77, 78, 82–84, 88–95, 97, 100–102, 105, 107, 108, 111, 113, 115, 116, 121, 125, 130–132, 134, 135, 137, 139–141, 143, 144, 146–165, 168–171, 173–177, 179, 181, 182, 186–188, 190, 194, 195, 197].

Physical symptoms and functional status

There were 57 evidence sources that reported outcomes related to physical symptoms and functional status. This category had the largest variety in the number of outcomes, which included physical effects specifically related to HNC such as swallowing, dry mouth, speech, dental status, fatigue, and pain. Dysphagia was reported in 26 evidence sources (26%) [35, 36, 39, 41, 55, 58, 59, 68, 70, 81, 84, 85, 94, 99, 121, 124, 125, 127, 128, 142, 143, 167, 173, 181, 193, 197], followed by dysphonia and dysarthria reported in 13 evidence sources (8%) [32, 65, 67, 81, 99, 118, 127, 143–145, 166, 191, 193], and xerostomia reported in 10 evidence sources (6%) [39, 99, 108, 125, 127, 133, 137, 143, 186, 197]. More general physical effects included fatigue, reported in six evidence sources (4%) [93, 100, 110, 118, 127, 193], and pain, reported in nine evidence sources (5%) [46, 53, 64, 99, 104, 125, 127, 143, 193]. Pain was assessed using five measures [46, 53, 64, 99, 104, 125, 127, 143, 193]. Dental status was assessed in three evidence sources (2%) [50, 127, 170]. General health outcomes were reported in three evidence sources (2%) [41, 50, 99].

Psychosocial impact

Psychological outcomes were reported in 13 evidence sources (8%), including feelings of anxiety, depression, distress and loneliness [44, 53, 57, 64, 70, 100, 109, 110, 112, 118, 127, 178, 193]. Across these 13 evidence sources, 17 measurement tools were used. Socioeconomic factors were reported in seven evidence sources (4%) and focused on patients’ employment, health insurance status, and cancer-related costs [47, 63, 66, 127, 130, 159, 192].

Quality of life

QoL was reported in 56 sources (34%) [39, 41, 43, 50, 51, 53, 54, 57, 64, 70–73, 75, 76, 80, 82, 84, 85, 87, 93, 94, 96–100, 104, 109, 112, 117, 118, 120, 124, 125, 127, 129, 130, 133, 142, 149, 154, 158, 163, 170, 172, 177, 181, 183–185, 189, 193–196]. There was a large variety of approaches to measure QoL, with 26 different assessment measures used by the 56 evidence sources that measured QoL. Half of the sources utilized only one QoL measure (n = 27)[39, 41, 43, 50, 54, 64, 70, 75, 76, 80, 84, 93, 98–100, 104, 109, 117, 118, 124, 129, 142, 149, 177, 184, 185, 194], while 22 (39%) used two different QoL measures[57, 71–73, 85, 87, 94, 96, 97, 125, 127, 133, 154, 158, 163, 170, 172, 181, 183, 189, 195, 196], and seven (12%) used three or more measures [51, 53, 82, 112, 120, 130, 193]. The EORTC-QLQ-C30, was the most used QoL assessment measure, used in 27 sources (28%) [71–73, 82, 93, 94, 96, 97, 100, 112, 118, 120, 125, 130, 133, 154, 158, 163, 170, 172, 181, 183, 184, 189, 193, 195, 196]. The HNC-specific questionnaire developed by the EORTC (QLQ-H&N35 and QLQ-H&N43), was the next most commonly used (n = 23; 23%—only two used the EORTC QLQ-H&N43 while the remaining used the QLQ-H&N35) [71–73, 82, 94, 96, 97, 112, 120, 125, 130, 133, 154, 163, 170, 172, 181, 189, 193, 195, 196]. An additional 23 assessment measures were used to measure QoL (University of Washington Quality of Life Questionnaire, Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy – Head & Neck, Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy General – Quality of Life, Performance Status Scale for Head & Neck Cancer Patients, Short Form 36 and 12 Surveys, Swallowing Quality of Life Instrument, M.D. Anderson Symptom Inventory, Research and Development Health-Related Quality of Life, EuroQol Five-Dimensions Instrument Scores, M.D. Anderson Dysphagia Inventory, Oral Health-Related Quality of Life, General Health Questionaire-20 Item, University of Michigan Xerostomia-Related Quality of Life, HNC Inventory, Social Provisions Scale, Aukland QOL, Implant Function Related to QOL, Xerostomia Questionnaire, Spitzer Quality of Life Index, Patient Concerns Inventory, Head and Neck Related Questionnaire and study-specific questionnaires) [39, 53, 54, 76, 82, 87, 99, 104, 117, 130, 149, 183, 185]. Only 17 of the 27 that utilized the EORTC-QLQC30 questionnaire used all the survey scales (63%) [71, 72, 94, 97, 112, 118, 120, 125, 133, 154, 163, 170, 172, 184, 189, 193, 195]. All but two sources reported global QoL (n = 25) [71–73, 82, 93, 94, 96, 97, 100, 112, 118, 120, 125, 130, 133, 154, 163, 170, 172, 181, 184, 189, 195, 196]. Overall, 22 evidence sources reported mean scores [71–73, 93, 94, 96, 97, 100, 112, 125, 130, 133, 154, 163, 170, 172, 181, 184, 189, 193, 195, 196]; the minimum mean score reported was 56.9 [73] and the maximum mean score reported was 83.3 [170]. Qualitative studies

Of the four evidence sources reporting qualitative data [54, 76, 128, 129], two were only qualitative [76, 128] and two were mixed-methods studies [54, 129]. Three evidence sources used thematic analysis [76, 128, 129] and one used content analysis [54]. The themes in the evidence sources revolved around 1) symptoms and QoL and 2) support. The theme of symptoms and QoL described the patient’s experience with HNC-specific symptoms and how to live with and manage these symptoms and the impact on patients’ QoL [54, 76, 128, 129]. Patients described support from family and healthcare providers as essential throughout their cancer journey [54, 129].

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive review of the evidence on long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC. The evidence sources reported a heterogeneous group of outcomes among individuals living beyond 5 years of HNC diagnosis, highlighting the magnitude and breadth of physical and psychosocial outcomes patients with HNC experience long-term. While this study found a large body of evidence exploring long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond cancer, there were some gaps identified in the evidence. Primarily, many evidence sources focused on the effectiveness of treatment, side effects of treatment, and cancer progression, suggesting future research is needed to identify and assess the impact of HNC and its treatment more comprehensively. For example, more evidence is needed to explore psychosocial outcomes because only four studies provided a rich understanding of the patient experience (using qualitative methodology). Furthermore, there was substantial variability in methods, follow-up period and outcome reporting among included evidence sources, indicating a potential role for reporting standards to examine long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC.

While reporting on a wide range of outcomes is appropriate among patients with HNC, because of the plethora of consequences related to HNC and its’ treatment, there was study-related heterogeneity which made synthesizing the evidence challenging. The substantial variability in outcomes reporting is consistent with other studies and highlights issues with between study heterogeneity due to outcome measurement and reporting, including terminology, approaches, and categorization [198–201]. The heterogeneity in outcome measurement and reporting poses challenges in synthesizing evidence on long-term outcomes for individuals living with and beyond HNC. This is particularly evident in HNC outcome research because of the lack of standardized reporting for common HNC-related symptoms including dysphagia, xerostomia, dysphonia, and dysarthria. For example, some sources reported xerostomia and dysphagia as complications, whereas other sources classified them as independent outcomes. Developing standardized outcome reporting would ensure consistent reporting and facilitate comparisons between studies, which is needed to generalize findings across diverse populations and subgroups [202]. The findings of this study can help provide guidance for future studies reporting on long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC. Moreover, future outcomes research should be informed by the perspectives of individuals living with and beyond HNC to begin to enhance the impact of future research exploring long-term outcomes in HNC.

Another issue related to lack of standardized reporting, was the inconsistent timing of the long-term follow-up period, which could be attributed to the lack of agreement within guidelines. Currently, there are no clear guidelines which inform follow-up of patients with HNC beyond 5-years after treatment. Many guidelines recommend follow-up at varying monthly intervals from diagnosis to the 5-year timeframe, and annually thereafter [203, 204]. However, there is little evidence supporting the efficacy of these approaches to surveillance [205, 206]. The respective roles of specialists and primary care practitioners in conducting follow-up procedures are also ambiguous [25, 207]. Primary care providers commonly manage the late effects of treatment but often feel unequipped to do so [25, 207]. Berkowitz et al. [205] found that while 81% of primary care practitioners felt responsible for providing long-term follow-up care, only 32% felt confident in managing the long-term effects of HNC treatment [207]. Data suggests patient-reported outcomes among individuals living with and beyond cancer (especially in the survivorship period after treatment completion) are lacking. This may be related to inconsistent follow-up by healthcare providers familiar with the nuances of the long-term outcomes [25, 206, 208, 209]. Future surveillance and follow-up guidelines should include recommendations on patient-reported outcomes at regular intervals.

Despite a preponderance of studies examining long-term outcomes among patients with HNC, there were several identified gaps in evidence, which should be the focus of future research in this area. First, there was a paucity of data exploring the role of gender on long-term outcomes among patients with HNC, which is an important consideration especially considering the high proportion of men diagnosed with HNC, and the role that sexual activity (HPV status) has in HNC epidemiology [210]. There is evidence to suggest that sex and gender play a role in outcomes from HNC [210, 211]. This is also related to another gap identified; evidence from low-middle-income countries where the incidence of HNC is highest and where gender norms may differ from high-income countries. The majority of sources were published in the United States, despite HNC being more prevalent in other geographical regions, especially in countries in South Asia such as Bangladesh, India, Pakistan and Sri Lanka [198–201]. The increased prevalence in these countries likely is related to variations in occupational and toxin exposures, healthcare resources, tobacco and alcohol use, sexual norms (HPV rates), and genetics [212–216]. Less than a third of included evidence sources documented these risk factors. While low-middle-income countries are often underrepresented in research, improved documentation of some well-known risk factors can help with the generalizability of the findings of studies from high-income countries to populations with higher rates of HNC. Moreover, the findings regarding long-term outcomes among patients with HNC in high-income countries, if generalizable to other populations can help advocate for the need for long-term follow-up [217–219]. While this review demonstrates that there is an abundance of long-term effects of HNC and its treatment, there is little evidence exploring interventions to improve or mitigate these long-term effects. Interventions from other cancer populations could potentially be adapted for use in individuals living with and beyond HNC. For example, an exercise program incorporating flexibility, range of motion and lymphedema exercises may improve swallowing and communication [220, 221], an 8-week Mindfulness-based therapy intervention may reduce symptoms of distress [221], and eHealth applications that allow patients to record symptoms, monitor their QoL and receive feedback may support self-management (223). Future research should focus on these areas to improve our understanding of long-term outcomes among patients with HNC and to improve these outcomes.

While this scoping review has many strengths, such as the breadth of terms used in the search strategy to identify a large number of studies and the rigour with which the study was conducted; there are some limitations to be considered when interpreting our findings. First, which is inherent with all reviews, is the potential for missing potential studies. Indeed, 12 studies were identified through handsearching that were not identified in our search. Secondly, given the breadth and heterogeneity in the identified studies, some aspects of the included evidence sources may not have been synthesized.

In conclusion, this study found a large body of evidence exploring long-term outcomes among individuals living with and beyond HNC; however, there were gaps in evidence exploring the relationship between psychosocial outcomes and a rich understanding of the patient experience. Additionally, the considerable variability in methods, outcome measurement and reporting suggests a more standardized approach to long-term outcome research, and adherence to reporting checklists, among patients with cancer is warranted.
