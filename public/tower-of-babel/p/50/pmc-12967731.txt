Experiences of Patients, Family Members and Nurses in Outpatient Cancer Care: A Multi‐Method Qualitative Study
Authors: Nerea Azcárate‐Cenoz, Cristina Alfaro‐Díaz, Ana Ojer Perea, María Ángeles Soteras Ramírez, Cristina Arellano Gil, Patricia Beorlegui Murillo, Nuria Esandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCancer significantly impacts patients and their families, disrupting their emotional, relational, and organisational dynamics. Outpatient oncology nurses play a pivotal role in addressing the complex and evolving needs of patients with cancer and their families. Including the perspectives of nurses, family members, and patients within this care setting may inform the development of appropriate care services responsive to their real needs. This study aimed to gain a comprehensive and integrated understanding of how nurses, patients, and family members experience cancer and caring in outpatient oncology consultations.MethodsA multi‐method qualitative study was conducted as part of a larger Experience‐Based Co‐Design project. Data were collected between January and May 2024 through non‐participant observations, field notes, and semi‐structured interviews with patients, family members, and nurses. Data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis, integrating findings across participant groups and data collection methods.ResultsThrough the integrated data analysis of observations, field notes, and individual stakeholders' interviews, three key themes emerged: (i) Cancer: a family affair; (ii) the needs of patients with cancer and their families; and (iii) the importance of establishing a trust‐based nurse‐family relationship.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive understanding of the experiences of patients, families, and nurses in outpatient oncology care, underscoring the critical role of a family‐oriented approach in care to provide support to families facing the multifaceted challenges posed by the illness. Insights from this research will inform the next phase of an Experience‐Based Co‐Design study to design a nurse‐led family support intervention for patients with cancer and their family members.Keywords: cancer, family member experience, family support, multi‐method, nurses experience, patient experience

Introduction

Cancer has a profound impact on both patients and their families, affecting the physical, psychological, emotional, and relational well‐being [1]. Patients with cancer face a life‐threatening illness that encompasses new challenges, including aggressive treatments and their side effects, as well as uncertainty about the future [2]. Family members, on their side, often experience intense emotions, such as anger, frustration, anxiety, and helplessness. They confront the demands of providing optimal care for their loved ones while attempting to balance their own personal, social, and professional lives [3, 4]. These circumstances disrupt the natural course of family life, potentially compromising its dynamics and functioning [5, 6]. Consequently, cancer can be understood as a “family affair” [7].

The global burden of cancer continues to rise, further intensifying its impact on families. Current global data indicate an incidence of nearly 20 million new cases of cancer worldwide, causing approximately 9.7 million deaths in 2022 [8]. Moreover, projections estimate that by 2050, annual cancer diagnoses will reach 35.3 million cases [8]. Given this growing prevalence and the increasing demand for cancer care, healthcare systems have shifted from traditional inpatient models to more innovative outpatient care approaches [9, 10]. It is estimated that currently, around 80%–90% of cancer care is provided in outpatient settings, enabled by advancements in technology, treatments, and innovative approaches to care delivery [11].

Within this context, outpatient oncology nurses play a pivotal role in managing the complex care needs of patients and their families [12]. As the primary point of contact, these nurses provide not only medical care but also crucial psychological and social support [9, 11, 12]. Their responsibilities extend beyond symptom management to encompass care coordination, patient education, and emotional support, all of which significantly contribute to improved patient and family health outcomes [13].

As oncology care increasingly shifts toward ambulatory settings, integrating a family‐oriented approach becomes essential to address the complex and interrelated needs of patients and their families, and to ensure that nurses provide comprehensive support to both patients and their loved ones [14, 15, 16]. This approach recognises the reciprocal impact of cancer, whereby the illness affects not only the patient but also family members, whose emotional, behavioural, and coping responses can, in turn, influence the illness experience and care process [17]. Within this framework, the concept of “family” is understood as the unit of care, encompassing the patient and other significant family members. Viewing the family as the unit of care offers a holistic and clinically relevant perspective, as it captures the shared experiences, needs, and coping processes of all family members across the cancer trajectory [18, 19]. Consequently, patients' illness experiences are examined in parallel with those of their family members, rather than in isolation. However, much of the existing literature has predominantly explored individual experiences of patients [20, 21, 22] and family members affected by cancer individually [23], often focusing only on patient‐caregiver dyads within inpatient or end‐of‐life contexts [24]. Additionally, the inclusion of nursing perspectives remains notably limited—particularly in outpatient care settings [25]. Integrating the perspectives of nurses along with those of patients and families is thus essential to gain a more complete and integrated understanding of the care experience [26].

This leaves a gap in understanding how these groups experience care in the oncology outpatient setting, where patients and family members rely heavily on nurses for both emotional support and complex illness guidance [11]. Understanding the experiences and perspectives of patients with cancer, family members, and oncology nurses will provide a comprehensive view of the cancer experience and care process [27].

To date, no study has examined the experiences and perspectives of nurses, patients, and family members in outpatient oncology settings. Thus, this study aims to gain a comprehensive and integrated understanding of how these groups experience cancer and caring during outpatient oncology care consultations.

Methods

Study Design

This study is part of a broader Experience‐Based Co‐Design (EBCD) project [18] that actively engages key stakeholders—patients, family members, and nurses—in the design of a nurse‐led family support intervention for families coping with cancer in oncology outpatient consultations [28, 29]. The larger study employs a combined approach, integrating theoretical, empirical, and experiential perspectives to develop a family support intervention [30]. Given the richness of the data obtained, this paper focuses on reporting the experiential phase, specifically the first two steps of the EBCD method, which employ a qualitative research approach combining non‐participant observation, field notes, and interviews as data collection methods [31]. An overview of the EBCD steps is illustrated in Figure 1.

Steps of the Experience‐Based Co‐design method.

Observations and field notes allowed researchers to obtain a real contextual understanding of the care environment, workflows, and interactions among patients, family members, and nurses in the outpatient oncology consultations [32]. Semi‐structured interviews allowed patients, family members, and nurses to share their experiences with cancer illness and nursing care, offering rich, in‐depth insights [32, 33].

This study is reported using the COREQ guidelines for reporting qualitative research (Data S1) [34].

Participant Recruitment

A purposive convenience sampling was used to recruit participants from outpatient oncology consultations in a leading cancer centre in northern Spain [35]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients, family members, and nurses to be engaged within outpatient oncology, haematology, or radiotherapy units; to be over 18 years of age; and to be fluent in Spanish. Participant recruitment was aimed at capturing a heterogeneous sample in terms of sociodemographic and family characteristics, such as cancer diagnosis, received treatment, marital status, number of children, and experience as a nurse, to ensure a diverse range of perspectives. Researchers recruited nurses working in the mentioned outpatient consultations, following a detailed explanation of the study. Patients and family members were contacted by their attending nurse or by the nursing director of the cancer centre. Those who expressed interest were referred to the researchers, who provided them with detailed information about the study, along with a written information sheet.

Data Collection

Data collection was conducted between January and May 2024 by three researchers. Initially, 24 h of non‐participant observations were conducted over 6 days in the outpatient consultations. An observation form (Data S2), enriched with field notes, was used by the researchers to systematically document the consultation environment and dynamics, interactions between the nurse, patients, and family members, and the main topics addressed during the consultations.

Subsequently, 22 semi‐structured face‐to‐face interviews were conducted in a setting outside the hospital to foster a more neutral and open environment, thereby encouraging participants to share their experiences with confidence and openness [31]. A single interview was carried out with each participant. Interviews were conducted in a private setting, with only the interviewer and the participant being present, to ensure a comfortable and confidential environment. Two tailored interview guides were employed. The guide designed for interviews with nurses explored their perceptions of the experiences of patients and family members throughout the illness, including its impact, common concerns and needs, their experiences in caring for the patient and the family living with cancer, as well as the approach to the care provided (Data S3). The guide directed at patients with cancer and their family members focused on their experiences with the illness, including challenges, worries, and needs, as well as their perspectives on the received nursing care (Data S4). All interviews were audio‐recorded, with those involving patients and family members additionally video‐recorded, as required by the EBCD methodology of the larger project to which this study belongs [31].

Data collection was conducted with attention to the quality, relevance, and diversity of participants' narratives [36]. This process continued until the research team considered that the information obtained was sufficiently rich and varied to allow a robust understanding of the phenomenon. To enhance data depth, the interview guides were used in a flexible manner, which facilitated the participants' narratives and allowed adaptation to their discourse and emerging nuances. In this regard, preliminary analysis of the initial interviews revealed emerging themes, enabling refinement of questions and strategies for subsequent interviews. This process of progressive and iterative inquiry facilitated an increasingly in‐depth understanding of the experience [37]. Additionally, observations and field notes collected during data gathering provided contextual richness that further contributed to the depth of the data [38].

Data Analysis

An integration of data from observations, field notes and individual interviews was carried out on the basis of the “following a thread” approach [39]. This approach preserved the richness of the qualitative data while maintaining methodological rigour and consists of four steps [40].

In the first step, each dataset was analysed using the most appropriate analytic method. In this study, observations, together with field notes, were analysed by N.A., N.E., and C.A. using a descriptive qualitative analysis [41] aimed at capturing contextual, relational, and interactional dynamics within outpatient oncology consultations. On the other hand, semi‐structured interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using inductive thematic analysis [42]. N.A. generated and organised initial codes using NVivo 12 software. Then, N.A., N.E., and C.A. grouped the codes into potential themes that captured relevant patterns. Subsequently, themes were reviewed by N.A., N.E., and C.A. to ensure their coherence and consistency with the dataset, refining them as necessary. Finally, N.A., N.E., and C.A. clearly defined and named the themes to capture their core meaning. This process was conducted separately for interviews with patients, family members, and nurses.

The second step of the integration focused on recognising related findings in each dataset that could serve as a guiding thread. This led to the third step, where key findings, categories, and codes related to the thread across datasets were organised into a structured set of data. For instance, across the different datasets, several findings pointed to the importance of the nurse's understanding of the family's situation, dynamics, and needs. This recurring theme served as a guiding thread, enabling researchers to identify and group findings from interviews, observations, and field notes that referred to how nurses recognized, interpreted, or engaged with family‐related knowledge. Through this process, a coherent and integrated dataset was constructed around this thread.

In the fourth step, findings from a specific thread were combined with those from other thematically related threads to develop a more comprehensive and integrated understanding of the data. For example, findings grouped under the thread related to the nurse's understanding of the family's situation, dynamics, and needs were integrated with findings from the thread focused on the nurse‐family relationship, enabling a richer interpretation of how nurses build and sustain therapeutic alliances with families.

Finally, participants were provided with reports containing illustrative quotes from their transcripts and invited to review and validate the findings to ensure their perspectives were accurately represented.

Ethical Considerations

This study was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the University of Navarra (Reference 2023.215) and obtained authorization from the cancer centre for its development. Data were anonymized, securely stored, and used exclusively for research purposes, ensuring confidentiality throughout the study.

For observations, verbal consent was obtained from nurses to allow the researchers to observe them in the outpatient consultations. Prior to each consultation, the nurse introduced the researcher to the patient and their family members and sought their verbal consent. Participants received detailed verbal and written information about the study, including an information sheet, and had the opportunity to ask questions. Participants who agreed to take part in the study signed an informed consent form.

The Needs of Patients with Cancer and their Families

“They (patients and family members) ask a ton of questions. They wanna know everything—what the illness is, what the side effects are, all the details. They really wanna know, ‘Okay, if this doesn't work, then what's next? What else can we try?’” [N‐7]

“Family members are people too—they wanna know what's going on. But like, how much you tell them? Honestly, it depends. There's no one‐size‐fits‐all rule, like, ‘Yeah, this is exactly what they should know every time.’ It really comes down to the person.” [P‐3]

“If I talked to her now, I'd totally understand everything. But at that moment, you're just so, so overwhelmed that you kind of take things in a weird way.” [P‐5]

“I think the moment of diagnosis is really tough for them because they're just not ready to take in all the information. At first, they're not even prepared for it. […] I think you have to go gradually, letting them process it, come to terms with it, and understand how it's going to affect their daily lives.” [N‐4]

“Everyone wants to know—like, if it's a young person, ‘Am I gonna be able to keep studying?’ If they're working, ‘Will I be able to keep working?’ […] ‘Will I still be able to take care of my people?’ Those are the kinds of worries they bring up. It's mainly that:’Will I be able to keep living my life like before, or is this going to hit me so hard that I won't be able to handle it and I'll have to get help?’” [N‐2]

“When it comes to wigs or headscarves, in the end, you have to figure it out on your own, and you don't always have the time for that. Having a ‘kit’ ready for when your hair falls out does give you a sense of peace. […] It's not the cancer itself; it's not going to kill you. But still, it's something you have to deal with.” [P‐6]

“I see the role of nursing kind of like what we talked about in education—with scaffolding. When you're hit with an illness like this, everything—your personal life, your family, your social world—just kind of falls apart. It's like saying, ‘I need someone to help hold me up, to build some kind of support around me so everything doesn't just crash down’.” [P‐3]

“You're so emotionally raw that just having someone there—a shoulder to lean on in that moment—feels incredibly important. And it's not just anyone; it's someone who truly understands what you're going through, who knows the process and can explain it to you. That brings a huge sense of relief. I felt so much relief. Being able to talk openly with the nurses about my fears and everything else has been such a comfort, because they really understand the whole process.” [FM‐3]

“Family members start to realise they need support too. And then there's the whole thing about how to juggle their own lives while being a caregiver. […] So, it's like everything just hits pause—work, everyday stuff… it all kind of comes to a stop.” [N‐1]

“For the distress, the uncertainty, and everything else… they (family members) need time to be heard. Just let them get everything off their chest. […] Sometimes it's just about validating their feelings, not brushing them off. Not saying, ‘Come on, don't worry,’ no. It's about really listening, like, ‘You're right, this is serious.’ […] They really need to be listened to—really listened to—in an assertive way, by someone who gets it, obviously.” [N‐7]

“We really do need it. It's tough because you don't want to make a fuss–you just want the nurses to take good care of your husband and for him to be okay. But after a while, the pressure builds up, and you start to feel you're drowning. And at some point, you just want to say ‘Please, someone tell me something. I need help’.” [FM‐2]

“The husband is standing in the corner of the room. No one talks to him. After a while, he asks the nurse what to do if the patient feels discomfort and what she can take for it. The nurse answers, looking only at him. The patient stays quiet.” [O‐15.01.2024]

Theme 1: Cancer, a Family Affair

Cancer, a family affair, refers to the recognition of cancer not only as an individual medical condition but as an illness that profoundly affects the entire family system. The diagnosis and progression of cancer alter family dynamics, often requiring significant adaptations in daily functioning. This theme also describes how cancer influences how families cope, make decisions, and mobilise internal and external resources, highlighting the interdependence between the patient's experience and the well‐being of the family as a whole.

Thus, “Cancer: A Family Affair” comprises two subthemes. The first, “A Change in Family Life,” captures the experiences of families during the initial moments following the diagnosis and its subsequent consequences for both the patient and their relatives. The second subtheme, “Reciprocal Family Concern and Suffering”, reflects how patients and family members conceal their own distress, thoughts, and emotions in an effort to protect one another and to prevent causing additional worry within the family.

A Change in Family Life

A cancer diagnosis represents a turning point in the daily life of the family, described by patients, their family members, and nurses as ‘a huge shock’. They referred to experiencing disbelief regarding the situation, especially when the diagnosis was an incidental finding [FM‐2]. This initial stage was often characterised by a profound emotional disorientation, as individuals struggled to comprehend and assimilate the reality of the illness [N‐3].

In the first days after receiving the diagnosis, patients and family members experience intense fear and uncertainty about the future. Family members specifically highlighted their fear of the potential loss of their loved one [FM‐7]. Nurses also identified how both patients and families grapple with these fears, noting that uncertainties about prognosis, quality of life, and continuity of family roles often emerge in these early moments [N‐6].

Although initial reactions reflect a shared emotional shock, participants also described how the broader impact of cancer on family life varies significantly depending on contextual and illness‐related factors [P‐5]. Findings revealed that the impact of cancer on the family depends on the type of cancer, as well as its prognosis, progression, and severity of the illness. Patients highlighted that the family's life stage plays a critical role in shaping the impact of the illness. Additionally, they referred to differences between the initial period following the cancer diagnosis and the later stages of the illness [N‐7].

Participants also agreed that the family's prior experience with the illness is another factor that may influence, either positively or negatively, the experience and coping process of the family with the new situation. These prior experiences may offer a sense of preparedness, but they can also add emotional weight [P‐2; N‐2].

Reciprocal Family Concern and Suffering

Living with an oncological illness generates for both patients and family members feelings of suffering and concern for one another. Both patients and their family members described experiencing not only their own distress but also deep concern for each other's well‐being.

From the patients' perspective, the family members who have been by their side throughout the illness are the ones who suffer the most [P‐5]. In particular, patients identified their spouses, as well as their granddaughters and mothers, as the persons they had observed experiencing the highest levels of emotional suffering [P‐7].

Family members, on their side, concurred with patients in stating that, because of the deep emotional bond they share with their loved one affected, the cancer diagnosis caused such profound suffering that they felt as though their own lives were coming to an end. They further noted that, although they were not the ones diagnosed with cancer, they experienced the process as if they themselves were living with the illness [FM‐4].

Suffering within the family was manifested as a constant and reciprocal concern among its members for one another's well‐being. Both patients and family members reported that their primary concern was not the illness or its treatment, but rather the emotional impact that the new situation had on their loved ones [P‐2; FM‐3].

Because of the illness‐related suffering and concern, family members sought to protect each other, creating a dynamic of mutual care and protection between the patient and their loved ones. In this sense, family members and nurses reported that patients often refrained from openly expressing their distress to shield their family members [N‐4]. Furthermore, they confirmed that, despite experiencing fear and concern for the patient, they also avoided sharing these emotions with them [FM‐5].

Theme 2: The Needs of Patients With Cancer and Their Families

This theme encompasses the care needs of patients with cancer and their families as identified through narratives provided by patients, family members, and nurses. It comprises two subthemes derived from the most frequently reported needs. The first subtheme, “Need for Information”, reflects the significance attributed by patients and families to receiving accurate, clear, and timely information from nursing professionals. The second subtheme, “Need for Family Support”, underscores the importance of addressing the needs of both patients and their family members, thereby recognising the family as the primary unit of care.

Need for Information

Participants emphasised the importance of nurses offering families comprehensive information about the illness, its progression, symptoms, and associated treatment [N‐7]. Participants referred to the type and extent of information provided by the nurse to the family should be tailored to the specific circumstances of each family, considering the current stage of the process they are in and what they can expect in the future [P‐3].

In this line, nurses, patients, and family members noted that, at the early stages of the illness, families are not prepared to process a large amount of information, as they are still assimilating the emotional impact of the diagnosis [P‐5]. Consequently, nurses observed that patients and family members retain only the aspects they perceived as most essential, emphasising that information should be gradually introduced, allowing families to process it at their own pace [N‐4].

In addition, patients, family members, and nurses reported that families need and seek additional information about the potential impact of cancer on their daily lives. According to nurses, patients and family members experience concern about how their routines might change because of the illness, what habits they may need to modify, and whether they will be able to maintain their previous way of life following the diagnosis [N‐2].

Although patients considered the information provided by nurses as appropriate, they expressed a desire for more practical guidance to help them adjust to the process. For instance, one patient highlighted how having resources prepared in advance—such as a “kit” for wigs or headscarves—could help alleviate the sense of uncertainty [P‐6].

Need for Family Support

During the interviews, patients and family members described how, throughout the oncological illness trajectory, they required family care and support from the nurse. In this regard, they highlighted the nurse's role as a “scaffolding” throughout the process, providing stability and guidance as the family undergoes significant changes [P‐3].

Participants recognised the nurse's accompaniment as a crucial element in delivering essential support to the family. The nurse's consistent presence and support throughout the process helped patients and their families feel more supported and less anxious and alone during consultations [FM‐3; N‐1].

Nurses reported that the support they provided helped family members cope with their emotional burden and better understand how to support their relative with cancer [N‐7]. Family members also described how stress became overwhelming over time, emphasising the need for nurses to guide families in coping with the challenges posed by the illness [FM‐2].

However, some family members did not always receive nursing care that considered the entire family. During observations, it was noted that in some consultations, nurses primarily focused on the patient and not all family members present were included in the conversation [O‐15.01.2024].

Theme 3: The Importance of Establishing Trust‐Based Nurse–Family Relationship

This theme explores the establishment and significance of trust‐based relationships between nurses and families within the context of oncology care. It comprises two interrelated subthemes. The first subtheme, “A Relationship Grounded in Trust and Mutual Knowledge”, highlights the centrality of the relational bond formed between nurses and the family as the unit of care, explicitly including the patient. Participants' narratives illustrate how trust is progressively built through nurses' interactions not only with patients but also with other family members. The second subtheme, “The Enabling Context for Establishing the Nurse–Family Relationship”, examines the contextual and organisational factors that shape and facilitate the development of this relational bond throughout the care trajectory.

A Relationship Grounded in Trust and Mutual Knowledge

Nurses, patients, and family members emphasised in their interviews the importance of the relationship that is established between the nurse and the family in the oncology outpatient consultations.

Nurses highlighted the importance of establishing a relationship with the family that enables them to understand the family's situation and to identify the needs emerging from the illness [N‐4]. Observations confirmed that as nurses became more familiar with the family, they gained deeper insight into family dynamics, roles, and organisational structures. Nurses' ability to recall personal details and provide care with closeness and warmth contributed to a sense of trust and reassurance [O‐10.01.2024].

Patients and family members highlighted the nurse's ability to recognise emotional changes and remember personal details as key relational qualities that fostered a sense of being understood and genuinely cared for [P‐6]. Observations reinforced these findings, as in most consultations, nurses demonstrated a comprehensive understanding of patients' and family members' situation [O‐10.01.2024].

Patients and family members also emphasised that the mutual understanding developed with the nurse fostered a trust‐based relationship that allowed both the patient and the family to feel comfortable sharing their concerns and addressing their questions, thereby promoting open communication and a more supportive care [FM‐3].

The trust‐based relationship between the family and the nurse is gradually strengthened as both parties develop a deeper understanding of one another. Nurses emphasised the importance of actively nurturing this trust and establishing a genuine connection with the family, ensuring they feel supported throughout the care process [N‐7].

The Enabling Context for Establishing the Nurse–Family Relationship

Patients, family members, and nurses described various elements that help to create an adequate context to provide humanised, family‐oriented care in outpatient consultations.

Standardise and Register Family‐Oriented Conversations

Nurses reported that family‐oriented conversations are neither structured nor standardized. They further noted that this lack of standardization may lead to conversations with the family being guided by what arises spontaneously in the moment, leaving many important topics for the family unaddressed [N‐7]. Observations during consultations further confirmed that the topics addressed during the conversations depend on the nurse's knowledge and relationship with the family [O‐10.01.2024]. In this sense, nurses pointed out that having a system in place would allow for deeper exploration of key topics while ensuring that no crucial aspects were overlooked.

In this regard, nurses reported that they do not usually document information related to the conversations they have with families, prioritising instead the recording of patients' vital signs, symptoms, and treatment‐related side effects. As a result, relevant data concerning the family's experience and the impact of the illness—information that could enhance care for both the patient and their family—often remain undocumented [N‐2]. Observations confirmed that no formal record of these conversations was kept [O‐15.01.2024]. Despite the referred challenges associated with maintaining family health records, nurses emphasised that it could facilitate ongoing conversations and enhance family support over time.

Nurses' Experience and Family‐Oriented Training

Nurses identified their professional experience in outpatient consultations, along with ongoing training in communication strategies and family‐oriented tools, as key elements that support their ability to engage effectively with patients and families.

Nurses acknowledged that much of their knowledge had been gained through direct exposure to complex situations over time and emphasized the value of additional training to refine their skills and enhance their ability to support families effectively [N‐7].

Time and Space Availability in the Consultations

Nurses reported insufficient time to dedicate to families and provide high‐quality care, resulting in a sense of powerlessness and professional frustration. Nurses attributed this lack of time to management and organisational practices [N‐4]. In this line, patients and family members emphasised the importance of nurses avoiding an appearance of being rushed when providing care, fostering a sense of reassurance and tranquillity [P‐3]. However, nurses acknowledged that limited time often prevented them from fully addressing concerns that arose during consultations.

Nurses underscored the absence of adequate physical spaces for conducting professional conversations with families. As confirmed during the observations, some consultation rooms were too small to accommodate a comfortable conversation between the nurse and the family [O‐10.01.2024]. Because of this limitation, nurses acknowledged frequently having conversations with families outside of consultation rooms, where privacy for the family was compromised. Nurses colloquially referred to this practice as “corridor care” [N‐2].

Patients and family members, along with observations, confirmed that they often received information and care from nurses in waiting areas [O‐11.01.2024]. This lack of privacy led patients to feel that their intimacy was exposed to strangers, causing discomfort [P‐8]. These situations not only compromised patients' and family members' sense of privacy, but also hindered the establishment of a safe and trusting environment for communication, potentially affecting the nurse‐family relationship.

Discussion

Findings show how the experiences and perspectives of patients, family members, and nurses intersect and influence the overall experience of cancer care in the outpatient oncology setting. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively analyse the experiences of these three groups within this specific context to offer a unique and integrated perspective. This understanding is essential for identifying the specific challenges faced by patients and families coping with cancer, as well as for recognising the key aspects that should be considered in nurse‐led family support interventions.

The results reflect how cancer significantly disrupted family dynamics and daily life, requiring families to reorganise responsibilities. These findings align with previous research highlighting that the diagnosis of cancer disrupts the family system, generating emotional, practical, and relational difficulties that demand adaptive strategies [1, 3, 43]. This underscores the critical need for oncology care to recognise the family as a dynamic system influenced by the illness. A comprehensive understanding of this impact can inform the development of nurse‐led interventions designed to support families affected by cancer and to enhance their capacity to effectively manage the illness.

Findings also highlight that families experience the illness through mutual concern, directing more attention toward the suffering of other family members over their own. This phenomenon clearly emerged in the interviews, with patients and family members trying to hide their own suffering to avoid worrying their loved ones. Concealing cancer‐related thoughts or worries from other family members has been defined in the literature as protective buffering [44], which can lead to communication problems in the family by opting for mutual protection from fear, sorrow, and worries [45]. According to a recent integrative review regarding protective buffering [44], nurses play a crucial role in mitigating this dynamic by facilitating open communication between patients and their families. Family support interventions have shown potential to strengthen family relationships and reduce the need to hide emotions and concerns [46, 47]. These findings underscore the importance of integrating nurse‐led interventions that actively address protective buffering, as unrecognised concealment of emotions may compromise family coping strategies, hinder effective caregiving, and indirectly affect patient outcomes. Recognising and mitigating this dynamic can therefore enhance both the psychological well‐being of patients and family members and the quality of care provided.

In this line, findings also revealed that nurses and families highlighted the need for nursing care to address the needs of the family unit, particularly in terms of emotional support and guidance in caregiving. Family members referred to feeling overwhelmed by the challenges of reorganising their daily lives around the illness and often struggled to balance caregiving with their own well‐being. In this sense, a family‐oriented approach in care acknowledges the relational dynamics within the family, recognising that the well‐being of the patient is intricately linked to the well‐being of their family members [17, 48]. Integrating family‐oriented practices into oncology outpatient care would allow nurses to improve their experience and enhance outcomes for patients and their families [48, 49]. This highlights the urgent need for healthcare systems to prioritise family‐oriented approaches as a standard component of oncology care delivery [19].

Participants affirmed that establishing such a care approach requires a strong relational foundation, with trust serving as a pivotal element of effective nurse‐family relationships and delivering high‐quality family‐oriented care. Our findings highlight that nurses, patients, and family members perceive trust‐based relationships as essential for fostering a sense of support and security throughout the illness process. Such engagement is particularly important in outpatient oncology settings, where the emotional and practical challenges faced by patients and families require a foundation of mutual understanding and collaboration [27]. In this sense, participants in this study emphasised that when the family trusted the nurse, they were more likely to share sensitive information about their worries and concerns, enabling the nurse to effectively address their needs. Similarly, previous research underscores that trust enhances the nurse‐family relationship by creating a safe space for dialogue, which is necessary for carrying out support interventions that respond to the real needs of the family [50]. These findings underscore that fostering trust is not only a relational goal but a critical component for effective family‐oriented oncology care. Establishing trust‐based relationships enables nurses to obtain more accurate insights into patient and family needs, thereby informing the tailoring of support interventions to align closely with their specific circumstances and priorities.

However, participants referred that there are barriers that hinder the process of building and sustaining this nurse‐family bond. Nurses and observations highlighted that time constraints and inadequate spaces limited meaningful family engagement. Nurses expressed frustration over the limited time available to thoroughly address family concerns, whereas the absence of private spaces often led to interactions occurring in corridors or waiting areas. Studies have identified common barriers, including staffing constraints, high workloads, time pressures, and inadequate physical environments, which hinder effective nurse–family engagement [51]. Addressing these issues results essential to support nurses in establishing and maintaining meaningful relationships with families, thereby enhancing the quality of family‐oriented care. Therefore, prioritising organisational support, staffing, and appropriate environments is fundamental not only to facilitate nurse‐family engagement but also to ensure that interventions targeting family coping can be delivered consistently and effectively.

Findings also underscore the challenges posed by the absence of a structured approach to maintain family conversations in outpatient oncology consultations. Nurses emphasised, and observations further confirmed, that these conversations are often restricted to topics that spontaneously arise during encounters, which may leave some family concerns unaddressed. This lack of standardisation not only reduces the consistency of care but also limits the ability to integrate these family conversations effectively into routine practice [52]. Developing standardised guidelines for family conversations could enhance consistency in care and ensure that nurses systematically address family needs.

In this sense, participants provided insights into how these conversations should be conducted in clinical practice to be meaningful and supportive for families. Participants highlighted the importance of creating a safe and private environment, allowing sufficient time for conversations, and ensuring that nurses acknowledge both the patient's and the family's perspectives. In particular, families valued when nurses facilitated open‐ended discussions that encouraged them to express their concerns without feeling rushed. Nurses also emphasised the need for structured conversations or guidelines that could help them address family concerns systematically, ensuring that no critical aspects of the family's experience were overlooked. These findings suggest that future family support interventions should incorporate these elements into a structured framework for nurse‐led family conversations in oncology outpatient care. Establishing clear guidelines for these interventions could improve the consistency of care and strengthen nurses' role in guiding families through the cancer experience, ensuring that their needs are recognised and addressed in a meaningful way [49, 53].

Strengths and Limitations

This study was conducted in a single cancer centre, which may limit the transferability of its findings to other contexts. However, this allowed for an in‐depth exploration of the specific dynamics within this setting, resulting in rich and detailed insights that are highly relevant to understanding how stakeholders experience cancer and caring in these outpatient oncology consultations. Moreover, this study forms part of a larger co‐design project aimed at developing a family support intervention in oncology [18]. The contextual specificity of this research is therefore necessary, as the intervention is being designed in close alignment with the particular needs of the patients, family members, and nurses in outpatient oncology settings.

A key strength of this study lies in the collaborative approach adopted for both data collection and analysis, which actively involved all members of the research team. Two researchers had prior experience in qualitative research and in studies involving patients, nurses, and family members, whereas the third researcher had more limited experience with qualitative methodologies. Although this variation in methodological expertise could be considered a limitation, it was addressed through ongoing collaboration, systematic discussion of emerging interpretations, and sustained collective reflexivity throughout the analytic process, thereby enhancing consistency and methodological rigor.

All members of the research team were women, a factor that may have influenced interactions with participants and shaped the interpretation of findings from a gendered perspective. In addition, the researchers' professional and personal backgrounds, including clinical nursing experience and personal encounters with cancer, may have contributed to pre‐existing assumptions regarding the phenomenon under investigation. To minimize potential bias, the research team engaged in a continuous reflexive process, approaching the data with openness while explicitly acknowledging and bracketing their prior experiences.

Conclusions

This study offers a comprehensive and original insight into the experiences of patients, family members, and nurses within outpatient oncology care, highlighting the critical role of a family‐oriented approach in addressing the complex challenges associated with cancer. The multi‐method design, which integrated interviews, observations, and field notes, strengthened the findings by capturing the lived experiences, perspectives, and unmet needs of all stakeholders involved.

These findings provide a strong foundation for future research aimed at developing and testing structured family support interventions that enhance families' coping capacities and resilience. In terms of educational development, the results underscore the need to embed family‐oriented care principles within nursing curricula to better prepare nurses to assess and respond to family needs in oncology settings. For clinical practice, the study reinforces the importance of conceptualising the family as the unit of care, systematically assessing family dynamics and needs, and implementing targeted strategies to support both patients and family members across the illness trajectory.

Finally, these findings will directly inform the next phase of a larger experience‐based co‐design (EBCD) study focused on the development of a nurse‐led family support intervention tailored to outpatient oncology consultations.
