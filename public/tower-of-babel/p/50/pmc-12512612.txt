Distinct genomic subgroups and mutational patterns in undifferentiated/dedifferentiated endometrial carcinoma
Authors: Chen-Yang Huang, Angel Chao, Chiao-Yun Lin, Steven G Rozen, An-Shine Chao, Chen-Bin Chang, Hsiu-Jung Tung, Ren-Chin Wu, Chyong-Huey Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometrial cancer represents a significant global health challenge, with undifferentiated and dedifferentiated endometrial carcinoma (UDEC) being a particularly aggressive subset.MethodsWe employed whole-exome sequencing (WES) to comprehensively characterize the molecular landscape of 29 UDECs in a single institution cohort. We analyzed driver mutations, molecular subgroups, SWI/SNF complex, DNA mismatch repair (MMR) pathways, and somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs).ResultsWe identified 5 (17%) ultramutated tumors, 14 (48%) DNA mismatch repair (MMR)-deficient tumors, and 10 (35%) mutation-low tumors in this cohort. Mutations in the SWI/SNF family and other driver genes are common, including PTEN, ARID1A, KMT2B, ARID1B, SMARCA4, and PIK3CA. Notably, ultramutated tumors demonstrated superior survival compared to the other two subtypes.ConclusionsOur analysis revealed distinct architectures and actionable alterations in UDEC. The identification of molecular subgroups provides a promising framework for prognostic stratification. Introduction

Endometrial cancer poses a substantial global health burden, with undifferentiated and dedifferentiated endometrial carcinoma (UDEC) representing a particularly challenging subset comprising 1‒9% of cases [1]. This malignancy may manifest in two distinct morphological patterns: pure undifferentiated carcinoma – which is characterized by dyscohesive tumor cells exhibiting a patternless architecture [2] and dedifferentiated carcinoma – which shows undifferentiated components interspersed with features of low-grade endometrioid carcinoma [1, 3, 4]. The clinical management of UDEC presents several critical issues, primarily stemming from the absence of prognostic markers and actionable targets [5]. Furthermore, the development of effective therapeutic strategies is hindered by both the aggressive nature of the tumor and the absence of standardized treatment protocols – resulting in unfavorable five-year overall survival (OS) rates ranging from 11 to 44% [1].

Despite its clinical significance, UDEC remains underrepresented in gynecologic cancer research. Previous investigations predominantly focused on targeted gene panels and revealed a notable degree of molecular heterogeneity. Genetic alterations identified in UDEC suggest that its pathogenesis involves several gene mutations associated with chromatin remodeling (ARID1A and ARID1B), cell signaling (PIK3CA), tumor suppression (TP53), cell adhesion (CTNNB1), phosphorylation-dependent proteasome degradation of oncoproteins (FBXW7), and the regulation of cell growth and division (PPP2R1A) [6–9]. Notably, approximately 60% of UDEC specimens harbor truncating mutations affecting components of the switch/sucrose non-fermenting (SWI/SNF) chromatin remodeling complex, particularly SMARCA4 and SMARCB1 [6–9]. Deficiencies in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) machinery have been documented in UDEC [6–9].

The pronounced genetic heterogeneity and limited molecular research on UDEC warrant the need for comprehensive genomic characterization. To address this knowledge gap, we employed whole-exome sequencing (WES) on UDEC specimens. Our effort encompassed multiple dimensions, including the identification of driver mutations, analysis of the SWI/SNF remodeling complex, assessment of MMR status, and evaluation of somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs). Additionally, our analytical framework incorporated the delineation of specific tumor subgroups, characterization of molecular signatures, and associations with survival outcomes. Through this approach, we sought to unveil a comprehensive molecular landscape of UDEC, thereby expanding our understanding of its pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Specimens and data

In this retrospective investigation spanning January 2003 through December 2022, we analyzed archived formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens from UDEC cases in our institutional database. Initial screening identified 51 tumor samples with corresponding clinical documentation. Following pathological review and quality assessment, 22 specimens were excluded due to diagnostic reclassification upon expert review (n = 11), suboptimal DNA quality metrics (n = 10), and insufficient tumor material (n = 1). The final analysis comprised 29 tumor specimens that met all inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Disease staging followed the 2023 Fédération Internationale de Gynécologie et d’Obstétrique (FIGO) guidelines [10]. Germline DNA was extracted from peripheral blood; when unavailable, non-neoplastic tissues (including lymph nodes or fallopian tubes) were used as matched controls for sequencing procedures. For dedifferentiated carcinoma cases, sequencing was performed on the undifferentiated components, which were macrodissected to exclude well/moderately differentiated components and non-tumor tissues. All tissue samples and associated clinical data were collected with the approval of the local institutional review board (reference number: 202102450B0). Owing to the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

A retrospective analysis identified patients diagnosed with uterine undifferentiated/dedifferentiated carcinoma (UDEC) from our institutional database, with subsequent molecular analysis performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on a BOND-MAX automated staining platform (Leica Biosystems, Nußloch, Germany) using a panel of antibodies targeting three distinct protein groups. For SWI/SNF nucleosome complex assessment, we utilized antibodies against BRG1 (clone EPNCIR111A, 1:100; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and INI1 (clone 25, 1:200; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Additional chromatin remodeling proteins were evaluated using antibodies against ARID1A (HPA005456 polyclonal, 1:200; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and ARID1B (clone 2D2, 1:200; Abcam). For MMR protein expression analysis, we employed anti-human MLH1 (clone GM011, 1:50; Genemed Biotechnologies, South San Francisco, CA, USA), MSH2 (clone G219-1129, 1:50; Zeta Corporation, Sierra Madre, CA, USA), MSH6 (clone EPR3945, 1:50; Abcam), and PMS2 (clone A16-4, 1:50; BD Biosciences) antibodies. Antigen retrieval was performed using an EDTA-based buffer (BOND Epitope Retrieval Solution 2, pH 9.0; Leica Biosystems) at 100 °C.

DNA extraction and whole-exome sequencing

Genomic DNA extraction was performed on 10-μm FFPE tissue sections using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). For peripheral blood samples, DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen) [11]. Whole-exome sequencing libraries were prepared using the Twist Human Core Exome EF Multiplex Complete Kit Enrichment (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA, USA). The workflow commenced with enzymatic fragmentation of high-quality genomic DNA (50 ng per sample) followed by library construction. The resulting libraries underwent paired-end sequencing on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to achieve whole-exome coverage.

Somatic variant calling

We employed Trimmomatic (version 0.39) to remove adapter-containing and poor-quality sequences. Processed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.15) with default parameters [12, 13]. Alignment quality assessment through Qualimap 2 (version 2.2.1) [14] demonstrated robust sequencing coverage, achieving median depths of 160 × for tumor specimens (range: 95–255 ×) and 136 × for normal samples (range: 74–452 ×) (Supplementary Table S1). We implemented a triple-caller consensus approach utilizing MuTect2 (version 4.1.6.0), Strelka2 (version 2.9.2), and VarScan (version 2.3.9) [15–17] – all executed with default parameters. To ensure high-confidence variant calls, we applied stringent filtering criteria requiring detection by at least two variant callers and established minimum read depth thresholds of 10 × for normal samples and 20 × for tumor samples. Additional variant-specific filtering included mutated allele fraction thresholds of 0.1 for C > T and G > A transitions and 0.05 for other substitutions, along with minimum supporting read requirements of seven reads for C > T and G > A variants and five reads for other variants. The filtered variants underwent annotation using wANNOVAR [18], with subsequent driver gene classification determined based on the COSMIC database [19].

Mutational signature reconstruction

Mutational signature reconstruction was performed using the mSigAct R package (version 2.2) in conjunction with the COSMIC database (version 3.2) [11]. The analysis incorporated single-base substitution (SBS) and indel signatures previously reported in endometrial cancer [20] with specific inclusion of SBS20 – a signature associated with POLD1 mutations. The analysis pipeline followed established methodologies for signature decomposition and attribution, with careful consideration of signature stability and biological plausibility.

Somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs) and biallelic loss calling

SCNAs were analyzed using HATCHet [21], implementing a global clustering approach to infer allele- and clone-specific copy numbers. The analysis pipeline began with the identification of germline heterozygous single nucleotide variants in normal samples using FreeBayes [22]. HATCHet’s matrix factorization framework was employed to jointly analyze read-depth ratios and B-allele frequencies across the genome. Copy-number solutions underwent manual review, with attention to distinguishing competing models. Genome doubling (GD) status was assigned in cases where tetraploid models demonstrated superior fit to the diploid model. We defined biallelic loss as the presence of either two truncating mutations (presumably occurring in trans form), a single truncating mutation with loss of heterozygosity (LOH), or total copy number loss in a gene [23].

Analysis of RPL22 gene mutations

Mutation analysis of the RPL22 gene was performed using a PCR-based approach with the KAPA HiFi HotStart PCR Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). We investigated two specific frameshift mutations exclusively found in tumor samples with MMR deficiency: p.K15Rfs*5 and p.K89Nfs*3. For RPL22K15Rfs*5 detection, we employed custom-designed primers (forward: 5’-TTTATACCTATGACGTTTCT-3’; reverse: 5’-GCATCCATGATTCCATCTTC-3’). The RPL22K89Nfs*3 mutation was analyzed using separate primer pairs (forward: 5’-ATTTTGAGTGCTGGGTTTTA-3’; reverse: 5’-AACTCTCTTTGCTGTTAGCA-3’). The thermal cycling conditions comprised an initial denaturation and hot start at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 20 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Following amplification, PCR products underwent purification and direct Sanger sequencing. Of the 29 samples processed, 26 were successfully sequenced and validated (Supplementary Table S2).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For comparing categorical variables, we employed Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Two primary survival endpoints were evaluated: recurrence-free survival (RFS) and OS. RFS was calculated from the diagnosis of UDEC to disease recurrence or last follow-up, whereas OS was measured from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Patients who presented with metastatic disease or had detectable tumor burden following surgery were excluded from the RFS analysis. Statistical analyses of survival data were performed using the survdiff and coxph functions from the R survival package. To control for multiple comparisons and minimize false discoveries, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Statistical significance thresholds were established at p < 0.05 for primary analyses and q values < 0.1 for multiple comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided.

Results

Clinical data

The study cohort comprised 29 patients with UDEC, with a median age of 53 years (range, 39–80 years). Detailed patient characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S2. At diagnosis, advanced disease (stage III or IV) was observed in 65.5% of patients (n = 19). The median follow-up duration was 22.9 months.

Genomic landscape and driver mutations in UDEC

Comprehensive WES analysis of the UDEC samples unveiled a mutational landscape comprising 196,125 somatic mutations, of which 61,264 were non-silent mutations affecting exonic regions or splice sites of 15,960 genes. The mutational burden varied widely across samples, ranging from 18 to 14,435 (median of 677 per tumor). Analysis of driver genes revealed that PTEN mutations were present in 76% of cases, followed by ARID1A (72%), KMT2B (62%), and PIK3CA (59%) (Fig. 2). Mutations in DNA polymerase genes – including four POLE exonuclease domain mutations (two V411L, one P436R, and one A456P) and a POLD1 D402N mutation – were also noted.

The oncoplot illustrates the distribution of tumor mutational burden (TMB, per MB), patient age, FIGO stage, whole genome doubling (WGD) status, genome instability index, and non-silent somatic alterations in established cancer-driver genes

Tumor suppressor genes predominantly harbored truncating mutations, particularly affecting PTEN, ARID1A, ARID1B, and TP53 (Fig. 3). Five tumors with MMR deficiency had frameshift mutation ARID1AD1850Tfs*33, resulting from a single base deletion in a G-homopolymer region. In the PIK3CA gene, a total of 24 distinct mutations were identified, including several actionable variants [24]: H1047R (three samples), E545K (two samples), E542K (one sample), and C420R (one sample) (Fig. 3C). Additionally, nine tumors displayed KRAS G12 mutations, distributed as G12D (three samples), G12V (three samples), G12C (two samples), and G12A (one sample) (Fig. 3E).

UDEC molecular subgroups

We classified UDECs into three molecular subgroups based on mutational signatures and burden, as follows: ultramutated (n = 5; 17.2%), mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR, n = 14; 48.3%), and mutation-low (n = 10; 34.5%) tumors. The ultramutated subgroup, characterized by mutational signatures indicative of polymerase proofreading deficiency (POLE or POLD1 mutation in the exonuclease domains), exhibited a median mutation count of 6,477 per sample (range: 4,738–14,435). The dMMR subgroup, defined by dMMR-associated mutational signatures and a high proportion of indels, exhibited a median mutation count of 978 per tumor (range: 236 − 9,511). Among these 14 dMMR UDECs, 13 specimens showed loss of MMR protein expression based on the IHC results (Supplementary Table S2). Concurrent loss of MLH1 and PMS2 expression was observed in most specimens (11 of 14, 78.6%). Germline pathogenic variants in the MLH1 and MSH2 genes were identified in five patients (Supplementary Table S2), whereas somatic pathogenic variants in MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 were detected in eight UDECs. In contrast, the mutation-low subgroup was characterized by substantially lower mutational burden, with a median of 46 non-silent mutations (range: 18–86) per tumor (Fig. 2). To evaluate the clinical relevance of our proposed three-group classification system, we conducted comparative analyses against established endometrial cancer classification frameworks (WHO and TCGA) [25, 26]. The prognostic outcomes and tumor classification concordance across these three different approaches are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1−2.

Genomic inactivation of the SWI/SNF remodeling complex genes

Genomic inactivation of the SWI/SNF complex proteins is a common feature of endometrial cancer [27, 28]. In the study cohort, biallelic loss of SWI/SNF complex components was identified in 19 of 29 cases (65.5%), specifically ARID1A, ARID1B, SMARCA4, or SMARCB1 genes. Across subgroups, ARID1A biallelic loss was significantly more frequent in the ultramutated (60.0%, 3/5) and dMMR (57%, 8/14) subgroups (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.039) (Fig. 4). MMR gene expression and mutational pattern in dMMR tumors

Indels within homopolymer sequences, a hallmark of microsatellite instability [29], were frequently identified in dMMR UDEC samples (Fig. 5). Among these mutations, the RPL22K15Rfs*5 mutation was present in 10 of the 14 dMMR tumors (71.4%), and the RPL22K89Nfs*3 mutation was detected in a tumor harboring a POLD1 mutation and dMMR. Both alterations arose from single-base pair deletions within T-homopolymer tracts at different loci, resulting in truncating mutations in the RPL22 gene. The RPL22K15Rfs*5 mutations were validated through Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Table S2). Other homopolymer mutations, including SETD1BH8Tfs*27 and SPRED3T54Pfs*57, were each identified in eight dMMR tumors. In contrast, such indels in homopolymers were nearly absent from the mutation-low tumors (Fig. 5). Supplementary Table S4 details all the indels at microsatellite sequences identified in this study. These findings highlight the unique gene mutations associated with MMR deficiency, which could be potentially used as biomarkers to identify UDECs with dMMR.

Characterization of molecular signatures

Mutational signature analysis was performed to elucidate the underlying mutational processes in UDEC and revealed distinct mutational patterns across the tumors (Fig. 6A). Supplementary table S5 lists the mutational activities of the study cohort. The median SBS per tumor was 1,337 (range 57–45,618, Fig. 6B). Clock-like mutational signatures (SBS1 and SBS5) emerged as the predominant activities, manifesting in 86.2% (25/29) of tumors. As expected, four ultramutated specimens exhibited mutations characteristic of POLE proofreading deficiency (SBS10a and SBS10b), with mutation counts of 8,632 (range 6,887–12,598). The remaining ultramutated specimen displayed POLD1-associated mutational activity (SBS20).

Mutational signature analysis across UDEC specimens. (A) Principal component analysis revealing clustering patterns based on single-base substitution (SBS; left) and insertion-deletion (ID; right) signatures. (B) Quantitative assessment of mutational activity and proportional contribution of distinct signatures to each tumor, with complete signature activities detailed in Supplementary Table S5

Consistent with previous findings [30], the POLD1-mutated tumor showed a high TMB – comparable to that observed in POLE-mutated tumors. Additionally, the mutational spectrum of the POLD1-mutated UDEC showed high cosine similarity with the POLD1-associated mutational signature, supporting its inclusion in the ultramutated group (Supplementary Table S6). We identified dMMR signatures – encompassing SBS6, SBS14, SBS15, SBS26, and SBS44 – in a total of 17 samples (58.6%), including specimens harboring concurrent POLE (two cases) and POLD1 (one case) mutations. This finding reinforces previous observations regarding the potential co-occurrence of dMMR-associated mutational processes with polymerase proofreading deficiencies [31].

Associations with survival outcomes

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that ultramutated tumors had significantly better clinical outcomes, characterized by more favorable RFS (log-rank test, p = 0.0006, Fig. 7A) and OS (log-rank test, p = 0.005, Fig. 7B) than dMMR or mutation-low tumors. Taken together, our findings suggested that ultramutated tumors and dMMR tumors presented with distinct mutational processes and clinical trajectories.

Discussion

Through sequencing and profiling of 29 UDEC specimens, we uncovered several critical insights into the genomic architecture of this aggressive malignancy – yielding findings with potential clinical implications. First, the tumor mutational profile was characterized by frequent alterations in key driver genes, with PTEN mutations identified in 76% of cases, followed by ARID1A, KMT2B, and PIK3CA. Three distinct molecular subgroups were classified in UDEC: ultramutated tumors driven by POLE or POLD1 mutations [32], dMMR tumors [33], and mutation-low tumors. Second, biallelic inactivation of SWI/SNF complex components was observed in two-thirds of cases, underscoring the pivotal role of chromatin remodeling dysfunction in the pathogenesis of UDEC. In parallel, a high prevalence of dMMR status (48.3%) was detected, predominantly associated with concurrent loss of MLH1 and PMS2 expression. Of note, the RPL22K15Rfs*5 frameshift mutation was identified in 71.4% of dMMR tumors. Third, survival analyses demonstrated that ultramutated tumors were associated with more favorable RFS and OS compared to other molecular subgroups. These prognostic associations are in accordance with prior observations in endometrial carcinoma [33], thus substantiating the clinical relevance of genetically informed classifications in gynecologic oncology.

From a molecular standpoint, our study revealed that the molecular mechanisms underlying UDEC are characterized by the disrupted signaling pathways and alterations of chromatin structure. Specifically, we identified recurrent mutations in PTEN and PIK3CA genes – which result in the aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade – along with aberrations in the chromatin remodeling factor ARID1A, together with alterations in the histone methyltransferase KMT2B. Collectively, these molecular perturbations – coupled with the high prevalence of biallelic inactivation of SWI/SNF complex components – can induce significant modifications in tumor chromatin architecture and nuclear organization, possibly incubating an altered epigenetic landscape. Given the prevalence and recurrence of chromatin-modifying alterations in our findings, we propose that UDEC should be considered a malignancy driven by chromatin dysregulation.

Another interesting finding was the high prevalence (14 of 29, 48.3%) of dMMR tumors in UDEC. Notably, the RPL22K15Rfs*5 frameshift mutation emerged as a common feature in dMMR UDEC. This finding is in accordance with prior studies showing that RPL22 – which potentiates p53 tumor suppressor activity – is commonly mutated across various dMMR malignancies [34–36]. Furthermore, we identified two additional frameshift mutations – SETD1BH8Tfs*27 and SPRED3T54Pfs*57 – that were also frequently present in dMMR UDECs. The SETD1BH8Tfs*27 mutation affects histone H3K4 methylation processes, which could lead to disrupted gene expression and genome instability [37]. The SPRED3T54Pfs*57 mutation may impact MAPK signaling regulation and cell proliferation [38]. The frameshift mutations observed in dMMR tumors likely result from microsatellite instability – the characteristic genomic consequence of mismatch repair deficiency [39]. The exclusive presence of these mutations in dMMR cases highlights their value as potential biomarkers in UDEC molecular classification.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings hold potential implications for prognostic stratification and personalized treatment. The survival advantage associated with the ultramutated and dMMR phenotypes can be attributed to the high mutational burden, which generates numerous cancer-associated neoantigens. This occurrence may stimulate a robust anti-tumor immune response, potentially underpinning the observed better outcomes. Endometrial cancer is traditionally classified into four molecular subtypes [25, 26]. Previous studies have specifically investigated UDEC molecular heterogeneity using targeted approaches. Accordingly, Rosa-Rosa et al. [9] analyzed 18 UDECs through targeted gene sequencing and fluorescent in situ hybridization, while Espinosa et al. [40] performed molecular profiling of 21 UDECs using targeted panel sequencing and immunohistochemical staining. Although we did not develop a novel classification framework, our study provides a comprehensive genomic analysis of UDEC to date and presents an alternative approach to molecular classification through whole-exome sequencing.

From a therapeutic standpoint, UDEC with dMMR may benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [41]. Recent clinical trials demonstrated that combining ICIs with standard chemotherapy can improve outcomes in patients with aggressive uterine malignancies [42, 43]. Given that solid tumors with POLE mutations have been associated with favorable responses to immunotherapy [44], it is plausible that dMMR UDECs may benefit from ICI-based approaches. Importantly, the identification of clinically actionable PIK3CA mutations (H1047R and E545K) in five UDEC samples suggests potential suitability for alpelisib [45], a selective PI3Kα inhibitor [46, 47]. Collectively, these observations highlight the potential therapeutic targets in UDEC.

We acknowledge that our genomic analysis was constrained by the limited sample size from a single institution. Validation of the findings from a larger cohort is necessary. Given the relative rarity of this entity, establishing a research consortium would provide the requisite power to generate robust conclusions. Such collaborative frameworks will be paramount to facilitate the expeditious translation of genomic discoveries into clinical applications, enabling refined prognostic stratification and molecularly guided therapeutic interventions.

Our WES analysis of UDEC reveals a complex molecular landscape characterized by distinct architectural patterns and clinically relevant alterations. The identification of three subgroups with distinct mutational profiles and survival outcomes provides a promising framework for prognostic stratification. Notably, our findings not only advance our understanding of UDEC biology but also illuminate potential therapeutic opportunities, particularly in the context of immunotherapy and targeted treatments. Large-scale international collaborations will be essential to validate these molecular insights and translate them into personalized treatment strategies.
