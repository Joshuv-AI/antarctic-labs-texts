Efficacy and safety of transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation in patients with acute mountain sickness: a study protocol for a double-blind, randomised, sham-controlled trial
Authors: Yu-kun Tian, Xin Wang, Xue Cao, Hong-ming Ji, Bi-Qing Wang, Jia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute mountain sickness (AMS) commonly occurs during rapid ascent to high altitudes and significantly affects the health and cognitive function of travellers and specialised personnel in high-altitude environments. Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a non-invasive, safe and portable neuromodulation technique with potential benefits for AMS. This protocol evaluates taVNS efficacy for AMS and associated cognitive impairment and explores its mechanisms via multiomics analyses.Methods and analysisThis is a single-centre, randomised, double-blind, sham-controlled study involving ≥158 participants with AMS, who will be randomly assigned to receive either active or sham taVNS for five consecutive days. The primary outcome is the change in the 2018 Lake Louise AMS Score at 24 hours. Secondary outcomes are the Visual Analogue Scale for headache, Stanford Sleepiness Scale, Groningen Sleep Quality Questionnaire, resting heart rate, resting blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, cerebral oxygen saturation, Corsi task score and the Picture Recall Subscale of the Wechsler Adult Memory Scale, which collectively assess symptom relief, physiological responses and cognitive function. The exploratory outcomes include faecal and urinary metabolomics.Ethics and disseminationAll investigations performed in this study were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Basic Research in Clinical Medicine, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (reference number: P24013-PJ13). The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and feedback for the study participants.Trial registration numberhttp://itmctr.ccebtcm.org.cn/; number: TMCTR2024000642.

Introduction

Acute mountain sickness (AMS) comprises non-specific symptoms that occur after rapid ascent to high altitudes before acclimatisation, including headaches, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms and dizziness. It typically manifests within 6–12 hours of exposure to elevations above 2500 m, with prevalence increasing with altitude, ranging from approximately 7% at 2200 m to 50–85% at 4500–5500 m.1 AMS typically resolves within 1 week of arrival at high altitude2; however, some cases may progress to severe altitude-related illnesses that can be fatal.3 Additionally, reduced atmospheric pressure and oxygen partial pressure during rapid ascent to high altitude can impair cognitive processing, attention and other brain functions, posing risks for individuals working at high elevations and mountaineers.4

Acetazolamide is widely recommended5 6; however, its use is limited by side effects, including limb numbness7 and potential cognitive impairment at high altitude.8 Therefore, wearable medical devices that can alleviate AMS warrant exploration, particularly for mountaineers and others who are undertaking rapid ascent.

In our previous clinical study, electroacupuncture significantly reduced Lake Louise AMS Score (LLS) compared with sham acupuncture.9 Metabolomic analysis of saliva before and after the intervention, and between groups, followed by pathway analysis, revealed that bilirubin, 5-aminoacetyltyrosine, D-ornithine and D-serine were the primary metabolites altered by electroacupuncture, providing insights into the mechanism underlying physical therapy in AMS. In a urinary metabolomic study by Lou et al,10 increased purine metabolites were associated with hypoxia, suggesting that 1-methyladenosine may serve as a highly sensitive biomarker for cellular hypoxia. Sibomana et al11 correlated urinary metabolomic findings with AMS susceptibility and severity. Although the small sample size precludes its use as an AMS biomarker, these findings provide insights into the physiological processes underlying AMS. Recent studies have indicated significant differences in the gut microbiota composition among high-altitude indigenous Tibetan, lowland indigenous Han Chinese and plateau-migrant Han Chinese populations.12 Moreover, distinct variations in bacterial genus abundance were observed at different altitudes.13 The gut microbiota, represented by the genus Prevotella, can regulate human acclimatisation to high altitudes by influencing pathways such as the renin-angiotensin system and glucose regulation, thereby contributing to the mechanisms underlying human high-altitude adaptation.

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that has shown promising results in treating neurological and psychiatric disorders.14 15 Although no reports currently exist on taVNS for high-altitude sickness, its efficacy has been demonstrated in the prevention and treatment of migraine headaches,1618 functional dyspepsia,19 high-altitude sleep disorders20 and refractory hypertension.21 Several studies have indicated its potential to enhance cognitive function.2224 The significant potential of taVNS in treating AMS, preventing cognitive impairment and improving the prognosis of AMS warrants further investigation.

Therefore, the present study aims to conduct a randomised, controlled, blinded trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of taVNS in patients with AMS. Additionally, faecal and urinary samples will be collected for metagenomic and metabolomic analyses, respectively, to further explore the mechanisms underlying the improvement of AMS symptoms.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This single-centre, prospective, double-blind, sham-controlled trial evaluates the effects and safety of taVNS in AMS. Two groups of participants, randomly assigned to a central randomisation system, are subjected to two stimulation conditions: taVNS and sham taVNS.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public are not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Study population and recruitment

In this study, we will recruit participants within 48 hours of high-altitude exposure at Tibet University, along with healthy control volunteers residing in the plains (Beijing, 43.5 m above sea level) from March 2025 to 2026. All participants sign an informed consent form before inclusion in the study. To acknowledge the time and inconvenience associated with study participation, all participants will receive compensation of ¥100 on completion.

Our preliminary research found that the LLS score decreased by 2.04±0.5 within 24 hours after sham acupuncture. Based on preliminary pilot studies and expert opinions, the LLS score is projected to decline by 2.6±1.25 at 24 hours post-treatment. With a two-tailed α=0.05 and 90% power, PASS V.15.0 software calculations indicate a minimum sample size of 126 participants (63 per group). After accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the final sample size is 158 participants. To control for learning effects in repeated cognitive assessments, an additional 79 healthy volunteers will be recruited to complete the same cognitive battery without any intervention.

Interventions

The taVNS methodology follows that described by Rong et al,15 as shown in figure 1. Bilateral auricular placement uses clamps with identical appearances. Each clamp features three carbon-impregnated silicone electrodes: one serves as a common contact on the posterior auricular surface, whereas the remaining two target distinct cutaneous sites, the concha and scapha. Crucially, only one of the two stimulating electrodes is functionally active per clamp. Within the verum taVNS group, the superior electrode (denoted by red markers) is connected to the transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation device. By contrast, the inferior electrode (marked in blue) remains electrically inactive. For the sham taVNS, the electrodes are energised in opposite directions to prevent stimulation of the vagus nerve.

Electrode placement for taVNS and sham taVNS. taVNS, transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation.

Stimulation, whether in the taVNS or sham taVNS groups, is administered using an electronic acupuncture treatment device (SDZ-IIB model) connected to specially made ear-clip leads. The participants’ auricles are routinely disinfected using 75% ethanol. Specially designed conductive silicone ear clips are secured to the ipsilateral auricular concha and connected to the same lead, delivering sparse–dense waves at 4/20 Hz with a current intensity ≤20 mA. During the initial session, the researcher gradually increases the current intensity while monitoring participants’ sensations and ceased adjustment once the maximum tolerable level is reached. Each stimulation lasted for 30 min, two times a day, for five consecutive days. Sham taVNS procedures and stimulation parameters are identical to those used in the verum group.

All the participants receive standardised training for self-administration. They were informed of the possibility of perceiving electrical stimulation and possible therapeutic outcomes. The training includes stimulator activation/deactivation, electrode placement on the concha and scapha, localisation of auricular stimulation sites, intensity adjustment and completion of an online diary using the WeChat† platform. Subsequent treatments are performed independently by the participants in their home environment. The participants are further instructed to document each treatment session immediately after administration, record the precise date and time and detail any adverse events that are temporally associated with the intervention. To enhance adherence, the research staff will send automated reminder messages before each scheduled session, and the investigator will verify all submitted diary entries two times a day. Treatment course completion is defined as attending at least 80% (8/10) of all the intervention sessions. Participants who discontinue the intervention will be encouraged to complete the outcome assessment whenever possible.

†WeChat is a multipurpose messaging, social media and mobile payment application (https://web.wechat.com/), used in this study for electronic diary functionality.

No intervention is administered to the healthy volunteers. They will undergo only the Corsi task and Picture Recall Subscale assessments of the Wechsler Adult Memory Scale, with the same follow-up schedule as the experimental group. Additionally, one faecal sample is collected for metagenomic analysis of the intestinal flora.

Emergency management

Considering the unique high-altitude environment, comprehensive preparations will be undertaken to manage potential emergencies. The specific emergency measures are as follows:

If participants experience intolerable AMS symptoms, the following measures will be implemented immediately:

Headache: First-line non-opioid analgesics (eg, ibuprofen or acetaminophen) are used for pain relief. Therapeutic doses are determined based on patient weight and disease severity. If the headache severely impairs daily activities, pain relief should be reassessed within 6 hours of the initial administration, and dose adjustment or switching to other analgesics may be required. When headache symptoms persist or worsen, oxygen saturation should be measured. If hypoxaemia is present, supplemental oxygen therapy should be considered.

Dyspepsia, nausea and vomiting: It should be first determined whether these symptoms are attributable to acute high-altitude sickness. Once the cause is identified, appropriate aetiological treatment will be administered, or the patient will be referred to a gastroenterologist if necessary. Adequate fluid intake should be ensured to prevent dehydration; if necessary, the patient may be recommended to receive intravenous rehydration at a hospital. If gastrointestinal symptoms do not improve after 24 hours of treatment, other causes of gastrointestinal diseases should be ruled out, and further examinations should be conducted.

Assessment of symptom deterioration: If symptoms worsen or fail to improve after 1–2 days of appropriate intervention, participants should be transferred to a qualified local hospital for further evaluation, including assessment of oxygen saturation, ECG and lung auscultation results. Timely communication with patients and their families is required to ensure that they understand the condition and the potential risks, and prompt medical consultation at a qualified local hospital is advised. If a participant presents with evidence of HAPE, defined as severe cough, dyspnoea at rest, cyanosis and rales, or HACE, defined as ataxia, altered consciousness or both, clinicians with licensed physician qualifications will evaluate the case and arrange immediate transfer to a qualified local hospital for further management. During transfer, medical personnel must accompany the participants throughout the process, and the vehicle should be equipped with the necessary first-aid facilities and medications. Participants transferred to the hospital will be continuously followed up by researchers regarding changes in their condition, and a comprehensive health assessment will be required after recovery.

Documentation and reporting of emergency management: Each emergency treatment and intervention must be thoroughly documented in the patient’s Case Report Form, including the types of drugs used, doses, patient responses and subsequent treatment measures. If a serious adverse event (SAE) is identified, it must be reported to the Ethics Committee within 24 hours.

Randomisation

The participants were centrally randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the experimental or control group using a web-based interactive response system provided by the Clinical Evaluation Centre of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. The system generated a unique randomisation number for each eligible participant and assigned them to the treatment groups (active or sham), ensuring allocation concealment.

Blinding

This study uses a double-blind design. An independent designer prepared two types of ear clips that are identical in appearance: one for active taVNS and one for sham stimulation. Each clip is assigned a unique identification number. After providing informed consent, each participant will receive a sequential number from the central randomisation system, which is linked to the identification number of the allocated ear clip. Only the independent designer will hold the allocation record, and unblinding will occur only when necessary. Investigators, data entry personnel, acupuncturists, outcome assessors, and statisticians are blinded to the group assignment.

To ensure successful blinding of the participants, the active and sham devices are identical in appearance and operation, as both produce the same auditory signal and use identical stimulation parameters. The only difference lies in the electrode placement; the active device stimulates the cymba conchae, whereas the sham device is applied to the scapha, an area not innervated by the vagus nerve. Both placements generate similar subjective sensations such as mild tingling or warmth, making them indistinguishable from the participant. The participants will be informed that both types of ear clips could potentially improve AMS-related symptoms or cognitive function. The success of blinding is assessed after the final intervention, as described in the blinding assessment subsection.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is improvement in LLS after 24 hours of treatment compared with baseline.

Secondary outcomes

The Visual Analogue Scale for headache and the Stanford Sleepiness Scale are assessed at baseline and at 6, 12, 48, 72, 96 and 120 hours after treatment. The Groningen Sleep Quality Questionnaire score, resting heart rate and resting blood pressure are measured at baseline and at 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 hours after treatment. Peripheral oxygen saturation, cerebral oxygen saturation, Corsi task25 score and the Picture Recall Subscale of the Wechsler Adult Memory Scale26 are assessed before treatment and at 24 and 120 hours after treatment and compared with baseline.

It is necessary to describe the scoring methods used for the two cognitive tests. The Corsi task is scored using the PsyToolkit (a free-to-use toolkit for demonstrating, programming and running cognitive-psychological experiments and surveys; https://www.psytoolkit.org). In this implementation, the computer screen starts with a sequence of two blocks. After the sequence is displayed, participants will hear the word ‘go’ and click the blocks with the mouse in the same order as shown previously. If the response is correct, the participant proceeds to the following sequence, with an increased number of blocks. If an error occurs, a second attempt is allowed; if the participant makes another error, the Corsi block span is recorded as the final score.

In the Picture Recall subscale of the Wechsler Adult Memory Scale, the researchers display 20 pictures of varying content to the participants for 1 min 30 s. Subsequently, all images are covered, and the participants are asked to recall the contents. One point is deducted for each incorrect answer and one point is awarded for each correct answer. In the picture recall subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, the researchers present 20 distinct images to the participants for 1 min 30 s. Subsequently, all images are obscured and the participants are asked to recall the contents. Each incorrect response deducts one point, while each correct response adds one point.

Adverse events

Any reported abnormalities will be evaluated by two independent blinded reviewers with access to event details and medical history. Confirmed adverse events were documented using an official adverse event report form. The severity of these events is rated on a 3-point Likert scale ranging from mild to severe. SAEs are defined as death, life-threatening conditions, prolonged hospitalisation or permanent and severe disability.

Exploratory outcomes

Exploratory outcome measures included pretreatment and post-treatment changes in gut microbiota metagenomic profiles and differences in urinary metabolomics between baseline and 24 hours post-treatment.

Samples of the gut microbiota are collected before and 120 hours after the first intervention. Trained staff collect faecal samples and place 5 g of mid-passage fresh faeces into 30 mL cryovials containing the preservative solution. The samples are stored at −80 °C within 12 hours of collection. Data analysis focuses on the effects of the intervention on the intestinal flora diversity and relative abundance at the species and genus levels. Faecal metabolomic analysis is employed to examine microbial composition and function, assess whether taVNS intervention promotes adaptive changes in the gut microbiota and identify potential metabolic pathways.

Data management and monitoring

All data are managed using an electronic data capture (EDC) system developed by the Institute of Basic Research in Clinical Medicine, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. Data entry is independently performed by two trained research assistants at each study site, with all entries subjected to dual verification. The EDC system incorporates automated validation checks to identify typographical errors, missing data and inconsistencies in real-time.

Discrepancies are resolved by cross-referencing source documents, reassessing participant records and reaching a consensus through team discussions. To ensure protocol adherence and timely detection of adverse events, the principal investigator and study coordinator will conduct daily reviews of the participants’ electronic diary entries submitted via the WeChat platform.

Before the final analysis, an independent statistician, not involved in the trial, will perform interim quality checks on the locked database to identify any anomalous data patterns or inconsistencies.

Statistical methods

Primary and secondary outcomes

All analyses follow the intention-to-treat principle and include all randomised participants in their originally assigned groups. Descriptive statistics are presented as means and SD for normally distributed continuous variables, medians and IQRs for non-normally distributed variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables.

For the primary outcome, the change in the 2018 LLS from baseline to 24 hours will be compared between the taVNS and sham groups using analysis of covariance with adjustment for the baseline score. Secondary continuous outcomes will be analysed using linear regression or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the distribution. Categorical outcomes are compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. For repeated measurements over the 5-day intervention period, a generalised estimating equation model is used to assess group-by-time interactions. A two-sided significance level of α=0.05 will be applied.

Subgroup analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses will examine whether the effects of treatment on the primary outcome differ according to age, sex and educational level. Age is dichotomised using the sample median. Educational level is categorised as junior college or below versus bachelor’s degree or above. For each subgroup, a linear regression model including a treatment-by-subgroup interaction term is used. Interaction p values will be reported, and results will be interpreted as hypothesis-generating due to limited power.

Blinding assessment

To evaluate the success of blinding, the participants will be asked to guess their treatment allocation (active taVNS, sham or uncertain) immediately after the final intervention. The proportion of correct guesses in each group is reported. Blinding success is considered adequate if the overall proportion of correct guesses does not significantly exceed 50%, as assessed using a one-sample binomial test. Exploratory analyses will also be used to calculate the Bang Blinding Index for each group. If blinding is found to be suboptimal, the potential impact on the outcome assessment will be addressed in the discussion.

Handling of non-adherence and missing data

The primary analysis follows the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomised participants in their originally assigned groups, regardless of adherence level. For ITT analysis, missing outcome data will be handled using multiple imputation or sensitivity analyses with best-worst-case scenarios.

A per-protocol (PP) sensitivity analysis will include only participants who complete at least 80% of the scheduled taVNS sessions and have no major protocol violations. Consistency between the ITT and PP analyses is assessed, and if the conclusions differ, the results are interpreted with caution.

Multiomics analyses

For urinary metabolomics, data preprocessing will be performed using XCMS in R. Principal component analysis and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis will be used to visualise group separation. Metabolites with variable importance in projection >1 and p<0.05 will be considered potential biomarkers. Pathway enrichment will be conducted using MetaboAnalyst.

For faecal metagenomics, sequencing will be performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. After quality trimming and assembly using MEGAHIT, gene prediction and annotation will be carried out using Prodigal and DIAMOND against public databases. Differences in species and functional abundance between groups will be tested using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Detailed bioinformatics procedures are provided in the online supplemental material.

Discussion

Several clinical studies have suggested the potential of taVNS in managing conditions associated with specific environmental stressors.27 AMS can be viewed as a symptom complex arising from maladaptation to extreme environments characterised by low atmospheric pressure and hypoxia. This incomplete acclimatisation is mediated mainly by dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system.28 Notably, taVNS has been shown to modulate autonomic function, and vagus nerve activation may help alleviate common AMS symptoms such as headaches29 30 and gastrointestinal symptoms.3133 As a non-invasive intervention, taVNS is considered a promising candidate for supporting high-altitude travellers and workers.

To substantiate its efficacy and safety, we will conduct a prospective, double-blind, randomised, sham-controlled trial to minimise bias and generate high-quality evidence on the effect of taVNS on LLS scores in patients with AMS. Secondary outcomes will include their influence of taVNS on cognitive performance and sleep quality. Urinary metabolomic and faecal metagenomic analyses will be performed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying taVNS. The non-invasive nature of sample collection also makes these biomarkers potential tools for future therapeutic evaluations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, considering the risk of AMS in patients, we did not impose restrictions on oxygen use or seeking medical care outside of the study setting. Instead, we meticulously documented the oxygen and medication use during medical visits and accounted for these factors in the statistical analyses. However, this approach may have influenced the efficacy of taVNS. Second, the study was primarily conducted in a university setting, where participants’ ages and educational backgrounds may limit the generalisability and broad applicability of the findings.

Trial status
