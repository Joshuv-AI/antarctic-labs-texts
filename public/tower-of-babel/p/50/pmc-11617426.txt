Management of Infected Dentigerous Cyst in an 8-year-old Patient Using Decompression Technique: A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Laresh N Mistry, Shantanu S Deshpande, Saudamini G More
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOdontogenic cysts can be treated in a number of ways, mostly aggressively by enucleation, less frequently conservatively. One such therapeutic approach is cyst decompression. It entails releasing the intraluminal pressure inside the cystic cavity, which shrinks the cyst and avoids the need for a more radical method of managing large cysts. This article presents the case study of an 8-year-old male child with an infected odontogenic cyst, treated with a unique decompression approach.How to cite this articleMistry LN, Deshpande SS, More SG. Introduction

Odontogenic cysts, originating from the odontogenic epithelium, develop in teeth-containing jaw regions. They are caused by the proliferation or degeneration of epithelium and can have developmental, inflammatory, odontogenic, or nonepithelial origins.1 Periapical cysts are the most common type of odontogenic cyst, accounting for 60% of all cases. They are primarily found in the maxilla. Dentigerous cysts make up 20.6% of all odontogenic cysts, accounting for 20% of all cysts on the mandible. The frequency of these cysts increases in the second and third decades of life.2

The treatment of odontogenic cystic lesions is controversial, with options including endodontic retreatment, apicoectomy, tooth extraction, or surgery to enucleate the lesion. Surgical enucleation is the fastest and most definitive treatment, but it may cause harmful side effects to the patient. Since 1892, researchers have been studying the marsupialization of odontogenic cystic lesions, with Thoma first reporting its decompression in 1958. This process involves everting and suturing the cyst lining to the surrounding mucosa, creating an open opening. Decompression is any method that lowers intraluminal pressure while keeping the opening open.3 Thus, mechanistically marsupialization may be similar to decompression but technically they do differ.

Decompression of odontogenic cysts is beneficial for pediatric and elderly patients. Catheterization involves rubber catheters, nasopharyngeal airways, intravenous tubing, nasal cannula tubing, and tuberculin syringes for large cysts containing important structures.3 The histological changes in the cystic capsule are much more discrete if it is compared with the changes in the marsupialization. This procedure eliminates the intramural pressure, activating bone formation within the lesion. This treatment requires monitoring and compliance by the patient.1 The following case report highlights the use of a novel decompression technique to treat an infected cyst in a child.

Case Description

History

An 8-year-old boy reported swelling in his lower left back region for 2 months, accompanied by increased pain. He had repeated swellings in the past 2–3 years, with periods of remission with frequent antibiotic usage. An extraoral examination revealed firm, diffuse swelling with no color change but a definite change in contour and consistency. Submandibular lymph nodes on the same side were enlarged, palpable, and tender. Intraoral examination revealed root stumps with 74 and 75. The left buccal vestibule was obliterated and the buccal plate on palpation exhibited eggshell cracking. A midline shift toward the buccal position and away from the contacts indicated potentially expanding pathology leading to migration (Fig. 1).

Intraoral examination of the child

The orthopantomogram (OPG) was initially advised by a private practitioner which showed a clearly defined radiolucent picture, extending from the mesial aspect of 75 to the distal aspect of 36, up to the inferior border of the mandible including a significant portion of the mandibular basal bone (Fig. 2). A spherical radiolucent lesion was detected through axial slices of a cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) of about 17.8 mm × 15.6 mm causing displacement of the mandibular canal toward the base of the mandible with a discontinuous gap in the buccal wall (Fig. 3).

The periapical radiolucent lesion was unilocular, measuring approximately 35 mm in length and 20 mm in breadth, and extending from the mesial aspect of tooth 75 to the cementoenamel junction to the distal aspect of the root of tooth 36, and almost reaching the lower border of the jaw

Multiple treatment options were discussed with the patient which included surgical enucleation, marsupialization, and decompression following extraction of 74 and 75. Routine blood investigations such as complete blood count (CBC), bleeding time (BT), and clotting time (CT) were advised after a discussion and decision-making process.

Diagnostic impressions were made and casts were created, with teeth 74 and 75 mechanically trimmed to gingiva level and Hawley's retainer was prepared. Teeth were extracted using a periosteal elevator after inferior alveolar nerve block anesthesia with 1:80,000 adrenaline. A small cystic lining portion was sent for histopathological examination without attempting to remove the entire lesion. A window was created in the acrylic plate at 75 and an endotracheal tube was inserted to reach the lesion's base. The tube was stabilized with cold cure acrylic and trimmed to the base plate level. Multiple composite plugs were prepared and given to the patient to prevent food particles from ingress. The patient was instructed on self-irrigation using a disposable syringe and needle with povidone-iodine solution as demonstrated by the operator for 3 days (Fig. 4). After 1 week of irrigation, most of the lesion healed, except near the tube lumen. The histopathological report revealed the cyst to be an infected dentigerous cyst (Fig. 5).

Histopathology report

It was important to understand that compliance faded in the COVID-19 pandemic from the patient's end. The patient was recalled for a 6- month follow-up but due to the COVID-19 pandemic returned after 12 months. A new OPG revealed mesial migration of premolars in the cystic cavity (Fig. 6). A new acrylic plate was constructed and the patient repeated the self-rinsing with betadine solution for 3 months after which a lingual arch with U-loop was constructed to compensate for space loss (Fig. 7). The final follow-up revealed the healing of the entire cystic cavity and eruption of first and second premolars within 18 months. This was confirmed by OPG and CBCT (Fig. 8). Discussion

Decompression and marsupialization are conservative treatments that lower pressure within a cyst and promote bone development, providing effective surgical access to lesions with minimal postoperative morbidity and intraoperative difficulties. Decompression has been used in numerous cases since its introduction, with success rates varying based on the cyst.4,5 Studies show no significant variations in the decrease rate based on the histologic diagnosis. Decompression time and size change are not set standards, but earlier research has shown a 65% reduction in size with maintenance over 8.4 months and an 81% reduction with 17.5 months of decompression.6

Decompression devices, or tubes, are used to drain pathological lesions in the jaws and facilitate their re-irrigation. In this case, tubes were created by cutting off a piece of tubing from a regular endotracheal tube. This tubing is not collapsible, easily accessible, and has a large diameter to prevent clogging. It can be adjusted for patient comfort and is suitable for easy insertion and removal.

Decompression and marsupialization are conservative treatments that lower pressure within cysts and promote bone development. They offer minimal postoperative morbidity and intraoperative difficulties, maintain pulp vitality in intracystic teeth, reduce lesion size, and reduce recurrence. This minimally invasive technique is less prone to complications than surgery, but requires extensive patient cooperation and is time-consuming. Decompression and irrigation therapy have been found to positively impact the histological features of cysts, with 64% of patients showing dedifferentiation and reduced cytokeratin-10 production after 9 months of treatment. The study examined 14 decompressed odontogenic keratocysts, revealing an average reduction of 65% in the radiolucent area. The alterations in cystic epithelium occur gradually, suggesting a 9-month treatment duration may be necessary for epithelial dedifferentiation.6

Schlieve et al. conducted a study to investigate the impact of decompression on the histologic diagnosis of odontogenic cysts and cyst-like lesions. They studied 25 individuals with cysts before undergoing enucleation and curettage. After decompression, the histological results showed that 91% of keratocystic odontogenic tumors, 67% of glandular odontogenic cysts, 75% of dentigerous cysts, and 100% of cystic ameloblastomas matched the preoperative biopsy diagnosis. The authors concluded that all decompressed lesions should be treated according to the initial diagnosis.7

Decompression has been recognized as a remedy for odontogenic cysts; however, the procedure outlined here is a new and inventive alternative. Timely intervention, patient compliance, and thorough follow-up are crucial components for achieving success with this approach. This procedure is simple, practical, and frequently yields reliable findings, particularly in pediatric patients.

Conclusion

The case report shows that dentigerous cysts which are commonly found in the mandibular arch can be managed by the decompression method. This novel method can be considered as a treatment of choice if a less invasive procedure is to be performed. It helps save substantial costs related to illness, resources, and finances for the patient. It can be easily done with few resources and has an excellent probability of success.

Clinical Significance

This novel technique of cyst management is less invasive and causes less harm to the surrounding structures compared to surgery. It helps to reduce the cystic cavity and then heal the lesion which could be ideal in cases of pediatric patients. It helps to preserve the permanent successors if it is related to primary teeth.
