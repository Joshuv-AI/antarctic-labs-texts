Maxillary sinus morphometry and root–sinus proximity in the posterior maxilla: a CBCT study
Authors: Marwa Madi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study was to quantify maxillary sinus anteroposterior (AP), mediolateral (ML), and superior–inferior (SI) dimensions at 5 mm and 10 mm above the sinus floor on cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and compare them by sex; classify posterior molar root–sinus relationships using the Tian classification (IS/CO/OS); and also to assess whether sinus dimensions differ across Tian proximity categories in a tooth-, side-, and sex-stratified manner. This cross-sectional study analyzed 120 CBCT scans (62 males, 58 females; age 20–50 years) obtained from the College of Dentistry, between March to September 2025. Sinus dimensions were measured bilaterally at 5 and 10 mm from the sinus floor (reference levels) at axial and coronal views. Root length of the maxillary third, second, and first molars were recorded. Root–sinus proximity was categorized using the Tian classification. Sex comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests; associations between sinus dimensions and Tian categories were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc comparisons. Sinus dimensions were significantly larger in males on both sides at both reference levels (all p ≤ 0.022 on the right; all p ≤ 0.007 on the left). The right AP length at 5 mm was 33.3 ± 4.9 mm in males versus 29.7 ± 4.6 mm in females (p < 0.001). Root lengths did not differ significantly between sexes for most molars, with the exception of the right second molar, which was significantly longer in males (p = 0.019). Tian distributions were generally comparable between sexes except for the left first molar (p = 0.026), where Type CO was more prevalent in females (73.1%) and Type IS was observed only in males (10.4%). SI height at 5 mm showed the most consistent associations with Tian category, with Type OS demonstrating lower values than Type CO and Type IS. All 10 significant associations withstood Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction (adjusted p-values: 0.002–0.021). Maxillary sinus dimensions showed consistent sexual dimorphism at both reference levels, with males exhibiting larger AP, ML, and SI dimensions than females. Molar root lengths were largely comparable between sexes, with the exception of the right second molar. Root–sinus proximity patterns were generally similar between sexes, and associations between sinus morphometry and Tian proximity categories were selective rather than uniform, with SI height at 5 mm being the most consistently associated parameter. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating sex-specific anatomical considerations into CBCT assessments to optimize the predictability of sinus augmentations and implant surgeries in the posterior maxilla.

Introduction

The posterior maxilla presents distinctive challenges for implant and periodontal surgery because maxillary sinus pneumatization and post-extraction alveolar remodeling can reduce available bone height and alter the three-dimensional (3D) anatomy of the sub-antral compartment (Wang et al. 2022). Consequently, sinus floor elevation (SFE) is widely used to facilitate implant placement; however, its predictability is influenced not only by residual bone height but also by the geometry of the sinus cavity at surgically relevant levels, which affects membrane elevation dynamics and graft stability (Stacchi et al. 2018).

Evidence suggests that anatomical parameters measured at reference levels from the sinus floor helps in surgical planning and risk identification (Brunello et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2022). While limited bone height increases the risk of membrane perforation and implant failure, wider sinus cavities are linked to less favorable healing and increased surgical technical difficulty (Lombardi et al. 2017; Stacchi et al. 2018). The mediolateral sinus width at the 10-mm level is particularly crucial for planning graft containment and the overall sinus surgical approach(Lombardi et al. 2017; Stacchi et al. 2018). In addition, pre-operative sinus floor morphology influences the “tenting effect” and final augmentation shape, which may alter regeneration patterns (Wang et al. 2022). Establishing standardized reference values for sinus length, width, and height provides clinicians with essential anatomical framework for predictable sinus augmentation and implant site planning.

In addition to sinus dimension, the relationship between posterior roots and the sinus floor is clinically important for assessing the surgical risk during extractions, endodontic surgery, periodontal surgery, and pre-implant site assessment (Shahidi et al. 2016). Close root proximity increases the potential for oroantral communication and odontogenic sinusitis, particularly when exacerbated by local infection (Altwaijri et al. 2024; Iliescu et al. 2025; Madi et al. 2024; Shokri et al. 2015). Several radiographic classification systems have been used to classify the tooth-sinus interface (Ragab et al. 2022; Shokri et al. 2015). Tian classification is commonly used to describe root-sinus relationships as intrasinus (IS), contact (CO), or outside the sinus (OS) (Tian et al. 2016).

Anatomical differences between sexes represents an additional factor that may influence clinical treatment planning (Avila-Ortiz et al. 2023; Bezada-Carrasco et al. 2021). Previous CBCT studies observed sexual dimorphism in maxillary sinus dimension, with males generally demonstrating larger sinus morphology than females, although the extent of variation differences vary across populations (Alshammari and Madfa 2026; Kandel et al. 2020; Madfa et al. 2025; Mathew and Jacob 2020; Prabhat et al. 2016; Saccucci et al. 2015; Zarate-Reyes et al. 2024).

Most studies on maxillary sinus morphometry have been conducted in non-Middle East populations, and data specific to the Eastern Province of Saudi population remain limited. Given that sinus dimensions and root/sinus relationships can vary across different sex, ethnic groups and geographic regions, population-specific data are essential for establishing locally relevant anatomical references to guide clinical decision‑making.

Accordingly, this CBCT study aimed to characterize posterior maxillary sinus morphology and its relationship to posterior molar roots using standardized measurements in a Saudi population from the Eastern Province. Specifically, to quantify anteroposterior length, mediolateral width, and superior–inferior height of the maxillary sinus at 5 mm and 10 mm superior to the sinus floor on the right and left sides; to classify the root–sinus relationship of maxillary posterior molars using the Tian classification (IS/CO/OS); and to examine whether sinus dimensions differ across Tian proximity categories in a tooth-, side-, and sex-stratified manner.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample selection

This cross-sectional study analyzed 120 CBCT scans (62 males, 58 females) obtained from the College of Dentistry, Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, between March to September 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB-2022-02-307), and the study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (Von Elm et al. 2007) for observational studies. All datasets were anonymized prior to analysis. Written informed consent had been provided at the time of imaging for use of CBCT records for research and publication purpose with patient confidentiality fully maintained.

The sample size was determined based on previous CBCT-based sinus morphometry studies with comparable designs, which reported adequate statistical power with sample sizes ranging from 100 to 150 subjects (Altwaijri et al. 2024; Bezada-Carrasco et al. 2021; Zarate-Reyes et al. 2024). A minimum of 50 subjects per group (male/female) was targeted to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) with 80% power at a significance level of 0.05 for independent-samples t-tests, yielding a total target of at least 100 subjects.

Eligible scans were obtained from non-smoking adults aged 20–50 years of both sexes who underwent CBCT for routine dental indications (e.g., extraction planning, endodontic treatment, implant-planning) and demonstrated adequate visualization of the maxillary sinus and posterior maxilla bilaterally, presence of maxillary posterior teeth with no more than one tooth missing bilaterally. Exclusion criteria included missing all maxillary posterior teeth in either sides, systemic diseases affecting bone metabolism, medication use known to influence bone turnover, craniofacial skeletal asymmetry, incomplete or partial CBCT scans, substantial motion or metallic artifacts obscuring the region of interest, and radiographic evidence of major osseous pathology affecting the posterior maxilla or sinus region.

CBCT scanning protocol

All CBCT scans were obtained using the KODAK 9500 Cone-Beam 3D System (Carestream, Rochester, NY, USA) with settings of 90 kV, 10 mA, 10.8 s exposure time, and 0.2-mm isotropic voxel size. The field of view was cylindrical, ranging from 15 to 20.6 cm in height and 9 to 18 cm in diameter. Images were evaluated using CS 3D Imaging software (version 3.4.3; Carestream Health Inc., Atlanta, GA, USA) with multiplanar reconstruction (axial, coronal, sagittal) after standard orientation.

Examiner calibration and reliability

All measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed using 10 randomly selected CBCT scans that were re-measured after a 15-day interval. Reliability for continuous variables (sinus dimensions and root lengths) was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and agreement for the categorical Tian classification was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa (κ).

Radiographic measurements

Measurements were recorded separately for the right and left sides using axial and coronal views.

Root length measurement

Root length of the maxillary third (#1), second (#2), and first molars (#3) were measured in millimeters on coronal views along the long axis of the tooth from the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the root apex. The longest root from the (mesiobuccal, distobuccal, and palatal) was recorded for analysis (Fig. 1). The root length measurements obtained from coronal CBCT sections represent the projected coronal root length rather than the true three-dimensional anatomical root length. Because maxillary molar roots frequently exhibit buccopalatal or mesiodistal curvature, a single coronal slice may not capture the full trajectory of a curved root, potentially underestimating the actual root length.

Maxillary Sinus morphometry at standardized levels

The sinus floor, defined as the most inferior cortical boundary of the maxillary sinus, served as the horizontal baseline for all vertical measurements. To ensure consistency across the 120 scans, each sinus was evaluated by scrolling through consecutive coronal slices from the anterior to the posterior wall. The slice which showed the lowest point of the sinus floor was identified and marked using the software’s reference line tool. The axial plane was then set at this level (0 mm) to establish the subsequent 5-mm and 10-mm superior reference levels. This procedure was performed independently for the right and left sides to accommodate potential anatomical asymmetry.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Quantitative data were expressed as range (minimum and maximum), mean, standard deviation and median and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Sinus dimensional measurements met the assumption of normality, whereas root length measurements did not. Sex comparisons (males vs. females) were performed separately for the right and left sides. Student t-test was used to compare two groups for normally distributed quantitative variables. And Mann Whitney test was used to compare non- normally distributed variables. Differences in Tian classification distributions between sexes were tested using the chi-square test. When any expected cell frequency was less than 5, Fisher’s Exact Test was used instead to ensure validity of the p-value.

To examine associations between sinus dimensions (at the 5-mm reference level) and root–sinus proximity, analyses were conducted stratified by sex, side, and tooth. The association analysis was restricted to the 5-mm reference level because it is closest to the sinus floor and most directly relevant to the root–sinus interface, whereas the 10-mm level reflects mid-cavity geometry and is less anatomically relevant for predicting root–sinus proximity.

When all three Tian categories (IS/CO/OS) were present and assumptions were satisfied, one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc comparisons was used; when only two Tian categories were present within a stratum, independent t-tests were performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 120 CBCT scans were analyzed (62 males, 58 females). All analyses were performed separately for the right and left sides.

Examiner reliability

Intra-examiner reliability was assessed on 10 randomly selected CBCT scans re-measured after a 15-day interval. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous measurements (sinus dimensions and root lengths) 0.95 (95% CI), indicating excellent agreement. Cohen’s kappa (κ) for the Tian classification was 0.97 (95% CI), indicating almost perfect agreement.

Sex differences in root length and sinus morphometry

The mean age of the study sample was 37 ± 7 years for males and 35 ± 8 years for females, with no statistically significant difference between sexes (t = 1.460, p = 0.147). The age range was 20–50 years for both groups.

Right side

Root lengths of the right third molar (#1) (p = 0.143) and first molar (#3) (p = 0.178) did not differ significantly between males and females. However, the root length of the second molar (#2) was significantly greater in males than in females (p = 0.019) (Table 1). No significant differences were observed between males and females in Tian classification distribution for any of the three molars (p > 0.05). For the third molar, the most classification type was CO (males: 60.0%; females: 57.1%), followed by OS (26.7% and 39.3%, respectively). For the second molar, CO also predominated (males: 69.1%; females: 72.7%). For the first molar, CO was the most prevalent category (males: 73.2%; females: 67.3%) (Table 1).

All sinus dimensions on the right side were significantly larger in males compared to females at both reference levels. At 5 mm above the sinus floor, the AP length was 33.3 ± 4.9 mm in males versus 29.7 ± 4.6 mm in females (p < 0.001), the ML width was 21.7 ± 4.9 mm versus 18.6 ± 4.2 mm (p < 0.001), and the SI height was 32.5 ± 6.8 mm versus 28.4 ± 5.6 mm (p < 0.001). At 10 mm, significant differences was also observed; AP length (34.4 ± 4.6 mm vs. 32.7 ± 3.7 mm; p = 0.022), ML width (24.8 ± 5.1 mm vs. 21.5 ± 3.9 mm; p < 0.001), and SI height (32.50 ± 6.80 mm vs. 28.20 ± 5.64 mm; p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Left side

The third molar (#1) was evaluated in 30 males and 23 females, the second molar (#2) in 53 males and 54 females, and the first molar (#3) in 48 males and 52 females. Root lengths did not differ significantly between males and females for any of the three molars: third molar (#1) (p = 0.212), second molar (#2) (p = 0.250), or first molar (#3) (p = 0.406).

The Tian classification distributions on the left side were comparable between sexes for the third molar and second molar. A statistically significant sex difference was observed for the left first molar (p = 0.026), where Type CO was more prevalent in females (73.1%) than males (56.2%), and Type IS was observed only in males (10.4%) (Table 2).

Males consistently displayed larger sinus dimensions than females bilaterally at both reference levels. However, root lengths were similar between genders, except for the right second molar (p = 0.019). While the Tian distribution was generally comparable between sexes, the left first molar showed a significant sex-based difference (p = 0.033), with Type IS occurring exclusively in males.

Associations between sinus morphometry and root–sinus proximity were selective, with SI height at 5 mm being the most consistently associated parameter. Across all significant comparisons, Type OS was associated with lower sinus dimensional values, indicating that larger sinuses are more likely to have roots extending into or contacting the sinus floor.

As a sensitivity analysis, the Benjamini Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to all 16 p-values from the subgroup association analyses (Tables 3 and 4). After FDR adjustment at q = 0.05, all 10 originally significant associations remained statistically significant (adjusted p-values ranging from 0.002 to 0.021), confirming that the findings particularly the consistent association between lower SI height at 5 mm and Type OS proximity withstand multiplicity correction. The six originally non-significant associations (p = 0.061–0.272) remained non-significant after adjustment.

Discussion

In this CBCT study of a Saudi population sample, clinically relevant findings were identified in the posterior maxilla. Maxillary sinus dimensions measured at standardized reference levels (5 mm and 10 mm superior to the sinus floor) showed consistent sexual dimorphism, with males demonstrating larger AP, ML, and SI dimensions bilaterally. In contrast, molar root length, recorded as the longest root, did not differ significantly between sexes on either side.

Associations between sinus morphometry and the Tian classification were selective, varying by tooth type, side, sex, and the specific morphometric parameter rather than following a uniform trend across all comparisons. This variability is clinically significant, as sinus configuration and width directly influence membrane elevation, graft containment, and the regenerative ‘tenting’ effect (Stacchi et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2022).

The observed finding of larger sinus dimensions in males is in agreement with previous studies (Alshammari and Madfa 2026; Madfa et al. 2025), suggesting role of sex as a critical factor in defining expected anatomical boundaries for surgical planning.

WAlthough direct comparison is limited by differences in measurement levels and reference landmarks across studies. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to report sinus dimensions at standardized 5-mm and 10-mm intra-sinus reference levels in a Saudi population, providing locally relevant anatomical benchmarks for clinicians in the region. However, careful CBCT assessment remains essential because sex alone does not define surgical morphology at the patient level (Madfa et al. 2025).

Although the mean ages of males and females in this study were comparable with no observed differences, the potential confounding effect of age on root-sinus proximity and sinus dimensions cannot be completely excluded. Maxillary sinus pneumatization is a progressive, age-dependent process, older individuals may demonstrate larger sinus volumes and more pneumatization than younger ones. In order to reduce this age-related confounding, individuals older than 50 years were excluded, and the requirement for the presence of posterior teeth with no more than one tooth missing bilaterally ensured that measurements reflected a dentate sinus floor rather than a remodeled edentulous ridge. Since age distribution was comparable between sexes in our study, the observed sexual dimorphism in sinus dimensions was unlikely confounded by age. To further validate these findings, future studies should utilize age-stratified data or multivariable adjustments to separate the distinct influences of age and sex on sinus dimensions.

Kolerman et al.(Kolerman et al. 2008) reported that 17% of their samples exhibited poor osteogenesis after lateral sinus augmentation, which was correlated with very large sinuses in both the mediolateral and buccopalatal dimensions. They suggested that larger sinuses may have lower osteogenic potential due to the excessive distance between osteogenic cell sources and peripheral angiogenesis, resulting in greater graft contraction and slower new bone formation over time. This is consistent with the established understanding that angiogenesis is essential for guided bone regeneration (Schwarz et al. 2008), with vascular supply originating from the peripheral bony walls and bone formation progressing centripetally toward the graft center (Boyne PJ 1980).

Similarly, Soardi et al. (Maria Soardi et al. 2011) reported a correlation between sinus cavity dimensions and histological outcomes of mineralized bone graft used in sinus floor elevation, in which sinuses with bucco-palatal width > 15 mm required prolonged integration time to achieve adequate bone formation. This hypothesis that sinus dimensions and morphology may influence new bone formation following lateral sinus lifting was supported by previous study with histologic and histomorphometric analyses (Lombardi et al. 2017), and may account for the considerable variability in outcomes reported across previous studies (Esfahanizadeh et al. 2012; Wainwright et al. 2016).

The dental–sinus interface remains clinically important in peri-implant and periodontal settings because close approximation or intrasinus extension may increase susceptibility to oroantral communication during surgical procedures and may contribute to odontogenic sinus disease in the presence of periodontal or apical infection (Altwaijri et al. 2024; Iliescu et al. 2025; Shokri et al. 2015).

In this study, Tian distributions were mostly comparable between sexes, except for the left first molar where Type IS occurred exclusively in males. This suggests a localized sex-related pattern rather than a generalized male predisposition for intrasinus relationship across the posterior teeth. The association between sinus morphometry and proximity was non-uniform; however, the SI dimension at the 5-mm level emerged as the most consistent predictor, particularly for the first molar. Generally, Type OS displayed lower SI values than Type CO or IS, implying that increased vertical sinus dimensions correlate with a higher likelihood of root protrusion or contact. Additionally, significant associations with AP length in specific teeth suggest that horizontal sinus geometry also influences proximity patterns.

These findings suggest that local sinus configuration may influence root–sinus proximity in certain settings. However, sinus dimensions alone are not a reliable predictor of Tian classification across all tooth positions and both sexes, indicating that the relationship between sinus size and root–sinus proximity is selective rather than universal.

The findings of the current study reinforce clinical implications. First, while sex-based differences in sinus dimensions provide useful context for anticipated anatomical ranges, they do not replace individualized CBCT-based assessment, particularly when the surgical approach depends on local geometry. Second, because Tian proximity patterns are tooth- and side-dependent and only partly explained by sinus morphometry, clinicians should evaluate both the 3D sinus cavity configuration and the tooth-specific root–sinus interface when assessing risk for surgical complications and odontogenic sinus involvement which is relevant in integrated perio-implant care, where periodontal and periapical pathology in close proximity to the sinus may contribute to mucosal disease and chronic symptoms (Altwaijri et al. 2024; Madi et al. 2024).

From a practical standpoint, the data suggest that when SI height at the 5-mm reference level falls below approximately 28 mm, the probability of an OS-type root–sinus relationship increases substantially. Across the significant strata in this study, Type OS was consistently associated with mean SI heights ranging from 23.3 to 28.2 mm, whereas Type CO and Type IS were associated with mean SI heights of 29.5–35.5 mm and 31.0–37.2 mm, respectively. Although formal diagnostic thresholds cannot be derived from a cross-sectional study, clinicians encountering SI heights below 28 mm at the 5-mm level on pre-operative CBCT should anticipate a greater likelihood of root separation from the sinus floor (OS pattern), which carries a lower risk of Schneiderian membrane perforation during extraction or implant placement or endodontic surgery. Conversely, SI heights exceeding 33 mm were more frequently associated with IS or CO patterns, where roots approximate or penetrate the sinus floor. Thus, increased caution is needed for oroantral communication incidence during surgical procedures and hence flap design modifications should be considered.

This study used standardized CBCT-based measurements at implant-relevant reference levels, bilateral assessment, sex stratification, and use of a structured root–sinus proximity classification. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-center design may limit generalizability to other populations. The analysis relied on linear rather than volumetric sinus metrics, and lack of comparison to edentulous maxilla preventing assessment of how post-extraction pneumatization may influence sinus morphometry. Furthermore, root length measurements were obtained from coronal CBCT views and therefore represent projected coronal lengths rather than true 3D anatomical root lengths; the degree of underestimation is expected to be proportional to the extent of root curvature, which was not quantified in this study.

The association between sinus morphometry and Tian classification was analyzed only at the 5-mm reference level, as it most closely corresponds to the root–sinus interface relevant to extraction, endodontic, and pre-implant planning. Although 10-mm dimensions describe mid-cavity geometry rather than the root-sinus interface, they likely influence proximity patterns indirectly through sinus pneumatization. Future studies utilizing multivariable models that include both reference levels may better clarify the independent effects of mid cavity and floorlevel anatomy on sinus morphometry.

While excluding individuals over 50 years minimized age-related confounding, it may limit the generalizability of these findings to older implant candidates. To address the risk of Type I error arising from multiple comparisons across sides, sexes, teeth, and sinus parameters, a Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) sensitivity analysis was performed. All 10 identified significant associations survived this correction (adjusted p ≤ 0.021), confirming the stability of the primary results. Nevertheless, near-significant trends in SI height (p = 0.061–0.068) and AP width (p = 0.077) are presented as exploratory results that may reach significance in larger samples.

Further prospective, multi-center studies including volumetric analysis and more diverse age cohorts are recommended. Specifically, investigating the impact of buccopalatal width and dentition status on surgical approach selection and regenerative dynamics to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how localized sinus morphometry affects long-term clinical success.

Conclusions

In this studied population, males exhibited significantly larger bilateral maxillary sinus dimensions at both 5 and 10 mm above the sinus floor, while molar root lengths excluding the right second molar demonstrated no sex-based dimorphism. Root-sinus proximity patterns remained largely consistent across sexes, with the exception of the left first molar.

Associations between sinus morphometry and the Tian classification were primarily localized to the 5-mm height level, where Type OS was associated with significantly reduced dimensions compared to Type CO and/or Type IS. These associations remained robust following Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment; however, due to their subgroup-specific nature, they remain exploratory and necessitate validation through larger, independent studies. At the same time, these findings highlight the necessity of individualized, CBCT-based evaluations of sinus geometry and the root sinus interface for surgical planning in the posterior maxilla.
