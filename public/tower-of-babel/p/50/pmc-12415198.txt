Substantial reductions in black carbon from both fossil fuels and biomass burning during China’s Clean Air Action
Authors: Junwen Liu, Fan Jiang, Qiongqiong Wang, Gan Zhang, Jun Li, Weihua Chen, Ping Ding, Sanyuan Zhu, Zhineng Cheng, Xiangyun Zhang, Qinge Sha, Zhijiong Huang, Xin Yuan, Junyu Zheng, Yanlin Zhang, Caiqing Yan, Chongguo Tian, Yingjun Chen, Jian Zhen Yu, Örjan Gustafsson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceChina has implemented stringent Clean Air Action (CAA) policies over the past decade, yet the effectiveness of controlling key black carbon (BC) sources—aerosols that are harmful to both the climate and health—remains unclear. Using a decade-long dual-carbon-isotope (δ13C–Δ14C) approach, this study quantitatively tracks major BC sources in South China and assesses the accuracy of bottom–up emission inventories (EIs). We find substantial reductions in BC from coal, petroleum, and biomass burning, but also reveal that biomass burning contributes 20 to 30% of the total BC, far exceeding EI estimates. These results reveal a systematic underestimation of biomass-derived BC in current inventories, underscoring the need to integrate EI models with atmospheric observations for more effective air pollution control and climate policy.

Aerosols of black carbon (BC), a tiny, dark, toxic, and climate-affecting particle, are produced through the incomplete combustion of fossil fuel (e.g., coal and petroleum) and biomass (e.g., vegetation fires, agricultural residue, and biofuel). In addition to being a major contributor to air pollution (1), BC is a strong light-absorbing climate pollutant that impacts radiative forcing and thus climate warming (2), with cascading environmental effects such as the melting of glaciers (3), destabilization of the atmospheric column, with effects on the severity of weather (4), and even the shifting of climatic zones (5).

However, current atmospheric models underestimate BC-related radiative effects by a factor of 2 to 3 compared with observation-based estimates in key emission and impact regions (6, 7). Among various potential factors, a central hypothesis posits that this discrepancy arises largely from uncertainties in the total emission fluxes and property-dependent source attributions of bottom–up emission inventories (EIs), which are essential for climate and air quality models as well as for mitigation policies. Thus, there is consequently a plea among major BC assessments for observation-based approaches to constrain sources to independently test predictions from EIs (8–10).

To mitigate the severe pollution of fine particulate matter (PM2.5, aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 μm), with BC as one major component, China initiated a stringent air pollution control policy in September 2013, termed the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan, with the aim of reducing the PM2.5 concentration by 15 to 25% by the year 2017 relative to that in 2012 in the three largest metropolitan regions, i.e., Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei (BTH), the Yangtze River Delta (YRD), and the Pearl River Delta (PRD) (11). These air pollution control policies were focused mainly on the industry, transportation, and residential sectors, which are highly dependent on the use of fossil fuels for energy consumption. In addition, several local governments and ministries have established targeted measures for controlling biomass burning (12). The national PM2.5 concentration sharply declined by 30 to 50% during the action period (13–15), as did the BC concentration (16, 17), especially in highly populated areas. For example, the BC concentration decreased by 38 to 67% from 2005 to 2020 in the BTH region (18, 19), by 45% from 2014 to 2018 in the YRD region (20), and by 60% from 2008 to 2017 in the PRD region (21). All these observations have implicitly assumed that the BC reductions across China were due to the air pollution control policies. While these reductions in the overall BC levels are naturally positive, such concentration-based measurements alone are not sufficient to apportion the BC decreases to specific source classes. Additionally, current modeling studies (15, 22) have suggested that reductions in fossil fuel-based emissions are the dominant driver of the observed decreases in both BC and PM2.5 concentrations. However, these studies may have overlooked the potential contributions of biomass burning, which may play a critical role in shaping air quality trends. To this end, isotope-based diagnostics provide the opportunity to fingerprint and quantitatively apportion the relative contributions to different BC emission sources and, thus, drivers of this recent reduction in atmospheric loading.

Observation-based measurement of dual-carbon isotope, i.e., stable carbon isotope (13C) and radiocarbon (14C), is a powerful forensic tool that allows direct calculation of the relative contributions of different major sources to atmospheric BC (23–25). However, most dual-carbon isotope measurements of BC in China have focused on air pollution events or have been limited to particular seasons/years (26, 27), with a lack of using isotopes to decipher the longer-term evolution in the relative source contributions to atmospheric BC. One earlier study reported year-round seasonally resolved dual-isotope source apportionment of BC aerosols from four hotspot regions in China (28). This investigation revealed that biomass burning is a ubiquitous source of atmospheric BC in China and in urbanized regions. Specifically, for the PRD region, Fang and coworkers reported that biomass burning contributed 21 to 32% of atmospheric BC, with the highest biomass burning contribution occurring in the winter (28). The current study expands this approach to present an 11-y record of variations in the dual-carbon isotopic signatures of BC in the major South China emission region of the PRD from 2008 to 2018 (Fig. 1). This time frame encompasses the implementation period of the Clean Air Action (CAA), i.e., the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan (2013–2017) and the 3-y Action Plan for Winning the Blue Sky Defense Battle (2018–2020).

Through this extended decadal-scale dual-carbon isotope record, we could decipher the long-term evolution of impacts from key anthropogenic activities on BC loadings and shed light on the drivers of BC reduction in China, which has remained unconstrained by concentration-related observations. In addition, the annual dynamics of BC source apportionment revealed in this study may be used as an independent benchmark for assessing EIs and diagnosing areas for further refinement.

Results and Discussion

Reduction in BC Concentrations During the CAA.

The aerosol optical depth (AOD) at the Nansha sampling site was representative of the PRD region during our observational period (2008–2018) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Both the Nansha site and the PRD region exhibited a consistent 26% reduction in AOD values, declining from 0.61 to 0.45 at Nansha and from 0.54 to 0.40 across the PRD region. When the analysis period was extended to 2024, the average AOD at the Nansha site (2008–2024) was 12% lower than that of typical urban areas (e.g., Guangzhou) and 13% higher than that of the PRD region overall. Despite these quantitative differences, we observed strong spatial correlations, with the Nansha site showing a highly significant correlation with both Guangzhou (r = 0.96, P < 0.001) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) and the PRD region (r = 0.97, P < 0.001) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), demonstrating that the Nansha site serves as an effective proxy for monitoring regional aerosol variations. Notably, although the AOD values exhibited minor variations from 2020 to 2023, the 2024 AOD reached its lowest level over the past 17 y.

The daily BC concentration at Nansha tended to decrease from 2008 to 2018, with the annual peak values progressively decreasing. For example, the maximum daily BC concentrations in 2008, 2013, and 2018 were 8.9, 6.6, and 5.1 μg/m3, respectively (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the variations of BC concentrations were significantly correlated with the years for the maximum value (r = 0.67, P < 0.05) and for the mean value (r = 0.89, P < 0.001), with decrease of 0.37 μg/m3 per year (Fig. 2A) and 0.14 μg/m3 per year (Fig. 2B), respectively. The mean BC concentrations during 2014–2018, i.e., the CAA period, were lower than those during the preaction period (2008–2013) in all months, with the greatest (1.9 ± 2.4 μg/m3) and lowest (0.29 ± 0.80 μg/m3) reductions occurring in the typical winter (December) and summer (July) months, respectively (Fig. 2B). On average, the annual BC concentration in 2018 was 1.2 ± 1.0 μg/m3, indicating a decrease of half compared with the concentration of 2.4 ± 1.4 μg/m3 in 2008. The timing of this decline coincided with the period when China implemented stringent measures to address nationwide air pollution. In short, the BC concentration at this regional site during the CAA period (2014–2018) was 41 ± 17% lower than in the preaction period (2008–2013) (Fig. 2D).

These high-frequency decadal ground-based measurement trends are consistent with the more indirect satellite-derived estimates of the PM2.5 reduction (40%, from 2006 to 2018) in the PRD region (14). Meteorological parameters such as wind speed, temperature, and relative humidity tended to increase in yearly values from 2008 to 2018 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Their correlations with BC concentrations were not statistically significant at the 95% CI (P > 0.05; SI Appendix, Fig. S3), indicating that the reduction in BC concentration was driven primarily by the CAA-influenced emission reductions rather than by meteorological factors. Our results are consistent with those of previous studies that reported that interannual variations in meteorological conditions play an insignificant role in the yearly reduction in PM2.5 pollution (13, 15), although unfavorable meteorological conditions can still exacerbate air pollution over short periods, such as hours or days. Since BC is directly produced by the incomplete combustion of carbon-containing materials, the strong positive correlation (r = 0.86, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Fig. S4) between the yearly concentrations of BC and PM2.5 suggests that combustion-related activities are the key source of PM2.5 pollution in the PRD region and that effective control of these sources could significantly improve both the air quality and the effects of BC on climate. However, BC is uniquely produced by incomplete combustion processes (e.g., coal combustion, petroleum combustion, and the burning of biofuels and crops), which also emit significant amounts of gaseous precursors, such as volatile organic compounds (VOCs), NH3, SO2, and NOx, which contribute to the formation of secondary PM2.5 components such as organic aerosols, NH4+, SO42−, and NO3−. Furthermore, BC particles undergo atmospheric aging processes, during which they become coated with these secondary compounds. As a result, controlling BC emissions provides substantial cobenefits for PM2.5 pollution reduction since elevated levels of BC and PM2.5 often originate from common combustion sources. This together explains the strong temporal correlation we observed between the BC and PM2.5 concentrations.

Decadal-Scale Dynamics of Radiocarbon (Δ14C) and Stable Carbon Isotope (δ13C) Signatures in BC.

The yearly trends for the signatures of δ13C and Δ14C in BC from 2008 to 2018 behaved differently. During the summer seasons, the δ13C signature showed negligible variation over the 11-y period, characterized by a weak correlation vs. time (r = 0.14) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). A similar phenomenon was observed in winter (r = 0.05). In contrast, the dynamics of Δ14C signatures are more sensitive to annual changes than are δ13C signatures, with r values of 0.50 and 0.30 from 2008 to 2018 for the Δ14C signatures in summer (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B) and winter (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D), respectively. However, importantly, the correlation between the isotopic signatures and year is not statistically significant at the 95% CI (p > 0.05), suggesting that the source mixture of BC likely did not change substantially on a year-by-year basis from 2008 to 2018 in South China.

The Δ14C trends reveal a gradual transition rather than an abrupt transition, with values showing an overall increase from 2008 to 2013 followed by a decline after 2014 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5F). While clear differences exist between the CAA and preaction periods, these changes occurred progressively rather than as sudden year-to-year shifts. The average Δ14C values were –748 ± 53‰ from 2008 to 2013 and –705 ± 49‰ from 2014 to 2018 for summer, and –686 ± 55‰ from 2008 to 2013 and –664 ± 83‰ from 201 to –2018 for winter (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In contrast, there were no significant differences in the average δ13C values before and during the CAA for either summer (–25.7 ± 0.2‰ vs. –25.9 ± 0.2‰) or winter (–26.1 ± 0.2‰ vs. –26.1 ± 0.4‰) seasons. In addition, the average Δ14C was greater and the δ13C was lower in winter than in summer for both periods. For example, the average Δ14C and δ13C values in winter were –686±55‰ and –26.1 ± 0.2‰, whereas the corresponding values were –748 ± 53‰ and –25.7 ± 0.2‰ in summer from 2008 to 2013 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Since biomass contains greater Δ14C and lower δ13C signatures than fossil fuel does (29, 30), the seasonality observed in this study suggests that winter BC is probably influenced more by biomass burning, whereas summer BC is influenced more by fossil fuel combustion. This phenomenon is common in populous urban regions in Asia such as Beijing (China) (31), Xi’an (China) (24), Delhi (India) (32), and receptor sites in South Asia (23, 33). A previous study (28) performed in the PRD region for a full year (October 2012 to September 2013) also reported that BC has higher Δ14C values and lower δ13C values in winter (Δ14C = –650 ± 30‰; δ13C = –26.2 ± 0.3‰) than in summer (Δ14C = –766 ± 73‰; δ13C = –24.2 ± 1.5‰).

Decadal Evolution in the Relative Source Contributions to BC in South China.

The relative contributions of biomass burning (fbb), coal combustion (fcoal), and liquid petroleum combustion (fpetrol) to BC were assessed using the dual-carbon isotope measurements from 2008 to 2018 (Fig. 3). In this highly urbanized region of China, incomplete combustion of fossil fuels (coal and petroleum) constituted the dominant source of BC over the decade, although biomass burning also made a significant contribution. The ranges of fcoal, fpetrol, and fbb were 31 to 44%, 28 to 47% and 11 to 40%, respectively, from 2008 to 2018 across all samples. The BC source apportionment results in this study are broadly consistent with those reported for other sites in China for shorter-term studies. For example, the fbb values in urban areas of North, West, and East China ranged from 17 to 50%, 17 to 62%, and 13 to 44%, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S1). In contrast, the fbb at our site was lower than that reported for remote regions in South China, such as 62% at Jianfeng Mountain. On average, fcoal, fpetrol, fbb values in this study were 38 ± 2%, 36 ± 2%, and 26 ± 4%, respectively, from 2008 to 2013 and 37 ± 2%, 34 ± 1%, and 29 ± 2% from 2014 to 2018 (CAA period), respectively (Fig. 3A), with the changes not statistically significant (P > 0.05). These findings indicate that the Chinese government’s air pollution control measures have slightly altered the relative contributions of key sources of atmospheric BC in South China, with an overall relative increase in the impact of biomass burning.

The concentrations of all BC subfractions tended to decrease from 2008 to 2018 (P < 0.05), with BC reduction rates from coal and petroleum combustion being approximately twice as high as those from biomass burning (Fig. 3 B–D). Compared with those during the preaction period, the BC concentrations from coal combustion, petroleum combustion, and biomass burning during the CAA period were reduced by 43 ± 17%, 43 ± 19%, and 34 ± 26%, respectively. This suggests that biomass burning control measures are nearly as effective as those targeting fossil fuel combustion during the CAA period.

Previous assessments have generally concluded that controlling fossil fuel usage was the main driver behind air pollution mitigation during the CAA period in China. For example, Zhang et al. (15) evaluated the contributions of specific CAA to primary PM2.5 emissions (e.g., primary organic aerosols and BC) throughout China using a national inventory (Multiresolution Emission Inventory for China, MEIC). They reported that ~90% of the reduction during the CAA was attributed to measures targeting fossil fuel-related sources, such as strengthening industrial emission standards, upgrading industrial boilers, phasing out small and polluting factories, and implementing stricter vehicle emission standards. This implies that fossil fuel combustion control is primarily responsible for BC reductions. Similarly, Liu et al. (22) modeled the long-term trend of the BC concentration in China and noted that the reduction in residential coal has led to a rapid decrease in the BC concentration since 2013. Some studies have suggested a potential impact of biomass burning control measures on BC reduction during the CAA period (19, 34); however, none of these studies have quantified this effect. Here, our study on the decadal record of source-diagnostic dual carbon isotope measurements quantitatively shows that the extent of the decrease in biomass burning emissions was as large as that for fossil fuel combustion sources of BC over South China during the CAA period.

Large Discrepancy Between Bottom–Up EIs and Isotopic Observations.

We also compared the predictions of BC-EIs in the PRD region with the ambient source-diagnostic constraints. This methodology was initially introduced in a study conducted over the Indian Ocean (25), and we extended it to an 11-y observational dataset in this study. Two widely used EIs in China, i.e., the Multi-resolution Emission Inventory for China (MEIC) (35) and the high-resolution (3 × 3 km) model-ready emission inventory developed by Jinan University (JNU) (36), were employed (SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3). A good correlation (R2 = 0.83, SI Appendix, Fig. S7) was observed between the two EIs from 2008 to 2018, with an average of 1.13 ± 0.23 for the MEIC/JNU ratio. In terms of the source apportionment (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), the MEIC suggests that industry (38 to 51%) and transportation (36 to 50%) are the largest contributors, followed by resident (9 to 13%), open biomass burning (1 to 2%), and power plant (<1%). For JNU (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), transportation is identified as the dominant source (67 to 82%), followed by industry (8 to 23%), open biomass burning (2 to 6%), residential fossil fuel (2 to 5%), residential biofuel (1 to 3%), and power plant (1 to 2%). Notably, the combined contributions of two major fossil sources, i.e., transportation and industry, are predicted by both EIs to represent a very high fraction (~90%), with only a very small difference between the CAA and the preaction period (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Hence, the EIs predict that ~90% of the BC reduction during the CAA would be attributed to the control of transportation and industry emissions. These findings for South China are similar to those of an EI-based study that estimated that these two sources contributed ~90% of the primary PM2.5 reduction in the PRD region (15).

However, there is a systemic discrepancy and underestimation in the EI-predicted contribution of biomass burning to BC compared with the ambient source-diagnostic isotopic measurements (Fig. 4). The observational isotope-based source constraints, including those of previous studies (28, 30, 37–39) performed in cities and the regional site in this study, reveal that the contribution of biomass burning to BC was in the range of 21 to 32% from 2008 to 2018, whereas this value was estimated to be only 4 to 9% by both EI methods (Fig. 4A). The contribution of biomass burning to the total BC reduction in South China was constrained to 22% using observational isotope-based diagnostics (Fig. 4B), which is a factor of 2 to 3 times greater than the predictions from technology-based estimates of activity and emission factors inherent in the estimates from EIs (10% for MEIC; 7% for JNU; Fig. 4 C and D, respectively). Notably, chemical-species-based receptor models, such as positive matrix factorization (PMF) and the multilinear engine (ME-2), have reported highly variable contributions of biomass burning to BC, ranging from less than 5% to as high as ~30% (Fig. 4A). This variability likely stems from the high sensitivity of these models to the selection and quality of input chemical species, which may compromise the objectivity of the results.

The large discrepancy between bottom–up EIs and isotopic observations likely contributes to the inconsistency between modeling simulations and the observational constraints of atmospheric BC. Our long-term measurements of BC concentrations differ from the model results obtained by Zhang et al. (44), whose simulations suggest a tendency for the BC concentration to increase from 2013 to 2017. Liu et al. (22) instead modeled a decrease in BC concentration from 2008 to 2018 in the PRD region, but both the simulated concentrations and reduction rates differed significantly from the presented observation-based constraints (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Furthermore, the model-predicted annual BC concentrations in the PRD region from 2008 to 2018 were 32 to 102% higher than those measured in the actual atmosphere at the Nansha site (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). The model overestimation is large enough to offset the measurement differences originating from the effect of the diluted urban/industrial plume at this site (45). In addition, the model presented opposite trends in terms of the reduction rates compared with the observational data for certain years (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B), and the correlation was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). The rates of change in the data from the Nansha receptor should align with the rates of change predicted by EI-based models if the simulated BC concentrations are sufficiently accurate. Overall, the discrepancies between the observations and model results, both in terms of concentrations and their temporal evolution, suggest that the current EIs need to be revisited and revised.

Our independent approach with multiyear δ13C and Δ14C observations shows that controlling biomass burning in the PRD (e.g., preventing the open burning of crop residues and wood) is also an effective approach for reducing the BC concentration in the air. An overwhelming dependency on only the EI methodology may lead to systematic biases and suboptimal scientific guidance for mitigation strategies. A combination of both EI model predictions and source-diagnostic measurements in the actual atmosphere may contribute to improved guidance for further emission reduction actions. Although our observations conclude in 2018, the findings retain key implications for current air quality policies. In 2023, China implemented the Air Quality Continuous Improvement Action Plan, with the goal of reducing national PM2.5 concentrations by 10% by 2025 relative to 2020 levels. However, the rate of PM2.5 reduction in China has slowed significantly in recent years (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). While the new plan covers nearly all emission sources—including biomass burning—our study identifies a critical gap: Current EIs systematically underestimate the contribution of biomass burning to BC. This underestimation may result in insufficient emphasis on biomass burning control measures in policy formulation. We therefore recommend an enhanced focus on biomass burning mitigation to achieve more effective and sustained air quality improvements.

Methods

PM2.5 Sample Collection and BC Concentration Determination.

Over 800 PM2.5 samples (24 h) from 2008 to 2018 were collected on quartz fiber filters using a high-volume air sampler at a well-established atmospheric monitoring site in Nansha (22.75°N, 113.60°E). The Nansha site is a typical suburban receptor located in the geometric center of the PRD region (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). As part of the governmental air quality monitoring network, this site has been shown to effectively represent regional pollution characteristics with strong correlations in PM2.5 and carbonaceous components across PRD monitoring stations (45, 46). Each PM2.5 sample was collected using a high-volume sampler onto a quartz fiber filter with a cumulative sampling time of 24 h. The BC concentrations based on PM2.5 samples were determined using a thermal-optical transmission analyzer (Sunset Laboratory) with the NIOSH 5040 protocol of the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (47).

Measurements of Radiocarbon (Δ14C) and Stable Carbon Isotope (δ13C) in BC.

The process of isolating BC was previously described by Liu et al. (48). In brief, optimized temperature programming was employed to remove the organic carbon fraction from the filter. The BC remaining on the filter was subsequently converted to CO2 for carbon isotope determination. The radiocarbon and stable carbon isotopic compositions were determined using a compact accelerator mass spectrometer system (AMS) manufactured by the National Electrostatics Corporation at the Guangzhou Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences and an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (MAT253) at Nanjing University of Information Science and Technology. The typical recovery of the isolated BC for isotopic measurements was 87 ± 5%, and previous tests have confirmed that this isolation protocol effectively minimizes cross-contamination from charring-derived BC (48). A total of 66 samples collected in July and December from 2008 to 2018 were selected for Δ14C and δ13C measurements (SI Appendix, Table S4). To ensure the representativeness of the isotopic analysis, we selected 6 samples per year, comprising three samples from the dry season (December, representing winter) and three samples from the wet season (July, representing summer). This approach accounts for seasonal variations in air mass influence, with the dry season dominated by continental air masses from the north and the wet season influenced by maritime air masses from the south (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Although the average BC concentration in the isotopic samples is greater than that in the overall dataset due to the requirement of sufficient BC mass for analysis, the annual BC concentrations trends for the isotopic samples and the full dataset strongly agree (r = 0.88, P < 0.001, SI Appendix, Fig. S15), demonstrating that the sampling strategy effectively captures long-term trends in terms of BC concentrations and sources. In addition, the correlation between the BC concentration and its radiocarbon (14C) signature was statistically insignificant (r = 0.18, P > 0.05) (SI Appendix, Fig. S16), and the relatively small interannual variation (average relative SD = 8.7%) in 14C values from 2008 to 2018 suggests that neither the sampling strategy, BC loading, nor potential pollution events exerted a measurable impacts on the source attribution results.
