The Effect of Food on the Pharmacokinetics and Gastrointestinal Tolerability of Bersiporocin, a Novel Oral Prolyl-tRNA-Synthetase Inhibitor
Authors: Seungri Lee, YeaRa Kwak, Jiyeon Park, Yewon Park, Kyung-Sang Yu, Jaeseong Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeBersiporocin is a first-in-class prolyl-tRNA-synthetase inhibitor under clinical development for the treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In a prior first-in-human study, the incidence of gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events (AEs) differed across prandial conditions. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of food on the pharmacokinetics (PKs) and safety profile of bersiporocin.Patients and MethodsThis randomized, open-label, single-dose, three-period crossover study enrolled 36 healthy Korean participants. Each participant received a single oral dose of bersiporocin 200 mg under three conditions: fasted (A), 30 minutes after a high-fat meal (B), and 2 hours after a high-fat meal (C), with a 7-day washout between periods. Plasma concentrations of bersiporocin and its active metabolite M8 were quantified using a validated LC–MS/MS assay, and PK parameters were determined by non-compartmental analysis. Safety was evaluated by monitoring AEs, clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, and electrocardiograms.ResultsA high-fat meal delayed the absorption of bersiporocin and M8. The median Tmax for bersiporocin increased from 3 hours under condition A to 6 hours under conditions B and C. However, systemic exposure, as assessed by AUClast, was comparable across conditions, with geometric mean ratios (90% confidence intervals) of 0.9380 (0.8500–1.0351) and 0.9944 (0.9000–1.0988) for B and C relative to A, respectively. Similar PK trends were observed for M8. A higher incidence of GI AEs was observed in condition B (45.7%) than in conditions A (25.7%) or C (24.2%). Systemic exposure did not differ between participants with and without vomiting.ConclusionA high-fat meal delayed the oral absorption rate of bersiporocin but did not have a clinically significant effect on systemic exposure to bersiporocin or M8. Given the increased incidence of GI AEs under early postprandial conditions, administration under fasted conditions or at least 2 hours postprandially may be appropriate for future clinical studies.

Introduction

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic and progressive interstitial lung disease and about 0.9 to 1.3 per 10,000 people are newly diagnosed with IPF every year.1 The IPF patients have grave prognosis and suffer from progressive dyspnea, reduced exercise capacity and recurrent lung infections.2,3 Although a few drugs, including pirfenidone and nintedanib, have been approved for the treatment of IPF, these agents only slow disease progression and do not halt disease progression completely. Consequently, the prognosis of patients with IPF remains poor, with a median survival time of only 77.2 months.4–6

The etiology of IPF is unknown; however, various predispositions, including genetic and environmental influences, are considered potential triggers of epithelial damage in the lung.7–9 Persistent inflammation in the lungs leads to excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) in the interstitial tissues, restricting the elastic movement of the lung.10 Collagen fragments generated by metalloproteinase (MMP)-mediated ECM degradation are elevated in the serum of IPF patients.11

Proline is a major constituent of collagen that plays a crucial role in maintaining collagen structure. Prolyl-tRNA-synthetase (PRS) is an enzyme that ligates proline to its transfer-RNA for collagen synthesis.12 PRS plays an important role in collagen synthesis by regulating ECM expression, as reported in various in vitro and in vivo studies.12–16 Overexpression of PRS has been observed in the lung tissues of IPF patients, and PRS is considered a potential therapeutic target of IPF.14

A novel first-in-class PRS inhibitor, bersiporocin (code name: DWN12088), is under clinical development by Daewoong Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Republic of Korea. In a bleomycin-induced IPF mouse model, bersiporocin significantly reduced collagen accumulation in lung and heart tissues and improved lung function.13,14 Bersiporocin has an active metabolite, M8, and together they exert antifibrotic effects. In a previous first-in-human clinical trial in healthy volunteers (NCT04767815), oral bersiporocin showed dose-proportional pharmacokinetic (PK) characteristics. It also reduced circulating levels of Type III Procollagen Peptide (PIIINP) in a dose-dependent manner.17 Bersiporocin was generally well tolerated among participants; however, gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events (AEs), such as nausea and vomiting, were reported in several participants. Notably, the incidence of GI AEs was approximately twofold higher after the morning dose than after the evening dose.18 Since the morning dose was administered in a fasted state and the evening dose was administered about 1.5 hours after a meal, it was suggested that food intake before dosing may influence GI tolerability and absorption of bersiporocin.

Based on these findings, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of food on the PKs and the safety of bersiporocin enteric-coated tablets.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB number: 06-2021-1140). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guideline (NCT04767815).

Study Participants

Healthy Korean participants aged between 19 and 55 with body mass index in a range of 18.0 and 27.0 kg/m2 were recruited. Participants without clinically significant findings based on their medical history, vital signs, clinical laboratory results, 12-lead electrocardiogram, and physical examination were included. Participants with a history of GI surgery that could affect drug absorption (except for simple appendectomy or polypectomy), or any GI disease were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent prior to the initiation of any study-related procedures.

Study Design and Procedures

This was a randomized, open-label, single-dose, 3-treatment, 3-period, 6-sequence crossover study (Figure 1). The eligible participants were randomly assigned to one of six sequences. In each period, a single oral bersiporocin 200 mg (two enteric-coated tablets of 100 mg) was administered with 150 mL of water under one of three different dosing conditions: fasted state (A), 30 minutes after a high-fat meal (B) and 2 hours after a high-fat meal (C). Each dosing was separated by a 7-day washout to prevent carryover effects. The high-fat meal was composed of 800 to 1000 kcal with more than 50% of fat content, while fasting required at least 10 hours of no food intake prior to dosing.

Bioanalytical Method

Serial blood samples were collected at pre-dose, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, 36, and 48 hours post-dose in each period. Blood samples (4 mL) were centrifuged at 4°C for 10 minutes, and plasma was stored at −70°C until analysis.

The plasma concentration of bersiporocin and M8 were quantified by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry with an electrospray ionization source in positive ion mode. Bersiporocin-d4 and phenformin-d5 were used as internal standards of bersiporocin and M8, respectively. Multiple reaction monitoring transitions were set at m/z 328.1 → 142.1 for bersiporocin, m/z 344.1 → 142.1 for M8, m/z 332.1 → 146.1 for bersiporocin-d4, and m/z 211.2 → 110.0 for phenformin-d5. The calibration curves were linear over the range of 2.5–1000 μg/L for both analytes, and in-study precision and accuracy (%CV and %bias) were within acceptable limits.

Pharmacokinetic Analysis

The PK parameters were analyzed using non-compartmental analysis with Phoenix WinNonlin® (version 8.5; Pharsight, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), based on the actual blood sample collection times. The maximum observed plasma concentration (Cmax) and the time to maximum plasma concentration (tmax) were determined directly from the observed plasma concentration-time profiles. The area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) from zero to the last measurable point (AUClast) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal linear interpolation. The AUC from 0 to infinity (AUCinf) was calculated as AUClast + Clast/λz, where Clast is the last measurable concentration and λz is the terminal elimination rate constant estimated from linear regression of the terminal phase of the log-linear plot of the plasma concentration-time curve. Additional parameters, including apparent volume of distribution during terminal phase after oral administration (Vd/F), apparent total clearance of the drug from the plasma after oral administration (CL/F), and metabolic ratio (MR) were also estimated. The MR was calculated as the AUCinf of M8 divided by the AUCinf of bersiporocin.

Safety Assessment

Safety was assessed by monitoring AEs, physical examinations, vital signs (blood pressure, pulse rate, and body temperature), 12-lead electrocardiograms and clinical laboratory tests (hematology, blood chemistry, coagulation, and urinalysis). AEs were summarized by treatment group according to severity and relationship to the study drug.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Study data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. PK parameters and the incidence of AEs were summarized by treatment groups. The geometric mean ratios (GMRs) of Cmax and AUClast were estimated for dosing under high-fat meal conditions (B and C) relative to the fasted condition (A) using a mixed-effect model. In the model, treatment, sequence, and period were included as fixed effects, and the subject nested within sequence was included as a random effect.

Results

Baseline Demographic Characteristics

A total of 36 subjects were randomized and 32 subjects completed the study as planned (Figure 1). Baseline demographic characteristics, including age, body weight, and height were similar across sequence groups (Table S1).

For M8, the active metabolite of bersiporocin, plasma concentration–time profiles also exhibited delayed absorption under fed conditions (Figures 2 and 3). The median tmax increased from 4 hours under fasted condition (A) to 8 hours under fed conditions (B, 30-minute postprandial; C, 2-hour postprandial). Corresponding Cmax values were 258.78, 222.13 and 233.71 µg/L, respectively. The GMRs (90% CIs) for AUClast were 1.0484 (0.9945–1.1053) and 1.0021 (0.9499–1.0572) for the 30-minutes (B) and 2-hours postprandial (C) groups, respectively, suggesting that systemic exposure to M8 was similar across dosing conditions (Table 1).

Safety

A total of 62 cases of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported. Among them, 59 cases were considered adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and 52 cases were GI AEs, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and abdominal pain. Most ADRs were mild and resolved spontaneously within a few hours. However, one subject experienced nausea that persisted for seven days after dosing bersiporocin under fasted conditions. No serious adverse events were reported.

GI AEs were more frequently observed when bersiporocin was administered with food. The proportions of subjects with GI AEs were 25.7%, 45.7%, and 24.2% under fasted conditions, 30-minute postprandial conditions, and 2-hour postprandial conditions, respectively (Table 2). The mean ± SD onset times of nausea and vomiting were 1.77 ± 0.18, 4.60 ± 2.27, and 5.15 ± 2.54 hours when bersiporocin was administered under fasted conditions (A), 30-minute postprandial conditions (B), and 2-hour postprandial conditions (C), respectively. Discussion

In this study, the mean plasma concentration-time profiles of bersiporocin and its active metabolite M8 showed delayed absorption under fed conditions, as evidenced by prolonged Tmax and decreased Cmax compared with the fasted state. A high-fat meal decreased the Cmax of bersiporocin by approximately 33–48%. However, overall systemic exposure, as measured by AUClast, did not differ significantly among dosing conditions, suggesting that food delayed the rate of absorption without affecting the extent of absorption or oral bioavailability.

From a PK perspective, these findings are consistent with the known effects of high-fat meals on gastric emptying. Following ingestion of a high-fat meal, gastric emptying time is prolonged, delaying the transit of enteric-coated tablets into the intestine.19 This physiological delay provides a plausible explanation for the prolonged Tmax and reduced Cmax observed in this study. Despite these changes in the rate of absorption, the terminal elimination slope remained unchanged, supporting the conclusion that the food effect was confined to the absorption phase rather than systemic disposition.

Bersiporocin, formulated as an enteric-coated tablet, was generally well tolerated across all dosing conditions. The most frequent AEs were GI in nature, primarily nausea and vomiting. The highest incidence was observed when bersiporocin was administered 30 minutes after a high-fat meal, whereas the incidence was lower and comparable between the fasted and 2-hour postprandial groups.

The predominance and timing of GI AEs suggest that local irritation, rather than systemic exposure, is the primary mechanism. Drug-induced nausea and vomiting can arise from either peripheral or central pathways. The former involves local GI irritation via vagal afferent signaling, whereas the latter involves systemically circulating drugs activating the chemoreceptor trigger zone.20 In this study, systemic exposure was comparable regardless of food intake, indicating that systemic effects were unlikely to account for the observed differences. Instead, the higher incidence of nausea and vomiting in the 30-minute postprandial group supports a peripheral mechanism driven by local GI irritation. One plausible explanation is that high-fat meals transiently alter the gastric environment—by raising intragastric pH and delaying gastric emptying—which may compromise the integrity of the enteric coating and lead to premature drug release. Early exposure of the gastric mucosa to bersiporocin may then activate vagal reflex pathways, resulting in nausea and vomiting. Consistent with this interpretation, pharmacokinetic exposures did not differ between subjects with and without vomiting.

Unlike conventional food-effect studies that compare fasted and fed states, this study incorporated two postprandial time points—30 minutes and 2 hours after a high-fat meal—to delineate the influence of meal timing on both systemic exposure and local tolerability. The 30-minute condition represents an early postprandial phase, during which gastric contents and elevated intragastric pH may compromise the integrity of the enteric coating and lead to premature drug release. Conversely, at 2 hours post-meal, gastric emptying is largely complete and intragastric pH has normalized, allowing the tablet to remain intact until it reaches the intestine. This design therefore enabled separation of local gastric effects from systemic exposure in the assessment of GI tolerability.

A limitation of this study is that it evaluated only single-dose administration; thus, the observed GI adverse event profile may not fully represent tolerability under repeated dosing conditions. However, the relatively short half-life of bersiporocin suggests minimal accumulation, which may reduce the likelihood of meaningful changes in tolerability with multiple dosing.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although a high-fat meal delayed the absorption of bersiporocin, it did not significantly alter systemic exposure to either the parent drug or its active metabolite, M8. Given the meal-related GI intolerance, bersiporocin should be administered under fasted conditions or at least 2 hours after a meal to ensure optimal tolerability. These findings provide a basis for dose administration in future clinical studies and may help optimize the tolerability of bersiporocin during clinical development.
