Ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health on age acceleration: the Cancer Survivors’ Trajectories of Ageing Research (C*STAR) study protocol
Authors: Nur Afifah Mursyida Zaujan, Mohd Razif Shahril, Suzana Shahar, Ezanee Azlina Mohamad Hanif, Nur Fa’izah Ab Muin, Razinah Sharif, Ponnusamy Subramaniam, Azizi Abdullah, Michael Fenech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAge acceleration in survivors of breast cancer is a critical issue because cancer and its treatment can increase structural and numerical chromosomal aberrations, while simultaneously shortening telomere length and changing ageing phenotype. Therefore, the current study will be using machine learning architectures to accurately predict the factors that contribute to age acceleration among survivors of breast cancer.Methods and analysisThe Cancer Survivors’ Trajectories of Ageing Research (C*STAR) is a hospital-based cross-sectional study involving multi-ethnic Malaysian survivors of breast cancer and a non-breast cancer control group, frequency-matched by age group (±5 years), sex and ethnicity. The three main stages of this study will be conducted in the predictive model development. First, a set of validated questionnaires will be used to collect the data on modifiable factors of ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health. Second, 3 mL non-fasting blood samples will be collected, and lymphocytes will be isolated to determine telomere length using real-time PCR as a biomarker of age acceleration. Lastly, a machine learning architecture will be deployed to identify modifiable factors that may contribute to age acceleration in survivors of breast cancer and controls, with these factors used as input and ageing biomarkers of telomere length as output. The study outcomes may serve as guidance to enhance the quality of life of survivors of breast cancer and hinder the recurrence of cancer while ageing successfully.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (JEP-2022-700) to carry out this study. Written informed consent will be obtained from each survivor of breast cancer and each cancer-free woman prior to participation. The results of this study will be published for future research and clinical applications.

Introduction

Breast cancer has garnered much attention across the globe due to its profound impact on female’s health. Breast cancer accounts for 27% of all cancer cases and 16% of cancer-related deaths among females worldwide in 2025, with countries previously exhibiting low incidence now experiencing annual increases of 3% to 5%.1 The statistics evidence that breast cancer is one of the most common cancer types plaguing the world. Early detection and treatment have increased the number of survivors of breast cancer. In the context of Malaysia, survivors of breast cancer recorded the highest survival rate at 66.8% when compared with other cancer survivors.2 Nonetheless, the survival rate of Malaysian survivors of breast cancer was still lower when compared with Western countries, such as the US (88.8%), Italy (85.9%), Germany (85.6%), Korea (84.0%) and Ireland (81.4%).2 This indicates the pressing need for more studies to determine ways to increase the survival rate and slow the age acceleration of Malaysian survivors of breast cancer.

Accelerated ageing is commonly justified by the characteristics of ageing phenotypes and biological features observed at earlier ages, as one’s phenotypes become older than expected for their chronological age.3 Characteristics of ageing phenotypes are observable in four domains that change over time: cognitive, psychological, physical and functional.4 At the same time, behavioural determinants are commonly assessed via a wide range of modifiable lifestyle factors, including sleep quality,5 physical activity,6 perceived stress7 and dietary intake.8 Undeniably, age acceleration brought on by cancer treatment affects both the ageing phenotypes and the quality of life of survivors.3 Despite the vast range of studies that have examined the ageing phenotypes among survivors of cancer,9 10 only a handful of studies have explored the ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health among Malaysian survivors of breast cancer. Data scarcity in ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health should support or develop the intervention plan in Malaysia targeted at survivors of breast cancer, stemming from the dearth of published studies on these topics. Therefore, this study substantially contributes to the literature by investigating the ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health among survivors of breast cancer in Malaysia.

Additionally, telomere length is widely recognised as an ageing biomarker to estimate biological age.11 Earlier findings unravelled that individuals with age-related illnesses had shorter telomere lengths when compared with healthy controls.12 A study that involved survivors of breast cancer reported that their telomere length decreased due to cancer therapy, excruciating pain and poor cognitive performance.13 Telomere shortening caused by cancer treatment can lead to cellular senescence and chromatin breakage within the telomere, thus resulting in telomere malfunction and chromosomal instability, which is a hallmark of ageing and cancer.12 14 Plausibly, the shortening of telomeres causes cancer survivors to experience accelerated ageing, which often includes comorbid conditions and functional decline.13 Notably, age acceleration has been related to telomere shortening.

Although the issues covered in past studies are worthwhile, none have applied machine learning algorithms for predictive model development to identify the factors that cause age acceleration among Malaysian survivors of breast cancer. Besides, none of the prior studies has used the factors of the ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health, which contribute to accelerating the ageing process, as datasets in machine learning, specifically among Malaysian survivors of breast cancer. Due to the vast potential of machine learning algorithms and their ability to handle dataset intricacies, their usage in research work has increased exponentially. For example, several previous studies developed predictive models using XGBoost, particularly hyperparameter-tuned models, and reported higher accuracy than other models.15 16 The accuracy metric displays the percentage of correct predictions made by the models. These findings from an abundance of previous studies demonstrate that the XGBoost model is indeed a valuable tool for physicians in clinical settings, achieving high predictive accuracy across a wide range of applications and outperforming other machine learning models.

This study is the first of its kind to assess age acceleration among the multi-ethnic Asian population of survivors of breast cancer by using telomere length as the ageing biomarker, in combination with a machine learning architecture. Reducing age acceleration among survivors of cancer lowers the detrimental effects of damage due to cancer and its treatment. Determining how age acceleration is affected by modifying behavioural determinants while considering health determinants may help survivors of cancer age gracefully and healthfully. By incorporating an ensemble machine learning architecture, this study substantially contributes to knowledge of ageing phenotypes and the behavioural and health determinants of ageing among multi-ethnic Malaysian survivors of breast cancer and controls.

Hence, this protocol paper aims to describe the methodology of the Cancer Survivors’ Trajectories of Ageing Research (C*STAR) study, which will investigate the status of ageing phenotypes, behavioural determinants of health, telomere length as an ageing biomarker and the development of an XGBoost predictive model to identify factors contributing to age acceleration among people who have survived breast cancer and their counterparts. The overarching research question guiding this study is: what biological and behavioural characteristics are associated with age acceleration among survivors of breast cancer compared with their counterparts?

Research methodology

Study design

The C*STAR study will be conducted among survivors of breast cancer and cancer-free controls using a hospital-based cross-sectional study design. The current study will employ three stages in its approach (figure 1). The first stage will be conducted to get all the information needed on characteristics of ageing phenotypes (cognitive status, body composition, physical fitness and functional status) and behavioural determinants of health (sleep quality, physical activity, perceived stress and dietary intake), simultaneously with the second stage on the blood samples collection for biomarker detection. The data collected in the first and second stages will be used in the third stage to identify ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health risk factors associated with an ageing biomarker, and to construct a predictive model using an ensemble machine learning technique. The variables of study (Stage 1) will be set as “input”, while the ageing biomarker (Stage 2) will be set as “output” in running the machine learning (Stage 3).

Study approaches to determine the ageing phenotypes, behavioural determinants of health on age acceleration and predictive model development.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the development of the research question, selection of outcome measures or design of the recruitment strategy for this study. No formal patient or public involvement activities were undertaken during the planning or design phases of the study. Individuals from the target population were involved during a pilot phase to test the feasibility, clarity and flow of the questionnaire and data collection procedures. Feedback from pilot testing was used to make minor refinements to questionnaire wording and data-collection processes to improve clarity and acceptability. This involvement was limited to feasibility assessment and did not extend to substantive study design decisions. Patients and the public were not involved in determining outcome measures or recruitment strategies. Following publication of the study findings, participants and relevant cancer survivor groups will be informed of the results. Study findings will be disseminated through a plain-language summary newsletter designed for a non-specialist audience.

Study location

Participants will be recruited from Hospital Canselor Tuanku Muhriz, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM), Kuala Lumpur, at the Surgery Clinic and Oncology Clinic, which provide breast cancer screening, treatment and routine follow-up care. These clinics allow the recruitment of a heterogeneous sample of multi-ethnic Malaysian survivors of breast cancer across cancer stages, hormone receptor subtypes and treatment modalities. Recruitment commenced in March 2023 and remained ongoing at the time of protocol submission (March 2025), with approximately 40% of the target sample enrolled. No interim or outcome analyses were conducted prior to protocol submission.

Ethics and dissemination

The ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and the Malaysian Good Clinical Practice Guideline will be adhered to in this study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (JEP-2022-700) to carry out this study. All prospective research participants will receive a briefing on the study protocol, risks and benefits. Written informed consent will be obtained from each consented participant prior to participation. Participants will be informed that they may discontinue their participation in this study at any time without affecting their medical care. Data security and confidentiality will be maintained throughout the study. The data from this study will be available on request from the corresponding author and may benefit future research and clinical applications. In addition, the findings of this study will be disseminated through publications, conferences and other academic platforms.

Study sampling

The purposive sampling technique will be used to recruit participants, with recruitment conducted in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The following lists the inclusion criteria for the recruitment of survivors of breast cancer in this study: (1) Primary female breast cancer (any stage at diagnosis), (2) Previously received a diagnosis of malignant breast cancer confirmed by histopathological report, (3) Completed active cancer treatment for at least the past 6 months (including surgery, chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy) and (4) Neither recurrent nor secondary cancer. The researcher will record the clinical characteristics of cancer staging and treatment modalities among survivors of breast cancer as confounding variables in the analysis, given their potential influence on phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health outcomes. The inclusion criteria for cancer-free control groups are female and confirmed to have no diagnosis of cancer by a medical doctor.

The exclusion criteria for both groups are as follows: (1) Has a limb deformity, (2) Unable to move without assistance, (3) Pregnant and breastfeeding female and (4) Diagnosed with psychiatric or neurological diseases verified from their medical reports during study recruitment. Those with limb deformity will be excluded because study participants must complete a set of physical fitness tests that mostly involve moving and using their hands and legs. This exclusion criterion is used to avoid bias in the scoring of physical fitness tests and other variables (eg, functional status and physical activity) among those without limb deformity. Both groups must consist of adult Malaysian females (18 to 60 years old) who can read and speak Malay or English, matched to cases at a 1:1 ratio. The sample size for this study is computed using G*Power V.3.1.9.7 (F test: linear multiple regression; Fixed model; R2 deviation from zero). The parameters used are a medium effect size (f2) of 0.15, 25 predictors, a 95% significance level and 80% power. The minimum sample size required for this study is 524, with 262 per group, accounting for 10% attrition.

Data collection

Prior to participant recruitment, the researcher obtained histopathological files from the clinic to screen potential participants. This file contains medical information for all study participants, including their registered identification number, contact information, cancer subtypes, cancer stages, receptor status, treatment type and date of diagnosis, as recorded by the researchers. During this stage, participants will be recruited based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, using information from the histopathological reports.

As for the control participants, referred to as the cancer-free female control group, they will be recruited in the same clinics as survivors of breast cancer, consisting of females who were diagnosed with benign breast conditions and undergoing routine follow-up or breast cancer screening. Their cancer-free status will be confirmed by reviewing their histopathological reports, which were verified by medical doctors. Both groups will be frequency-matched at a 1:1 ratio by age group (±5 years), sex and ethnicity throughout data collection to ensure comparability. Participants outside the age range of 18–60 years will be directly excluded at this stage. Each potential participant who meets the inclusion and exclusion criteria will be approached in the clinics during the data collection session, and those who provide written consent will be recruited as study participants.

After a written consent is obtained from the study participant, the researcher-administered interview will be conducted during the participant’s follow-up appointment using a standardised, validated questionnaire as a study instrument (table 1). Cognitive status, body composition, physical fitness and functional status reflect ageing phenotypes, whereas dietary intake, sleep patterns, physical activity and perceived stress reflect behavioural determinants of health. Other information on health habits, including smoking history, alcohol consumption and other comorbidities, such as diabetes and heart disease, will be gathered through a general questionnaire. The collected data for these variables, including clinical variables such as cancer type, stage and treatment received, will serve as input features for the XGBoost machine learning predictive model to identify factors contributing to age acceleration among survivors of breast cancer and control group participants. The data on telomere length as an ageing biomarker will be included as an output feature. By incorporating ageing phenotypes (input feature), behavioural determinants of health (input feature) and telomere length data (output feature), the XGBoost algorithm will capture the non-linear associations among these variables, enabling comprehensive prediction of ageing outcomes.

The estimated time to complete the questionnaire will be within the range of 45–60 min per participant. Next, the trained phlebotomist will conduct a venipuncture procedure under aseptic conditions using a 21-gauge BD Safety-Lok butterfly needle (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to minimise participant discomfort. The blood sample will be directly transferred into a 3 mL BD Vacutainer EDTA tube (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to prevent coagulation, and the tube will be inverted several times to mix the blood with anticoagulant, to preserve the sample’s integrity for the ageing biomarker’s analysis. Each tube will be labelled with the participant’s ID number and date of blood sample collection, to prevent misidentification and ensure confidentiality. Lastly, the collected blood samples will be immediately stored at the Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia Medical Molecular Biology Institute (UMBI UKM) at −80°C for further analysis of ageing biomarkers.

Ageing biomarker: telomere length determination

The O’Callaghan and Fenech’s17 method will be deployed in this study by introducing an oligomer standard to generate absolute telomere length (aTL) values (kb/genome diploid). To provide a base-pair length estimation for specimen telomere lengths, the curve will be based on the results of serial dilutions of an oligomer standard containing 14 copies of the TTAGGG telomeric sequence (for 84 base pairs in length). Telomere (TTAGGG)14 and single copy gene 36B4 (CAGCAAGTGGG- AAGGTGTAATCCGTCTCCACAGACAAGGCC-AGGACTCGTTTGTACCCGTTGATGATAGAA- TGGG) (Bio Basics, Canada) are the sequences of the standard oligomer (5’−3’) that will be used in this assay. The following PCR primers will be employed: teloF (CGGTTTGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTGGGTT-TGGGTTTGGGTT), teloR (GGCTTGCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCT- TACCCTTACCCT), 36B4F (CAGCAAGTGGGAAGGTGTAATCC) and 36B4R (CCCATTCTATCATCAACGGGTACAA) (Integrated DNA Technologies, USA). The PCR mastermix for both telomere and 36B4 will be prepared according to the protocol for the Blastaq 2X qPCR MasterMix Kit (Applied Biological Materials, Canada). All samples will be run on the Mic PCR (Biomolecular Systems, UK). Cycling conditions (for both telomere and 36B4 amplicons) will be set at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min, then a dissociation (or melt) curve. The aTL will be estimated by dividing kb/telomere reaction by the number of copies in the 36B4 diploid genome.

Machine learning architecture

The numerical dataset of ageing phenotypes, behavioural determinants of health and telomere length will be used in the Jupyter Notebook, and this study will implement a two-step machine learning pipeline for data pre-processing and XGBoost model development (figure 2).

First, pre-processing, which comprises data cleaning, data transformation, handling imbalanced data and feature selection, will be conducted to prepare the dataset for further analysis in predictive model development using the XGBoost algorithm. The data will be cleaned by handling missing values, dropping duplicate columns and rows and removing outliers. The outlier removal methods are based on the distribution of the data, that is, the IQR method is for non-normally distributed datasets, while the Z-score method is for normally distributed datasets. The data will then be transformed using a Min-Max scaler. The min-max technique finds the lowest value (min(xi)) and maximum value (max(xi)) from the dataset, providing an easy way of scaling the data into a given range (0–1).18 The technique involves subtracting the minimum value from each data point, then dividing the result by the range max(xi)−min(xi). It sets a fixed range for the dataset’s features, typically 0 to 1. The formula used for min-max scaling is as follows:

(1) X`= X-min⁡(X)max⁡(X)-min⁡(X)

Whereas X` represent the normalised value. The values of min(X) and max(X) represent the minimum and maximum values in the dataset.

Next, the synthetic minority over-sampling with Gaussian noise (SMOGN) technique, a combination of synthetic minority over-sampling technique for regression and the introduction of Gaussian noise techniques, will be used to address imbalanced regression and oversample the training dataset.19 To ensure balanced data distribution, the SMOGN algorithm will partition into Bins R (rare and important partitions: higher-relevance examples) and Bins N (normal and less important partitions: lower-relevance examples).19

Feature selection will be performed using recursive feature elimination (RFE) to remove weak features until the optimal features remain. Features with p values greater than the significance level will be removed. RFE will also rank the features according to importance and based on the coefficient values of the attributes.20 These selected features will be implemented in the next step of XGBoost model development. This RFE method ensures that only the most relevant predictors are included in the XGBoost predictive model, reducing overfitting and improving model interpretability.

Next, to develop a predictive XGBoost model, the dataset will be split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. The training dataset will serve as a basis for predicting telomere length using the XGBoostRegressor algorithm and will subsequently be used to produce class predictions on the test data. Several hyperparameter tuning methods defined as “multi-tree XGBoost” will be used to optimise the model, including controlling the overfitting, model complexity and training efficiency.21 The accuracy_score() function from the scikit-learn library, using mean absolute error (MAE), will be used to evaluate the finalised XGBoost model’s predictions for the target variable.22 The particle swarm optimisation will be implemented for XGBoost hyperparameter optimisation, which iteratively searches for the best hyperparameter settings using a swarm of particles. During the iteration, every particle in the swarm will be updated using these two equations:

(2) Vid(t+1) = w.Vid (t)+c1.r1 .(pid−Xid(t))+c2.r2.(pgd −Xid(t))

(3) Xid (t+1) = Xid (t)+Vid (t+1)

with Vid (t+1) and Xid (t+1) as the updated and current particle velocities, whereas Xid (t+1) and Xid (t) are the updated and current particle positions, c1 and c2 are the positive constants and r1 and r2 are normalised unit random numbers within the range (0–1), and w is the inertia weight.23

The explainable artificial intelligence methods of local interpretable model agnostic explanation (LIME) or Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) will be implemented to rank the features according to their importance based on their effect on the model outcome.24 These methods will improve the model’s interpretability and accuracy. LIME is commonly used to explain machine learning model predictions by modifying the feature values of a single data sample and observing the resulting change in the output. LIME trains an interpretable model (eg, a linear regression model, a decision tree, etc) that is weighted by the proximity of sampled instances to the required instance and conducts tests to determine how the model’s predictions change when the data is modified.25 Additionally, SHAP will employ a game-theoretical approach to represent its contribution to the prediction by assigning a value to each input feature, while controlling for all possible feature combinations.25 Besides, SHAP produces a collection of feature weights to explain the model’s predictions, accounting for feature interactions and providing a more precise explanation of the model’s behaviour.25 The SHAP algorithm is as follows:

All in all, the machine learning architecture protocol planned for this study will provide a robust, systematic machine learning pipeline for predicting which factors in ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health affect age acceleration between survivors of breast cancer and their counterparts.

Analysis and model evaluation

The IBM SPSS for Windows V.29.0 software will be deployed for data analysis. Mean and SD scores will be used to represent numerical variables, while percentages and frequencies will be used for categorical data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test will be performed to test the normality of each parameter. The data will be presented as median (IQR) rather than mean (SD) if the normality test fails at the 5% significance level. The variances in quality of life, dietary intake, anthropometric measurements, physical fitness tests and telomere length between survivors of breast cancer and their controls will be determined using an independent t-test for normally distributed data and a Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. The data will be presented as percentages and frequencies for the categorical variables retrieved for physical activity, subjective stress, sleep quality, cognitive assessment and functional status. Lastly, the model will be evaluated using MAE, and the selected model will be used to identify factors contributing to accelerated ageing among survivors of breast cancer and their controls.

Discussion

The identification and modification of modifiable factors underlying ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants of health may be proposed to slow ageing and promote healthy survivorship among survivors of cancer. Besides, studies on which of the modifiable factors contribute to age acceleration among multi-ethnic survivors of breast cancer and their controls in Malaysia are scarce. Hence, to support the survivorship of survivors of breast cancer, the C*STAR study will assess modifiable factors among study participants and identify associated factors to slow the acceleration of ageing. Comprehending how age acceleration is affected by modifying behavioural and health determinants can help survivors of breast cancer age gracefully and have a high level of well-being.

Besides, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to investigate modifiable factors contributing to age acceleration and telomere shortening by integrating ensemble machine learning with the XGBoost model among survivors of breast cancer in Malaysia. The study outcomes will elucidate the complex, often non-linear relationship and inform survivorship among people with a history of breast cancer. The novel findings may interest researchers in cancer survival. This study is impactful on society, particularly in sustaining the survival rates among survivors of breast cancer. In addition, this study benefits the industry by suggesting the potential development of an advanced intelligent system that offers personalised recommendations to reverse accelerated ageing.

The main limitation of this study is the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference regarding the relationships between ageing phenotypes and behavioural determinants among survivors of breast cancer, including effects attributable to cancer, cancer treatments or post-treatment lifestyle changes. Nevertheless, this design is appropriate for the current protocol, as it enables comprehensive characterisation of ageing-related profiles and the identification of key biological and behavioural factors associated with age acceleration in a well-defined population. The findings will inform hypothesis generation and support the design of future longitudinal and interventional studies. In addition, although validated self-report instruments will be used to assess physical activity (IPAQ), sleep quality (PSQI) and dietary intake (FFQ), such measures may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. This limitation will be mitigated by using widely accepted questionnaires with established reliability and validity, along with standardised administration procedures to enhance data quality and comparability.

Conclusions

The study outcomes may be used to make viable recommendations, particularly to cancer survivors and those who care for them, to better control their risk factors and bridge the age gap between their biological and chronological ages. This study should be viewed as a novel and pertinent contribution to comprehending how ageing phenotypes, as well as behavioural and health factors, can contribute to age acceleration among survivors of breast cancer. The outcomes of this study are beneficial for enhancing the quality of life of cancer survivors and helping them age gracefully. The results of this study have implications for academics and may be useful for future research aimed at improving the quality of life of survivors of cancer.
