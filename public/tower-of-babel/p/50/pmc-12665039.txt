Computational approaches to enzymatic reaction assignment: a review of methods, validations, and future directions
Authors: Luke Kennedy, Mary-Ellen Harper, Miroslava Cuperlovic-Culf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCharacterizing the proteins and molecules that underpin cellular metabolism is fundamental to advancing our understanding of biological processes. However, the rapidly expanding repertoire of newly identified proteins and metabolites presents significant challenges for experimental characterization and functional analysis. Computational approaches can be used to identify and elucidate catalytic relationships between enzymes and their substrates and provide powerful tools that support biological research and applications in biochemical engineering, and drug discovery. In this review, we describe the problem of reaction assignment for predicting enzymatic reactions leveraging structural, network, and high-throughput experimental data. Also considered are theoretical perspectives motivating the design of computational methods, available resources, and validation techniques. Current and future computational approaches for enzymatic reaction assignment are expected to advance in tandem with technologies for experimental analysis of metabolism, such as metabolomics, and flux-based methods, to expand our understanding of metabolism.

Introduction

Background

Reaction assignment is a special case of the protein function annotation problem defining enzymatic functions of proteins catalyzing conversion of substrate molecules to products. In practice, often we are given a protein, and/or biological molecule, and the goal is to predict the associated enzyme-catalyzed reaction. Within the scope of reaction assignment are endogenous biomolecules, e.g. metabolites, as well as exogenous molecules including drugs, products of the gut microbiome, and environmental chemicals which all may undergo enzymatic reactions. Beyond this, enzymes also catalyze reactions with macromolecules such as posttranslational modifications (PTMs) of proteins [1] and methylation of DNA [2]. Within metabolic networks, proteins can also transport molecules across membranes [3]; however, as these processes do not induce chemical transformations, they are generally out-of-scope for reaction assignment methods, requiring specialized approaches [4]. Likewise, enzymatic reactions on macromolecules are frequently excluded or have dedicated methods [5, 6]. Reaction assignment methods aim to discover novel chemical relationships for fully uncharacterized, orphan, metabolites and enzymes [7–9], or partially characterized species (Fig. 1). Practically, this problem is addressed by the task of annotating functional classifications or activity values related to enzymatic reactions from data corresponding to metabolites and proteins. While this is directly motivated for characterizing novel metabolic reactions and enzyme functions to provide a greater understanding of cellular biology, several downstream tasks also benefit from these developments. Namely, reaction assignment techniques have been applied for use-cases in drug discovery [10] and metabolic or protein engineering [11, 12] by extending their application to exogenous metabolites and perturbed proteins or biological conditions.

Schematic of reaction assignment. General framework for computational reaction assignment methods with the goal of determining high confidence pairs of metabolites and enzymes that are likely to fulfil a catalytic reaction. Assigned reactions may fill knowledge gaps in orphan reactions, having (i) known substrate(s) and product(s) but undefined enzyme(s), (ii) new proteins having enzyme characteristics but unknown substrate(s) and product(s), and (iii) metabolites having no known reactions or enzyme catalysts.

Despite the vast network of known metabolic reactions, many reactions and enzymatic functions remain unknown. This has resulted largely from the outpacing of our ability to discover new features of metabolism compared to our ability to characterize features of metabolism. Illustrating this, the most comprehensive enzyme function database, BRENDA [13], reports functional data for ~90 000 different enzymes based on literature evidence, yet an additional 18 million genes are predicted to have enzymatic functions but lack experimental evidence [13, 14]. Exacerbating this knowledge gap is the discovery of novel metabolites through advances in metabolomics technologies. Since 2018, over 2500 newly detected metabolites have been added to the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) [15, 16], growing the total number of detected metabolites to ~24 400 (https://hmdb.ca/statistics). Fewer than 17% of these metabolites are associated with reactions in the most comprehensive model of human metabolism, the Human1 genome-scale metabolic model [17]. Furthermore, even these metabolites likely have other, currently undetermined reactions in different states and systems.

With these knowledge gaps in metabolism, the potential benefits of accurate enzymatic reaction assignment are profound. The use of such computational tools to assist biological research is exemplified in the closely related problem of protein structure prediction, which has seen great success driven by the accuracy of methods like AlphaFold [18–20]. Despite limited abilities on challenging biology (e.g. conformational switches, and disordered proteins), these methods have led to new biological discoveries and advanced the field [21, 22]. A similar advance in functional prediction has not been realized. This divide is most striking when comparing improvements in the open competitions for the critical assessment of protein structure prediction and function annotation, Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction (CASP) [20, 23] and Critical Assessment of Function Annotation (CAFA) [24, 25], respectively. These recurring challenges evaluate the state-of-the-art in protein structure prediction and functional annotation from primary amino acid sequences by comparing novel experimentally determined protein structures and protein gene ontology (GO) function annotations to computationally predicted structures and functions submitted by teams in the months prior to experimental determination. Since the inception of CAFA in 2010, dramatic gains to structure prediction accuracy in CASP are contrasted with only modest improvements for GO term annotation in CAFA (Fig. 2A).

While the general protein function annotation addressed by CAFA is not identical to the enzyme reaction assignment problem, many of the challenges illustrated by CAFA apply to both the general function annotation and reaction assignment tasks. Notably, ground truth evidence for protein and enzyme functions is highly variable in their information content and accuracy stemming from the wide variety of evolving experimental methods and biological or chemical conditions. In contrast, X-ray crystallography, Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and, recently, cryo-electron microscopy contribute the most to structure determinations. Despite variability across equipment and experimental conditions, the atomistic coordinate outcomes are common across these methods, giving consistent ground-truths for structure prediction.

Another challenge for function annotation arises from the fact that the distributions of known protein and enzyme functions are highly skewed as seen in enzyme classifications through enzyme commission (EC) numbers. These classifications describe enzymatic reactions through hierarchical four-numbered codes (e.g. EC 2.5.1.- classifies all alkyl or acyl transferases, and EC 2.5.1.18 denotes specifically glutathione transferases). In SwissProt, EC numbers associate 5621 unique reactions with protein sequences (UniProt release 1, 2025)—yet ~62% of these function annotations correspond to only 5% of existing ECs (Fig. 2B) and over half of the remaining ECs are associated with less than five proteins. Two other major factors complicate reaction assignment. First is the discovery of novel types of enzymatic reactions, as evident in the growth of databases like ENZYME [29] (Fig. 2C and D). Second, enzymatic functional definitions (e.g. an EC number) often do not correspond to identical catalytic mechanisms [30].

Collectively, these biases in the form of over- and under-studied functions, and inconsistent or missing information within our knowledge bases present a challenging landscape for accurate function prediction [31–33]. These, at least in part, explain the divergence in predictive accuracy between structure predictions and function annotation methods.

Reaction assignment methods primarily utilize data on protein and metabolite structures [34–36], high throughput biochemical assays [37–39], and/or system-level information like metabolic networks [40–42]. The specific tasks within reaction assignment include protein-based, metabolite-based, and reaction-based tasks, with the goal to assign reactions to proteins, metabolites or the reverse task of assigning proteins/metabolites to reactions to annotate orphan metabolites, proteins, and reactions. These tasks are not necessarily distinct within the overall goal of reaction assignment, and methods often address multiple overlapping tasks. The specific outcomes for these tasks are primarily functional classifications, such as annotations of GO terms or EC numbers to associate an enzymatic reaction to a protein. A simple binary classification is also often employed, indicating whether a protein-metabolite pair is an enzyme-substrate pair. Alternatives to these functional classifications include numeric enzyme properties describing catalytic activity such as enzyme turnover \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${k}_{cat}$\end{document} [43, 44] and Michaelis constants \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${K}_M$\end{document} [45], or dissociation constants \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${K}_d$\end{document}, related to ligand interactions [46]. Due to the relative scarcity of accurate kinetic property information, functional classification encompasses most reaction assignment methods.

Computational enzymatic reaction assignment

We broadly classify the methods of reaction assignment into three categories: (i) structure-based, (ii) network-based, and (iii) omics-based. Though each of these methodological categories tackle the problem of identifying novel reactions between metabolites and proteins, we view these distinctly as each defines the problem of reaction assignment differently according to the type of data employed with corresponding differences in methods and unique strengths and weaknesses. An overview of each method class is detailed here, highlighting notable methods and their impacts.

Structure-based methods for reaction assignment

Structure-based methods which, for simplicity, encompass both protein and molecule sequence and structures are the most prominent approaches for reaction assignment. These methods have lower requirements compared to omics- and network-based methods which may demand expensive experimental data or detailed metabolic knowledge. Structure-based methods can leverage the extensive available information about protein and metabolite structures for their development and show great versatility in their applications. Either protein-based or metabolite-based approaches can be similarly explored, and the scope of such methods can scale from specific classes of enzymatic reactions or generalize to the universe of known proteins and metabolites. Many theoretical and technical motivations for these structure-based reaction assignment methods were recently reviewed by Tripathi et al. [47]. While these methods have been widely used, it should be noted that they are limited by biases in sequence and structure [31, 48] coverages and a lack-of metabolic context, which network- and omics-based techniques can better address.

Reaction assignment by structural homology

Structure-activity-relationship of proteins and molecules has been most notable in QSAR (quantitative structure-activity relationship) for drug discovery [49], yet it has also been successful in reaction assignment. This approach generally relies on the assumption that metabolite and protein functions (e.g. catalytic reactions) are a characteristic of their sequence and structure, such that enzymatic reactions can be assigned based on this information [50–52] (Fig. 3A). This approach allows for similarity searches akin to BLAST over enzymatic reactions rather than protein sequences to assign putative enzymes to novel reactions. Fingerprint algorithms for molecular representation include the common ECFP (extended-connectivity fingerprints) algorithm [61], which encodes a molecule’s properties through iterations of hash functions on successively larger substructures. Structural combinations for reaction discovery and assignment

Among alternatives to homology methods, combinatorial methods uniquely allow for the exploration of novel metabolism from known enzymatic reactions. This technique combines known metabolism through pairs of characterized enzymatic transformations (i.e. bond changes) and substrates to generate novel enzymatic reactions. A plausible scope of undiscovered metabolism is generated by reconsidering the known aspects of metabolism, identifying reactions which may exist but have not yet been measured. A prominent application of this is based on BNICE.ch [71, 72], which has generated upwards of 5 million plausible metabolic reactions strictly from existing metabolites and enzyme reaction rules in databases [73, 74]. The complimentary BridgIT methods [7, 74] proposes enzymes for these novel and already existing orphan enzymatic reactions on the basis of reaction structure similarity between unassociated metabolic reaction and the known reactions of enzymes. Despite the many expected false positives in these combinatorially generated reactions, predicted reactions have later been discovered including the conversion of L-tryptophan to staurosporine [74], enzyme-catalyzed reactions for flavonoids [75], or a wide variety of novel Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database reactions [7].

Similarity-free enzymatic reaction assignment from structures

The direct prediction of enzymatic reactions and functions from structures beyond explicit homology-based similarity measures has been explored for decades [76–79], wherein machine learning or other statistical models describe the direct relationship between enzymatic reactions and structural features (Fig. 3B) rather than inferring function from pairs of similar protein/molecule structures [Equation (2)]. These models assign enzyme functions from structural characteristics in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${X}_{enzyme}$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${Y}_{molecule}$\end{document} of amino acid and atom features (e.g. the number of helixes and sheets in proteins [76] or reactant atoms in molecules [78]) as in Equation (1). Popular frameworks for this type of modeling include large-scale protein and chemical language models (PLMs and CLMs, respectively) that are trained on amino acid sequences or SMILES sequences (see Section Large-scale deep learning applications to structure-based reaction assignment), and graph-based learning of protein and molecular structures (see Section Network-based methods for reaction assignment).

Applications of these ideas in literature encompass a rich variety of frameworks. Here, a handful of recent interesting findings are discussed. CLEAN [80] (contrastive learning–enabled enzyme annotation) assigns EC numbers from protein sequences through a contrastive learning schema, wherein learned protein representations for function prediction are generated through training on pairs of enzymes from the same EC and different ECs. This can be thought of as implicit homology modeling with enzyme function context and exhibited strong performance in several test settings. CLEAN accurately predicted the functions of orphan halogenases that were then validated through in vitro enzymatic assays, whereas comparable models failed. While CLEAN relies on protein sequences alone, several methods use both protein and molecule information. For example, the ESP (enzyme-substrate prediction) model [36] accurately identifies substrate-enzyme pairs, leveraging a graph neural network (GNN) on 2D molecular graphs and PLM protein sequence embeddings from ESM-1b [81]. Wang et al. [82] extend a similar framework to predict potential enzyme active sites for enzymes with substrate-product pairs. SPEPP (substrate–product pair-based enzyme promiscuity prediction) [83] improves upon ESP in classifying true and false substrate-product-enzyme triads via a transformer architecture on deep learning enzyme and substrate-product pair embeddings (though it is limited to assuming substrate-product pairs from full reactions on the basis of shared atoms (>50%), rather than directly using full reactions or individual molecules as for ESP).

The ProSmith model [84] by Kroll et al. extends a transformer network using PLM (ESM-1b) and CLM (ChemBERTa-2 [85]) embeddings for protein-small molecule interactions and offers state of the art in several tasks, including enzyme-substrate classification and drug-ligand affinity. The ProSmith demonstrates the applicability of such frameworks to generalize beyond just enzymatic reactions to areas of interest like drug discovery. A recent related success is the DRAGONFLY model [86] proposed by Atz et al., which predicts drug-protein interactions based on 2D molecular graphs, 3D protein structure graphs, and known bioactivity values in the ChEMBL database. The authors demonstrate modulation of PPARγ activity by predicted ligands.

Structure-based approaches have produced interesting findings and with an increasing availability of experimental protein and metabolite data one can expect that their accuracy will increase. Transparency in the models, appropriate utilization of large-scale Machine Learning (ML) approaches—this is especially relevant for PLMs and CLMs and detailed in Section Similarity-free enzymatic reaction assignment from structures—focusing on applications matching the training data as well as extensive validation and testing on novel data are essential prerequisites to making these computational approaches impactful to biological research.

Large-scale deep learning applications to structure-based reaction assignment

Broader advances in deep learning have primarily impacted structure-based reaction assignment. While these “foundation” models have also been explored in omics modalities [87], only structural models, in the form of CLMs and PLMs, have been employed for reaction assignment thus far. These models (detailed in the excellent reviews [88, 89]) encode the chemical and physical properties of biomolecules as numeric vectors. Akin to large language models (LLMs) for natural language text, PLMs and CLMs undergo self-supervised training on the alphabet of amino acids or SMILES characters from extremely large protein sequence and chemical datasets. Relevant functional data are sometimes incorporated into training, such as quantum properties for chemicals [90] and protein GO term annotations [91, 92], to give functionally-informed representations.

Generic models for proteins and chemical representations have been leveraged for many downstream tasks including reaction assignment, with ESM [81, 93] for proteins and ChemBERTa [85, 94] for molecules as prominent examples. These types of representations are also emerging directly in biological databases, with the notable addition of ProtT5 [95] protein embeddings to UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/help/downloads#embeddings).

These advances have resulted in numerous exciting applications in reaction assignment, particularly using multi-modal approaches. Methods such as SPEPP and ProSmith incorporate both molecular and protein information through large-scale language modeling, resulting in strong performance. These methods present a promising avenue for reaction assignment, expanding the scope and pushing the boundaries of assignment methods. For example, Sicherman and Radinsky [96] devise a method to explicitly model enzyme-substrate interactions for reaction assignment through a docking-aware attention mechanism, whereas Wang and co-workers [82] were able to predict specific enzyme active sites for a given substrate. This also extends to generative modeling, demonstrated by the EnzymeFlow model [97] to generate catalytic pockets for specific substrates to design novel enzymes. Enzymatic property prediction also appears amenable to language modeling, with proKcat from Hu et al. [98] incorporating functional data modalities with sequence and structure representations incorporation.

The most attention has been given to protein-ligand prediction interaction methods, which encompass enzyme-substrate interaction prediction. These techniques represent an intersection of structure prediction and reaction assignment, with the potential to simultaneously uncover novel enzymatic reactions and the underlying molecular interactions (detailed further in Section In silico validation of novel enzymatic reactions). Already, numerous applications of the large-scale multi-modal capabilities of recent deep learning techniques illustrate their potential in enzymatic reaction assignment. It is certain that these will continue to shape the field, coupled with further advances in deep learning.

Network-based methods for reaction assignment

The evolution of enzymatic reactions is driven in part by metabolic demands of the cell [99], steering the functions of enzymes and metabolites at a system level. Thus, a full understanding of each reaction requires understanding of the network of metabolic interactions. Recognizing that this context may be missed by reaction assignment methods, many methods incorporate metabolic system information in the form of networks/graphs for reaction prediction.

The additional context of metabolic networks in reaction assignment allows for predicted reactions to be placed within the overall metabolism of the organism, directly updating the metabolic system. This allows inherent validation by simulating the metabolic system if experimental data are available. In contrast, structure-based techniques assign reactions independently at the level of individual enzymatic reactions, without integrating into existing metabolism and thus must be validated through other means. One downside to note is that although these network-based approaches can easily be applied to different organisms and metabolic systems, generally, these systems must already be well-characterized for effective reaction assignment.

These metabolic networks can be derived as knowledge-based networks, where metabolites and enzymes are connected based on experimental evidence for individual enzymatic reactions from literature sources, data-based where a network is derived from patterns in data, or hybrid where known reactions are used to optimize data driven networks. Extensively curated resources, such as KEGG [100, 101], MetaCyc [102], and RECON [103] provide knowledge-based network information for many species. Reaction assignment through network gap-filling

The structure of a network describing the known features of metabolism can be used to make predictions about any missing or unknown aspects of the network. An example problem formulation is the “gap-filling” of metabolic networks which identifies incomplete areas of the network and proposes enzymatic reactions to complete it. Network completeness is typically defined based on the consistency of simulated data from the network with experimental measurements, such as biomass production and simulations via flux balance analysis [107–109]. Proposed reactions are the minimal sets of reactions required to maximize the agreement of simulations with experimental phenotypes. Several methods to inform these assigned reactions have been developed; commonly they are assigned via known reactions/enzymes in other species. Alternatives consider sequence homology to known enzymes [110], possibly uncovering novel enzymes, or the conservation of metabolic reactions [111] to inform if a proposed reaction is likely within an evolutionary context.

Gap-filling algorithms designed to satisfy a metabolic flux-based objective have been successfully applied to metabolic networks for the discovery of novel reactions for metabolites [111, 112] and novel promiscuous functions of known enzymes [41].

Graph learning on metabolic networks for reaction assignment

In “classical” flux-based approaches, gap-filling and reaction assignment are achieved through optimization function analysis with consideration for experimental phenotypes. Alternatively, graph learning methods such as GNNs (see Information Box 1) have demonstrated strong capabilities using strictly metabolic network topology features to discriminate between correct and incorrect enzymatic reaction annotations for proteins [40] and predicting missing reactions in metabolic networks [114, 115] (Fig. 5). These methods assign enzymatic reactions purely from the characteristics of the metabolic system, avoiding the hand selected objectives used in gap-filling. This allows straightforward application to any metabolic network but must be weighed against reduced interpretability compared to gap-filling.

GNNs for metabolic reactions

Deep learning on graphs is useful for describing the natural connections found in molecular bonds, amino acid residues, and metabolic reactions. Like other machine and deep learning methods, GNNs applied to graphs produce numeric vectors that, through training, learn to capture the inherent characteristics within graph data and can be applied to many downstream tasks.

The flexibility of graph learning has also been explored to integrate other data modalities into metabolic networks. The ELP (enzymatic link prediction) model [116] combines both molecular structure fingerprints and the neighboring metabolic network information in a neural network to classify and predict enzymatic reactions between metabolite pairs. This approach achieves strong performance on bacterial and human KEGG metabolic networks and in recovering artificially removed reactions.

Generic hypergraph learning methods have been applied to ELP in metabolic networks, including Clique-Closure based Coordinated Matrix Minimization (C3MM) [118] and Neural Hyperlink Prediction (NHP) [119], as well as specific methods designed for this task. The MATBoost algorithm [120] is an early approach developed for this applied to testing to metabolic networks spanning eukaryotes, bacteria, and archaea. Two recent hypergraph learning methods for reaction assignment demonstrate improved performance over these baselines. The CHESHIRE (Chebyshev spectral hyperlink predictor) [121] and CLOSEgaps [42] models effectively predict reaction hypergraphs. Specific consideration is given to co-factor metabolites in predicted reactions by CHESHIRE, and CLOSEgaps incorporates metabolite structure similarity for prediction. The authors of these also test the recovery of experimental phenotypes through flux-based analysis of predicted metabolic networks, with networks from CHESHRE and CLOSEgaps being comparable, and both outperforming generic hypergraph methods like NHP and standard gap-filling methods for predicting phenotypes.

Graph and hypergraph learning provides an interesting framework for enzymatic reaction assignment, though further work is needed to determine the practical benefits of such methods over existing gap-filling methods. Notably, these proposed methods have not yet led to novel biological discoveries. One potential benefit may be in applications to poorly characterized organisms for which classical methods cannot model accuracy, but this remains to be seen.

Related to gap-filling and graph learning approaches, reaction assignment in a network context is explored through the methods which combine novel sets of known metabolites and enzyme reaction rules, such as BNICE [71] and BridgeIT [7] (as described in Section Computational enzymatic reaction assignment). The putative reactions proposed by these methods can be integrated into existing metabolic networks to propose expanded networks, allowing for network-level validation of added reactions as in predicting experimental phenotypes [7]. Additionally, metabolic pathways of novel reactions in these expanded networks have been demonstrated and leveraged in metabolic engineering contexts for one-carbon metabolism [122], and biosynthesis of the medicinal compound noscapine [123].

Omics-based methods for reaction assignment

Omics-based methods of reaction assignment leverage the patterns of gene expression, metabolite, or protein abundance for determining possible enzymatic reactions. These approaches benefit from the biological context provided by the experimental data informing reaction assignment, meaning that the metabolism of specific diseases and sample populations can be explored, but require crucial considerations for experimental and sample variability to ensure reliability. Though structure-based and network-based methods can suggest the possibility of an enzymatic reaction, insights from omics data provide the complementary view of revealing which reactions can occur in specific cell types or organisms.

Reaction assignment through co-expression

Commonly, omics-based methods rely on measures of co-expression or correlation, where novel functions are assigned to enzymes based on their expression patterns in relation to known enzymes or metabolites within a metabolic system (Fig. 6). Direct statistical methods are effective and interpretable, though deconvolving indirect and direct relationships to infer enzymatic reaction dynamics are non-obvious [129] and strongly dependent on sample size and characteristics, which can lead to misinterpretation of indirect or non-metabolic relationships.

Reaction assignment across omic modalities. Measurements of metabolites, transcripts, and/or proteins across biological samples are used to compute associations between biological species and causal relationships are inferred from these, such as enzymatic reactions, through various computational approaches.

Seeking to address some of these limitations are partial correlation-based methods that aim to delineate direct (i.e. catalytic reactions) and indirect relationships between metabolites and enzymes. Krumsiek et al. [38] introduces a Gaussian graphical model constructed on a partial Pearson correlation network of metabolite abundances. This approach effectively recovers known interactions between metabolites from metabolomics data and further distinguishes direct and indirect relationships between metabolites, with experimental evidence from literature supporting several predicted causal relationships. A caveat of this approach is the significant number of false positives and large number of samples necessary for partial correlation analysis, making reaction assignment challenging in many circumstances.

Alternatively, many approaches take the strategy of using correlation or co-expression as indirect features for interaction prediction, typically training a machine learning model to predict enzymatic function or reactions from the correlations [39, 130, 131]. These models leverage the information contained in correlations, often in combination with other data, to predict causal relationships between enzymes and metabolites. Toubiana et al. [39] predicts the existence of metabolic pathways in tomatoes using metabolomics-based correlation network features in a classifier model. Predicted metabolic pathways from this model were validated in vivo with the discovery of β-alanine and L-tryptophan degradation pathways in tomatoes. Likewise, Kennedy et al. [131] incorporate metabolomics and transcriptomics features in a machine learning framework for classifying protein and enzyme functions. Transporters of glutathione proposed by this method were supported in silico with structural analysis and experimental associations from literature.

Mathematical modeling of metabolic dynamics for reaction assignment

Alternate mathematical modeling of metabolite and enzyme fluctuations are explored for more direct modeling of causal relationships compared to correlation/co-expression networks [132, 133]. For example, the recent work of Li et al. [134] estimates causality in metabolite-metabolite and metabolite-enzyme pairs from metabolomic and transcriptomic data combined with metabolic network structure. Inverse Jacobian matrices and particularly differential Jacobian describing changes in metabolite concentrations between conditions are estimated from enzyme activity fluctuations (inferred from gene expression) that influence reaction rates within the metabolic network. Resulting inverse Jacobians inform the causal relationships between metabolite and enzymes with putative interactions derived from down/upstream regulation or direct enzyme-substrate activity. Inclusion of prior network information makes optimization tasks possible, although once again many samples are necessary. This unique approach has revealed multiple metabolic interactions between metabolites and enzymes [135, 136].

Impacts of novel high-throughput technologies

Continual improvements in high-throughput experimental techniques allow for increasingly granular data to complement reaction assignment methods, such as pathway flux alterations from gene knockdowns at genome-scale [137, 138]. Chemoproteomic analyses [139, 140] allow simultaneous experimental investigations of enzyme-metabolite interactions, greatly broadening the scope of omics-based approaches to potentially include non-enzymatic interactions. While these have led to exciting experimental findings, reaction assignment methods for these data have yet to emerge. Additionally, improvements in single-cell technologies make cellular-level metabolic networks and reaction discovery feasible. The recent METAFlux method [141] explores this by refining metabolic networks according to constructed gene expression clusters in single cell RNAseq data for flux balance analysis. Though this method has been employed for characterizing active pathways in metabolism, the application to cell-group level reaction discovery is an exciting future area of exploration.

The many simultaneous and complimentary advances in experimental techniques coupled with decreasing costs of “classical” omics experiments provide a tail wind to omics-based reaction assignment methods. With this in mind, we expect both the power of these computational tools, and their usefulness to researchers to grow substantially in the coming years.

Benchmarks and validations

Validating predicted reaction assignments is arguably the most crucial step of the reaction assignment pipeline. When possible, predicted reactions can be validated using experimental measurements, where prediction is hypothesis generating for experimentation. However, prior to experimentation and thanks to significant availability of related metabolic reaction information, predicted reactions can and can be validated in silico against proven related information. Ground-truth information needs to be well-organized and expertly curated in order to provide reliable sources. For each type of prediction result and specifics of the available data, it is essential to choose right methodology for final assessment of validity. Thus, in establishing reaction assignment methods, it is essential to establish, as part of the prediction pipeline, links to ground-truth metabolic knowledge and choose in silico validation tools and benchmarks to support the predicted enzymatic reactions by these methods.

Reaction assignment data sources

Databases containing known metabolic reactions for various organisms vary in their curation, information content, and primary focus. A number of recent reviews have delineated characteristics of available metabolic reaction databases [142, 143]. Extending beyond primary data sources, several applications compile metabolic reactions from databases and predictive modeling [72]. Databases containing metabolic reaction information range from highly specialized containing detailed information about enzymes such as BRENDA [13] to collections of information about metabolic reactions providing information about enzyme, metabolites and reactions including KEGG [100, 101], Rhea [144], SABIO-RK [145], and MetaCyc [102]. General databases of biological molecules such as the protein database—UniProt [146] metabolites—HMDB [16] or lipids—LIPID MAPS [147] also contain some metabolic reaction information in combination to other types of information about the molecules (e.g. molecular properties, cellular localization etc.). Information about reactions organized in pathways and networks, is available in some databases, with more complete information combined into genome-scale models of metabolism, available in the BiGG models database [148]. Specifically related to reaction assignment, orphan proteins and metabolites without known metabolic functions are abundant in general databases such as UniProt and HMDB, suggesting significant undiscovered metabolism [74, 149–151] as targets for reaction assignment. Prešern and Goličnik [143] provide a detailed list of available metabolic reaction databases with relevant information for their use as a resource for different applications including reaction prediction validation. Although there is an increased understanding for the need for standardization of nomenclature and formatting of information for more effective Artificial Intelligence (AI) utilization [143] including reaction prediction and validation, there is still major discrepancy in these resources requiring further effort from wider research community.

One such effort is in the development of compilations of metabolic reaction information from many databases and publications specifically for benchmarking and validation of enzymatic reaction assignment methods (Table 2). The Classification and Retrieval of Enzymes (CARE) [162] benchmark is a comprehensive resource for enzymatic reaction assignment composed of manually curated EC-protein pairs from SwissProt with EC-reaction pairs from the EnzymeMap [164] and ECReact [165] datasets. CARE further stratifies this dataset into several benchmark tasks of various levels of difficulty, including datasets of promiscuous enzymes annotated to multiple EC numbers, or data split on reaction types (e.g. EC 2.5.1.- alkyl and acyl transferase reactions are exclusive to the test dataset).

Reliable benchmarks are essential resources for proper evaluation, comparison, and development of reaction assignment methods. However, existing datasets are largely limited to amino acid sequences for enzymes coupled with reactant and product SMILES, and EC numbers. Validating assigned reactions

Reaction assignment model evaluation and validation

Several strategies have been used to promote robustness and generalizability of the methods, ensuring no overfitting or information leakage between test and training sets in model building and utilization. When working with sequences and structures, careful consideration of the data is needed to avoid biases and information leakage in method evaluation. In the case when testing and training data contain only homologous species, the effectiveness for unknown, novel proteins and chemicals would not be explored. Proper evaluation to avoid this limitation can be achieved through structural data splits, such that the evaluation data are structurally distinct from the training data. For proteins, this can be achieved by thresholding sequence or structural similarity (e.g. TM-score [166]) between data splits. With respect to molecules, scaffold splits [156] are commonly applied to divide the data such that each split contains unique fundamental molecular substructures.

With new experimental discoveries, it is possible to validate methods on a temporal basis, where methods can be evaluated on newly discovered enzymatic reactions. In this framework, data available only up to a specified cut-off date are used in model development, followed by model evaluation using data produced after the cut-off date. Temporal splits are an effective validation technique as they reduce potential biases (i.e. a date cutoff is difficult to find that would favor a particular method). CASP and CAFA employ this strategy, where evaluation is applied on structures and functions discovered in the 6 months following method submissions, making data leakage almost impossible.

Beyond these, recent proposed methods aim to produce tougher data splits for molecules and minimize information leakage [157]. For example, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering has been proposed as a more accurate structural split over scaffold splitting [157]. Here, molecular fingerprints are reduced via UMAP [158] to identify structurally distinct clusters of fingerprints which determines molecules for method evaluation. Another alternative, Klarner et al. [152] introduce spectral splitting which explicitly aims to minimize overlap by maximizing covariate shift in the training and test splits. For both alternatives, model performance is worse relative to the standard scaffold split schema. This indicates reduced data leakage under the proposed strategies and provides a clearer understanding of a model’s true generalization capabilities. These critical considerations for addressing limitations in current evaluation strategies are essential for improving reaction assignment methods and driving meaningful biological discoveries.

Data leakage and evaluation methods have been less explored in protein modeling compared to molecular modeling; however, these challenges are evident [153, 154]. Proposed alternatives aim to minimize structural overlap, where distinct protein sequence or structure clusters are identified and selected for method evaluation to reduce data leakage [155, 159]—similarly to UMAP clustering.

Most relevant to enzymatic reaction assignment, data splitting methods on reactions are rare. Two recent methods explore reaction mechanism-based data splits. Bradshaw et al. [160] investigated reaction split in the context of organic chemistry reactions, and method CLIPZyme [161] applying it for enzymatic reaction assignment. To our knowledge, no other reaction-based splits have been explored, and we further note that no specialized techniques have been explored for evaluations of methods using metabolic network or omics profile data, despite (at least conceptual) feasibility. For example, an evolutionary split could be applied to split datasets according to the evolutionary relationships of the underlying organisms to evaluate methods to evolutionary distinct metabolism. The benefits of such evaluations are illustrated by the recent work of Yılmaz et al.[163] exploring the impact of over- and under-studied protein biases in protein–protein interaction (PPi) networks on link prediction methods. These findings likely extend to the many over- and under-studied reactions in metabolic networks, meaning bias-aware evaluations proposed for PPi networks could also benefit reaction assignment methods on metabolic networks.

In silico validation of novel enzymatic reactions

Viewing reaction prediction models as a means to generate novel hypothetical reactions, following model validation, true utility of the approach can only be determined with confirmation of the predicted enzymatic reactions. While biochemical experiments offer the most definitive evidence, computational simulations can provide a complimentary lens to examine substrates and enzymes. Molecular simulations are commonly employed for this, including molecular mechanics (MM) [167–170], molecular docking (“docking”) [171, 172], and molecular dynamics (MD) [173, 174]. In general MM simulations approximate interaction between molecules and atoms with Newtonian mechanics, either calculating optimal interaction energies and structures (MM or docking) over time steps to provide information about many-body dynamic behavior (MD). In this way, MD simulates interaction between different partners, e.g. enzymes and metabolites, viewed over time. Docking methods computing interaction forces only between specific molecular partners, e.g. a small molecule ligand and relevant protein, provide information about the binding strength and interaction site. Docking has seen success in screening large libraries of potential enzyme-substrate pairs to discover novel enzymatic reactions [168, 175]. Both MD and docking can be used to determine interaction between substrate and products and their proposed enzyme partners as well as analysis of transition state of the complex system.

MM simulations are however not sufficient for understanding specific atomic interactions and, in particular, for enzymatic reactions which involve essential non-covalent interactions [176, 177] that are poorly described by classical physics [178]. Therefore, quantum mechanics (QM) is necessary to capture the details of electron, bond, and stereochemistry changes within enzymatic reactions. QM simulation offers superior accuracy over MM at the significant computational cost, with simulations currently possible for hundreds of atoms [179] compared to tens of thousands of atoms by MD [180]. Hybrid QM/MM methods apply QM-level simulation to the most relevant atoms while the remainder of the system is abstracted to the MM-level, allowing for biologically relevant simulations [181–184]; though model parameterization poses obstacles for reliable simulation [184–187]. Despite challenges, QM methods remain the state-of-the-art for enzymatic reaction modeling, leading to a number of biochemical discoveries [176, 179, 180]. Ongoing advancements in their efficiency and robustness (e.g. through ML augmentation [188]) will continue to expand their utility with improving computational resources.

Complimenting these methods, recently proposed DL methods for generating protein-ligand interaction poses and energies—which are trained to recover experimentally determined interactions—have shown strong performance [189–192]. These methods have rapidly progressed in recent years [190, 191, 193], with potential for highly efficient and accurate predictions of interactions in the near future. However, a number of roadblocks for these methods are yet to be fully overcome. Most notably, invalid docking poses (e.g. chiral clashes, or invalid chemical bonds) hamper the utility of such models, as identified by the PoseBusters benchmarks [194], which has since been incorporated as common benchmarks for new methods. Furthermore, generalizing to structurally distinct proteins and ligands remains a significant challenge. Recent investigations suggest that state-of-the-art methods, including AlphaFold 3 [191] and RoseTTAFold [190], struggle to learn underlying interaction dynamics, leading to an inability to predict novel or perturbed interactions [195].

Ultimately, these direct simulations, and Deep Learning (DL)-based alternatives, provide useful tools for investigating the outcomes of enzymatic reaction assignments before biochemical characterization. Computational feasibility and accuracy will likely continue to improve in the future, leading to further use of these techniques.

Major remaining challenges

Most current methods focus on predicting the likelihood of reaction involvement for enzymes or metabolites. For more complete reaction assignment, enzyme-substrate relationships must be quantified, which then allows approximations by algorithmic methods. Enzymatic properties such as affinity and catalytic rates are natural quantities of function and substrate specific. Binary classification labels of enzyme-metabolite pairs as true or false enzyme-substrate pairs are also intuitive, though the definition of negative interactions requires consideration. Known enzymatic reactions are defined according to experimental data; however, negative interactions (i.e. the absence of interaction between an enzyme and a metabolite) are rarely reported or quantified—leading to a dramatic skew in the distribution of our knowledge of enzyme-metabolite interactions, favoring positive interactions.

Although past approaches have produced negative enzyme-metabolite pairs through random sampling of shuffled enzyme-substrate pairs [44, 196, 197], most recent methods leverage structural similarity of known substrates and enzymes to inform negative interactions [36, 198, 199]. This gives “hard” datasets where negative pairs are structurally similar to the true positive interactions but assumed to not interact in nature [36]. This assumption, however, is dubious as interactions between non-substrate metabolites and enzymes may be common [140, 200]. Designing effective negative enzyme-substrate datasets has thus been noted as an open challenge [199].

Additionally, many other interactions exist between proteins and small molecules: activation, inhibition, transport, and cofactor/enzyme binding. Distinguishing these types of interactions is important in understanding biology and the scope of enzymatic reactions. However, these types of interactions are relatively uncharacterized and thus sparse within metabolic databases and models, which pose a challenge for reaction assignment methods. Designing methods specifically for tasks such as transporter interactions where no chemical transformation occurs [4, 131, 201, 202], or constraining interactions to known catalytic pockets of enzymes [82, 97], improve the granularity and interpretability of interaction predictions.

Other aspects of reaction assignment introduce further challenges, like the use of EC numbers and GO terms, where incorrect functional annotations can arise from the curation and underlying experimental data [203, 204]. Ultimately, these illustrate the many challenges and advances made in the understanding and accurate definition of enzymes and their interactions with metabolites, along with their impact on reaction assignment methods and downstream biological research.

Conclusion

As biological research expands, our understanding of metabolism struggles to keep pace with the millions of proteins and thousands of metabolites discovered in high-throughput experiments. This underscores the critical need for computational approaches to function prediction. Reaction assignment methods predict the functions of metabolites and proteins through enzymatic reactions and their substrates-product pairs. Here, three data domains are prominent: protein and molecular structure-based, network-based, and omics-based assignment which address metabolite-, protein-, and reaction-based tasks to assign functions or orphan aspects of metabolism. Computational approaches in each domain have already uncovered many novel metabolic reactions, enzyme and gene functions, and advanced system-level understanding of metabolism, providing a useful tool for biological researchers.

Along with those discussed here, many other interesting approaches for reaction assignment have been developed. These include methods that predict enzymatic reactions from scientific text, biomedical knowledge graphs, or based on unique outputs like predicting enzymatic assay outcomes. Coupled with the many exciting advances in experimental techniques for studying, novel reaction assignment approaches will continue to emerge and find continued success in the future.

We expect that the capabilities of reaction assignment methods will continue to improve as data availability increases, computational costs decrease, and further attention is drawn to this promising research domain. In particular, maturing experimental technologies like single-cell and chemoproteomics, combined with advancing computational tools for molecular simulation and deep learning for multi-modal integration will provide tail winds for fine-grained understanding and prediction of enzymatic reactions. Further advances in this area will shape our understanding of fundamental metabolism and accelerate downstream goals in metabolic and protein engineering as well as drug discovery.
