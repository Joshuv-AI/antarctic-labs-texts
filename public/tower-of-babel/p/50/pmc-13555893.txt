Undergraduate nursing students’ perceptions, needs, and expectations regarding large language model–based virtual patients: a qualitative study
Authors: Jianwen Zeng, Xule Zhu, Xin Liu, Sixie Li, Shiying Shen, Qiong Zhang, Shihua Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunication competence is a core component of clinical competence among undergraduate nursing students. However, conventional communication teaching is often constrained by limited contextual authenticity, insufficient opportunities for repeated practice, and inadequate feedback support. Large language model–based virtual patients (LLM-VPs) offer a new approach to nursing communication training, but limited evidence exists on how undergraduate nursing students, based on their prior communication-learning experiences, anticipate the educational value, functional requirements, and boundaries of such systems.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore undergraduate nursing students’ anticipated perceptions, functional needs, preliminary acceptance, and views on potential risks and boundaries regarding the use of LLM-VPs for communication training before they had used a dedicated LLM-VP system. The findings were intended to provide early evidence for the integration of this technology into nursing education.MethodsThis study employed a descriptive qualitative design. A total of 15 undergraduate nursing students were recruited through convenience sampling. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews after written informed consent had been obtained from each participant. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently until theoretical saturation was reached. The collected data were coded and analyzed using thematic analysis, with the assistance of NVivo 14 software.ResultsFour main themes were identified: emerging educational gaps and competence needs; expected system form and design requirements; mechanisms underlying trust and sustained use; and perceived risks and boundaries of applicability. Overall, nursing students expressed cautiously optimistic expectations toward LLM-VPs and articulated diverse functional expectations for aligning these systems with nursing-specific learning needs.ConclusionsFrom the anticipatory perspective of undergraduate nursing students, LLM-VPs have practical appeal and potential utility in nursing communication education. However, their educational value remains conditional and requires further validation in real-use studies. Students expected LLM-VPs to function not as replacements for real clinical encounters but as supplementary training tools that support repeated practice, immediate feedback, and preclinical rehearsal. Future implementation should be grounded in nursing-specific adaptation, deep curricular integration, rigorous knowledge verification, ethical governance, and empirical evaluation of real-world educational effects to support standardized, cautious, and sustainable use in nursing education.

Introduction

Effective patient communication is a foundational competency for ensuring patient safety, improving the quality of care, and enhancing patient outcomes [1, 2]. It should therefore be systematically strengthened during the training of health care professionals. According to Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, learners can effectively acquire relevant skills only through concrete experience [3, 4]. In medical and nursing education, providing students with extensive opportunities to engage with authentic clinical situations is essential. Training involving patient encounters not only enhances nursing students’ communication competence but also facilitates the development of their information-gathering skills [5]. At the same time, simulation-based education, as a cornerstone of medical and nursing training, provides learners with safe and repeatable opportunities for clinical practice by creating realistic practice environments, thereby strengthening practical skill development while reducing patients’ exposure to potential risks [6]. Although simulation-based education has substantial pedagogical value, traditional simulation approaches still face multiple practical constraints. Conventional training modalities, such as interactions with real patients or standardized patients, often impose psychological pressure on learners and offer limited opportunities for repeated practice [7, 8]. In addition, these approaches are frequently constrained by several factors, including patient availability, the costs of recruiting and training actors, and the logistical demands of organizing such activities [9]. Other methods, such as peer role-play, are widely used because of their accessibility and feasibility; however, students often struggle to effectively simulate critical communication skills and emotional expression because of limited knowledge, inadequate performance ability, and feelings of embarrassment [10].

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has been reshaping medical and health professions education by advancing personalized learning through adaptive learning platforms, virtual simulation systems, and diagnostic tools, thereby optimizing instructional models, improving knowledge retention, and enhancing student learning outcomes [11, 12]. In particular, the development of large language models (LLMs) has enabled the generation of conversational responses that align closely with human cognitive processes through the modeling of grammatical and semantic patterns underlying human communication [8]. Moreover, in processing large volumes of medical literature and electronic health records (EHRs), LLMs have demonstrated the ability to extract detailed clinical information from patient discharge summaries [13]. In medical and nursing education, LLMs have broad potential applications, including self-directed learning, scenario-based simulation, and writing support. Functionally, they extend beyond the limitations of traditional learning approaches in 4 principal dimensions: temporal and spatial flexibility, learner engagement, efficiency, and personalized support [14]. Given these capabilities and advantages, LLMs may facilitate the implementation of high-fidelity simulation by enabling the development of virtual patients that can accurately emulate real patients in educational settings, thereby alleviating some of the challenges associated with traditional simulation and driving transformation in conventional teaching approaches.

At present, LLM-based virtual patients (LLM-VPs) has attracted substantial attention [15]. Existing studies have primarily focused on their feasibility in medical education and their potential to improve communication skills and learning outcomes. Overall, this body of research supports the value of LLM-VPs as an effective approach for communication training in medical students. In nursing education, similar positive effects on nursing students’ nurse-patient communication have also been reported [5, 16, 17]. However, most prior research has focused on the effects of LLM-VPs on individual learning outcomes after implementation. Less attention has been given to the preimplementation perspectives of nursing students as intended end users, particularly their anticipated views, functional needs, and concerns shaped by prior communication-learning experiences before formal system development, optimization, and curricular integration.

For nursing students who have not yet used a dedicated LLM-VP system, their perspectives should not be interpreted as evaluations of actual system effectiveness or educational fit. Nevertheless, such perspectives can provide important early evidence for learner-centered design, context-sensitive development for nursing education, and subsequent implementation research. Without this preimplementation evidence, system functions may diverge from nursing students’ actual learning difficulties, the core competencies targeted in nursing communication training, and the practical contexts in which such systems would be used. This mismatch may ultimately compromise future acceptability, educational relevance, and implementation sustainability.

The present qualitative study therefore explored undergraduate nursing students’ perspectives on LLM-VPs. Because participants had not previously used LLM-VPs, the study did not aim to evaluate actual effectiveness. Instead, drawing on students’ existing experiences with communication learning, it examined their anticipated perceptions, needs, expected value, preliminary acceptance, and potential concerns regarding the use of LLM-VPs for communication learning and training. Specifically, the study addressed four research questions: (1) What core difficulties and unmet needs do undergraduate nursing students experience in current communication learning and training? (2) Based on their prior learning experiences, what forms of support and key design features do undergraduate nursing students expect LLM-VPs to provide in future communication training? (3) What factors may influence undergraduate nursing students’ preliminary trust, acceptance, and expectations for future use of LLM-VPs? (4) How do undergraduate nursing students anticipate the potential risks, ethical concerns, and boundaries of applicability of LLM-VPs in communication training?

By clarifying these preimplementation perspectives, this study may inform the learner-centered design, context-specific development, and preliminary curricular integration of LLM-VPs in nursing education. It also provides early empirical evidence for developing nursing communication training models that are grounded in learner needs and that better connect classroom learning with clinical practice.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design [18] to explore undergraduate nursing students’ perceptions of and expectations for the use of LLM-VPs in communication learning. Data were collected through face-to-face semistructured interviews. This design was selected because it allows participants to provide relevant information in a structured manner while retaining the freedom to express their personal views [19], thereby generating credible data that closely reflect nursing students’ authentic perspectives on the use of LLM-VPs for communication learning. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [20] (Supplementary Material 1).

Setting and participants

Participants were recruited from Hangzhou Normal University in Hangzhou, China, between December 2025 and February 2026. In this qualitative study, purposive sampling was used to recruit participants who met criteria relevant to the study topic, thereby ensuring the relevance and depth of the sample. The final sample size was determined according to the principle of data saturation, that is, when no new information emerged and responses became repetitive [21].

The first author distributed recruitment information through online communication channels commonly used by students, including WeChat (Tencent) and DingTalk (Alibaba). The recruitment notice described the study purpose, eligibility criteria, interview arrangements, voluntary nature of participation, and researcher contact information. Students who were interested in the study contacted the first author directly. The first author then assessed eligibility and arranged the interview. Course instructors, college administrators, and teaching management staff were not involved in recruitment, eligibility screening, or interview scheduling and were not informed of participant identities. Before the interviews, all participants confirmed that they had not previously used LLM-VPs and had not participated in any formal learning, training, or demonstration activities involving LLM-VPs. All recruited participants volunteered to take part in the study and were able to communicate in Mandarin Chinese. No eligible students who contacted the research team declined participation. Data collection

Data were collected through semistructured interviews conducted face-to-face by 1 researcher who had received formal training in qualitative research and had prior interviewing experience. We adopted the interview framework proposed by Krueger and Casey [22], and, in line with the study objectives, developed a preliminary interview guide based on a review of the literature, discussions within the research team, and consultation with qualitative research experts. The guide was then pilot-tested with 3 undergraduate nursing students; data from the pilot interviews were not included in the formal study. The interview questions were subsequently refined on the basis of participant feedback and the pilot interview content, and the final interview guide was established (Supplementary Material 2).

All interviews were conducted in a quiet and private environment to ensure continuity of thought and minimize interruptions. Before each interview, the researcher reiterated the study purpose and interview content and obtained both verbal and written informed consent, including permission for audio recording. Before the formal interview began, the researcher first asked whether participants had heard of or understood LLM-VPs. The researcher then provided all participants with a standardized conceptual explanation covering the basic meaning of LLM-VPs, their main functions, and their potential application scenarios in nursing communication training. A brief comprehension check was conducted before the interview proceeded. During the interviews, the researcher used responsive, interpretive, summarizing, and reiterative techniques to encourage participants to share their views and expectations regarding the use of LLM-VPs in communication learning. All interviews were audio-recorded anonymously.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis [23]. The analysis team comprised nursing professionals with diverse research expertise, including nursing informatics, nursing education, and psychological and mental health nursing, and this supported a broader interpretation of the data [22, 24]. First, the first author transcribed the original audio data verbatim using a speech-to-text tool. The transcripts were then checked independently by 2 authors for accuracy and compiled into Word documents. These documents were subsequently imported into NVivo 14 (Lumivero) for further analysis.

The authors repeatedly read the transcripts in detail to obtain an overall understanding of the interview data. The data were then examined carefully, with individual concepts or sentences selected as meaning units. After the original expressions had been identified, the authors condensed and coded the data, grouped codes with similar meanings, and assigned descriptive labels. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between the 2 authors, with reference to the original audio recordings when necessary. The entire coding process was supervised by another researcher to enhance credibility, and this researcher also participated in random checks of the analysis to ensure consistency.

The resulting categories were then further merged into higher-order categories, with continuous attention to their alignment with the research questions. Each research question was analyzed separately. When new insights emerged, they were discussed in team meetings, and revisions were proposed until consensus was reached among all authors.

Ethics

The study was carried out in accordance with the ethical standards established in the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of School of Public Health and Nursing, Hangzhou Normal University (No. 2,026,009). Before the interviews, the researcher explained the study purpose, interview content, use of audio recordings, data storage procedures, and participant rights, and obtained written informed consent. All data were deidentified by replacing names with study codes, and identifiable information was removed or masked. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored in encrypted files accessible only to the research team. Findings were reported in aggregate form. Participation was entirely voluntary. Students could refuse participation or withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. Participation, nonparticipation, or withdrawal had no effect on course grades, academic evaluation, practicum or internship opportunities, or relationships with instructors, the college, or the university. Course instructors and teaching management staff were not involved in recruitment or interviews and were not informed of participant identities.

Thematic overview of the interview data

Analysis of the interview data yielded 4 major themes: (1) emergence of pedagogical gaps and competency needs (Drivers), (2) expectations for the ideal system form and design requirements (Needs), (3) mechanisms shaping trust and sustained use (Willingness), and (4) perceived risks and boundaries of applicability (Challenges).

Overall, undergraduate nursing students’ views of LLM-VPs showed a clear dual character. On the one hand, participants explicitly recognized substantial gaps in current communication learning and training and regarded LLM-VPs as a promising digital training tool with the potential to bridge the divide between classroom learning and clinical practice. On the other hand, they did not express unconditional optimism toward the technology. Instead, they simultaneously highlighted important limitations related to realism, knowledge accuracy, ethical governance, technological maturity, and the educational boundaries of appropriate use. Emergence of pedagogical gaps and competency needs

This theme captures nursing students’ reflections on the limitations of traditional communication teaching and on their own communication difficulties. Participants suggested that although current approaches provide some basic training value, they are insufficient to meet the demands of authentic clinical communication development. They also described their communication challenges not as a single problem of “poor communication skills,” but as a layered combination of cognitive, emotional, and practical difficulties.

Limited transferability of traditional teaching to real clinical practice

Participants reported that communication teaching in nursing programs was mainly delivered through classroom role-play, with teachers or students acting as patients, the use of standardized patients, or in some cases lecture-based teaching supported only by PowerPoint slides. They perceived these approaches as limited by insufficient clinical realism, restricted opportunities for repeated practice, and inadequate feedback and instructional guidance. Participants’ self-perceived communication difficulties extended beyond verbal expression alone and spanned 3 interrelated dimensions: logical organization, emotional responsiveness, and management of complex situations. Specifically, they described difficulties in asking structured questions, managing psychological tension during real interactions, and responding appropriately in emotionally charged, complex, or professionally demanding situations.My questioning is not very logical—it tends to be rather disorganized, and I may miss some very important questions. … When faced with some of the patient’s emotions, I may not know how to handle them. Expectations for the ideal system form and design requirements

Participants’ expectations for LLM-VPs extended beyond a simple “conversational virtual patient.” Instead, they envisioned a comprehensive learning system that could integrate realistic scenario simulation, personalized feedback, knowledge support, and opportunities for sustained practice. In their view, an ideal LLM-VP should resemble real clinical encounters while also functioning as an effective educational tool; it should deliver accurate content while remaining easy to use; and it should not only enable practice but also identify and correct mistakes.

Realistic and multifaceted interactive experiences

Participants were generally dissatisfied with static, single-format, and highly templated patient simulations. They expected the system to provide training targets and interaction processes that more closely reflected clinical complexity. Realism, in their view, should include diversity in specialties, diseases, personalities, age groups, and stages of illness, as well as family communication scenarios and differences in expression associated with educational background, including dialect variation. Participants also emphasized that patient responses should not appear overly idealized or unrealistically cooperative; instead, language should more closely resemble the direct, vague, or partially uncooperative communication often encountered in real hospital settings.There should be multiple types of patients—for example, different diseases, ages, and personalities should be available to choose from. (N10)It should be more like a real patient. Its answers or understanding of questions should not be too perfect, because this is actually the case in most real clinical settings. (N6)

In addition, participants preferred multimodal interaction rather than a single communication format. They believed that features such as voice-based communication, humanlike avatars, facial expressions, microexpressions, and emotionally responsive behaviors would substantially enhance immersion and realism. Notably, preferences regarding appearance were not uniform. Although most participants favored humanlike representations, some considered cartoon-style avatars easier to accept and less anxiety-provoking. Intelligent learning and assessment mechanisms

Participants emphasized that the system should not be limited to dialogue practice alone, as such functionality would be too narrow. First, they expected the system to support modular learning across domains such as internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics, and pediatrics. In terms of training pathways, they expected progression from simple to complex tasks, with training stratified according to learner level or areas of weakness, thereby enabling adaptive difficulty adjustment. They also expected the system to save training records and display trajectories of progress, thereby supporting a more intelligent form of technology-enabled learning. Second, participants expected personalized feedback, including identification of problems, summarization of omissions, and generation of performance reports. They also expected the system to be aligned with course materials and linked to relevant clinical guidelines, thereby creating a closed learning loop of practice, feedback, remediation, and repeated practice.I hope there could be different modules, such as internal medicine and surgery, so that everyone can choose according to their own needs. Real-world boundaries of implementation

Overall, although participants recognized the potential value of LLM-VPs for communication training, they were also acutely aware of substantial challenges related to realism, knowledge accuracy, technical stability, data ethics, and educational fit. In other words, LLM-VPs were not viewed as inherently effective teaching tools; rather, their educational value was considered highly dependent on the quality of system design, mechanisms for content governance, and the extent to which they were meaningfully integrated into curricula.

Inherent limits in Simulating real patients

Most participants pointed out that the system might have clear limitations in reproducing real patients with high fidelity. In their view, because LLM-VPs are ultimately AI-based systems, they are unlikely to fully replicate the subjectivity, emotional richness, and highly variable interactive responses of real patients. Participants also worried that the system might adopt a mechanical, stereotyped, and overly procedural question-and-answer style, lacking the everyday language, narrative texture, and interpersonal warmth found in real communication, thereby weakening immersion and authenticity. This concern was especially salient in relation to humanistic care. Participants emphasized that empathy is not conveyed solely through verbal responses, but also through bodily movement, facial expression, and the immediacy of in-person presence—precisely the elements that virtual systems may struggle to represent adequately. These views suggest that nursing students regarded LLM-VPs more as a supplementary simulation tool for nurse-patient communication than as a full substitute for real patient encounters.It is mechanical and rigid, and the simulation is not realistic. … It is also somewhat difficult to cultivate empathy, because this is a virtual patient. Discussion

This qualitative study explored undergraduate nursing students’ anticipated perceptions of using LLM-VPs for communication learning and training. Participants’ views did not reflect simple acceptance or rejection. Rather, they indicated a form of conditional acceptance shaped by unmet needs in current communication training, the perceived credibility of the system, and the extent to which LLM-VPs could be meaningfully integrated into nursing curricula. Because participants had not used a dedicated LLM-VP system, the value they described should be interpreted as perceived potential value rather than demonstrated educational effectiveness. Students generally viewed LLM-VPs as a potentially accessible, repeatable, low-risk, and feedback-oriented transitional learning environment that could help bridge classroom instruction and clinical practice, rather than as a substitute for teachers, standardized patients, or real patient encounters.

The demand for LLM-VPs expressed by nursing students can be understood as a response to structural limitations in conventional communication training. Difficulties in organizing clinical information, responding to patients’ emotions, and managing complex interpersonal situations may be related to limited opportunities for authentic, frequent, low-risk, and repeatable practice, as well as insufficient formative feedback. Nursing education has long faced a substantial gap between the theoretical knowledge students acquire in academic settings and the practical skills required in authentic clinical environments, partly because of reduced learning opportunities and the lack of structured frameworks for translating knowledge into practice [27, 28]. According to Kolb’s experiential learning theory [3, 4], the development of communication competence in nursing depends on repeated cycles of concrete experience and active experimentation. Traditional communication teaching, however, often lacks the lived experience of illness and the emotional depth of authentic encounters, leaving learners disengaged and limiting deeper reflection. In addition, differences in background and developmental goals may create discrepancies between teachers’ and students’ perceptions of instructional needs, which may in turn affect educational outcomes [29]. Taken together, the problems reported by students should not be understood merely as deficits in individual expressive ability, but rather as learner-level manifestations of the limitations of traditional teaching.

Course structure, teaching methods, and simulation fidelity jointly shape students’ self-perceived readiness for practice and their ability to transfer learning into practice [26]. Accordingly, students expected LLM-VPs to function not merely as conversational agents but as educational systems capable of supporting experiential, reflective, and progressive learning. First, their potential effectiveness in nursing education depends heavily on nursing-specific relevance and contextual authenticity. Nursing communication involves not only information gathering but also emotional reassurance, health education, family communication, facilitation of adherence, and humanistic care [30, 31]. Students consequently expected LLM-VPs to support diverse patient scenarios and to use multimodal features to approximate clinical language, patient responses, and nonverbal cues. They also considered LLM-VPs attractive because such systems could integrate several elements that are often fragmented in traditional communication training: scenario exposure, repeated practice, immediate feedback, and self-correction. In this sense, an ideal LLM-VP system would support a continuous learning loop of practice, feedback, remediation, and further practice. Such a design may help address students’ perceived lack of preparedness for clinical work, which is often intensified by fragmented curricula, inconsistent teaching strategies, and insufficient integration between academic learning and clinical practice [32].

The findings also suggest that nursing students’ anticipated adoption of LLM-VPs is strongly pragmatic and task oriented. Their willingness to continue using such systems does not arise automatically, but is built on explicit evaluations of expected benefits and practical conditions. Their willingness to use such systems was not automatic; it depended on perceived benefits and realistic conditions of use. This pattern can be interpreted through concepts from the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) [33]. When students perceive performance expectancy—that is, when they believe the system can provide tangible, transferable, and educationally meaningful benefits—positive use intentions are more likely to emerge. Effort expectancy, reflected here in concerns about cost, convenience, and the burden of learning to use the system, indicates that nursing students already operate under substantial academic load [34, 35]. Under such circumstances, any educational technology that demands additional cognitive effort or involves complicated operating procedures is likely to encounter resistance in practice. At the same time, curriculum embedding and teacher or peer evaluation—forms of social influence—appeared to have dual effects. These factors may function as facilitating conditions, but they may also become sources of passive compliance if imposed rigidly. This distinction is important. When an interactive system is combined with appropriate pedagogical strategies, it can enhance educational realism and promote reflection [36]. Moreover, when incorporated into a well-designed curriculum, high-fidelity simulation may provide greater educational benefit to learners [37, 38]. Therefore, the effective application of LLM-VPs in nursing education depends not only on the system itself but also on institutional arrangements and flexible instructional design by teachers. More importantly, in simulation-based educational technologies such as LLM-VPs, system realism and content accuracy constitute the foundation of trust. Students are more likely to engage with these systems when they perceive the scenarios as clinically realistic and the responses as professionally accurate. Sustained adoption is therefore unlikely to be driven by technological novelty alone; rather, it depends on a multidimensional mechanism involving usefulness, ease of use, credibility, and curricular embeddedness.

Existing medical education studies have begun to examine the use of LLMs in simulation-based training. Borg et al. integrated an LLM with a social robotic platform to create more authentic virtual patient encounters for clinical reasoning training [39]. Öncü et al. reported that a ChatGPT-4o–driven virtual standardized patient could be used to assess intern physicians’ competencies in clinical case management [40]. Wang et al. showed that ChatGPT was feasible as a standardized patient in simulated medical assessment [41]. A recent scoping review of LLM-based virtual patients in medical education identified several limitations in current systems, including reliance on scripted and relatively narrow scenarios, limited use of real electronic health record data, simplified patient personalities and emotional styles, restricted multimodal interaction, inadequate longitudinal tracking of learner progress, and limited support for modular training, tiered learning, intelligent feedback, and automated assessment [15]. In nursing education, research on LLM-VPs remains more limited. For example, Kim et al. reported that GPT-based virtual patients may help novice nurses improve health assessment and communication abilities [17]. Overall, LLM-VP research in both medicine and nursing remains at an early stage. Existing studies have primarily examined immediate effects on communication skills or clinical competence, whereas less attention has been given to learners’ needs, sustained adoption mechanisms, and the curricular conditions under which these systems may generate educational value. The expectations and concerns identified in this study should therefore be understood in relation to both the current stage of technological development and the unresolved challenges of educational implementation.

Notably, participants’ anticipated attitudes toward LLM-VPs were accompanied by a clear awareness of boundaries and a strong capacity for risk recognition, rather than unidirectional technological optimism. Students recognized that virtual patients cannot fully replace the embodied presence, emotional complexity, and relational tension of real patient interactions. This is especially true in the cultivation of humanistic care and deep empathy, where authentic clinical contact remains irreplaceable. Some of these limitations may be alleviated through integration with more advanced technological ecosystems, such as social robots, mixed reality (MR), and virtual reality (VR), to support higher levels of interaction [39, 42]. Students were also highly sensitive to the accuracy and timeliness of knowledge content. This is unsurprising, as any incorrect professional information, fabricated content, or inappropriate communication guidance in nursing education may directly compromise learning quality and even distort clinical understanding [43, 44]. Future LLM-VP systems could incorporate retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [45] and knowledge graphs (KGs) [46] to improve the accuracy, reasoning quality, and contextual relevance of system outputs. If real clinical cases are used to support system development, collaboration between developers and hospitals will be required to obtain authorized access to such data. Concerns related to privacy leakage may be partly addressed through local model deployment [47] or privacy-preserving large language model approaches based on adaptive compression [48]. Risks such as student overreliance on the system or gradual assimilation to AI-mediated communication patterns will still require active teacher guidance and broader institutional education. The central question is therefore not whether LLM-VPs can be introduced into nursing education, but whether they can be appropriately positioned, designed, validated, and governed.

Although this study was based on anticipated perceptions rather than actual user experience, it provides several preliminary implications for nursing education. First, LLM-VPs may be most appropriately positioned as supplementary and transitional training tools to support preclinical preparation, extracurricular reinforcement, and formative feedback, rather than as replacements for teacher guidance, standardized patients, or real patient contact. Second, the realization of their educational value depends on curricular integration rather than mere technical availability. To enhance sustained use and learning outcomes, LLM-VPs should be aligned with nurse-patient communication courses, objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) training, pre-internship preparation, and reflective assignments. Third, teachers remain central to this process and should be responsible for setting learning goals, interpreting feedback, correcting errors, and guiding reflection so as to avoid students’ mechanical dependence on system outputs. Finally, developers and administrators should recognize that students expect an educational product designed with nursing expert involvement, multiple levels of nursing-specific scenarios, actionable feedback, and mechanisms for error correction. Governance structures addressing content quality control, privacy protection, ethical review, and operational stability should be developed in parallel. Without such safeguards, the introduction of LLM-VPs may remain a conceptual demonstration rather than a substantive improvement in nursing education.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were undergraduate nursing students recruited from a university in Hangzhou, China. The findings are therefore context dependent, and their transferability should be examined in other regions, educational systems, and cultural settings. Second, participants had not engaged in long-term or systematic use of a specific LLM-VP system. Their views were based primarily on prior understanding, expectations, and preliminary judgments about LLM-VPs. Accordingly, this study is better suited to identifying learner needs, adoption conditions, and implementation concerns than to evaluating actual educational effectiveness or sustained use behavior. Third, this study included only student perspectives and did not incorporate views from other stakeholders, such as nursing educators, clinical preceptors, administrators, or system developers. Finally, because LLM-related technologies are evolving rapidly, students’ current perceptions may be time sensitive and should be reassessed as these systems mature and become more widely implemented.

Conclusions

This study showed that undergraduate nursing students held cautiously positive and clearly bounded expectations regarding LLM-based virtual patients. They did not view LLM-VPs as a replacement for real patient contact. Rather, they perceived them as a potential supplementary bridge between classroom learning and clinical practice that may help address limitations in current communication training, including insufficient authenticity, limited practice opportunities, delayed or inadequate feedback, and weak transfer to clinical settings. Students’ anticipated willingness to use LLM-VPs was shaped by perceived usefulness, nursing relevance, curricular embeddedness, and system credibility. Future research should examine the feasibility, acceptability, and learning effects of LLM-VPs in authentic nursing education settings and should address implementation conditions such as knowledge verification, ethical governance, and teacher guidance to support cautious, standardized, and evidence-informed educational integration.
