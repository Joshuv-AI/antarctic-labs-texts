Development of a Novel HIF-2α PET Tracer for Noninvasive Tumor Hypoxia Imaging
Authors: Yanli Wang, Yongle Wang, Ping Bai, Jian Cui, Yanting Zhou, Hongyan Li, Lili Wu, Haiyan Wang, Shiming Bai, Ru Ya, Xiaoye Bai, Zhanru Liu, Tonghua Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hypoxia is a major driver of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance, with hypoxia-inducible factor 2α (HIF-2α) playing a critical regulatory role. To address the current lack of noninvasive imaging tools for HIF-2α, we developed [18F]-TC-S 7009, the F-18 labeled PET tracer designed to target HIF-2α. The probe was synthesized via copper-mediated fluorination, achieving high radiochemical purity and strong binding affinity for HIF-2α (K d = 81 nM). In vivo PET/CT imaging in U87 glioblastoma-bearing mice demonstrated efficient blood–brain barrier penetration, modest tumor uptake, and favorable tumor-to-background contrast. Blocking experiments confirmed receptor-specific binding. Region-specific brain accumulation was observed in the hippocampus, striatum, and hypothalamus. These findings establish [18F]-TC-S 7009 as a promising noninvasive molecular imaging tool for visualizing HIF-2α activity, providing a foundation for future investigations into hypoxia-driven tumor biology and the development of targeted therapies.

Introduction

The hypoxic tumor microenvironment is a hallmark of many malignant tumors, playing a critical role in their progression, aggressiveness, and resistance to treatment. Central to this adaptation is the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway, regulated by heterodimeric transcription factors composed of an oxygen-sensitive HIF-α subunit , (e.g., HIF-1α and HIF-2α) and a stable HIF-β subunit (ARNT). − These transcription factors activate the expression of genes involved in angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and cell survival under oxygen-deprived conditions. Aberrant activation of HIF pathways is closely associated with tumor invasiveness, therapeutic resistance, and poor clinical outcomes.

While both HIF-1α and HIF-2α are key mediators of the hypoxia response, they exhibit distinct roles in tumor biology. − HIF-2α has been identified as a predominant oncogenic driver in certain cancers, such as clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and glioblastoma. Unlike HIF-1α, which primarily regulates acute responses to hypoxia, HIF-2α contributes to sustained hypoxic signaling by modulating genes associated with angiogenesis (e.g., VEGFA), cell cycle regulation (e.g., Cyclin D1), and invasion (e.g., CXCR4). , Elevated levels of HIF-2α correlate with increased tumor aggressiveness, resistance to treatment, and reduced patient survival. These findings have established HIF-2α as an important therapeutic target for hypoxia-driven tumors.

In recent years, significant efforts have been made to develop small-molecule modulators targeting HIF-2α. , These include HIF-2α inhibitors such as PT2399 (Figure and its analogs, which demonstrate strong efficacy in preclinical cancer models, particularly in renal cell carcinoma. , The discovery of PT2385 (Figure , the first-in-class selective HIF-2α inhibitor, was groundbreaking as it confirmed the drug ability of the PAS-B domain of HIF-2α, which had previously been considered undruggable. , Advances have also been made in developing HIF-2α agonists and degraders to better modulate the hypoxic response. Despite these advancements, a significant gap exists in imaging tools that can noninvasively visualize HIF-2α activity in vivo, hindering our ability to monitor therapeutic outcomes and understand HIF-2α dynamics in tumor biology.

Selected HIF-2α inhibitors and tumor PET probes.

Molecular imaging plays a pivotal role in cancer diagnosis, staging, and therapeutic monitoring. , Positron emission tomography (PET) is widely used for tumor imaging, enabling noninvasive and high-resolution visualization of molecular processes in vivo. − Current PET imaging probes for tumors predominantly focus on metabolism (e.g., 18F-FDG) or hypoxia (e.g., 18F-FMISO and 64Cu-ATSM), As shown in Figure . However, these probes provide indirect measures of the hypoxic tumor environment and are not specific for HIF-2α. This lack of specificity limits our ability to study the spatial and temporal dynamics of HIF-2α expression in vivo, as well as to monitor the therapeutic effects of HIF-2α inhibitors.

In this study, we address this gap by developing the F-18 labeled PET imaging agent targeting HIF-2α. Our probe is based on TC-S 7009, a potent and selective small-molecule inhibitor of HIF-2α with a dissociation constant (K d) of 81 nM for HIF-2α and markedly lower affinity for HIF-1α (K d > 5 μM). TC-S 7009 binds to the PAS-B domain of HIF-2α, disrupting its heterodimerization with ARNT, thereby inhibiting DNA binding activity and downstream gene expression. The combination of TC-S 7009’s high selectivity and F-18 PET imaging’s sensitivity enables precise visualization of HIF-2α in tumors, facilitating insights into its role in tumor biology and treatment response.

The primary objective of this study was to develop and validate an F-18 labeled PET imaging agent for HIF-2α. Specifically, we aimed to Develop an efficient radiolabeling method for conjugating F-18 to TC-S 7009 while maintaining its biological activity; Evaluate the probe’s binding affinity, specificity, and inhibition of HIF-2α in vitro; Demonstrate the imaging efficacy of the probe in a glioblastoma mouse model, with a focus on biodistribution, tumor targeting, and imaging resolution. This study represents a pioneering effort to develop an HIF-2α-specific PET imaging probe, filling a critical gap in molecular imaging. By enabling noninvasive visualization of HIF-2α activity, this technology has the potential to advance the understanding of tumor hypoxia biology, improve the precision of cancer diagnosis, and facilitate the evaluation of novel HIF-2α-targeted therapies.

Results and Discussion

PET Radioligand Design

Developing PET imaging probes based on known target ligands is a well-established and effective strategy. , To design a PET radioligand that specifically targets HIF-2α, we selected the reported high-efficiency HIF-2α inhibitor TC-S 7009 and replaced its fluorine atom with F-18 to achieve radiolabeling.

The successful development of a HIF-2α PET radioligand requires consideration of several critical factors, particularly for imaging the brain or tumors. First, candidate compounds must exhibit sufficient binding affinity and selectivity to generate a strong imaging signal for the target. Additionally, for imaging specific organs, such as the brain, the candidate compounds must have good blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability and uptake capabilities. TC-S 7009 is an efficient HIF-2α inhibitor with a dissociation constant (K d) of 81 nM and significant selectivity for HIF-2α over HIF-1α (K d > 5 μM). This excellent selectivity is attributed to the ability of TC-S 7009 to specifically interact with the PAS-B domain of HIF-2α (a critical binding cavity), thereby inhibiting the dimerization and activity of HIF-2α. Additionally, TC-S 7009 has demonstrated potential applications in hypoxia-driven cancer models, making it a suitable candidate for radiolabeling and PET imaging. According to structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis and molecular docking studies, the fluorine atom in the TC-S 7009 molecule is located at a critical position in the PAS-B binding pocket, and its presence is essential for maintaining high binding affinity and selectivity for HIF-2α. Therefore, the radiolabeling design employed a direct substitution approach, replacing the fluorine atom in the TC-S 7009 molecule with an 18F atom to achieve 18F labeling without further modification to the molecular framework.

Through this design, the F-18 labeled compound [18F]­TC-S 7009 was generated. This compound retained the binding affinity and selectivity of the parent compound while incorporating the characteristics of a PET radioligand, making it a promising HIF-2α PET imaging probe, As shown in Figure . This innovative design provides a powerful molecular tool for studying hypoxia-related diseases and evaluating HIF-2α-targeted therapies.

Radiosynthesis of [18F]-TC-S 7009

As shown in Scheme , [18F]-TC-S 7009 was prepared via a copper-mediated [18F]­fluorination approach using the aryl boronate ester precursor (TC-S 7009 pre) in the presence of [Cu­(OTf)2(py)4], KOTf, K2CO3, and pyridine. Briefly, [18F]­fluoride generated in a cyclotron was loaded onto a QMA-Light Sep-Pak cartridge preconditioned with KOTf. The trapped activity was then eluted using an acetonitrile–water mixture containing KOTf and K2CO3. The eluate was dried by azeotropic distillation under a stream of nitrogen at 100–120 °C with additional aliquots of dry acetonitrile (2 × 0.5 mL) to ensure complete water removal. A fresh DMF solution of TC-S 7009 pre, [Cu­(OTf)2(py)4], and pyridine was subsequently added, and the mixture was heated at 120 °C for 20 min. After cooling, the reaction mixture was quenched with a 0.1% TFA aqueous solution and purified by semipreparative HPLC. The entire process was completed within 60–90 min after the end of bombardment (EOB), yielding [18F]-TC-S 7009 in an uncorrected radiochemical yield of 8.72–11.03% (based on trapped [18F]­fluoride) and a radiochemical purity of over 95%.

PET Imaging of [18F]-TC-S 7009 in Wild-Type Mice

We conducted dynamic PET/CT imaging studies to evaluate the potential of [18F]-TC-S 7009 as a HIF-2α PET radiotracer. The imaging agent was administered via tail vein injection at doses ranging from 3.7 to 5.55 MBq. This was followed by a 60 min dynamic PET scan and a 10 min CT scan. As shown in Figure , [18F]-TC-S 7009 efficiently penetrated the blood-brain barrier (BBB), reaching a peak standardized uptake value (SUV) of 0.62 approximately 1 min postinjection. Analysis of the time-activity curves (TACs) indicated that [18F]-TC-S 7009 rapidly crossed the BBB and exhibited stable binding throughout the 60 min imaging period. This prolonged retention in the brain highlights the potential diagnostic value of this tracer in brain tumors.

To validate the specificity of [18F]-TC-S 7009 binding, we performed a self-blocking experiment. Mice were pretreated with a nonradioactive dose of TC-S 7009 (0.4 mg/kg) 5 min before radiotracer injection. For blocking, unlabeled TC-S 7009 (0.4 mg/kg i.v.) provided approximately 500–800-fold molar excess over the injected tracer mass, consistent with receptor saturation. As shown in Figure A and B, the blocking group exhibited high brain uptake during the first minute after injection. However, as receptor sites became saturated, tracer binding gradually decreased. Comparative TAC analysis showed significantly reduced radioactivity in pretreated mice compared to the baseline group, confirming receptor-specific binding.

Furthermore, because HIF-2α plays critical roles in the whole brain as well as specific brain regions such as the hypothalamus, hippocampus, and striatum, a region-specific analysis of [18F]-TC-S 7009 uptake was performed. In the hypothalamus, HIF-2α is primarily involved in regulating energy metabolism and adaptation to hypoxic conditions. In the hippocampus, it provides neuroprotection against hypoxia by modulating neuronal metabolism. In the striatum, HIF-2α is closely associated with motor control and metabolic adaptation, particularly supporting neural function under localized hypoxia. Based on these characteristics, we analyzed [18F]-TC-S 7009 uptakes across the whole brain, striatum, hippocampus, and hypothalamus. Data analysis was conducted using the fusion module of PMOD software (version 4.01, PMOD Technologies Ltd., Zurich, Switzerland) in combination with the Ma-Benveniste-Mirrione mouse VOI brain atlas. As shown in Figure C, [18F]-TC-S 7009 showed robust accumulation in brain regions known for HIF-2α activity, including the striatum, hippocampus, and hypothalamus. In vivo blocking studies resulted in a statistically significant reduction in tracer uptake across all these regions, confirming the receptor specific binding of the tracer to HIF-2α. The specificity was not only evident in whole-brain SUV values but also consistent across regional uptake quantification, validating the probe’s ability to selectively target HIF-2α in physiologically relevant neuroanatomical domains, laying the groundwork for future exploration in brain tumor imaging.

The tumors were located on the dorsal side of the mice’s forepaws (as indicated in the ″Tumor″ region in Figures and ). PET scans were conducted at 10, 30, and 60 min postinjection of the tracer. Figure compares SUV values of tumor-bearing mice and normal mice at different time points. In tumor-bearing mice, the SUV value in the tumor region was 0.2168 ± 0.0025 at 10 min, 0.1648 ± 0.0072 at 30 min, and 0.0761 ± 0.0107 at 60 min. In contrast, normal mice exhibited significantly lower SUV values of 0.0365 ± 0.0019 at 10 min, 0.0286 ± 0.0023 at 30 min, and 0.0197 ± 0.0024 at 60 min. At all-time points, the SUV values in the tumor region of tumor-bearing mice were significantly higher than those in normal mice, indicating that [18F]-TC-S 7009 has strong tumor-targeting specificity.

Figure visually demonstrates that the tumor region (marked as ″Tumor″) showed a significant uptake signal as early as 10 min after tracer injection, with the SUV reaching its peak. This indicates that [18F]-TC-S 7009 can rapidly target tumor regions. Subsequently, the tumor SUV values gradually decreased at 30 and 60 min but remained significantly higher than in normal tissues. This time-dependent change may be related to the metabolism and clearance of the tracer in tumor tissues while also demonstrating strong stability of binding in the tumor region.

Combining the imaging and data, the SUV values in the tumor regions of tumor-bearing mice remained significantly higher than in normal mice, with a clear contrast. This supports the high tumor-targeting specificity of [18F]-TC-S 7009. Additionally, the high uptake and strong temporal stability of the tracer in tumor regions suggest its potential as a PET imaging probe. The tracer can rapidly target tumors and maintain high specificity and contrast over 60 min. This provides robust data support for further research and application in tumor imaging.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a novel HIF-2α-targeted tumor hypoxia PET imaging agent, [18F]-TC-S 7009, and systematically evaluated its imaging performance in wild-type mice and nude mice bearing U87 cell-derived tumors. The results demonstrate that [18F]-TC-S 7009 possesses exceptional tumor-targeting specificity and rapid targeting capabilities. Notably, in the tumor-bearing mouse model, the SUV in the tumor region was significantly higher than in normal tissues, peaking within 10 min postinjection. Despite a gradual decline in signal, the tracer maintained a favorable tumor-to-background contrast for up to 60 min. Additionally, [18F]-TC-S 7009 exhibited stable distribution in the whole brain and specific regions such as the hypothalamus, hippocampus, and striatum, supporting its diagnostic potential for brain-related diseases. These findings position [18F]-TC-S 7009 as a groundbreaking tool for imaging HIF-2α in hypoxic tumors, offering a valuable resource for studying hypoxia-related diseases and developing targeted therapies.

Discussion

The development of a novel HIF-2α-targeted hypoxia imaging agent, [18F]-TC-S 7009, marks a significant advancement in the field of PET imaging. HIF-2α is a critical transcription factor in hypoxic tumor microenvironments, with its upregulation closely linked to tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metabolic adaptation. As a highly specific HIF-2α imaging probe, [18F]-TC-S 7009 efficiently and rapidly targets hypoxic regions with high HIF-2α expression, enabling clear visualization of the tumor hypoxic microenvironment through PET imaging.

Experimental results revealed that in the U87 tumor-bearing mouse model, the SUV in the tumor region was significantly higher than in normal tissues and reached its peak within 10 min postinjection. This rapid and specific binding in hypoxic regions highlights the probe’s potential to accurately map the distribution and dynamic changes of tumor hypoxia. Blocking experiments further validated the probe’s specificity for HIF-2α, as presaturation of HIF-2α binding sites with a nonradioactive ligand significantly reduced tracer uptake, confirming its target specificity.

While [18F]-TC-S 7009 achieves clear tumor-to-background contrast, the absolute tumor SUV peaks modestly and then declines. This kinetic profile likely reflects the interplay of target affinity/residence time, physicochemical properties that favor rapid distribution and washout, metabolic clearance, and intratumoral heterogeneity of hypoxia and perfusion. Several routes can reasonably increase binding and retention in next-generation analogs: (1) enhance PAS-B engagement to increase affinity and reduce k_off through targeted substitutions and conformational constraint; (2) tune lipophilicity, TPSA, and rotatable bonds to balance permeability with reduced nonspecific partitioning and slower efflux; (3) incorporate metabolically stabilizing elements (e.g., deuteration or bioisosteres at labile sites) to limit early clearance; (4) screen against P-gp/BCRP liabilities and optimize formulation/cold-mass to minimize nonspecific binding while maintaining saturable target occupancy; and (5) consider imaging-protocol refinements (delayed static acquisitions and/or kinetic modeling) to better capture specific binding. Collectively, these strategies are expected to raise absolute SUV and tumor retention without compromising specificity.

Importantly, this study represents an example of an HIF-2α-targeted imaging agent successfully applied to visualize hypoxia in vivo. The high uptake of [18F]-TC-S 7009 in tumor regions, coupled with low background uptake in normal tissues, underscores its potential as a reliable tool for imaging tumor hypoxia. This innovation offers not only a diagnostic advantage for hypoxia-related tumors but also critical insights for designing targeted therapies within hypoxic tumor microenvironments.

In summary, the development of a novel HIF-2α-targeted PET imaging agent, [18F]-TC-S 7009, provides a transformative tool for tumor hypoxia imaging. Its outstanding performance in PET imaging establishes a solid foundation for its broad application in the diagnosis and treatment of hypoxia-related diseases and aligns with growing interest in developing molecularly selective hypoxia imaging tools. Future work will explore the application of [18F]-TC-S 7009 in other solid tumor models to further evaluate its hypoxia-targeting potential.

The present in vivo evaluation was performed in U87 xenografts to establish proof-of-mechanism for HIF-2α imaging. Given the central role of HIF-2α in VHL-deficient ccRCC, we will next assess [18F]-TC-S 7009 in a ccRCC model (e.g., 786-O) to corroborate target engagement and clinical relevance.

Experimental Section

General Information

All reagents and solvents were ACS reagent grade or higher and used as received. NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AMX-400 spectrometer; chemical shifts are reported in δ (ppm) referenced to TMS, coupling constants (J) in Hz, and multiplicities as s, d, t, q, m, or br. Analytical TLC was performed on silica gel 60 F254 plates, and products were purified by silica gel column chromatography. Analytical HPLC was conducted on an Agilent 1100 Series system (diode-array detector, quaternary pump, vacuum degasser, autosampler). Mass spectra were obtained on an Agilent 6310 ion-trap (ESI) coupled to an Agilent 1200 Series HPLC. The purity of all final compounds was ≥ 95% by analytical HPLC.

Radiochemistry

Noncarrier-added 18F-fluoride was generated through the nuclear reaction 18O­(p, n) 18F using an IBA Kuibe-100 cyclotron and water enriched to 97% in 18O (Sigma-Aldrich).

Ethical Approval and Animal Preparation

All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) at West China Hospital.

Anesthesia and Imaging Protocol

The procedures for rodent imaging studies were consistent with those described in our previous work. Briefly, mice (n = 3 per group) were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (Patterson Vet Supply, Inc., Greeley, CO, USA) in 2 L/min medical oxygen and maintained at 1% isoflurane in a PET/CT scanner. A 60 min PET scan was initiated following intravenous tail vein injection of 3.7–5.5 MBq of the radiotracer. For blocking experiments, mice were preinjected with an unlabeled standard (0.4 mg/kg) 5 min before the start of the PET scan. A computed tomography (CT) scan was performed after each PET scan for anatomical coregistration.

Data Acquisition and Image Processing

Dynamic PET data were reconstructed with a three-dimensional maximum-likelihood expectation-maximization (3D-MLEM) algorithm, yielding an effective spatial resolution of ∼ 1 mm full width at half-maximum (fwhm). PET images were exported as DICOM files and processed in PMOD 4.01 (PMOD Technologies, Zurich, Switzerland). PET and CT data sets were coregistered in PMOD and displayed as PET/CT fusion overlays. Tumor regions of interest (ROIs) were contoured on CT and propagated to PET; fusion panels include an SUV color bar and a physical scale bar.
