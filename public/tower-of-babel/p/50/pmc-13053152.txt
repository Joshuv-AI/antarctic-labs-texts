Comparison of Two ICE‐Guided Transseptal Puncture Strategies for Zero‐Fluoroscopy AF Ablation—A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Chun‐Kai Chen, An‐Li Yu, Su‐Huan Chang, Yen‐Siou Chen, Chih‐Chieh Yu, Ling‐Ping Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTransseptal puncture (TSP) is a critical step in left atrial (LA) access for atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) combined with electroanatomical mapping now enables zero‐fluoroscopy TSP in experienced practice. Two ICE‐guided TSP strategies are used in contemporary clinical settings; however, their efficiency, safety, and learning curves have not been directly compared.MethodsThis prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted at National Taiwan University Hospital between February 2023 and April 2024. Patients undergoing first‐time AF ablation were randomized to two TSP strategies: Protocol A advanced the transseptal sheath from the superior vena cava to the fossa ovalis under ICE guidance, whereas Protocol B used a mapping catheter to identify the fossa and guide sheath positioning. Procedural duration, TSP metrics, and learning curve were assessed. Patients were followed for periprocedural complications and AF recurrence for one year.ResultsForty‐three patients were enrolled (mean age 63.2 ± 9.8 years, 72.1% male). Zero fluoroscopy was achieved in all procedures, with no complications or crossover. Protocol A demonstrated shorter initial TSP times than Protocol B (8.5 ± 3.7 vs. 12.0 ± 5.4 min, p = 0.018). Protocol B exhibited a steeper learning curve, achieving comparable efficiency after approximately 10 cases. Both strategies achieved successful LA access and 100% pulmonary vein isolation, with no differences in complication rates or one‐year AF recurrence.ConclusionsBoth ICE‐guided strategies are safe and effective for zero‐fluoroscopy TSP. Protocol A offers greater early efficiency, whereas Protocol B demonstrates rapid adaptability, supporting practical positioning of these complementary strategies in radiation‐free AF ablation.Keywords: atrial fibrillation ablation, intracardiac echocardiography, learning curve, Transseptal puncture, zero‐fluoroscopy

Introduction

Radiation exposure during electrophysiological procedures poses significant risks to patients, contributing to both stochastic effects, such as increased lifetime risk of malignancy, and deterministic effects, such as radiation‐induced dermatitis [1, 2, 3]. Studies indicate that physicians with high procedural volumes exhibit a higher incidence of malignancy compared to those with lower procedural frequencies [4]. Furthermore, there is a disproportionate prevalence of left‐sided brain tumors among these high‐volume practitioners [5]. Consequently, the principle of ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) has been widely endorsed by international academic societies to minimize radiation exposure during interventional procedures [6, 7, 8, 9].

Fluoroscopy has been the primary tool for decades to monitor and track catheter movement within the body for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. The integration of electroanatomical mapping (EAM) systems has enabled catheter visualization through impedance changes or magnetic field alterations. Minimal fluoroscopy techniques were initially applied in pediatric patients with paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia [10, 11, 12] and have since demonstrated safety in zero‐fluoroscopy procedures [13, 14]. The incorporation of intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) has further advanced radiation‐free approaches [15]. ICE facilitates real‐time monitoring during ablation, allowing early detection of complications, such as thrombus formation in the left atrium (LA) or pericardial effusion, and has become a standard component in complex ablation procedures worldwide [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. However, transseptal puncture (TSP) remains a challenging step to perform without fluoroscopy due to limited resolution, the dynamic nature of the beating heart, and constraints of 2D imaging, which complicate visualization of the guidewire, puncture needle tip, and sheath.

Two TSP strategies have been proposed, with retrospective analyses suggesting comparable efficiencies and complication rates [19]. However, detailed comparative analyses of these strategies are lacking. This prospective, randomized clinical trial evaluates these two TSP strategies regarding procedural duration, complication rates, and the learning curve for an electrophysiologist experienced in traditional fluoroscopy‐guided techniques.

Methods

This study was a prospective, randomized controlled trial, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board at National Taiwan University Hospital (protocol number: 202209049RIPA). The study was registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT07028502). All the patients provided informed consent. This document was prepared in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Analyses of Randomized Controlled Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Patient Enrollment

From February 2023 to April 2024, patients admitted to the cardiovascular center for first‐time atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation were enrolled consecutively. All patients underwent electrocardiogram‐gated cardiac computed tomography (CT) before admission. A transesophageal echocardiogram performed one day before the procedure excluded the presence of thrombus in the LA or LA appendage. Once thrombus and other contraindications for the ablation procedure were excluded, patients were invited to participate, and informed consent was obtained.

Procedures Before TSP

Under sedation, all the patients underwent real‐time echocardiography‐guided puncture to obtain vascular access via the femoral veins, with two venous accesses on the right and two on the left, and with or without a small femoral artery access for hemodynamic monitoring and activated clotting time sampling. A SoundStar ultrasound catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) was advanced to the right atrium (RA) through the left femoral vein. A detailed anatomical map was then created, encompassing the RA (including the RA appendage, tricuspid annulus, coronary sinus (CS) orifice, and superior vena cava (SVC)), LA (including the left atrial appendage, pulmonary veins (PVs)), esophagus, and descending aorta; the phrenic nerve was marked on the map if SVC isolation was planned later in the procedure. The ICE catheter was then positioned to visualize the septum and left superior PV. A DecaNav catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) was then introduced into the RA to create a matrix of the RA and inferior vena cava for VIZIGO (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) visualization, and was subsequently placed in the CS.

TSP Strategies and Randomization

Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to Protocol A or Protocol B using the Research electronic data capture (REDCap) system, with a block size of six to ensure balanced group assignments. The allocation sequence was concealed until assignment to maintain blinding of the randomization process, and the operator was blinded to the random allocation sequence until the TSP procedure began. Briefly, Protocol A utilized ICE‐guided visualization to track the movement of the sheath as it was pulled back from the SVC to the septum. Protocol B used a visible mapping catheter to guide the sheath toward the septum. Procedural details for each protocol are outlined in Supplemental Table 1.

In Protocol A, the ICE catheter was initially tilted posteriorly and rightward to visualize the SVC. Two J‐tip guidewires were inserted into the SVC. Next, the short femoral sheath was exchanged for an SL0 8.5 Fr, 63 cm sheath (St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN). As the tip of the SL0 dilator is echolucent, careful observation of the guidewire's echogenicity was necessary, as it became obscured by the advancing dilator. When the echolucent segment was within the SVC, the J‐tip guidewire was retracted back into the dilator. The entire sheath/dilator/wire set was then slowly pulled back while gradually releasing the ICE's rightward tilt to track the set's tip. Once the tip descended into the septal area, the ICE was fully returned to a neutral position, facing the septum and left superior PV (LSPV). After observing tenting on the ICE screen, the ICE imaging was slightly rotated clockwise and counterclockwise to confirm the alignment of the puncture set tip with the septal tenting. Upon confirmation, the guidewire was gently advanced against the septum, whereas electrocautery was applied until the tip of the guidewire passed through the septum. Once successful traversal of the septum, tension was released, allowing the wire to resume a J‐shape. The wire was then advanced to the LSPV under ICE guidance, with the dilator and sheath following along the wire under ICE visualization. A 20‐pole deflectable LassoNav circular mapping catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) was subsequently introduced into the LSPV to provide additional support for the second TSP, which was performed with the same technique using a VIZIGO sheath.

In Protocol B, two J‐tip wires were inserted into the SVC as in Protocol A. However, the SL0 sheath was advanced to the infra‐diaphragmatic level. The wire and dilator were then removed, and the sheath was directed toward the fossa ovalis with the guidance of a mapping catheter, which was visualized on the 3D mapping system. Subsequently, the mapping catheter was replaced with the J‐tip wire and dilator. With gentle pressure, septal tenting was observed. The subsequent steps were consistent with those in Protocol A. The second puncture followed the same procedure, except that the sheath used was a VIZIGO (Figure 1).

Procedures After TSP

After the completion of TSP, both groups underwent the same procedural workflow. Briefly, a LA bipolar map was first acquired using the LassoNav mapping catheter, with particular attention to the PV regions and posterior wall. PV siolation was guided by 3D CT imaging, targeting the left PVs first, followed by the right PVs. The ablation procedure was directed by the VISITAG SURPOINT module, adhering to the following parameters: (1) a tag size of 6 mm with greater than 15% overlapping between each tag (i.e., tag distance < 5 mm); (2) a contact force greater than 5 g but less than 20 g; (3) an RF power setting of 40 W; and (4) a target ablation index value of 400 for the posterior and inferior walls and 450 for the anterior and superior walls of the LA. After observing the subsidence of PV potentials (indicating entry block), exit block was assessed by pacing every bipole on LassoNav catheter within the PVs. Attempts were made to achieve local PV capture, and dissociation from the PV potentials to the LA should be observed to indicate exit block. If leakage from the PV to the LA or vice versa was detected, additional ablation was performed to seal the gap. After confirming isolation of the left and right PVs, isoproterenol infusion up to 20 ug/min was administered to evaluate dormant conduction and to assess for other potential non‐PV foci. Non‐PV foci were pursued if persistent or constantly triggered AF.

Follow Up

Patients were discharged one day after the procedure if no complications were observed and were subsequently followed at outpatient clinics at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months for clinical interviews, physical examinations, and 12‐lead electrocardiograms. Holter monitoring was performed at 3 and 12 months if no clinical recurrence was suspected. If recurrence was suspected, documentation was pursued either by a 12‐lead electrocardiogram (≥ 10 s) or by a single‐lead electrocardiogram recording of ≥ 30 s, with interpretation confirmed by an electrophysiologist.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were TSP time (measured from femoral access to completion of the first and second TSP) and the need for fluoroscopy conversion. Secondary outcomes included procedural complications (e.g., pericardial effusion, tamponade, or strokes) and AF recurrence rates over one year. AF recurrence was defined as AF documented by a 12‐lead electrocardiogram or by a single‐lead electrogram or Holter recording of at least 30 s. Electrocardiograms were obtained either at scheduled visits or triggered by clinical symptoms.

Sample Size Estimation

A t‐test was used to calculate the sample size required for detecting a difference between two TSP methods. Controlling baseline values with 80% power and a type I error of 0.05, the required sample size to detect a large‐strength effect size (0.8) between the two groups was 21 participants per group. Accounting for a 5% dropout rate, the target enrollment was set at 23 participants per group [22].

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as mean values ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Independent t‐tests and Chi‐square tests (or Fisher's exact test for small counts) were used for comparisons. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards models were used for follow‐up analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.

Results

A total of 55 patients were admitted for scheduled AF ablation. Of these, seven patients were excluded due to undergoing repeat ablation, and five patients declined participation in the trial. Ultimately, 43 patients were randomized. Baseline demographics and comorbidities were evenly distributed between the two groups (Table 1). The mean age was 63.2 ± 9.8 years, with a 72.1% men predominance.

Discussion

This study aimed to optimize the workflow for zero‐fluoroscopy TSP. While the mapping catheter‐guiding approach (Protocol B) initially required longer procedural time than the pull‐back approach (Protocol A), this difference diminished after approximately 10 cases, reflecting a steep learning curve. Notably, no cases required conversion to fluoroscopy, and no procedural failures were observed in either group. These findings suggest that new practitioners can adopt either approach based on operators' preference and comfort, as both protocols demonstrated comparable safety and efficacy.

The learning curve for zero‐fluoroscopy TSP stabilizes after approximately 10 cases. After the 10th case, the procedure time became consistently stable with no significant differences between Protocol A and B. This analysis was based on the comparable stability achieved thereafter. This demonstrates that once the initial learning curve is overcome, both workflows are equally efficient and reproducible. Our data is consistent with findings reported by Cha et al. and other researchers [16, 23]. However, most previous studies, including the current study, have been conducted by experienced practitioners with established transseptal skills. The learning curve for new operators initiating TSP using an ICE‐guided technique remains unclear. In our previous experience with zero‐fluoroscopy ablation for paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, novice operators faced no significant challenges when learning the ablation techniques based on a 3D mapping system rather than fluoroscopy [14]. Based on these observations, we anticipate that new learners can successfully adopt the ICE‐guided TSP technique with minimal difficulty.

No complications occurred in this study; however, it is important to note that the procedures were performed by an experienced operator. Several important considerations should be highlighted. First, when the ICE catheter is tilted posteriorly, it tends to shift leftward. This can be corrected by applying slight rightward rotation to the ICE catheter. Second, both the SL0 and Vizigo dilator shafts are echolucent. This characteristic can be leveraged to infer the approach of the advancing dilator tip when the guidewire becomes straightened, followed by signal attenuation as it is fully covered by the dilator (indirect visualization strategy). These ICE cues were further integrated with tactile feedback and multiplanar ICE confirmation to ensure that the guidewire tip was positioned at the dilator tip. Confirmation of this relative positioning, together with clear visualization of anatomical landmarks (SVC and fossa ovalis) and EAM integration, facilitated the subsequent TSP step. Importantly, advancement or septal crossing was performed only after reliable confirmation of tip location. Conversely, because the dilator shaft itself is echolucent, continuous visualization of the entire assembly may be intermittently limited depending on imaging plane alignment and catheter orientation during withdrawal from the SVC to the interatrial septum. This phenomenon reflects imaging‐plane and operator‐dependent factors rather than an inherent limitation of ICE‐guided TSP and was primarily encountered during early workflow adaptation rather than representing a persistent procedural constraint. Third, careful attention must be paid to the septal tenting created by the puncture set, as the tenting could be caused by the dilator shaft rather than the tip. Therefore, it is crucial to confirm that the puncture set tip is aligned with the tenting before proceeding. This is particularly challenging during the second puncture, as the echo window becomes less clear with the presence of other catheters. To confirm the alignment, we recommend rotating the ICE catheter slightly in both directions to assess the eccentricity of the tenting on the ICE screen. True tip‐to‐septum alignment is confirmed if any rotation away from the central plane results in a symmetrical reduction in tenting prominence. If asymmetry is observed, repositioning should be executed. A slight protrusion of the guidewire may serve as an adjunct marker to confirm the tip position. Fourth, tracking the tip of the transseptal needle during advancement into the LA can be difficult. There are three main reasons for this. First, as the transseptal set is advanced, the heart can be displaced from the original ICE imaging plane, resulting in a loss of tracking. Second, the heart is constantly beating and the patient is breathing. Because the relative position of the ICE catheter tip within the heart is continuously changing, the puncture set tip may occasionally move out of the imaging plane. Third, it is easy to mistakenly identify the shaft of the assembly as the tip, a common error in all echocardiography‐guided procedures. The first two issues reflect inherent technical limitations of current ICE imaging, while the third can be mitigated through improved operator technique and experience. To address this, we replaced the metal needle with an electrified J‐tip wire in this study. However, three potential drawbacks of this approach should be noted. First, unlike the conventional TSP needle, the J‐tip wire lacks a pre‐shaped curve, which can sometimes hinder the TSP set from adequately aligning with the fossa, particularly in cases of a large right atrium. This absence of an intrinsic curve is especially problematic when using a fixed sheath like the SL0. While deflectable sheaths, such as Agilis (St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN) and Vizigo, allow for curve adjustment by rotating the knob, the SL0 may lack sufficient push force to maintain optimal alignment with the septal tenting. Consequently, the set may shift out of position, particularly in Protocol B during the withdrawal of the mapping catheter or when assembling the puncture set with the dilator. To mitigate this issue, we manually shaped the SL0 set before advancing it into the puncture site, leveraging the flexibility of the SL0 material to facilitate the adjustment. Second, the body of the J‐tip wire lacks sufficient strength, and the absence of adequate support when advancing the set through the septum may present challenges. Third, there were concerns about a higher possibility of carrying the risk of thermal impact, tissue charring, and systemic embolism with electrified guidewires, as noted by a previous in vitro study [24]. However, in a randomized controlled trial comparing trans‐septal needle and electrified J wire technique, the asymptomatic embolic events were comparable between groups. A power setting of 20 W and minimized delivery time was utilized in the trial [25]. In our protocol, we also minimized our power setting and power delivery time. We did not observe significant dilator damage or embolic events in our cohort.

To address these challenges, following the completion of this study, we transitioned to using an electrified GMS‐1 guidewire (0.025‐in., pigtail configuration; Toray Medical Co. Ltd., Japan) in place of the J‐tip wire [26]. This approach offers three key advantages. First, the tip of the GMS‐1 wire is very soft, allowing it to remain safely outside the dilator. This improves the visibility of the set as it moves from the SVC toward the septum. Second, with brief electrification, the tip of the wire can pass through the septum and automatically curve into a circular shape in the LA, which is much safer compared to a needle tip. As a result, direct visualization of the wire's tip is no longer required. Third, the robust support provided by the wire's shaft also facilitates the advancement of the dilator and sheath across the septum. This is particularly helpful when using the Vizigo, as its tapered design and the longer length of the dilator outside the sheath, compared to the SL0, make crossing the septum more challenging. However, one limitation remains: like the J‐tip wire, the GMS‐1 wire lacks the pre‐shaped supportive curve inherent in the BRK needle.

An EAM system‐compatible transseptal needle, the NRG Transseptal Needle (Baylis Medical Company Inc., Montreal, Canada), is available but has not seen widespread use due to its high cost. Some studies have compared various approaches for performing TSP, including metal needle, electrified wires, and the NRG needle [24]. These studies have reported differences in lesion sizes, but the clinical impact of these variations has not been adequately addressed, and the long‐term effect remains unknown. Electrified wires were considered to possess a greater risk of melting or deforming the dilators [27]. However, we did not observe the phenomenon in our experiences.

Although both protocols demonstrate comparative efficiency, Protocol B involves the exchange of the wire/dilator for the mapping catheter and then back to the wire/dilator as necessary steps in the procedure. These exchanges increase the risk of air embolism and require more coordination and assistance to ensure a smooth transition between the components. These additional steps introduce complexity and can potentially prolong the procedural duration, making Protocol B less favorable compared to Protocol A for routine cases. Therefore, based on procedural simplicity and early efficiency, Protocol A may be considered the preferred first‐line ICE‐guided strategy in standard anatomical settings. However, because we did not prospectively quantify coordination burden or air‐related events, this assessment should be interpreted as an experience‐based consideration rather than an objective comparison. Furthermore, in cases with particular anatomical challenges, such as when the SVC cannot be clearly identified or tracking the TSP set is impossible using ICE, Protocol B remains a reliable alternative for completing the procedure under zero‐fluoroscopy conditions.

In this study, pre‐procedural cardiac CT was routinely performed to facilitate image integration with the three‐dimensional electroanatomical mapping system and to provide detailed delineation of cardiac anatomy, particularly of the left atrium and pulmonary veins. This approach reflects our institutional workflow and should not be interpreted as an intrinsic requirement for ICE‐guided zero‐fluoroscopy TSP, as some experienced centers perform these procedures without routine pre‐procedural CT imaging. In addition, intracardiac thrombus was excluded by confirming homogeneous contrast opacification of the left atrium and left atrial appendage during the arterial contrast phase. However, with the improved spatial resolution and anatomical visualization provided by contemporary ICE, the routine use of pre‐procedural CT may become unnecessary or offer limited incremental benefit in selected patients. Consequently, further reduction in radiation exposure may be achieved. In the present study, the estimated radiation dose associated with pre‐procedural cardiac CT was 4.78 ± 1.42 mSv. Four cases were excluded from this analysis: one patient did not undergo pre‐procedural cardiac imaging, one underwent cardiac MRI instead of CT, and two underwent cardiac CT at outside institutions where radiation dose information was unavailable. Four‐dimensional (4D) ICE technology has already been adopted in cardiac interventional procedures worldwide and is gradually gaining broader utilization, though its cost remains uncertain [28, 29]. One key advantage of 4D ICE is its stability to eliminate the challenge of catheters dropping out of the echo plane, thereby addressing a significant difficulty in performing TSP. Furthermore, replacing the materials used for dilators with echogenic alternatives could streamline the zero‐fluoroscopy procedure by improving visualization. With these advancements, performing ablation in a completely fluoroscopy‐free environment may become a standard of care, leaving little justification for continued reliance on radiation‐based techniques.

Limitations

First, the single‐center, single‐operator design limits the generalizability of these findings. While this design does not allow for broader applicability across different centers or operators, it offers the advantage of consistent data quality. Since the same operator performed all procedures, the variability that could arise from difference in technique or interpretation between multiple operators is minimized. This ensures uniformity in following protocols and measuring outcomes, leading to more reliable and reproducible results within the scope of the study. However, because all procedures were performed by a single experienced operator, the learning curve observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously with respect to external validity. Prior proficiency in ICE‐guided workflows and 3D EAM likely increased baseline procedural efficiency and shortened the apparent learning curve. Therefore, the observed stabilization at approximately 10 cases should not be considered a generalizable benchmark for training less experienced operators. Rather, the early‐to‐late comparison in our cohort may reflect a workflow adoption curve (standardization of procedural steps, team coordination, imaging integration, and catheter manipulation within a fluoroscopy‐free paradigm) rather than a de novo technical learning curve in operators without ICE/EAM experience. Therefore, the duration and magnitude of the learning curve may be greater for less experienced operators and in institutions with different staffing models, equipment, mapping platforms, or case severity. Nonetheless, the findings support the feasibility of implementing a fluoroscopy‐free strategy using ICE and 3D mapping with a measurable early adoption phase. Future multicenter studies including multiple operators across a range of baseline experience levels are warranted to establish more generalizable training requirements and to define how institutional factors modify the adoption curve. Second, puncture‐site location was not prospectively classified or quantified; thus, potential differences between protocols or sheath types (fixed‐curve vs. steerable) with respect to targeting an ideal fossa location could not be evaluated. Third, AF recurrence was a secondary endpoint and the present study was not powered to detect modest between‐protocol differences in long‐term arrhythmia outcomes. Therefore, the numerically higher recurrence rate observed in Protocol B should be considered hypothesis‐generating rather than evidence of superiority or inferiority, and the lack of statistical significance should not be interpreted as equivalence. Additionally, symptom‐based follow‐up may underestimate AF recurrence, especially asymptomatic episodes. Scheduled long term Holter ECG monitoring or continuous monitoring (e.g., implantable loop recorder) would enable more robust detection in future studies. Furthermore, the study's relatively small sample size limits the ability to detect subtle differences between the groups and may reduce statistical power. Although the findings provide valuable insights, additional multi‐center studies involving multiple operators with varied levels of experience would be needed to further validate these results and enhance their applicability to broader clinical settings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that both ICE‐guided TSP protocols are safe and effective for zero‐fluoroscopy AF ablation. Protocol A initially offers shorter procedural times, while Protocol B requires a brief learning curve to reach comparable efficiency. Notably, neither protocol required conversion to fluoroscopy, and no procedural complications were observed. With continued advancements in ICE technology and the development of echogenic materials, the efficiency and safety of zero‐fluoroscopy workflows are expected to further improve, reinforcing the role of radiation‐free techniques in interventional cardiology.
