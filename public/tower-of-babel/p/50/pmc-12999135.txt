The Role of 3-dimensional Imaging and Telemedicine in Breast Augmentation Consultations: A Prospective Cohort Study of 1153 Cases and a Randomized Controlled Trial in Thailand
Authors: Chatchai Pruksapong, Lunchana Permpool
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study evaluated the effectiveness of 3-dimensional (3D) imaging-based telemedicine consultations compared with traditional face-to-face consultations, focusing on patient satisfaction, decision-making, and the potential applications of 3D imaging data. The introduction of 3D imaging technology offers new opportunities for detailed analysis of breast anatomy, providing insights previously unavailable to surgeons.Methods:A prospective cohort study was conducted to analyze the correlation among breast anatomy, implant dimensions, parity status, and surgical procedures using data from 3D surface imaging. Additionally, a randomized controlled trial was performed to investigate the efficacy and patient satisfaction of 3D imaging combined with telemedicine in preoperative consultations.Results:A total of 1153 patients were included in the cohort study, revealing significant correlations among parity status, the addition of mastopexy to breast augmentation, and implant selection. The average time to revision in secondary augmentation procedures was 5.2 ± 3.45 years. In the randomized controlled trial, 120 patients were enrolled, with 60 in the 3D telemedicine group and 60 in the conventional face-to-face consultation group. The 3D telemedicine group demonstrated significantly shorter face-to-face consultation times (10.14 ± 2.23 versus 22.43 ± 5.45 min) and higher patient satisfaction scores compared with the conventional group.Conclusions:Three-dimensional surface imaging technology provides comprehensive breast parameter assessments, including breast volume, width, and projection, enabling enhanced preoperative planning and individualized implant selection. When combined with telemedicine, this technology significantly reduces face-to-face consultation time while improving patient satisfaction, making it a valuable tool in modern breast augmentation practice.

Takeaways

Question: How can 3-dimensional (3D) surface imaging enhance the breast augmentation consultation process?

Findings: Three-dimensional surface imaging can help surgeons reduce face-to-face consultation time while enhancing patient satisfaction. Additionally, 3D parameters assist in optimizing surgical planning and provide insights into the correlation among parous status, ptosis severity, and the implant size selected by the patient.

Meaning: Three-dimensional surface imaging serves as a valuable tool for both surgeons and patients in breast augmentation practice.

INTRODUCTION

Breast augmentation remains one of the most frequently performed aesthetic procedures worldwide. The International Society of Aesthetic Plastic Surgery reported 2.17 million breast implant procedures in 2022, representing nearly half of all breast-related procedures. Although primarily performed in women (98%), it also ranks among the top cosmetic operations in men (2%).1,2

Patient satisfaction is central to successful breast augmentation. Dissatisfaction may lead to negative reviews, legal disputes, or reputational harm. Therefore, preoperative consultation plays a critical role in aligning patient expectations with realistic outcomes. Traditional visualization tools—such as implant sizers placed inside a bra or comparing before-and-after photographs—often fail to accurately represent postoperative results, since they cannot account for implant projection, placement, or anatomical variation.

Recent advances in 3-dimensional (3D) surface imaging have transformed preoperative planning in aesthetic surgery.3,4 Using specialized software, surgeons can generate precise virtual simulations that show the expected postoperative outcome for various implant sizes, shapes, and positions. These simulations help patients better understand realistic results, improve communication, and enhance decision-making.5,6

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of telemedicine in aesthetic practice.7–9 Combining 3D imaging with online consultations allowed continued patient engagement during lockdowns, while minimizing physical contact and infection risk. This hybrid model also expanded access for local and international patients, allowing them to make informed surgical decisions remotely.

Today, 3D imaging integrated with telemedicine has become part of the standard workflow for breast augmentation consultations.10 It allows surgeons to evaluate breast parameters quantitatively—volume, width, and projection—while visually demonstrating outcomes, improving both patient comprehension and confidence. Furthermore, the collected 3D data can enhance predictive modeling, refine surgical techniques, and optimize implant selection.

The present study compared 3D imaging–assisted telemedicine consultations with conventional in-person consultations in terms of patient satisfaction, consultation efficiency, and preoperative decision-making. In addition, it evaluated 3D-derived anatomical parameters to explore how these measurements supported individualized implant planning in Thai patients seeking breast augmentation.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This study comprised a prospective cohort and a randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted between November 2021 and October 2024 at Phramongkutklao Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of the Royal Thai Army Medical Department, and the trial was registered under TCTR20241201001. Written informed consent was obtained for study participation and photograph publication.

Study Design and Participants

Eligible participants were women aged 20 years or older seeking breast augmentation who agreed to preoperative 3D surface imaging and telemedicine consultation.

The cohort study included all eligible patients, whereas the RCT enrolled primary augmentation candidates only, excluding secondary augmentation cases or those declining randomization.

The sample size for the RCT was calculated for a superiority design using a 2-sample t test with α set at 0.05 and 80% power. Based on an expected standardized effect size (Cohen d = 0.72), a minimum of 30 participants per group (n = 60 total) was required.

Data Collection and 3D Imaging Protocol

For each patient, electronic medical records were reviewed for demographics, parity, reasons for surgery, and revision history. Preoperative photographs were captured in 5 standard views (frontal, bilateral 45 and 90 degrees lateral) extending from the neck to the waist.

Images were uploaded into the Crisalix 3D simulation software (Crisalix SA, Lausanne, Switzerland),11 which generated simulated breast outcomes based on selected implant sizes (Figs. 1–4). Output parameters included breast volume, width, projection, and sternal notch-to-nipple distance, as well as the chosen implant’s volume, diameter, and projection.

Outcome Measures and Statistical Analysis

Outcome measures included breast and implant parameters, consultation duration, and satisfaction scores. For continuous variables, analysis of variance with Bonferroni correction was used; categorical variables were compared by the χ2 or Fisher exact test. Correlations were analyzed using the Pearson coefficient, with a P value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in STATA/SE 17 (StataCorp).

RESULTS

Part I

Prospective Cohort Study

Patient Demographics

A total of 1153 patients were analyzed: 1139 women (98.8%) and 14 transgender individuals (1.2%). The mean age was 32.4 ± 7.1 years; 56.1% were aged 25–35 years. Mean weight, height, and body mass index (BMI) were 51.8 ± 8.6 kg, 160.0 ± 5.7 cm, and 20.2 ± 3.0 kg/m2, respectively. Weight and BMI correlated positively with sternal notch-to-nipple distance (Figs. 5–7). Parity distribution included 40.8% nulliparous and 59.2% parous patients. Primary augmentation accounted for 79.3%, and secondary, for 20.7% of cases.

Ptosis and Procedures

Breast ptosis distribution was as follows: none, 62.6%; grade 1, 20.7%; grade 2, 9.9%; and grade 3, 6.8%. Operative patterns included augmentation alone (79.8%), augmentation with periareolar mastopexy (12.1%), vertical mastopexy (7.5%), and inverted-T (0.6%). Additional liposuction or nipple reduction was performed in approximately 3%–4%.

Implant Characteristics

Implants were placed subpectorally in 91.8% and prepectorally in 8.2%. Symmetrical sizes were used in 70.4%, and asymmetrical sizes in 29.6%. Smooth implants were selected in 59.8%, and textured implants in 40.2% (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Breast augmentation ranks among the most common aesthetic procedures globally and in Thailand, where manufacturer data estimate approximately 30,000 cases annually. It remains one of the most financially significant procedures in cosmetic medicine, contributing to Thailand’s medical tourism strategy. Preoperative consultation is a critical determinant of success. Conventional implant sizer trials give limited insight, as they only show how implants appear beneath clothing, not actual postoperative contours. These limitations underscore the need for advanced visualization tools.

Three-dimensional surface imaging, introduced in the early 2000s, provides accurate virtual simulations that improve communication, informed consent, and satisfaction. Studies confirm a high correlation with magnetic resonance imaging for volume estimation, validating its clinical reliability.11–14 It enables real-time comparison of various implant shapes and sizes, fostering realistic patient expectations and facilitating shared decision-making.15,16

The COVID-19 pandemic expanded the role of telemedicine in aesthetic practice. In Thailand, surgeons increasingly combined 3D imaging with online consultations, ensuring continuity of care and patient safety. Even postpandemic, this hybrid system remains efficient and well-accepted, reducing unnecessary clinic time and improving convenience.

In this large Thai cohort, the majority of patients were aged 25–35 years with relatively low BMI (~20 kg/m2), consistent with Eastern body profiles.17,18 Nearly 41% were nulliparous. The primary to secondary augmentation ratio was 4:1. Most (83.3%) presented with no or mild ptosis, whereas 16.7% required mastopexy.

The subpectoral approach (91.8%) dominated, reflecting surgeons’ preference for low-BMI patients with thin soft tissue, as it provides better implant coverage and natural contours.19–21 Smooth implants were favored (59.8%), though many patients still chose textured implants after counseling regarding BIA-ALCL and capsular contracture risks. Asymmetry correction was required in approximately 30% of patients, guided by 3D volume data. These findings highlight the necessity of individualized planning.

The average interval for revision was 5.2 years, mainly for size change (≈70% of revisions). Capsular contracture and implant rupture accounted for smaller proportions. Over one-third of revision cases required mastopexy, supporting the need for careful preoperative analysis to reduce secondary surgery.

Three-dimensional imaging objectively quantified breast volume, width, projection, and sternal notch-to-nipple distance, enabling correlation with implant parameters. Nulliparous women exhibited smaller dimensions, explaining their tendency toward smaller implants. Parous patients, with stretched tissues and wider diameters, tended to choose larger volumes. Patients with greater ptosis required mastopexy, not simply larger implants, to restore upper-pole fullness. These findings align with Swanson et al.16

In augmentation with mastopexy, implant selection was conservative across parity groups, emphasizing proportionate correction rather than enlargement. Visualization through 3D imaging helped avoid oversizing and set realistic expectations, particularly for patients requesting disproportionate volumes.

The RCT confirmed that 3D imaging combined with telemedicine reduced face-to-face consultation time by more than 50% while increasing satisfaction. Patients appreciated being able to review simulations remotely, leading to focused and efficient in-person discussions. This workflow remained beneficial after the pandemic, especially during seasonal illness surges, improving both patient experience and clinic efficiency.

To the authors’ knowledge, this represents the first Thai prospective study integrating 3D imaging and telemedicine for breast augmentation and one of the largest Asian cohorts to date. The data offer valuable regional insights into anatomical parameters and aesthetic preferences, supporting evidence-based surgical planning.

Limitations include the inability to blind patients and the potential reduction in accuracy in cases of severe ptosis, where lower pole capture may be incomplete. Despite these, the findings demonstrate strong applicability and real-world utility of 3D imaging in both local and international contexts.

CONCLUSIONS

Three-dimensional surface imaging provides precise assessment of breast volume, width, and projection, improving individualized implant selection and preoperative planning. When integrated with telemedicine, it significantly decreases consultation time and enhances patient satisfaction without compromising safety or outcomes. This technology represents an effective, patient-centered advancement in modern breast augmentation practice, supporting both clinical efficiency and informed decision-making.
