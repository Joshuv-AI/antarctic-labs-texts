Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a prototypical autoimmune connective tissue disease characterised by multisystem inflammation and complex immune dysregulation.1 Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), represents a major clinical complication of SLE, arising from immune-mediated endothelial injury, hypercoagulability and impaired fibrinolysis.2 3 Epidemiological studies indicate that SLE is associated with a markedly increased risk of VTE, with approximately a fourfold higher risk of PE and a sixfold higher risk of DVT compared with the general population.3 4 These thrombotic complications contribute substantially to disability and mortality, underscoring the need for improved risk stratification and effective preventive strategies.

Current thromboprophylaxis recommendations in SLE primarily focus on patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) or persistent antiphospholipid antibody (aPL) positivity. International guidelines recommend low-dose aspirin (75–100 mg/day) for primary prevention in asymptomatic aPL carriers and selected high-risk aPL-positive patients. However, no consensus exists regarding thromboprophylaxis in aPL-negative SLE patients, leading to considerable variability in clinical practice.5 6 In real-world settings, low-dose aspirin is frequently prescribed for primary thromboprophylaxis in aPL-negative patients perceived to be at increased thrombotic or cardiovascular risk.4 7 This practice is largely based on observational evidence and extrapolated from aPL-positive populations, despite the absence of robust randomised controlled trial (RCT) data. Although aspirin has a favourable safety profile and may confer benefits in the primary prevention of cardiovascular events, its efficacy in preventing VTE remains limited, suggesting that current strategies may be insufficient for certain high-risk subgroups.8 9

Emerging evidence indicates that thrombotic risk in SLE is not solely determined by aPL status. A subset of aPL-negative patients remains at increased risk, particularly those with elevated D-dimer levels.10 11 D-dimer, a sensitive biomarker reflecting ongoing coagulation activation and fibrinolysis, has been associated with both disease activity and thrombotic risk in SLE.12 13 Notably, a recent study reported VTE incidence rates of up to 42% among SLE patients with D-dimer levels >2.0 µg/mL,11 thereby identifying a clinically relevant high-risk population for whom optimal preventive strategies remain undefined.

Rivaroxaban, an oral direct factor Xa inhibitor, has been extensively validated for the prevention and treatment of VTE in diverse high-risk populations.14 15 Compared with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), rivaroxaban provides comparable efficacy, a favourable safety profile and greater convenience because of fixed dosing and the lack of routine coagulation monitoring.16 17 However, prospective evidence supporting its use for primary thromboprophylaxis in aPL-negative SLE patients at elevated thrombotic risk remains limited.

In the absence of an established standard of care, aspirin represents a pragmatic comparator that reflects real-world clinical practice. Therefore, we designed the PREVENT-SLE trial, a multicentre, prospective RCT, to compare the efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban vs aspirin for the primary prevention of VTE in aPL-negative SLE patients at elevated thrombotic risk, as defined by increased D-dimer levels or other prothrombotic features. This study aims to provide high-quality evidence to inform risk-adapted thromboprophylaxis strategies and guide clinical decision-making in this understudied population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is a multicentre, prospective, open-label RCT designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban versus aspirin for the primary prevention of VTE in patients with SLE at high thrombotic risk who are persistently negative for aPLs. The PREVENT-SLE trial will be conducted at three tertiary academic hospitals in China: The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (coordinating centre), Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital and Panyu Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. Patient recruitment is planned between January 2026 and January 2027, with a 12-month follow-up period. The overall study design is illustrated in figure 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) diagnosis of SLE according to the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology classification criteria; (2) age ≥18 years; (3) negative for aPLs on two separate tests ≥12 weeks apart (including negative lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin IgG/IgM <40 GPL/MPL and anti-β2 glycoprotein I IgG/IgM <40 U/mL); (4) no history of thromboembolic events; and (5) high thrombotic risk.

The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) active bleeding or known high bleeding risk (eg, platelet count <50 × 109 /L or gastrointestinal bleeding within the past 6 months); (2) severe hepatic dysfunction (Child–Pugh class C or alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase >5× upper limit of normal); (3) severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, current haemodialysis or kidney transplant recipients); (4) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (5) current use of therapeutic-dose anticoagulants (eg, low-molecular-weight heparin or VKAs); (6) comorbid conditions requiring anticoagulation (eg, atrial fibrillation, mechanical heart valves or recent percutaneous coronary intervention); (7) active malignancy or life expectancy <2 years; (8) a SLE disease activity index (SLEDAI) score >12 or requiring initiation/escalation to high-dose glucocorticoids or intensive immunosuppressive therapy (patients may be re-screened once disease stabilises); (9) participation in another interventional clinical trial; (10) inability or refusal to provide written informed consent; and (11) study investigators or affiliated personnel.

Definition of high thrombotic risk

High thrombotic risk was defined as the presence of at least one of the following criteria: (1) D-dimer level >2.0 µg/mL (fibrinogen equivalent units (FEU)) in the absence of major acute provoking factors (eg, recent surgery, trauma, active infection or pregnancy/postpartum); or (2) an SLE-VTE risk score ≥100.

The SLE-VTE score has been previously developed and validated in SLE populations and is incorporated in this study as a predefined tool for risk enrichment.18 To ensure comparability of D-dimer measurements across centres, all laboratories will use standardised assay methods and report results in FEU. Calibration procedures and quality control will be harmonised across sites prior to study initiation.

To minimise misclassification due to transient elevations of D-dimer, patients with conditions known to acutely increase D-dimer levels—such as active infection, recent surgery or trauma, and pregnancy or postpartum status—will be excluded at screening. Repeat testing may be performed when clinically indicated to confirm persistently elevated levels. In addition, the 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk estimated by the China-PAR model will be collected for descriptive purposes and exploratory subgroup analyses but will not be used to determine eligibility.19

Randomisation and blinding

Eligible patients will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to receive either rivaroxaban 10 mg one time per day or enteric-coated aspirin 100 mg one time per day. Randomisation will be stratified by study site using a centralised, computer-generated block randomisation sequence with concealed allocation. Given the pragmatic nature of the intervention, this trial is designed as open-label. However, several measures have been implemented to minimise potential performance and ascertainment bias. All thrombotic and bleeding outcomes will be adjudicated by an independent endpoint adjudication committee (EAC), whose members will remain blinded to treatment allocation. Clinical data submitted for adjudication will be anonymised and will not contain any treatment-related information.

All study endpoints are defined using objective and prespecified diagnostic criteria, including imaging-confirmed VTE and bleeding events classified according to the criteria of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH). All suspected thrombotic and bleeding events will be mandatorily reported and adjudicated by the blinded EAC. Investigators across participating centres will receive standardised training to ensure consistent event identification and reporting. An independent data and safety monitoring board (DSMB) will oversee study conduct, data quality, and safety monitoring throughout the trial. Interventions

Patients in the rivaroxaban group will receive rivaroxaban 10 mg orally one time per day. Patients in the aspirin group will receive enteric-coated aspirin 100 mg orally one time per day. Concomitant SLE therapies (eg, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants and biologics) will be permitted and managed according to standard clinical practice. Study medication will be discontinued in the event of major bleeding or other clinically significant adverse events. Subsequent clinical management will be determined by the treating physicians and overseen by the DSMB.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on a superiority hypothesis using a time-to-event framework. Previous observational studies suggest a relatively high incidence of VTE among patients with SLE at elevated thrombotic risk, although rates vary by risk stratification and follow-up duration (10%–12.8%).20,22 In the present study, the 12-month incidence of VTE in the control group (aspirin group) is conservatively assumed to be approximately 10%.

A clinically meaningful treatment effect was defined as an absolute risk reduction of 4% (from 10% to 6%), corresponding to a HR of 0.60. This effect size was selected to balance clinical relevance and feasibility in the absence of direct RCT evidence in this specific population.

The sample size was calculated using the log-rank test, with a two-sided α level of 0.05 and 80% power. Under these assumptions, 228 patients are required in each group. Allowing for an anticipated attrition rate of 10%, the total sample size is set at 456 patients. All sample size calculations were performed using PASS software (version 16.0; NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA).

Follow-up and data collection

All participants will undergo scheduled follow-up visits at baseline and at months 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 after randomisation. Participants who undergo surgery or experience prolonged immobilisation during the study period will receive an additional unscheduled visit within 7 days to reassess VTE risk (table 2). The primary efficacy and safety analyses will be based on the 12-month randomised follow-up period. Participants may be invited to enter an extended observational follow-up of up to 24 months to capture late thrombotic events and delayed bleeding complications; data from this period will be considered exploratory. All participating centres will retain complete raw imaging data, which will be centrally collected and transferred to the Department of Radiology at the Second Xiangya Hospital for adjudication.

All CTPA scans will be independently reviewed by two board-certified radiologists who are blinded to treatment allocation and clinical information. Imaging interpretation will follow a predefined standardised protocol. A diagnosis of PE will require agreement between the two reviewers. In cases of disagreement, a third senior radiologist will perform an additional blinded assessment, and the final diagnosis will be determined by consensus.

Data quality control and management

All study data, including clinical records, laboratory results and imaging data, will be systematically recorded in standardised case report forms (CRFs). Completed CRFs will be reviewed and verified by study monitors prior to submission to the data management team. Data entry will be performed using a dual-entry system by two independent personnel, followed by validation and discrepancy resolution through predefined query procedures. All discrepancies identified during verification will be immediately communicated to the researchers through query forms, which will be subsequently archived for reference. A dedicated data management team will oversee data cleaning and analysis, ensuring the integrity and precision of the dataset.

Standard operating procedures will be established to ensure uniformity in data collection, entry and quality control processes. All study personnel involved in the study will receive comprehensive training on these standard operating procedures to guarantee adherence to protocols. Regular data audits and monitoring will be conducted to maintain compliance and enhance the reliability of the study data. After data entry and verification, CRFs will be systematically archived in sequential order with corresponding indexing for easy retrieval. Electronic data files, including databases, validation scripts, analysis programmes, results, coding manuals and documentation, will be organised categorically and securely backed up on multiple storage media to prevent data loss or damage. All original records will be stored in accordance with regulatory guidelines for the required retention period, ensuring their availability for future reference and audits.

Statistical analysis

This trial is designed as a superiority trial to evaluate whether rivaroxaban reduces the risk of VTE compared with aspirin. Continuous variables will be assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as means with SD or medians with IQRs, as appropriate. Categorical variables will be summarised as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics will be described by treatment group; formal statistical testing of baseline differences will not be emphasised, as randomisation is expected to ensure comparability between groups.

The primary efficacy analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, whereby all randomised participants will be analysed according to their assigned treatment group, regardless of adherence or protocol deviations. Time to first objectively confirmed VTE event detected during the prespecified follow-up period will be analysed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and compared using the log-rank test. HRs and corresponding 95% CIs will be estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. The primary inference will be based on an unadjusted Cox model with treatment group as the main exposure. A prespecified adjusted model including age, baseline D-dimer level, SLEDAI score and renal involvement will be performed as a supportive analysis to improve precision and assess robustness. Event time will be defined as the date of first objective detection of VTE during scheduled assessments or clinically indicated evaluations. Participants without events will be censored at the date of their last follow-up.

Secondary time-to-event outcomes, including arterial thrombotic events, major bleeding and all-cause mortality, will be analysed using similar survival methods. Longitudinal changes in disease activity will be analysed using mixed-effects models for repeated measures, accounting for within-subject correlation.

Prespecified subgroup analyses will be performed according to baseline D-dimer levels, age, disease activity and presence of lupus nephritis. Interaction terms between treatment assignment and subgroup variables will be included in Cox models to assess whether treatment effects differ across subgroups. Temporal analyses will be performed across early (0–3 months) and late (3–12 months) follow-up periods to assess the timing and persistence of treatment effects. A per-protocol (PP) analysis will be conducted as a sensitivity analysis, including participants who adhered to the assigned intervention without major protocol deviations. Consistency between the ITT and PP analyses will be assessed as a measure of robustness. For time-to-event outcomes, participants lost to follow-up will be censored at their last documented assessment. Multiple imputation will be applied for missing baseline covariates where appropriate; otherwise, complete-case analysis will be performed. All statistical tests will be two-sided, and a P value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses will be performed using R software (version 4.3.0).

Results

Recruitment commenced in January 2026 at the three participating centres. Baseline characteristics, primary and secondary outcomes and safety data will be reported after completion of enrolment and the prespecified follow-up period. A single interim safety analysis will be conducted by the independent DSMB after approximately 50% of participants have completed the 12-month follow-up. This analysis will be limited to safety outcomes, and no formal efficacy-based stopping rule is planned. The final efficacy analysis will be performed after completion of the prespecified follow-up period. All findings will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement.

Discussion

Thromboembolic complications remain a major contributor to morbidity and premature mortality in patients with SLE, particularly among those with persistent systemic inflammation and prothrombotic biological features. While current clinical guidelines provide clear recommendations for anticoagulation in patients with established APS or persistent aPL positivity, the management of aPL-negative SLE patients with objectively elevated thrombotic risk remains inadequately defined.5 6 This evidence gap has led to substantial heterogeneity in clinical practice and may contribute to under-recognition and undertreatment of high-risk individuals.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the pathophysiology of thrombosis in SLE extends beyond aPL-mediated mechanisms. Chronic immune activation, complement component activation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, endothelial dysfunction and cytokine-driven activation of coagulation pathways collectively contribute to a prothrombotic state.4 23 24 In this context, D-dimer represents a clinically accessible biomarker reflecting ongoing coagulation activation and fibrinolysis. Observational studies have shown that markedly elevated D-dimer levels are associated with an increased risk of VTE in SLE patients, independent of aPL status.11 12 However, no RCTs have validated a D-dimer-guided strategy for primary thromboprophylaxis in this population.

Low-dose aspirin is widely used in clinical practice for primary thromboprophylaxis in SLE, despite limited evidence supporting its efficacy in preventing fibrin-rich VTE.7 In aPL-negative populations, its use is largely extrapolated from studies conducted in aPL-positive individuals and is supported primarily by its favourable safety profile rather than robust randomised evidence. As a result, current prophylactic strategies for aPL-negative SLE patients remain largely empirical. In this setting, aspirin represents a pragmatic comparator that reflects real-world practice across participating centres, although its effectiveness in this population remains uncertain.

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have demonstrated efficacy in preventing VTE across several high-risk populations.25,27 However, evidence from autoimmune disease populations, particularly APS, has raised concerns regarding their effectiveness in specific pathophysiological contexts. The RAPS trial suggested that rivaroxaban was non-inferior to VKAs with respect to laboratory surrogate markers in patients with thrombotic APS, although it was not powered for clinical efficacy outcomes.28 In contrast, Pengo et al29 reported a significantly higher rate of recurrent thrombotic events, particularly arterial events, among high-risk triple-positive APS patients treated with rivaroxaban compared with VKAs. Similarly, the RCT by Ordi-Ros et al30 demonstrated that DOACs were less effective than VKAs in preventing recurrent thrombosis in APS, especially for arterial events. Collectively, these findings indicate that the efficacy of DOACs in autoimmune-associated thrombosis may depend on the underlying pathogenic mechanisms. APS represents a distinct prothrombotic phenotype characterised by antibody-mediated endothelial and platelet activation. This mechanism differs from the inflammation-driven and coagulation-related pathways that are thought to underlie thrombosis in aPL-negative SLE. Therefore, the unfavourable outcomes observed with DOACs in APS cannot be directly extrapolated to aPL-negative SLE populations.

The PREVENT-SLE trial is designed to address this critical evidence gap. By focusing on aPL-negative SLE patients with clearly defined high thrombotic risk, operationalised by elevated D-dimer levels and SLE-specific VTE risk scores, this study is expected to provide the first prospective randomised evidence comparing rivaroxaban with aspirin for primary VTE prevention in this population.

A key strength of this trial is its pragmatic yet methodologically rigorous design. The multicentre structure ensures generalisability, while protocol-mandated imaging surveillance at predefined time points enables systematic detection of both symptomatic and asymptomatic VTE events, thereby reducing the risk of outcome under-ascertainment. In addition, serial D-dimer measurements collected during follow-up may help elucidate the temporal relationship between dynamic coagulation activity and thrombotic events in SLE.

If rivaroxaban demonstrates superior efficacy with an acceptable safety profile, the findings of PREVENT-SLE may have important implications for clinical practice and the development of clinical guidelines. Such results may support a shift from a phenotype-based (APS-centred) approach towards a biomarker-informed, risk-adapted thromboprophylaxis strategy. This paradigm could facilitate earlier and more targeted intervention in patients who are currently under-recognised, ultimately reducing thrombotic burden and improving long-term outcomes in SLE.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the open-label design may introduce performance and ascertainment bias. However, this risk is mitigated by centralised randomisation, blinded endpoint adjudication, blinded central imaging review and the use of objective, prespecified outcome criteria. Second, although D-dimer is incorporated as a key inclusion criterion to reflect real-world clinical decision-making, it is a nonspecific biomarker that may be influenced by infection, inflammation or renal dysfunction, all of which are frequently encountered in SLE. To minimise potential misclassification, a conservative threshold was applied, and repeat testing may be performed when clinically indicated. Third, patients with SLEDAI scores >12 are excluded to reduce confounding related to highly active disease. While this approach improves internal validity, it may limit generalisability to patients experiencing severe disease flares. In addition, patients with prior VTE, diagnosed APS or established indications for anticoagulation were excluded to define a homogeneous primary prevention population, which may restrict extrapolation of findings to secondary prevention settings or broader clinical contexts. Fourth, although the SLE-VTE score is used for risk enrichment, its predictive performance in aPL-negative populations has not been fully validated. Accordingly, the study includes a prespecified evaluation of its discriminatory performance and calibration. Finally, although a 12-month follow-up period is sufficient to detect clinically meaningful differences in incident VTE, it may not fully capture long-term outcomes, including delayed bleeding events or sustained thrombotic benefit. Extended follow-up through post-trial observational studies or registry-based analyses may be required to evaluate long-term safety and efficacy.

Conclusion

The PREVENT-SLE trial is designed to address a critical evidence gap in the primary prevention of VTE among aPL-negative SLE patients at elevated thrombotic risk. By directly comparing rivaroxaban with aspirin in a biomarker-defined population, this study is expected to provide the first prospective randomised evidence in this setting. If rivaroxaban demonstrates superior efficacy with an acceptable safety profile, these findings may support a shift towards biomarker- and risk-informed thromboprophylaxis strategies and contribute to the future refinement of clinical guidelines for thrombosis prevention in SLE.
