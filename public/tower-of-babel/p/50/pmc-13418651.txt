My Journey Through Assisted Living Facilities
Authors: William B Applegate
Published: 2026

Background

I am now retired but previously was a leader and active clinician in Geriatric Medicine and a part time clinician in Palliative Medicine. Recently, I wound up as a complex patient over a 6‐month period in two different assisted living programs/facilities. Although many such facilities provide excellent care, my experience was unfortunate and is the focus of this paper [1, 2, 3, 4]. Assisted living facilities (ALFs) are designed to provide some ADL care but, as described by Zimmerman et al. [2], the various types of ALFs are under evolving pressure regarding financing, patient medical acuity, health care services, and regulation. Medical eligibility models range from those where patients are somewhat ambulatory and require some assistance with medication and modest personal care to those where patients have major multimorbidity requiring substantial nursing care and complicated medication regimens. Existing models and costs vary in level of service provided, are usually not part of an integrated health care system, and may require only minimum RN staffing levels and do not require a medical director [1, 2, 3, 4].

ALFs have limited ability to meet the needs of medically complex residents, and such patients and their families are often not informed nor aware of care limitations on admission [2, 4]. This was true for my wife and daughter. When I was admitted, I required the highest level of care, equivalent to that provided by facilities classified as “nursing homes” which require more intensive nursing care and medical oversight. Nursing homes receive both national and state oversight, while ALFs only receive oversight from state and local agencies [2]. In our local area, for very disabled persons in need of long‐term care, ALFs are often seen as equivalent to nursing homes. In our community, the monthly cost for an assisted living facility is about $4500 a month (starting at $2300 month), depending on the type of assistance needed, and for a nursing home, about $5000 per month [5].

ALFs now encompass about half of all long‐term care beds in the US [3]. I previously knew that the atmosphere of many ALFs at times adversely affected the morale of physicians and other members of the clinical team. However, I did not fully perceive the extent of the negative impact on individual patients. My critical comments here are based on my own unfortunate clinical experiences in only two ALFs. I do acknowledge that there are many such facilities that provide a higher level of care or care for classes of less disabled persons. My comments are also framed by my previous clinical experience as a physician caring for older persons in several similar facilities.

Process of Care

Prior to my first admission in assisted living, I had spent considerable time as a patient in hospital, on a rehabilitation unit, and on a skilled nursing unit (these areas are not discussed in this paper). My clinical history began after I had two falls with head injuries, developed persistent delirium thought related to the head injuries and possibly polypharmacy, and had a hip fracture. On admission to the first ALF, I was primarily wheelchair bound. I was still suffering from delirium and required assistance with bathing and dressing.

During this entire clinical journey, neither physicians nor staff ever adequately discussed my condition or care goals with my wife or me, or whether an ALF or a traditional nursing home would have been more appropriate for my level of care. In retrospect, I believe my physicians' goals for my care focused more on palliative care in anticipation of death rather than improvement in function, which was primary for both my wife and me. It seems to me that this difference in goals prevented meaningful discussions and resulted in tension between my providers and my wife and me.

Problems With Facility Staff

In these ALFs, the combination of inadequate and marginally qualified staff plus mediocre administrative staff leadership both contributed to a frustrating environment. We witnessed angry interactions between staff and patients, and families, especially after public visiting hours. Facility staff were defensive, even hostile to my wife and me if ever presented with constructive criticism. At one point, a conference with my wife and me illustrated these problems. Facility administrative leadership set up a meeting with both my wife and me with four nursing and administrative leaders to discuss my care. There were no physicians present. It turns out that the intent of the meeting and most of the discussion was focused on staff defense of their processes of care and my wife and me were both lectured regarding the appropriate care being provided and the problems our criticisms were causing them. Neither my wife nor I were given any opportunity to describe or discuss our concerns.

Problems With Physician Care

My opinion has always been that physicians who cared for patients in ALFs were very needed to provide better care in these settings [3, 4], but the nature of the environment did not allow them to provide much leadership in goal setting or team care. In my case, team meetings with the physicians seemed sporadic at best, and physicians were often not present on site [3, 6]. In addition, there were relatively few nurse practitioners or physicians' assistants on site to help manage or direct care. In fact, one national study found that less than half of physicians with patients in ALFs actually performed a majority of their visits on site [3]. This may vary based on the acuity of the patient's morbidity. I believe that physicians working in this inadequate setting are often forced to accept lower standards for nursing and other staff care. At one point when I complained bitterly about the flawed care in one ALF, one of my senior physicians replied, “where else do you think you can go, Bill?”

At both ALFs I was heavily treated with narcotics for my pain and with multiple medications which were changed very frequently. At one point, when I was discharged from ALF to home, my narcotics were changed to substantial doses of a different narcotic. This resulted in my having deep somnolence at home, requiring readmission to an assisted living facility. This all occurred despite my wife's comments to my physicians that I was over‐medicated. Fortunately, a senior Geriatric Psychiatrist (a personal friend) also became partially involved in my care with a special focus on polypharmacy.

In retrospect, my primary physicians (full‐time geriatricians) had little impact on the course of my care while I was in AL. I believe that overall management in these facilities was driven by administrative leadership focused on financial performance with a culture that limited the potential impact of any physician attempts at overall institutional leadership.

My Outcome

To complete the description of my case, fortunately, both a senior physician in Geriatric Medicine and a senior retired geriatric psychiatrist friend became involved in my care after debate arose over my persistent delirium, the length of my care, and polypharmacy. My senior geriatric psychiatrist colleague supported my wife and daughter in ultimately recommending for me to be admitted again to the hospital to manage these issues. Also, the consulting senior physician in Geriatrics insisted on further evaluation of the fact that my cerebral ventricles were somewhat dilated on cerebral imaging. This led to a diagnosis of normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) and a vascular shunt was placed followed by clearing of my cognition and substantial improvement in my physical function. This improvement then put my wife in a position where she could take me home, with agreement from my physician, and she arranged for me to get activity therapy at the local YMCA. Within 6 weeks, I improved from wheelchair‐bound to walking with only a cane. My cognition improved to the point where I could write this personal narrative.

Recommendations

Some of my colleagues and national medical groups have performed outstanding roles in evaluating, developing, or recommending strategies for assisted living [2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. The assisted living sector is the largest provider of long‐term care in the United States [2, 8]. For many ALFs, the objectives, organization, leadership, financing, and strategy have been too limited. For instance, one study demonstrated the large number of deficiencies of care citations in ALFs in one state and demonstrated further that citations of care problems had little effect on change on a long‐term basis [4]. This seems to indicate a need for more state or national regulatory oversight with substantial fines for serious deficiencies. ALFs started out as “board and care” enterprises, and this history may be used at times to support a perspective of limited state and local regulation. This seems to me to deny the national trend that persons in ALFs often have clinical needs similar to patients requiring sophisticated nursing home care [2, 4]. At the very least, for ALFs from a national perspective, specific goals and care evaluation should be more clearly defined and documented for any ALFs that provide any health or medical care (see Table 1). Zimmerman et al. [2] have produced a comprehensive discussion of the problems and strategies to be considered. These can include: (a) those needing a longer‐term focus on clinical physical care and improvement in function with a chance of discharge back to community living, versus (b) those thought to be dying where the primary agreed goal (documented discussion and consent with patient/family) should be amelioration of suffering versus (c) those requiring maintenance of current clinical status and function to enhance residence in a congregate care facility. Each of these goals should be clearly established and reviewed regularly [4].
