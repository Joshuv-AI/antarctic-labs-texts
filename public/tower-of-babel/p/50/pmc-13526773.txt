Global CMIP6-based datasets of vegetation-responsive and feedback-deflated potential evapotranspiration
Authors: Daeha Kim, Seulchan Lee, Minha Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional potential evapotranspiration (PET) formulations often neglect evolving vegetation structure, physiological responses to rising atmospheric CO2 concentration and vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and feedback-driven amplification of atmospheric aridity. To address these limitations, we present a global gridded dataset of vegetation-responsive PET (PETveg) and its feedback-deflated counterpart, wet-environment PET (WETveg), derived consistently from 12 Earth system models in the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). PETveg is computed using a physically and physiologically based two-source Penman–Monteith formulation that explicitly accounts for leaf area index, atmospheric CO2, and VPD through dynamic aerodynamic and surface resistance terms. WETveg is obtained by applying the complementary evaporation principle to reconstruct the atmospheric state that would prevail over a saturated surface, thereby removing land–atmosphere feedback amplification of air temperature and VPD. The dataset spans the historical period (1850–2014) and four future Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5) for 2015–2100, provided on a common 1° × 1° grid at monthly resolution. Together, PETveg and WETveg offer internally consistent yet physically distinct representations of atmospheric evaporative demand, enabling transparent evaluation of PET formulation uncertainty in hydrological, ecological, and climate-impact studies.

Background & Summary

Potential evapotranspiration (PET) is a fundamental climatic variable used widely in hydrology, drought monitoring, ecohydrology, and climate-impact assessments1–4, as it represents the atmospheric water demand at the land surface. PET is routinely employed to estimate actual evapotranspiration, diagnose water deficiency, and quantify aridity and drought indices across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales5. Hence, long-term changes in PET are often interpreted as direct indicators of intensifying atmospheric drying under climate change6. However, such interpretations depend considerably on how PET is formulated and how land–atmosphere interactions are represented.

Over recent decades, multiple lines of evidence indicate that terrestrial ecosystems have undergone substantial structural and physiological changes, including widespread increases in leaf area index (LAI) associated with Earth’s greening7, as well as shifts in stomatal behavior driven by rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations (Ca)8 and vapor pressure deficit (VPD)9. Rising Ca has been identified as the dominant driver of the greening signal, with additional contributions from climate variability, nitrogen deposition, and land-use change10,11. Both observational evidence and model projections indicate that LAI is expected to continue increasing under a warming climate, albeit with diminishing sensitivity at higher Ca as vegetation responses saturate7,12,13.

These changes modify both aerodynamic and physiological controls on evapotranspiration: increasing LAI alters surface roughness and turbulent exchange, while concurrent adjustments in stomatal density and conductance regulate transpiration in response to Ca fertilization and atmospheric dryness10. Consistent with this body of evidence, the ensemble mean of 12 models from Coupled Model Intercomparison Project phase 6 (CMIP6)14 (Table S1) shows a spatially coherent greening signal in the historical period (1985–2014), with highest mean LAI values exceeding ~4–5 in tropical forest regions and lower values (<1) across arid and high-latitude regions (Fig. 1a). Under Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP)15 5–8.5, projected LAI increases are widespread, with positive anomalies dominating most vegetated land areas during 2071–2100 relative to the historical baseline (Fig. 1b). The multi-model ensemble mean indicates a global-scale increase in LAI on the order of ~0.4 by the end of the twenty-first century, while the inter-model spread remains substantial with a standard deviation reaching ~0.6–0.8 (Fig. 1c).

Observational and modeling studies suggest that, despite these evolving vegetation controls, historical trends in PET have remained relatively modest, reflecting a partial compensation between warming, greening, and physiological regulation16. In contrast, under future warming scenarios, rising Ca and associated physiological responses are expected to increasingly offset radiatively driven changes in evaporative demand12,17,18.

Typically, conventional PET formulations—most commonly the standardized Penman–Monteith19 and the open-water Penman20 equations—have been applied using near-surface temperature and VPD as externally imposed drivers. These atmospheric water demands, however, are not independent of land-surface moisture conditions. Over water-limited regions, reduced evaporative cooling enhances sensible heating, elevating air temperature and VPD through land–atmosphere feedbacks21,22. Thus, PET estimates derived directly from these amplified atmospheric states may reflect the consequences of limited surface water availability rather than an independent climatic forcing capable of driving basin-scale water balance23. Inattentive use of such formulations can artificially inflate PET and exaggerate its long-term trends, particularly when applied to climate projections in which land–atmosphere coupling intensifies24.

In the standardized Penman–Monteith framework19, aerodynamic resistance (ra) is computed assuming a fixed reference grass height using Monin–Obukhov similarity theory, such that temporal variations in ra arise only from changes in wind speed. Rising LAI introduces two concurrent effects on area-weighted ra: increasing surface roughness reduces ra over the vegetated fraction, while the expanding vegetation fraction (fc) shifts the area weighting toward the lower vegetated ra. The two-source formulation25 resolves both effects separately and combines them through area weighting by vegetation and bare-soil fractions. The resulting multi-model ensemble yields a net decrease in area-weighted ra when LAI-dependent roughness is accounted for, whereas considering wind-speed changes alone under the fixed reference-grass assumption yields weak or near-neutral trends (Fig. 2). This contrast highlights the importance of dynamically consistent ra formulations when interpreting long-term surface energy-flux projections26.

Likewise, canopy-scale surface resistance (rs) is not fixed but governed by stomatal physiology responding to Ca and VPD. The optimal stomatal conductance27 framework implies that elevated Ca directly reduces stomatal conductance (increasing rs), and rising VPD amplifies this reduction. Applied at the canopy scale, this framework produces rs values that are systematically lower than the 70 s m−1 assumed in the standard Penman–Monteith formulation, with pronounced spatial variability arising from the combined effects of vegetation and climate conditions (Fig. 3).

During the historical period (1985–2014), low rs values are not confined to densely vegetated regions but also occur in sparsely vegetated and arid areas where bare-soil evaporation dominates, whereas higher rs values emerge primarily in humid and semi-humid regions with extensive vegetation cover where stomatal regulation governs surface fluxes (Fig. 3a,c). Under SSP5-8.5, rs increases across most land surfaces, with global mean anomalies reaching approximately 40–50 s m−1 by the end of the 21st century when land–atmosphere feedbacks are retained (Fig. 3b,d). Removing these feedbacks through thermodynamic reconstruction of the atmospheric state that would prevail over a saturated surface yields a lower VPD, which in turn produces a smaller increase in optimal-conductance rs24. This indicates that a large fraction of the apparent stomatal closure arises from feedback-amplified atmospheric drying rather than from direct radiative warming alone.

In response to these limitations, recent advances have sought to improve the physical and physiological realism of PET formulations12,28. Vegetation-responsive approaches explicitly incorporate LAI-dependent aerodynamic properties and stomatal regulation linked to Ca and VPD, allowing PET to reflect changes in canopy structure and plant water use strategies. Complementary evaporation theories29,30 further provide a thermodynamic framework to account for land–atmosphere feedbacks by reconstructing the atmospheric state that would prevail over a hypothetically saturated surface, thereby deflating feedback-amplified PET. Despite these developments, numerous hydrologic and climate-impact studies still rely heavily on conventional PET formulations that do not explicitly address either evolving vegetation controls or feedback-induced atmospheric amplification.

Here, we present a global dataset of vegetation-responsive PET (PETveg) and its feedback-deflated counterpart (WETveg) for historical and future projections under four SSPs of CMIP6. PETveg is computed using a physically and physiologically based two-source formulation25 that explicitly accounts for LAI, Ca, and VPD through ra and rs terms. It provides vegetation-responsive demand under the prevailing atmospheric state—appropriate for applications where conventional PET is used as an externally forced climatic driver. In turn, WETveg is obtained by applying the linear complementary evaporation principle29 to PETveg. It restores the independence of PET from precipitation that is required in offline modeling frameworks24, representing demand under a thermodynamically deflated, feedback-free atmospheric condition—appropriate for impact analyses of projected changes where feedback inflation would otherwise bias long-term trends. By making both PETveg and WETveg publicly available in a standardized, gridded format, users can more transparently assess the sensitivity of hydrological, ecological, and drought-related analyses to PET formulation choices under historical and future climate conditions.

Methods

CMIP6 climate model simulations

The datasets were derived exclusively from simulations from 12 CMIP6 models (Table S1). Monthly climate variables required for estimating PET were obtained from the historical simulations and four future SSPs: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. The historical period spans 1850–2014, and future projections cover 2015–2100. The 12 model realizations were selected based on the availability of variables required for internally consistent PET estimates. The required climate variables include surface net radiation (Rn), 2 m air temperature (Ta), relative humidity (RH), 2 m wind speed (u), precipitation (P), and LAI. Rn was calculated as the sum of latent and sensible heat fluxes, and u values were derived from 10 m wind speed using a power-law vertical profile30. Simulated evapotranspiration (ET) from each CMIP6 model was additionally obtained for use in the wet-location benchmark described in the Technical Validation section.

We also used the gridded Ca datasets developed by Cheng et al.31. Their historical records span the period 1850–2013, while future Ca for 2015–2100 are provided under the SSP trajectories. Since values for 2014 were missing, we estimated them by linear interpolation using the corresponding monthly values from 2013 and 2015. The temporal interpolations were performed separately using SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 trajectories, and the resulting estimates were averaged to obtain a single set of Ca for 2014.

All input variables were collected as monthly averaged fields from the CMIP6 archive via the cloud-based data access route provided through the partnership between the Earth System Grid Federation (ESGF) and Pangeo (https://pangeo-data.github.io/pangeo-cmip6-cloud/index.html). Historical (1850–2014) and future (2015–2100) simulations were retrieved under the “historical” and “ssp126”, “ssp245”, “ssp370”, and “ssp585” experiment identifiers, respectively. Prior to PET calculation, the inputs were bilinearly interpolated to a common 1° × 1° horizontal resolution to ensure spatial consistency across models and scenarios.

Deflating PET using the complementary evaporation principle (CEP)

The feedback-deflated evaporative demand, WETveg, was derived following the graphical CEP described by Crago & Qualls29. This approach reconstructs the near-surface air temperature and vapor pressure that would occur above a hypothetically saturated land surface under the same radiative forcing, thereby removing the amplification of evaporative demand associated with land–atmosphere feedbacks in water-limited environments.

The procedure assumes that near-surface air temperature and vapor pressure are constrained along a conserved moist-enthalpy trajectory in T–e space as surface moisture becomes limiting and available energy is repartitioned between latent and sensible heat fluxes. Two thermodynamic states are distinguished along this trajectory: the wet-surface equilibrium state, at which the surface itself is saturated, and the wet-environment state, which characterizes the overlying air. Because entrainment from the boundary layer leaves air immediately above a saturated surface typically subsaturated, the wet-environment state lies off the saturation curve36.

Following Kim & Choi24, the wet-surface equilibrium state was first determined as the intersection between the energy-balance constraint and the saturation vapor-pressure curve, computed separately for bare-soil and vegetation components under the same Rn and u. The wet-environment air temperature and vapor pressure were then obtained by applying the entrainment-adjusted linear constraint of the graphical CEP framework29, which intersects the moist-enthalpy trajectory at a subsaturated point consistent with typical boundary-layer entrainment.

These reconstructed wet-environment values of T and e were substituted for the ambient values in the same two-source Penman–Monteith formulation used to compute PETveg, with Rn, u, LAI, and physiological parameters retained unchanged. The resulting WETveg thus represents vegetation-responsive evaporative demand under atmospheric conditions deflated to exclude first-order land–atmosphere feedback effects; second-order adjustments to Rn under the reconstructed state are not iterated, consistent with the linear CEP framework29.

Data Records

The dataset described in this study consists of NetCDF files containing estimates of vegetation-responsive PETveg and its feedback-deflated counterpart (WETveg), derived from CMIP6 projections. The dataset covers the historical period (1850–2014) and four future SSPs (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5) for 2015–2100. All data files are publicly archived at an open-access repository37. Users concatenating historical and SSP files should note that Ca input for 2014 was obtained by temporal interpolation (see Methods); all other variables are continuous across the 2014/2015 boundary.

Each NetCDF file contains a single evaporative-demand variable (either PETveg or WETveg) from one CMIP6 model for either the historical simulation or one of the four SSP scenarios. File names follow a consistent convention that identifies the variable, temporal aggregation scale, CMIP6 model name, scenario, and temporal coverage—for example, PETveg_mon_ACCESS-ESM1-5_ssp585_201501-210012.nc. Unlike native CMIP6 outputs, all variables in this dataset are provided on a common regular latitude–longitude grid at 1° × 1° spatial resolution, obtained through bilinear interpolation of input fields prior to PET and WET calculations.

The core dimensions included in each file are time (monthly), latitude, and longitude. Evaporative demand values are provided as monthly mean water flux expressed in units of [mm d−1]. Land grid cells are retained in the dataset following the land–sea masks of the originating CMIP6 models, while ocean grid cells (land fraction <5%) are masked.

A detailed description of the file organization, naming conventions, variable definitions, and metadata structure is provided in an accompanying README file included in the data repository. This documentation is intended to support transparent reuse of the dataset in hydrological, ecological, and climate-impact applications.

Technical Validation

To assess the internal consistency and physical plausibility of the PETveg and WETveg products, we benchmark them against four alternative PET formulations and against ESM-simulated ET at wet locations where soil moisture is non-limiting. The four comparison formulations span the range of approaches commonly applied to climate-model outputs and represent distinct assumptions about vegetation response, land–atmosphere coupling, and reference surface (Table 1).

Structural differences among the six PET formulations in reference surface, aerodynamic and stomatal treatment, and feedback deflation.

The standardized reference-crop PET (PETrc) of the Food and Agriculture Organization19 assumes a short grass reference surface with fixed h = 0.12 m, LAI = 2.88, and rs = 70 s m−1, representing conventional water requirement without vegetation structural or physiological response. The open-water Penman formulation (PETow) is the upper benchmark of radiatively and aerodynamically driven demand, widely used in lake and reservoir hydrology20. The CO2-corrected Penman–Monteith formulation (PETY2019) of Yang et al.12 retains the reference-crop geometry of PETrc but introduces a linear increase in rs with Ca, so that it partially offsets radiatively driven demand increases under warming, while VPD is still amplified. Finally, the radiation-based wet-surface formulation (WETY2019) of Yang & Roderick38, derived from maximum-evaporation theory over the global ocean, is conceptually close to the net radiation scaling proposed by Milly & Dunne39. WETY2019 removes land–atmosphere feedbacks implicitly through its wet-surface construction but does not retain vegetation physiology, providing a structurally distinct counterpart to WETveg.

The climatological spatial patterns of the six formulations for 1985–2014 are shown in Fig. 4. PETrc and PETow (Fig. 4a,b) exhibit the highest magnitudes globally, with values exceeding 2,000 mm yr−1 across the subtropical drylands of North Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and Australia, reflecting the combined influence of high radiation, high VPD, and fixed or absent surface resistance. PETow is systematically higher than PETrc in humid mid-latitude regions because open-water evaporation has no stomatal resistance. PETY2019 (Fig. 4c) follows a similar subtropical-maximum pattern to PETrc but with slightly reduced magnitudes, consistent with the modest Ca-induced increase in rs. In contrast, WETY2019 (Fig. 4d) shows a markedly different spatial structure: the subtropical-dryland maxima largely disappear, and the global pattern instead follows the latitudinal gradient of net radiation, with highest values in equatorial and low-latitude humid regions. This reflects its radiation-based construction, which does not respond to feedback-amplified atmospheric aridity.

PETveg (Fig. 4e) sits between the conventional and wet-surface formulations: it retains the general aridity-influenced pattern of PETrc but with lower magnitudes across water-limited regions, reflecting the Medlyn-based stomatal closure in response to elevated Ca and VPD. WETveg (Fig. 4f) exhibits the most subdued spatial field among all six, with subtropical maxima removed and a spatial structure closer to WETY2019—consistent with its thermodynamic reconstruction of the wet-environment state. Together, the six panels illustrate a stepwise deflation of evaporative demand as additional processes are incorporated: from conventional formulations (PETrc, PETow) driven by the prevailing atmospheric state, through physiology-enabled formulations (PETY2019, PETveg) that partially offset radiatively driven demand, to feedback-removed formulations (WETY2019, WETveg) that recover a more radiation-controlled latitudinal pattern.

Under SSP5-8.5, projected changes in evaporative demand for 2071–2100 relative to 1985–2014 diverge strongly across the six formulations (Fig. 5). PETrc and PETow (Fig. 5a,b) show the largest increases, exceeding 300 mm yr−1 across most vegetated land areas and reaching particularly high values in South America, tropical Africa, and South Asia, where feedback-amplified warming and VPD directly inflate demand. PETY2019 (Fig. 5c) moderates these increases through its Ca-induced rs response but retains the spatial signature of feedback-driven amplification because it does not reconstruct the underlying atmospheric state. WETY2019 (Fig. 5d) shows more muted increases, reflecting its radiation-based construction that excludes VPD and aerodynamic feedback effects. PETveg (Fig. 5e) further dampens the projected increases through combined physiological responses to Ca and VPD under retained feedbacks, while WETveg (Fig. 5f) exhibits the smallest changes overall, with near-zero anomalies across most mid-latitude regions.

These contrasts are summarized in the global-mean time-series of Fig. 5g. The six formulations track each other closely during the historical period but diverge progressively after ~2000 under SSP5-8.5 forcing. By 2100, the multi-model mean increase in PETrc reaches ~270 mm yr−1, compared with ~160 mm yr−1 for PETY2019, ~110 mm yr−1 for PETveg, and ~55 mm yr−1 for WETveg—a factor-of-five spread between the most and least inflated estimates. The progressive reduction from PETrc to WETveg reflects the cumulative effect of incorporating physiological responses to Ca and VPD, and subsequently removing feedback-amplified atmospheric aridity.

The magnitude of this spread has substantial implications for climate-impact applications that use PET as an externally forced driver. Offline hydrological, agricultural, and drought-index models forced with conventional PET would project substantially larger future aridification, larger reductions in runoff, and more severe agricultural drought than models forced with physiologically and thermodynamically consistent demand5,6,23. Recent studies have shown that neglecting vegetation physiological responses overestimates projected declines in water availability by roughly 20–40% across global land12,28, while neglecting land–atmosphere feedback amplification further inflates projected trends23,24. The stepwise deflation evident in Fig. 5 is therefore not a minor technical correction but a first-order source of uncertainty in twenty-first-century projections of continental drying and drought risk.

At wet locations where soil moisture is non-limiting, actual ET simulated by an ESM approximates the atmospheric evaporative demand, providing an internal benchmark against which PET formulations can be evaluated39. We identified wet locations on a per-model, per-month basis as grid cells where the relative difference between PETveg and WETveg fell below 1%, reflecting conditions in which land–atmosphere feedbacks are inactive and feedback deflation leaves evaporative demand essentially unchanged. To ensure sufficient temporal sampling for the regression diagnostics, only grid cells with at least 120 wet months over each 30-year analysis period were retained. The resulting wet-location mask (Fig. S1) is concentrated in the tropical humid regions of South America, Central Africa, and the Maritime Continent during 1985–2014, with a contraction evident for 2071–2100 under SSP5-8.5, consistent with the projected widespread shift from energy-limited to water-limited ecosystem regimes reported by Denissen et al.40.

Figure 6 compares each of the six PET formulations against ESM-simulated ET at wet locations for the historical period 1985–2014. The multi-model ensemble mean PETveg and WETveg both reproduce ESM ET closely, with the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) = 0.87, Pearson r = 0.98, percent bias (p-bias) of approximately −5%, and the root mean square error (RMSE) = 0.21 mm d−1 (Fig. 6e,f). The conventional formulations show larger deviations: PETrc and PETY2019 exhibit comparable negative biases (~−11%) with NSE around 0.5, indicating that wet-location ESM ET is slightly underestimated when the reference-crop geometry is applied to climate-model forcing. PETow tracks ESM ET with the smallest p-bias (−0.37%) but a lower regression slope (0.82), reflecting its open-water construction. WETY2019 shows the largest positive bias (+16.79%) with NSE = −0.07, indicating that the radiation-based wet-surface formulation systematically overestimates terrestrial evaporative demands. The consistent performance of PETveg and WETveg across both metrics and their near-unity regression slopes support the physical plausibility of the two-source formulation under non-limiting moisture conditions.

Under SSP5-8.5 for 2071–2100 (Fig. 7), the relative skill of the six formulations shifts in physically interpretable ways. PETveg and WETveg retain the highest NSE values (0.82 and 0.81, respectively) with slopes near unity and p-bias of approximately −3%, indicating that their consistency with ESM ET at wet locations is preserved under warming. PETrc declines modestly (NSE = 0.78), while PETY2019 and WETY2019 deteriorate more substantially (NSE = 0.57 and −1.23, respectively). PETrc’s improved NSE at wet locations is not evidence of improved global skill; Fig. 5a shows it still produces the largest global-mean demand increase, driven by feedback-amplified inflation over water-limited regions that the wet-location benchmark excludes. The WETY2019 bias grows from +16.79% to +18.80%, suggesting that radiation-based wet-surface formulations become increasingly inconsistent with ESM-simulated ET as surface temperatures rise and the s/(s + 0.24γ) coefficient approaches unity. The stability of PETveg and WETveg across both periods indicates that the two-source physiological and thermodynamic treatments remain internally consistent with ESM-simulated wet-surface ET under a wide range of climatic conditions, supporting their applicability for climate-impact analyses across historical and future periods.
