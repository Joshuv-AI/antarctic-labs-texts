A Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)-derived AAGC Model for In-hospital Mortality Prediction in Sepsis Incorporating Age, APACHE II Score, Glasgow Coma Scale, and Creatinine: A Prospective Study
Authors: Hemant Gulia, Mohit Suhag, Nikhil Bagal, Prity R Deshwal, Sandeep Kumar, Rahul K Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsSepsis remains a major global cause of critical illness and death. Existing scores like Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) vary widely, highlighting the need for earlier, reliable risk identification. We aimed to identify key predictors of mortality among inpatients with sepsis and develop a simplified data-driven predictive model using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method.Patients and methodsA prospective cohort study was carried out involving 150 adult patients diagnosed with sepsis and admitted to a tertiary care hospital in India between November 2024 and April 2025. Demographic, clinical, and biochemical data were recorded at admission. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was used for variable selection, followed by multivariable logistic regression to develop the final model. Model performance was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis.ResultsOf the 150 patients, 86 (57.3%) died during hospitalization. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99) and strong calibration (mean absolute error: 0.011; 90th percentile: 0.026). High respiratory rate (RR), culture-negative sepsis, respiratory failure, and septic shock were also independently associated with mortality.ConclusionThis LASSO-based AAGC model offers a robust, interpretable tool for early mortality prediction in sepsis, thereby supporting timely interventions and improving patient outcomes. Introduction

Sepsis occurs when the immune system overreacts to an infection, leading to life-threatening organ dysfunction. Sepsis is a leading contributor to critical illness and death worldwide.1,2 It is a major global health burden, causing an estimated 19.4 million cases and 5.3 million deaths annually. The Global Burden of Disease 2017 study expanded this understanding by analyzing sepsis trends across 195 countries, 282 causes, and all age-groups from 1990 to 2017.3 In India, an estimated 11.3 million sepsis cases and approximately 2.9 million related deaths were reported in 2017, highlighting the critical need for improved prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, especially in low- and middle-income settings.1

Despite advances in critical care, early and accurate risk stratification in sepsis remains a challenge, largely because existing scoring systems have important limitations. Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) scores are widely used for predicting mortality in sepsis, but their performance varies considerably across different populations and settings. For example, a study in a tertiary care hospital in a low-middle-income country found that qSOFA had high specificity, but its sensitivity was variable, especially in nonintensive care unit (ICU) patients.4,5

Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II, SOFA, and qSOFA scores are widely used for predicting mortality in sepsis, but their performance varies though their predictive value is not consistent across all settings; for example, in a 2022 ICU cohort study comparing APACHE II, SOFA, simplified acute physiology score II (SAPS II), and Outcome and Assessment Information Set (OASIS) in sepsis/septic shock patients, AUCs ranged from ~0.80 (APACHE II) to ~0.90 (SAPS II).6 Many of these tools were developed using Western ICU cohorts and may not fully capture the demographic and clinical variations seen in low- and middle-income countries like India.7 Furthermore, most studies have evaluated these scores in isolation rather than exploring integrated models that combine clinical, biochemical, and physiological factors.8,9

Given these limitations, this study aimed to analyze demo-graphic, clinical, and laboratory data to understand how each variable relates to mortality and to develop a predictive model with the help of least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-based logistic regression to highlight the most influential predictors.

Patients and Methods

For machine learning model construction, this prospective cohort observational study was conducted at the ICUs of Maharishi Markandeshwar Institute of Medical Sciences and Research, a tertiary care teaching hospital in Ambala (India), from November 2024 to April 2025. The bed occupancy index of the hospital was 0.80. Thus, the number of bed days of the hospital was 101,616 (348 × 365 × 0.80).

All adult ICU patients were consecutively screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years; and (2) sepsis diagnosis based on the Sepsis 3.0 definition, irrespective of whether sepsis was present at admission or developed before ICU transfer during hospitalization.10 Enrollment continued until achieving the goal of obtaining 150 patients, which had been estimated using the Riley et al. method, a target shrinkage of 0.90, expected R2 of 0.15 and an anticipated mortality rate of 41%.11,12 Allowing a ±8% margin of error for estimating mortality prevalence required 145 patients. Therefore, a sample size of 150 patients was determined to meet the methodological requirements for model development. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients had a known terminal illness with a prognosis of less than 6 months; (2) declined participation; (3) inability to provide informed consent; and (4) patients readmitted to the ICU during the same hospitalization. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and when a patient was unable to provide consent due to their condition, it was obtained from a legally authorized representative or family member. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Ethical Committee (Project No: IEC-3075). Data collection followed a consistent protocol across all cases.

Clinical and demographic data were collected prospectively using a preformatted case record form from all eligible patients at the time of ICU admission. Demographic variables included age, gender, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption. Clinical data collected at admission included respiratory rate (RR), blood pressure (BP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation (SpO2), arterial pH, serum sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), and creatinine levels. In addition, the source of culture and infection was documented. All the collected data were completely anonymized, and no unique identifiers were recorded. Patients were followed until ICU discharge, and survival status at discharge was recorded as the primary outcome. All data were cross-checked with electronic medical records to ensure accuracy and completeness.

The qSOFA score was mainly used for risk stratification, identifying patients with suspected infection who were at increased risk for organ dysfunction, ICU admission, or mortality. It consists of RR, SBP and level of consciousness, which were taken at the bedside upon admission. The qSOFA score was calculated using three clinical parameters: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), SBP, and RR. Each parameter was assigned a binary value – Altered mentation (GCS) ≤ 14 (yes/no), SBP < 100 mm Hg (yes/no), and RR > 22 breaths per minute (yes/no). One point was awarded for each parameter meeting the cutoff and zero if not. The final qSOFA score, ranging from 0 to 3, represented the simple sum of these three binary variables.

Another tool used for risk assessment was the APACHE II score, which was computed within the first 24 hours of ICU stay, using available physiological and laboratory parameters. Points were allocated to the worst values of each variable as per the protocol of the APACHE II scoring system calculation. Age and chronic health were also assigned points similarly, and a total APACHE score was obtained

The primary outcome of this study was in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization and recorded as a binary variable (yes/no).

The whole data was randomly divided into a 7:3 ratio, i.e., 70% of patients were assigned to training, and the remaining 30% of patients were assigned to the test dataset. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the association between predictor variables and survival status. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was used for variable selection to decrease the problem of overfitting by shrinking insignificant coefficients toward 0 and keeping only the most informative predictors. The penalty parameter (λ) was selected using cross-validation; the variables selected by LASSO were used in multivariable logistic regression to estimate ORs with 95% CIs.

Model performance was primarily evaluated by measuring the discriminative ability of the model. It was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and measured by the area under the ROC curve (AUROC). A plot of sensitivity (true-positive rate) against 1 – specificity (false positive rate) was plotted for consecutive cut-offs for the predicted mortality.

A nomogram model was developed using multivariable logistic regression. Further, the accuracy of the model was verified by bootstrap validation through computer resampling for 1,000 repetitions of simple random sampling with replacement. Calibration curves were used to assess the consistency between the model's predicted probabilities and the actual outcomes. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses and reporting followed the transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis (TRIPOD) criteria for a prediction model study, as represented in supplementary material S1.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages to illustrate patient characteristics. Depending on the data type and distribution, appropriate statistical tests, including the student's t-test, Mann–Whitney U test or Chi-square test, were applied to compare differences between groups. For variables with non-normal distributions, medians were reported to better represent the central tendency. The AUROC was used to evaluate the model. The p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data processing and analysis were performed using R software (version 4.3.2).

Risk stratification scores showed strong predictive value. In the qSOFA model, each one-point increase was associated with a sixfold rise in mortality risk (OR = 6.219, 95% CI: 3.239–13.625, p < 0.0001). Elevated RR (OR = 4.219, p < 0.0001) and altered mental state (GCS component: OR = 3.860, p < 0.0001) were significant predictors, while SBP was borderline significant (OR = 1.917, p = 0.053).

The APACHE II system also demonstrated uniform prognostic power. Acute physiology score component (OR = 1.121, p < 0.0001) and APACHE II total score (OR = 1.119, p < 0.0001) were independently linked with mortality. Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation predicted mortality had the greatest effect size (OR = 31.925, 95% CI: 6.388–187.290, p < 0.0001), which underscores its strong predictive power. Patients who were in the highest category of risk (category 4) were 16 times more likely to die (OR = 16.000, p = 0.009).

A higher RR significantly increased the risk of mortality in sepsis patients, with each unit rise associated with 17% higher odds of death (OR = 1.169, 95% CI: 1.080–1.278, p < 0.0001). In addition, the diagnosis of septic shock was strongly associated with mortality, conferring more than a sixfold higher risk (OR = 6.486, 95% CI: 3.135–14.018, p < 0.0001).

Variable Selection Based on the LASSO Regression

To refine predictors of mortality, this study applied the LASSO regression. The coefficient path and cross-validation plots are shown in Figures 1 and 2.

(A) LASSO coefficient path plot showing the trajectories of variable coefficients as the regularization parameter (log λ) increases. Coefficients shrinking to zero indicate the exclusion of a variable; (B) LASSO cross-validation plot displaying binomial deviance across log λ values. Red dots denote mean deviance with standard errors. The left dashed line marks the λ with minimum deviance, and the right dashed line indicates the largest λ within one standard error

At the optimal tuning parameter (λ_min), the model retained 22 variables with non-zero coefficients. However, using the more conservative λ_1se criterion, the model was reduced to four key predictors: Age, APACHE II score, GCS, and creatinine. The model was subsequently named AAGC, derived from the initials of these predictors. These four variables demonstrated the strongest and most consistent association with outcome and were subsequently carried forward for multivariable logistic regression analysis. The predictive AAGC model was constructed using the LASSO-logistic regression equation, with detailed information on the LASSO model provided in supplementary materials S2 and S3.

Mortality ~ Age + APACHE II score + GCS + Creatinine

To better understand the relationships among the four predictors selected by LASSO, a correlation heatmap was generated (supplementary material S4). The analysis showed that most of these variables had only weak interrelationships, suggesting that each contributed unique information to the model. Age showed mild positive correlations with APACHE II score (r = 0.20) and creatinine (r = 0.17), and was nearly uncorrelated with GCS (r = 0.03). Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score and GCS displayed a strong negative correlation (r = −0.76), consistent with the inverse relationship between illness severity and neurological function. Creatinine showed weak correlations with the APACHE II score (r = 0.22) and age (r = 0.17) and a negligible correlation with the GCS (r = −0.04).

These findings suggest that the overall pattern showed no significant overlap among the selected predictors, confirming that each variable contributed independently to the multivariable analysis.

As shown in Figure 3A, a nomogram was developed, using the final variables from the LASSO-based AAGC model (age, APACHE II score, GCS, and creatinine). Each predictor was assigned a point value, which was weighted, and the total points corresponded to the estimated probability of mortality. For instance, higher age, elevated creatinine levels, a higher APACHE II score and a lower GCS contributed to an increased predicted risk. The nomogram provides an intuitive graphical tool to facilitate individualized risk estimation at the bedside, supporting personalized clinical decision-making in patients with sepsis.

The performance of the last LASSO-based AAGC model, which included age, APACHE II score, GCS and creatinine, was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. The model showed excellent discrimination power with an AUC of 0.95 with 95% CI ranging from 0.92 to 0.99, as represented in Figure 2A. This shows that the model distinguished between survivors and nonsurvivors in ICU patients Table 1.

(A) ROC curve demonstrating the discriminative ability of the LASSO model; (B) Calibration plot assessing the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, indicating the model's calibration accuracy; (C) Predicted probability distribution illustrating the distinction between survivors and nonsurvivors

The LASSO model's calibration was evaluated using a bootstrap resampling procedure with 1,000 repetitions. The calibration plot showed outstanding agreement between predicted and actual mortality, as shown in Figure 2B. The bias-corrected curve fell very close to the ideal 45° line with only slight deviations over risk probabilities Table 2.

A mean absolute error of 0.011 was calculated, and the 90th percentile (0.9 quantile) of absolute error was 0.026, indicating that for 90% of the predictions, the deviation from the true observed outcomes was less than 2.6%. This confirmed that the model demonstrated good calibration and reliability in predicting patient mortality for sepsis. The distribution of predicted probabilities for nonsurvival is shown in Figure 2C. Patients who did not survive (red curve) had a higher predicted probability of death, clustering toward the right side of the distribution (mean ~ 0.75). In contrast, survivors (green curve) were more concentrated toward lower predicted probabilities (mean ~ 0.25). Although some overlaps were observed between the two groups, the separation of the curves indicates that the model is able to discriminate reasonably well between survival and nonsurvival outcomes Table 3.

Decision Curve Analysis (DCA)

Figure 3B presents the decision curve analysis demonstrating the potential clinical utility of the AAGC model. The analysis showed that the LASSO-based AAGC model provided a greater net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (0.1–0.8) compared with the default strategies of treating all patients or treating none. This suggests that using the model to guide clinical decision-making may improve risk stratification by identifying patients at higher risk of in-hospital mortality while avoiding unnecessary interventions in lower risk individuals. These findings indicate that the model may have practical value in supporting clinical decision-making in critically ill sepsis patients.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a mortality prediction AAGC model for sepsis patients using LASSO-selected variables and logistic regression. Of the 51 candidate variables, age, APACHE II score, GCS and creatinine emerged as the most consistent and powerful predictors. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC ≈ 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99) and very good calibration (mean absolute error: 0.011; 90th-percentile error: 0.026). These findings suggest the model may be both accurate and clinically useful in stratifying risk among sepsis patients.

In this study, sepsis cases were observed among both male and female patients, with no statistically significant difference between groups. Similar findings have been reported in an Indian ICU study, where 62.1% of sepsis patients were male.7 Another study from a tertiary care center in South India reported higher SOFA scores among males.13 However, these findings were not statistically significant and were based on unadjusted analyses; no conclusions can be drawn regarding gender-based differences in the incidence of sepsis.

In this study, most sepsis patients had significant comorbidities (92%) with high rates of smoking (54.7%) and alcohol use (46.7%). Septic shock was common (65.3%), and urine and blood were the primary infection sites. Besides, the majority of the population (57.4%) needed ventilator support. These findings are also consolidated by a tertiary care ICU study conducted in 2025 in Karnataka state with high comorbidity burdens, frequent urinary and bloodstream infections, and a substantial number of patients on ventilators among sepsis cases. The study did not particularly look into the lifestyle factors that include smoking or alcohol consumption; the findings also agree with this study in the sense that patients who have chronic conditions and those requiring invasive supports usually present with severe disease courses.14

The finding in this study that culture-negative status was associated with increased odds of mortality (OR = 3.394, p = 0.018) is intriguing and aligns with newer evidence. In patients with septic shock, culture-negative cases have been observed to carry higher mortality compared to culture-positive ones, potentially due to delayed targeted therapy or diagnostic uncertainty.15 Another study comparing outcomes of culture-positive vs culture-negative sepsis reported that although early mortality was similar, culture-positive patients had worse late mortality (beyond 30 days) in some cohorts.16

In this study, the APACHE II score demonstrated strong discriminatory power for predicting mortality among sepsis patients (AUC = 0.95, p < 0.001), highlighting its reliability as a prognostic tool. This finding is in line with the study by Ali et al., who also identified the APACHE II score as a strong predictor of mortality among ICU patients with sepsis. They reported an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.74–0.85) with a cutoff value of 26, reinforcing the score's effectiveness in recognizing patients at higher risk of death early in their clinical course.17 Similarly, Colussi et al. found that early application of prognostic scores, including APACHE II and qSOFA, effectively stratified mortality risk in septic patients admitted to the emergency department, with APACHE II demonstrating superior predictive performance (AUC = 0.83) compared to other scoring systems.18 All these studies line up showing that APACHE II really holds up as a tool for predicting mortality. In our analysis, the model actually performed even better, probably because we combined clinical and lab data. That mix seems to sharpen the accuracy of the AAGC model when you are trying to sort out risk in big hospital settings.

A comparable nomogram was developed by Yang et al. among sepsis patients with encephalopathy, integrating predictors such as age, GCS, lactate, bilirubin, RDW, RR, temperature, and vasopressor use, achieving AUCs of 0.763 in training and 0.753 in validation sets. In contrast, this AAGC model, which includes age, GCS, APACHE II score and creatinine, achieved an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI 0.92–0.99). The higher discrimination in this cohort could reflect differences in patient inclusion (all sepsis vs sepsis-associated encephalopathy subset), predictor selection via LASSO and the strength of physiological scoring. Furthermore, whereas Yang's study demonstrated an “adequate fit” in calibration and favorable net benefit over SOFA and logistic organ dysfunction system (LODS) in decision curve analysis, our results showed tight calibration (mean error: 0.011, 90th-percentile error: 0.026) and superior net benefit across wide thresholds, suggesting potentially greater robustness and clinical utility in a broader sepsis population.19

Elevated creatinine was a predictor in our model, indicating that renal dysfunction remains a key determinant of mortality. A large multicenter retrospective study in China similarly found creatinine among independent risk factors (alongside age, albumin, D-dimer) for 28-day mortality.20

The AAGC model showed outstanding performance (AUC ≈ 0.95), which is higher than or at least comparable to many existing risk prediction tools. For instance, a recent study that developed a nomogram for predicting 28-day sepsis mortality using factors like mechanical ventilation, lactate, oxygenation index and BUN reported AUC values of only about 0.80–0.82.21 In another model predicting mortality in sepsis patients, the ROC curve was evaluated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, yielding a bootstrap-adjusted AUC of 0.857 (95% CI: 0.799–0.912) which demonstrated that the model maintained comparable statistical strength after internal validation.22 While those models performed well, our model distinguishes itself by combining clinical, physiological, and biochemical markers, resulting in stronger and more accurate predictive power. Also, decision curve analysis further demonstrated that the proposed model may provide meaningful clinical benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities, supporting its potential utility in guiding early risk stratification in sepsis patients.

This study has a few limitations, as it was conducted in a single tertiary care center with a limited sample size, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Few predictors included in the model, including age and variables derived from the APACHE II score, may share overlapping clinical information, introducing potential collinearity and limiting the interpretability of individual predictor contributions. Some laboratory and follow-up data were unavailable for all patients, potentially influencing the model's accuracy. The relatively high AUC observed in this study may partly reflect optimism due to internal validation using a random training-testing split, raising the possibility of model overfitting. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse patient groups, along with real-time data collection, are needed to confirm and improve the performance of this prediction model. Combining it with emerging biomarkers and advanced machine learning tools could further strengthen early sepsis detection and make outcome prediction more precise and clinically useful.

Conclusion

This study highlights the prognostic value of clinical and biochemical variables, alongside validated scoring systems, in predicting outcomes among sepsis patients. In multivariate analysis, demographic factors showed poor discriminatory power while physiological parameters, especially RR and the components of qSOFA, emerged as significant independent predictors of mortality. Among the risk stratification tools, APACHE and qSOFA scores proved to have strong discriminatory power, supporting their role in identifying high-risk patients early in the course of illness. The presence of septic shock and respiratory failure further augmented the mortality risk.

Overall, these findings highlighted the importance of incorporating bedside clinical assessment into standardized scoring systems to achieve accurate risk stratification and early intervention in sepsis. Future studies should further validate these predictors in larger multicenter cohorts and explore the use of machine learning models to enhance early prognostication.
