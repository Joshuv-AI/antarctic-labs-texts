Association of impaired levels with outcomes for cervical fracture dislocation using the Japanese diagnosis procedure combination database
Authors: Kazuma Doi, Naoki Otani, Norihiko Inoue, Junichi Mizuno, Kiyohide Fushimi, Atsuo Yoshino
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Study Design:Retrospective study of data abstracted from the diagnosis procedure combination (DPC) database.Objecitives:The etiology of cervical fracture dislocation (CFD) remains unknown because of the limited clinical investigations that involve approximately 100 individuals. This study investigated the association of CFD-impaired levels with patient outcomes.Methods:This study included 4653 adult patients with a definitive CFD diagnosis from the DPC database. The database consisted of nationwide inpatient data collected from >1000 acute care hospitals in Japan. This DPC database contains information regarding the hospitalization course, such as diagnosis, treatment, medical history, complications, and hospitalization outcomes. We included 866 CFD patients whose impaired levels were specified. We compared the outcomes between the patients whose CFD occurred in the upper cervical levels (“high” group) and the intermediate-low levels (“low” group). This study determined 121 pairs of patients after one-to-one propensity score matching (PSM). The main outcomes included inhospital death, 30-day mortality, and major complications after admission. The secondary outcomes were the length of hospital stay, discharged home rate, and improvement in Barthel index.Results:No differences in inhospital death and 30-day mortality were found between the high and low groups, even after adjusting for PSM (P > 0.05, respectively). Moreover, this analysis revealed that the high group exhibited a higher incidence of respiratory complications than the low group (37.2% vs. 24.8%; P = 0.0256).Conclusions:This study revealed no difference in mortality but a significantly higher incidence of respiratory complications with high-level CFD compared to low-level CFD.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical fracture dislocation (CFD) demonstrates a relatively poor prognosis due to severe instability and spinal cord injury (SCI).[1] Studies reported that the higher the SCI level, the greater the respiratory complication risks.[23] However, the same theory remains unknown in CFD because of the extremely limited number of patients being reported. Moreover, no studies have investigated the outcomes based on the CFD-impaired levels. Therefore, the present study investigated the association of the CFD-impaired levels on the outcomes using the diagnosis procedure combination (DPC) database, which is a Japanese original database containing inpatient data collected from >1000 acute care hospitals in Japan.

METHODS

Data source

The DPC database is a Japanese database obtained from administrative claims from >1000 hospitals and approximately 50% of all acute care inpatient.[4] This database contains information on the hospitalization course, diagnosis, medical treatment, medical history, complications, and hospitalization outcomes. The database includes the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes for primary and secondary diagnoses, comorbidities on admission, and complications after admission.

Study participants

This study included 4653 inpatients with a definitive CFD diagnosis (ICD-10 codes: S122) in primary or comorbid diseases from 2010 to 2021. The exclusion criteria were (1) ≤20 years old, (2) previous CFD-related admission, (3) died ≤24 h after admission, (4) scheduled admission, and (5) multiple trauma affecting clinical outcomes, including severe head injury or pelvic fracture [Figure 1]. This study enrolled 2750 patients, including 2083 men and 667 women. Among these patients, this study included 866 CFD patients whose impaired levels were specified. These patients were categorized into two groups following the impaired levels: The high group, consisting of patients whose impaired levels were C2/3 or C3/4 (n = 200), and the “low” group, including patients whose levels were C4/5, C5/6, or C6/7 (n = 666). One-to-one propensity score matching (PSM) determined 121 pairs of patients (standardized mean difference of <0.1). Data extraction

Clinical characteristics on admission were specified from the DPC database, including age, sex, and preexisting comorbid conditions evaluated by Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), Barthel index (BI), and body mass index. Similarly, variables, including steroid pulse therapy, vasopressor use, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, hemodialysis, SCI (ICD-10 codes; S141, S199, and T093), ventilation, and surgical treatments, were extracted. This study categorized CCI scores into four groups following the total scores for comorbidities:[5] None (0 points), low (1–2 points), intermediate (3–4 points), and high (≥5 points). Main clinical outcomes were inhospital death, 30-day mortality, and major complications after admission, including cardiac events, cerebral infarction, delirium, gastrointestinal bleeding or ulcer, ileus, pulmonary embolism or deep-vein thrombosis, respiratory diseases, sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, coagulopathy, shock, and urinary tract infection.

Major complications were specified from ICD-10 codes as cardiac events (I099, I110, I20.x–I25.x, I50.x, and I518), cerebral infarction (I630–I635 and I639), delirium (F050, F051, and F059), gastrointestinal bleeding or ulcer (K25.x., K26.x, K279, K550, K572, K626, K631, K921, and K922), ileus (K560–567 and K913), pulmonary embolism (I269) or deep-vein thrombosis (I802), respiratory diseases (B59, J11.x–J12.x, J13, J14, J15.x, J160, J18.x, J690, J704, J841, J849, J95.x, J960.x, J969.x, J981, and Z930), sepsis (A207, A227, A241, A267, A282, A327, A394, A40.x, A41.x A427, A548, B007, B349, and B377) or disseminated intravascular coagulation (ICD-10 code: D65.x) or coagulopathy (D689), shock (R57.x, T 794, and T811), and urinary tract infection (A022, A181, N10, N12, N390, and T835).

Secondary outcomes include length of hospital stay, discharged home rate (the rate of patients being discharged directly to home), and improvement in BI (the difference between BI scores at admission and discharge).

Statistical analysis

Software R version 4.1.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was conducted for all statistical analyses, with Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test for comparison of continuous data and the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for comparison of categorical data between the groups. One-to-one PSM was conducted in the high and low groups. A two-tailed significance level of P < 0.05 was defined in each analysis. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

RESULTS

Table 3 shows the primary and secondary outcomes after PSM adjustment. Inhospital death was not significant between the two groups (12.4% vs. 9.1% in the high and low groups, respectively; P > 0.05). Similarly, 30-mortality was not significantly different (P > 0.05). Among complications after admission, only respiratory diseases were significantly higher in the high group than in the low group (37.2% vs. 24.8%; P = 0.0256). The secondary outcomes were not significantly different (respectively, P > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

The present study revealed no association between the impaired levels in CFD and mortality. Our previous research demonstrated that 30-mortality was higher in the early surgery group than in the delayed surgery group.[6] Therefore, high mortality was indicated to be caused by postinjury or postsurgical stress, not the impaired levels themselves.

Furthermore, the present study revealed that only respiratory complications were significantly more common in the high group after adjusting for PSM. This result was compatible with some literature on SCI alone.[23]

The first possible hypothesis is that both groups include approximately 80% of patients with CFD and SCI, which may have caused similar results with SCI alone reports. Pulmonary complications, such as pneumonia, atelectasis, and respiratory failures, are the leading causes of morbidity in patients with higher SCI levels.[27] The higher the SCI level, the more severe the respiratory impairment.[2] The risk of pneumonia involves various factors, such as injury level, mechanical ventilation tracheostomy, and dysphagia.[8] Respiratory muscle paralysis causes lesser secretion, bacterial accumulation, and resultant respiratory infections.[8] Moreover, lung compliance reduction decreases lung volumes and changes the lung’s mechanical properties.[7] These pathologies may result because respiratory complications were more prevalent in the high group of patients with CFD without SCI.

Another explanation is that ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) may be associated with higher respiratory complications. The ratio of ventilator users was not significantly higher but was larger in the high group. Respiratory support and early ventilator weaning are crucial to reduce the risk of VAP for patients with SCI.[9] Mechanical ventilation and prolonged tracheostomy increase the risk of pneumonia.[8] Patients with pneumonia achieved poorer functional recovery and shorter survival compared to SCI patients without pneumonia.[10]

The incidence of glucocorticoid administration, including oral prednisolone medication and methylprednisolone pulse therapy, was not different between the two groups. Besides, surgical invasiveness may influence the incidence of respiratory complications. However, the proportion of patients who underwent surgery was not significant between the two groups. Therefore, steroid use and surgical invasiveness could not be concluded to cause a direct greater risk of respiratory complications in the high group.

Limitations to the study

This study has various limitations. We made our best efforts to minimize the bias by PSM, but the following limitations still exist.(1) Clinical information that can identify the severity or level of SCI (Frankel Classification or American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale), type of dislocation, and presence of interlocking is lacking. In addition, the cause of inhospital death is missing in the DPC database.(2) The database contains information only during hospitalization. Therefore, the precise time of injury is uncertain. The timing of admission and injury is speculated as almost the same day, so that makes no large difference.(3) The strategy of surgery (surgical approaches and timings varied) or conservative treatments depends on the facilities and surgeons. The surgeon’s preferences and radiological findings, such as traumatic disc herniation, deformity, and malalignment, may have caused selection bias, especially in surgical treatments.

CONCLUSIONS

This nationwide study investigated the largest data collection regarding the impaired level effects on outcomes for CFD. This study provided valuable and original perspectives into the ongoing discussions over the effects of CFD-impaired levels. We revealed no difference in mortality but a significantly higher incidence of respiratory complications with high-level CFD compared to low-level CFD. Further clinical research for CFD with more detailed data or randomized controlled trials is warranted to elucidate new findings.
