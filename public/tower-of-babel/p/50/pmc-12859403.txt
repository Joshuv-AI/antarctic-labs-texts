A facile chemical strategy to synthesize precise AAV-protein conjugates for targeted gene delivery
Authors: Quan Pham, Jake Glicksman, Boyang Han, Seyed Sadegh Shahraeini, Delilah Jewel, Conor Loynd, Soumya Jyoti Singha Roy, Abhishek Chatterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent adeno-associated virus (AAV) gene therapies are limited by a lack of tissue-specificity, leading to toxic off-target impacts and increased therapeutic costs. More target-specific AAV vectors can offer safer and less expensive therapies. Antibody-like proteins can be used to retarget AAV vectors to unique cell-surface receptors. However, fusing such proteins onto the delicate AAV capsid proteins is complicated; only small and well-folded proteins are tolerated and the architecture of such fusions are highly restricted. Here, we report a versatile chemical strategy to covalently attach recombinant proteins onto the AAV capsid, using a combination of genetic code expansion (GCE) and bioorthogonal conjugation chemistry. This method is efficient, allows excellent control over the site of attachment, and the number of proteins attached on the AAV capsid. Systematic optimization of these parameters allowed us to generate highly active AAV2-anti-HER2 nanobody conjugates that significantly outperform their counterparts generated through genetic fusion. This conjugate exhibited excellent efficiency and selectivity toward HER2+ cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we demonstrate that our strategy can be used to attach diverse recombinant proteins, including superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP) and a full-length antibody, creating novel opportunities to engineer AAV.Keywords: MT: Regular Issue, adeno-associated virus, genetic code expansion, noncanonical amino acid, bioorthogonal conjugation, tumor-targeting

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) mediated delivery of therapeutic genetic cargo in vivo has advanced at a rapid pace in recent years, enabling first-in-class human gene therapies such as Luxturna, Zolgensma, and Hemgenix.1,2,3,4,5 The remarkable success of AAV vectors can be attributed to their unique features such as non-pathogenic nature, low immunogenicity, and the ability to provide long-term gene expression.1,2,3 However, our lack of control over the tropism of available AAV vectors remains a key challenge for developing next-generation gene therapies with superior efficacy and safety profiles. Due to the current reliance on native AAV serotypes, which exhibit suboptimal tissue specificity, very high doses are typically required to achieve sufficient on-target delivery, which leads to unintended off-target effects and high costs of treatment.1,2,3,4,5,6

Numerous strategies have been explored to improve the tissue-specificity of AAV vectors,4 which broadly falls into two categories: directed evolution based approaches, and rational incorporation of known targeting elements onto the virus capsid. Directed evolution based methods typically rely on generating a large library of capsid variants through different strategies, including error-prone mutagenesis,7,8,9 DNA-shuffling of existing capsid variants,10,11,12,13 or the insertion of a randomized peptide loop at permissive sites within the capsid protein,14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21 followed by their selection in vitro or in live animals for desired tropism. This approach has been used to develop several AAV mutants that exhibit improved selectivity for transducing specific types of target cells. Nevertheless, directed evolution of AAV capsids is a time-consuming and resource-intensive process, and an optimal outcome is not always guaranteed. Additionally, the improved properties of the resulting capsid variants do not always translate across preclinical models.

Attaching a known targeting element onto the virus capsid, which can selectively bind a cell-surface receptor expressed on the target tissue, has also been used as an alternative method for retargeting existing AAV vectors. Small retargeting ligands, such as short peptides and aptamers have been used for such proof-of-concept applications,4,22,23,24,25 but the clinical utility of these approaches are limited by suboptimal affinity, selectivity, and in vivo stability of such ligands. Antibodies and similar proteins (e.g., affibodies and DARPINs) can be readily evolved to bind desired cell-surface receptors with excellent affinity and selectivity.26,27,28 These proteins have been used with much success in clinical settings for targeted delivery of other classes of therapeutics, such as antibody-drug conjugates and bispecific antibodies.29,30 These proteins are particularly attractive candidates for retargeting AAV, but displaying them on the complex and delicate virus capsid is non-trivial due to perturbations of capsid assembly and packaging. It has been possible to fuse small antibody-like proteins onto the N-terminus, or at an internal loop of minor capsid proteins of AAV.31,32,33,34,35,36,37 However, only small and well-folded proteins are compatible with such fusion, and even then, it can disrupt capsid architecture and packaging efficiency. It has also been possible to attach proteins onto the AAV capsid using HUH tag and DNA linkers.38 However, the use of the bacteria-derived HUH protein and DNA as a linker raise concerns over the immunogenicity and stability of such conjugates in vivo.

Post-packaging chemical labeling of the virus capsid is an attractive alternative for attaching retargeting groups, since it does not disrupt the virus assembly process.4 Native amino acids such as lysine, arginine, and tyrosine,25,39,40,41 as well as noncanonical amino acids (ncAA)s with bioorthogonal conjugation handles22,23,24,42,43,44,45,46,47 have been used to functionalize the AAV capsids for various applications, including retargeting. However, linking the AAV capsid and a protein through chemical conjugation is challenging due to the slow reaction kinetics of traditional bioorthogonal conjugation reactions.48,49 Here we report a general strategy to efficiently generate any AAV-protein conjugate with unparalleled control over the attachment sites and the stoichiometry of capsid labeling. Using the genetic code expansion (GCE) technology,50,51,52 a ncAA with a bioorthogonal conjugations handle can be site-specifically introduced onto the AAV2 capsid as well as in the recombinant protein with complete site control. Next, the recombinant protein can be covalently attached onto the AAV2 capsid using a bifunctional linker using an ultra-fast bioconjugation chemistry. Using this method, we generated AAV2 conjugates with anti-HER2 nanobody as well as a full-length anti-HER2 antibody, which exhibit efficient and selective transduction of HER2+ cell lines. The AAV2-nanobody conjugate was systematically optimized by exploring attachment sites, linker length, and stoichiometry of labeling. The optimized conjugate enabled transduction of HER2+ tumor xenografts in mice with remarkable efficiency and specificity, showing little off-target infectivity, including in the liver. The ability to attach any recombinant protein onto the AAV capsid using this method will unlock numerous powerful opportunities to engineer its properties to develop next-generation gene-therapy vectors.

Results

Development of an efficient protein-protein conjugation strategy using IEDDA

We have previously used strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) to attach small-molecule reagents (e.g., peptides and fluorophores) to the AAV2 capsid. However, attaching much larger proteins in this manner poses greater challenges. First, the intrinsic kinetics for protein attachment are much slower compared to small molecules using the same reaction, due to the slower diffusion rates and increased steric bulk of proteins. Second, small molecule reagents can be used at high concentrations (e.g., high μM to mM) to accelerate the reaction and drive it to completion. Achieving such high concentrations with proteins is challenging due to practical reasons such as solubility, aggregation, and accessing sufficient quantities of these bespoke reagents.48 To identify conjugation methods that are better-suited for this purpose, we first attempted to link together two sfGFP molecules as a model system. To this end, we ribosomally incorporated a ncAA containing an azide handle (AzK) to sfGFP at position 151 using the pyrrolysyl-tRNA synthetase (PylRS)/tRNA pair (Figure 1A). The AzK residue was functionalized with different bifunctional linkers for subsequent protein-protein crosslinking. First, we used the DBCO (dibenzyl cyclooctyne)-PEG4-DBCO linker (Figure 1B) to conjugate two proteins using strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC).53 DBCO-PEG4-DBCO was used to label sfGFP-151-AzK to generate 2, which was purified, and incubated with fresh sfGFP-151-AzK in equimolar ratio to generate the target protein-protein conjugate 3 (Figure 1B; Figure S1). Even though the formation of the expected product was observed by SDS-PAGE (Figure 1C), it was slow and remained far from complete even after prolonged incubation, suggesting SPAAC is not well-suited to bring together two macromolecules efficiently.

Inverse electron demand Diels-Alder (IEDDA) reaction has recently emerged as a faster bioorthogonal conjugation,54 which could be better suited for the efficient synthesis of protein-protein conjugates. This strategy has been used to generate simple protein-protein conjugates in recent years.48,54,55,56 To explore this possibility, we functionalized sfGFP-151-AzK separately with two synthetic bifunctional linkers, DBCO-Tz (tetrazine) and DBCO-PEG4-TCO (trans-cyclooctene), to generate conjugates 4 and 5, respectively (Figure 1B). Incubating purified 4 and 5 in equimolar ratio resulted in a remarkably rapid formation of the cross-linked product 6 within minutes in excellent yields (>80%). The formation of 6 was confirmed by both SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure 1D), as well as by whole-protein electrospray-ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) analysis (Figures 1E and S1). The reaction was found to be nearly complete; a small amount of remaining monomeric sfGFP (<20%) was due to the reduction of the azide functionality (Figures 1E and S1).

A facile strategy to generate AAV-protein conjugates

Next, we tested the feasibility of using this strategy to link together AAV and recombinant proteins. Previously, we and others have used the PylRS/tRNA pair to site-specifically incorporate AzK into the AAV capsid,4,22,23,24,42,43,44,45,46,47 and functionalized the resulting azide handle for capsid labeling by SPAAC. Furthermore, we have developed technology for selectively incorporating AzK into minor capsid proteins, such as VP1-only or VP2-only (5 copies per capsid each), or VP1+VP2 (10 copies per capsid).23 The ability to control the site and stoichiometry of capsid modification in this manner helped us demonstrate that the retargeting of AAV2 using a small peptide ligand worked best when approximately 10 ligands are introduced per capsid; higher degrees of capsid modification were detrimental. Guided by these insights, we focused on attaching recombinant proteins only onto the minor capsid protein VP1 of AAV2 (∼5 per capsid) to avoid perturbations from excessive labeling.

We incorporated AzK into the most surface-exposed site 454 of the minor capsid protein VP1, which was previously shown to tolerate the ncAA mutagenesis, yielding virus VP1-454AzK.22,23,57,58,59 To achieve selective incorporation of AzK to VP1, its expression in the overlapping capsid protein gene was uncoupled by deleting its translation start sites. VP1 gene with a TAG codon at site 454 was then resupplied in trans from a separate Cap gene lacking VP2, VP3 expression by deleting their translation start sites (Figure 2A). In addition, we also generated a related AAV2 mutant KO.VP1-454AzK, which cannot bind its native primary receptor heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG), generated by mutating key HSPG-binding residues (R585A and R588A) on all capsid proteins.22,23 Ablating the native receptor recognition was previously shown to reduce off-target transduction and, when combined with appropriate targeting ligands, can facilitate retargeting to a new receptor.22,23

The packaging yields (genomic titer) and infectivity of VP1-454AzK, was comparable to the wild-type (WT) virus (Figures 2B, 2C, and S4). KO.VP1-454AzK yields were also similar to WT-AAV2, but the virus was non-infective, as expected. During the packaging of VP1-454AzK, when AzK was omitted from the medium as a negative control, we still observed robust virus packaging (by titering packaged genomes), but the resulting virus was non-infective (Figures 2C and S4). This is because capsids with only VP2 and VP3 can still form in the absence of VP1 expression, but the resulting capsids are not infective due to the essentiality of VP1 during cellular entry for endosomal escape.

As a proof-of-concept, we first linked together sfGFP to AAV2 through IEDDA. To this end, we functionalized VP1-454AzK and sfGFP-151-AzK with DBCO-Tz and DBCO-PEG4-TCO, respectively (Figure S5A). Incubation of Tz-functionalized AAV with 1.0 μM of TCO-functionalized sfGFP for 4 h resulted in efficient formation of the AAV-sfGFP conjugate through the VP1 capsid protein, as shown by an upward shift of the VP1 band in SDS-PAGE. The workflow described here provides a straightforward method to link recombinant proteins (Figure S5B) onto AAV capsids.

Site-specific AAV2-nanobody conjugates retargeted to the HER2 receptor

This method can be used to attach antibody-like proteins onto the AAV capsid for retargeting it to a desired receptor. To explore this possibility, we first selected the HER2 receptor, which is overexpressed on a variety of cancer cells, and which can be selectively and efficiently targeted using well-established antibodies, nanobodies, etc.60 As a proof-of-concept, we used the HER2-targeting nanobody (Nb; clone 5F7) for attachment onto the AAV capsid.61 AzK was successfully introduced to the permissive 69 site of the Nb, using an orthogonal MmPylRS/tRNA pair (Figure S2).

Next, we aimed to attach Nb-69-AzK to KO.VP1-454AzK, which cannot bind its native primary receptor HSPG, and is an ideal scaffold for retargeting.22,23 KO.VP1-454AzK and 5F7-69-AzK were linked together using our AAV-protein conjugation strategy (Figures 2D and S2). Upon mixing the Tz-functionalized AAV2 and TCO-functionalized nanobody for 4 h, we observed efficient formation of the VP1-nanobody conjugate (KO.VP1-454-Nb) by SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure 2E). The transduction efficiencies of WT-AAV2, KO.VP1-454-AzK, and KO.VP1-454-Nb were assessed using the HER2-overexpressing cell line SK-BR-3 (Figures 2F and S6A), as well as the HEK293T cell line that does not overexpress this receptor. Each virus encoded a WT EGFP reporter gene, driven by a CMV promoter, the expression of which was monitored via flow cytometry.

As expected, KO.VP1-454AzK was non-infectious due to the detargeting mutations introduced at the HSPG-binding site (Figures 2F and 2G). The attachment of nanobody to KO.VP1-454AzK (i.e., KO.VP1-454Nb) was found to dramatically enhance its infectivity toward HER2+ SK-BR-3 cells. KO.VP1-454Nb also showed >8-fold improved transduction efficiency relative to WT-AAV2 (Figures 2F and S6A). By contrast, nanobody attachment onto KO.VP1-454AzK resulted in only a minor increase of its transduction efficiency toward HEK293T cells, which do not overexpress the HER2 receptor; the resulting virus KO.VP1-454Nb was >5-fold less infective relative to WT-AAV2 (Figures 2G and S6B). To further confirm that the enhanced infectivity of KO.VP1-454Nb toward SK-BR-3 cells is mediated by the target receptor binding (HER2), we showed that adding free nanobody inhibits the transduction efficiency of KO.VP1-454Nb in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 2H and S7). These experiments demonstrate that the attachment of antibody fragments provides an effective strategy to rationally reprogram the infectivity of AAV capsids toward new receptors.

Site, stoichiometry, and linker optimization for the AAV2-nanobody conjugate

A potential key strength of our approach is the high degree of control it provides on various aspects of the AAV-protein conjugation process, including the following: (1) the site of attachment both on the virus and the protein; (2) the number of proteins attached per capsid, by controlling the number of ncAAs introduced per capsid; and (3) the length and chemical properties of the linker. Optimization of these parameters can fine-tune the properties of AAV-protein conjugates and further improve efficiency. To test this hypothesis, we first explored the impact of the site of attachment on the performance of the AAV2-nanobody conjugates. In addition to the original 454 site, we also introduced AzK at three additional surface-exposed sites within VP1 (Q263, T456, and R588), to generate KO.VP1-263AzK, KO.VP1-456AzK, and KO.VP1-588AzK (Figure 3A). The anti-HER2 5F7 Nb was successfully conjugated onto each AAV2 mutant (Figure 3B). The transduction efficiency of each AAV2.KO mutant was evaluated toward SK-BR-3 cells before and after nanobody conjugation at a constant multiplicity of infection (MOI). In each case, the KO virus was noninfectious, but nanobody attachment resulted in high levels of infectivity toward SK-BR-3 cells (Figures 3C and S8A), but not toward HEK293T cells (Figures S8B and S8C). The site of nanobody attachment was found to have a significant impact on transduction efficiency, with the KO.VP1-263AzK and KO.VP1-588AzK showing the lowest and highest activity, respectively.

We also investigated the impact of the linker length between the AAV2 and Nb on retargeting efficiency. A combination of bifunctional synthetic linkers with different polyethylene glycol lengths were tested (Figures 1B and 3D show linker structures): AAV mutant KO.VP1-588AzK, which enabled the most efficient retargeting, was labeled with either DBCO-Tz or DBCO-PEG12-Tz, while the Nb-69-AzK was labeled with either DBCO-PEG4-TCO or DBCO-PEG12-TCO (Figures 3D and S2). By linking the resulting Tz-functionalized AAV and TCO-functionalized 5F7 samples in different combinations, we generated a series of four AAV-nanobody conjugates with increasing linker length. All of these conjugates exhibited robust transduction efficiency toward SK-BR-3 cells (Figures 3E and S9). The linker-length had a modest but significant impact, with the longest linker showing ∼50% higher transduction efficiency relative to the shortest linker. Together, site- and linker-optimization led to a significant improvement in retargeting efficiency relative to our starting point.

So far, we have generated the AAV-protein conjugates by introducing the ncAA handle only in VP1, which is present in approximately 5 copies per capsid. We sought to probe how the attachment of a higher number of nanobodies on the virus capsid would affect the retargeting efficiency. To this end, we incorporated AzK at all 60 copies of the AAV.KO capsid (Figure 3F). When we subjected the resulting virus to the conjugation protocol established previously, successful nanobody conjugation to all three capsid proteins was observed by SDS-PAGE (Figure 3G). However, the conversion was incomplete (∼50%), likely due to excessive crowding on the capsid upon attaching a large number of proteins. Upon infecting SK-BR-3 cells, we observed a drastic decrease in the infectivity of this virus relative to KO.VP1-588Nb (Figures 3H and S10), highlighting the detrimental impact of over-modifying the capsid and the importance of controlled labeling. Similarly, simultaneous modification of VP1 and VP2 with nanobody was also associated with low infectivity.

Genetic approaches have also been used to display small antibody-like proteins on the AAV capsid,4 via N-terminal fusion with the minor capsid protein VP2, or insertion into a permissive loop on a minor capsid protein. We compared the activity of our optimized AAV2-Nb conjugate with such reported genetic fusion constructs using the same nanobody.31,32,33,34,35,36,37 Following the pioneering work by Koch-Nolte31 and Buchholz et al.,34 we inserted the 5F7 nanobody at the loop of the variable region IV of VP1, or fused it to the-N terminus of VP2 to generate constructs KO.VP1-Loop-Nb and KO.VP2-N-term-Nb, respectively (Figure 3I). Successful fusion/insertion of nanobody to the target minor capsid proteins was confirmed by SDS-PAGE (Figure 3J). While both fusion constructs exhibited successful retargeting to SK-BR-3 cells, our optimized AAV2-nanobody conjugate KO.VP1-588Nb significantly outperformed both of them, showing approximately 4- and 15-fold higher infectivity relative to KO.VP1-Loop-Nb and KO.VP2-N-term-Nb, respectively (Figures 3K and S11).

Synthesis of a full-length antibody-AAV conjugate

While some small and well-folded proteins such as nanobodies can also be displayed on the AAV capsid through genetic fusion, larger and more complex (e.g., multi-subunit) proteins are refractory to such strategies. By contrast, our method of generating protein-AAV conjugates can be theoretically extended to any recombinant protein that can be site-specifically chemically modified, either through ncAA mutagenesis or alternative strategies. To show that our approach can be used to generate functional conjugates between AAV and large and multi-subunit proteins, we aimed to attach the full-length anti-HER2 antibody trastuzumab onto the AAV capsid. Given the broad and rich toolbox of antibodies available against countless targets, the ability to generate precise AAV-antibody conjugates is expected to be valuable for numerous applications. To this end, we first expressed full-length trastuzumab incorporating AzK at site 122 in the light chain (LC) using the Expi293 expression system. Incorporation of AzK into the resulting antibody was confirmed by MS analysis (Figure S3). Then, to generate the AAV2-trastuzumab conjugate through IEDDA, KO.VP1-588AzK was labeled using DBCO-PEG12-Tz, and trastuzumab-LC-122-AzK was labeled with DBCO-PEG12-TCO (Figures 4A and S3). Incubating Tz-labeled AAV2 and TCO-labeled trastuzumab at room temperature for 4 h resulted in efficient conjugate formation (Figure 4B). Attachment of trastuzumab onto non-infective KO.VP1-588AzK resulted in significantly higher infectivity relative to WT-AAV2 toward HER2+ SK-BR-3 cells (Figures 4C and S12A), but not HEK293T cells (Figures S12B and S12C), confirming successful retargeting. The ability to attach a full-length antibody—a 150 kDa protein with four subunits—onto the AAV2 capsid showcases the generality of our approach, which can be further exploited to generate a diverse class of AAV-protein conjugates.

Efficient and selective gene delivery by AAV2-nanobody conjugates in vivo

In AAV gene therapy, a key goal is to create capsids that selectively deliver genetic cargo to specific tissues upon systemic administration. However, existing AAV vectors exhibit off-target infectivity, especially toward the liver. Encouraged by the efficient and selective gene delivery exhibited by our optimized AAV2-5F7 conjugates toward HER2+ cancer cells in vitro, we sought to characterize its efficacy in vivo in a mouse model. To this end, we first characterized the transduction efficiency of KO.VP1-588Nb toward BT474 cells, another HER2-overexpressing cancer cell line that enables more facile xenograft formation. Evaluating the transduction efficiencies toward BT474 cells revealed the same infectivity pattern observed for SK-BR-3: KO.VP1-588AzK was non-infectious but attachment of 5F7 to generate KO.VP1-588Nb resulted in robust transduction of these cells, at a significantly higher level relative to WT-AAV2 (Figures 5A and S13).

Next, three groups of mice (n = 8) were injected with 5.00 × 106 BT474 cells/mouse for xenograft formation, and tumor volume was allowed to expand for approximately 3 weeks. When the tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, identical doses (1010 genome copies per animal) of WT, KO.VP1-588AzK, and KO.VP1-588-Nb viruses encoding a luciferase reporter gene were delivered to each group of mice by tail-vein injection. Whole-body luminescence imaging of the animals was performed on a weekly basis starting from the 2nd week (Figures 5C and S14). After 5 weeks, the animals were sacrificed and tumors and livers were harvested for transgene titering. As expected, luminescence imaging of mice infected with WT-AAV2 on the 2nd week revealed low levels of luminescence originating largely from the liver (Figures 5C and 5D). By contrast, KO.VP1-588-Nb exhibited a massively higher levels of luminescence (>85-fold), and the signal originated selectively from the tumor. Not surprisingly, as the tumor rapidly expanded in the subsequent weeks, the luminescence levels from tumor reduced due to the dilution of the delivered transgene (Figure S14). Nonetheless, qPCR analysis of the tumor samples harvested after 5 weeks still showed >19-fold more efficient transgene delivery into the tumor by KO.VP1-588-Nb relative to WT-AAV2 (Figure 5F), further corroborating the imaging experiments. By contrast, qPCR analysis showed severely attenuated (>45-fold lower) transgene delivery into the liver by KO.VP1-588Nb relative to WT-AAV2 (Figure 5E). These results show that the chemical conjugation of antibody fragments can remarkably improve both the efficiency and selectivity of AAV-mediated gene delivery in vivo. While KO.VP1-588-AzK exhibits limited infectivity in vitro in cell culture models, consistent with prior findings, it does demonstrate some infectivity in vivo.62

Discussion

The ability to display proteins such as antibody fragments onto the AAV capsid has been explored as an attractive avenue to rationally engineer the properties of this promising gene therapy vector. However, due to the delicate nature of the intricate AAV capsid, traditional genetic approaches have been severe restricted in their scope; such approaches are only compatible with small and well-folded proteins, and the number of different ways proteins can be grafted onto the capsid proteins remains severely restricted.

The chemical strategy we report here enables the synthesis of AAV-protein conjugates with a much higher degree of versatility. The ncAA AzK can be introduced into a variety of different sites within the capsid protein with high efficiency, enabling one to systematically explore how different attachment sites impact the property of the resulting conjugate. By introducing the ncAA into different groups of capsid proteins, it is also possible to control the number of attachment sites on the capsid. We show that both factors significantly influence the retargeting efficiency of the conjugate.

It has been possible to genetically encode ncAAs that contain tetrazine and TCO functionalities,63,64,65 which can be used to directly introduce these groups on AAV. However, our two-step approach, involving incorporation of AzK followed by the attachment of TCO or tetrazine, offers a few advantages as follows. (1) AzK is well-tolerated at numerous positions on the AAV capsid,4,22,23 whereas incorporation of other bulkier ncAAs in this context has been more challenging. (2) The use of a bifunctional linker provides additional opportunity to fine-tune the properties of the conjugate by manipulating the length and the chemical features of the linker.

Here, we simply optimized the length of the PEG linker, and showed that the longer linkers provide better function. However, the physical/chemical properties of the linker can also be systematically optimized in the future; similar efforts in the development of antibody-drug conjugates have revealed that the linker properties can significantly affect their therapeutic efficacy.

Overall, optimization of site and stoichiometry of attachment, as well as the linker length, resulted in a highly active AAV2-5F7 nanobody conjugate that significantly outperforms its counterparts produced using established genetic fusion methods. In mouse models, the optimized AAV2-5F7 nanobody conjugate facilitated remarkably efficient transgene delivery into a HER2+ tumor xenograft, while exhibiting attenuated off-target delivery to liver.

We used this strategy to efficiently attach three different recombinant proteins, sfGFP, 5F7 nanobody, and trastuzumab (a full-length antibody) onto the minor capsid protein VP1 of AAV2, underscoring its compatibility with diverse proteins. It is notable that complex proteins such as full-length antibodies are not compatible with capsid protein fusion. The ability to seamlessly attach recombinant proteins onto the AAV capsid in this manner also unlocks a novel approach for AAV engineering.

Conclusion

In summary, we show that site-specific chemical attachment of recombinant proteins onto the AAV capsid, using a combination of GCE and ultra-fast bioorthogonal conjugation chemistry, is a versatile technology that could be used to engineer retargeted vectors. Given the art of developing antibodies against desired cell-surface receptors is well established, this provides a highly modular and rational avenue to generate efficient and selective gene therapy vectors. Finally, attachment of recombinant proteins onto the AAV capsid can also enable rational engineering of other therapeutically relevant properties such as immunogenicity and pharmacokinetics.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HEK293T cells, Expi293 were obtained previously.22,66 SK-BR-3 cells were a gift from Prof. Eranthie Weerapana, Boston College Chemistry, and BT-474 cells was a gift from Charles River Laboratory. All cell lines were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 in DMEM-high glucose (HyClone) supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, final concentration of 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning). Expi293 cells were cultured at 37°C, 8% CO2, in Expi293 Expression Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 0.5× antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in shaken ventilated Erlenmeyer flasks at 125 rpm (revolutions per minute).

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed on at least 3 biological replicates. For the animal experiments, each vector was tested on eight animals in parallel to have sufficient confidence. The bar graphs show average of all measurements, and also display individual data points associated with each average. The error bars show the associated standard deviation.

Cloning and plasmids

Transformation was done in Top10 cells using BioRad electroporator. DNA oligo synthesis and Sanger sequencing were performed by Genewiz. Phusion polymerase was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase was purchased from Takara, and T4 DNA Ligase was purchased from QIAGEN.

pET22b-T5-sfGFP-151TAG for expressing sfGFP-151-AzK was obtained previously.67 pET22b-T5-5F7-69TAG for expressing 5F7 Nb-69-AzK was generated by QuickChange site-directed mutagenesis from pET22b-T5-5F7-69TGA66 using 5F7-69TAG-F and 5F7-69TAG-R primers. pcDNA3.1-Her2-Ab-LC-122TAG for expressing Anti-HER2-Ab-LC-122-AzK was generated by overlap extension PCR on pcDNA3.1-Her2-Ab66 using pcDNA-LC-SpeI-F, HerAb-LC-122TAG-R, HerAb-LC-122TAG-F, and pcDNA-LC-SbfI-R primers. The PCR product was digested and cloned into pcDNA3.1-Her2-Ab using SpeI and SbfI restriction sites.

pIDTSmart-MbPylRS-4xPyOtR-ITR-GFP encoding the EGFP transgene and PylRS/tRNA pair for AzK incorporation was generated from the original plasmid pIDTsmart-MbPylRS.22 4xPyOtR cassette was amplified by PrimeSTAR polymerase on pIDTsmart-4xPyOtR58 using NheI-HTS25-F and SbfI-HTS25-R primers. The PCR product was digested with restriction enzymes NheI and SbfI and cloned into pIDTsmart-MbPylRS using SpeI and SbfI restriction sites, yielding the intermediate plasmid pIDTSmart-MbPylRS-4xPyOtR. Next, ITR-EFGP was digested from the plasmid pIDTSmart-8xPyltR-ITR-GFP22 with restriction enzymes SbfI, and cloned into pIDTSmart-MbPylRS-4xPyOtR using SbfI restriction site. ITR-FLUC was amplified by PrimeStar polymerase from pAAV.CMV.ffLuciferase.SV40 (Addgene) using FLUC-cargo-SbfI-F and FLUC-cargo-SbfI-R primers. The PCR product was digested and cloned into pIDTSmart-MbPylRS-4xPyOtR-ITR-GFP using SbfI restriction site, yielding pIDTSmart-MbPylRS-4xPyOtR-ITR-FLUC.

pHelper encoding adenovirus helper genes, pIDTSmart-RC2-WT, pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP1-CMV-VP1, pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP12-CMV-VP12 encoding AAV2 Rep and Cap genes were obtained previously.23 To detarget AAV from heparan sulfate, two mutations R585A and R588A were made by QuickChange site-directed mutagenesis from pIDTSmart-RC2-WT using 585A/588A-F, 585A/588A-R primers, yielding pIDTsmart-RC2.KO. The resulting plasmid was used as a template to clone with R585A and R588A mutations at all VP1,2,3 as previously described.5 For the incorporation of ncAA into different sites of VP1, overlap-extension PCR on pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP1-CMV-VP1 was done to introduce TAG codon to CMV-VP1 gene using SbfI-CMV-F, Cap2-Bsu36I-R, and suitable overlap primers. The PCR products were digested and cloned into pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP1-CMV-VP1 using NheI and Bsu36I restriction sites. For the incorporation of ncAA into different sites of VP1+VP2, overlap-extension PCR on pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP2-CMV-VP2 was done to introduce TAG codon to CMV-VP2 gene using SbfI-CMV-F, Cap2-Mlul-R, and suitable overlap primers.

pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP1-CMV-VP1-loop-Nb encoding AAV2 Rep and Cap genes with Nb loop insertion at VP1 was generated by 2 steps overlap-extension PCR, and cloned into pIDTSmart-RC2.KO-ΔVP1-CMV-VP1 using SbfI and Bsu36I restriction sites. The first overlap-extension PCR was done to fuse CMV-N terminus-VP1 (codons 1–452) with anti-Her2 Nanobody (clone 5F7) gene, flanked by 5xGGGGS flexible linker using CMV-SbfI-F, VP1half-GS-R, GS-5F7-F, 5F7-GA-Bsu-R primers. The second overlap-extension PCR was done to fuse CMV-N terminus-VP1-Nb with C terminus-VP1 genes (codons 450–735) using CMV-SbfI-F, VP1-2nd half-F, 5F7-overlapVP-R, Cap2-Bsu36I-R primers. pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP2-CMV-5F7-VP2 encoding AAV2 Rep and Cap genes with Nb N terminus fusion at VP2 was generated by overlap-extension PCR to fuse anti-Her2 Nanobody to N terminus of VP2, flanked by 1xGGGGS linker, using NheI-5F7-F, 5F7-VP2 overlap-F, 5F7-VP2 overlap-R, Cap2-Bsu36I-R primers. The PCR product was digested and cloned into pIDTSmart-RC2-ΔVP2-CMV-VP2 using NheI and Bsu36I restriction sites.

Production of ncAA-containing AAV

AAV2 was produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with 3 plasmids: pHelper, pIDTsmart-MbPylRS-4xPyOtR-ITR-GFP, and a plasmid containing suitable AAV Rep-Cap genes in 1:1:1 molar ratio.23 Specifically, pIDTsmart-RC2-WT, pIDTsmart-RC2-ΔVP1-CMV-VP1-TAG, pIDTsmart-RC2-ΔVP12-CMV-VP12-TAG, and pIDTsmart-RC2-TAG were used to generate AAV2-WT, AAV2-AzK, AAV2-VP1-AzK, and AAV2-VP1,2-AzK, respectively. Total DNAs (57 μg) of 3 plasmids (pHelper, pIDTsmart-MbPylRS-4xPyOtR-ITR-GFP, and a suitable Re-Cap gene) were used for a 15 cm dish of HEK293T cells and viruses were harvested 120 h post-transfection. The DNAs were premixed in serum free media (DMEM) and PEI (Sigma) was added (3.5 μL per μg DNA) so that the final concentration of DNA is approximately 50 ng per μL media. The mixture was allowed to sit at room temperature (RT) for 15 min before adding dropwise to the cells, followed by the addition of AzK (Iris Biotech) to the final concentration of 1 mM. Virus was extracted from cells by 2 cycles of snap freeze-thaw in dry ice-ethanol and 37°C water bath. Cell lysate was combined with media and was mixed with 30% volume of 40% PEG 8,000 (Fisher BioReagents) to precipitate the virus overnight at 4°C. On the next day, virus was spun down at 5,000 × g for 30 min, then resuspended in 2 mL of salty PBS (300 mM NaCl), supplemented with 2 μL universal nuclease and nutated at RT for 15 min. During that time, 250 μL slurry of AVB-agarose was packed in a column and equilibrated with 10× column volume of PBS. Virus was loaded to the column and flowed down by gravity and then reloaded twice. The column was washed with 30× column volume of DPBS (Dulbecco's Phosphate Buffered Saline). Virus was quickly eluted by 2 mL 0.1 M glycine pH 2.8 and neutralized with 200 μL 1M Tris pH 8. Virus was concentrated and buffer-exchanged to AAV buffer (1× DPBS, 300 μM NaCl, and 0.001% Pluronic F68) using an Amicon 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off centrifugal filter. Glycerol was added to the final concentration of 10% and virus was titered by AAVpro Titration Kit Ver.2 (Takara), followed the manufacturer’s instructions. For animal studies, the viruses were sterile filtered by passing through a 0.2 μm, 4 mm filter. The content of endotoxin in the virus samples were assayed and found to be < 0.900 endotoxin unit (EU)/mL using the ToxinSensorTM Chromogenic LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit (Genscript).

Protein expression

sfGFP-151-AzK,67 AntiHer2-Nanobody (Clone 5F7)-69-AzK66 were expressed in BL21(DE3) cells and were purified by Ni-NTA resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed the previously described methods. Full-length trastuzumab-LC-122-AzK was expressed in Expi293 cells, expression was purified by Pierce Protein G Agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed the previously described methods.68

For animal studies, AntiHer2-Nanobody was sterile filtered by passing through a 0.2 μm, 4 mm filter. Endotoxin was removed from AntiHer2-Nanobody sample by Pierce High Capacity Endotoxin Removal Spin Columns, capacity = 0.5 mL (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed the manufacturer’s instructions.

Protein-protein conjugation

GFP coupling: 100 μM sfGFP-151AzK and 50 mM DBCO-PEG4-DBCO (Broadpharm) or DBCO-sulfo-Tz (Broadpharm) or DBCO-PEG4-TCO (Broadpharm) were diluted in 1% DMSO in DPBS (pH 7.4) to a final concentration of 10 μM and 100 μM, respectively. Conjugations were done in 50 μL scale in 0.5 mL tubes by nutating the mixtures at 22°C for 12 h. The modified proteins were buffer exchanged in DPBS using Amicon 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off centrifugal filters device to remove excess DBCO probes. sfGFP containing DBCO and sfGFP-151-AzK concentration were adjusted to 10 μM and mixed together in 1:1 molar ratio. GFP coupling was done by nutating the mixture at RT for 16 h sfGFP containing TCO and Tz concentration were adjusted to 10 μM and mixed together in 1:1 molar ratio. GFP coupling was done by nutating the mixture at RT and quenched by the addition of 50-fold excess DBCO-sulfo-Tz.

Nanobody-virus conjugation

AntiHer2-Nb-69-AzK and 50 mM DBCO-PEG4-TCO or DBCO-PEG12-TCO (Broadpharm) (100 μM) were diluted in 1% DMSO in DPBS (pH 7.4) to final concentration of 10 μM and 100 μM, respectively. Conjugations were done in 200 μL scale in 0.5 mL tubes by nutating the mixtures at 22°C for 16 h. The modified proteins were buffer exchanged in DPBS using ultracentrifugal device 0.5 mL 10 kDa to remove excess DBCO probes. DBCO-sulfo-Tz or DBCO-PEG12-Tz (Broadpharm) (50 mM) were diluted in AzK-containing virus solutions to a final concentration of 100 μM. Reactions were done in 1.5 mL tubes by nutating the mixtures at 22°C for 16 h, before complete buffer exchange into AAV buffer by ultracentrifugal device Amicon 100 kDa to remove excess DBCO probes. Virus containing Tz was re-titered and nanobody containing TCO was added to the final concentration of 1 μM. Conjugation was done in 1.5 mL tubes by nutating the mixtures at 22°C for 4 h.

Full-length antibody-virus conjugation

Trastuzumab-LC-122-AzK and 50mM DBCO-PEG12-TCO (5 mg/mL) were diluted in 1% DMSO in DPBS (pH 7.4) to final concentration of 1 mg/mL and 100 μM, respectively. Conjugations were done in 200 μL scale in 0.5 mL tubes by nutating the mixtures at 22°C for 16 h. The modified proteins were buffer exchanged in DPBS using ultracentrifugal device 0.5 mL 50 kDa to remove excess DBCO probes. DBCO-PEG12-Tz (50 mM) was diluted in AzK-containing virus solutions to a final concentration of 100 μM. Reactions were done in 0.5 mL tubes by nutating the mixtures at 22°C for 16 h, before complete buffer exchange into AAV buffer by ultracentrifugal device Amicon 100 kDa to remove excess DBCO probes. Virus containing Tz was retitered and antibody containing TCO was added to the final concentration of 0.5 μM. Conjugation was done in 0.5 mL tubes by nutating the mixtures at 22°C for 4 h.

LC-MS analysis

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was done using Agilent Technologies, 1260 Infinity ESI-TOF, Phenomenex, Aeris 3.6 μm WIDEPORE XB-C8 column, LC Column 100 × 4.6 mm. Separation was performed with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min, and with mobile phase of solvent A (water-MeCN-0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in 95:5:0.1 ratio) and solvent B (MeCN-water-0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in 95: 5: 0.1 ratio); 0–1 min 5% B, 1–8 min 99% B, 8–9 min 99% B to 1% B, 9–10 min 1% B to 5% B, 10–14 min 5% B. Deconvolution was done using Magtran software, and peak intensities for AzK containing proteins and their corresponding linker conjugated products were compared.

For the LC-MS analysis of trastuzumab-LC-122-AzK and DBCO-PEG12-TCO adduct, the antibodies were reduced with 5 mM DTT at 55°C for 10 min. The reduced antibodies (25 μL, 1 mg/mL) were treated with 1 μL Remove-iT PNGase F (NEB) for 2 h at 37°C to remove the antibody glycosylation. Due to the instability of the TCO during antibody treatment for mass spectrometry, trastuzumab-LC-122-AzK and DBCO-PEG12-TCO adduct was reacted 200 μM tetrazine-aniline for 10 min at RT prior to LC-MS analysis.

SDS-PAGE

Approximately 5 μg of sfGFP and 1010 genome copies of viruses were loaded per lane. The samples were heated in SDS-loading buffer containing 100 mM DTT for 1 min under reducing condition, then analyzed with 10% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) containing 0.1% (w/v) SDS. The SDS-PAGE was performed at 120 V for approximately 2 h. sfGFP were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue and imaged on the ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad) under Coomassie blue setting. Viruses and attached proteins were stained with SYPRO Orange Protein Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed the manufacturer’s instructions, and imaged with Dylight 540.

Assaying the infectivity of AAV2 in vitro and flow cytometry

Infectivity in HEK293T cells was assayed as previously described.22 For SKBR3 cells, 2.5 × 106 cells (counted by Bio-Rad TC20 Automated Cell Counter) were seeded per 24-well plate 30 h prior to infection. For BT474 cells, 5.0 × 106 cells were seeded per 24-well plate 24 h prior to infection. When cells had reached the desired confluency, viruses were added to each well in a constant MOI, along with 5 mM sodium butyrate (Sigma-Aldrich) to enhance the expression of AAV2-encoded transgenes. 48 h post-infection, media was removed and 200 μL RT DPBS was added per well. Infectivity was visualized by EGFP expression using a Zeiss Axio Observer fluorescence microscope with an XCite Series 120Q light source and Zeiss filter 44 (excitation 475/40 nm, beamsplitter 500 nm, emission 530/50 nm). Cells were harvested by 5 min incubation in 100 μL warm 0.25% trypsin-EDTA at 37°C, followed by quenching with 200 μL of cold DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were resuspended and pelleted in 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes by centrifugation at 2,500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded and cells were gently resuspended in 200 μL of cold PBS and passed through a 35 μm strainer cap of a 5 mL tube (Falcon). Flow cytometry was performed on a BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) using the fluorescein isothiocyanateand PerCP settings (excitation wavelength: 488 nm, standard filter: 533/30, 670LP, respectively). Data were processed using FlowJo, version 10.8.1.

Animal studies, in vivo imaging, and transgene titering

Animals were prepared and in vivo imaging was performed by Charles River Laboratories (Worcester, MA). Forty female mice (8 weeks old) were xenografted with 5.0 × 106 BT-474 cells. Tumors were allowed to expand and were measured with handheld digital calipers connected to a laptop computer. Tumor volume is calculated using a (L × W2)/2 formula. By default, the width is considered the shorter of the two measurements. Approximately 3 weeks after tumor expansion, 8 mice were randomly pooled such that the average tumor per group volume is 100 mm3; 1.0 × 1011 gc each of WT, KO.VP1-588-AzK, and KO. VP1-588-Nb encoding the firefly luciferase reporter was used to infect the animals by tail-vein injection. Starting on day 14 post-injection, luminescence was measured weekly using the IVIS Lumina III imaging system. Animals were sacrificed at day 35 and tumors, livers were collected and stored in RNA later. The tissue samples were ground and homogenized in a BeadbugTM3 microtube homogenizer, 115V (Benchmark Scientific) using prefilled 2.0 mL tubes with impact zirconium Beads (Benchmark Scientific). Total DNA was isolated from about 25 mg tissue sample using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNAs were ethanol precipitated for an hour and resuspended in milliQ water. AAV transgene was titered by AAVpro Titration Kit Ver.2 (Takara), following the manufacturer’s instructions.
