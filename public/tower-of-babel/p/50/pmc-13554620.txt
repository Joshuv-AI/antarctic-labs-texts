Machine learning model MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN in image segmentation for the detection of the long head of biceps in arthroscopy images
Authors: Norman Schumann, Matthias Königshausen, Ole Somberg, Thomas Rosteius, Thomas A Schildhauer, Maria A Bernstorff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSurgical decision-making during arthroscopic procedures often relies on the subjective visual assessment of tissue morphology, particularly the redness of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHB), which serves as an indicator for potential surgical intervention. This subjectivity introduces variability across clinicians, underscoring the need for objective, data-driven methods. The present study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of applying the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN deep learning model for automated segmentation of the LHB in arthroscopic images, thereby converting subjective visual assessments into quantifiable, reproducible measurements.MethodsThis original research article is a retrospective cohort analysis of arthroscopic images obtained from 124 participants (84 male, 40 female) who underwent arthroscopic procedures between March 2015 and May 2019 at a single institution. From a comprehensive database of 130,000 arthroscopic images, 15,000 images featuring the LHB were identified, and 200 were selected for manual annotation by an experienced physician. The MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model, combining a ResNet50 backbone with a Feature Pyramid Network and a mask prediction branch, was trained for 10,000 iterations using the AdamW optimizer (learning rate 1×10-5) with transfer learning from COCO-pretrained weights. Model performance was assessed through expert evaluation on 400 randomly selected validation images and through automated pixel-level evaluation computing Precision, Recall, F1-score (Dice coefficient), and Intersection over Union (IoU). A baseline comparison with a U-Net (ResNet34 encoder) and three classical segmentation methods was performed. The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE guidelines.ResultsExpert evaluation of 400 validation images yielded a precision of 0.88. The automated pixel-level evaluation on the same 400 images yielded a mean precision of 0.83, recall of 0.86, F1-score of 0.83, and IoU of 0.76 (median IoU: 0.90). The model demonstrated consistent performance across varying tendon orientations and background conditions, and effectively assigned low relevance scores (0.1–0.3) to imaging artifacts. In the baseline comparison, a U-Net achieved a higher mean IoU of 0.91 (Wilcoxon p < 0.001). The combined training loss converged to 0.0809 after 10,000 iterations.ConclusionsThe MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model may serve as a tool for objective and reproducible segmentation of the LHB in arthroscopic images, with performance corroborated by both expert assessment and automated pixel-level evaluation. These findings suggest that deep learning-based image segmentation has the potential to reduce subjectivity in surgical decision-making. Limitations include the single-center design, limited demographic diversity, and the relatively small training dataset. Multi-center validation with a more diverse patient cohort is recommended.Keywords: image segmentation, deep learning, mask R-CNN, long head of biceps tendon, arthroscopy, surgical decision-making, convolutional neural network

Introduction

In modern medicine, surgical decision-making often relies on the interpretation of imaging data, especially when evaluating morphological changes in tissues and organs. These alterations are usually detected through endoscopic, arthroscopic, and laparoscopic procedures, where the surgeon visually assesses the condition of internal structures. In orthopedic surgery, specifically during arthroscopic procedures, one such example is the redness of the long biceps tendon, a common indicator for potential surgical intervention. However, the subjective nature of human perception contributes significantly to these assessments, leading to variability in the decision-making process. As medicine continues to progress, an increasing need to establish more objective criteria to guide surgical decisions is apparent – this minimizes the dependence on the personal judgment of individual clinicians.1–7

One promising solution to this problem lies in the application of advanced imaging techniques, which have become an indispensable tool in contemporary medicine. Imaging modalities such as MRI, CT scans, and ultrasound have long been utilized to visualize internal structures, but these technologies are now being complemented by more sophisticated methods of image analysis, including computer vision and machine learning algorithms, implemented with tools like Python 3.11 and PyTorch. Specifically, image segmentation, a technique in computer vision that divides an image into multiple non-overlapping regions, each corresponding to a distinct object or area, holds the potential to revolutionize medical diagnostics. By applying image segmentation techniques to surgical images, it is feasible to delineate areas of interest, like the long biceps tendon, with a high degree of precision. This facilitates the quantification of morphological changes, such as redness, in a manner that is both objective and reproducible. 8

In this context, it is pertinent to delineate the hierarchy of the underlying computational disciplines. Artificial Intelligence (AI) constitutes the overarching field concerned with enabling machines to execute tasks that traditionally require human cognition. Machine Learning (ML), a subset of AI, employs statistical methods to identify patterns in data without explicit task-specific programming. Deep Learning (DL), a specialized branch of ML, leverages multi-layered neural networks to extract hierarchical feature representations directly from raw data. CNNs such as ResNet50 belong to this category and have demonstrated particular efficacy in medical image analysis. The growing impact of these approaches across surgical disciplines is evidenced by recent meta-analyses in neurosurgery, reporting strong predictive performance in survival forecasting, 9 tumor recurrence detection, 10 and ischemia prediction. 11

The need for objectivity in medical imaging is paramount, particularly with regard to surgical indications. Surgeons often need to make quick decisions during procedures, and having access to objective data can significantly enhance the precision and dependability of these decisions. This is particularly true in the case of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHB) where inflammation of the tendon and surrounding rotator cuff indicates clinically symptomatic LHB tendinitis, commonly presenting as anterior shoulder pain. However, solely relying on preoperative imaging might not be sufficient to determine the significance of LHB tendinitis. The interpretation of inflammation, a subjective characteristic, can vary due to factors like lighting conditions, the surgeon’s experience, and even the endoscope’s angle. By developing image segmentation techniques that can accurately quantify the inflammation’s degree, we can establish clear, objective thresholds indicating when a surgical intervention is required. This diminishes the variability in surgical decision-making and ensures that patients receive the best possible care based on consistent, data-driven criteria. 12

From a technical standpoint, developing image segmentation algorithms necessitates a comprehensive understanding of both computer vision and medical imaging. Traditional segmentation methods usually depend on manual delineation by trained clinicians, a process that is not only time-consuming but also prone to human error. Nonetheless, recent advancements in machine learning, especially deep learning techniques like convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have allowed the automation of the segmentation process with extraordinary accuracy. These algorithms can be trained on extensive datasets of medical images, thereby learning to identify subtle patterns and features not immediately visible to the human eye. In the context of the long biceps tendon, such algorithms could be trained to detect various degrees of redness and associate these with clinical outcomes. This provides surgeons with a valuable tool for making more knowledgeable decisions. 13

Furthermore, the utilization of these advanced imaging techniques is not confined to the long biceps tendon. Comparable methods can be implemented across a broad array of medical conditions, where alterations in tissue morphology serve as indicators of disease or injury. For instance, in the radiology sector, specifically in oncology, image segmentation facilitates tumor identification and the monitoring of their size and growth over time (e.g., 14). Artificial intelligence (AI) has been progressively amplifying tumor detection in radiological images. In cardiology, this methodology proves useful for analysis of heart structure and function. By expanding these techniques to other areas like orthopedic surgery and incorporating them into educational tools for medical students and resident doctors, we can aim to construct a more cohesive and objective framework for medical diagnostics and education. 8

This study aims to explore the potential of medical image segmentation in addressing the subjectivity inherent in surgical decision-making, particularly in relation to the long biceps tendon. We aim to analyze surgical images, correlating the degree of redness with clinical observations to determine if it is possible to establish objective criteria for surgical intervention. Rather than focusing on the medical implications of tendon redness, this research emphasizes the technical and data-driven aspects of image segmentation as a tool to enhance surgical decision-making. Utilizing this approach, we hope to answer a crucial question: Can image segmentation techniques provide the objectivity required for more consistent and reliable surgical decisions?

Our discussion is based on the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN architecture, 15 a deep learning model used for instance segmentation tasks. This model combines two widely used architectures, ResNet50 and Feature Pyramid Network (FPN), with an additional mask prediction branch.

ResNet50 is a CNN typically employed in image classification tasks. With its 50 layers and residual connections, this architecture manages to surmount the vanishing gradient problem, enabling it to learn more profound and intricate image representations (see 16).

FPN is a feature extraction technique that merges attributes from various layers of a CNN to enhance object detection and segmentation performance. Utilizing a top-down approach, it integrates high-level semantic features with low-level spatial features (see 17).

MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN exploits the advantages of ResNet50 and FPN for instance segmentation, a task that involves identifying and segmenting individual objects within an image. Unlike conventional object recognition models, this architecture incorporates a mask prediction branch to predict object masks pixel-by-pixel, in addition to the classification and bounding box prediction branches. 18

This study aimed to apply image segmentation and machine learning techniques, as exemplified by the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model, to increase objectivity in medical imaging during arthroscopic surgery, particularly regarding the shoulder. By utilizing these sophisticated methods, traditional subjective decision-making processes of surgeons - like assessing the intensity of redness of the long biceps tendon - can be converted into a data-driven approach grounded on quantifiable parameters. This permits more uniform and reproducible evaluations, offering a standardized model for clinical decision-making.

Methods

Before this cross -sectional study began, we obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Ruhr University of Bochum (registration number: 16-5859, October 2016) This ensured compliance with guidelines on managing sensitive personal data. After a comprehensive pre-review process, the project received a positive ethical vote. The images used for segmentation were gathered during arthroscopic procedures conducted between March 2015 and May 2019. The study included a total of 124 participants (m = 84, f = 40).

The training dataset was prepared from a comprehensive database of 130,000 arthroscopic images, as thoroughly reviewed by a medical expert. From this selection, 15,000 images explicitly featuring the long biceps tendon were extracted. Subsequently, a random assortment of 200 images was designated for model training. These images underwent manual annotation by a seasoned physician, who meticulously outlined the region of the long biceps tendon present in each image. This painstaking annotation served as reliable ground truth data for the segmentation model, ultimately equipping the algorithm to independently identify and segment the tendon in unseen images.

In addition to the expert-based assessment, an automated pixel-level evaluation was performed on the same 400 validation images, computing Precision, Recall, F1-score (Dice coefficient), and Intersection over Union (IoU) by comparing predicted masks against ground-truth annotations at full resolution (1920×1080 pixels).

This is a retrospective cohort analysis. Due to the retrospective study design, it was not necessary to inform and obtain written informed consent from the patients.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024. All patient data was de-identified prior to analysis; arthroscopic images are anonymous. The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE guidelines. 19 Patients were selected based on the availability of arthroscopic procedures in which the long biceps tendon was visualized, representing a consecutive case series from a single institution.

The image segmentation was performed using the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model. This architecture consists of two primary components (Figure 1).

In addition to classifying and locating objects, the MASKRCNN architecture integrates a mask prediction branch, enabling sophisticated pixel-level object masking. In this study, our focus is the long biceps tendon, rendered with fine detail by the mask prediction function.

The model was trained for 10,000 iterations using the AdamW optimizer, a variant of Adam that includes weight decay to help prevent overfitting by penalizing large weights. The initial learning rate was set to 1e-5, a common choice for fine-tuning pre-trained models on new tasks. During training, the model processed each input image and produced four outputs:

By the end of the 10,000 training iterations, the combined loss value, encompassing classification, bounding box, and mask loss, was 0.0808, indicating good performance on the training dataset.

After the training phase, we validated the model to confirm its ability to accurately segment the long biceps tendon in new, previously unseen images. The performance metrics, such as accuracy on the validation dataset, are critical for assessing the model’s generalization ability. This will be further evaluated in subsequent phases of the research.

In summary, the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model was successfully trained on a dataset of 200 manually annotated arthroscopic images. Leveraging the ResNet50 architecture and FPN, the model accurately segmented the long biceps tendon with a low loss rate, demonstrating its potential for objective image analysis in medical applications. The AdamW optimizer, set at a learning rate of 1e-5, provided stable and effective training. This model shows significant promise for improving the objectivity of surgical decision-making and could be extended to other areas of medical imaging for further validation and testing on larger datasets.

Results

The results of this study showcase the competency of the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model in precisely segmenting the LHB in arthroscopic images, thereby laying the groundwork for objective surgical decision-making. The subsequent sections expound on performance metrics, visual representation analysis, and hurdles faced throughout the segmentation process.

Despite the relatively small size of the training set, which consists of 200 manually annotated images, the segmentation algorithm achieved remarkable performance. When evaluated by experts against a randomly selected validation set of 400 automatically segmented images, the model scored a precision rate of 0.88. This score indicates a high level of accuracy in correctly identifying the LHB and excluding non-target structures from the segmentation. The final combined loss, encompassing classification loss (0.006), bounding box regression loss (0.0145), RPN bounding box regression loss (0.062), and mask loss (0.0542), reached 0.0809 by the end of the 10,000 training iterations. This detail further underscores the reliability of the algorithm in analyzing previously unseen images.

The automated pixel-level evaluation on the 400 validation images yielded a precision of 0.83, a recall of 0.86, a F1-score of 0.83, and a mean IoU of 0.76 (median IoU: 0.90). The high median IoU indicates that the vast majority of images were segmented with excellent spatial accuracy, while a small number of particularly challenging cases reduced the overall mean.

The pixel-level recall of 0.86 demonstrates that the algorithm captures the substantial majority of the tendon area delineated in the ground-truth annotations, while the F1-score of 0.83 confirms balanced performance across both precision and recall. These findings, taken together with the expert-evaluated precision of 0.88, provide converging evidence for the reliability of the segmentation.

The spatial orientation of the LHB within the arthroscopic images was a critical variable considered during algorithm training. We classified visual representations into four distinct categories which accounted for various backgrounds and orientations. These categories included (Figure 3).

(a) Tendon in the foreground with a dark background. (b) Tendon in the foreground with light background. (c) Red tendon “lipstick” in the foreground with red background. (d) Embedded Tendon with a monochrome background. (e) Tendon with perspective and similar structures.

The model’s performance was consistent across these categories, with the LHB demonstrating detectability regardless of its orientation or the type of background. Figure 1 features examples of these categories and underscores the algorithm’s capacity to accurately identify the LHB in various scenarios. In images with either a dark or light background (e.g., Figure 3(a) and 3(b)), the algorithm successfully segmented the LHB with great confidence.

Artifacts, such as the “smudge effect” depicted in Figure 4, were another consideration during the analysis. The smudge effect never scored higher than 0.5, most often falling between 0.1 and 0.3. This range suggests that the algorithm consistently recognized these artifacts as low-relevance elements, minimizing their impact on segmentation outcomes. The model’s ability to handle these minor distortions was primarily due to its capacity to prioritize the main features of the LHB over less relevant background noise. This was particularly noticeable in images with monochromatic backgrounds, such as Figure 3(c) and 3(d), where artifact emergence was more common but was effectively managed by the model.

Example for “smudge effect” in Figure 3(e) with score 0.26.

To contextualize these results, the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model was compared against a U-Net with a ResNet34 encoder and three classical segmentation approaches. The U-Net achieved a mean IoU of 0.91 and a Dice coefficient of 0.94 (precision 0.95, recall 0.94), while the Mask R-CNN achieved a mean IoU of 0.76 and a Dice of 0.83 (precision 0.83, recall 0.86); the difference was statistically significant (paired Wilcoxon, p < 0.001). It should be noted that while U-Net achieved higher pixel-level accuracy, Mask R-CNN offers bounding box detection, multi-instance handling, and per-detection confidence scores, capabilities that may prove valuable in more complex clinical scenarios involving multiple anatomical structures.

Discussion

The findings from this study confirm the efficiency of the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model in segmenting the LHB in arthroscopic images.

The MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model achieved a precision score of 0.88 during validation, demonstrating its reliable identification of the LHB with minimal false positives. This metric is particularly significant given the variability inherent in arthroscopic imaging, where factors like lighting, occlusions, and anatomical complexity pose substantial challenges. Compared to other studies employing machine learning for medical segmentation, this result aligns with similar applications. For instance, a study that used U-Net for tumor segmentation in MRI images reported comparable precision scores, underlining the robustness of CNNs for feature extraction and classification in noisy datasets. 1

The pixel-level evaluation corroborates this finding, yielding a precision of 0.83 and a recall of 0.86 on the 400 validation images (mean IoU 0.76, median 0.90). The convergence of expert-assessed and pixel-level precision lends additional confidence to the model’s reliability.

Despite a relatively small training dataset of 200 manually annotated images, the model’s performance underscores the utility of transfer learning. By fine-tuning pre-trained weights for a domain-specific task, the model capitalized on generalized feature representations, enabling accurate segmentation even with limited data. This finding corroborates previous research emphasizing the value of pre-trained architectures like ResNet in medical imaging. 13

In clinical practice, high precision is crucial in minimizing false positives, especially in applications such as arthroscopic surgery where inaccurate segmentation could lead to incorrect surgical decisions. The MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model’s ability to maintain precision across a variety of scenarios – including inconsistent lighting and tendon orientations – addresses a significant limitation of manual assessments. Studies in related domains, like automated segmentation of cardiac structures, emphasize the necessity of dependable identification across diverse conditions. 4

The model’s ability to detect and segment the LHB under challenging conditions, such as monochrome backgrounds or embedded anatomical contexts, further underscores its applicability. These findings align with previous research on deep learning in laparoscopy, which demonstrated that FPN-based architectures excel at preserving spatial features in low-contrast environments. 20 By extending segmentation to arthroscopic images, this study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of deep learning in real-time surgical decision support.

A significant strength of the model was its capability to lessen the impact of artifacts, like the “smudge effect”. By providing low-relevance scores (0.1–0.3) to non-essential features, the algorithm prioritized the LHB over background noise effectively. This robustness is consistent with outcomes from other applications of MASK R-CNN in endoscopy, where resistance to artifacts has been an essential element in making reliable segmentations. 3 Improving this robustness is vital because artifacts typically mask crucial anatomical details in clinical settings.

Beyond the smudge effect, arthroscopic imaging is subject to motion blur from rapid camera movement, specular reflections caused by the endoscope’s light interacting with wet tissue surfaces, fluid turbidity from blood or irrigation, and lens condensation. A systematic evaluation of performance stratified by artifact type remains an important direction for future investigation.

The demonstrated success of the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model in segmenting the LHB has broader implications for medical imaging. The precision achieved in this study suggests the potential for deploying similar architectures in other domains, such as oncology or neurology. For instance, CNN-based segmentation has been demonstrated to improve tumor delineation in MRI and CT scans, where accurate boundaries are essential for treatment planning. 14 Extending this approach to orthopedic imaging could provide clinicians with objective, data-driven insights, thereby reducing variability in diagnoses and improving patient outcomes.

Additionally, the model’s performance underscores the importance of dataset quality over quantity. It should be acknowledged at this point that involving multiple experts to identify the long biceps tendon would be beneficial for improving interobserver reliability. In addition, pre-study patient stratification based on clinical presentation would enhance the quality of the study results.

The manual annotation of 200 images ensured high-quality ground truth data, enabling effective learning despite a limited dataset size. This finding underscores the necessity of meticulous dataset curation in medical applications, as recently emphasized by reviews on segmentation methodologies. 8

While the results are promising, there are still some limitations. The dataset’s demographic and anatomical diversity was limited, potentially impacting the model’s generalizability. Strengthening the dataset by including a wider range of patients and imaging conditions would improve the findings. Moreover, implementing additional preprocessing techniques, such as contrast enhancement or artifact suppression, could amplify the segmentation accuracy.

In particular, the study population of 124 participants (84 male, 40 female) from a single institution may not fully represent the anatomical and demographic variation encountered in the broader patient population. A multi-center validation incorporating a more diverse cohort is recommended.

Future research should explore ensemble methods by integrating the MASK R-CNN with other architectures such as U-Net or Transformer-based models. This approach aims to capitalize on their complementary strengths. Preliminary studies imply that hybrid models can improve segmentation performance in complex medical images. 21 In addition, the real-time deployment of these models in surgical workflows should be evaluated to assess their utility in dynamic, high-pressure environments.

Among these, vision Transformer architectures such as Swin-UNet and SegFormer warrant particular attention, though their higher computational and data requirements may necessitate larger training datasets than are currently available for this application.

The broader implications of these results extend beyond orthopedic applications. By adapting the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model to other anatomical regions or medical conditions, professionals in the healthcare industry could potentially benefit from more standardized diagnostic tools. For instance, in the detection of pulmonary nodules, segmentation models of this nature could enhance early diagnosis and treatment planning. Likewise, in gastroenterology, automated identification of abnormalities during endoscopic procedures could facilitate the early detection of diseases like colorectal cancer. 1

Another promising avenue entails the use of deep learning models in medical education. Trainee surgeons could interact with annotated datasets and automated segmentation tools to bolster their understanding of anatomical variations and surgical landmarks. This approach was highlighted in studies where AI-powered tools acted as augmentations in simulation training environments. 22

Conclusion

The performance of the MASKRCNN_RESNET50_FPN model in segmenting the LHB suggests its potential as a tool for objective and trustworthy image analysis in arthroscopic surgery. By tackling obstacles like artifact management and inconsistent image quality, the study advances the ongoing effort to incorporate deep learning into clinical practice. These outcomes serve as a stepping stone for wider applications in medical imaging, highlighting the necessity for continued interdisciplinary cooperation to enhance patient care via innovative technologies.

By focusing on objective metrics and standardized methods, this research underscores the transformative potential of AI in medicine. Future work should expand upon these findings by integrating larger datasets, refining preprocessing techniques, and validating models in real-world clinical settings. The ultimate goal is to establish AI-driven solutions as indispensable tools in modern medical practice, supporting consistent and high-quality care for patients across diverse specialties.
