Topology and peripheral functionality govern intramolecular π–π stacking, solid-state self-assembly and chiroptical activity in rigid bilayer nanographenes
Authors: Bin Hu, Ke-Lin Zhu, Ye Zhang, Jiaqi Liang, Zhi-Ao Li, Yu-Bo Guo, Dan-Yang Wang, Chen Cui, Han-Yuan Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise control over interlayer organization in nanographenes is a central challenge in carbon materials science because stacking geometry governs their electronic, supramolecular and chiroptical behaviors. Here we report a topological design strategy for rigid bilayer nanographenes with extensive intramolecular π–π overlap and tunable chiroptical properties. Two covalently locked extended helicenes, 1 and 2, were synthesized through a concise Sonogashira/Diels–Alder sequence followed by a kinetically controlled Scholl oxidation forming thirteen C–C bonds. Structural analysis revealed highly ordered bilayer architectures, while peripheral triflyloxy substitution in 2 markedly altered solid-state self-assembly and increased CPL brightness. Comparison with the topological isomer EP9H establishes a clear topology–property relationship across ground- and excited-state behaviour: although the more open topology of EP9H favours stronger intrinsic chiral induction, functionalization of 2 enhances molar absorptivity and overall chiroptical output.

Introduction

Controlling the aggregation and layer packing of graphene is critical, as interlayer registry, stacking order, and twist angle profoundly influence its electronic, optical, and mechanical properties.1–4 Distinct stacking configurations—including AB, ABC, and twisted arrangements—generate markedly different band structures and moiré superlattices, which can host correlated insulating and superconducting phases.5–10 Accordingly, precise manipulation of stacking geometry has become a central strategy for engineering graphene-based materials with tunable quantum and device functionalities.11–13

Covalent interlayer linking14–16 has emerged as a powerful approach to achieving atomic-level control over nanographene packing.17,18 By enforcing defined geometries, these strategies enhance intermolecular coupling, charge transport, and chiroptical responses, while improving thermal and photophysical stability.19–25 Recent developments in dimerization, edge fusion,26–29 and defect-anchored coupling30 have produced robust, optoelectronically active nanocarbon architectures. However, scalability,31 reversibility,32,33 and positional precision34 remain significant challenges, necessitating continued efforts to correlate molecular design with macroscopic properties.35–59

To expand this approach, we employed a topological design strategy. Building on our group's recent classification of topological isomers in polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs),60 we identified that the target bilayer compound 1 constitutes a topological isomer of our previously reported extended pentadecabenzo[9]helicene (EP9H).61 While retaining identical carbon atom counts, 1 features a distinct connectivity designed to maximize intramolecular π-surface hybridization. This design provides a platform to investigate how topological variation governs chiroptical performance beyond the effects of simple heteroatom substitution. We report the synthesis, structural characterization, and chiroptical properties of extended helicenes 1 and 2, both exhibiting substantial interlayer overlap within a rigid bilayer framework (Scheme 1). These molecules were constructed from a common precursor via sequential Sonogashira and Diels–Alder reactions, followed by a high-efficiency Scholl oxidation that formed thirteen new C–C bonds, achieving an average yield of ∼82% per bond. Crucially, the final planarization step proceeded under kinetic control: adjusting the reaction time enabled the selective formation of either the fully tert-butylated 1 or the bis-triflyloxy-functionalized 2. Notably, 2 represents a rare example of a chiral nanographene bearing bis-triflyloxy substituents, a structural motif whose optical and electrochemical properties remain underexplored. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction reveals that both compounds adopt rigid, bilayered nanographene architectures stabilized by extensive intramolecular π–π interactions. Crystallographic analysis further indicates that both 1 and 2 crystallize as racemates consisting of (P)- and (M)-enantiomers. The electron-withdrawing triflyloxy groups in 2 significantly influence interlayer coupling and modulate chiroptical activity. Both compounds exhibit strong visible-range absorption and emission, pronounced circular dichroism (CD), and intense CPL responses, establishing a modular platform for constructing covalently linked, chiral bilayer nanographenes with tunable optoelectronic functionality.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of 1 and 2

Compound 3 underwent a Sonogashira coupling with (3-ethynylphenyl)ethynyltriisopropylsilane (4) to furnish compound 5 in 94% yield. The sterically demanding triisopropylsilyl (TIPS) group in 5 enabled a thermally induced Diels–Alder reaction with 3,4-bis(4-tert-butylphenyl)-2,5-diphenylcyclopenta-2,4-dien-1-one (6), affording compound 7 in 80% yield. In the same reaction mixture, subsequent tetrabutylammonium fluoride (TBAF) treatment efficiently removed the TIPS protecting group, generating the corresponding terminal alkyne, which immediately underwent a second Sonogashira coupling with compound 3 to yield compound 8 in 63% yield. Subjecting compound 8 to a further Diels–Alder reaction with 6 produced precursor 9 in 64% yield.

Oxidative cyclodehydrogenation of 9 with 2,3-dichloro-5,6-dicyano-1,4-benzoquinone (DDQ) and trifluoromethanesulfonic acid (TfOH) induced a Scholl reaction, affording the deep-red solid products 1 (8% overall yield; 82% average yield per bond) or 2 (14% overall yield; 86% average yield per bond) (Scheme 2). Notably, the conversion of 9 to 1 involved the formation of 13 new C–C bonds, corresponding to an average yield of approximately 82% per bond. Furthermore, time-dependent analysis revealed that extending the reaction to 1 hour led to OTf substitution of two tert-butyl groups at symmetry-related positions and then result in 2. This transformation is proposed to proceed through a steric-strain-driven overoxidation/nucleophilic-trapping cascade. Following the initial cyclization, further oxidation of the conjugated core likely generates an electrophilic radical cation intermediate. Subsequently, triflate anions undergo selective nucleophilic ipso attack at the sterically congested edge carbon centers, triggering elimination of the bulky tert-butyl substituents, presumably as tert-butyl cations, and concomitant covalent installation of the triflate groups.62,63

Structure characterization of 1 and 2

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) high-resolution mass spectrometry confirmed the successful oxidative cyclization of precursor 9 into compounds 1 and 2. In both cases, the experimentally observed isotopic distributions closely matched the simulated patterns, validating the structural integrity of the final products. For compound 1 (m/z calcd: 2637.2828 [M]+; found: 2637.2851), the molecular ion appeared 26 Da lower than that of the precursor, consistent with the loss of 26 hydrogen atoms during the formation of 13 new C–C bonds via the Scholl reaction (Fig. S27). In contrast, 2 (m/z calcd: 2821.0459 [M]+; found: 2821.0471) exhibited a molecular ion 184 Da higher than 1, indicative of the substitution of two tert-butyl groups with two triflyloxy (–OTf) moieties (Fig. S28).

To further probe the structural relationship among nanographene isomers, we compared compound 1 with the previously reported analogue EP9H (m/z calcd 2633.2520 [M]+; found 2633.2493). The observed 4 Da mass difference between 1 and EP9H arises despite their identical carbon counts (C200), suggesting a subtle yet topologically significant structural divergence. According to the PAH topological classification recently developed in our group, 1 and EP9H represent a pair of generalized topological isomers, characterized by an identical number of carbon atoms but distinct degrees of cyclodehydrogenation (Δ4H). This result highlights that molecular topology plays a determinative role in governing the extent of planarization during Scholl reactions, ultimately leading to structurally and electronically distinct nanographene frameworks from isocarbon precursors.

Single crystals of 1·4CS2·5.5C2H5OH64 were obtained by slow evaporation from CS2/C2H5OH (4 : 1, v/v) at room temperature. X-ray diffraction analysis reveals that compound 1 crystallizes in a centrosymmetric space group as a racemic mixture of (P)-1 and (M)-1 (Scheme 3a and b). A similar result was observed for compound 2, which crystallized as 2·7CS2 (ref. 64) under comparable conditions, also forming a racemate comprising (P)-2 and (M)-2 (Scheme 3c and d). Structurally, both compounds display a rigid, bilayer graphene-like framework characterized by prominent intramolecular π–π overlap between two nearly co-facial layers, each comprising two fused hexabenzocoronene (HBC) units. Specifically, 1 displays direct vertical overlap across 14 benzene rings, with interlayer C–C distances of 3.1–4.1 Å, highlighting strong intramolecular π-stacking stabilization (Fig. S30 and Table S2). Its extended [11]helicene core further adopts a markedly twisted conformation, with torsion angles ranging from 1.5° to 176.6° and an average value of 51.2° (Table S3). Compound 2 adopts a similar bilayer architecture stabilized by π–π interactions across the 14 overlapping hexatomic rings; however, the introduction of electron-withdrawing and bulky triflyloxy substituents induces subtle structural perturbations. These substituents likely introduce local steric strain, which results in marginally expanded interlayer C–C distances of 3.2–3.9 Å, accompanied by a slight reduction in the average torsion angle to 47.0°, with individual torsion angles ranging from 2.8° to 169.6° (Fig. S34 and Tables S4, S5).

The crystal packing is primarily driven by C–H⋯π interactions between the tert-butyl protons and adjacent HBC rings, with contact distances of 3.5–3.8 Å for 1 and 3.4–3.6 Å for 2 (Fig. S31 and S35). Critically, a comparison of 1 with its generalized topological isomer EP9H highlights the decisive role of molecular topology. While EP9H exhibits a relatively open conformation with moderate torsion angles averaging 25.1° and interlayer distances of 2.9–3.7 Å, 1 adopts a much more contorted geometry (average torsion 51.2°). This divergence stems directly from their distinct topological connectivities; despite sharing the same carbon count, the specific fusing mode in 1 enforces a tighter, highly twisted folding pattern, demonstrating how topological design can be leveraged to engineer nanographenes with radically different spatial confinements and degrees of planarization.

Beyond the intramolecular topology, detailed examination of the crystal packing reveals that the peripheral substituents govern the higher-order supramolecular assembly of these bilayer nanographenes. In the case of the fully tert-butylated compound 1, the molecules adopt an interleaved and tightly packed arrangement in the solid state (Fig. S32). This staggered packing effectively prevents the formation of continuous porous structures. In stark contrast, the introduction of bulky, electron-withdrawing OTf substituents in compound 2 leads to a dramatic alteration in the packing landscape. Rather than adopting a dense staggered arrangement, the OTf groups serve as structure-directing motifs, promoting the self-assembly of the molecules into a square box architecture. These intermolecular interactions give rise to a two-dimensional grid-like supramolecular network (Fig. S36). Crystallographic packing analysis reveals that the resulting open framework contains well-defined rhombic cavities, which are occupied by carbon disulfide solvent molecules.

Compounds 1 and 2 exhibited excellent solubility in mixed solvent systems of (CD3)2CO/CS2 (2 : 1, v/v) and CDCl3/CS2 (2 : 1, v/v), respectively, which facilitated the acquisition of high-resolution NMR data. Both spectra displayed sharp, well-defined signals, indicating that the molecules were well-dispersed without significant aggregation-induced broadening at the measured concentrations and temperatures.

For compound 1, the 1H NMR spectrum (Scheme 4, top) revealed a distinct set of signals spanning a wide aromatic range (7.44–11.30 ppm). Notably, the proton H19 was observed at the extreme low field of 11.30 ppm, reflecting the unique steric environment in the congested region. Here, the proton is forced into the deshielding zone of the aromatic ring currents due to significant steric compression between the two aromatic layers. Similarly, protons located in other sterically congested regions, such as H38, H18, and H20, appeared in the 9.82–10.24 ppm range. The remaining aromatic protons (e.g., H1–H16), located at the periphery of the HBC units, were distributed between 7.44 and 8.17 ppm.

The 1H NMR spectrum of 2 (Scheme 4, bottom) shows chemical shifts that differ from those of 1. The characteristic proton H19 is observed at 11.08 ppm. This slight upfield shift, compared to 1, suggests that the functionalization induces subtle conformational changes in 2. Notably, the signal distribution is more dispersed than in 1, indicating that the electron-withdrawing properties of the OTf groups alter the magnetic environments of the aromatic protons along the backbone. The transition from the all-hydrocarbon scaffold (1) to the OTf-functionalized derivative (2) enables the specific assignment of protons based on their electronic responses. The low-field region is particularly sensitive to these structural modifications, confirming the successful synthesis of 2.

To clarify the origin of the unusual magnetic environments observed in the NMR spectra, we examined the aromaticity and π-electron delocalization of compounds 1 and 2 using nucleus-independent chemical shift (NICS) and anisotropy of the induced current density (AICD) calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level. The NICS(1)zz maps (Fig. S39 and S40) reveal a markedly non-uniform distribution of aromatic character across the fused bilayer frameworks. In both structures, the outer peripheral rings are strongly aromatic, consistent with localized Clar sextets, whereas the inner core regions exhibit substantially weaker aromaticity, with some rings displaying weakly antiaromatic or non-aromatic character. Such spatial differentiation reflects the constraints imposed by the specific fusion topology. In agreement with this picture, the AICD plots (Fig. S37 and S38) display continuous diatropic currents mainly along the outer rim of the hexabenzocoronene subunits, indicating that π-delocalization is concentrated along the periphery. Notably, compounds 1 and 2 show nearly identical NICS and AICD profiles, indicating that the essential aromatic framework and ground-state electronic structure are determined largely by the rigid bilayer topology and are only marginally affected by peripheral triflyloxy substitution.

Optical and electrochemical properties of 1 and 2

The UV-vis absorption spectrum of compound 1 in CH2Cl2 displays a dominant band at 413 nm (ε = 8.44 × 104 M−1 cm−1), accompanied by shoulder bands at 360 and 433 nm and a weaker band at 505 nm. In comparison, compound 2 exhibits three major absorptions at 368, 412, and 507 nm, with an additional shoulder at 454 nm (Scheme 5a). Importantly, compound 2 shows a substantially higher molar absorptivity across the entire spectral range (ε = 1.33 × 105 M−1 cm−1), with its 412 nm band being markedly more intense than the 413 nm band of 1 (Fig. S41 and S44). This enhancement is attributed to the introduction of electron-withdrawing OTf groups, which increase electronic transition probabilities. The analogue EP9H (ε = 1.35 × 105 M−1 cm−1) exhibits distinct spectral features, reflecting the influence of molecular topology on the extent of π-conjugation.

Fluorescence spectra further highlight the impact of molecular topology. In CH2Cl2, compound 1 shows an emission maximum at 671 nm, whereas compound 2 emits at 667 nm (Scheme 5b). Compared to EP9H, the emission of 1 is red-shifted by 13 nm, while 2 is only 4 nm blue-shifted relative to 1. The fluorescence quantum yields (ΦF) of 1 (13%) and 2 (11%) both exceed that of EP9H (10%). Similarly, their fluorescence lifetimes (τ) of 14.6 ns (1) and 14.5 ns (2) are longer than that of EP9H (12.3 ns) (Fig. S43 and S46). The enhanced photophysical properties of compounds 1 and 2 suggest that their highly fused, rigid bilayer architectures—arising from the formation of 13 new C–C bonds—effectively suppress non-radiative decay pathways (knr) with greater efficiency than the more flexible topological framework of EP9H. This comparison underscores the central role of molecular topology in dictating the electronic structure and defining the optical gap of nanographene systems. In contrast, peripheral substituents, while influential, primarily serve to fine-tune the photophysical behavior, exerting secondary effects on emission and absorption characteristics. In addition, the molar absorptivities (ε), emission maxima (λem), and fluorescence quantum yields (ΦF) of compounds 1 and 2 are comparable to those of several reported single-layer analogues (Table S14).

The electrochemical properties of compounds 1 and 2 were investigated in CH2Cl2 by cyclic voltammetry (CV) and differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), with all potentials referenced to the Fc+/Fc redox couple (Scheme 5c). Compound 1 exhibited five reversible oxidation waves with half-wave potentials (E1/2) of 0.40, 0.56, 0.82, 1.11, and 1.36 V, together with two reduction events at −1.17 and −1.61 V. By contrast, compound 2 displayed oxidation waves that were anodically shifted relative to those of 1, appearing at 0.77, 1.04, 1.29, and 1.52 V, as well as three reduction events at −0.75, −1.31, and −1.54 V. The positive shifts in the oxidation potentials of 2 are consistent with the electron-withdrawing character of the OTf substituents, which stabilizes the HOMO level and increases the ionization energy. Notably, the first reduction potential of 2 is also substantially shifted to a more positive value compared with that of 1 (−0.75 versus −1.17 V), indicating that the OTf groups also lower the LUMO level and thereby facilitate electron uptake. Collectively, these electrochemical changes demonstrate that replacing peripheral tert-butyl groups with OTf substituents modulates both frontier molecular orbitals, leading to simultaneous stabilization of the HOMO and LUMO energy levels.

To gain deeper insight into the electronic structures of 1 and 2, time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations were performed using the B3LYP functional with the 6-31G(d) basis set. The simulated absorption spectra showed good agreement with experimental UV-vis data, validating the computational model (Fig. S51 and S52). For compound 1, the longest-wavelength electronic transition is calculated at 664 nm (f = 0.0141), whereas compound 2 exhibits a comparable transition at 665 nm (f = 0.0130) (Fig. S53 and S54). Molecular orbital analysis revealed that in 1, the HOMO is primarily localized on the central [11]helicene framework, particularly within the region of interlayer overlap (Scheme 5d). This localization indicates that the twisted π-framework governs the electronic behavior. A comparable distribution was observed for 2, demonstrating that the –OTf substituents do not substantially alter the spatial characteristics of the frontier orbitals. Moreover, the computed HOMO–LUMO energy gaps closely match the experimentally determined optical band gaps, reinforcing the consistency between theoretical predictions and observed photophysical properties.

Chiral characteristics

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis confirmed the molecular structure of compound 1. To investigate its chiroptical properties, enantiomers of 1 were successfully resolved by chiral HPLC on a CHIRALPAK IB N-5 column, which yielded two well-separated peaks corresponding to (P)-1 and (M)-1. The same separation method was successfully applied to compound 2, affording its pure enantiomers. The successful resolution of both 1 and 2 demonstrates their configurational stability as discrete chiral entities at ambient temperature.

CD spectroscopy revealed distinct and symmetric chiroptical responses for both enantiomers of compounds 1 and 2 (Scheme 6a and b). The CD spectra of (P)-1 and (M)-1 exhibited perfect mirror-image symmetry across the 350–750 nm range, with well-defined Cotton effects at 369, 424, 496, and 616 nm (Fig. S57 and Table S8).Compound 1 exhibits the highest absorption dissymmetry factor (|gabs|), reaching 1.56 × 10−2 at 616 nm. Similarly, the enantiomers of compound 2 displayed mirror-symmetric CD profiles with Cotton effects at 372, 426, 499, and 608 nm (Fig. S58 and Table S9). Compound 2 exhibits the highest |gabs| reaching 2.08 × 10−2 at 608 nm. Compared to 1, the enhancement of |gabs| in 2 underscores the influence of the electron-withdrawing triflyloxy substituents on augmenting the magnetic transition dipole moment. However, these values remain slightly lower than that of the topological isomer EP9H (2.76 × 10−2 at 640 nm). Collectively, these results indicate that whereas the rigid bilayer topology establishes a high baseline of chiroptical activity and permits further tuning through peripheral functionalization, the folded open topology of EP9H is intrinsically more conducive to ground-state chiral induction.

CPL measurements further revealed strong chiroptical activity in the excited states (Scheme 6c and d). Compounds (P)-1 and (M)-1 showed symmetric CPL emission between 550–850 nm, with luminescence dissymmetry factors (|glum|) of 2.60 × 10−2 and 2.56 × 10−2 at 683 nm, respectively (Fig. S60). Compound 2 exhibited slightly higher values, with |glum| reaching 3.02 × 10−2 and 3.06 × 10−2 at 667 nm (Fig. S62). Although the CPL anisotropy of 2 is only moderately enhanced relative to 1, a more substantial difference is observed in the CPL brightness (BCPL), calculated as BCPL = ε × Φf × |glum|/2. Compound 2 achieves a BCPL of 223.8 M−1 cm−1, significantly exceeding that of 1 (142.6 M−1 cm−1), primarily due to the higher molar absorptivity of the OTf-substituted framework. Notably, comparison with EP9H reveals a distinct topology-dependent structure–property relationship. EP9H displays stronger chiroptical emission, with a higher |glum| of 4.5 × 10−2 and a larger BCPL of 304 M−1 cm−1, consistent with its more contorted open topology being intrinsically more favorable for amplifying the magnetic dipole contribution to emission. In contrast, the highly fused rigid bilayer frameworks of 1 and 2 provide a robust and tunable platform in which targeted derivatization can be exploited to enhance overall luminescence brightness. Furthermore, compared with simpler single-layer analogues, whose |gabs| and |glum| values are typically limited to the 10−4–10−3 range, compounds 1 and 2 exhibit enhanced chiroptical responses that reach the 10−2 regime. Accordingly, the BCPL value of compound 2 reaches 224 M−1 cm−1, placing it among the higher-performance examples reported for related single-layer structures (Table S14)

Time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) simulations were employed to elucidate the origin of the enhanced chiroptical responses. The calculated circular dichroism (CD) spectra closely matched the experimental data, confirming the absolute stereochemistry of both compounds (Scheme 6e). To probe the underlying mechanism, theoretical absorption dissymmetry factors (gabs) were evaluated using the expression: g = 4|µm| × |µe| × cos θ/(|µm|2 + |µe|2), where θ is the angle between the magnetic (µm) and electric (µe) transition dipole moments.65 For the S0 → S1 transition of compound 1, a gabs of 6.15 × 10−2 was obtained, with a dipole magnitude ratio |µm|/|µe| of 0.025 and θ = 139.7° (cos θ = −0.76) (Table S10). In comparison, compound 2 exhibited a slightly higher gabs of 6.83 × 10−2, with |µm|/|µe| = 0.023 and θ = 33.7° (cos θ = 0.83) (Table S11). Notably, these theoretical values are larger than their experimental counterparts. This deviation, frequently observed in rigid chiral nanocarbons, likely arises from the absence of vibronic coupling (Herzberg–Teller effects) in standard vertical transition calculations. In experimental spectra, vibronic coupling enables the weakly allowed S0 → S1 transition to borrow intensity from higher-energy electric-dipole-allowed states, effectively enhancing |µe|. As gabs is inversely proportional to |µe|, this intensity borrowing leads to reduced experimental dissymmetry factors.66–70

Despite this quantitative disparity, the theoretical trend aligns with experimental observations. The enhanced gabs in compound 2 primarily originates from a more favorable alignment between electric and magnetic transition dipoles, rather than increased magnetic dipole strength. These findings highlight the pivotal role of bilayer topology, modulated by steric and electronic effects of peripheral substituents, in tuning transition dipole vector alignment and optimizing intrinsic chiroptical responses.

Conclusions

In summary, we have established a topological design strategy to construct two covalently locked, graphene-like bilayer extended helicenes (1 and 2), featuring extensive intramolecular π–π overlap within a rigid, highly fused framework. These structures were synthesized via a concise Sonogashira/Diels–Alder sequence followed by a kinetically controlled Scholl oxidation that formed thirteen new C–C bonds. Reaction-time control enabled selective formation of either the tert-butylated derivative 1 or the bis(triflyloxy)-substituted analogue 2. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction reveals that both molecules adopt highly ordered bilayer conformations with pronounced interlayer registry. Despite crystallizing as racemates, their configurationally stable chiral skeletons enabled successful chiral resolution. A comparison with the topological isomer EP9H highlights the central role of covalent connectivity in shaping the electronic structure and chiroptical behaviour of these systems. Notably, a unified topology–property relationship emerges across both ground-state and excited-state descriptors. Whereas the more flexible and open architecture of EP9H supports stronger intrinsic chiral induction, giving rise to larger gabs and glum values, the highly fused bilayer framework of 2 offers a rigid and well-defined scaffold for substituent-driven electronic modulation. Furthermore, the introduction of electron-withdrawing triflyloxy groups substantially enhances the molar absorptivity, thereby leading to a pronounced increase in CPL brightness (BCPL). Taken together, these findings identify the combination of topological editing and peripheral functionalization as a powerful strategy for controlling the optoelectronic, chiroptical, and solid-state assembly behaviours of covalently linked bilayer nanographenes.
