Incidence and risk factors of spondylolysis in youth football: a longitudinal study among male Norwegian players
Authors: Nicolay Morland, David Tovi, Benjamin Clarsen, Cecilie B Rygh, Lena Kristin Bache-Mathiesen, Hege Havstad Clemm, Hilde Gundersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSpondylolysis is a common source of low back pain (LBP) among adolescent athletes. We aimed to investigate the occurrence of LBP and spondylolysis, and the potential associations of weekly training and match exposure, and maturity status with spondylolysis among male youth football players.MethodsMale players (n=98) from six teams were followed over three consecutive seasons (U15–U17). Training and match exposure (min), and pain/injuries were self-reported weekly. Clinical examinations were conducted if a player reported LBP and abstained from football training/matches for 1 week or reported LBP for 4 consecutive weeks despite participating. MRI was used to determine if the LBP was due to spondylolysis. The maturity level was estimated biannually by X-ray of the left hand, along with body height and weight. Risk factor analyses estimated the association between an increase in total weekly training and match exposure and the risk of spondylolysis, and between maturity factors and the risk of spondylolysis.ResultsOf 98 players, 46 reported back pain at least once, 18 fulfilled the criteria for a clinical examination and 13 underwent an MRI scan, identifying 12 cases of spondylolysis. Risk factor analyses showed a significant association between increased total weekly training and match exposure and the risk of spondylolysis. No association was shown between any maturity measure and the occurrence of spondylolysis.ConclusionIncreased training and match exposure in the weeks before injury was significantly related to a diagnosis of spondylolysis. Maturity measures were not related to increased risk.Keywords: Football, Adolescent, Spondylolysis, Surveillance, Back injuries

Introduction

Spondylolysis is a common source of low back pain (LBP) among adolescent athletes,1 with an incidence two to five times higher than in non-athletes.2 Recently, LBP has emerged as a common issue in football, affecting more than half of young football players.3,5 Selhorst and colleagues found that 48% of adolescent male football players experiencing LBP were diagnosed with spondylolysis.6

Load and maturation have been established as important risk factors for injuries in youth football players,7 with training load being the most commonly reported risk factor of LBP in adolescent athletes.1 7 8 Training load is the total, cumulative physical demand placed on an athlete’s body during exercise using parameters of intensity, duration and frequency. During adolescence, many systems mature at different rates, and training exposure (duration and frequency) and intensity typically increase with age.9 It is unclear how load and maturation influence the risk of spondylolysis. Only a few studies have studied spondylolysis in adolescent football players.10,12 None have investigated training exposure, and only two have examined maturity as a potential risk factor.11 12

In football, repeated high-impact actions such as sprinting, jumping and cutting have been shown to increase bone turnover and subsequent bone accrual.13 However, when loading exceeds the bone’s ability to adapt, bone stress injuries, including stress reactions and stress fractures, occur.14

Understanding the association between load and maturation and the risk of spondylolysis may enable individualised football training programmes to prevent this injury. This study investigated training exposure and maturity as potential risk factors for spondylolysis in football. Additionally, we sought to describe the prevalence of LBP and the incidence and prevalence of spondylolysis in male adolescent football players.

Methods

The current study is part of a longitudinal research project involving 115 male football players (U14–U18) from Western Norway.15

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

Our study involved a specific age group pertinent to the research question, and by including teams from elite to non-elite, we attempted not to exclude based on socioeconomic background, though we only recruited boys. The author group consisted of four women and three men with diverse professional backgrounds and expertise. The influence of anthropometrics, age and gender on spondylolysis is discussed.

Patient and public involvement statement

The participants were not patients, and patient involvement was not included in any part of the research process.

Participants

Six teams (n=98) were followed during three consecutive seasons (U15–U17). Most players competed at the highest regional or national level (85% in 2019, 91% in 2020 and 92% in 2021). The number of respondents varied each season, due to the inclusion of new players and players withdrawing from the project (figure 1). At inclusion, players’ average age of commencement of structured football training was 5.8 years (SD 1.1) (n=59) and trained on average 4.5 times per week (SD 1.6) (n=78) of organised football. Teams at the highest regional and national levels had five or more organised matches or training activities per week during the season.

Overview of recruitment and withdrawals. Reasons for withdrawal included discontinuing football participation, moving to another city, time constraints related to completing questionnaires, and unwillingness to participate in physical testing.

Anthropometrics and maturity

Body height was measured with a stadiometer (Seca 206 and Seca 217, Hamburg, Germany). Body weight was measured, and body composition was estimated using an eight-polar bioimpedance method using multifrequency current (InBody 720, Biospace).

As a measure of bone maturity, bone age was estimated from X-rays of the left hand using Siemens Ysio Max with the integrated imaging system FLUORPSPO Compacts (software V.VE10; Siemens Healthineers; Germany) according to a previously outlined protocol.16 Bone age was assessed using the Greulich and Pyle method for bone maturity evaluation, applied via the BoneXpert stand-alone software (V.3.1.4; Visiana, Holte, Denmark). All measurements were performed using standard procedures biannually.

BoneXpert was additionally used to estimate each player’s age at peak height velocity (aPHV) based on the first X-ray taken for each player. A median split of aPHV was used to categorise the players as early or late maturers.17 Bone age used in the analyses of players with spondylolysis was set to the date of the biannual measure closest before the MRI examination, and the chronological age of these players was set to the date of the MRI examination.

Questionnaire data

To track weekly training and match exposure (minutes per week) and pain/injuries, players completed an electronic questionnaire (Briteback AB, Norrköping, Sweden) distributed via SMS on Sundays (153 weeks). Full questionnaire in the online supplemental appendix. Non-responders received SMS reminders at 24 and 48 hours, and the remaining non-responders were contacted by telephone.

Questionnaire responses were monitored weekly. NM and DT examined and diagnosed players who reported back pain that either (1) completely prevented training and match play for 1 week, or (2) persisted for 4 consecutive weeks, regardless of training and match participation. MRI scans were requested based on clinical findings to determine whether LBP was caused by spondylosis.

During the study period, 53 players reported LBP. Of these, 18 met the criteria for clinical examination. Two declined the examination, and one had no LBP confirmed. The remaining 15 players with LBP were followed up on. Based on clinical indications, 13 players were referred for MRI to obtain a medical diagnosis (ie, spondylolysis; figure 2).

Recurrent cases of spondylolysis were classified as a recurrent injury if follow-up MRI images showed changes on the same site (ie, unilateral right side L5 injury) as the original injury, and as a new injury if it occurred in a different site (ie, left side L5 or L4). For LBP in general, we counted cases only once per individual regardless of the number of times each individual reported LBP.

Statistical analyses

Player characteristics were presented with mean and SD. The annual and 3-year cumulative incidence of LBP and spondylolysis was expressed as percentages, and incidence rates were calculated as the number of new cases per 1000 training and match hours.

Since the data were self-reported and longitudinal,18 we assumed that 35% of the missing training exposure data were missing at random. Before risk factor analyses, missing data were imputed using multiple imputation with predictive mean matching.19 Resultant density plots and quartiles were almost identical, and the mean differed by ∼1% between the original data and the imputed data.

Potential associations between the probability of a new spondylolysis diagnosis (dependent variable) and chronological age, bone age and aPHV (independent variables) were analysed using univariable generalised linear mixed-effects models with a logit link (GLMM), with player and time as random effects to account for within-player correlations.

The relationship between spondylolysis and total weekly training and match exposure, including data from all players at various time points before diagnosis (1-week, 2-week, 3-week, 4-week and 5-week time lags), was evaluated using univariable GLMMs.

To account for the potential cumulative association among long-term training, match exposure and spondylosis, we used two approaches. First, we investigated the relationship between spondylolysis and a 5-week exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) of total weekly training and match exposure using a univariable linear mixed-effects model. This approach assumes that the importance of each week declines exponentially with each time lag, so that the week before the current week is most important, and the 5th week before the current week is the least important.20 However, the EWMA aggregation increases the risk of Type II errors.21 We therefore also ran a GLMM with a distributed lag specification that included all time lags simultaneously.22 This approach assumes that each week contributes unequally to the cumulative association, meaning it can explore which is most important between weeks 1–5 before the current week.21 Due to the sample size, we could not perform more advanced analyses that could additionally account for within-week non-linearity between exposure and probability of spondylolysis. Since spondylolysis was a rare outcome, ORs can be interpreted as approximate risk ratios.20

Analyses were performed in R (V.4.3.1) using the lme4 (V.1.1–34) and mice (V.3.16) packages. P values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Prevalence and incidence of LBP and spondylolysis

The cumulative incidence of LBP over the 3-year study period was 47%, meaning nearly half of the 98 players experienced LBP at least once. The annual cumulative incidence of LBP, defined as the proportion of players who reported LBP at least once within each year, was 30% (24/79) in 2019, 35% (31/89) in 2020 and 23% (15/66) in 2021. The incidence rate over the study period was 0.84 cases per 1000 training and match hours.

Of 14 MRI scans, 12 were diagnosed with spondylolysis (one player had spondylolysis at two different levels 1 year apart), one with disc prolapse and one had normal structural findings. The injuries were distributed with six bilateral injuries and six unilateral injuries. All affected players competed at the highest regional or national level.

The cumulative incidence of spondylolysis was 12% (12/98), and the incidence rate was 0.22 cases per 1000 training and match hours over the 3-year study period. The annual cumulative incidence of spondylolysis was 5.1% (4/79) in 2019, 5.6% (5/89) in 2020 and 4.5% (3/66) in 2021.

Influence of maturity

For players with spondylolysis, the mean chronological age at diagnosis was 16 (0.9) years, the mean bone age was 15 (1.0) years and the mean aPHV was 14 (1.3) years. 11 of the 12 spondylolysis cases occurred after aPHV. Three cases occurred in players maturing at a younger age (aPHV <13.9), and nine in players maturing at an older age (aPHV ≥13.9). No significant findings were identified between spondylolysis risk and chronological age, bone age or aPHV (online supplemental appendix).

Discussion

The present study investigated the occurrence of LBP and spondylolysis. It examined whether maturity status, weekly training and match exposure were associated with the risk of developing spondylolysis in male youth football players. Our findings revealed that nearly half of the players (47%) experienced LBP at least once, and 12% were diagnosed with spondylosis. Among players who experienced LBP-induced cessation of training for 1 week or symptoms for four consecutive weeks, 80% had MRI findings of spondylolysis. We discovered an association between total weekly training and match exposure and the risk of spondylolysis. No relationship was identified between the risk of spondylolysis and maturity status.

Our LBP cumulative 12-month injury incidence (23–35%) is lower than studies among adolescent athletes, which have reported rates of 33–57%.24 25

Spondylolysis was the most common imaging finding in the examined players. This is similar to recent reviews on LBP in adolescent athletes.1 7 8 Only two studies have reported the incidence of spondylolysis in adolescent football players, ranging from 0.02 to 0.2/1000 hours.23 26 Our incidence rate of spondylolysis (0.22/1000 hours) is nearly identical to that in a study using a similar cohort and imaging methodology (0.2/1000 hours).23 The lowest incidence was reported among academy female football players, but only for spondylolisthesis,26 and thus likely missed lower-grade injuries. Male athletes are 1.5–4.5 times more likely to have a spondylolysis than female athletes.2 6 One reason for this could be that females mature earlier than males and, in many sports and contexts, increase their training exposure at a later stage of maturation. It has also been proposed that the greater incidence in males is a result of the larger male sporting population.27 A third study on adolescent football players did not directly report the incidence of spondylolysis.28 We calculated, based on their reported individual training hours and injuries, an incidence of approximately 0.05–0.08/1000 hours. Injuries were only registered if players were unable to train for 1 week, and they may therefore also have missed lower-grade injuries.

Training exposure and spondylolysis

To our knowledge, no previous studies have considered training exposure as a risk factor for spondylolysis in adolescent football players. Risk factor analyses revealed a significant association between total weekly training and match exposure and the risk of spondylolysis in the 3 weeks leading up to the injury/start of symptoms. This suggests that spondylolysis is a consequence of cumulative loading, rather than acute trauma.

A handful of studies have assessed the relationship between training exposure and LBP in adolescents. Sundell et al29 found that adolescents participating in sports >6 hours/week had an increased risk of LBP, while our cohort averaged 8.8 hours/week. Schroeder et al30 studied paediatric patients with LBP. They found that adolescent athletes (sports ≥5 days or ≥20 hours/week) had a significantly higher prevalence of spondylolysis (32% vs 2%) than non-athletes (sports ≤3 days or ≤5 hours/week). This fits with all our spondylolysis injuries occurring among players at the highest regional or national level, participating in football activity (training and matches) at least 5 days per week. Nevertheless, more studies with larger sample sizes on the topic are needed.

Maturity as a risk factor

We found no association between any maturity measure and spondylolysis. A review concluded that it is unclear whether maturity status is a risk factor for injuries in adolescent football players.31 Potentially, other factors, such as growth rate, movement patterns, training load or individual anatomical differences, play a greater role in the development of spondylolysis than the timing of maturation.

Age of onset of PHV has been studied as a potential risk factor of injuries in adolescent football players,1731,33 but results have been inconsistent. Of the 12 spondylolysis cases in our study, nine occurred in players who reached PHV at an older age (≥13.9 years) and three occurred in players who reached PHV at a younger age (<13.9 years). This is in line with the findings of van der Sluis et al.17

When examining the time of injury relative to each player’s individual aPHV, 11 of the 12 spondylolysis cases occurred after aPHV. The findings match those of Monasterio et al,34 who included players with U12 data. This is supported by bone mineralisation potentially being delayed/lagging in periods of rapid growth, that is, there is a period of relative skeletal weakness during the adolescent growth period. Blimkie et al35 and Faulkner et al36 also specifically emphasise the importance of studying the different growth-related injuries separately to avoid the wash-out effect of injuries that typically occur in different phases of maturation, which we have done in this study.

Clinical implications

Clinicians should have a strong suspicion of spondylolysis in players who report LBP that results in withdrawal from training for at least 1 week, or pain that persists for several weeks, particularly after periods of high training exposure. We encourage coaches, parents and support staff to take early reports of back pain in adolescent athletes seriously, to ensure they receive proper medical attention.

Increased training exposure in the weeks before injury was significantly related to a diagnosis of spondylolysis. Football involves the generation of high impact forces as a result of sprinting, jumping and changing direction. Load magnitude has been shown to be an important factor in skeletal adaptation;37 therefore, appropriate training periodisation strategies that carefully manage the magnitude and progression of loading may help optimise bone adaptation while reducing the risk of bone stress injuries.

However, these early findings should be supported by more studies with higher sample sizes before these recommendations can be integrated into formal training load guidelines.

Strengths and limitations

Several methodological factors should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, although we had many observations of training exposure, we had few cases of spondylolysis, which may have limited our ability to detect potential relationships between risk factors and spondylolysis. The significant findings between training exposure and spondylosis may have been due to overfitting. However, in the distributed lag model, which was more prone to overfitting than the univariable models, most p values were no longer significant. Regardless, these results are early findings, and we implore future investigations with larger sample sizes to use multivariable approaches for confounder adjustment and more advanced models to explore theories of the relationship between training exposure and spondylosis.

It is unclear how common spondylolysis is in the asymptomatic high-training-exposure athletic population. It has been examined in one study by Tsutsui et al,11 who found that 50.4% of players had asymptomatic findings (unilateral bone marrow oedema of the pedicle). Still, these were more prone to progression to symptomatic bilateral findings. Though the MRI findings may have been incidental, the players were all symptomatic, and the findings fit their symptoms.

The two players who fit the inclusion criteria but declined examination citing follow-up by general practitioner (GP) might have been missed cases of spondylolysis. We may also have missed cases among players that did not report regularly enough and/or did not have severe enough symptoms to be picked up with our collection of self-reported symptoms. However, there is a fine line between picking up on all the ‘true cases’, without over-diagnosing due to false positives. More knowledge about MRI findings in asymptomatic individuals is important to have if applying more liberal inclusion criteria.

Second, a potential limitation of the present study is the use of self-reported data on training exposure and pain/injuries. Self-report depends on participant compliance, and data may be negatively impacted by recall bias or misinterpretation. However, self-reporting is particularly valuable for monitoring overuse injuries and subjective symptoms, as these often do not result in time loss or medical consultation. Consequently, traditional time-loss definitions tend to underestimate the true injury burden, whereas regular self-reporting enables early detection of issues that could otherwise go unnoticed. This was combined with regular inspection of the data by two physiotherapists, enabling further detection of injuries and problems that may otherwise have been missed.38 Two players that fit the criteria declined examination citing follow-up by GP as reason, and these two players were able to return to play in a short amount of time without major time loss and without meeting criteria for examination at a later point in time, and thus we do not think we have missed out on any true cases of spondylolysis that needed medical attention or load monitoring in order to get better.

Furthermore, a strength of this study is the inclusion of players from teams at different levels and training environments. All players diagnosed with spondylolysis competed at a high level but represented diverse team contexts. This strengthens the generalisability of the findings and suggests that the observed injury pattern is not limited to a single team culture or training style.

A strength in the present study is the use of MRI, the preferred imaging modality for early-stage detection of spondylolysis,39 as standard radiography often fails to detect early-stage (low-grade) spondylolysis.40

We used the bone age measurement closest to the time of diagnosis, that is, the date of MRI examination. Since bone maturation can progress rapidly during adolescence, we cannot exclude the possibility that variation in the time between bone age measurement and diagnosis of spondylolysis based on MRI findings may have led to the negative findings in our risk factor analysis. In future studies, we recommend assessing bone age concurrently with the imaging diagnosis to improve accuracy.

Finally, it is worth noting that part of the data was collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, while training routines and matches were affected by public health restrictions. Although training and match exposure, as well as pain and injuries, were continuously monitored throughout this period, we cannot exclude the possibility that these unusual circumstances may have influenced the findings, despite recording new incidences of spondylolysis. No matches were played, but training exposure remained high compared with prepandemic levels.

Conclusion

Spondylolysis was diagnosed in 80% of players who experienced cessation in training for 1 or more weeks from LBP, or LBP for 4 consecutive weeks. Increased training exposure in the weeks before injury was significantly related to a spondylolysis diagnosis. This injury often requires extended time away from sport. By taking early LBP reports seriously and adjusting training load accordingly, coaches and support staff may help prevent progression to spondylolysis. Clinicians should have a strong suspicion of spondylolysis in players who report LBP resulting in withdrawal from training for at least 1 week, or pain that persists during training, particularly after periods of high training exposure.
