Optimization of accelerated life test design process for gears and bearings using equivalent damage method
Authors: Jae-Hyun Kim, Beom-Soo Kim, Jung-Ho Park, Dong-Joo Moon, Suchul Kim, Young-Jun Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract This study proposes an optimal accelerated life test (ALT) design process for gears and bearings using a novel equivalent damage method (EDM). The process first calculates the damage incurred by each component under existing load duration distribution (LDD) conditions. It then generates an optimal ALT that applies equivalent damage while minimizing the total test time. We formulate this as a multi-objective optimization (MOO) problem with the objectives of minimizing both the total test time and the deviation from the damage profile of the LDD. The damage error for each component is treated as a constraint. The MOO problem is solved using the NSGA-III algorithm. To demonstrate its efficacy, the process is applied to a rotorcraft test gearbox. We compare the resulting ALT’s test time against the existing LDD and analyze the solution distribution on the final Pareto front. The results yield an optimal ALT that significantly reduces test time while preserving damage equivalency, thereby validating the effectiveness of the proposed design process.Keywords: Accelerated life test, Equivalent damage method, Gears, Bearings, Multi-objective optimizationSubject terms: Engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Gears and bearings are essential components of power transmission systems, with applications spanning numerous industries, including aerospace, automotive, agricultural machinery, shipbuilding, and home appliances1–4. A wide variety of these components—such as parallel shaft, planetary, and bevel gears, alongside roller, ball, and journal bearings—are utilized based on specific system requirements.

Operating for extended periods in harsh environments, these components are intricately interconnected within complex mechanical systems. This exposure and interconnectedness make them highly susceptible to critical defects5. The failure of a single gear or bearing can lead to catastrophic gearbox malfunctions, causing system-wide failures or operational shutdowns that affect interconnected machinery6,7. Therefore, to mitigate these risks, it is crucial that gears and bearings are designed from the outset to ensure a sufficient lifespan under their intended operating conditions.

Following the design stage, life tests are essential to verify that gears and bearings meet their required operational lifespan. These tests are typically based on a load duration distribution (LDD), which is established from the system’s expected operating conditions and defines a series of test cases based on load, speed, and duration8.

However, modern product development cycles have been significantly compressed by intense market competition, creating a pressing need for more rapid reliability evaluations. Furthermore, the considerable cost and manpower required for life testing are directly proportional to the test duration, making time reduction crucial for minimizing resource expenditure. To address these challenges, accelerated life tests (ALTs) are frequently employed. An ALT can provide reliability data in a fraction of the time by subjecting components to conditions more strenuous than those specified in the LDD, thereby simulating a lifetime of wear in a shorter period9,10.

An ALT aims to induce product failure more rapidly by applying stress levels that exceed normal operating conditions, thereby simulating a lifetime of wear in a condensed timeframe11. This approach significantly shortens test times, reduces costs, and facilitates the swift identification of failure modes and mechanisms. Reflecting its utility, ALT is a subject of active research across diverse engineering fields. Studies have demonstrated its successful application in areas such as advanced CMOS technologies12, Micro Electro Mechanical Systems (MEMS)13, high-brightness LEDs14, aerospace structural components15, solid absorber coatings16, and satellite electronics17, with some methods achieving substantial reductions in total test time18.

Given their critical role and susceptibility to failure in power transmission systems, gears and bearings have been a major focus of ALT research. For gears, studies have explored various facets of ALT, including the optimization of test parameters19, the use of Monte Carlo simulations to reduce required test cycles20, the application of linear cumulative damage theory to gear pumps21, the development of life-stress models for planetary reducers22, and the analysis of powertrain components using the Weibull-Inverse power law23. Similarly, ALT has been effectively applied to bearings in various contexts, such as for main shafts in wind turbines24 and within electric motors25.

While these studies validate the effectiveness of applying diverse models to individual gears or bearings, developing an ALT process that simultaneously optimizes for multiple, interconnected components within a single gearbox remains a significant challenge.

Most of the aforementioned ALT studies on gears and bearings involve applying loads harsher than the actual LDD. Consequently, estimating the lifespan under real operating conditions is not possible unless the results from these harsh conditions are appropriately processed. Therefore, models such as life distributions and stress-parameter relationships are necessary to interpret the results obtained under accelerated conditions. In practice, commonly used life distributions for ALTs include the exponential, Weibull, and lognormal distributions. For stress-parameter relationships, frequently employed models include the linear, Arrhenius, inverse power, and exponential/loglinear models26.

In other words, existing studies inevitably present limitations as the process of selecting a model type and determining its various coefficients involves numerous mathematical approximations, subjective intervention from the designer, and empirical methods. A further limitation is that reasonable results can only be derived if information on vulnerable failure modes is known before the test to determine the coefficients. In fact, Sung has argued that the development of general guidelines for selecting an ALT plan is essential26.

Therefore, this paper proposes an optimal ALT design process that minimizes test time using an optimization algorithm without the need for a designer’s subjective input, empirical methods, or prior information on vulnerable failure modes. The design process proposed herein first calculates the damage on each gear and bearing under the existing LDD conditions. It then designs an optimal ALT that applies equivalent damage while simultaneously minimizing the total test time.

While this approach is similar to existing methods in that it must apply harsher loads or speeds than the LDD conditions to inflict equivalent damage, its key distinction lies elsewhere. The process of determining test conditions—such as load, speed, and duration—does not require subjective designer intervention or prior information on failure modes.

Analytically deriving an optimal solution that simultaneously minimizes both test time and the deviation in damage from the existing LDD is intractable. The problem involves numerous complex constraints and design variables—such as the permissible ranges for load and speed, their maximum values, and test durations—making the computational cost of exploring all possible combinations within a target design range prohibitive.

To address such complexity, multi-objective optimization (MOO) techniques are frequently employed in scenarios where analytical solutions are not feasible. Reflecting its versatility, MOO has been successfully applied to diverse fields, including energy-saving strategies27, drug design28, farming system optimization29, and building retrofit planning30.

Within mechanical engineering, MOO has also been widely applied to the optimal design of gears and bearings. For instance, researchers have used algorithms like NSGA-II and NSGA-III to optimize the macro and micro geometries of various gears and bearings31–34. However, these studies have focused exclusively on optimizing the design specifications of the components themselves. The application of MOO to the design of an ALT for these components remains an unexplored area of research.

Damage of gears and bearings using the stress-life curve

Gears and bearings are subjected to repeated cyclic loading, which can lead to failure even when the applied stress is below the material’s yield or ultimate strength. This phenomenon, known as fatigue failure, occurs as stress is applied over numerous cycles.

Fatigue failure typically initiates as a microscopic crack at a stress concentration point, such as a material discontinuity or inclusion. As cyclic loading continues, this crack propagates, increasing in size and further concentrating stress at its tip. This process eventually culminates in the complete fracture of the component35,36. Therefore, the design of gears and bearings must account for not only static failure modes but also resistance to fatigue failure.

Due to the statistical nature of fatigue, determining a material’s fatigue strength requires extensive repetitive testing. Each test involves subjecting a specimen to a cyclic stress level below its yield or ultimate strength until failure occurs. This process is then repeated at progressively lower stress levels, which inevitably leads to a higher number of cycles to failure37.

The results of this iterative testing are plotted to create a Stress-Life (S-N) curve, with stress on the y-axis and the number of cycles to failure on the x-axis. This curve is crucial for design, as it defines the maximum allowable stress for a given number of cycles.

From the S-N curve, damage can be quantified. For a given operating stress, the damage is defined as the ratio of the actual number of applied cycles to the total number of cycles the material can withstand at that stress level before failure. This value, often expressed as a percentage, serves as a quantitative measure of the fatigue damage a gear or bearing incurs under a specific load condition38–40.

Real-world mechanical systems operate under various conditions, subjecting them to a wide range of loads across multiple load cases. Therefore, a comprehensive damage assessment for gears and bearings must consider the effects of all these different loads.

To achieve this, the Palmgren-Miner cycle-ratio summation theory is commonly used. This rule states that the total cumulative damage is the linear sum of the damage incurred during each individual load case40,41. By summing the damage from each load case, one can determine the total damage inflicted on a component under a given LDD. This cumulative value allows engineers to assess whether a gear or bearing will operate without failure throughout its intended service life42.

The permissible range of speeds and torques that can be applied to a mechanical system varies significantly depending on test conditions, such as the power source, rated output, and lubrication method. If a designer arbitrarily sets the design range for these variables, the resulting optimal ALT could specify conditions that are physically impossible to achieve in the test environment. To prevent this, our study uses the ratios of increase or decrease relative to the speed and torque in the existing LDD as the design variables. Since the conditions in the existing LDD are already known to be within the permissible test range, this approach ensures that the optimized ALT conditions remain feasible and can be directly applied to the actual test.

Optimization

An appropriate Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO) algorithm must be selected to design the optimal ALT using the equivalent damage method. While various MOO algorithms are utilized across engineering fields—such as NSGA-II, NSGA-III, ACO, and SQP31,43–46—this study selected NSGA-III for its distinct advantages over other methods, particularly NSGA-II. According to previous studies, NSGA-III offers several key advantages, including a wider and more uniform spread of solutions on its Pareto front, faster convergence times, and a greater hypervolume indicator value, which signifies a higher quality set of optimal solutions33,47,48. Therefore, to derive high-quality solutions efficiently, the optimal ALT design in this study was performed using the NSGA-III algorithm. The detailed procedure for NSGA-III is described in the work of Deb and Jain49,50, and a flowchart is provided in Fig. 3. For this study, we implemented the real-coded NSGA-III algorithm, using the binary tournament method for parent selection, with the probabilities for both crossover and mutation set to 5%51,52.

Generally, a damage error margin of less than 5% is applied to account for uncertainties in material fatigue properties53. Therefore, the damage error of less than 2% achieved in this study is well within the acceptable range and is not expected to significantly impact the reliability assessment54. This result can be interpreted as a meaningful trade-off: a substantial test time reduction of over 3,000 h was achieved while maintaining practical reliability.

Furthermore, designers can select other solutions from the 172 rank-1 individuals on the Pareto front to meet different requirements. For instance, if a larger damage error is permissible, a plan with an even shorter test time can be selected. This flexibility allows for the convenient design and execution of ALTs tailored to specific project requirements and test conditions.

To further evaluate the optimal ALT design, the distribution of solutions on the final Pareto front was analyzed.

As shown in Fig. 5, a clear trade-off exists between minimizing test time and minimizing damage error. For solutions with a total test time between 6,700 and 7,100 h, the total damage error remained consistently below 3%. This indicates that the test time can be reduced by 30–34% compared to the original LDD while maintaining a high degree of damage equivalence.

However, as the total test time was reduced further—to below 6,300 h—the norm of the damage error increased to 3.5–4%. This suggests that excessively shortening the test time makes it more challenging to preserve damage equivalence.

These results demonstrate that the Pareto front provides the designer with the flexibility to select an optimal solution that balances these competing objectives based on specific project goals. This flexibility is examined in greater detail below.

Case 1 (Prioritizing Test Time Reduction): In industries with rapid product development cycles, minimizing the test duration is often the primary objective. In this scenario, a designer can select the solution with the shortest test time from the Pareto front. As shown by the red squares in Fig. 6, the results of our case study indicate that the test time can be reduced from 10,125 h to approximately 6,200 h—a significant improvement. However, this time reduction comes at the cost of decreased accuracy; the norm of the damage error increases to about 3.4%. This trade-off highlights that while prioritizing a shorter test duration is advantageous, it can compromise the fidelity of the life test. Therefore, the designer must first define an acceptable error margin and then select the solution that minimizes test time while remaining within that constraint.

The optimal ALT design process proposed in this study is distinguished from previous approaches in that it does not yield a single optimal solution. Instead, by providing a Pareto front of optimal solutions, it offers the designer the flexibility to select a plan that best fits their specific requirements.

Unlike traditional ALT methods that typically generate a fixed solution, our process allows for the derivation of numerous optimal plans. This enables the designer to quantitatively assess the trade-off between test time reduction and damage error. Such an approach is expected to facilitate more efficient and tailored ALT designs in diverse applications, including aircraft gearboxes, automotive transmissions, and wind turbines.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to develop an optimal ALT design process by combining a Palmgren-Miner’s rule-based equivalent damage method with a multi-objective optimization algorithm. Unlike conventional probabilistic approaches, this research proposes an equivalent damage method that designs test conditions to replicate the damage from the existing LDD, thereby avoiding the need for statistical estimation. Since ALT design is a multivariable problem, the NSGA-III genetic algorithm was utilized to find the optimal solution.

To verify the applicability of the proposed process, a case study was conducted on an actual test gearbox. Through multi-objective optimization, a Pareto front was derived. An examination of one solution from the Pareto front showed that the proposed process could reduce the test time by approximately 30.4% with a maximum damage error of only 1.87%.

The optimal ALT design process proposed in this study is expected to enable a faster and more efficient design of ALTs. As this method is applicable to any system with an LDD and S-N curve, it can be readily applied to various other systems.

While this study developed the methodology and demonstrated its applicability, it has a limitation in that experimental verification was not performed. Future research should include the validation of this method through actual ALTs.
