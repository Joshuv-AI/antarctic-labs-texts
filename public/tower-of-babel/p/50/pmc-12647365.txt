Blood Pressure Variability and 90‐Day Functional Outcome in Branch Atheromatous Disease‐Related Stroke: A Multicenter Prospective Study
Authors: Yaping Zhou, Shengde Li, Haizhou Hu, Yi‐Cheng Zhu, Bin Peng, Lixin Zhou, Jun Ni, BAD‐study investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBranch atheromatous disease (BAD)‐related stroke shows distinct prognostic features from other stroke subtypes, with modifiable prognostic factors remaining inconclusive. The present research investigated the association between systolic blood pressure variability (BPV) and 90‐day functional outcomes of BAD‐related stroke. We enrolled 423 patients (median age 60 years; 70.2% male) with radiologically confirmed BAD from a prospective multicenter study in China. BPV was assessed using standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and variation independent of the mean (VIM) of systolic blood pressure measurements during hospitalization. The primary outcome was a poor functional outcome at 90 days, defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score >2. The secondary outcome was early neurological deterioration (END) within 7 days. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between BPV and outcomes. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted. Overall, 13.9% of patients experienced poor functional outcome. A higher BPV was associated with increased risk of END. Compared with the lowest tertile, patients in the highest tertile of systolic BPV had a significantly increased risk of poor functional outcome (OR: 3.10 for SD, 2.77 for CV, and 2.97 for VIM; all p < 0.05, p for trend <0.05 for all indices). Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis results were consistent with the primary findings. In conclusion, elevated systolic BPV during the acute phase is independently associated with END and poor 90‐day functional outcome in BAD‐related stroke, highlighting the importance of BPV monitoring and blood pressure stabilization in the management of BAD‐related stroke.

Introduction

Branch atheromatous disease (BAD)‐related stroke is a distinct subtype of ischemic stroke caused by stenosis or occlusion at the origin of cerebral deep penetrating arteries (1, 2). Accounting for 9.1%–25.9% of all ischemic strokes (3, 4, 5), BAD‐related stroke gained increasing attention for its distinct clinical and prognostic features. While BAD‐related stroke showed a high incidence of early neurological deterioration (END) and unfavorable outcomes, with 13.5%–61% of patients showing modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score > 2 at 90 days (4, 6, 7), the underlying mechanisms and modifiable prognostic predictors remain unclear. Although atherosclerosis is implicated, traditional vascular risk factors show limited prognostic value, while standard treatments for ischemic stroke often fail to improve BAD outcomes (6). The significant disability burden, distinct prognostic features from other subtypes of ischemic strokes, and lack of effective therapies underscore the urgency of identifying modifiable prognostic factors for BAD‐related stroke.

Blood pressure was the most prevalent modifiable risk factor for stroke (8, 9). Beyond the absolute value of blood pressure, growing evidence highlights the prognostic significance of blood pressure variability (BPV) (10, 11, 12), with elevated BPV linked to greater risk of disability, mortality, and recurrence of stroke (11, 13, 14, 15). However, although the association between BPV and ischemic stroke has been widely evaluated, the role of BPV in the specific subtype of BAD remains unclear. Since BAD shows distinct clinical and prognostic features from other subtypes of stroke, focusing on BAD may offer valuable insights for disease management. Furthermore, since BPV exhibits significant heterogeneity across ischemic stroke subtypes and occlusion sites (16, 17), focusing on the specific prognostic impact of BPV in BAD is still necessary.

Previous research indicated that stroke patients with poor collateral circulation were prone to be influenced by BPV (16). Considering the anatomical features of BAD lacking collateral circulation (1, 2), BAD may be vulnerable to systemic blood pressure fluctuations. Therefore, the present study focused on BAD‐related stroke and aimed to evaluate the association between systolic BPV and the 90‐day functional outcome of this specific subtype of stroke.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The BAD‐study was a nationwide, multicenter, prospective, observational cohort study performed in 20 Chinese hospitals from June 2021 to June 2023 (6, 18). We enrolled patients aged 18–80 years within 72 h after the onset of BAD‐related stroke. The BAD was diagnosed based on the radiological criteria, which have been described in detail in our previous research (6). Briefly, BAD‐related stroke manifested as diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) confirmed isolated subcortical infarct in the lenticulostriate artery (LSA) or paramedian pontine artery (PPA) territory with no ≥50% stenosis on the parent artery of the criminal vessel. The infarct lesion should be comma‐like and fan‐shaped, extending from bottom to top coronally or ≥3 layers axially for LSA or extending axially in the pons from deep to ventral for PPA. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and angiographic examinations were conducted for each patient before their inclusion in the study. For the present analysis, patients with complete information on daily blood pressure records were included.

The BAD‐study was approved by the review board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (No. ZS‐2982B). All the participants provided written informed consent.

Data Collection

Demographic and Clinical Data

Baseline data were collected including the demographics (age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index [BMI]), medical history (smoking status, drinking, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and stroke), therapies (antihypertensive therapy, intravenous thrombolysis, dual antiplatelet therapy, use of intravenous tirofiban, glucose‐lowering medications, and lipid‐lowering therapy), clinical assessments (neurological symptoms, stroke severity, and END), laboratory data, and radiological results. The stroke severity was evaluated at admission using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores. END was defined as neurological deterioration within 7 days of symptom onset, including an increase in NIHSS score of ≥4 points, a motor score increase ≥1 point, or ≥3 recurrent stereotyped episodes or progression to persistent deficits in cases of capsule or pontine warning syndromes (6).

Blood Pressure Measurement and Parameters of BPV

A detailed description of blood pressure monitoring was provided in our previously published protocol (18). Briefly, brachial blood pressure was measured in a seated or supine position by trained nurses using an automated device with appropriately sized cuffs, following standard procedures (19) upon arrival at the hospital, at enrollment, within 72 h after enrollment, and during follow‐up. The recorded blood pressure parameters included: blood pressure at arrival, at enrollment, maximum systolic blood pressure (SBP) within 24 h, maximum SBP between 24–48 h, maximum SBP between 48‐72 h, and blood pressure at day 7 post‐enrollment. To minimize potential confounding effects from acute‐phase psychological stress and hospital transfers, we utilized blood pressure data obtained after the first 24 h of enrollment. Therefore, three SBP readings, specifically the maximum within 24–48 h, the maximum within 48–72 h, and the day‐7 SBP, were used for BPV evaluation.

The systolic BPV was evaluated using standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and variation independent of the mean (VIM). The CV (percentages) was calculated as SD/mean × 100 (20). The VIM (unit) was defined as VIM = M x × SD/meanx, where M was the average of mean SBP, and x was derived from the formula: SD = k × meanx (20). While the SD and CV indicated absolute variability and relative variability, respectively, the VIM was independent of mean SBP (20). Moreover, the mean SBP levels (mm Hg) were also assessed by averaging the measured SBP values.

Clinical Outcome

The primary outcome was a 90‐day poor functional prognosis. Patients were followed up at 90 days after enrollment via face‐to‐face interviews or by telephone. The functional prognosis was evaluated using the mRS score by a neurologist blinded to the baseline data. A poor functional outcome was defined as mRS score > 2. The secondary outcome was the presence of END within 7 days after BAD‐related stroke onset.

Statistical Methods

The baseline data were presented according to the clinical outcomes. Continuous variables were displayed as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range for normally and non‐normally distributed data, respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with percentages. To compare data between groups, Student's t‐test (or Mann–Whitney test), Kruskal–Wallis test, and the Chi‐square (χ 2) test (or Fisher's exact test) were performed for continuous and categorical variables as appropriate, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to explore the association between systolic BPV parameters and clinical outcomes. Covariables adjusted in multivariable logistic regression were selected based on established stroke risk factors, prior research findings, and significant associations identified in univariate analyses. The results were displayed as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Moreover, subgroup analyses were performed to examine the robustness of the association. To further eliminate the potential confounding, sensitivity analyses were performed among patients without END and without intravenous thrombolysis. The present study was designed as an exploratory association analysis, which did not presuppose a predictive efficacy test. However, the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and reclassification analyses results were also reported for descriptive completeness and as descriptive measures of model discrimination.

All the statistical analyses were conducted using Stata Statistical Software (version 18.0, Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA), EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats. com, X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, Massachusetts, USA), and statistical software packages R (http://www.R‐project.org, The R Foundation). A two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significance.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 423 eligible patients were included in the analyses (Figure 1). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 423 patients were summarized in Table 1. The median age was 60 (53–68) years, with 70.2% (n = 297) being male. Patients with a poor functional outcome accounted for 13.9% (n = 59) of all participants. Compared with the good outcome group, patients with poor outcomes showed significantly older age, higher NIHSS score at admission, increased incidence of END, and a higher percentage of intravenous tirofiban usage (all p < 0.05). Association Between Systolic BPV and END

The association between systolic BPV and END was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models. The elevated systolic BPV parameters were significantly associated with an increased risk of END (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model, higher systolic BP variability was independently associated with END, with OR = 1.39 for SD, 1.40 for CV, and 1.41 for VIM (all p < 0.05). However, the association between mean SBP and END was not significant.

Association Between Systolic BPV and Functional Outcome

Multivariable logistic regression analyses revealed the association between systolic BPV and the functional outcome of BAD‐related stroke. The results across the three BPV indicators—SD, CV, and VIM of SBP—consistently suggested that elevated systolic BPV was significantly associated with an increased risk of poor functional outcome (Table 3 and Table S2 in the data supplement). In the crude model, the risk for poor outcome in the top tertile of SD, CV, and VIM of SBP increased by 2.95, 2.55, and 2.55 times that of the bottom tertile, respectively (all p < 0.05). When fully adjusted for covariables including age, sex, and NIHSS score at admission, END, history of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, use of intravenous tirofiban, and mean SBP, the top tertile of SD, CV, and VIM of SBP showed 3.10, 2.77, and 2.97 times of poor outcome risk against the bottom tertile, respectively (all p < 0.05). The risk‐increasing trends across the tertiles were significant (all p‐values for trend < 0.05).

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed based on several baseline risk categories, including age, sex, stroke severity, END, criminal artery, hypertension, diabetes, and prior stroke (Figure 2, Figure S1–S2 in the data supplement). The results revealed no significant heterogeneity in the association between systolic BPV and outcomes of BAD‐related stroke (all p for interaction >0.05), except for the subgroup divided by medical history of hypertension. Stronger associations were observed in patients with hypertension (p for interaction <0.05).

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis restricted to patients without END yielded results consistent with those of the overall cohort (Table S3 in the data supplement). Considering that intravenous thrombolysis was more frequent in the patients with the highest tertile of BPV—who may undergo pre‐procedural blood pressure lowering—we also performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients who underwent thrombolysis to minimize confounding from medication. The results consistently supported the positive and independent association between systolic BPV and poor functional outcome (Table S4 in the data supplement).

ROC and Reclassification Analysis

The AUCs for SD, CV, and VIM in discriminating 90‐day poor functional outcome were 0.620, 0.611, and 0.598, respectively (Table S5 in the data supplement). When adding the BPV parameters to clinical risk factors, including age, sex, NIHSS score at admission, END, history of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and use of intravenous tirofiban, the corresponding AUCs were 0.848 for SD, 0.845 for CV, and 0.842 for VIM, respectively. Although the AUC increments were non‐significant, the net reclassification improvement (NRI) suggested significant improvement in risk ordering.

Discussion

In this multicenter, MRI‐based prospective cohort study, we investigated the association between systolic BPV and 90‐day functional outcomes in BAD‐related stroke. A higher BPV was associated with an increased risk of END at 7 days. Elevated systolic BPV, measured by SD, CV, and VIM, was significantly and consistently associated with poor functional outcome, independent of mean SBP level. This association remained consistent across subgroups and was particularly pronounced in patients with hypertension. To our knowledge, our study is the first focusing on the prognostic association between BPV and BAD‐related stroke, suggesting the importance of stabilizing blood pressure during the acute phase of this distinct stroke.

BAD‐related stroke has drawn increasing attention due to its high risk of stroke warning syndromes and END, both linked to poor outcomes (1, 2, 21). Although treatments such as intravenous thrombolysis (3, 22, 23, 24), antiplatelet (25, 26), and anticoagulant therapies (27, 28) have been explored, evidence remains limited or inconsistent. Consequently, identifying new therapeutic strategies and reliable prognostic biomarkers is essential. Previous studies have proposed prognostic markers, such as homocysteine (29), C‐reactive protein (29), vertebral artery hemodynamics (30), and basilar artery dorsal plaque (31), yet results have varied. Our prior multicenter study found only NIHSS at admission, END, and age to be independent predictors of 90‐day outcomes (6). Given the high prevalence of hypertension in BAD (6), interest has grown in BPV as a potential prognostic factor. While BPV has shown predictive value in other cardiovascular diseases (32, 33), the role of BPV in BAD‐related stroke has been undetermined. Our study provided new insights into the association between BPV and functional outcomes in BAD and is the first to demonstrate the prognostic significance of BPV in BAD‐related stroke. Although it remains unclear whether elevated BPV is a direct cause of poor prognosis or merely a marker for patients predisposed to poor outcomes, our findings emphasize the importance of maintaining blood pressure stability and closely monitoring patients with high BPV during the acute phase of BAD‐related stroke.

While the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying BPV and the prognosis of BAD‐related stroke remain to be fully elucidated, there are several potential explanations. First, cerebral autoregulation and collateral circulation are critical for maintaining perfusion after ischemic stroke (34, 35). In BAD‐related stroke, limited collateral supply (1, 2) and impaired autoregulation following ischemic events (35) may increase the susceptibility to blood pressure fluctuations, thereby exacerbating cerebral hypoperfusion and reperfusion injury. Second, increased BPV may reflect autonomic dysfunction, which impairs cerebral perfusion and further disrupts autoregulation, worsening ischemic damage (36, 37). In our subgroup analysis, higher BPV was associated with worse 90‐day functional outcomes among patients with prior hypertension, which may suggest increased vulnerability to short‐term blood pressure fluctuations in this subgroup. A plausible mechanism is that chronic hypertension may impair cerebral autoregulation and increase arterial stiffness (38), which may render patients with hypertension more vulnerable to short‐term blood pressure fluctuations. Moreover, elevated BPV also tends to coexist with adverse vascular conditions—particularly arterial stiffness and atherosclerosis—which are themselves linked to poor outcomes (39, 40) and may further exacerbate vascular injury (41, 42, 43). The bidirectional interplay between BPV and vascular pathology may underlie the stronger prognostic impact observed in hypertension patients. These pathophysiological considerations offer a biologically plausible—yet preliminary—explanation that warrants prospective confirmation.

Our findings are consistent with previous research. Prior studies have demonstrated that BPV holds significant prognostic value in stroke, irrespective of whether it is measured over short‐, medium‐, or long‐term intervals, and regardless of whether blood pressures are measured at fixed or random time points. A large population‐based study demonstrated a significant association between reading‐to‐reading BPV during the subacute phase and 3‐month functional outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke (44). In that study (44), blood pressure was randomly measured during hospitalization, from 72 h of symptom onset to discharge. To further assess BPV using fixed‐interval measurements, Fukuda et al. (20). evaluated day‐by‐day BPV for 2,566 stroke patients from a multicenter, hospital‐based prospective registry. The results consistently demonstrated that increased BPV was significantly correlated with poor functional outcome at 3 months, independent of mean blood pressure (20). Evidence also supports the prognostic value of BPV during both short‐term (within 24–72 h) and longer‐term (day‐by‐day or visit‐to‐visit) (14, 45, 46, 47). Although the prognostic significance of BPV in ischemic stroke is well established, our study focused specifically on patients with BAD, a subtype that presents distinct pathophysiological and prognostic features. The focused design allowed for an in‐depth analysis of BPV in BAD, providing specific insights into this distinct stroke subtype that were unavailable in previous research.

The appropriate strategies for blood pressure intervention after ischemic stroke remain challenging (48). Blood pressure is increased acutely in more than 80% of patients after ischemic stroke onset (49). However, despite the high prevalence of elevated blood pressure, antihypertensive treatments during the acute stage may not reduce the risk of poor functional outcomes or mortality at 90 days (50, 51, 52). While numerous trials have compared different blood pressure goals and medications (50, 51, 52), which failed to find prognostic superiority, previous studies primarily manipulated the mean or absolute blood pressure level rather than the dynamic fluctuation of blood pressure. Research about therapies targeted at lowering BPV is limited. A meta‐analysis showed that calcium channel blockers demonstrate superior efficacy in reducing stroke risk compared to β‐blockers (53). The superior efficacy is believed primarily attributable to the capacity of calcium channel blockers to maintain low systolic BPV in addition to lowering mean SBP (54). However, while reduced BPV under antihypertensive therapy has been shown to play a role in stroke prevention, its potential as a post‐stroke therapeutic target remains speculative. Prospective studies are warranted to specifically determine whether targeted BPV minimization, independent of mean or absolute blood pressure levels, brings a prognostic benefit and to identify practical protocols for achieving blood pressure stability. Our results added new evidence about the association between BPV and the prognosis of a specific stroke subtype. Our study, along with previous research, highlighted the important role of BPV in stroke prognosis and emphasized the need to maintain hemodynamic stability during the acute phase. Although specific guidelines for antihypertensive therapy for BAD are currently scarce, stability‐focused and individualized approaches appear prudent, such as avoiding large short‐term blood pressure fluctuations, optimizing general care (such as ensuring adequate hydration, controlling pain, anxiety, and fever), and close monitoring with gradual titration during antihypertensive therapy. More prospective clinical trials are needed to determine the prognostic benefits and appropriate therapeutic strategies targeting BPV.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of the study is that it represents the first investigation focusing on the relationship between BPV and functional outcomes in BAD‐related stroke. All the BAD‐related stroke patients were diagnosed following strict radiological criteria and enrolled from a multicenter prospective cohort. Our research still has some limitations. First, the blood pressure measurements in our study were randomized with limited records. The infrequent measurements of blood pressure used for evaluating BPV may underestimate the true degree of association (20). The BPV in our study may reflect between‐day fluctuation, yet it was less precise than BPV metrics derived from denser series. Therefore, the BPV findings may be exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Large sample‐sized studies with more frequent and fixed‐timed blood pressure measurements from stroke onset will be helpful to further confirm the correlations. Secondly, the study was conducted among the Chinese population. Whether our findings can be generalized to other populations remains to be determined. Moreover, since the present research was an observational study without protocolized antihypertensive therapy, antihypertensive medication was documented using a structured case‐report form only at enrollment to capture pre‐existing treatment. The in‐hospital antihypertensive management was conducted by clinicians, while the dosage, types, and changes were not recorded. Accordingly, the potential impact of in‐hospital antihypertensive administrations on BPV could not be determined, which may introduce residual confounding. Finally, due to the nature of observational research, the causal relationship between BPV and clinical outcome should be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal randomized controlled trials with interventions targeted at BPV are needed to further clarify the causal relationship.

Conclusion

The present study indicated that systolic BPV is independently associated with END and 90‐day functional outcome in acute BAD‐related stroke. Elevated systolic BPV was significantly associated with poor functional outcome, independently of mean SBP and END. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to identify BPV as a potentially therapeutic prognostic factor in acute BAD‐related stroke, highlighting the importance of stabilizing SBP and providing close care for patients with high BPV in the acute phase.
