Vitamin B6 produced by gut microbiome regulates host behavioral phenotypes through dopaminergic metabolism
Authors: Doyeon Kim, Minshu Li, Thi Ha Nguyen, Yu-Jeong Choi, Sojeong Jang, Minhyeong Kim, Young-Kwang Kim, Min-Kyoo Shin, Arvie Camille V de Guzman, Sunghyouk Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe gut microbiome modulates host neuropathology, but the mechanisms linking specific microbial genes and metabolites to host phenotypes remain poorly defined. Here, we identify microbiome-derived vitamin B6 (VB6) and its biosynthesis gene as key regulators of host dopaminergic homeostasis. Metagenomic analysis of fecal samples from Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients revealed enrichment of biosynthetic pathways for pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP), the active form of VB6, and tyrosine decarboxylase genes. Using E. coli–C. elegans symbiotic models, we demonstrate that the bacterial pdxJ gene, encoding a key enzyme in de novo VB6 synthesis, is essential in regulating host dopaminergic homeostasis. Colonization with pdxJ-deficient bacteria led to reduced host VB6 and dopamine levels, reduced dopaminergic enzyme activity, and altered motor behavior, which were all rescued by VB6 supplementation. In PD-relevant C. elegans models, bacterial PLP biosynthesis modulated α-synuclein aggregation and behavioral deficits associated with human LRRK2 mutations. In mice, colonization with pdxJ-deficient bacteria reduced serum VB6 levels, decreased tyrosine hydroxylase staining in the substantia nigra, and impaired motor coordination, which were rescued by VB6 supplementation. Overall, our results define a bacterial pdxJ–PLP–dopamine axis that links gut microbial metabolism to host dopaminergic phenotypes and suggest bacterial VB6 biosynthesis as a potential modifier of PD risk and a context-dependent therapeutic target.Keywords: C. elegans, vitamin B6, pyridoxal-5-phosphate, gut microbiome, Parkinson’s disease

Introduction

The gut microbiome has emerged as a critical regulator of host physiology beyond its traditional roles in nutrient digestion, extending its influence to immune modulation, metabolic homeostasis, and neurological functions. 1 , 2 Central to these microbiome-host interactions are microbial metabolites, including neurotransmitters, enzyme cofactors, and essential vitamins that cannot be synthesized by hosts. 3 , 4 Among these microbiota-derived compounds, B-group vitamins have gained increasing attention for their neurological roles. Specifically, vitamins B6, B12, folate, and other B-complex vitamins serve as essential cofactors in enzymatic reactions critical for neurotransmitter synthesis, one-carbon metabolism, and redox balance in neuronal tissues. 5-7 In addition, deficiencies in these vitamins have been linked to increased risk of Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and other neurodegenerative disorders. 8 , 9

Parkinson's disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized by progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, leading to motor abnormalities including bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability. 10-12 While genetic mutations in LRRK2, SNCA (encoding α-synuclein), PINK1, and Parkin have been implicated in familial cases, 13 the majority of PD cases are sporadic, suggesting complex environmental and metabolic contributions to disease etiology. 14 Still, sporadic PD cases often exhibit dysfunctions in the same molecular pathways regulated by the familial genes, suggesting shared downstream mechanisms of neurodegeneration. 15 , 16 Recent microbiome research has revealed significant gut dysbiosis in PD patients, with fewer short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria and more pro-inflammatory species. 17 , 18 These shifts support the “gut-brain axis”, where altered metabolism, inflammation, and gut barrier disruption contribute to the PD pathogenesis. 19-21 Of particular interest is the emerging relationship between vitamins supplied by bacteria, such as VB6, and dopaminergic functions. 22 VB6 encompasses a family of interconvertible vitamers, including pyridoxine (PN), pyridoxal (PL), and pyridoxamine (PM), which are phosphorylated to the active form, pyridoxal-5'-phosphate (PLP). 23 PLP acts as a cofactor in over 140 human enzymatic reactions, including key steps in neurotransmitter biosynthesis. 23 It is particularly essential for aromatic amino acid decarboxylase (AADC, or DOPA decarboxylase, DDC), which converts L-dopa to dopamine, and for regulating tyrosine hydroxylase activity. 24 , 25 PLP also supports the synthesis of serotonin and GABA, making it a central regulator of neurotransmitter homeostasis. 26

Interestingly, a paradox has emerged regarding VB6 in Parkinson's disease, particularly with L-dopa therapy. 27 Early clinical observations demonstrated that VB6 supplementation dramatically reduced L-dopa efficacy by enhancing ectopic decarboxylation to dopamine, reducing bioavailability to the brain. 28-31 This led to concerns about VB6 intake in PD patients and the development of peripheral AADC inhibitors such as carbidopa and benserazide. 32 Paradoxically, epidemiological studies suggest higher dietary VB6 intake is associated with reduced PD risk, 5 , 33 and PD patients exhibit lower plasma VB6 levels than controls. 34 Furthermore, VB6 deficiency is linked to elevated homocysteine, increased oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and impaired antioxidant defenses, 35-37 all of which are implicated in PD pathogenesis. These Janus-faced effects of VB6 represent a significant knowledge gap in the relationship between VB6 and PD. Adding another layer of complexity, the gut microbiome produces VB6 and has its own L-dopa metabolism. Recent studies have found that bacterial tyrosine decarboxylase (TDC), particularly in Enterococcus species, converts L-dopa to dopamine in the intestinal lumen, competing with host absorption and potentially reducing L-dopa’s therapeutic efficacy. 38 , 39 Importantly, bacterial tyrosine decarboxylase, like mammalian AADC, is PLP-dependent, raising the possibility that microbiota-derived VB6 may influence both microbial and host dopamine metabolism.

Therefore, despite the growing interest in the relationship between microbiota-derived VB6 and PD, fundamental questions remain unanswered. Specifically, whether and how bacterial VB6 biosynthetic pathways and genes causally affect host VB6 levels and neurological functions are not known. In addition, the mechanistic relationship between VB6 and PD-relevant molecular pathologies, including α-synuclein aggregation and LRRK2-dependent behavioral abnormalities, remains to be elucidated. In this study, we investigated the “microbiome-VB6-dopamine” axis both phenotypically and mechanistically across multiple systems utilizing PD-patient metagenomics, a nematode–bacteria symbiotic system, and a mouse model.

Method

Strains

C. elegans strains and preparation

Caenorhabditis elegans strains were maintained and utilized for experimental analysis under standardized conditions. Wild-type N2 worms were used as controls, while mutant strains CB1112 [cat-2(e112)] and CB1141 [cat-4(e1141)] were employed to assess the impact of catecholamine pathway deficiencies. To model PD pathophysiology, we utilized transgenic strains NL5901 (pkIs2386 [α-synuclein:YFP unc-119(+)]), WLZ1 (wlzIs1 [snb-1p:Hsa-LRRK2 + lin15(+)]), and WLZ3 (wlzIs3 [snb-1p:Hsa-LRRK2(G2019S) + lin15(+)]), each expressing human PD-associated genetic elements.

All C. elegans strains were maintained at 20 °C on nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 as a food source. 40 Strains were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC, University of Minnesota, Twin Cities, MN). Experimental protocols adhered to established guidelines for nematode handling and maintenance to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Bacterial strain

All bacterial strains used in this study were stored as glycerol stocks at −80 °C and revived by incubation in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium at 37 °C prior to use. Escherichia coli K-12 substrains W3110 and MG1655 were obtained from the Korean Collection for Type Culture (KCTC). KEGG annotation indicates that both harbor the vitamin B6/PLP biosynthetic pathways, including a complete pyridoxal phosphate biosynthesis module (M00124). For the knockout strains, we used Keio collection strains, which are derived from the BW25113 substrain. As we used the knockout of all the genes of the PLP pathway, such as epd, pdxB, serC, pdxA and pdxJ, it is self-explanatory that the PLP pathway is in the BW25113 strain. The E. coli Keio Collection was sourced from Dharmacon, and PLP synthesis pathway gene knockout bacteria used for BSR and α-synuclein accumulation assessments were selected using kanamycin, with E. coli K-12 BW25113 serving as the parent strain. To construct plasmids for bacterial overexpression of vitamin B6 pathway genes, the coding sequences of pdxK and pdxJ were obtained from the Escherichia coli K-12 substrain MG1655 reference genome sequence NC_000913.3. Each coding sequence was synthesized and cloned into a pBAD expression plasmid downstream of the araBAD promoter by VectorBuilder. The constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli K-12 BW25113. Transformants were selected on LB agar supplemented with ampicillin. For overexpression, single colonies were inoculated into LB with ampicillin and cultured at 37 °C with shaking to OD600 ~0.5, 41 then induced with L-arabinose (10 mM) for 1 h at 37 °C as previously described. 42 Heat-killed bacteria were prepared by incubating bacterial suspensions at 70 °C for 1 h immediately before use. 43

Basal slowing response assay

For BSR analysis, synchronized L1-stage C. elegans were incubated at 20 °C on NGM plates seeded with distinct bacterial strains until they reached the L4 stage. 44 Additional experimental groups were maintained under identical conditions on NGM plates supplemented with 1 mM pyridoxal. Upon reaching the L4 stage, approximately 30–50 worms were collected and washed with M9 buffer before being transferred to assay plates using M9 buffer. Two NGM plates were used: an empty NGM plate and an NGM plate containing a ring-shaped lawn of E. coli OP50. Worms were placed at the center of each plate and allowed to acclimate for five minutes before their locomotor activity was assessed. Basal slowing response was quantified by measuring body bend frequency over a 20-s period. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the BSR percentage was calculated. 45 The assay was performed using three different C. elegans strains: N2 (wild-type), cat-2, and WLZ3.

%BSR = (Movement in the blank NGM plate) — (Movement in the ring-shaped NGM plate)/(Movement in the blank NGM plate).

Knockdown and mRNA quantification

To compare PLP metabolism based on the relative expression of pyridoxal kinase (pdxK) in C. elegans, modified NGM plates were prepared according to established protocols. 46 E. coli HT115 carrying the L4440 vector, either expressing pdxK dsRNA or serving as an empty vector control, was cultured overnight in LB medium at 37 °C. For RNAi induction, HT115 (DE3) was supplemented with 1 mM IPTG as described in prior studies. 47 A 1 mL suspension of E. coli was spread onto each NGM plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A synchronized population of L1-stage N2 C. elegans was transferred onto RNAi-treated NGM plates and maintained at 20 °C until adulthood. To assess pdxK expression in C. elegans, approximately 1000 worms were cultured on RNAi bacteria from the L1 stage to L4 + 1 d at 20 °C. Worms were then harvested for mRNA extraction and gene expression analysis using RT-qPCR. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR were as follows: pdxK forward: 5’-TGGAGGAGCTGTACGAAGGA-3’, 5’-TCCGAATGAGGCACTGCATT-3’. bas-1 forward: 5’-TCACATGGAAATCGTGCCCA-3’, 5’-GACAGTCGCCAGGAACATGA-3’. Actin-1 was used as an internal reference for normalization with the following primers: forward: 5’-GCTTCAGTGAGGAGGACTGG-3’, 5’-GTCGGTATGGGACAGAAGGA-3’.

LC-MS sample preparation

Approximately 4000 L4-stage worms were collected for the quantification of VB6 and dopamine. Worms were transferred into 1.5 mL tubes and subjected to lysis using a homogenizer for three minutes. Metabolites were extracted via a two-phase extraction method using methanol and chloroform. 48 The upper methanol phase was separated and evaporated using a SpeedVac. For bacterial metabolite extraction, E. coli K-12 substrains were grown in LB medium under identical conditions to an optical density (OD600) of approximately 1.2. Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 20 min), and cell pellets were collected while removing the LB medium. The pellets were homogenized in methanol for 3 min, followed by two-phase extraction and SpeedVac evaporation of the methanol phase. Mouse brain sampling was performed as previously described. 49 For dopamine quantification, samples were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid, whereas VB6 measurements utilized 100 mM ammonium acetate. 50 , 51 Standards used for comparative analysis included pyridoxal hydrochloride (Sigma–Aldrich), pyridoxal-5-phosphate hydrate (Sigma–Aldrich), and dopamine hydrochloride (Sigma–Aldrich). For mouse blood sampling, orbital blood collection was performed, followed by centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 6 min) to separate serum. A 50 µL aliquot of serum was subjected to two-phase extraction using methanol and chloroform, followed by SpeedVac drying. Samples were dissolved in either 0.1% formic acid in water or 100 mM ammonium acetate prior to analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Dopamine mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a Q-Exactive Focus Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The liquid chromatography (LC) system used was a Waters Acquity UPLC. Mobile phase buffers consisted of acetonitrile (Sigma–Aldrich) and methanol (Sigma–Aldrich), with ammonium acetate (Sigma–Aldrich) and formic acid (TCI) added to the solvent system. Sample separation was achieved using a Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm) in combination with a BEH C18 VanGuard Pre-column (2.1 × 5 mm, 1.7 µm). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the flow rate was set at 0.2 mL/min. The solvent gradient was as follows: solvent B 0–2 min, 0%; 2–9 min, 10%; 9–11 min, 95%; 11–13 min, 95%; 13–15 min, 0%; and 0% was maintained until 18 min before the next sample injection (10 µL). 50 Mass spectrometer conditions included positive ion mode using SIM mode, with dopamine detection at an m/z of 154.0. Additional parameters were set as follows: capillary temperature, 250 °C; spray voltage, 2.50 kV; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 400 °C; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10; sweep gas flow rate, 2; S-lens RF level, 50; resolution, 35,000.

For the quantification of pyridoxal-5-phosphate (PLP) and other VB6 species, an Agilent 6400 Series Triple Quadrupole (QQQ) mass spectrometer was used in Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode. PLP was measured in positive mode with an optimized mass transition of m/z 248.1 > 150 at a collision energy of 12 V. Instrument settings were as follows: gas temperature, 300 °C; sheath gas flow, 12; sheath gas heater, 350 °C; capillary voltage, 4,000 V. The LC system utilized was an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UPLC. Chromatographic separation was performed using the Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column with a VanGuard Pre-column, under a flow rate of 0.18 mL/min at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (5 mM ammonium acetate in water, adjusted to pH 3 with acetic acid) and solvent B (100% methanol, Sigma–Aldrich). The solvent gradient was: 0–7 min, 1% B; 7–14 min, 20%; 14–18 min, 40%; 18–18.10 min, 70%; 18.10–21 min, 90%; 21–24 min, 100%; 24–24.10 min, 1%; and 1% was maintained until 28 min before the next sample injection (10 µL). 51

Animals

To evaluate behavioral effects modulated by gut microbiota, 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice were used, with 8 mice per experimental group. Mice received antibiotics via oral administration through drinking water. The treatment involved an antibiotic cocktail (vancomycin 0.5 mg/mL, ampicillin 1 mg/mL, kanamycin 1 mg/mL, and metronidazole 1 mg/mL) for 1 d, followed by kanamycin (1 mg/mL) in drinking water for an additional 1 week. 52 Microbiota depletion was evaluated by qPCR targeting the bacterial 16S rRNA gene using total DNA extracted from fecal samples. 53 The primer sequences used for qPCR were as follows: the universal bacterial 16S rRNA primer 518F 5’-CCAGCAGCCGCGGTAATACG-3’, 800R 5’-TACCAGGGTATCTAATCC-3’. Mice were maintained on a pyridoxine-deficient diet (Envigo, TD.180172) for 6 weeks to restrict dietary intake of VB6 precursors. This dietary condition was used to minimize exogenous sources of PLP and its precursors (PN, PL, and PM), thereby allowing for more accurate assessment of microbial contributions to host VB6 metabolism. For gut colonization with knockout bacteria, mice were administered by oral gavage with 1.6 × 10 8 CFU/0.2 mL of bacteria twice per week. 52 To assess the effects of gut microbiota on behavior, an additional group of mice was supplied with 2 mg/kg pyridoxal in drinking water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Seoul National University (Approval No. SNU-240422-2) and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Brain sample preparation

Mouse brain samples were obtained after transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and subsequently post-fixed at 4 °C overnight. The preserved tissues underwent a sequential dehydration process using graded ethanol concentrations, followed by xylene clearing. 54 Paraffin embedding was carried out with an automated tissue processor. Substantia nigra and striatum sections of 4 μm thickness were sliced using a microtome and mounted onto glass slides for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical assessments.

Immunohistochemistry

Sliced brain sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series from 100% to 50%. The sections were washed with TBS containing 0.025% Triton X-100, followed by incubation with a primary antibody against tyrosine hydroxylase (TH; 1:1000, AB152, Sigma–Aldrich) diluted in 1% BSA in TBS, overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBS containing 0.025% Triton X-100, sections were incubated with Real EnVision/HRP secondary antibody (Dako, K5007) for 1 h at room temperature. Chromogenic signals were developed using DAB substrate solution (DaKo, K5007), and slides were rinsed with TBS followed by running tap water. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining, and sections were rinsed in distilled water. Tissues were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol and xylene series and mounted using Permount mounting solution (SP15-500, Fisher Scientific). Slides were air-dried at room temperature until microscopic analysis. The intensity of immunoreactivity was measured as integrated density using ImageJ (NIH), following background subtraction.

Parkinson’s disease model assay

Alpha-synuclein accumulation

To assess the molecular pathology of Parkinson’s disease induced by gut microbiota-derived PLP, the transgenic C. elegans strain NL5901, which expresses α-synuclein: YFP, was utilized. To examine the impact of various gut bacterial strains on efficacy, we monitored and analyzed alpha-synuclein aggregation in NL5901 worms using a previously established protocol with minor adjustments. 55 Synchronized NL5901 worms were grown at 20 °C on NGM plates seeded with E. coli K-12 BW25113 knockout strains (∆pdxK, ∆pdxJ, and ∆pdxA) until L4 stage + 2 d. Fifteen randomly selected worms were transferred onto a glass slide pre-coated with 2% agarose. A 1 µL drop of 10 mM levamisole was applied to immobilize the worms, and a coverslip was placed over them. Confocal imaging was performed using a Leica TCS8 microscope to monitor α-synuclein expression and YFP signal intensity. Quantification of visible spherical-shaped aggregates was conducted using ImageJ software. No additional plugins were applied for protein aggregation analysis. The experiment was repeated three times for statistical reliability.

Mouse Rotarod test

To assess motor coordination and balance, the Rotarod test was conducted once per week using a Rotarod (HUAYON). The test was performed under an accelerating protocol, starting at a speed of 4 rpm, with a gradual acceleration rate of 0.3 rpm/s. 56 , 57 Each mouse underwent three consecutive trials per session, with a minimum inter-trial interval of 15 min to minimize fatigue. The latency to fall (time spent on the rod before falling), the speed at which the mouse fell, and the total distance traveled on the rotating rod were recorded as measures of motor performance. The average of the three trials was used for statistical analysis.

Data analysis

All graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.0 (graphpad.com). Gene expression data from real-time PCR and peak intensity data from LC–MS/MS were expressed as mean ± standard error (SEM) from three independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA. A p-value of 0.05 was indicated by an asterisk, while “ns” denoted non-significant differences.

Results

PLP synthesis between Parkinson’s disease patients and healthy controls

Previous studies have suggested that vitamin B6 (VB6) may impair L-dopa efficacy in patients with Parkinson’s disease by promoting its peripheral conversion. 29-31 , 58 This effect is primarily mediated by PLP, the active form of VB6, which acts as a cofactor for the enzyme responsible for converting L-dopa to dopamine in peripheral tissues, thereby potentially influencing symptom severity and treatment response.

Building on this, recent evidence suggests that disruptions in the gut microbiota are associated with Parkinson’s disease. Given that PD patients exhibit distinct alterations in gut microbial community composition, 59 , 60 we hypothesized that these microbial shifts may contribute to changes in VB6 metabolism, potentially impacting disease progression and responsiveness to L-dopa treatment. To investigate a possible link between host VB6 level and gut microbial metabolism, we re-analyzed published fecal metagenomic datasets from PD patients and matched healthy controls, 59 with a focus on microbial pathways involved in PLP biosynthesis.

Using two complementary microbiome analysis frameworks, MaAsLin2 and ANCOM-BC, 61 , 62 we identified a consistent and significant enrichment of PLP-associated biosynthetic pathways in the microbiome of PD patients. Notably, genes encoding enzymes involved in the de novo biosynthesis of VB6 exhibited increased relative abundance in PD samples (Figure 1a–c). For example, elevated levels were observed for KGO K00275 (pyridoxamine 5'-phosphate oxidase) and K03474 (pyridoxine 5-phosphate synthase), indicating a microbial community primed for elevated PLP production. Metagenomic functional profiling further revealed a parallel upregulation of PLP-dependent enzymatic modules (Figure 1d).

As PLP is a critical cofactor for tyrosine decarboxylase (TDC), catalyzing the production of dopamine from L-dopa (Figure 1e), 63 we next analyzed the abundance of bacterial tdc genes in the PD patient microbiome. The result showed that bacterial tdc gene abundance was significantly upregulated in PD samples in both analysis methods (Figure 1f). This indicates that elevated tdc gene abundance is consistent with prior findings 64 and suggests that interaction with bacterial PLP may enhance peripheral consumption of L-dopa by lowering its brain availability and overall treatment effects.

While these results highlight a potentially detrimental role of microbial PLP biosynthesis during L-dopa therapy, the functional impact of PLP on fundamental PD pathology may vary across biological systems and contexts. To that end, we next examined PLP’s role in experimental models under different physiological constraints.

Bacterial PLP regulates host dopaminergic motor behavior

Based on the above, we hypothesized that individual bacterial strains may differ in their capacity to synthesize VB6, despite possessing complete PLP biosynthesis pathways, and therefore may differentially modulate PLP-dependent enzymatic activity. To test this, we employed an E. coli (bacteria)- C. elegans (host) model with E. coli OP50, E. coli K-12, and three E. coli K-12 substrains (W3110, MG1655, and BW25113) as gut microbiota. This is an established model for controlled gut microbiome manipulation and in vivo assessment of neuronal functions. 65 , 66 We evaluated strain-specific effects on host motor behavior using the basal slowing response (BSR) assay, a dopamine-dependent motor function readout. 44 Worms exhibited distinct BSR depending on the bacterial strain diet, with reduced BSR in worms fed with W3110 and MG1655 compared to OP50. Notably, supplementation with 1 mM PL, a VB6 and precursor of PLP, significantly rescued the BSR percentage, suggesting that the differential effects may be due to variation in bacterial PLP biosynthesis (Figure 2a). To examine this relationship, we quantified bacterial PLP levels via LC–MS/MS (Supplementary Figure 1b). Bacterial PLP content, varying among strains, exhibited the same pattern as the BSR, with the W3110, MG1655 strains showing the lower level and the BW25113 the higher level (Figure 2a, b). This supports the idea that differences in bacterial PLP synthesis may contribute to host dopaminergic behavior. As the host’s PLP synthesis can also modulate the dopaminergic functions, we asked if the bacterial PLP synthesis can help when the host’s PLP synthesis is not sufficient. To that end, RNAi-mediated knockdown (KD) of pdxK, encoding pyridoxal kinase that converts PL to PLP, 67 was performed (Supplementary Figure 2a). Silencing of pdxK significantly decreased both BSR and host PLP levels, whereas PL supplementation restored both PLP levels and BSR (Figure 2c and Supplementary Figure 2b), demonstrating that bacteria-derived PLP can compensate functionally when the host PLP synthesis is compromised (Figure 2c). The dopaminergic behavioral recovery in the pdxK-KD host by PL supplementation seems to be due to the restoration of PLP-dependent host dopamine-producing dopa decarboxylase (DDC) enzyme activity, as we observed lower dopamine levels in the pdxK-KD host (Supplementary Figure 2c) and E. coli lacks the DDC activity. Overall, these findings show the critical roles of bacterial PLP synthesis in regulating host dopaminergic functions in C. elegans.

Live bacterial colonization enables PLP transfer to the host

As our data suggested a possible link between bacterial PLP synthesis and host DDC activity, we tested if the bacteria used as a food source colonize the host gut. To that end, we transformed E. coli BW25113 to express an mCherry fluorescent reporter, enabling visualization of bacterial localization within the intestine of a live host. Fluorescence imaging showed that bacterial colonies were primarily concentrated in the anterior region of the intestine (Figure 2d). Similarly, bacterial colonization was observed in cat-2 mutant worms, which lack tyrosine hydroxylase and cannot endogenously synthesize L-dopa, 44 confirming that both wild-type and dopamine-deficient hosts supported gut microbiota colonization (Supplementary Figure 2d). To investigate whether active colonization is necessary for PLP transfer and function, worms were fed with heat-killed mCherry BW25113 bacteria. Compared to live bacteria, heat-killed strains exhibited substantially reduced colonization efficiency (Figure 2d and e), and this was accompanied by a significant decrease in host PLP levels in both N2 and cat-2 mutants (Figure 2f and Supplementary Figure 2e). Together, these results suggest that gut colonization by live PLP-producing bacteria seems to be required for affecting host PLP availability regardless of host tyrosine metabolism, reinforcing the functional importance of microbiota-host proximity in neuroactive vitamin exchange.

PLP regulates the host dopamine biosynthetic enzyme

Given the suggested modulation of host DDC enzyme activity by bacteria-derived PLP, we next sought to further confirm this interaction by using genetic models on host dopamine synthesis pathways. First, the cat-2 mutant model was employed to evaluate whether PLP modulates host DDC activity. We assessed the BSR recovery through DDC-generated dopamine upon supplementation with PL and L-dopa. Treatment with PL significantly increased BSR, producing effects comparable to L-dopa treatment, while co-supplementation with both PL and L-dopa resulted in a near complete recovery in BSR (Figure 2g). Given these behavioral observations, we further sought to determine whether these effects were accompanied by corresponding changes in dopamine biosynthesis that reflect the actual enzyme activity. Quantification of dopamine levels in worms treated with PL and L-dopa significantly increased the dopamine level (Figure 2h), showing that both are important in dopamine biosynthesis within the cat-2 model (Figure 2h). However, in cat-2 subjected to bas-1 RNAi (Supplementary Figure 2f), which encodes the PLP-dependent DDC responsible for dopamine synthesis, 44 neither PL nor L-dopa alone was sufficient to restore BSR (Figure 2i). A partial recovery upon the combined supplementation with both PL and L-dopa might be due to the residual DDC activity upon bas-1 RNAi. Together, these findings demonstrate that bacterial PLP enhances host dopamine synthesis by promoting host enzymatic activity, thereby influencing behavior and potentially linking microbial metabolism to neurodegenerative diseases like Parkinson’s.

Bacterial de novo synthesis pathways contribute to PLP and its precursors

With the effects of bacteria on PLP synthesis on host dopaminergic activity, we wanted to find out the identity of the bacterial genes that can ultimately affect host DDC enzymatic activity. At this point, the quantitative contribution of bacterial genes to VB6 production and their impact on host dopaminergic function remains poorly defined due to the complexity of VB6 metabolic pathways (Figure 3a). Given this, we focused on bacterial genes involved in the synthesis of PLP and its close VB6 species, including pyridoxal (PL), pyridoxine (PN), and pyridoxamine (PM), in modulating host enzyme activity. These VB6 species are precursors for PLP and are synthesized through the de novo pathway via an array of genes and then converted to PLP through the salvage pathway (Figure 3a). 68 Notably, as PLP is largely unable to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), the cerebral availability of PLP is likely determined by the levels of its precursors (PL, PN, and PM), which can more readily traverse the BBB and then be converted to PLP in the brain of higher animals. 67 , 69 , 70 This underscores the need to evaluate not only the microbial genes involved in PLP synthesis but also the levels of the diffusible precursors, which can influence host neurochemical homeostasis.

We therefore assessed the metabolic contribution of bacterial PLP biosynthetic genes by using targeted knockouts (KO) of key enzymes involved in de novo and salvage pathways and then quantifying PLP and precursor levels in these knockout bacteria (Figure 3b–e). Bacteria with KO in the salvage pathway gene pdxK exhibited reduced PLP levels. In comparison, precursor levels did not exhibit consistent changes, with PL, PN, and PM showing increased, stable, and decreased levels, respectively, indicating that the conversion to PLP was selectively impaired (Figure 3b–e). In comparison, KO in a de novo pathway gene pdxJ 71 led to a significant reduction in PLP levels, accompanied by a significant decrease in all precursors that can be metabolized to PLP by the host. Supplementation with PL greatly increased the PLP level in the pdxJ-KO strain, while its level remained unchanged in a salvage pathway gene pdxK-KO strain. These results underscore the essential function of VB6 de novo synthesis genes, particularly pdxJ, in supplying VB6, including both PLP and its precursors.

Bacterial PLP synthesis genes regulate host behavior

With the roles of bacterial PLP synthesis genes in host DDC activity, we moved on to assess the physiological relevance of those genes in the host. For that, we fed wild-type C. elegans with various mutant bacteria for PLP synthesis pathways and evaluated motor behavior using the BSR assay. Hosts colonized with pdxK-KO bacteria exhibited no significant differences in BSR or PLP levels compared to controls, suggesting that one salvage pathway deficiency could be compensated by other salvage pathways or de novo VB6 synthesis (Figure 3f, g). 72 In contrast, hosts colonized with bacteria deficient in any one of the five de novo VB6 biosynthetic genes, including pdxJ and pdxA, 71 exhibited a significant reduction in BSR, suggesting the essential roles of de novo VB6 biosynthesis in maintaining host motor function (Figure 3f). In addition, supplementation with PL successfully rescued BSR in all of these groups (Figure 3f) with consistent recoveries in PLP levels (Figure 3g), showing that PL-derived PLP directly contributes to host behavior.

To further confirm that these host behavioral effects are due to the modulation of host dopamine level by de novo VB6 synthesis genes in bacteria, we quantified the dopamine level in C. elegans fed with pdxJ-KO bacteria. Worms colonized with pdxJ-KO bacteria exhibited a large reduction in dopamine levels, which was restored upon PL supplementation (Figure 3h). This trend paralleled the behavioral deficits observed in pdxJ-KO in BSR assays (Figure 3f), suggesting that the de novo VB6 synthesis gene, pdxJ, can affect host behavior by modulating host dopamine production. Combined with the connection between pdxJ and PLP production shown in previous sections and the established role of host DDC enzyme in dopamine production, our results suggest that bacterial PLP synthesis gene, i.e., pdxJ, can affect host DDC enzyme activity, resulting in the modulation of the dopaminergic behavior.

The effects of the pdxJ gene in various C. elegans PD models

With the bacterial de novo VB6 biosynthesis genes involved in host dopamine synthesis and behavior, we next sought to evaluate their impact in actual PD models. As the first model, we examined their effects on NL5901 transgenic worms expressing human α-synuclein, a hallmark pathological feature of PD. 73 Worms were colonized with PLP synthesis-KO bacterial strains, and α-synuclein aggregation in the host was quantified. Notably, deletion of genes in the de novo pathway, including pdxJ, resulted in a marked increase in α-synuclein aggregation (Figure 4a and b). In contrast, deletion of pdxK, a salvage pathway gene, did not induce notable changes in aggregation, consistent with the BSR and host PLP levels observed in previous experiments (see Figure 3f and g). These results suggest that pdxJ-KO bacteria may exacerbate α-synuclein pathology in the host, in addition to modulating host BSR and dopamine metabolism.

In another PD-relevant cat-2 background model, we tested whether bacterial PLP could enhance the BSR. As this model has a low baseline BSR, the worsening by KO bacteria would afford limited dynamic range for further reduction. 44 Therefore, we employed an overexpression (OE) bacterial strain to assess the BSR rescue. We first confirmed that overexpression of pdxK and pdxJ genes could substantially increase bacterial PLP levels (Figure 4c). In the cat-2 model, colonization with bacteria overexpressing pdxK and pdxJ led to a significant increase in BSR (Figure 4d), accompanied by elevated dopamine levels in the worms (Figure 4e). Unlike the results with the KO strains in the WT worms in the previous section, those of OE strains are largely unaffected by compensation effects from alternative pathways, 41 which can be largely expected.

Then, we turned to yet another model, LRRK2 (WLZ3), which exhibits behavioral deficits and locomotor dysfunction with the hyperactive human LRRK2 G2019S mutation. This is an established PD model for rescue experiments targeting dopamine-associated pathways 74-76 as in the cat-2 model. In the LRRK2 (WLZ3) C. elegans worms, we assessed the effect of PLP on BSR by supplementing PL and comparing its efficacy to L-dopa. Supplementation with PL significantly enhanced BSR, producing an effect comparable to L-dopa (Figure 4f). We also observed synergy between PL and L-dopa. We then evaluated the effects of bacterial OE of either pdxK or pdxJ. LRRK2 worms colonized with pdxK- or pdxJ-OE bacteria exhibited a significant increase in BSR (Figure 4g). Consistently, dopamine measurements revealed elevated levels in LRRK2 worms colonized with the OE strains (Figure 4h), suggesting that bacterial PLP biosynthesis enhances dopaminergic signaling in the LRRK2-associated PD model. Overall, our results in different PD worm models showed that bacterial PLP synthesis genes can modulate disease-related pathways in those strains.

Bacterial PLP biosynthetic genes modulate serum VB6 in mice

With C. elegans lacking compartmentalized nervous systems, the above results may need to be confirmed in the context of mammalian systems, and, therefore, we sought to investigate the effects of gut bacteria-derived PLP in a higher-order organism. To this end, we assessed the impact of bacterial PLP biosynthesis using a mouse model re-colonized with either WT or pdxJ-KO bacteria after antibiotic treatment to deplete existing gut microbiota 52 (Supplementary Figure 3a). Additionally, the effect of PL-supplementation was tested in both groups to assess the role of PLP precursor (Figure 5a). For all groups, we used VB6 depleted chow to see the effect of PL supplementation more clearly. In the PL-supplemented group, the WT-colonized mice exhibited a slight increase in body weight, but, the body weight values of all four groups fell within the normal range 77 (Supplementary Figure 3b). No weight difference was observed between the WT and pdxJ-KO bacteria-colonized groups without PL-supplementation.

To assess the availability of bacteria-derived PLP precursors to the brain, we measured serum levels of its precursors (PL and PN). Levels of these precursors were significantly reduced in mice colonized with pdxJ-KO bacteria (Figure 5b and c), mirroring the patterns observed in PLP-synthesis gene KO bacteria (see Figure 3b and d), and suggesting that gut microbiota influence vitamin status in mammalian host serum. 78 This reduction in circulating VB6 may indicate a diminished supply of the precursors that can be converted to PLP in the brain, given the limited BBB permeability of PLP and the established roles of these precursors in maintaining brain PLP levels. 67 , 69 , 70 Importantly, PL-supplementation rescued the decrease in serum VB6 levels caused by the colonization of the pdxJ-KO bacteria. (Figure 5b and c), This shows that bacterial de novo VB6 synthesis genes contribute to the availability of PLP precursors in mouse serum and may ultimately modulate brain dopaminergic function.

Bacterial VB6 synthesis regulates mouse behavior via the dopaminergic pathway

To see the actual physiological effects of modulation of serum VB6 levels by de novo synthesis-deficient gut bacteria colonization, we looked at the brain tissues of the animals used above. Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to assess the expression of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), a marker of dopaminergic neurons, 79 in the striatum and substantia nigra (Figure 5d, Supplementary Figure 4a), the latter being the key region involved in dopaminergic neuron and motor control. 80

Between mice colonized with WT and pdxJ-KO bacteria, TH intensity in the striatum remained unchanged between groups (Supplementary Figure 4a and b). In contrast, a significant reduction was observed in the substantia nigra of mice colonized with pdxJ-KO bacteria (Figure 5d and e). PLP measurements from whole-brain slices revealed decreased PLP levels in these mice (Supplementary Figure 5a), most probably due to the reduced VB6 supply from blood, resulting in decreased PLP synthesis in the brain. This also indicates the regional sensitivity of the substantia nigra to lower PLP levels. Additionally, PL supplementation had no significant effect in the WT-colonized mice but increased the TH staining density in the pdxJ-KO colonization group, resulting in the normalization of the TH density in that group (Figure 5d and e). Furthermore, PLP and dopamine levels were also normalized upon PL supplementation in the pdxJ-KO colonized mice (Supplementary Figure 5a and b). These suggest that PLP synthesized in the brain from bacteria-derived PLP precursors influences dopamine production and dopaminergic neuron integrity in the substantia nigra. 81 , 82 To exclude the possibility that the reduced TH staining in the pdxJ-KO colonization group may be due to non-specific inflammation, we assessed GFAP and Iba1 staining (Supplementary Figure 6a). Neither marker showed significant changes in the striatum or substantia nigra (Supplementary Figure 6b–e), indicating that the loss of TH in the substantia nigra likely reflects impaired dopaminergic metabolism due to PLP deficiency 83 , 84 rather than nonspecific inflammatory responses in the brain.

Given the role of the substantia nigra in regulating host motor functions, we also assessed mouse behavior to evaluate the functional contribution of bacteria-derived VB6. Motor function evaluated for 6 weeks using the rotarod test showed no significant changes during the first week. However, at week 6, mice colonized with pdxJ-KO bacteria showed a significant decline in motor performance compared with WT-colonized ones, as evidenced by reduced drop speed, distance, and shorter latency to fall (Figure 5f–h, Supplementary Figure 7a–c). Notably, the PL supplementation rescued the motor function decline in the pdxJ-KO colonization group (Supplementary Figure 7a–c), indicating that an exogenous PLP precursor rescues behavioral defects linked to reduced VB6 from bacteria.

Taken together, these results reveal that PLP synthesized from bacteria-derived VB6 is essential for maintaining dopaminergic neuronal integrity in the mouse brain, particularly within the substantia nigra, and plays a critical role in regulating host motor behavior. These findings highlight the importance of sustained bacterial VB6 biosynthesis in supporting host enzyme activity in the brain and suggest its potential relevance to the pathophysiology of Parkinson’s disease.

Discussion

Previous studies have observed apparent associations between gut microbial VB6 and host Parkinson’s disease physiology. 35 , 85 Here, we provide direct experimental evidence that specific bacterial genes controlling PLP biosynthesis, most notably pdxJ, can shape host neurotransmitter homeostasis. The gene was identified with E. coli as a model gut bacterium, which has relevance to our purpose for the following reasons. First, it is a representative species containing pdxJ and is almost universally present in the human adult gut microbiome, supporting relevance to PD susceptibility in the general population. Second, increases in Enterobacteriaceae, including E.coli, have been reported in PD, and their abundance correlates with the severity of motor dysfunction in PD. 86 Third, gene-level mechanistic insight, a major focus of the study, requires a single species isogenic system, as the use of multiple species would introduce species-dependent variables that confound interpretation. Admittedly, not all gut bacteria use pdxJ for VB6 de novo synthesis, and many species, particularly within the dominant Firmicutes phylum, rely on pdx1-dependent pathways. Nevertheless, structural homology and mechanistic similarities between pdxJ and pdx1 have been noted, indicating convergent evolution. 68 Therefore, it is plausible that the de novo VB6 biosynthesis may have similar implications in bacterial species that use analogous genes. Furthermore, the emerging keystone species concept supports the mechanistic use of E. coli, despite its lower abundance compared to Firmicutes. 87 Even low-abundance taxa can exert disproportionately large effects on host physiology through specialized metabolic or immunomodulatory functions. For example, experimental depletion of rare taxa has been shown to disrupt mucosal immunity more strongly than eliminating dominant species, underscoring the functional weight of metabolically influential but numerically minor species. 88 Another consideration on the bacterial model is strain-level metabolic heterogeneity. Importantly, this is observed even among strains of E. coli that possess a complete PLP biosynthesis pathway in our study. Consistent with our results showing different VB6 production among E. coli substrains, previous studies noted strain-dependent metabolic activities. 89 Therefore, although species-level metagenomics has been widely used in microbiome analysis, the simple presence of an enzyme gene in a species should not be equated with metabolic activities or functions, and experimental validation at the strain level is highly warranted.

Early pre-carbidopa era studies showed that VB6 supplementation reduced L-dopa efficacy by enhancing its peripheral decarboxylation. 29-31 , 90 While this effect was initially attributed to host physiology, recent work has extended the effect to the gut microbiome, implicating the bacterial TDC enzyme. 38 , 39 Our results provide deeper insights by showing that bacterial PLP biosynthesis, the essential cofactor for TDC, facilitates this antagonism. Therefore, microbial metabolic capacity constitutes a significant therapeutic liability during L-dopa treatment. Conversely, however, our experimental data reveal that this same bacterial pathway serves a previously underappreciated protective function in the absence of drug therapy. We found that microbial VB6 de novo synthesis supports systemic and brain VB6 homeostasis, thereby sustaining dopaminergic integrity and motor functions. These indicate that bacterial VB6 production can buffer the host against micronutrient scarcity, extending the microbiota’s role beyond passive nutrient provision to active participation in neurochemical homeostasis.

This context-dependent duality, antagonistic during therapy yet protective in homeostasis, may at least partially help resolve the long-standing “vitamin B6 paradox” in PD. Notably, our findings align with clinical reports linking low PLP levels to neurotoxic shifts in the kynurenine pathway and increased dopaminergic vulnerability, 91 as well as epidemiological evidence that high VB6 intake reduces PD risk. 5 Taken together, bacterial VB6 functions as a ‘Janus-faced’ determinant whose impact depends on the host’s physiological and therapeutic states. For healthy individuals or treatment-naïve PD patients, it may defend against neurodegeneration, whereas in PD patients on L-dopa, it can compromise pharmacokinetic efficacy.

These human implications of our systems and results are worth further discussion. Previous studies have independently linked VB6 status and gut microbial dysbiosis to PD susceptibility. Higher dietary VB6 intake has been associated with reduced PD risk, 5 , 33 whereas PD patients show lower circulating VB6 levels than healthy controls. 34 In parallel, PD has been repeatedly associated with alterations in gut microbial composition and function, 18 , 60 although the mechanisms linking these microbial changes to disease biology remain unclear. Our findings connect these observations by suggesting that microbial VB6 metabolism may affect host PLP availability and dopamine-related pathways, completing the three-partite relationship. Because humans cannot synthesize VB6 de novo, host VB6 status depends on external sources, including diet and potentially VB6 production by gut bacteria. 78 Therefore, variation in microbial VB6 biosynthetic and salvage capacity may influence the availability of microbiota-derived VB6 and consequently host PLP-dependent metabolism. In this context, our human metagenomic analysis showing enrichment of microbial PLP biosynthesis-related genes and bacterial tdc genes in PD fecal microbiomes suggests that the PD-associated microbiome may influence both dopaminergic homeostasis and L-dopa pharmacokinetic efficacy. From a translational perspective, these findings position microbial VB6 production as a dynamic therapeutic target. Future precision strategies may need to address bacterial VB6 synthesis or VB6 availability in at-risk or treatment-naïve individuals, while selectively limiting the microbial PLP-TDC to optimize outcomes in patients under chronic L-dopa therapy. This provides the human context for interpreting the worm and mouse findings below.

We showed that the bacterial VB6 biosynthetic gene pdxJ modulates PD-related phenotypes in two distinct C. elegans models. This suggests that microbial VB6 may act through multiple pathways, and some of the possibilities merit further discussion. In the LRRK2 model (WLZ3), expressing the human LRRK2 variant (G2019S) causes significant dopamine deficiency and behavioral deficits. 74 Consistent with the rescues observed with previous dopamine supplementation 74 and our PL and L-dopa treatment, pdxJ overexpression restored dopamine levels and reversed behavioral dysfunctions. Since LRRK2 kinase hyperactivity drives these pathology, 76 bacterial VB6 likely mitigates adverse phenotypes by modulating LRRK2-dependent pathways. In the α-synuclein (NL5901) model, bacterial pdxJ deficiency increased age-dependent α-synuclein aggregation. Given that dopamine inhibits alpha synuclein fibrilization, either through adduct formation or conformational changes, 92-95 the reduced VB6 levels from pdxJ deficiency likely compromised the dopamine synthesis, thereby promoting α-synuclein aggregation. The results from these systems propose VB6 as a mechanistic antagonist tightly linked to the disease-relevant pathways. Beyond these specific pathways, VB6 may also function as a broad physiological antagonist or antioxidant, mitigating harmful effects in the PD models. For example, as a cofactor for kynurenine aminotransferase (KAT), PLP can redirect kynurenine metabolism toward the neuroprotective kynurenic acid (KA) away from neurotoxic quinolinic acid (QA). 96-98 Additionally, PLP facilitates the conversion of homocysteine to cysteine, a constituent of glutathione that provides protection against oxidative stress commonly present in PD neurons. 99 , 100 In these general mechanisms, VB6 may rescue the PD phenotypes in the LRRK2 model in a kinase-independent manner. In our mouse results, the data align with previous reports that the substantia nigra is more sensitive than the striatum to fluctuations in dopamine levels. 81 , 82 Intestinal colonization with the pdxJ-KO strain significantly reduced TH expression in the substantia nigra and impaired motor coordination, yet neither GFAP nor Iba1 levels were elevated. This inflammation-independent mechanism of VB6 is consistent with a previous rheumatoid arthritis study, 101 and supports a model in which bacteria-derived VB6 deficiency primarily impairs local dopaminergic metabolism rather than triggering neuroinflammatory cascades. Although low PLP levels have been associated with inflammation in other chronic diseases, 102 , 103 our findings suggest an inflammation-independent impairment of dopaminergic function. Together, these places VB6 at the intersection of microbial metabolism and neuronal homeostasis, acting as a metabolic determinant of dopaminergic resilience rather than an immune modulator.

Although the present study focuses on PD, the microbiome-VB6 relationship may also be relevant to other neurodegenerative movement disorders, particularly Huntington’s disease. HD differs from PD in genetic cause and primary pathology, but both disorders involve motor dysfunction, basal ganglia involvement, disrupted neurotransmitter metabolism, including dopaminergic changes, oxidative stress, and emerging gut–brain axis alterations. 104 Human HD studies have reported gut dysbiosis, and independent metabolomic and proteomic studies have described altered PLP-related metabolism, including reduced pyridoxal kinase. 104 Together, these reports suggest that microbiota-associated PLP metabolism, kynurenine pathway regulation, redox balance, and neurotransmitter homeostasis may represent overlapping processes between PD and HD. However, the L-dopa/TDC component of the Janus effect is likely specific to PD therapy. In HD, microbial VB6 metabolism would more likely act through broader neurometabolic support rather than L-dopa pharmacokinetics. Whether microbiota-derived VB6/PLP metabolism modifies HD-related neurometabolic phenotypes remains an interesting future research direction.

In this study, our findings define microbial VB6 metabolism as a context-dependent regulator of host dopaminergic function and PD pathology. By connecting bacterial pdxJ activity with PLP availability and neuronal integrity across species, we uncover a mechanistic link between gut microbial cofactors and host neurometabolic homeostasis. The overall workflow of the study is presented in the supplementary section (Supplementary Figure 8). The dual nature of bacterial PLP, protective under physiological conditions yet antagonistic during L-dopa therapy, emphasizes the importance of microbial metabolism in shaping both PD onset and treatment response. Overall, we identified the bacterial gene-pathway-metabolite axis (pdxJ - de novo vitamin B6 synthesis - PLP/PL) as a key mechanism through which the gut microbiota modulates dopamine-related neurometabolic functions in the host.
