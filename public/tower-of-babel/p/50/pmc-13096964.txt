Mechanical activation of PIEZO1 drives AP-1–dependent PTGS2/PGE2 signaling and corneal inflammation
Authors: Zongzheng Zou, Xiuqing Qian, Jifeng Ren, Yuqing Wang, Yuqi Liu, Aojun Luo, Mengyuan Guo, Hui Tong, Ruiran Li, Shanshan Li, Haixia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAbnormal mechanical stimulation, such as eye rubbing, is a critical risk factor for corneal diseases, yet the molecular mechanisms linking mechanical stimulation to inflammation remain unclear. Here, we show that mechanical stretch directly activates the mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1 in human corneal fibroblasts, leading to Ca2+ influx and activation of the AP-1 transcription factor. This signaling cascade drives robust induction of PTGS2 and secretion of its downstream effector PGE2, which in turn promotes corneal inflammation and impairs fibroblast repair functions. Using a mouse eye-rubbing model, we demonstrate that PIEZO1 inhibition with GsMTx4 significantly reduces corneal thickening, neutrophil infiltration, and inflammatory gene expression. These findings identify a PIEZO1–Ca2+–AP-1–PTGS2/PGE2 axis as a core mechanotransduction pathway driving corneal inflammation. Given the widespread expression of PIEZO1, our results provide mechanistic insight into how abnormal mechanical forces trigger inflammatory responses, highlighting a potential framework for understanding mechano-inflammation across tissues.

Introduction

Repeated or excessive eye rubbing is a significant risk factor for the development of various corneal diseases, with keratoconus being particularly common.1,2,3 Keratoconus is characterized by corneal thinning, ectasia, progressive vision loss, and, in severe cases, perforation, which can ultimately result in profound visual impairment. The stromal layer, which constitutes approximately 90% of the corneal thickness,4 is primarily composed of collagen fibers, glycosaminoglycans, and corneal fibroblasts. This layer plays a critical role in maintaining the cornea’s structural integrity and mechanical strength. Notably, recent studies suggest that local stromal inflammatory responses may contribute to the pathogenesis of keratoconus.5 Daily behaviors such as eye rubbing, poor sleeping positions, improper contact lens use, and refractive surgery can impose abnormal mechanical stress on the cornea, potentially triggering inflammatory responses.6,7,8 However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which these mechanical stimuli induce inflammation in the corneal stroma remain incompletely understood.

Mechanosensitive ion channels (MSCs) are specialized proteins located in the cell membrane that can detect and respond to external mechanical stimuli, such as tension, compression, and shear forces.9,10 These channels play a crucial role in various biological processes, including cell growth, migration, differentiation, and tissue repair. MSCs facilitate changes in cellular ion flux by transducing mechanical signals, thereby influencing cellular function and behavior.11 For instance, in osteoarthritis, mechanical forces stimulate the interaction between TRPV4 and PIEZO1 channels, leading to an increased expression of inflammatory factors in chondrocytes.12,13 In the context of lung inflammation, macrophages detect circulating hydrostatic pressure in the lungs via the PIEZO1 ion channel protein on their membranes, subsequently releasing inflammatory factors that trigger the innate immune response.14 This indicates that mechanical stimulation can provoke tissue inflammatory responses through the activation of specific MSCs.

As a relatively closed structure, the physiological activities of the eyeball—such as intraocular pressure generated by aqueous humor circulation, the fluid shear force produced by blinking tears, and posterior cerebrospinal hydraulics—maintain structural and functional homeostasis. These mechanical stimuli exert continuous physiological stress on the cells within the eye, and any abnormal mechanical changes may lead to tissue decompensation and lesions. In recent years, the role of MSCs, such as the PIEZO and TRP families, in the perception and response to mechanical stimuli in ocular tissues has gained increasing recognition. For instance, in conjunctival epithelial cells, simulated eye rubbing has been shown to activate the p38 MAPK/CREB signaling pathway, upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines, and induce neutrophil infiltration by activating the Piezo1 channel, which facilitates Ca2+ influx.15 Furthermore, overactivation of Trpv1, Trpa1, and Piezo2 has been found to exacerbate the disease phenotype in a mouse model of dry eye disease.8 In a contact lens-wearing model, the activation of Trpv1 and Trpa1 may promote inflammation by modulating cornea-resident immune cells, such as MHC II+ cells and γδT cells.6 These studies highlight the significant contribution of MSCs to ocular surface lesions, including dry eye and conjunctival and corneal inflammation. However, the mechanisms underlying non-physiological mechanical stimulation, such as that caused by eye rubbing on corneal tissue, remain inadequately understood. In particular, the molecular mechanisms by which repeated stretching or frictional stimulation (e.g., from eye rubbing) is converted into intracellular pathological signals through MSCs, ultimately leading to corneal inflammation and tissue remodeling, have yet to be elucidated.

In this study, we systematically elucidated the pathological processes underlying corneal inflammation induced by eye rubbing at both the cellular and tissue levels. We achieved this by combining an in vitro periodic mechanical stretch model with an experimental mouse model that simulates eye rubbing in vivo. Importantly, we identified the core molecular mechanism by which mechanical stimulation directly activates the PIEZO1 in human corneal fibroblasts (HCFs). This activation induces Ca2+ influx, which subsequently drives activator protein-1 (AP-1)-dependent transcriptional activation and upregulates the expression of the PTGS2/Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) axis, significantly promoting corneal inflammation and tissue damage. Based on this mechanism, we validated the effectiveness of the Piezo1-specific inhibitor GsMTx4 and the commonly used PTGS2 inhibitor celecoxib in preventing and alleviating corneal inflammation and associated stromal lesions induced by mechanical stimulation. Furthermore, our findings highlight the critical role of the “PIEZO1-PTGS2/PGE2 signaling axis” in the development of corneal inflammation induced by mechanical stimulation. Collectively, these results define cellular sensing and signaling pathways activated by abnormal mechanical forces, such as eye rubbing, in the context of corneal inflammation.

Results

Mechanical stretching induces inflammation in HCFs

To investigate the effect of mechanical stretching on HCFs, we analyzed gene expression differences between unstretched and stretched fibroblasts using RNA sequencing. As illustrated in the volcano plot, 321 genes were upregulated in HCFs following stretching, while the expression of 485 genes was downregulated (Figure 1A). Subsequently, we conducted KEGG enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed genes, revealing that the TNF, NF-κB, and IL-17 inflammation-related signaling pathways were among the top 20 pathways that were upregulated by enrichment (Figure 1B). GSEA analysis indicated that the TNF (hsa04668), NF-κB (hsa04064), and IL-17 (hsa04657) signaling pathways exhibited significant positive enrichment (p < 0.05, FDR <0.25) under stretching conditions, with enrichment scores (ESs) of 0.339, 0.409, and 0.487, respectively (Figure 1C). These findings suggest that mechanical stretching significantly activates the TNF, NF-κB, and IL-17 signaling pathways in HCFs, thereby promoting cellular inflammation. Furthermore, the overlap among the three inflammation-related signaling pathways was visualized and analyzed using a Venn diagram (Figure 1D). Notably, the common intersection of these pathways revealed seven shared genes: PTGS2, NFKBIA, CXCL2, RELA, CXCL1, CXCL5, and TNFAIP3 (Figure 1E). Mechanical stretching induces an increase in PTGS2 expression in HCFs via PIEZO1

In the transcriptomic results described above, we found that mechanical stretching induced an increase in PTGS2 gene expression. PCR experiments confirmed that stretching stimulation could significantly enhance PTGS2 mRNA expression in HCFs (Figure 2A). To investigate whether MSCs are involved in the regulation of PTGS2 expression in response to mechanical forces, we first screened for MSCs expressed in normal HCFs. We observed that among the tested genes, the expression level of the PIEZO1 gene was the highest, followed by the TRPV2 gene, while the PIEZO2 gene was almost not expressed (Figure 2B). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that PIEZO1 protein was localized on the membrane of HCFs (Figure 2C). To determine whether PIEOZ1 regulates PTGS2 expression, we applied GsMTx4, an inhibitor of PIEZO1, in vitro. Under the influence of stretching stimulation, the additional application of GsMTx4 significantly reduced PTGS2 mRNA expression (Figure 2D). Immunofluorescence staining and western blotting experiments further demonstrated that stretching stimulation could significantly increase PTGS2 protein expression, while the additional application of GsMTx4 markedly inhibited the overexpression of PTGS2 (Figures 2E–2H). Additionally, we assessed PGE2 expression. To more rigorously verify that PIEZO1 is the mechanosensitive channel mediating these responses, we silenced PIEZO1 using siRNA. Knockdown of PIEZO1 significantly decreased Piezo1 mRNA and protein levels in HCFs (Figures S1A–S1C), and strongly reduced mechanically activated whole-cell currents as measured by patch clamp (Figures S1D and S1E). Moreover, PIEZO1 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition with GsMTx4 attenuated the stretch-induced increase in intracellular Ca2+ content detected by a Ca2+ assay (Figure S1F). Together, these results confirm that PIEZO1 constitutes the major mechanosensitive conductance in HCFs. Both pharmacological inhibition with GsMTx4 and genetic silencing of PIEZO1 by siRNA produced comparable effects in suppressing mechanically activated currents and Ca2+ influx, indicating equivalent inhibitory efficiency.

PIEZO1 regulates the expression of PTGS2 through the AP-1 transcription factor

To further investigate the mechanism by which force stimulation regulates PTGS2 expression through PIEZO1, we examined the upstream transcription factors associated with PTGS2 expression. Transcription factor predictions were conducted using multiple databases, and a Venn diagram identified JUN as the intersecting factor (Figure 3A). The relative scores of the two predicted binding sites upstream of the PTGS2 gene were 0.94 and 0.89, respectively (Figure 3B). As one of the core members of the AP-1 transcription dimer, we hypothesized that the AP-1 transcription factor may play a role in regulating PTGS2 expression in response to mechanical force through PIEZO1. In western blotting experiments, stretching stimulation significantly increased the protein expression ratios of p-JUN/JUN and p-FOS/FOS, indicating that mechanical stretching could activate the AP-1 transcription factor and promote its phosphorylation (Figures 3C–3E). However, the additional application of GsMTx4 significantly reduced the increase in the protein expression ratios of p-JUN/JUN and p-FOS/FOS induced by force stimulation (Figures 3C–3E). Notably, this effect was partial and did not completely reverse the activation of the AP-1 transcription factor. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the expression of p-JUN in the nucleus increased following stretch stimulation, while the addition of GsMTx4 led to a decrease in nuclear p-JUN expression (Figures 3F, 3G, S2A, and S2B). Dual-luciferase reporter assays further confirmed that the additional application of GsMTx4 inhibited the increase in AP-1 transcriptional activity induced by mechanical stretching (Figure 3H). Moreover, the removal of extracellular calcium also inhibited the increase in AP-1 transcriptional activity resulting from mechanical stretching (Figure 3H). To further investigate the role of AP-1 transcription factors in regulating PTGS2 expression, we employed the AP-1 transcription factor inhibitor SR 11302. The additional use of SR 11302 significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of PTGS2 under stretching stimulation (Figure 3I). In line with these observations, the pharmacological inhibition of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), a canonical upstream kinase of AP-1, markedly attenuated stretch-induced AP-1 activation. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that JNK inhibition decreased nuclear p-JUN accumulation in stretched HCFs to near-baseline levels (Figures S2C and S2D). Consistently, AP-1 reporter assays revealed that JNK blockade significantly reduced stretch-induced AP-1 transcriptional activity (Figure S2E), supporting the notion that a PIEZO1–Ca2+–JNK-AP-1 cascade drives PTGS2 induction in response to mechanical stretching.

The activation of AP-1 by mechanical stretching is associated with Ca2+ influx through PIEZO1

In the experiments described above, we found that the removal of extracellular Ca2+ can influence the activity of the AP-1 transcription factor during mechanical stretching. First, we verified the functionality of the PIEZO1 channel. In flow cytometry experiments, mechanical stretching induced a significant increase in the intracellular Ca2+ concentration, while the combined application of GsMTx4 inhibited this increase and restored the level to normal (Figures 4A and 4B). This finding was further supported by ELISA experiments designed to detect Ca2+ (Figure 4C). Subsequently, we administered Yoda1, a small-molecule agonist of PIEZO1, to functionally mimic the activation of PIEZO1 by mechanical stretch. In live-cell real-time imaging experiments involving Ca2+, the application of Yoda1 induces intracellular Ca2+ transients that peak after 100 s (Video S1). However, in the absence of external Ca2+, Yoda1 could not elicit intracellular Ca2+ transients (Figures 4D and 4F). These results suggest that the PIEZO1 channel protein is the primary channel responsible for Ca2+ influx in response to stretching stimulation. Western blotting experiments and immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that, in the absence of extracellular calcium, stretching stimulation could not activate the intracellular AP-1 transcription factor (Figures 4G–4I and S1). Consequently, the protein expression ratios of p-JUN/JUN and p-FOS/FOS returned to normal levels (Figures 4G–4I and S3). Eye rubbing stimulation induces corneal inflammation in mice

Based on the results of the in vitro cell study, we constructed a mouse eye rubbing model to specifically investigate the effect of PIEZO1 on the cornea in response to eye rubbing stimulation. As illustrated in the figure, a motor-driven cotton swab arm performs a reciprocating rocking motion, rubbing the mouse’s eyelids back and forth to simulate the behavior of eye rubbing (Figure 5A). In this model, the force exerted on the mouse’s eye is measured by attaching a miniature flexible pressure sensor (Figures 5A, 5B, and S4). Following the eye rubbing, optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging revealed a significant increase in the thickness of the central cornea in the mice (Figures 5C and 5D). Additionally, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated that the corneal stromal structure was loose and infiltrated by inflammatory cells after eye rubbing (Figure 5E). PCR experiments indicated that the expression of corneal inflammation-related factors increased in the mice post-eye rubbing (Figure 5F). Subconjunctival injection of GsMTx4 alleviates corneal inflammation induced by eye rubbing stimulation

To investigate the role of Piezo1 in eye rubbing-induced inflammation in mice, we administered GsMTx4 subconjunctivally to inhibit Piezo1 function. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the Piezo1 channel protein was expressed in both the corneal epithelium and stromal layer, with a higher expression observed in the epithelial layer (Figure 6E). We measured Ca content and found that corneal Ca2+ levels significantly increased following eye rubbing (Figure 6D). After inhibiting the Piezo1 channel, the Ca2+ content in the corneas of the mice returned to normal levels (Figure 6D). Furthermore, we observed that the application of GsMTx4 significantly reduced the increase in central corneal thickness and the infiltration of stromal neutrophil induced by eye rubbing (Figures 6A–6C). Additionally, Eye rubbing also strongly upregulated Ptgs2 expression in both epithelial and stromal compartments, and this effect was substantially suppressed by GsMTx4 (Figures 6F and 6G). Consistent with the central role of PTGS2 in prostaglandin synthesis, corneal PGE2 levels were significantly elevated after eye rubbing and were reduced to near-control levels by GsMTx4 treatment (Figure S5A). Flow cytometry experiments demonstrated that eye rubbing stimulation increased the proportion of neutrophils among corneal cells, while the additional application of GsMTx4 partially reduced this proportion (Figures 6H and S5B). Given that eye rubbing is a significant high-risk factor for the onset of keratoconus, we analyzed single-cell corneal data from patients with keratoconus GEO: GSE285060).5 The results showed that PIEZO1 channel protein was expressed in both epithelial and stromal cells of the human cornea, with higher expression observed in the epithelial cells (Figure S6E). Furthermore, in patients with keratoconus, the expression levels of PIEZO1 and PTGS2 were increased (Figure S6F). These findings are consistent with our observations in the mouse model, further supporting the potential role of Piezo1 in corneal inflammation.

Inhibition of PTGS2 can partially restore the functionality of HCFs subjected to mechanical stretching

To further investigate the effect of PTGS2 on the function of HCFs under stretch stimulation, we utilized Celecoxib, a PTGS2 inhibitor, and Yoda1, a PIEZO1 agonist. Initially, the direct treatment of HCFs with Yoda1 induced a Ca2+-dependent increase in PTGS2 expression (Figures 7A–7C). This indicates that Yoda1 can partially mimic the activation of PIEZO1 through stretch stimulation. Wound-Healing Assay demonstrated that normal HCFs were capable of repairing scratches within 24 h (Figure 7D). However, Yoda1 significantly inhibited the migration and repair abilities of these fibroblasts (Figures 7D and 7E). The combined application of Celecoxib partially restored the migration and repair capabilities of the cells 24 h post-scratch (Figures 7D and 7E). These findings suggest that PTGS2 can inhibit the migration and repair capabilities of HCFs under simulated stretch stimulation, although it is not the sole contributing factor. Discussion

The cornea, a crucial optical and barrier tissue located in the anterior segment of the eyeball, is vulnerable to disturbances caused by abnormal mechanical forces affecting its homeostasis. Repeated eye rubbing has been widely recognized as a significant risk factor for various corneal lesions, notably keratoconus. Keratoconus is characterized by progressive thinning and ectasia of the corneal stroma, the primary structural layer of the cornea, which also exhibits sustained inflammation. This stromal inflammatory microenvironment is thought to drive pathological tissue remodeling and damage in keratoconus.5 However, the specific molecular mechanisms initiated by mechanical stimulation that trigger this stromal inflammation and ultimately lead to tissue damage remain poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated the role of the PIEZO1 channel protein in the corneal inflammatory response to mechanical stimulation. Mechanistically, we elucidate that mechanical stimulation triggers Ca2+ influx by directly activating the mechanosensitive channel PIEZO1 in HCFs. This Ca2+ influx subsequently drives the activation of the AP-1 transcription factor, ultimately upregulating the core signaling pathway responsible for the expression of PTGS2, a key pro-inflammatory mediator, along with its product, PGE2. Furthermore, we confirmed the efficacy of the Piezo1-specific inhibitor GsMTx4 and the clinically utilized PTGS2 inhibitor Celecoxib in effectively preventing and alleviating corneal inflammation induced by experimental eye rubbing and the stretching of cell-associated pathologies in mice. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved in corneal inflammation induced by mechanical force but also offer a promising targeted therapeutic strategy for the prevention and treatment of corneal diseases associated with eye rubbing.

Force stimulation can induce inflammation in corneal cells, impair cell proliferation, and decrease collagen secretion.16,17,18 For instance, prolonged contact lens wear can increase contact pressure and shear stress in the corneal epithelium. This mechanical stimulation elevates the expression of interleukins IL-6 and IL-1β, MMP9, and pro-apoptotic genes in corneal epithelial cells, leading to inflammatory responses.19 Similarly, in patients with dry eye, tear hyperosmolality can exacerbate the condition by inducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors from corneal epithelial cells.20 This process is dependent on the TRPV1 channel protein, which converts the hyperosmolality signal of tears into an intracellular chemical signal.20 Critically, eye rubbing exerts complex biomechanical forces on the cornea - primarily compressive pressure and surface friction. This external mechanical insult induces macroscopic tissue deformation within the corneal stroma. At the microscopic scale, however, we reason that the primary consequence for stromal cells is plasma-membrane deformation. To replicate the fundamental biomechanical effects of eye rubbing at the cellular level, we applied controlled, uniform cyclic stretching to HCFs. In this system, we observed that mechanical stretching activated several inflammatory pathways in HCFs, resulting in the upregulation of inflammatory factors, including PTGS2, and triggering an inflammatory response in corneal cells. Corneal inflammation was also evident in the mouse eye rubbing model we developed. Furthermore, mechanical stretching inhibited the migration, collagen synthesis, and proliferation of HCFs, thereby impairing cell function. We also detected the expression of relevant MSCs in HCFs. In normal cells, the expression level of the PIEZO1 channel protein was the highest, followed by TRPV2, while other channel proteins exhibited lower expression levels, and PIEZO2 was nearly undetectable. These findings align with the results reported by Fukuoka S et al.15 Interestingly, the application of GsMTx4, a functional inhibitor of PIEZO1, suppressed the expression of PTGS2 and PGE2 under mechanical stretch. We propose that PIEZO1 plays a crucial role in the mechanotransduction of stretch-stimulated signals in HCFs, leading to the increased expression of downstream PTGS2 inflammatory factors.

Solis AG et al. discovered that the PIEZO1 channel protein on lung myeloid cells can detect periodic mechanical stress in the lungs, leading to the activation of the innate immune response.14 This process depends on the activation of AP-1 transcription factors through PIEZO1-mediated Ca2+ influx signaling.14 Similarly, in hair follicle stem cells, PIEZO1 senses the mechanical stimuli exerted by the extracellular matrix and activates the AP-1 transcription factor, which subsequently regulates cellular activity.21 Our findings are consistent with this, suggesting that mechanical stretching increases intracellular calcium levels via PIEZO1, and that this Ca2+ influx is essential for the activation of AP-1 and its nuclear translocation. The removal of extracellular Ca2+ or the application of GsMTx4 significantly inhibited both the activation and transcriptional activity of AP-1. In addition, AP-1 serves as a convergence point for multiple stress and inflammatory signals. Its activation promotes the transcriptional upregulation of PTGS2,22,23,24,25 a process that is significantly supported by the effective inhibition of PTGS2 expression and PGE2 secretion through the use of AP-1 inhibitors, such as SR11302. Notably, while the inhibition of force-stimulated AP-1 activation by GsMTx4 was partial, the complete elimination of Ca2+ fully inhibited AP-1 activation. We speculate that other MSCs may also contribute to this process. In addition to PIEZO1, HCFs express various TRP superfamily channel proteins. Other TRP family members, such as TRPV1, TRPC6, TRPM3, TRPC4, and TRPM8, can also activate AP-1 through calcium-dependent pathways.26,27,28,29,30 This multi-channel complementary mechanism may account for some of the inhibitory effects of GsMTx4; when PIEZO1 is blocked, the TRP channels may retain some AP-1 activity. It is important to note that the functional coupling between different channel proteins may also play a role,31,32,33 which is a focus for future research.

The PTGS2/PGE2 axis was identified as the primary inflammatory effector in this signaling pathway. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that stretching forces significantly activated three critical inflammatory pathways: TNF, NF-κB, and IL-17. Venn diagram analysis unexpectedly indicated that PTGS2 was the common core intersection gene among these three pathways, with its differential expression being the most pronounced. Experimental results confirmed that both in vitro stretching and in vivo eye rubbing strongly induced the expression of PTGS2 and increased PGE2 levels. The role of PTGS2/PGE2 in inflammation is well established; PTGS2/PGE2 can inhibit cell proliferation, promote apoptosis, and recruit and activate immune cells (e.g., neutrophils).34,35,36,37,38 This is highly consistent with the phenotypes of corneal thickening (edema) and the infiltration of neutrophil, particularly the increased proportion of neutrophils observed in vivo. PTGS2/PGE2 also plays a crucial role in regulating tissue repair and regeneration. In fibroblasts, PTGS2/PGE2 can inhibit the migration and repair of vocal cords, skin, and airways.39,40,41 Similarly, we found that the inhibition of PTGS2 (using Celecoxib) restored the proliferative capacity of HCFs that had been inhibited by stretching stimulation in vitro and partially restored their migration and repair abilities, which were impaired by Yoda1. This suggests that PTGS2/PGE2 not only mediates inflammation but may also be involved in the impairment of cell repair functions under mechanical stimulation. However, Celecoxib did not significantly improve the Stretch-induced decrease in COL1 expression, indicating that the collagen metabolism disturbance induced by mechanical stimulation may be more directly regulated by other factors (e.g., MMPs) or pathways (such as TGF-β).42,43 To further explore this possibility, we re-analyzed our transcriptomic data and performed loss-of-function experiments in stretched HCFs, which identified MMP1 and MMP12 as key collagen-degrading enzymes, as simultaneous knockdown of these two MMPs partially restored COL1 expression under mechanical stretch. Additionally, PTGS2/PGE2 may contribute to the development and progression of keratoconus. Patients with keratoconus exhibit higher levels of PGE2 compared to healthy individuals, suggesting that it may serve as a biomarker for the condition.44 This highlights the complex role of PGE2 in tissue remodeling, and its long-term effects and association with keratoconus pathology warrant further investigation.

To validate the aforementioned mechanism, we successfully developed and implemented a quantified mouse eye rubbing model. This model utilizes a motor to operate a cotton swab arm that rubs a mouse’s eyelids back and forth, simulating the action of a human knuckle rubbing the eye. The model utilizes a motor to drive a cotton swab arm that gently rubs a mouse’s eyelids back and forth. This procedure is intended to replicate the action of a human knuckle rubbing the eye. The total number of eye rubs is calibrated to simulate the cumulative effects of pathological eye rubbing over the span of a year.45 The model effectively induced a corneal inflammatory phenotype, characterized by a significant increase in central corneal thickness (measured by OCT), a loose stromal structure with neutrophil infiltration, and upregulation of mRNA expression of inflammatory cytokines, including Ptgs2, Mmp3, Tnfa, Il-1b, and Il-6. Similar to the mouse eye rubbing model we developed, after approximately 5 min of eye rubbing stimulation applied to the eyelids of rats and rabbits, there was an increase in inflammatory infiltration in the rat conjunctiva, and the rabbit cornea exhibited slight edema.46,47 Building on the mouse eye rubbing model developed previously, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of the targeted intervention “PIEZO1-PTGS2/PGE2 axis”. In vitro and in vivo experiments consistently demonstrated that specific blockade of PIEZO1 (using GsMTx4) or inhibition of its downstream key effector molecule, PTGS2 (with Celecoxib), can effectively reduce the inflammatory response induced by mechanical force. GsMTx4 significantly alleviated corneal thickening, neutrophil infiltration, and Ptgs2 expression caused by eye rubbing in vivo. Celecoxib partially mitigated the inhibition of fibroblast migration and proliferation induced by mechanical stimulation in vitro. As a strategy targeting upstream mechanoreceptors, GsMTx4 may offer a more precise intervention, particularly for preventing inflammatory cascades triggered by abnormal mechanical forces, such as habitual eye rubbing and shape-deprivation myopia.48 Celecoxib, a widely used PTGS2 inhibitor in clinical practice, has promising prospects for repurposing as a topical treatment for ocular conditions, although its long-term safety on the ocular surface requires thorough evaluation. These findings provide a crucial foundation for developing new strategies aimed at preventing and treating corneal inflammation and complications associated with eye rubbing.

This study systematically elucidates a molecular mechanism by which abnormal mechanical force activates the MSC PIEZO1 in HCFs. This activation triggers Ca2+ influx, which subsequently drives AP-1-dependent transcriptional activation and upregulates the expression of the PTGS2/PGE2 axis, thereby initiating the corneal inflammatory response. We successfully established and validated a quantified mouse eye rubbing model and demonstrated the efficacy of targeting the upstream components of this signaling axis, specifically using the PIEZO1 inhibitor GsMTx4, to alleviate inflammation. These findings improve understanding of how mechanical forces initiate corneal inflammation and identify the PIEZO1-PTGS2/PGE2 axis as a potential intervention target for corneal lesions associated with eye rubbing.

Limitations of the study

This study has certain limitations and highlights valuable directions for future research. First, the research primarily focuses on HCF. Since eye rubbing can also physically affect corneal epithelial cells, nerve endings, and resident immune cells, future studies should explore the roles of these additional cell types and their functional interactions with fibroblasts within the inflammatory network. Second, although our mouse eye-rubbing model effectively simulates acute and subacute inflammatory responses, it does not fully capture the chronic progression of diseases such as keratoconus. Developing a chronic mechanical stimulation model is essential for investigating long-term corneal remodeling, including sustained morphological and biomechanical changes. Finally, the partial inhibition of AP-1 activation by GsMTx4 and the limited effect of celecoxib on collagen synthesis suggest that signaling pathways beyond PIEZO-1-PTGS2/PGE are also involved. Therefore, future research should further investigate the contributions of other MSCs, such as TRPV2 and TRPT1, in corneal inflammation induced by eye rubbing, as well as their relationship with the functional coupling of PIEZO1. Additionally, the roles of other corneal histiocytes and immune cells represent important avenues for future research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

The experimental subjects in this study were SPF-grade C57BL/6J male mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, all of which were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The mice were acclimated for one week in the barrier environment of the Laboratory Animal Center at Capital Medical University, where the temperature was maintained at 22±2°C, humidity at 50±10%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle was implemented. They were provided with standard rodent feed and sterilized water ad libitum. All animal experiments were conducted in strict compliance with the International Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH) and received approval from the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (Ethics Batch No.: AEEI-2024-139). Throughout the experimentation process, the principles of animal welfare were prioritized, including minimizing the number of experimental animals used and implementing appropriate measures to alleviate any suffering experienced by the animals.

Cell culture

The primary HCFs utilized in this experiment were obtained from Procell (Wuhan, China) and identified through Vimentin immunofluorescence and STR species analysis. The medium employed for cell culture consisted of DMEM/F-12 (Vivacell, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Vivacell, Shanghai, China).

Method details

Construction and detection of a mouse eye rubbing model

The mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 0.6% sodium pentobarbital and subsequently positioned under the eye rubbing device. This device is motor-driven, allowing the arm to swing back and forth, with one end of the swing arm connected to a medical cotton swab. The device was adjusted so that the cotton swab made contact with the mouse's eyelid, enabling the swing arm to move the cotton swab up and down across the mouse's eye, thereby simulating the eye rubbing process. To quantitatively assess the force exerted on the mouse's eye, a miniature force sensor was placed in the vicinity of the eye to measure the frictional force applied to the eyelid during rubbing, which averaged 0.0898 N. Following the methodology outlined by Torres-Netto EA et al., the frequency of the reciprocating rocking machine was set to 35 cycles per minute, with a treatment duration of 1 hour per day over four days, resulting in a total of 10,500 eye rubs (approximately the total number of eye rubs in a year)16. After completing the eye rubbing procedure, the cornea of each mouse was imaged and analyzed using OCT (Optoprobe, Huntingdon, UK).

Stretch assay, and RNA sequencing

Cells were seeded in a flexible basal six-well plate (Flexcell, Burlington, NC, USA), and once cell confluency reached 90%, they were placed in a cell stretch loading culture system (FX-5000T, Burlington, NC, USA) for stretching. The stretching parameters included a frequency of 0.5 Hz, an amplitude of 15%, and a duration of 6 hours, resulting in a total of 10,800 stretches. Cells were pre-treated with GsMTx4 (5 μM), SR 11302 (10 μM), Celecoxib (10 μM) and SP600125 (5 μM) for 1 hour prior to the application of mechanical stretch or other stimulations, and the inhibitor was maintained in the culture medium throughout the stimulation period. Following the stretching procedure, the cells were carefully collected and sent to Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for RNA sequencing analysis.

Single-cell data processing and analysis

Single-cell transcriptomic profiles were processed using the Seurat package (version 4.4.3). Mitochondrial and ribosomal genes were removed, and profiles with fewer than 500 expressed genes or >10% mitochondrial gene expression were excluded. Doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder. Normalization was performed with the LogNormalize method, and mitochondrial gene variation was regressed out. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on highly variable genes, followed by clustering with the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) method using the first 15 principal components at a resolution of 0.1, resulting in 6 unsupervised cell clusters, visualized with UMAP. Cell type classification was based on differential expression analysis using the FindMarkers function to identify cluster-specific marker genes. Differential gene expression was analyzed using DESeq2 with a significance threshold of p<0.05. Gene expression distributions were visualized using UMAP and violin plots.

Patch-clamp experiment

Patch-clamp experiments were performed in standard cell attached recordings utilizing an EPC-10 amplifier (HEKA Elektronik, Lambrecht, Germany). Patch elec-trodes were pulled from borosilicate capillaries (Sutter Instruments) on a micropipette puller (P-97, Sutter Instruments). Electrodes with resistances of 4–5 MΩ were used. Currents were sampled at 50 kHz and filtered at 1 kHz. All experiments were done at room temperature. For recordings from Human corneal fibroblasts, pipette were filled with a solution consisting of (in mM) 130 NaCl, 5 KCl, 10 HEPES, 1 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 TEA-Cl (pH 7.3 with NaOH) and external solution used to zero the membrane potential consisted of (in mM) 140 KCl, 10 HEPES, 1 MgCl2, 10 glucose (pH 7.3 with KOH). For cell-attached recordings membrane patches were stimulated with brief negative pressure pulses through the recording electrode using a Clampex controlled pressure clamp HSPC-1 device (ALA-scientific). Otherwise stated, stretch-activated channels were recorded at a holding potential of -80 mV with pressure steps from 0 to -150 mm Hg (-10 mm Hg increments).

RT-qPCR

RNA isolation from HCFs and mouse corneas was performed using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (Transgen Biotech, Beijing, China). cDNA synthesis was conducted with HiScript III All-in-One RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Real-time PCR was executed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and a Real-Time PCR cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The specific reaction conditions included pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, denaturation at 95 °C for 10 seconds, annealing at 60 °C for 30 seconds, extension at 65 °C for 5 seconds, followed by a total of 40 cycles. The primer sequences are provided in Table S1. mRNA levels in each sample were calculated by normalizing the expression of β-actin. Relative mRNA expression levels were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Immunofluorescence staining

For processing mouse corneal sections, mouse eyeball tissue is flash-frozen in Tissue-Tek OCT compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan) and cut into 7 μm thick sections. The corneal sections and cells are fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature. Samples are then permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes. For PIEZO1 staining, Tritonx-100 was not used to permeabilize the cell membrane. Following this, blocking is performed with 5% donkey serum for 1 hour at room temperature. The samples are incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the sections are stained with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody for 90 minutes at room temperature and counterstained with DAPI for 5 minutes. All stained samples are observed and photographed using an ultra-high-resolution confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After completing the cell stretching experiment, the cell supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. For the mouse cornea experiment, collect the mouse corneas, perform homogenization and lysis, and centrifuge to collect the supernatant. PGE2 in the medium was measured using an ELISA kit (Meimian, Jiangsu, China), and the absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Western blotting

After stretching the cells, remove the cell culture medium and immediately add Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay Buffer to the cells. Lyse the cells on ice for 15 minutes to extract total cell protein. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Place each set of 30 μg of protein into the wells of a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and perform electrophoresis. After electrophoresis, transfer the proteins from the gel to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Block the PVDF membranes with 5% BSA for 1 hour at room temperature. After three washes with TBST, add the primary antibody and incubate overnight at 4 °C. The following day, incubate the membranes with secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase for 2 hours at room temperature. The PVDF membranes were then treated with a luminescent solution (Biosharp, Hefei, China), and the blot was visualized using a Bio-Rad Molecular Imager ChemiDoc XRS System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). β-Actin was used as a loading control.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

Cells were seeded in flexible basal six-well plates, and when cell confluency reached 80%, the pAP1-TA-luc plasmid (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and the pRL-TK plasmid (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were transfected into the cells at a 10:1 ratio using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). After 6 hours, the medium was replaced, and the cells were cultured for an additional 24 hours before conducting the stretching experiment. Immediately following the stretching, the cells were lysed with reporter lysis buffer for 20 minutes. The lysate was then collected and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 minutes to remove the supernatant. Firefly luciferase detection buffer and Renilla luciferase detection buffer were added sequentially, and the readings were measured using a SpectraMax i3X microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The ratio of the two luciferase activities was calculated for statistical analysis.

Flow cytometry

For the analysis of corneal neutrophils via flow cytometry, mice subjected to eye rubbing were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. The eyeballs were excised and washed with phosphate-buffered saline containing antibiotics. The cornea was then cut from the eyeball and digested using 15 mg/ml Dispase II (Macklin, Shanghai, China) for 3 hours to separate the corneal epithelium from the stromal layer. The corneal epithelium and stroma were subsequently digested with trypsin (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and collagenase A (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) for 1 hour, respectively. Following digestion, 1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher) was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 minutes. The supernatant was collected, and the cells were washed twice. Flow cytometry antibodies were added, and the mixture was incubated at 4°C for 30 minutes in the dark. After washing the cells, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes. The assays were analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa Flow Cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). For flow cytometry analysis of fluorescence intensity related to cellular Ca2+, the following procedure should be followed: After incubating the cells with the Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe, remove the medium and wash the cells three times with DPBS. Digest the cells using EDTA-free trypsin (Thermo Fisher) and centrifuge at 2,000 rpm for 5 minutes. Sample loading is then performed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company).

Detection of calcium ion content

For experiments involving HCFs, after stretching the cells, remove the cell culture medium and wash once with DPBS. Add 200 μl of lysate to each well of the 6-well plate. After complete lysis, centrifuge at 10,000 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C and collect the supernatant. For the mouse cornea experiments, following the eye rubbing procedure, excise the mouse cornea and add lysate to grind and lyse the tissue. The intracellular calcium ion content is measured using a calcium content chromogenic assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Calcium content was normalized to total cells per well or per cornea. Add 150 μl of the assay working solution to each well and mix thoroughly. Incubate for 10 minutes at room temperature in the dark. The absorbance is measured at 575 nm using a microplate reader for statistical analysis.

Calcium imaging

Cells were seeded into glass-bottom dishes (Biosharp, Hefei, China), and the medium was removed when the cells reached 90% confluence. The cells were rinsed three times with DPBS, after which 1 μM Fluo-4 AM calcium dye was added, and the dishes were incubated for 30 minutes. Following three additional washes with DPBS, 0.9 ml of standard DMEM/F-12 medium and decalcified DMEM/F-12 medium (Macklin, Shanghai, China) were added, respectively. The cell culture dishes were then placed on a STED ultra-high-resolution microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) for real-time observation. After one minute of observation, 100 μl of Yoda1 solution with an initial concentration of 100 μM was added, allowing it to slowly diffuse into the medium to achieve a final concentration of 10 μM. Real-time observation continued for 10 minutes, during which the fluorescence signal of Ca2+ was recorded.

H&E staining

The mouse eyeball was excised and immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde fixative solution at 4 °C for 24 hours. A cut was made at the posterior aspect of the eyeball, and it was fixed again for an additional 24 hours. Following dehydration and clearing, paraffin blocks were prepared, and tissue sections with a thickness of 5 μm were obtained using a paraffin microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated using a gradient of ethanol, after which they were sequentially stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The tissues were initially stained with Harris hematoxylin for 8 minutes, rapidly differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid in ethanol for a few seconds, returned to blue using running water, and subsequently stained with a 0.5% eosin aqueous solution for 1 minute. After staining, the samples were dehydrated using a gradient of ethanol and cleared with xylene before being mounted with neutral gum. Upon completion of the staining process, a full-frame image scan was conducted using a fully automated slide scanning system (3D HISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) to observe the pathological changes in the corneal tissue.

Wound-Healing Assay

Cells are seeded into a 24-well plate with a marked line on the bottom. Once the cell growth density reaches 100%, use the tip of a pipette to create an even scratch from the center of the plate. Discard the medium in the wells and rinse off the detached cells with phosphate-buffered saline. Prepare fresh medium, adding Yoda1 to the Yoda1 group and both Yoda1 and Celecoxib to the Yoda1 Celecoxib group, respectively. Yoda1 is used at a concentration of 10 μM, and Celecoxib is also used at a concentration of 10 μM. At 0 hours, 12 hours, and 24 hours post-scratching, photograph the scratched area of the cells using a standard light microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Measure the scratch area using ImageJ software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20 software (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL). Statistical graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Experiments involving two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed Student's t-test, while experiments with more than two groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. parametric tests (Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA), all quantitative data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test to ensure the assumption of normal distribution was met. For one-way ANOVA, post hoc comparisons were conducted using the LSD test if homogeneity of variances was confirmed; otherwise, Tamhane's T2 test was employed. The ‘n’ number represents independent biological replicates from distinct cell preparations or individual animals, unless otherwise specified. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ns, not significant.
