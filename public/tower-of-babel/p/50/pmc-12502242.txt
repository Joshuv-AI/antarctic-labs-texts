Association between weekend warrior physical activity patterns, physical activity intensity, and osteoporosis in adults over 50 years old: a cross-sectional study based on the NHANES 2017–2020
Authors: Jia Xu, Changmao Dai, Xiaolan Sun, Feier Chen, Maojun Cheng, Liyang Li, Xueping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAgainst the backdrop of global aging, declining bone health and the rising prevalence of osteoporosis(OP) in adults over 50 years old have become significant public health concerns. Physical activity is recognized as an effective non-pharmacological intervention for OP prevention. While current guidelines recommend 150 + minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity(MPA) for general health promotion, the association between weekend warrior physical activity patterns, physical activity intensity, and OP in adults over 50 years old remains unclear.MethodsThis study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey(NHANES) 2017–2020 to investigate the associations between physical activity patterns (inactive, weekend warrior, and regular activity), physical activity intensity, and OP in adults over 50 years old. Weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed as the primary analytical method.ResultsA cohort of 3,539 participants was included in this study, among whom 52.9% were female. Among adults over 50 years old, compared to the inactive group, the weekend warrior group was associated with an 89% reduced risk of OP (OR: 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.36, P = 0.024), demonstrated significantly higher femoral bone mineral density, regardless of the intensity level of physical activity performed. The results of the subgroup and sensitivity analyses were aligned with those of the primary analysis, further enhancing the robustness of the findings.ConclusionOur study revealed that among adults over 50 years old, achieving the WHO-recommended 150 + minutes of MPA within 1–2 days per week was associated with significantly reduced OP risk compared to physical inactivity. Furthermore, for individuals unable to engage in daily exercise, low-frequency but goal-oriented attainment of the WHO-prescribed physical activity duration may provide a viable alternative strategy for OP risk mitigation.Keywords: Bone mineral density, Inactive, Regular activity, NHANES

Osteoporosis is a metabolic bone disorder characterized by reduced bone mineral density, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and disruption of bone homeostasis. Middle-aged and older individuals are at higher risk of developing OP due to age-related impairment of bone formation equilibrium [1]. OP has become a major public health concern in China, with a prevalence of 19.2% among individuals aged 50 and above, rising to 32.0% in those aged 65 and older adults [2]. The most severe consequence of OP is fragility fracture. Within one year following a hip fracture, 23.5% of patients aged 50 or older adults die [3], and approximately 50% experience permanent disability [4]. In the United States, the economic burden attributable to OP is projected to exceed $95 billion annually by 2040 [5]. In the UK, fragility fractures impose an estimated annual burden of £4.4 billion on the National Health Service (NHS) [6], while Projections indicate that the annual incidence of major fragility fractures in China will reach 4.83 million by 2035 and escalate to 5.99 million by 2050, with associated medical costs soaring to 174.5 billion CNY [7].

Bone mineral density is a widely utilized metric for assessing skeletal health. Femoral bone mineral density serves as a highly effective indicator for OP screening and diagnosis, as defined by the World Health Organization criterion of a BMD T-score ≤ − 2.5 at the femoral neck. This metric demonstrates a strong correlation with all-cause and cause-specific mortality in OP patients [8]. Hip fractures, in particular, can be devastating for older adults. Given the global increase in life expectancy and the substantial socioeconomic burden imposed by OP and fragility fractures on healthcare systems and individuals, the implementation of effective OP prevention strategies is of critical importance.

Physical activity is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure.For patients with OP, weight-bearing physical activities (e.g., weightlifting, squats, push-ups) apply mechanical loading to stimulate bone tissue, promoting bone regeneration and mineralization, thereby significantly improving BMD. Impact-based physical activities (e.g., jumping rope, hopping) induce high-intensity transient stress on bones, accelerating bone remodeling and effectively enhancing BMD and bone strength. However, due to age-related physiological decline, these activities should be cautiously selected for older adults to avoid exercise-related injuries [9]. Older adults can improve their health status through appropriate daily exercise [10]. Engaging in PA confers multiple health benefits. For older populations, systematic review [11] indicate that even low-intensity PA interventions can enhance skeletal health and prevent OP. High-intensity PA interventions, particularly those involving resistance training or combined exercise modalities, demonstrate greater efficacy in improving lumbar spine bone mineral density [12]. Extensive research confirms a positive correlation between regular exercise and increased BMD across all age groups, particularly in middle-aged and older adults [13]. The World Health Organization (WHO) [14] recommends that adults aged 65 + engage in at least 3–5 days of PA per week to maintain health. Similarly, China’s Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Primary OP (2022) suggest that individuals aged 50 + should perform either:150–300 min of moderate-intensity exercise weekly, or 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise weekly. A meta-analysis of 59 studies revealed that, compared to inactive individuals, PA interventions may improve femoral neck BMD12. However, the interrelationships between distinct PA patterns—specifically the "Weekend Warrior" pattern (characterized by concentrated exercise within limited days) and physical activity intensity levels (moderate-intensity, vigorous-intensity)—along with their differential impacts on OP in adults over 50 years old, have not been sufficiently explored. These gaps highlight a crucial research frontier, as clarifying the differential effects of PA regimens could enable more personalized and effective strategies for OP prevention in high-risk populations.

Given the well-documented benefits of PA for skeletal health, the current investigation focuses on elucidating the differential impacts of various PA patterns on OP risk and progression in adult populations. Our analysis utilizes comprehensive data from NHANES 2017–2020. The NHANES database (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes) provides extensive details, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and health-related information, offering a robust foundation for evaluating how different PA modalities influence bone health outcomes. Our study seeks to identify the most effective PA patterns for preserving skeletal health in adults over 50 years old. The findings will inform targeted, evidence-based public health strategies and personalized preventive interventions for this population.

Methods

NHANES and study participants

NHANES is a biennial survey that recruits a nationally representative sample of non-institutionalized civilians. It collects and analyzes health-related data through interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States [15, 16]. NHANES provides insights into various health parameters, including lifestyle, fitness, diet, and mental health [17]. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics.

For our study, we utilized NHANES data from 2017–2020, as this period included measurements of physical activity and BMD in participants over 50 years old. The participant inclusion and exclusion process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, the study comprised 15,560 participants. We first excluded individuals with incomplete Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) data, which precluded determination of their exercise patterns, leaving 9,685 eligible subjects. Subsequently, we further excluded participants lacking BMD measurements, resulting in a final analytical sample of 3,539 individuals (Fig. 1).

Physical activity

Participants' self-reported PA levels were measured using the GPAQ, with particular focus on the frequency and duration of leisure-time physical activities [18]. This validated instrument specifically captures bouts of MPA and VPA lasting at least 10 continuous minutes.According to the official NHANES guidelines: MPA‌ refers to any exercise, fitness, or recreational activity that causes a moderate increase in breathing or heart rate (e.g., brisk walking, cycling, swimming, or volleyball), with an intensity level of 4 METs. VPA‌ refers to any intense exercise, fitness, or recreational activity that causes a substantial increase in breathing or heart rate (e.g., running or basketball), with an intensity level of 8 METs.(MET = Metabolic Equivalent of Task, where 1 MET ≈ 3.5 ml/kg/min oxygen consumption at rest.) Total weekly PA was calculated by summing minutes of MPA and VPA, with intensity-adjusted conversion (1 min of VPA = 2 min of MPA) to account for metabolic equivalent differences [19]. A Physical activity patterns were defined as follows [11, 20]:

Inactive: not reporting any moderate- or vigorous-intensity physical activities or reporting < 150 min/wk in moderate-intensity and < 75 min/wk in vigorous-intensity activities.

Inactive: not reporting any moderate- or vigorous-intensity physical activities or reporting < 150 min/wk in moderate-intensity and < 75 min/wk in vigorous-intensity activities.

Weekend warrior: reporting at least 150 min/wk in moderate-intensity or at least 75 min/wk in vigorous-intensity activities from 1 or 2 sessions.

Weekend warrior: reporting at least 150 min/wk in moderate-intensity or at least 75 min/wk in vigorous-intensity activities from 1 or 2 sessions.

Regularly active: reporting at least 150 min/wk in moderate-intensity or at least 75 min/wk in vigorous-intensity activities from 3 or more sessions.

Regularly active: reporting at least 150 min/wk in moderate-intensity or at least 75 min/wk in vigorous-intensity activities from 3 or more sessions.

For participants exceeding the recommended 150 min of weekly PA, we employed a refined four-category classification system to more precisely assess PA intensity patterns [21]:

MPA-Total: all leisure-time physical activities were performed at moderate intensity. MPA-Dominant: classified when weekly moderate-intensity PA time exceeded vigorous-intensity PA time.

MPA-Total: all leisure-time physical activities were performed at moderate intensity. MPA-Dominant: classified when weekly moderate-intensity PA time exceeded vigorous-intensity PA time.

VPA-Dominant: classified when weekly vigorous-intensity PA time exceeded moderate-intensity PA time.

VPA-Dominant: classified when weekly vigorous-intensity PA time exceeded moderate-intensity PA time.

VPA-Total: all activities were performed at vigorous intensity.

VPA-Total: all activities were performed at vigorous intensity.

This classification approach enables a more granular examination of how varying PA frequencies and intensities influence OP risk in adults over 50 years old.

OP assessment

Bone mineral density measurements were obtained using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans performed with a Hologic QDR-4500A fan-beam densitometer (Hologic, Inc., Bedford, MA, United States). The applicability and exclusion criteria for DXA scans are provided in Fig. 1. Given the catastrophic consequences of hip fractures (particularly femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures) in older populations—where previous studies demonstrate 1-year post-fracture mortality rates reaching 20%–30% [22], over 50% of survivors permanently losing independent mobility, and 10%–20% requiring long-term care, imposing substantial burdens on public healthcare systems [23]—our study selected femoral BMD as the diagnostic criterion for OP. Using World Health Organization classification criteria, OP was defined as a BMD value ≥ 2.5 standard deviations below the young adult reference mean at any femoral region. Based on established literature, the study measured bone mineral density at four femoral sites [24].

Covariates

Based on previous literature [25–27], the covariates included in this study encompass:

demographic factors: gender, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, and PIR ( PIR was calculated by the NHANES as the ratio of total family income to the federal poverty threshold);

demographic factors: gender, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, and PIR ( PIR was calculated by the NHANES as the ratio of total family income to the federal poverty threshold);

anthropometric and lifestyle factors: waist circumference, body mass index, sleep duration [28, 29] (stratified into weekday and weekend sleep due to typical variations), and sedentary behavior;

anthropometric and lifestyle factors: waist circumference, body mass index, sleep duration [28, 29] (stratified into weekday and weekend sleep due to typical variations), and sedentary behavior;

tobacco use (categorized as: never smokers [< 100 lifetime cigarettes], former smokers, and current smokers);

tobacco use (categorized as: never smokers [< 100 lifetime cigarettes], former smokers, and current smokers);

alcohol use (classified as: non-drinkers [abstinent or no alcohol in the past year], light [≤ 3 drinks/month], moderate [> 3 drinks/month but ≤ 4 drinks/week], and heavy [> 4 drinks/week]).

alcohol use (classified as: non-drinkers [abstinent or no alcohol in the past year], light [≤ 3 drinks/month], moderate [> 3 drinks/month but ≤ 4 drinks/week], and heavy [> 4 drinks/week]).

To evaluate the robustness of the primary association between physical activity and osteoporosis, participants with a history of cancer were excluded, accounting for established impacts of cancer type and treatment modalities on bone mineral density. Cancer status was assessed through self-reported physician-diagnosed history during the household interview (NHANES questionnaire item: has a doctor ever told you that you have cancer?). Participants responding 'yes' were classified as cancer survivors, while 'no' indicated no prior diagnosis.To ensure robustness, missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation via the mice package in R, preserving dataset integrity while minimizing bias [30].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). Continuous variables are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with post-hoc pairwise comparisons conducted using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test. Categorical variables are expressed as proportions with 95% CI and were analyzed using chi-square tests, with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons for significant overall effects.Weighted multivariable logistic regression models were employed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between weekly physical activity patterns, physical activity intensity, and OP. In our analytical framework: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race, educational attainment, and PIR; Model 3 further incorporated adjustments for sedentary time, BMI, waist circumference, tobacco use, alcohol use, and weekday/weekend sleep duration. Subsequently, weighted multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the effects of distinct physical activity patterns on total femoral BMD and femoral neck BMD.The robustness of the findings was rigorously verified through stratified analyses and sensitivity analyses involving the exclusion of cancer participants or the use of alternative definitions of weekend warriors. All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Notably, lifestyle variations such as alcohol use and tobacco use also demonstrated significant divergence across PA patterns (p < 0.001). Demographically, the WW group exhibited a disproportionately higher representation of younger adults, males, and non-Hispanic White individuals compared to the Inactive group.

Association between physical activity patterns and OP

As demonstrated in Table 2, physical activity patterns were categorized into inactive, WW, and RA. In Model 2, after adjusting for general demographic characteristics, both the Weekend Warrior group and the Regularly Active group demonstrated reduced osteoporosis risk compared to the Inactive group. In the fully adjusted Model 3, compared to the inactive group, both WW and RA participants showed reduced OP risk, though with differential significance: WW group: OR = 0.11 (95% CI: 0.03–0.36, P = 0.024); RA group: OR = 0.69 (95% CI: 0.47–1.00, P = 0.117). These findings suggest that achieving ≥ 1–2 weekly bouts of physical activity—even in a compressed weekend warrior pattern—may confer osteoprotective benefits for adults over 50 years old, with the WW strategy appearing particularly effective in attenuating OP risk compared to sustained inactivity.

Stratified analyses and sensitivity analyses

Based on the aforementioned findings, we further conducted stratified analyses by sex, age, race, education attainment, and BMI to examine whether weekend warrior groups exhibited consistent beneficial effects on femoral neck density (a diagnostic criterion for OP) compared to inactive groups across these subgroups (Table 5). We observed that, compared to inactive groups, the weekend warrior group showed a non-significant negative association with femoral neck density among Mexican Americans and individuals with less than high school education (p > 0.05). Moreover, no significant interaction effects were detected across subgroups.

Discussion

Through a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data from 2017 to 2020, our study confirmed an inverse association between physical activity and OP in individuals over 50 years old. Compared to inactive participants, people with a "weekend warrior" physical activity pattern had significantly greater femoral bone density and reduced osteoporosis risk, with these effects being independent of exercise intensity.

This study found that the weekend warrior group exhibited a reduced risk of osteoporosis compared to the inactive group. Based on existing literature, we hypothesize that this protective effect may be mediated through the following mechanisms: although the weekend warrior regimen typically involves moderate-intensity activity, its intermittent high-volume loading pattern may resemble the osteogenic biomechanical stimuli observed in high-intensity interval training (HIIT) [33, 34]. This specific intermittent mechanical loading elicits more sustained osteogenic effects on bone tissue compared to continuous low-intensity activities or sedentary paradigms [35]. Skeletal mechanoadaptation follows the "mechanostat threshold" principle, whereby intermittent high-magnitude loading potentiates greater osteoanabolic responses than prolonged low-intensity stimulation [36]. Secondly, the pulsatile nature of intermittent high-volume physical activity confers endocrine advantages by eliciting more robust episodic release of osteoanabolic hormones (e.g., growth hormone) [37]. This subsequently induces favorable cyclical shifts in bone turnover markers (BTMs), characterized by significant increases in bone formation coupled with suppressed resorption. Such "pulsatile osteometabolic effects" may be dampened during continuous conventional activities [38]. Finally, at the psychobehavioral level, the WW exercise paradigm mitigates decision fatigue inherent in daily workout regimens. Its goal-concentrated implementation likely enhances exercise adherence and physiological benefits through reinforced achievement perception [39, 40]. Notably, however, completing > 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) within only 1–2 sessions may paradoxically elevate acute skeletal injury risk.

Our study concurrently observed that while the RA group demonstrated reduced OP risk compared to sedentary controls, its effect magnitude was less pronounced than that of the WW cohort. This differential efficacy may principally stem from distinct exercise paradigms. Current evidence [41, 42] unequivocally demonstrates that non-weight-bearing activities (e.g., cycling, swimming) confer significantly weaker BMD improvements than impact weight-bearing exercises (e.g., running, jumping). This fundamental disparity may explain the inconsistent outcomes in the RA group—when RA cohorts include high proportions of non-weight-bearing activities, their osteoprotective effects become diluted. This suggests that the net skeletal benefit of exercise is contingent upon its capacity to generate osteogenic mechanical stimuli. Furthermore, populations maintaining high weekly exercise frequency demonstrate higher susceptibility to overtraining, which may contribute to negative outcomes through insufficient skeletal recovery (e.g., accelerated bone resorption) and elevated oxidative stress [43]. Notably, our findings revealed no direct association between PA intensity and OP risk—a result discordant with systematic reviews emphasizing high-intensity exercise benefits. This discrepancy may originate from inadequate control for exercise modality in prior studies [44]: when non-weight-bearing activities are present as confounders, the correlation between intensity metrics and osseous response becomes attenuated. Collectively, for adults over 50 years, the WW regimen represents a viable physical activity frequency alternative—particularly for time-constrained individuals or those unable to sustain daily exercise—delivering significant skeletal benefits when performed within personal tolerance thresholds and meeting weekly recommendations (≥ 150 min). Conversely, for unrestricted populations, optimizing the proportion of osteogenic weight-bearing activities within regular exercise routines may yield more pronounced skeletal health manifestations. Consequently, clinical practice should prioritize personalized exercise prescriptions tailored to individual health status and functional capacity.

Our study possesses several strengths, including the utilization of a large, nationally representative sample from the 2017–2020 NHANES dataset. Compared to prior studies [45, 46] , our research employed rigorous statistical analyses that incorporated a broader range of lifestyle and health-related covariates influencing OP, and examined their effects on BMD, thereby enhancing the credibility of our conclusions. Furthermore, we explored associations between distinct physical activity patterns and OP risk even among individuals meeting recommended activity duration thresholds. Notably, our study extended beyond the traditional focus on postmenopausal women to include all adults aged 50 years old, providing more generalizable evidence for public health strategies. Additionally, by investigating the interplay between physical activity duration, exercise intensity, and OP risk, we offer novel perspectives on developing detailed exercise prescriptions for skeletal health in aging populations. These findings advance OP prevention strategies through a multidimensional framework.

However, several limitations warrant emphasis in this study: first, the GPAQ lacks granularity to differentiate mechanical loading effects on bone metabolism across exercise modalities—specifically, its inability to distinguish weight-bearing from non-weight-bearing activities. Second, self-reported PA data remain subject to reporting bias; although the GPAQ is a validated and widely implemented instrument, objective measures such as accelerometry would provide more precise assessment of activity levels.Third, despite adjustment for numerous confounders, dataset limitations precluded ascertainment of subjects' developmental exercise history. The WW cohort may include former athletes whose current skeletal advantages could originate from weight-bearing activities during adolescence—rather than current regimens—as mechano-stimulation around peak height velocity may confer lifelong osteogenic advantages [47]. Such residual benefits could confound the causal inference between WW patterns and osteoporosis risk. Given the exploratory nature of this investigation, future prospective cohort studies should rigorously validate the current findings while overcoming the aforementioned limitations.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that adults aged ≥ 50 years who achieve the WHO-recommended 150-min weekly activity target through 1–2 leisure-time sessions exhibit significantly improved femoral BMD and reduced osteoporosis risk compared to inactive counterparts—without necessitating deliberate pursuit of specific intensity patterns. While our primary focus reveals protective associations between WW patterns and skeletal health, the potential injury risks inherent in such regimens warrant implementation of progressive transition protocols for previously sedentary older adults. Collectively, our study proposes the following protocol: firstly, Prioritize accumulating 150 min/week of physical activity within individual tolerance thresholds. Sencondly, initiate with single weekly sessions of moderate-intensity activity, progressively increasing frequency. Finally, before considering activity diversification, emphasize osteogenic weight-bearing exercises that confer skeletal advantages.
