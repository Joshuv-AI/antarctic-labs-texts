Acute kidney injury in acute decompensated heart failure: clinical predictors and In-hospital outcomes at a tertiary center
Authors: Yosra Turkistani, Fawaz Khateb, Fatma Aboul-Enein, Ghidi Saddiqi, Danah Laimooniah, Turki Qari, Jomana Qari, Sara Alharbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) frequently complicates acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) and is associated with adverse outcomes. Understanding its incidence, risk factors, and clinical consequences is essential for improving patient care.Methods We conducted a retrospective observational study of 512 patients admitted with ADHF to King Abdullah Medical City, Makkah, between January 2023 and January 2024. Demographic, clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory data were reviewed. AKI was defined using KDIGO criteria as a serum creatinine rise ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent predictors. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval 24-1280), with consent waived in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.Results AKI developed in 25% of patients (n = 122). Diabetes mellitus was an independent predictor (OR 2.26, 95% CI: 1.36–3.75, p < 0.001), and age ≥70 years was significantly associated (p = 0.002). Continuous diuretic infusion, however, was linked to lower AKI risk (OR 0.27, 95% CI: 0.18–0.42, p < 0.001). Patients with AKI had longer hospital stays compared with those without (12.0 ± 10.9 vs. 8.6 ± 12.3 days, p = 0.004). In-hospital mortality was higher in the AKI group (4.1% vs. 1.5%), though not statistically significant (OR 2.73, 95% CI: 0.82–9.10, p = 0.10).Conclusions AKI is a common complication among patients hospitalized with ADHF, particularly in older adults and those with diabetes. Continuous diuretic infusion may reduce risk. Early recognition and tailored management are critical to preventing kidney injury and improving outcomes. Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a leading cause of hospital admission and mortality worldwide, affecting an ­estimated 64 million individuals [1]. Acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) is a particularly severe form of HF, with an in-hospital mortality rate of approximately 10% [2]. Despite the advances in pharmacological management with beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors, patients still often develop complications like acute kidney injury (AKI) and present an enormous burden on health systems and patient quality of life [3,4].

AKI is a serious and frequent complication in the course of ADHF, developing in up to 40% of hospitalized patients [5]. Renal dysfunction in this context represents the consequence of several interrelated hemodynamic and non-hemodynamic factors, among them renal venous congestion, arterial hypoperfusion, and systemic inflammation [6–8]. Renal venous congestion is a major contributor to AKI in acute HF, also to worsening of HF, a vicious circle commonly described as cardiorenal syndrome. Consequently, effective decongestion can improve long-term renal outcomes, even if it causes a transient rise in creatinine levels [9].

However, diuretics are often associated with worsening renal function, which can lead to suboptimal dosing or even discontinuation of life-saving HF therapies (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, RAASi) [10,11]. Even though ∼90% of HF patients receive diuretics, the risk of renal impairment necessitates a carefully balanced approach to relieve congestion [12–14].

The prevalence of AKI is very high in HF, with an association with worse outcomes. Understanding the predictors and consequences of AKI is crucial for patient management. However, local data, especially from Middle Eastern populations, remains limited. Our study addresses this gap by providing insight into AKI incidence and clinical predictors, including the role of infusion-based diuretic therapy, among patients hospitalized with acute HF at King Abdullah Medical City (KAMC).

Methods

This retrospective observational study evaluated ADHF admissions at KAMC between January 1, 2023 and January 31, 2024. All admitted ADHF patients were included, most with Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF), although those with EF ≥40% were not excluded.

We collected data on demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, medication history (including admission medications), echocardiographic findings, key laboratory values, and outcomes (in-hospital complications, HF readmissions, discharge medications). Ethnicity and race data were not collected, as they were not relevant to the study objectives; the focus was on clinical predictors and outcomes.

All patients admitted with ADHF during the study period and managed at KAMC were included, excluding those with AKI from non-cardiac causes (e.g. sepsis, nephrotoxin exposure). AKI was defined according to KDIGO criteria as an increase in serum creatinine of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 h [15]. The flow of patient inclusion and exclusion is summarized in Figure 1.

Flow diagram of patient inclusion and exclusion. Of 512 screened patients, 4 were excluded due to missing AKI assessment, leaving 508 for analysis (122 developed AKI, 386 did not). AKI = acute kidney injury.

The data were anonymized using serial codes and analyzed using SPSS software. Continuous variables were compared using t tests (or Mann–Whitney tests if the data were non-normal), and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of AKI, including variables significant on univariate analysis or of known clinical importance. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. No artificial intelligence (AI) tools were used in this study.

The study protocol was approved by the King Abdullah Medical City Biomedical Research Ethics Committee, with approval number 24-1280. Ethical considerations included a consent waiver, ensuring patient confidentiality, and secure data storage.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The study included a total of 512 patients diagnosed with ADHF. The mean age of the cohort was 60.5 years, with males comprising 72.6% of the study population. Of the 512 patients, 25% (n = 122) developed AKI during hospitalization. Patients with AKI had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus 100 (82%) vs. 258 (66.8%) in the non-AKI group, p = 0.001. Baseline serum creatinine levels were slightly higher in AKI patients (2.0 vs. 1.9 mg/dL), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.40). NT-proBNP levels were 7,318 [1,813–14,451] vs. 3,652 [1,441–6,921] pg/mL (p = 0.53), and BNP levels were 1693 [441–2583] vs. 990 [472–2288] pg/mL (p = 0.64). Overall, 315 (73.7%) of patients had reduced LVEF <40%. This proportion was similar in the AKI and non-AKI groups, 80 (79.1%) vs 235 (72.6%), p = 0.551. Conversely, 149 (26.3%) of patients had an LVEF of 40% or higher, comprising 119 (27.4%) in the non-AKI group and 30 (20.9%) in the AKI group (p = 0.551). The severity of mitral regurgitation (MR) in the cohort was as follows: none in 147 (29.0%) patients, mild in 156 (30.8%), moderate in 116 (22.9%), and severe in 88 (17.4%). The incidence of AKI tended to vary by MR grade: 31 (25.4%), 48 (39.3%), 28 (23.0%), 15 (12.3%) for none/mild/moderate/severe MR, respectively, but this trend did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.074; see Table 1)

Clinical predictors of AKI

Our analysis demonstrated that certain clinical characteristics were strongly associated with an increased risk of developing AKI. Multivariate logistic regression identified diabetes mellitus (OR 2.26, 95% CI: 1.36 to 3.75, p < 0.001), advanced age ≥70 years (p = 0.002), and diuretic infusion therapy (OR 0.27, 95% CI: 0.18 to 0.42, p < 0.001) as independent predictors of AKI. Elevated baseline blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were also associated with higher odds of AKI but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08). These findings are visually summarized in Figure 2. Male and female patients had comparable risk of AKI (p = 0.885). AKI was observed less frequently in patients with a history of ischemic heart disease (IHD) 34 (27.9%) vs. 88 (72.1%), p = 0.001. We found no significant relationship between the etiology of heart failure and the occurrence of AKI (p = 0.258, Table 2). For instance, the proportion of non-ischemic cardiomyopathy was similar in AKI vs non-AKI (approximately 69.7% vs 63.7% of those subsets), and other etiologies showed no clear differences.

Impact on the choice of pharmacological therapy

Standard HF medications, such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), and spironolactone, were administered less often in AKI patients. ACE inhibitors and ARBs were administered to 42 (34.4%) of patients with AKI, compared to 80 (65.6%) of those without AKI (p = 0.003), indicating that clinicians were less likely to continue these medications in the setting of AKI. Nearly all patients in both groups received diuretics, with approximately 87% in each group (p > 0.05).

Impact of diuretic administration on AKI

Among patients receiving various diuretic regimens, those on IV loop diuretics had the highest incidence of AKI, at 23 (18.9%) in that subgroup, whereas those managed with oral diuretics only had the lowest incidence, at 6 (4.9%). Although IV and mixed methods showed higher unadjusted AKI incidence rates, multivariate logistic regression revealed that diuretic infusion therapy was independently associated with significantly lower odds of AKI (OR 0.27, 95% CI: 0.18–0.42, p < 0.001).

This suggests that patients receiving continuous infusion were likely a high-risk subset (hence the greater unadjusted AKI incidence), and after adjusting for illness severity and other factors, continuous infusion therapy itself was associated with a lower risk of AKI. In other words, the higher crude AKI rates with IV infusion reflect confounding by indication, whereas a controlled infusion may actually mitigate kidney injury risk compared to intermittent boluses.

In-hospital outcomes

Patients with AKI had prolonged hospital stays compared to those without AKI (12 ± 10.9 days vs. 8.6 ± 12.3 days, p = 0.004; see Table 4). In-hospital mortality was higher in the AKI group (4.1% vs. 1.5% in non-AKI), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (OR 2.73, 95% CI 0.82–9.10; p = 0.10). Table 4 summarizes other in-hospital outcomes and laboratory differences. Notably, by discharge, AKI patients had worse renal function markers (e.g. higher peak creatinine and BUN, lower eGFR), consistent with the definition of AKI, and slightly lower hemoglobin levels, whereas electrolyte levels were only minimally different. The association between the number of hospitalizations for heart failure within the previous year and the occurrence of acute kidney injury (AKI) was assessed. We found no significant association between the number of HF hospitalizations in the previous year and the risk of AKI during the index admission (p = 0.834, Table 5). Patients with multiple prior admissions did not have higher AKI rates than those with none or one prior admission.

Discussion

This study highlights the significant burden of AKI among patients with ADHF managed at a tertiary center. The 25% incidence of AKI in our cohort lies within the broad range reported in prior studies (approximately 15–40% of hospitalized ADHF patients) [16]. This aligns with Shetty et al. [17] and Zhou et al. [18], who observed similar AKI rates, while variations in AKI rates were observed in other studies, such as Xu et al. [19], reporting a lower incidence of 16.9% among HF patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, likely due to procedural differences and risk factors. Conversely, Ronco et al. [20] noted higher AKI rates (30–40%), potentially reflecting differing diagnostic criteria and patient demographics. The study identified diabetes mellitus (OR 2.26, p < 0.001) as an independent predictor of AKI, consistent with findings from Ronco et al. [20], That underscore the significant role of diabetes in renal dysfunction among HF patients. Although advanced age (≥70 years) and elevated baseline BUN showed significant associations with higher AKI risk on univariate analysis (both had notable p values), neither proved to be an independent predictor in the multivariate logistic regression. In our adjusted model, the effects of age and BUN were accounted for by other covariates, indicating that these factors were not independently driving AKI risk once other variables were taken into consideration. In practical terms, older patients and those with high BUN were more likely to develop AKI; however, this was likely due to the presence of other risk factors (e.g. worse baseline renal function or comorbidities). When these factors were included in the model, age and BUN per se lost statistical significance. Interestingly, ischemic heart disease appeared to be less common among AKI patients (27.9% vs. 72.1%, p = 0.001). This finding was unexpected, as ischemic heart disease is often associated with cardiorenal syndrome in other studies. One possible explanation is that patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy in our cohort might have received more aggressive upfront management (such as revascularization or careful optimization of HF therapy), which could have preserved renal perfusion and mitigated AKI during admission. Additionally, those without IHD (e.g. patients with non-ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy) may have had more acute inflammation or higher diuretic requirements, predisposing them to AKI. It is also plausible that IHD patients were managed more cautiously with regard to renal function (given their known cardiac risk), or that unmeasured confounders (such as use of certain medications like SGLT2 inhibitors or baseline renal reserve) played a role. Notably, Shrestha et al. [21] observed a lack of significant renal tubular injury in ADHF patients who developed AKI, as urinary NGAL levels rose only modestly in those who subsequently developed AKI. This finding suggests that AKI in acute HF is largely functional (hemodynamic) rather than due to intrinsic structural damage, reinforcing the notion that maintaining renal perfusion (e.g. through prompt revascularization or careful hemodynamic support in ischemic HF) can limit true kidney injury, Banerjee et al. likewise highlight the need to distinguish these functional (hemodynamic) AKI episodes from true structural renal injury in acute HF [9]. Classically, a low fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa <1%) or urea (FeUrea <35%) indicates a pre-renal, hemodynamic cause of AKI, whereas higher values suggest intrinsic structural injury. However, loop diuretics can raise FeNa even in volume-depleted states, so FeUrea is often used as an alternative index in diuretic-treated patients. Importantly, urine sediment examination (e.g. finding muddy brown casts characteristic of acute tubular necrosis) frequently outperforms FeNa/FeUrea in distinguishing structural AKI from a functional decline [22]. Additionally, emerging biomarkers of tubular injury, such as NGAL and KIM-1, can detect structural kidney damage earlier than creatinine levels rise [21], although these are not yet part of routine practice in acute HF management. This perspective aligns with our observations and may help explain why aggressively managed ischemic HF patients in our cohort appeared less prone to severe AKI. This paradoxical result warrants further investigation to determine whether a protective mechanism exists or if it reflects characteristics unique to our patient population.

Subgroup analysis of diuretic administration revealed significant variations in AKI incidence. Intravenous loop diuretics were associated with the highest incidence (18.9%), while oral-only administration resulted in the lowest incidence (4.9%). We observed, in line with Zhou et al. [18], that more aggressive diuresis (IV loops) was associated with higher AKI incidence, whereas oral-only diuretic therapy carried a much lower risk. This highlights the need to balance the efficacy of decongestion against the risk of AKI.

Notably, our adjusted analysis revealed a protective association with diuretic infusion, despite higher unadjusted AKI rates in these patients. This apparent contradiction can be explained by confounding factors sicker patients were more likely to receive continuous infusion, inflating the unadjusted AKI incidence in that group. After controlling for baseline risk, continuous infusion was found to be protective. One possible reason is that a steady infusion provides gradual diuresis and avoids abrupt intravascular volume shifts, potentially safeguarding renal perfusion compared to repeated high-dose boluses.

This finding contrasts with raw AKI incidence data, which showed higher rates using infusion and mixed methods. The adjusted association highlights the importance of accounting for confounding factors, such as illness severity, which can influence diuretic choice and AKI risk.

In-hospital outcomes were notably worse for patients with AKI, with prolonged hospital stays (12.02 ± 10.9 days vs. 8.6 ± 12.3 days, p = 0.004) and higher mortality rates (4.1% vs. 1.5% in non-AKI, OR 2.73, 95% CI 0.82–9.10; p = 0.10). Our results are in line with prior reports that AKI during HF hospitalization portends higher mortality and morbidity [16]. For instance, See et al. [23] and Shirakabe et al. [24] similarly documented increased mortality and complications in HF patients who develop AKI. A recent meta-analysis further found that AKI in HF is associated with a ∼3.6-fold increase in in-hospital death risk [16]. Taken together, these comparisons underscore that our single-center findings align with broader trends in the literature regarding AKI frequency, predictors, and outcomes in acute heart failure.

However, the prognostic impact of WRF may depend on its etiology; Banerjee et al. observed that patients who developed AKI while achieving decongestion had lower mortality than those who avoided AKI but remained congested [9]. Thus, a ‘functional’ AKI accompanying successful therapy appears relatively benign, emphasizing the need to differentiate it from true renal injury when assessing patient outcomes.

This study provides region-specific data from a Middle Eastern advanced heart failure center, which is underrepresented in the literature. Most prior studies examining AKI in ADHF were conducted in Western or East Asian populations, and Middle Eastern cohorts remain significantly underrepresented. Our data thus add meaningful insight into how demographic factors, regional differences in comorbidities (e.g. higher diabetes prevalence), and local treatment practices (such as routine diuretic infusion therapy) might uniquely influence AKI risk and outcomes. Our cohort’s notably high diabetes prevalence and routine use of continuous diuretic infusion (a practice less common in Western settings) may partly explain some differences. In fact, we found that continuous infusion was associated with a lower risk of AKI after adjustment (despite higher unadjusted rates of AKI), a nuance that prior Western studies have not reported. These regional and practice differences add to the literature by suggesting that tailored management strategies (informed by local patient profiles and hospital practices) can influence AKI outcomes in acute HF.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center analysis, which introduces potential selection bias and may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, we did not standardize diuretic dosing; for example, we did not convert all diuretic regimens to a furosemide-equivalent dose. As a result, variability in dosing and route of administration could have influenced AKI incidence and was not fully accounted for in our analysis. In future studies, we plan to standardize loop diuretic dosing by converting all agents to furosemide-equivalent doses, which will enhance the precision of dose-response assessments and help clarify whether the risk of AKI is related to the route of administration, total diuretic burden, or patient-specific factors. Third, there may be unmeasured confounding factors. While we collected comprehensive clinical data, factors such as the exact timing of interventions, fluid balance status, or other nephrotoxic exposures were not captured and could have influenced the risk of AKI. Fourth, due to data limitations, we were unable to include cardiogenic shock or vasopressor use, both of which could significantly influence renal outcomes. Fifth, the relatively short study period and lack of long-term follow-up data mean we could not assess post-discharge renal outcomes or recovery from AKI. Sixth, our cohort was predominantly male (72.6%) with a very high prevalence of diabetes mellitus. This demographic skew may limit the generalizability of our findings or introduce bias, as patient sex and comorbidities can influence AKI risk. Finally, our definition of AKI (based on KDIGO criteria) may have captured mostly in-hospital changes, so subtler forms of renal dysfunction might have been missed. These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting our results. Ideally, future studies should be prospective and multi-center to confirm and expand upon our findings.

Clinical implications

Based on our findings, clinicians managing acute HF should be vigilant about AKI, especially in high-risk patients. Our data suggest that patients with certain risk factors, notably diabetes mellitus, merit closer monitoring of renal function during aggressive diuresis. To help reduce the incidence of AKI, clinicians could implement renal-sparing strategies. For example, adjusting diuretic protocols such as using a continuous infusion at a controlled rate (as practiced at our center) or combining loop diuretics with adjunctive diuretics may achieve adequate diuresis with lower doses. High-risk patients might also benefit from more frequent serum creatinine checks and strict intake/output monitoring, while early nephrology consultation is advised when signs of worsening renal function appear. Furthermore, adhering to guidelines for HF management while being mindful of renal perfusion is crucial. Clinicians should avoid hypotension and nephrotoxic medications (NSAIDs, certain antibiotics) during treatment. In cases of diuretic resistance or severe volume overload, alternatives such as ultrafiltration may be considered with caution, balancing the benefits of congestion relief against potential renal effects. In summary, an individualized approach that balances effective decongestion with renal protection utilizing strategies such as careful diuretic titration, hemodynamic support to maintain renal perfusion, and regular monitoring should be adopted for patients at risk of AKI. These strategies, informed by our study, aim to improve patient safety and outcomes by proactively mitigating AKI risk in acute heart failure care.
