Preoperative focused echocardiography on patients with fractured neck of femur. ECHONOF-III trial: study protocol for a multicenter randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ximena Cid-Serra, Alistair Royse, Andrea Bowyer, David Canty, Daniel Sessler, Sandy Clarke-Errey, Andre Denault, Dina LoGiudice, Guy Ludbrook, D-Yin Lin, Yang Yang, Kavi Haji, Lisa Zucherrelli, Andrew J Palmer, Benjamin Piper, Thar Nyan Lwin, Stefan Dieleman, David Eccleston, Alwin Chuan, Richard Cooper, Gregory Henderson, Colin Royse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHip fractures are common in older people and are associated with high perioperative mortality. Prompt surgical intervention within the first 48 h of fraction reduces complications; however, surgical urgency often precludes a comprehensive preoperative cardiac evaluation. Preliminary data suggests that performing a focused cardiac ultrasound (FCU) before surgery may reduce postoperative complications. We therefore propose to test the primary hypothesis that FCU reduces a 30-day composite of mortality and serious complications.MethodsWe plan a definitive multicenter pragmatic randomized trial that will enroll 2000 adults with hip fractures. Participants will be randomized before surgery to either receive FCU as part of their preoperative assessment or to routine care without FCU. FCU is a brief, 10-min, goal-directed echocardiography performed at the patient’s bedside. FCU will be conducted by physicians trained in FCU and the information gathered will be shared immediately with all clinicians involved in the patient’s care (anesthesiologists, orthopedic surgeons, geriatricians). The primary outcome will be a 30-day composite of all-cause mortality, hospital readmission, acute kidney injury, cardiac failure, and myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery. Secondary and exploratory outcomes include hospital length of stay, days to return to original residence, postoperative recovery, and quality of life. Additionally, a health cost analysis will be conducted to weigh costs against benefits.DiscussionOur large RCT aims to determine whether preoperative focused ultrasound examinations of hip fracture patients reduce serious postoperative complications, improves the quality of recovery, improves life quality, and is cost-effective.Trial registrationRegistration number: ACTRN12622001546741. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Hip fractures, often resulting from falls, are a prevalent and serious medical problem among frail and elderly individuals, with cases expected to reach 4.5 million per year globally by 2050 [1]. Despite advancements in healthcare, outcomes for hip fracture patients remain poor, characterized by high mortality rates and significant long-term care needs related to the high prevalence of medical comorbidities, advanced age, and frailty [2]. Successful hip surgery may continue to negatively impacts patients and potentially places a substantial burden on their families, communities, and healthcare systems, leading to increased costs and resource demands. Although urgent surgery is crucial for restoring independence and mobility, it is among the highest-risk medical procedures. Approximately 10% of patients die within 30 days of surgery, and 26% within a year [3]. Factors such as older age, male gender, and comorbidities have been recognized to increase mortality risk [4]. Many of the adverse outcomes are attributable to underlying cardiac and respiratory diseases, which are often undiagnosed at the time of surgery [5].

Focused echocardiography or focused cardiac ultrasound (FCU) is an abbreviated form of transthoracic echocardiography that may detect significant heart disease. It is simple to perform, taking only 5–10 min, and can be performed at the bedside by any trained practitioner. FCU accurately evaluates conditions like heart failure, aortic stenosis, and pulmonary hypertension, which are independent risk factors for postoperative death. By identifying heart disease and shock states accurately, medical teams can manage patients more effectively, maximizing safety and optimizing outcomes [6].

In a randomized pilot study involving 100 patients with hip fractures, the use of FCU led to a 39% reduction in a composite outcome of death, acute kidney injury, stroke, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, or cardiac arrest within 30 days post-surgery [7, 8]. Despite its potential, the widespread adoption of FCU faces challenges due to the significant costs of equipment and training and the lack of definitive evidence showing improved patient outcomes. Therefore, a large pragmatic randomized trial is necessary.

This clinical trial will involve 2000 participants across 12 sites, following a similar protocol as the pilot study. It aims to evaluate whether adding an FCU prior to surgery in patients with fractured neck of femur reduces the incidence of a 30-day composite outcome of all-cause mortality, hospital readmission, acute kidney injury, cardiac failure, or myocardial injury in noncardiac surgery.

Objectives {7}

The study aims to determine whether routine FCU before hip fracture surgery leads to a reduced composite outcome of all-cause mortality, cardiac failure, myocardial injury in noncardiac surgery, acute kidney injury, or hospital readmission at 30 days post-surgery in hip fracture patients compared to patients who do not receive preoperative FCU (the current standard of care).

The secondary objectives of this trial are to evaluate the effect of preoperative FCU in patients with hip fracture on postoperative recovery and to assess the cost-effectiveness of this intervention.

Trial design {8}

This is a parallel group, pragmatic, randomized controlled multicenter trial in which patients with hip fractures are randomly allocated to receive or not receive preoperative FCU in a 1:1 allocation before hip fracture surgery.

Eligibility criteria {10}

Inclusion criteria

Adults presenting with isolated, primary, non-metastatic hip fracture, where surgery is expected within 48 h after hospital admission.

Eligibility is determined according to the initial clinical expectation at the time of admission. Patients who are anticipated to undergo surgery within 48 h, but whose procedures are subsequently delayed remain eligible for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria include prior hip surgery on the affected side, likely metastatic cancer, where survival is unlikely in the 24 h from admission, or who have had an echocardiography or FCU in the last 30 days before the admission.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Participant consent will be obtained as the primary method of consent. For participants unable to provide their own consent, in-person or telephone consent will be sought from relatives or individuals with authorized medical treatment decision-maker.

If the patient can provide consent, a research nurse will approach them directly, explain the study, provide a hard copy of the Participant Information and Consent Form (PICF), and obtain written consent.

If the patient is unable to consent, the research nurse will identify the authorized person responsible for providing consent, typically the same individual who would consent for the patient’s surgery. If this person is present, the researcher will inform them in person; if they are not present, the researcher will attempt to contact them via telephone, using pre-approved telephone scripts. In these instances, verbal consent will be accepted, witnessed by a third party who is not involved in the research project, with written consent obtained at a later time when feasible.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Not applicable as this protocol does not involve acquiring data or biological specimens for ancillary studies.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The control group represents the current standard of care. The preoperative assessment is conducted based on medical history, physical examination, blood investigations, and electrocardiogram (ECG). However, the current standard of care does not involve a routine focused cardiac ultrasound.

Intervention description {11a}

In addition to clinical evaluation, participants randomized to the FCU group will receive a preoperative FCU performed at the patient’s bedside prior to surgery following the University of Melbourne iHeartScan protocol [9]. The clinician who performs the FCU will be qualified to the equivalent of a University Graduate Certificate in Clinical Ultrasound and be specifically trained in the iHeartScan protocol. This protocol relies on 2D imaging and Color Flow Doppler for all cardiac assessments, and it is the same protocol used in the pilot study upon which this trial is based. The following cardiac variables are evaluated: size and systolic function of the left and right ventricle; interatrial septum movement; hemodynamically significant regurgitation or stenosis of the cardiac valves (typically moderate to severe stenosis or regurgitation); presence or absence of pericardial effusion; diameter and collapsibility of the inferior vena cava; and a final comment of the hemodynamic state [10]. In line with a pragmatic trial, any qualified member of the treating team may perform the FCU using the ultrasound machine available at their center and will complete a written iHeartScan report summarizing the main findings. The information will be available to all members of the treating team before and after surgery, including surgeons, anesthesiologists, intensivists, internal medicine physicians, ortho-geriatricians, and emergency medicine physicians.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

In the FCU group, the ultrasound examination will be discontinued at any time if it is causing discomfort or pain or upon the participant’s request.

Participants in the control group will not routinely receive an FCU. However, in cases of clinical deterioration or hemodynamic instability, there is no restriction for the anesthesiologist, or any other clinician involved in patient care to perform an FCU if deemed necessary.

These instances will be recorded but not reported as protocol deviations. Participants will remain in the study, and the data will be analyzed using an intention-to-treat approach.

Drawing from previous experiences, we have devised several strategies to enhance recruitment and adherence to the intervention.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

This is a pragmatic study. No concomitant care or investigations are prohibited. If deemed necessary by the treating team, a comprehensive echocardiography can be requested for patients in both groups.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

There is no payment provided to participants for their involvement in this trial. While participants will not receive direct compensation, this research may contribute to advancements in patient care that will benefit the wider community in the long term. The protocol does not include provisions for ancillary care or compensation for trial-related harms.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is a 30-day composite outcome of the incidence of at least one of the following: all-cause mortality, hospital readmission, acute kidney injury, decompensated heart failure, and myocardial injury in noncardiac surgery. The occurrence of this composite outcome will be compared between the FCU and control groups using an average relative effect test.

Data related to the primary outcome will be collected by designated research nurses through hospital visits, review of hospital records and database, and by telephone, and verified against the death register and through the patient’s family doctor or hospital records if required. Supportive documentation and test results will be collected for all events and reviewed by the endpoint adjudication committee. The primary outcome data will be verified by two independent reviewers blinded to allocation. In instances where there is a disagreement between the two adjudicators, a third adjudicator will arbitrate the decision.

Secondary outcomes

1-year composite outcome incidence: The incidence of the composite outcome (all-cause mortality, hospital readmission, acute kidney injury, decompensated heart failure, and myocardial injury in noncardiac surgery) at 1-year post-surgery in both groups.

Time until death or end of follow-up: The average number of days from surgery to death or end of follow-up, whichever occurs first, will be compared between groups.

Days Alive and at Home at Day 30 (DAH30): The average number of days patients are alive and back to their usual residence within 30 days after surgery will be compared between groups.

Hospital length of stay: The number of days from hospital admission to hospital discharge will be compared between groups.

In-hospital infection incidence: The incidence of reported in-hospital infections, including hospital-acquired pneumonia, surgical site infections (superficial and deep), and urinary tract infections, will be compared between groups.

In-hospital respiratory pathology: The incidence of imaging-diagnosed respiratory pathology while patients are in the hospital will be compared between groups. Lung imaging includes chest X-ray, CT chest scan, or lung ultrasound, and pathologies include pleural effusion, atelectasis, consolidation, or pneumothorax.

Other outcomes

Destination post discharge: The proportion of patients in both groups discharged to the previous place of residence, rehabilitation center, new aged care facility, and other.

Length of stay in rehabilitation hospital: The average number of days from admission to rehabilitation hospital to discharge will be compared between groups.

Polypharmacy: The proportion of participants taking five or more medications will be compared between groups at day 30 post-surgery.

Frailty: Measured using the Clinical Frailty Scale preoperatively, at 30 days, and 1 year post-surgery. Mean scores at these three time points will be compared between groups.

Quality of recovery: Measured using the Postoperative Quality of Recovery Scale (PostopQRS), a validated multidimensional scale assessing physiological, cognitive, activities of daily living, emotive, and nociceptive (including pain) domains. This will be performed preoperatively, on day 1, days 3–5, at 30 days, and at 1 year. The proportion of complete postoperative recovery (return to baseline) at each timepoint will be compared between groups.

Independent living: Assessed using the Lawton and Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) Scale, performed before surgery, at 30 days, and at 1 year. This scale assesses the skills necessary to live independently in the community. Mean scores will be compared between groups.

Qualitative assessment of personal impact: This will be assessed using the PROMEQ survey scale. It will be conducted before surgery, at 30 days, and at 1 year.

Health economic analysis: Additional costs of routine FCU will be balanced against other costs due to changes in the duration of hospital stay, type of ward, and medical resource consumption due to improved survival. Consent will be sought to obtain the total costs of care in each group over the study period. Healthcare resource consumption, including hospitalizations, general practice and specialist consultations, investigations, and medications, will be obtained. Units of healthcare resources consumed will be combined with Australian unit costs derived from the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme, Medicare, and the Australian Refined Diagnosis Related Groups (AR-DRGs). Additional costs of the intervention will be accounted for. Health utility, a summary measure of the quality of life, will be measured using the EQ-5D-5L, a standard, internationally validated instrument with Australian population norms. The EQ-5D-5L will be administered in written (or verbal) form (takes less than 1 min to complete) at baseline as part of the preoperative patient assessment and at 30 days and 12 months as part of the follow-up.

Sample size {14}

Sample size determination was performed by a consulting statistician at the University of Melbourne. Estimates are based on our pilot data with a control group incidence of the primary outcome of 23.5% and a relative effect size of 39.6%. We will choose a conservative but clinically important relative effect size of 25%. For a two-tailed design, with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.9, a sample size of 984 participants per group will be required. A single-blinded interim analysis will be conducted after 50% of patients have been recruited according to the method of O’Brien and Fleming. The trial will be stopped if the primary outcome measure is different at p < 0.0026. To allow for this interim analysis, the sample size will be increased to 992, which will be rounded to 1000 participants in each group.

Recruitment {15}

Eligible patients will be identified from hospital admission records or the emergency booking theater system. The research staff will screen them for eligibility, and then they will be approached to explain the study and obtain consent. Following consent, they will be randomized to the standard of care or standard of care plus FCU groups.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Randomization will be managed by a clinical research organization (CRO): the Neuroscience Trials Australia (NTA). Randomization codes will be stratified by type of surgery (prosthesis—hemiarthroplasty vs osteosynthesis—femoral nail) and by the tertiary or secondary hospital in uneven blocks using a web-based automatic enrollment system at participating institutions to facilitate recruitment across time zones and out-of-hours cases. The use of uneven blocks for each stratum ensures a close balance of numbers in each group at any time during the trial.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The allocation sequence will be concealed by the web-based enrollment system.

Implementation {16c}

The web-based enrollment system generates and conceals the allocation sequence. At each center, a research nurse coordinator or medical researcher is responsible for approaching suitable candidates, obtaining informed consent, and enrolling them in the study. Once participants have consented, they use the web-based enrollment system in REDCap to allocate them to either the intervention or control group.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and care providers are not blinded to group allocation. As the inclusion of the FCU in the clinical records is essential for all members of the treating team to access it, and in accordance with a pragmatic trial, the research staff who will collect outcome data cannot be blinded.

However, the members of the Endpoints Adjudication Committee and the Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) will be blinded. They will receive the list of participants and their data without information on group allocation, allowing them to perform a blind analysis.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Not applicable as neither the participants nor the clinicians are blinded.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Data will be collected by research nurses through hospital visits, review of hospital records and databases, and by telephone. This data will be verified against the death register and through the patient’s general practitioner (or family doctor) or hospital records if required.

Supportive documentation and test results will be collected for all events and reviewed by the endpoint adjudication committee. Data will be stored in the REDCap electronic Case Report Form (eCRF) and the PostopQRS web database, both used in multiple studies at Melbourne Health. Data will be re-identifiable to allow for multiple follow-up points and endpoint verification. However, all data will be de-identified for analysis and presentation, including publication.

Types of data collected:

I.Demographic information: Obtained by a researcher directly from the patient and hospital records at the time of recruitment.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

Most of the required information is gathered while the patient is in the hospital or immediately after discharge. However, we anticipate some participants might be lost to follow-up after 30 days. Therefore, patient selection is crucial; we prioritize those keen on participating, avoiding persistent efforts to convince reluctant individuals. We clearly explain the time commitment and data involved in the surveys, which is usually the main source of potential missing data. After hospital discharge, surveys are performed over the phone to reduce potential data loss, as participants do not need to come back to the hospital. The rest of the clinical outcomes are collected from medical records.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The statistical methods have been constructed by a team statistician and in consultation with the senior statistician of the Outcomes Research Division of the Cleveland Clinic, OH, USA (A/Prof Mascher).

Primary outcome analysis: Intention to treat analysis will be performed. The primary composite outcome will be compared between FCU and control groups using a distinct effect generalized estimating equation (GEE) model, specifically, an average relative effect test to determine the common treatment effect across the six components. The heterogeneity of the treatment effects across components will be formally assessed by component interaction test in the GEE. This approach considers the different frequencies of each component, reducing the risk of a single component dominating the composite outcome.

Secondary outcome analysis: All secondary outcomes will be compared between the two groups, with methodology depending on outcome type. The same GEE model used for the 30-day composite will be repeated at 1 year, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis will be used for the time to event data, and comparison of proportions will be used for event data.

Interim analyses {21b}

A single-blinded interim analysis will be conducted by the DSMB after 50% of patients have been recruited according to the method of O’Brien and Fleming. The trial will be stopped if the primary outcome measure is different at p < 0.0026.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

Subgroup analysis: Subgroup analyses will assess the treatment-by-subgroup interaction within the GEE model framework for the following three factors, which are considered important contributors to the heterogeneity of treatment effect: (1) age 65–75 versus age > 75 years; (2) presence of cardiac symptoms versus asymptomatic; and (3) living at home versus aged care facility, (4) hospitals, (5) type of surgical implant (hemiarthroplasty versus femoral nail).

Health economic analysis will be performed by our team health economist. Additional costs of routine FCU will be balanced against other costs due to changes in the duration of hospital stay, type of ward, and medical resource consumption due to improved survival. Consent will be sought to obtain the total costs of care in each group over the study period. Healthcare resource consumption in each group, including hospitalizations, general practice and specialist consultations, investigations, and medications, will be obtained. Units of healthcare resources consumed will be combined with Australian unit costs derived from the Pharmaceutical Benefit Scheme, Medicare, and the AR-DRGs. Additional costs of the intervention will be accounted for. Additionally, health utility, a summary measure of quality of life, will be measured using the EQ-5D-5L, a standard, internationally validated instrument with Australian population norms. The EQ-5D-5L will be administered in written form (takes < 1 min to complete) at baseline as part of the preoperative patient assessment and at 30 days and 12 months as part of the patient follow-up assessment. Health utility differences between each group, combined with survival differences, will allow the calculation of quality-adjusted life expectancy and, thus, incremental costs per quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained. It is hypothesized that the additional costs of intervention will be partially or fully offset by reductions in the cost of hospitalization. Changes in QALYs will be balanced against differences in total costs for each group, enabling assessment of the incremental cost-effectiveness of the intervention versus the current standard of care for the study period. In addition, a Markov simulation model will be developed using TreeAge Decision Analysis software to project trial results to patient lifetimes. Univariate sensitivity analysis will be performed to identify parameters with the greatest impact on projected costs and clinical outcomes. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis, involving simultaneous sampling for distributions of key parameters, will be used to account for uncertainty in multiple parameters.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Stringent efforts will be made to avoid missing data through careful follow-up at multiple time points by research staff and frequent data quality assessment, noting any reasons given for loss to follow-up where available. If only a few component values are missing, and this appears to be balanced between the randomized groups, the main analysis will assume the major event for missing components did not happen. Depending on the extent and nature of the missingness, multiple imputation may also be employed to handle missing data, using a separate imputation model for each treatment group.

An independent DSMB comprising a statistician, clinical monitor, clinician, and a member of the Melbourne Health Ethics Committee oversees trial safety and data integrity. The DSMB will perform an interim blind analysis when 50% of recruitment is achieved.

Adverse events are monitored by our CRO and reported to the coordinating team and the chief principal investigator. In accordance with regulatory guidelines and site policy, adverse events may also be reported to the trial sponsor or the reviewing ethics committee.

Discussion

Patients presenting with hip fractures are typically elderly and frail. In addition to osteoporosis and risk factors for falls, many of these patients have cardiovascular diseases that influence their prognosis [3, 4, 17]. This trial aims to establish proof of concept regarding the benefits of using FCU in the preoperative assessment of patients with hip fractures, with the potential for broader application to perioperative settings in general.

The addition of FCU has demonstrated the provision of relevant clinical information to anesthesiologists, leading to changes in management during surgery and the immediate postoperative period. These changes include adjustments in fluid management, the need for inotropes, and the determination of the post-surgery destination unit [6, 18]. By influencing the clinician’s decision-making process and management plan, FCU may improve clinical outcomes, as suggested by our pilot data. For this intervention to have a significant impact, decision-making doctors need to be familiar with this technique. Fortunately, it is feasible to conduct such a study in Australia, as FCU has been used by anesthesiologists for over 15 years, with approximately 3500 Australian anesthesiologists trained in this tool.

Furthermore, in this trial, we ensure that the information gathered through FCU is also available to all clinicians involved in patient care and integrated into their assessments. We believe that this approach will enhance its impact on the long-term outcomes. For instance, if FCU identifies heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), the patient may be placed on HFrEF medical treatment by the geriatrician or physician involved in their postoperative care, benefiting from the proven mortality and outcome improvements associated with these medications. Similarly, if significant aortic stenosis is detected and the patient is a candidate for transcatheter aortic valve implantation or aortic valve replacement surgery, appropriate assessment and organization can be undertaken.

This trial faces several challenges inherent to conducting a multicenter study. Coordinating and ensuring adherence to the protocol require rigorous supervision and efficient communication. Once sites are initiated after a brief initial stage, we have observed exceptional acceptance and compliance from participants, their families, and their medical teams. This makes us optimistic about achieving our recruitment goals.
