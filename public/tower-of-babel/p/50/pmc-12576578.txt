M2 Macrophage‐Derived Migrasomes Mediate Ischaemia‐Induced Retinal Neovascularization by Targeting TREM2
Authors: Bingyan Li, Junyu Chen, Junye Zhu, Haixiang Zhou, Qiuxiang Zhang, Hui Deng, Haipeng Wen, Fan Xu, Fen Tang, Shigeo Yoshida, Yedi Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRetinal neovascular diseases are leading causes of global blindness. Migrasomes, organelles released during cell migration, play a role in intercellular communication and are present in M2 macrophages, which are critical to the pathology of retinal neovascular diseases. This study investigates the involvement of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes in ischaemia‐induced retinal neovascularization (RNV). Migrasomes are isolated from macrophages and characterized by Western blotting and transmission electron microscopy. Compared with controls, M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes significantly enhance human retinal microvascular endothelial cell (HREC) functions by Cell Counting Kit‐8, transwell, and tube formation assays, and markedly contribute to the pathological retinal angiogenesis of oxygen‐induced retinopathy (OIR) mice. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is selected as the potential downstream target of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes by proteomic analysis. Moreover, the depletion of M2 macrophages in OIR retinas reduces the levels of migrasomes and TREM2. BTC and PLA1A overexpression in HRECs could attenuate decreased HREC functions induced by sh‐TREM2 M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes. These findings demonstrate that TREM2‐enriched M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes contribute to pathological RNV in vivo and positively regulate HREC functions in vitro through targeting TREM2‐BTC/PLA1A, which may serve as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for retinal neovascular diseases.Keywords: ischaemia‐induced retinal neovascularization, M2 macrophage, migrasome, retinal microvascular endothelial cell, TREM2

Introduction

Retinal neovascular diseases encompass a range of ophthalmic conditions driven by pathological angiogenesis, such as retinopathy of prematurity, proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), and retinal vein occlusion, all of which lead to substantial visual impairment (Campochiaro and Akhlaq 2021; Fevereiro‐Martins et al. 2023). Current therapeutic strategies for retinal neovascularization (RNV) primarily include retinal laser photocoagulation, vitrectomy, and intravitreal administration of anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (anti‐VEGF) agents (Alsoudi et al. 2024; Alsoudi et al. 2024). Despite the widespread clinical use of anti‐VEGF therapy (Arrigo et al. 2022), it presents several drawbacks, including high cost, limited efficacy in certain patients, and potential disruption of normal retinal architecture (Xu et al. 2022). Thus, further exploration of the mechanisms underlying RNV and new therapeutic targets remains imperative.

Macrophages, key components of the mononuclear phagocyte system, are innate immune cells distributed across various tissues (Shapouri‐Moghaddam et al. 2018). Depending on the stimuli they encounter, macrophages adopt distinct phenotypes and functions (Kadomoto et al. 2021). The primary classifications are the pro‐inflammatory, anti‐tumour M1 type and the tissue‐repairing, pro‐tumour M2 type (Fu et al. 2020). Numerous studies have demonstrated that M2 macrophages play a pivotal role in promoting angiogenesis (Fu et al. 2020; Martin and Gurevich 2021). M2 macrophages secrete pro‐angiogenic factors that drive vascular growth in conditions such as cancer and skin flap transplantation (Lee et al. 2020; Luo et al. 2024). In the preretinal fibrovascular membranes of patients with PDR and retinas of oxygen‐induced retinopathy (OIR) mice, macrophages were predominantly polarized towards the M2 phenotype, exhibiting clustered infiltration (Zhu et al. 2017; Kobayashi et al. 2015). Our preliminary findings further confirmed that M2‐polarized macrophages promoted RNV in OIR mice (Zhou et al. 2015). lncRNA‐MM2P and the Th2 cytokine IL‐19 were positively associated with macrophage M2 polarization, thus regulating RNV (Wang et al. 2024; Zou et al. 2022). Nevertheless, the precise role of M2 macrophages in retinal angiogenesis requires deeper investigation.

Retractable fibres, long tubular structures formed during cell movement, give rise to vesicles at their tips and intersections, and these vesicular organelles are known as migrasomes (Ma et al. 2015). Migrasomes, ranging from 500 to 3000 nm in size (Yu and Yu 2022), are distinct from exosomes based on specific protein markers, including N‐deacetylase and N‐sulfotransferase 1 (NDST1), EGF domain‐specific O‐linked N‐acetylglucosamine transferase (EOGT), phosphatidylinositol glycan anchor biosynthesis class K (PIGK), and carboxypeptidase Q (CPQ) (Zhao et al. 2019). Migrasomes have been detected in various cell types across humans and animals, including immune cells such as macrophages, microglia, monocytes, and neutrophils (Jiang et al. 2023), and they play essential roles in numerous physiological and pathological processes. Moreover, the association between migrasomes and macrophages is particularly noteworthy. Migrasomes derived from CD5L‐enriched macrophages have been shown to trigger complement activation, resulting in blood‐brain barrier damage in cerebral amyloid angiopathy (Hu et al. 2023). In several tumours, a significant positive correlation has been observed between macrophage abundance and migrasome score in the tumour microenvironment (Qin et al. 2022). Acting as recipient cells for migrasomes, macrophages presented increased phagocytic activity after internalizing migrasomes derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells containing the antimicrobial peptide dermcidin (Li et al. 2023). Cell uptake of extracellular vesicles, which can facilitate cellular communication and exchange of substances, depends on multiple pathways, such as clathrin‐coated pit‐mediated endocytosis, caveolae‐mediated endocytosis, fast endophilin‐mediated endocytosis, clathrin‐independent carrier/glycosylphosphatidyl inositol‐anchored protein‐enriched early endocytic compartment endocytosis, macropinocytosis, and phagocytosis (Kryvenko and Vadász 2024; Zhang et al. 2023). Macropinocytosis and phagocytosis processes are the major internalization pathways of migrasomes due to the limitation of their size (Linnane et al. 2022). Migrasomes modulate processes such as mitochondrial quality control (Jiao et al. 2021) and pro‐angiogenic factor release (Zhang et al. 2022). Migrasomes have also been implicated in regulating angiogenesis. VEGFA and CXCL12 of migrasomes derived from monocytes could enhance angiogenesis in chicken embryos, which also influenced monocyte chemotaxis (Zhang et al. 2022). However, the role of migrasomes in RNV remains largely unexplored, and this study seeks to elucidate the mechanisms by which M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes contribute to ischaemia‐induced RNV.

Our findings demonstrate that M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes accelerate RNV in both in vitro and in vivo models by using the OIR mouse model and human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRECs). Further results of our study identify TREM2‐BTC/PLA1A as potential targets, which may serve as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for pathological RNV.

Materials and Methods

OIR Mouse Model

C57BL/6J mice, sourced from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Company (Changsha, Hunan, China), were used in this study. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (approval no. 20240199) and followed the ARVO Statement on the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

To generate the OIR mouse model, mouse pups, along with their nursing mothers, were exposed to 75% oxygen in an oxygen chamber from postnatal day (P) 7 to P12. After this hyperoxia period, they were returned to room air (RA) until P17. Age‐matched pups maintained in RA for the entire experiment were used as controls (Connor et al. 2009).

Intravitreal Injections

Intravitreal injections were administered to the pups at P12. Each eye received 0.5 µL of migrasomes (2 µg/µL), mannosylated clodronate liposomes (MCLs), and control liposomes (Encapsula Nano Sciences, Brentwood, TN, USA) using a 33‐G needle on a Hamilton syringe (Hamilton, Reno, NV, USA) (Zhou et al. 2015; Zou et al. 2022). At P17, all injected mice were sacrificed, and their eyeballs were collected for analysis.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Eyeballs were embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound. Cross‐sections (20 µm) were prepared using a Leica CM1950 cryostat (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Sections were fixed in acetone for 10 min, washed 3 times with PBST, blocked with buffer (5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS) for 1 h, and incubated with antibodies.

Retinas were dissected from eyeballs, fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and washed 3 times with PBST for 15 min each. Retinas were sequentially soaked in 50% and 100% methanol, blocked in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 1 h, and incubated with antibodies at 4°C overnight.

Cells on coverglasses were fixed with 4% PFA for 20–30 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 10 min. The samples were then washed with PBS 3 times, 10 min each. Blocking was performed using 5% BSA in PBS for 20–30 min. After blocking, cells were incubated with the primary antibody at 4°C overnight.

Retinas, eyeball sections, and cell samples were then mounted with medium (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and imaged using an Axio Imager M2 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), a DM4000B microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), and a High Intelligent and Sensitive SIM (CSR Biotech, Guangzhou, China). Avascular regions and pathological neovascular tufts were quantified as a percentage of the total retinal area using ImageJ (https://imagej.net) according to the protocol described (Connor et al. 2009).

The antibodies used were as follows: fluorescein‐labelled isolectin B4 (1:150; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA), Alexa Fluor 647 anti‐mouse CD206 (1:100; Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), anti‐NDST1 antibody (1:100; ProteinTech Group, Chicago, IL, USA), anti‐TREM2 antibody (1:100; Bioss, Beijing, China), Alexa Fluor 647‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (1:500; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), PE/Cyanine7 anti‐mouse F4/80 antibody (1:100; Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), anti‐mouse CD206 antibody (1:100; Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), Alexa Fluor 546‐conjugated goat anti‐rat lgG (H+L) (1:500; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (1:500; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Cell Culture

The RAW264.7 macrophage cell line (QiDa Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. For M2 polarization, RAW264.7 cells were treated with recombinant mouse IL‐4 (rmIL4, 20 ng/mL; Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) and recombinant mouse IL‐13 (rmIL13, 20 ng/mL; Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ, USA). RAW264.7 cells were transduced with lentivirus carrying shTREM2 (sequence: 5’‐GACCCTCTAGATGACCAAGAT‐3’; BrainVTA, Wuhan, China). The lentiviral transduction was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol.

HRECs (Cell Systems, Kirkland, WA, USA) were cultured in an endothelial cell growth medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% growth factor, and 1% gentamicin/amphotericin (QiDa Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. For overexpressing ISG20, IFITM1, BTC, and PLA1A, coding sequences of human ISG20 gene (NM_002201.6), IFITM1 (NM_003641.5), BTC (NM_001729.4), and PLA1A (NM_015900.4) were separately cloned into the pEXP‐RB‐Mam‐EGFP2 vector (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used for transfection.

Isolation of Migrasomes

The protocol for migrasome isolation differs from that of exosomes, primarily due to their larger size and distinct biogenesis. Briefly, cells were cultured in 150 mm dishes coated with 0.1 µg/mL fibronectin (Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). After digestion with trypsin, the cell suspensions were collected for a crude isolation of migrasomes by ultracentrifugation. All subsequent procedures were performed at 4°C. To remove cells and large debris, sequential centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min and 4000 × g for 20 min was performed. The remaining pellet, containing migrasomes, was further centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and centrifuged again at 20,000 × g for 30 min before analysis (Hu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). Protein concentration of the samples was measured using the BCA Protein Quantitation Kit (Boster Biological Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China). The identity of the isolated vesicles as migrasomes was confirmed by Western blotting analysis of specific marker proteins and characteristic morphological examination using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

For TEM, a 1%–2% phosphotungstic acid (PTA) solution was prepared with phosphate buffer. Formvar‐coated copper grids were placed on a paraffin dish and labelled. A 10 µL aliquot of the sample suspension was pipetted onto each grid and allowed to stand at room temperature or in a 37°C oven for 1–5 min. Excess liquid was removed using filter paper. The grids were then stained with 10 µL of PTA for 1–2 min, followed by the removal of excess stain with filter paper. The grids were washed with distilled water, repeating the process 2–3 times to remove residual PTA, and air‐dried. The samples were then examined using a TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit, FEI Company).

Migrasomes Productivity Analysis

For fluorescence imaging and migrasome quantification, cells were initially seeded in 24‐well plates at 3×104 cells/500 µL. After 24 h, IL‐4 and IL‐13 were added, and incubation continued for another 24 h. Cells were then rinsed twice with HBSS and incubated with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA, 1:200 dilution; Biolite Biotech, Xian, China) for 20 min. Afterwards, the cells were rinsed twice with HBSS before fluorescence imaging and migrasome quantification. For quantification, 6–8 random fields of each well were acquired. The numbers of cells and migrasomes were manually quantified. The value of n represented the number of biological replicates in each treatment group.

Internalization Test

After being pre‐treated with 5 µM Cytochalasin D (KKL, Ashland, VA, USA) and 50 µM Ethylisopropylamiloride (EIPA; KKL, Ashland, VA, USA), HRECs transfected with green fluorescent protein (GFP) were incubated with migrasomes labelled with red fluorescent protein (RFP) for 24 h. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate the internalization of migrasomes by HRECs.

CCK‐8 Assay

For the CCK‐8 assay, HRECs were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 1×10⁴ cells/100 µL. Following starvation, migrasomes were added for 24 h. And 10 µL of CCK‐8 reagent was added and incubated for 1 h.

Transwell Migration Assay

500 µL of human plasma fibronectin (5 µg/mL) was added to the lower chambers of a transwell migration system (8 µm pore size; Corning, New York, USA) and incubated for 1 h. Afterwards, the medium was replaced with medium containing varying concentrations of migrasomes, and the upper chambers were filled with 2×104 HRECs/100 µL. After 24 h, the upper chambers were gently washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. Cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 15 min, and images were taken for analysis using ImageJ software.

Tube Formation Assay

Growth factor–reduced Matrigel matrix (Corning, New York, USA) was thawed at 4°C. After starving HRECs for 8 h, the cells were digested and resuspended in a medium containing migrasomes at a concentration of 2×104 cells/100 µL. 50 µL of Matrigel matrix was added to a 96‐well plate and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 30 min before adding 100 µL of resuspended HRECs. After 14–16 h of incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, and tube formation was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Real‐Time Quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells and mouse retinas using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and cDNA was synthesized using the First‐Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RT‐qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Premix (Takara Bio, Tokyo, Japan) on an Applied Biosystems instrument (Waltham, MA, USA). GAPDH and β‐actin served as the reference genes. The primer sequences were as follows: GAPDH (forward: 5’‐GGTTGTCTCCTGCGACTTCA‐3’; reverse: 5’‐TGGTCCAGGGTTTCTTACTCC‐3’), β‐actin (forward: 5’‐CCTGGCACCCAGCACAAT‐3’; reverse: 5’‐GGGCCGGACTCGTCATAC‐3’), NDST1 (forward: 5’‐CTTCTGCCTGTTCAGCGTTTT‐3’; reverse: 5’‐CAGTGGGTCTGTTCGGGAA‐3’), EOGT (forward: 5’‐TTCTTCTTGCACAATAACAGGCA‐3’; reverse: 5’‐AGGACTTCTCATAACCCCAGC‐3’), PIGK (forward: 5’‐TAGAAGCGTCAAGAGGCTAGG‐3’; reverse: 5’‐CAGGAGCGGACTCTGTCAT‐3’), TREM2 (forward: 5’‐CTGGAACCGTCACCATCACTC‐3’; reverse: 5’‐CGAAACTCGATGACTCCTCGG‐3’), IFITM1 (forward: 5’‐CCAAGGTCCACCGTGATTAAC‐3’; reverse: 5’‐ACCAGTTCAAGAAGAGGGTGTT‐3’), PLA1A (forward: 5’‐CCTCCTGACTACTTCCAGTGC‐3’; reverse: 5’‐TGCTGCTTAGGTATGGTGATCTT‐3’), BTC (forward: 5’‐CCTGGGTCTAGTGATCCTTCA‐3’; reverse: 5’‐CTTTCCGCTTTGATTGTGTGG‐3’), CD38 (forward: 5’‐CAACTCTGTCTTGGCGTCAGT‐3’; reverse: 5’‐CCCATACACTTTGGCAGTCTACA‐3’), ISG20 (forward: 5’‐CTCGTTGCAGCCTCGTGAA‐3’; reverse: 5’‐CGGGTTCTGTAATCGGTGATCTC‐3’), and ITGA9 (forward: 5’‐TCGTTCTTCGGCTACGCAG‐3’; reverse: 5’‐CCCCATCCACTCATCATCGC‐3’). Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–△△CT method.

Western Blotting

Cells and mouse retinas were lysed using RIPA buffer (Boster Biological Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China) containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All sample protein concentrations were measured by BCA assay, and the loading amount was normalized to 20 or 60 µg per lane. Proteins were separated using sodium dodecyl sulphate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence kit (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The antibodies used were as follows: anti‐NDST1 (1:1000; ProteinTech Group, Chicago, IL, USA), anti‐PIGK (1:2000; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti‐calnexin (1:10000; ProteinTech Group, Chicago, IL, USA), anti‐TREM2 (1:1000; Bioss, Beijing, China), anti‐β‐actin (1:50000; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and anti‐α‐tubulin (1:5000; ProteinTech Group, Chicago, IL, USA).

Pressure Cycling Technology (PCT) Based Protein Digestion and Desalting

The migrasome sample was placed into PCT tubes and treated with 30 µL of lysis buffer (6 M urea and 2 M thiourea), 5 µL of 200 mM tris(2‐carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), and 2.5 µL of 800 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for reduction and alkylation. This procedure was carried out at 45,000 psi, alternating between 30 s of high pressure and 10 s of ambient pressure per cycle at 30°C, for a total of 90 cycles. Following this, 2.5 µg of trypsin (in 10 µL) and 1.25 µg of rLys‐C (in 2.5 µL) were added for protein digestion, which was performed under 20,000 psi using 50‐s high‐pressure intervals and 10‐s ambient pressure intervals at 30°C for 120 cycles. The resulting tryptic peptides were transferred to 1.5 mL tubes, and digestion was terminated by adding 15 µL of 10% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). After verifying the sample pH, desalting was performed using the SOLAμ SPE Plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Proteomic Analysis

For the proteomic analysis, data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) was performed on all samples using a nanoflow Vanquish Neo UHPLC System coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, USA), equipped with a FAIMS Pro interface (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, USA).

Buffer A consisted of 2% ACN and 98% H2O with 0.1% formic acid (FA), while Buffer B contained 80% ACN in H2O with 0.1% FA. All reagents used were of MS‐grade quality. Peptide concentration was maintained at 0.2 µg/µL, and 1 µL was injected per sample, resulting in a loading amount of 200 ng per DDA run. Peptides were initially loaded onto a precolumn (3 µm, 100 Å, 5 mm×300 µm i.d.) at a flow rate of 300 µL/min. The separation was achieved using a 60‐min LC gradient: 5% to 27% Buffer B over the first 50 min, followed by 27% to 40% Buffer B from 50 to 60 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min, using an analytical column (1.9 µm, 120 Å, 150 mm × 75 µm i.d.).

Mass spectrometry was conducted under the following conditions: the trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS) accumulation time was set at 2 ms, with a parallel accumulation‐serial fragmentation (PASEF) MS scan range of 100–1700 m/z and a 1/k0 scan range of 0.6–1.6. The signal detection threshold for MS scans was set at 5000 counts per second (cts/s). For PASEF collision‐induced dissociation (CID), the collision energy ranged between 20 and 59 eV, with 10 PASEF MS/MS scans per acquisition, covering a charge range of 0–5. The signal detection threshold for MS/MS scans was set at 2500 cts/s.

Mass spectrometry data were analyzed using Fragpipe (version 19.1). Trypsin and LysC were selected as the proteolytic enzymes, allowing for up to 2 missed cleavages. A mass tolerance of 10 ppm was applied to both precursor and fragment ions, and peptide identifications were confirmed with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.01. MSFragger was used as the search engine, and both unique and razor peptides were considered in the final analysis.

mRNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from samples, assessed for integrity by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA), and concentration by NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, USA). mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse‐transcribed to synthesize cDNA. After end repair, A‐tailing, and adaptor ligation, PCR amplification was performed to construct the sequencing libraries, followed by purification. Library quality was evaluated with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, USA) and an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE150).

Statistical Analyses

p values were presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was determined using Student's t‐tests and one‐way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett's or Tukey's post‐hoc tests for multiple comparisons. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The bioinformatics analyses were performed using R (v.4.0), with missing values imputed as zero. Principal component analysis was utilized to illustrate group separation, employing the stats package in R. For Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, the clusterProfiler package was used.

Results

Recognition of Migrasomes in Macrophages and the Retinas of OIR Mice

To investigate the role of migrasomes in retinal angiogenesis, immunofluorescence staining was performed on retinas at the peak of pathological neovascularization (P17) in OIR and RA mice. The number of migrasomes (NDST1‐labelled) was markedly elevated in the OIR group, and the distribution between neovascular regions (IB4‐labelled) and M2 macrophages (CD206‐labelled and F4/80‐labelled) closely overlapped (Figure 1A–D).

M0 and M2 macrophages were cultured, and the successful M2 polarization of macrophages was confirmed by RT‐qPCR (Figure S1). And migrasomes were visualized by immunofluorescence staining using WGA. Although migrasomes were observed in both groups, M2 macrophages produced 3.86 times migrasomes than M0 macrophages (Figure 1E,F). To further characterize the migrasomes, macrophage‐derived migrasomes were isolated in vitro via ultra‐high‐speed centrifugation, and Western blotting analysis demonstrated that the negative control protein calnexin was nearly undetectable in migrasome samples, whereas the migrasome markers NDST1 and PIGK were significantly expressed (Figure 1G). Additionally, TEM revealed a distinct “pomegranate‐like” structure, confirming the successful extraction of migrasomes (Figure 1H). These results indicated that M2 macrophages contributed to migrasome production and suggested a possible association between migrasomes and retinal angiogenesis.

M2 Macrophage‐Derived Migrasomes Promoted RNV and Functions of HRECs

To investigate whether macrophage‐derived migrasomes regulate RNV, intravitreal injections of M0/M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes and PBS were administered at P12 in OIR mouse models (Figure 2A). Compared to the control group (PBS), the avascular area increased by 1.86‐fold in M0 macrophage‐derived migrasomes group and by 1.81‐fold in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes group. Moreover, the neovascular tufts area increased by 2.20‐fold in the M0 macrophage‐derived migrasomes group and by 3.40‐fold in the M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes group (Figure 2B–D). These results indicated that M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes contributed to the significant angiogenic effect of OIR mice. To further assess the impact of migrasomes on angiogenesis, in vitro experiments were conducted using HRECs (Figure 2E). Internalization test revealed that RFP‐labelled migrasomes were present in HREC cytoplasm (Figure 2F). Following pre‐treatment of HRECs with 5 µM Cytochalasin D (the inhibitor of macropinocytosis and phagocytosis) and 50 µM EIPA (the inhibitor of macropinocytosis) for 1, 3, and 6 h, respectively, RFP‐labelled migrasomes were added and cultured for 24 h. Figure S2 revealed that migrasome internalization persisted after 1‐ and 3‐h pre‐treatment with Cytochalasin D and EIPA; And 6‐h pre‐treatment resulted in substantial accumulation of non‐internalized migrasomes in the culture medium and markedly decreased cellular uptake. Moreover, the suppression of migrasome internalization was stronger in the Cytochalasin D treatment group. Also, Figure 2G showed the suppressive effects of Cytochalasin D and EIPA on migrasome internalization. These results presented that macropinocytosis and phagocytosis were both internalization pathways of HRECs for migrasomes. The CCK‐8 assay indicated M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes enhanced HREC viability and proliferation, with the most pronounced effect observed at 10 µg/mL (Figure 2H), which was used for subsequent experiments. Additionally, migrasome‐treated HRECs exhibited significantly increased migration and tube formation abilities, with the cell migration rate being 1.26‐fold and the total length of tube being 1.19‐fold compared to control (Figure 2I–L). Collectively, these in vivo and in vitro findings demonstrated that M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes strongly induced pathological retinal vessel growth, and HRECs could be the recipient cells of migrasomes through macropinocytosis and phagocytosis pathways.

Proteomic Analysis and Preliminary Validation of the Key Molecule TREM2

These results confirmed that M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes accelerated RNV both in vivo and in vitro. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which migrasomes regulate retinal angiogenesis, proteomic analysis was conducted by using the samples of 3 different groups: M0 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, and M2 macrophage cell bodies (Figure 3A). Comparative analysis revealed that, relative to M2 macrophage cell bodies, 2400 proteins were significantly downregulated and 454 proteins were significantly upregulated in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, as illustrated by volcano plots and heatmaps (Figure 3B,D). A comparison between M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes and M2 cell bodies showed 9 downregulated and 102 upregulated proteins (Figure 3C,E).

An overlap analysis of the up‐regulated proteins between the M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes vs. M2 cell body group and the M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes vs. M0 migrasomes group revealed 53 specific commonly up‐regulated proteins. To explore the biological processes and pathways associated with these differentially expressed proteins, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis highlighted the enrichment of glycoprotein biosynthetic processes in biological processes (BP), Golgi trans cisterna in cellular components (CC), and glycosyltransferase activity in molecular functions (MF) (Figure 3F). KEGG pathway analysis showed significant enrichment in glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis (Figure 3G). According to KEGG pathway analysis, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes was significantly enriched compared to M0 migrasomes. By GO analysis, the M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes were involved in aminoglycan biosynthetic process, Golgi trans cisterna and glycosyltransferase activity (Figure S3). In addition, compared to M2 cell body group, the KEGG pathway analysis of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes was significantly enriched in spliceosome. In GO analysis, they were involved in RNA splicing via transesterification reactions with bulged adenosine as nucleophile, spliceosomal complex, and ribonucleoprotein complex binding (Figure S4).

Among 53 significantly expressed proteins identified through interaction analysis, 5 proteins—TREM2, PBXIP1, SLC25A45, MPZL1, and MRC1—were notably altered in OIR mouse retinas (Figure 3H) (Zou et al. 2022). Through an extensive literature review, TREM2 was selected for further study due to its known involvement in angiogenesis and immune regulation.

Expression and Distribution of TREM2 in OIR and M2 macrophages

To investigate the role of TREM2 in retinal angiogenesis, protein expression levels of TREM2 were assessed, revealing significantly elevated TREM2 protein levels in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes compared to M2 macrophage cell bodies or M0 macrophage‐derived migrasomes (Figure 4A). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the colocalization of migrasomes and TREM2 in M2 macrophages (Figure 4B). RT‐qPCR and Western blotting analyses further demonstrated elevated TREM2 expression in the OIR group compared to controls, with mRNA levels being 2.38‐fold and protein levels being 1.52‐fold (Figure 4C,D). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining of eyeball sections indicated that TREM2 distribution in the OIR mice strongly overlapped with neovascular tufts (IB4‐labelled) and M2 macrophages (CD206‐labelled) (Figure 4E–H). These results suggested that TREM2 may be a key functional factor in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, contributing to the promotion of RNV.

Correlation of TREM2 and M2 Macrophages in RNV of OIR

To confirm the relationship between TREM2 and M2 macrophages in the retinas of OIR mice, intravitreal injections of MCLs were performed to deplete M2 macrophages (Figure 5A). Immunofluorescence staining and quantification of CD206+ cells confirmed the successful depletion of M2 macrophages by intravitreal injection of MCLs in OIR mice (Figure S5). Additionally, staining of CD206 and TREM2 at OIR P17 showed a significant reduction in TREM2 expression following M2 macrophage depletion (Figure 5B,C). Depletion of M2 macrophages was also accompanied by decreased expression of migrasome maker NDST1 and TREM2 through immunofluorescence staining (Figure 5D–G) and mRNA expression of migrasome markers (NDST1, EOGT, and PIGK) in the OIR retinas (Figure 5H–J). These results confirmed that M2 macrophages were a key source of migrasomes.

TREM2 Knockdown Attenuated M2 Macrophage‐Derived Migrasomes‐Induced Angiogenesis

To further investigate the role of TREM2 in angiogenesis, we used lentivirus to knock down TREM2 expression in RAW264.7 cells and subsequently isolated migrasomes. First, we confirmed the successful knockdown of TREM2 using Western blotting, with expression in the knockdown group reduced to 41% of the control (Figure 6A). The effect of TREM2 knockdown on neovascularization was determined by both in vivo and in vitro experiments (Figure 6B). Next, M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes isolated from the TREM2 knockdown group and the NC group were intravitreally injected into OIR mice. Immunofluorescence analysis showed TREM2 knockdown apparently reduced the pro‐angiogenic effect of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes on RNV through quantitative analysis of neovascularization and the avascular areas, with the neovascular tufts reduced to 52% and avascular area reduced to 49% of the NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes group (Figure 6C–E). CCK‐8, tube formation, and transwell assays demonstrated that TREM2 knockdown M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, compared to NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, significantly reduced HREC functions, with cell viability and proliferation reduced to 74%, cell migration rate to 62%, and total tube length to 61% (Figure 6F–J). Overall, TREM2 knockdown attenuated the pro‐angiogenic effect of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes on RNV and HREC functions.

Identification of Potential Angiogenic Targets of HRECs Mediated by TREM2 in M2 Macrophage‐Derived Migrasomes

To further investigate the downstream mechanisms by which M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes containing TREM2 modulate neovascularization. HRECs were treated with NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, sh‐TREM2 M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes or PBS and followed with mRNA sequencing (Figure 7A). Differential expression analysis revealed that, compared to the control group, the NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes treated group exhibited 353 upregulated and 37 downregulated genes (Figure 7B). Additionally, 12 genes were upregulated, and 69 genes were downregulated after sh‐TREM2 M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes treatment compared with the NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes treated group (Figure 7C). To gain insights into the biological functions of these differentially expressed genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted (Figure S6).

To narrow down potential angiogenesis‐related targets, we intersected the 353 upregulated genes in the NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes treated group compared to the control and the 69 downregulated genes in the sh‐TREM2 M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes treated group compared to the NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes treated group. Among these, 6 genes—ISG20, IFITM1, BTC, PLA1A, ITGA9, and CD38—were also significantly upregulated in the OIR retinas (Figure 7D) (Zou et al. 2022). Validation by RT‐qPCR further confirmed that TREM2 knockdown attenuated the high expression of ISG20, IFITM1, BTC, and PLA1A in HRECs induced by NC M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes (Figure 7E). To elucidate the specific downstream targets mediating TREM2‐dependent pro‐angiogenic effects of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes on HRECs, 4 target genes (ISG20, IFITM1, BTC, and PLA1A) were overexpressed in HRECs (Figure 7F). BTC and PLA1A, rather than ISG20 or IFITM1, significantly rescued the suppression of cell viability and proliferation, migration, and in vitro vasculogenic capacities induced by sh‐TREM2 M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes through CCK‐8, transwell, and tube formation assays (Figure 7G–K). Thus, these results demonstrated that TREM2 was involved in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, which could contribute to pro‐angiogenic functions by mediating BTC and PLA1A genes.

Discussion

The M1/M2 polarization of macrophages is largely influenced by metabolic and immune regulatory pathways, such as glycolysis (Zhang et al. 2023) and inflammation (Wu et al. 2021). Numerous studies have demonstrated that M2 macrophages play a dominant role in angiogenesis‐related diseases (Lee et al. 2020; Yao et al. 2023). M2‐polarized macrophages can promote tumour angiogenesis by secreting angiogenic cytokines (Zhong et al. 2015). The mechanisms by which M2 macrophages promote angiogenesis are multifaceted, involving autophagy, exosomes, and post‐transcriptional modifications (such as N6‐methyladenosine and ubiquitination) (Luo et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2023; Yan et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024).

Migrasomes, a type of newly discovered organelle, are closely associated with cell migration (Huang et al. 2019), facilitating the lateral or horizontal transfer of cellular contents during intercellular communication (Yu and Yu 2022). Migrasomes play essential roles in various physiological processes, such as embryonic development, vascular homeostasis, material transport, and mitochondrial quality control (Zhang et al. 2025). By comparison, they differ notably from exosomes in several ways: exosomes are produced via multivesicular body fusion with the plasma membrane, while TSPAN4 and cholesterol are prerequisites for the formation of migrasomes (Xu et al. 2023). Additionally, migrasomes are significantly larger, ranging from 500 to 3000 nm in diameter (Yu and Yu 2022), compared to exosomes, which measure around 40 to 160 nm (Kalluri and LeBleu 2020). The formation of migrasomes is migration‐ and ECM‐dependent, while exosomes are not. However, the role of macrophage‐derived migrasomes in RNV and their underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study confirmed that migrasomes were abundant in the retinas of OIR mice and were closely associated with pathological neovascular tufts and M2 macrophages. Compared to M0 macrophages, M2 macrophages produced significantly more migrasomes (Figure 1). HRECs were the recipient cells of migrasomes. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed a marked increase in retinal angiogenesis in the presence of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes (Figure 2). These findings indicated that migrasomes were intricately linked to ischaemia‐induced retinal vessel growth and that M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes significantly promoted retinal angiogenesis.

TREM2, an immune receptor expressed in various myeloid cells such as macrophages and microglia (Sun et al. 2023), is closely linked to angiogenesis (Chen et al. 2023). Its expression was notably higher in tumour‐associated macrophages (TAMs) and nonclassical monocytes compared to M1‐like IFN‐γ‐induced cells and certain B cell subsets (Sun et al. 2023), aligning with our proteomic analysis, in vivo and in vitro experimental results (Figures 3 and 4). TREM2 promoted glioma progression and angiogenesis mediated by microglia/brain macrophages (Chen et al. 2023). Furthermore, TREM2+ macrophages have been found to suppress fibrosis while promoting wound healing and vessel growth in xenogeneic skin transplantation (Henn et al. 2021). Thus, TREM2 emerged as a key functional factor in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, which was also highly expressed in the OIR model (Figure 4). Moreover, depletion of M2 macrophages significantly reduced both TREM2 expression and migrasome formation (Figure 5). These findings collectively demonstrated that M2 macrophages promoted RNV via migrasomes and TREM2. Additionally, the knockdown of TREM2 could suppress pro‐neovascularization effect and enhanced HREC functions induced by M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes (Figure 6). KEGG analysis highlighted significant enrichment in chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate biosynthesis, heparan sulfate/heparin biosynthesis, and keratan sulfate biosynthesis, which deserved further investigation in RNV (Figure 3G). Previous studies indicated that chondroitin sulfate enhanced dermal regeneration by accelerating vessel growth and collagen deposition through the secretion of VEGF, PDGF, and bFGF (Yan et al. 2013). Keratan sulphate played a key role in pathophysiological processes, such as inflammation and nerve regeneration, and could act as a therapeutic target, including as a suppressor of cartilage damage (Pomin 2015). The RNA‐seq analysis did not enrich for any genes involved in VEGF signalling or broader angiogenesis pathways, other than the 6 genes already listed in Figure 7E. In contrast, the majority of genes enriched in the KEGG and GO analyses (Figure S6) were associated with immune regulation, such as Toll‐like receptor signalling pathway, chemokine signalling pathway, and cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction. These indicated that TREM2‐enriched migrasomes in our study may regulated RNV independent of the VEGF pathway. ISG20, IFITM1, BTC, and PLA1A were overexpressed to further investigate the downstream mechanisms by which TREM2 derived from M2 macrophage migrasomes on HRECs. We found that overexpression of BTC and PLA1A obviously attenuated the inhibitory effect of HREC functions induced by sh‐TREM2 M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes. Our study presented that TREM2, involved in M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes, accelerated RNV by mediating BTC and PLA1A genes (Figure 7). Abu El‐Asrar et al. discovered that PLA1A was highly expressed in the vitreous fluid of patients with PDR (Abu El‐Asrar et al. 2014). And BTC has been confirmed to promote functions of endothelial cells and angiogenesis through the Ptdlns 3’‐kinase/Akt pathway (Kim et al. 2003).

Exosomes, also as extracellular vesicles, regulate RNV primarily through the following 2 mechanisms: (1) Exosomes derived from various cell sources regulate pathological retinal angiogenesis through their inherent signalling molecules. One study showed that exosomes derived from Tsp‐1+ microglia negatively regulated RNV by decreasing miR‐27a‐5p levels of exosomes and maintaining Smad3 expression of endothelial cells (Luo et al. 2023). Also, exosomal miR‐155‐5p derived from activated microglia promoted the activation of resting microglia through the Socs1/NF‐κB pathway, ultimately leading to abnormal proliferation of endothelial cells and abnormal retinal angiogenesis (Chen et al. 2023). (2) Utilizing exosomes as drug or gene carriers to enhance the inhibitory effects of these therapeutic agents on pathological retinal blood vessels. After connecting exosomes with the anti‐angiogenic drug KV11 by using an anchoring peptide CP05, the delivery efficiency of KV11 was significantly enhanced than KV11 alone and improved its inhibitory effects on RNV and vascular leakage (Dong et al. 2021). And another study indicated that miR‐205, which possessed anti‐angiogenic properties, presented stronger inhibitory effects on pathological RNV and vascular leakage when encapsulated within exosomes (Zhang et al. 2025). However, currently, no studies have indicated the functions of migrasomes in RNV. Only a study demonstrated that migrasomes secreted by retinal pigment epithelial cells could promote the development of proliferative vitreoretinopathy, such as epiretinal membrane formation and disordered structure of retina (Wu et al. 2022).

Migrasomes hold potential for clinical applications in previous studies. For example, the level of migrasomes in urine may serve as a novel indicator of early podocyte injury in renal diseases (Yang et al. 2024). Another study pointed out that nanoparticles could encapsulate the migrasomes and block their intrinsic migration effect on cells, thus reducing the metastasis of tumour cells (Cheng et al. 2022). Our study also demonstrates that TREM2‐enriched migrasomes possess prospect for clinical application in RNV: (1) Their presence in body fluids gives them an advantage as biomarkers for diagnosis of RNV; (2) M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes regulates the development of RNV by targeting downstream molecules (such as BTC and PLA1A), which provides therapeutic targets in clinical treatment; (3) as a kind of extracellular vesicles, it also has the potential to serve as a delivery system for drug loading or enhancing the specificity of drug action. However, challenges remain in clinical application, including safety, non‐standardized isolation and purification processes, and low productivity that hinder large‐scale production, which deserve further in‐depth research and exploration.

This study underscores the role of M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes in the process of RNV via TREM2. However, several limitations should be considered. First, compared with gradient centrifugation, the limitations of ultracentrifugation of migrasomes exist, such as tedious, time‐consuming, lower productivity and purity, and a lack of standardized protocols (Deng et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025). The extracted migrasomes of our studies are validated using specific markers and TEM, providing assurance for their use in vitro and in vivo experiments. Second, drawbacks of the usage of MCLs exist that are not unavoidable: the lack of specificity of MCLs is the critical limitation. MCLs also could deplete M2 microglia (Li et al. 2022) and may consequently exert unintended impacts on the in vivo experimental results. Third, GO and KEGG analyses of proteomic analysis have predicted pathways that may participate in RNV, requiring further investigation.

In conclusion, TREM2‐enriched migrasomes derived from M2 macrophages are internalized by HRECs through macropinocytosis and phagocytosis. Subsequently, these internalized migrasomes promote pathological RNV and functions of HRECs by activating downstream molecules (such as BTC and PLA1A). Therefore, targeting M2 macrophage‐derived migrasomes and TREM2‐BTC/PLA1A presents potential therapeutic strategies for retinal neovascular diseases (Figure 8).
