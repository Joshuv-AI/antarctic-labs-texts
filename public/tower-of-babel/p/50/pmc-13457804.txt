Association between acute infarct topography and cerebral microbleed location in acute ischemic stroke: a retrospective cross-sectional magnetic resonance imaging study
Authors: Lingzhi Cheng, Yuewan Fu, Yichao Wu, Rouyan Wu, Chunyun Ha, Xiao Li, Liping Zhang, Lianying Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are important imaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease and are frequently detected in patients with stroke. However, imaging evidence regarding spatial associations between infarct topography and CMBs detected on the index magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) remains limited. This study aims to investigate the association between acute infarct location and CMB location on brain MRI.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included cranial MRI data from patients with AIS. Acute infarcts were identified on diffusion-weighted imaging/apparent diffusion coefficient (DWI/ADC) and classified into six predefined neuroanatomical regions. CMBs detected on the index MRI examination were identified on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) and recorded using the same regional scheme. Two experienced readers independently interpreted the images under blinded conditions, and disagreements were resolved by consensus adjudication. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate factors associated with the presence of CMBs after limited adjustment for sex, age, and infarct location. Exploratory regression analyses were further performed to assess associations between infarct location and region-specific CMBs.ResultsA total of 138 patients with AIS were included, among whom 79 (57.2%) had CMBs detected on SWI. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, after limited adjustment for sex, age, and infarct location, corona radiata infarction was associated with the presence of CMBs [odds ratio (OR) =3.61, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.52–8.61, P=0.004]. Exploratory univariable analyses showed that cortical infarction was associated with cortical CMBs (OR =2.90, 95% CI: 1.37–6.13, P=0.005), while corona radiata infarction was associated with corona radiata CMBs (OR =3.82, 95% CI: 1.83–8.00, P<0.001). In addition, increasing age (OR =1.11, 95% CI: 1.02–1.20, P=0.013) and thalamic infarction (OR =5.36, 95% CI: 1.18–24.46, P=0.030) showed exploratory associations with cerebellar CMBs.ConclusionsIn this retrospective single-center MRI study, corona radiata infarction was associated with CMBs detected on the index MRI examination after limited adjustment for demographic variables and infarct location. Region-specific associations between acute infarct location and CMB location were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, given the cross-sectional design, limited covariate adjustment, small sample size, and potential scanner heterogeneity. Further prospective multicenter studies using standardized MRI protocols and more comprehensive clinical and imaging covariates are needed to validate these findings.

Introduction

Stroke remains a major cause of death and disability worldwide and is closely associated with age, geographic region, ethnicity, and medical history (1,2). Ischemic stroke (IS) accounts for the majority of stroke cases. Acute IS (AIS) results from interruption of cerebral blood supply and can lead to substantial neurological deficits, particularly when critical vascular territories or extensive brain regions are involved (3). In addition to the acute ischemic lesion itself, coexisting neuroimaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease may provide important information regarding the underlying vascular substrate in patients with AIS.

Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are small hypointense lesions detected on magnetic susceptibility-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences and are generally considered imaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease (4). They mainly reflect hemosiderin deposition related to underlying vascular pathology. Clinically, CMBs are commonly detected using T2*-weighted gradient-recalled echo imaging or susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). Compared with conventional T2*-weighted imaging, SWI incorporates phase information and provides higher sensitivity for susceptibility-related lesions, thereby improving the detection of CMBs and helping distinguish them from certain mimics such as calcification (5). In clinical imaging studies, CMBs detected on the index MRI examination are usually interpreted as pre-existing or chronic manifestations of microangiopathic injury, unless longitudinal imaging demonstrates new lesion development.

The anatomical location of CMBs has clinical and pathological implications, and CMBs in different regions may reflect different small vessel disease backgrounds (6-11). Previous studies have also shown that CMBs are associated with intracranial hemorrhage, recurrent ischemic events, cognitive decline, and adverse outcomes after stroke treatment (6-8,12-19). However, most existing studies have focused on the presence, burden, or broad distribution patterns of CMBs, whereas less attention has been paid to whether the anatomical location of acute infarction is associated with the location of CMBs detected during the same MRI examination.

Because infarcts in different neuroanatomical regions may reflect different vascular territories and mechanisms, infarct topography may be related to the coexisting burden and location of small vessel disease markers. Nevertheless, in a retrospective cross-sectional design, such an analysis can only evaluate imaging coexistence and spatial association, rather than temporal or causal relationships. Therefore, using diffusion-weighted imaging/apparent diffusion coefficient (DWI/ADC) to identify acute infarct location and SWI to detect CMBs, this study aimed to explore the association between acute infarct topography and CMB location in patients with AIS. We hypothesized that infarcts in specific neuroanatomical regions may show region-related associations with CMBs detected on the index MRI examination. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0466/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient population

This was a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study. Consecutive patients who underwent brain MRI at Dongzhimen Hospital between January 2016 and October 2023 and were finally diagnosed with AIS were screened for eligibility. All included patients underwent routine brain MRI, DWI/ADC, and SWI during the same MRI examination and had DWI/ADC-confirmed acute infarction lesions. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Dongzhimen Hospital, Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (No. 2023DZMEC-099-02). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical and imaging data.

Diagnostic criteria for AIS

The diagnosis of AIS was based on the general principles of the 2019 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines for the early management of AIS, combined with clinical presentation and MRI findings. All included patients had acute ischemic infarction lesions defined as focal hyperintensity on DWI with corresponding ADC reduction, indicating restricted diffusion. Intracranial hemorrhage and nonvascular stroke mimics were excluded based on clinical and imaging evaluation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: (I) clinically diagnosed AIS with exclusion of intracranial hemorrhage and nonvascular stroke mimics; (II) DWI/ADC-confirmed acute infarction lesions that could be anatomically localized; and (III) completion of routine brain MRI, DWI/ADC, and SWI sequences during the same MRI examination.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (I) age <18 years; (II) poor image quality due to motion artifacts, susceptibility artifacts, or incomplete images that precluded evaluation of acute infarction lesions or CMBs; (III) intracranial tumors, vascular malformations, cavernous malformations, or other structural lesions potentially affecting CMB assessment; (IV) incomplete MRI sequences; or (V) missing key clinical or imaging data required for statistical analysis.

MRI acquisition

MRI examinations were performed using SIEMENS MAGNETOM Verio 3.0-T and MAGNETOM Amira 1.5-T scanners equipped with standard head coils. All patients underwent axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), DWI, SWI, and sagittal T1WI.

MRI parameters were as follows (matrix = frequency encoding × phase encoding): axial T1WI, repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) =1,800/7 ms, matrix =256×192, slice thickness =5.0 mm; axial T2WI, TR/TE =4,430/114 ms, matrix =384×319, slice thickness =5.0 mm; axial FLAIR, TR/TE =7,500/112 ms, matrix =256×192, slice thickness =3.0 mm; axial DWI, TR/TE =3,800/82 ms, matrix =160×112, slice thickness =5.0 mm; axial SWI, TR/TE =49/40 ms, matrix =256×186, slice thickness =2.0 mm; sagittal T1WI, TR/TE =2,370/7 ms, matrix =256×242, slice thickness =3.0 mm.

Because the sensitivity of SWI for CMB detection may be affected by magnetic field strength and acquisition parameters, the use of both 1.5-T and 3.0-T scanners during the study period may have introduced measurement heterogeneity. Due to incomplete scanner information in some early cases, magnetic field strength could not be fully incorporated into the regression adjustment and was therefore addressed as a study limitation.

Imaging assessment and predefined regional coding

All MRI data were retrieved from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Two senior radiologists with more than 10 years of experience in neuroimaging independently reviewed all images under blinded conditions. Each reader was blinded to the other reader’s assessment and to the clinical information. Disagreements were resolved through joint review and consensus adjudication. Because the final analysis was based on consensus results, inter-reader agreement statistics were not separately calculated.

Acute infarcts were defined as focal hyperintensity on DWI with corresponding hypointensity on ADC maps, indicating restricted diffusion. CMBs were defined as small, round or ovoid focal hypointense lesions on SWI. To reduce misclassification, lesion morphology, continuity across adjacent slices, and corresponding findings on conventional sequences, including T1WI, T2WI, and FLAIR, were reviewed. Lesions considered more likely to represent vascular flow voids, calcifications, cavernous malformations, hemorrhagic transformation, or susceptibility artifacts were not classified as CMBs.

To compare acute infarct location and CMB location within the same anatomical framework, a predefined six-region neuroanatomical coding scheme was used. Acute infarcts and CMBs were recorded as involving the cortical region, corona radiata, thalamus, corpus callosum, brainstem, or cerebellum. This scheme was developed with reference to established CMB visual rating frameworks, including the Microbleed Anatomical Rating Scale (MARS), the Brain Observer MicroBleed Scale (BOMBS), and the lobar/deep/infratentorial distribution framework recommended in Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging (STRIVE) 1/STRIVE-2 small vessel disease neuroimaging standards (20-22), while also considering the actual infarct distribution in this AIS cohort. This six-region scheme was intended as a practical patient-level coding framework for statistical analysis rather than a replacement for standardized CMB rating systems. Each region was coded as absent or present (0= not involved, 1= involved).

Outcomes and variable definitions

The primary outcome variable was the presence of CMBs detected on the index MRI examination (0= no CMB detected, 1= at least one CMB detected). Secondary outcome variables included the occurrence of CMBs in each predefined neuroanatomical region.

Acute infarct locations and CMB locations were recorded separately using the same six-region coding scheme. If multiple regions were involved in the same patient, all involved regions were coded as positive. Lesion counts were used only for descriptive statistics, whereas all regression analyses were performed at the patient level.

Sample size and patient selection process

Patients were consecutively screened from the PACS database between January 2016 and October 2023. The sample size was determined by all available cases that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria within the predefined study period, and no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Consecutive inclusion was used to reduce selection bias and improve representativeness.

The patient selection process included preliminary AIS screening, verification of MRI sequence completeness, confirmation of DWI/ADC-defined acute infarction, assessment of SWI image quality, exclusion of structural or hemorrhagic lesions affecting CMB assessment, and verification of clinical and imaging data completeness. A total of 138 patients with AIS were finally included. The detailed screening and exclusion process is shown in Figure 1.

Statistical analysis

Raw data were organized, coded, and checked using Microsoft Excel 2016 (Table 1), and all statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0. All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variable coding used in the statistical analyses

Patients were stratified into CMB-positive and CMB-negative groups according to SWI findings. Demographic variables and infarct location distributions were compared between the two groups. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage).

The primary outcome variable was the presence of CMBs detected on the index MRI examination. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using the presence of CMBs as the dependent variable. Age, sex, and the six infarct locations were entered into the model to evaluate the association between acute infarct location and the presence of CMBs. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Model goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test.

All regression analyses were performed at the patient level. Exploratory univariable logistic regression analyses were then performed to evaluate associations between infarct location and region-specific CMBs. Because multiple region-specific comparisons were conducted, a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of 0.05/6=0.0083 was applied. Findings with P<0.05 but not meeting the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold were interpreted only as exploratory signals.

Patients with missing key clinical or imaging variables, including age, sex, infarct location, and CMB assessment, were excluded during patient selection. Therefore, there were no missing values for the primary variables included in the regression analyses. Missing data for noncritical clinical variables were described qualitatively but were not incorporated into the primary multivariable models.

Baseline characteristics and imaging distribution

A total of 138 patients with AIS were included in the final analysis, including 94 men (68.1%) and 44 women (31.9%). The age range was 21–88 years, with a mean age of 64.5±12.4 years. According to SWI findings, CMBs were detected in 79 patients (57.2%), whereas 59 patients (42.8%) had no CMBs detected on SWI. Factors associated with the presence of CMBs

Using the presence of CMBs as the primary outcome variable (0= absent, 1= present), sex, age, and the six infarct locations were entered into an exploratory limited-adjustment multivariable logistic regression model. Because several key clinical confounders were incompletely available, this model was considered a limited-adjustment model. The H-L test suggested no evidence of poor model fit (P=0.893). After limited adjustment for sex, age, and infarct location, corona radiata infarction was associated with the presence of CMBs (OR =3.61, 95% CI: 1.52–8.61, P=0.004) (Table 4). This result should be interpreted as an imaging association identified after limited covariate adjustment rather than as evidence of an independent causal relationship.

Exploratory region-specific logistic regression analyses

To explore potential associations between acute infarct location and region-specific CMBs, univariable logistic regression analyses were performed using the presence of CMBs in each brain region as a patient-level dependent variable. Because the same patient could have multiple infarct regions and multiple CMB locations, and because multiple region-specific comparisons were performed, Bonferroni correction was applied with an adjusted significance threshold of 0.05/6=0.0083. Cortical infarction was associated with cortical CMBs (OR =2.90, 95% CI: 1.37–6.13, P=0.005), and corona radiata infarction was associated with corona radiata CMBs (OR =3.82, 95% CI: 1.83–8.00, P<0.001). Both associations remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction. Increasing age and thalamic infarction showed signals of association with cerebellar CMBs, but these findings did not meet the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold and were therefore interpreted only as exploratory findings (Table 5).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cross-sectional study, CMBs were detected on the index MRI examination in 57.2% of patients with AIS. CMBs are important neuroimaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease, and updated neuroimaging standards emphasize standardized reporting of their number, anatomical distribution, and associated imaging characteristics (22). However, direct comparison of CMB detection rates across studies should be made cautiously because detection may vary according to MRI field strength, SWI acquisition parameters, patient characteristics, and study design. Importantly, the present study evaluated CMBs detected on the index MRI examination and could not distinguish pre-existing chronic CMBs from newly developed lesions after acute ischemia or reperfusion. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as imaging associations between acute infarct location and CMB location identified during the same MRI examination rather than as evidence of temporal sequence or causality.

We observed that corona radiata infarction was associated with the presence of CMBs after limited adjustment for sex, age, and infarct locations. The corona radiata is a common region involved in cerebral small vessel disease-related imaging abnormalities. Previous studies have shown that CMBs are associated with the risk of intracranial hemorrhage and recurrent ischemic events after IS or transient ischemic attack (6), and that imaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease are associated with adverse outcomes after acute stroke (23). Therefore, the observed coexistence of corona radiata infarction and CMBs may reflect a greater burden of concomitant small vessel disease markers in a subset of patients with AIS. However, because important covariates, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, reperfusion therapy, white matter hyperintensity burden, lacunes, stroke subtype, and MRI field strength, were not comprehensively adjusted for, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of an independent relationship. Rather, it should be regarded as an imaging association identified under limited covariate adjustment.

In the exploratory region-specific analyses, cortical infarction was associated with cortical CMBs, and corona radiata infarction was associated with corona radiata CMBs. After Bonferroni correction, these same-region associations remained statistically significant, whereas findings involving cerebellar CMBs did not meet the adjusted significance threshold and were interpreted only as exploratory signals. Prior studies suggest that the anatomical distribution of CMBs may provide indirect clues regarding underlying vascular pathology, with lobar or cortical-subcortical CMBs more commonly associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy and deep or infratentorial CMBs more commonly associated with hypertensive arteriopathy (24). In the present study, a predefined six-region binary coding scheme was used to place DWI/ADC-defined acute infarcts and SWI-detected CMBs within the same patient-level anatomical framework. This approach cannot replace standardized rating systems such as MARS or BOMBS and does not provide more granular lobar, deep, or infratentorial topographic information. Therefore, the present findings should be viewed as an imaging-based supplement to existing knowledge regarding spatial patterns of cerebral small vessel disease rather than as evidence supporting a specific biological mechanism.

Regarding cerebellar CMBs, exploratory analyses suggested possible associations with age and thalamic infarction. However, only nine patients had cerebellar CMBs, and the confidence intervals were wide, indicating potential sparse-event bias and unstable estimates. Previous studies have suggested that intracranial arterial dolichoectasia is associated with small vessel disease markers such as lacunes, CMBs, and white matter hyperintensities (25), and that vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia may be related to posterior circulation CMBs (26). Potential mechanisms proposed in prior literature include hemodynamic disturbance, structural arterial wall injury, and matrix metalloproteinase-mediated vascular remodeling and blood-brain barrier alterations (27,28). These prior findings provide contextual background for infratentorial CMB patterns; however, such mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study. Therefore, they should be regarded only as speculative hypotheses. A recent case report describing Hyperintense Acute Reperfusion Marker (HARM)-associated ipsilateral blooming of CMBs in IS suggested that both HARM and CMBs may be related to blood-brain barrier disruption and raised the possibility that acute ischemia- or reperfusion-related blood-brain barrier alterations may influence susceptibility-based imaging findings interpreted as CMBs (29). However, that evidence was limited to a single case report, and longitudinal follow-up imaging was unavailable in the present study. Consequently, we could not determine whether the observed CMBs represented chronic pre-existing lesions, newly developed lesions, or transient susceptibility-related findings associated with acute blood-brain barrier disruption.

One potential contribution of the present study is that DWI/ADC-defined acute infarcts and SWI-detected CMBs were evaluated within the same predefined anatomical framework at the patient level. This approach suggests that certain infarct locations may show region-related coexistence with CMBs and may provide a basis for future hypothesis-driven studies using prospective, multicenter designs and standardized MRI protocols. However, based on the current dataset, the present study cannot determine whether acute infarct location predicts the development or progression of CMBs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study, and the study population was mainly derived from emergency and inpatient settings. Therefore, selection bias may have been present, and the generalizability of the findings beyond this single-center clinical cohort is limited. Second, because of the retrospective nature of the study, the baseline comparison between the CMB-positive and CMB-negative groups included only sex, age, and the six infarct locations. Important clinical and imaging covariates, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking, atrial fibrillation, prior IS, prior intracerebral hemorrhage, antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, reperfusion therapy, white matter hyperintensity burden, lacunes, stroke subtype, and renal function, were not systematically available. Therefore, residual confounding is unavoidable.

Third, the study covered a relatively long period and used both 1.5-T and 3.0-T MRI scanners. Because SWI sensitivity for CMB detection may be influenced by magnetic field strength, slice thickness, echo time, and sequence parameters, scanner and protocol heterogeneity may have introduced measurement bias. Fourth, the predefined six-region binary coding scheme was useful for patient-level statistical analysis but was relatively coarse. It should not be considered a substitute for standardized CMB rating systems and may have underestimated the importance of more granular spatial information. In addition, the region-specific analyses involved multiple comparisons, and the same patient could have multiple infarct regions and multiple CMB locations; therefore, observations across regions were not fully independent, and these analyses should be regarded as exploratory.

Furthermore, inter-reader agreement statistics were not reported. Although two readers independently assessed the images under blinded conditions and disagreements were resolved by consensus adjudication, observer-related bias could not be fully excluded. Finally, the number of patients with cerebellar CMBs was small, resulting in unstable model estimates; therefore, the cerebellar CMB analyses should not be considered confirmatory. In addition, because longitudinal follow-up imaging was not available, this study could not distinguish chronic pre-existing CMBs from newly developed lesions after acute ischemia or reperfusion.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cross-sectional MRI study, corona radiata infarction was associated with the presence of CMBs detected on the index MRI examination in patients with AIS after limited adjustment. Region-specific exploratory analyses further suggested possible spatial associations between acute infarct location and CMB location. However, because of the retrospective design, incomplete adjustment for vascular risk factors, scanner heterogeneity, and multiple comparisons, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further prospective multicenter studies using standardized MRI protocols and more comprehensive clinical covariates are required for external validation.
