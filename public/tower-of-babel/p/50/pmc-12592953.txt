Performance of Trigger Tools in Identifying Adverse Drug Events in Emergency Intensive Care Unit: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Nandini Prakash, Adusumilli P Kumar, Aruna C Ramesh, Dharini Boopathi, M Deepalakshmi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo investigate how well trigger tools function while evaluating adverse drug reactions (ADRs) among patients in emergency intensive care units (EICUs) and to assess the sensitivity of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement: Global Trigger Tool (IHI: GTT) for reporting adverse drug events among patients admitted to EICUs.MethodsA prospective, continuous, single-center research study was conducted for 9 months in the Department of Accident and Emergency Medicine. Triggers were defined based on the IHI GTT and Menat trigger tool. Each flagged case was reviewed for 15 minutes, which provides an equitable balance between efficiency and thoroughness to confirm the presence of an ADR. Performance metrics were calculated and analyzed.ResultsThree hundred medical records were collected, and 167 cases were randomly analyzed using 87 triggers. The prevalence of ADRs was 28%. The trigger tools showed 83.8% sensitivity and 48.7% specificity. The results indicated that the negative predictive value was 92.3%, while the positive predictive value was 72.9%.ConclusionTrigger tools are a useful way to identify ADRs, even though issues with specificity and false positives still exist. Further improvements in technology and ongoing development will probably make these instruments even more effective and efficient in the dynamic environment of EICUs.How to cite this articlePrakash N, Kumar AP, Ramesh AC, Boopathi D, Deepalaksmi M. Highlights

The research demonstrates that trigger tool greatly enhances adverse drug reaction (ADR) detection in emergency settings, particularly for Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) 0–3 patients with Emergency Severity Index (ESI)-3. It is highly sensitive and has a 92% negative predictive value, thus ruling out non-ADRs and improving patient safety as well as supporting timely clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Intensive care units (ICUs) are severely challenged to identify ADRs because of the severity of patient conditions and polypharmacy.1 The conventional means, such as spontaneous reporting, tend to be underreported and lacking in data. With a view to overcoming the limitations, active surveillance methods like the trigger tool method (TTM) have been put into practice. A “trigger” is a sign or occurrence in the patient record that can indicate an ADR, which is validated when investigated further. Trigger tool method has both manual and automated systems for screening possible ADRs and exhibits more sensitivity and specificity than traditional approaches.2,3

Adverse drug reactions are most common in emergency ICUs where patients are severely ill and on multiple medications. Therefore, effective monitoring, such as TTM, is essential to reduce harm and enhance outcomes.4,5 This research assesses the efficacy of trigger tools in identifying ADRs in an ICU environment, quantifying their sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), and exploring implementation issues.6,7

Methods

A 9-month prospective observational study was carried out in the Accident and Emergency Department of a tertiary care hospital in Bengaluru. Informed consent and ethics approval (Letter No: EC-23/100-PG-M PHARMA) were taken before patients were triaged by the ESI and monitored by MEWSs. Patients with psychiatric illness and patients admitted to EICU who did not receive medications were excluded from the study.

Even-day admissions were studied by the TTM and odd-day admissions by conventional methods. Triggers were evaluated within 20 minutes of admission through patient records and investigations. Identified ADRs were recorded, and causality, seriousness, severity, predictability, and preventability were determined through the Naranjo Scale, Modified Hartwig and Siegel Scale, and the Schumock and Thornton Scale. Adverse drug reaction reports were categorized according to the National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention (NCC MERP) harm categories (Table 1).

Results

Out of 330 enrolled, 300 patients were included in the analysis. Ages varied from 18 to 94 years and comprised 52.3% male participants. Out of a total of 300 patients studied, 167 were placed in the TTM group (with tool), and 133 were in the conventional group (without tool). This distribution is further broken down by gender: In the TTM group, 86 were male, and 81 were female; in the conventional group, 71 were male, and 62 were female. No patient was lost to follow-up.

Emergency Severity Index-3 was triaged in the majority of the patients with a MEWS of 1 (normal). Out of them, 74 patients suffered from at least one possible ADR, and 84 ADRs occurred. According to the Naranjo Scale, 63.1% were considered probable and 36.9% possible. Hospitalization due to most ADRs resulted and were categorized as moderate severity and definitely preventable. Predictability was 76%.

The prevalence of ADR was 28%, and 37.12% were identified through TTM compared to 16.54% through traditional methods. The IHI GTT and Menat trigger tools picked up 18 and 30 positive triggers, respectively, out of 53 and 34 total triggers. Trigger tools are indirect screening methods used to find possible adverse drug events (ADEs), including ADRs. They identify clues, known as “triggers,” in a patient's chart that are often linked to harm or medication errors. These tools do not measure ADRs directly. Instead, they highlight potential signs of harm that need additional clinical review to determine if an ADR took place. Triggers like “positive blood culture” or “C. diff infection” are not ADRs, but may result from or be associated with drug-related harm and hence help identify ADRs when followed up clinically (Table 2).

The trigger tools showed 83.8% sensitivity and 48.7% specificity, reflecting high detection accuracy for true ADRs, but with a significant false positive rate. The PPV was 72.9%, and the NPV was 92.3%, reaffirming that flagged cases frequently consisted of true ADRs, whereas non-flagged cases seldom did.

Discussion

The ESI is an established triage system, and the outcomes of this study are different from others, with ESI-3 being the most frequent category (84%). Modified Early Warning Score in combination with ESI further stratified ADR risk.8,9

Supporting literature shows that TTM increases ADR detection by up to 70% and can reduce preventable ADRs by 50%. Our sensitivity and specificity results (83.8 and 48.7%, respectively) were similar to previous studies, such as Pandya et al. and Goruntla et al., confirming the method's reliability.

The Naranjo Scale continuously helps recognize and control ADRs. In comparison with research by Gadde and Morales-Ríos, our results of 63.1% likely and 36.9% potential ADRs compare very well, reiterating the strength of the assessment tools applied.10

Trigger association information identified that patients with ESI-3 and MEWS scores of 0–3 had the most frequent ADR occurrence. Other medical interventions and complications—such as extended emergency department (ED) stays, administration of antiemetics, renal impairment, hypo/hyperglycemia, and electrolyte disturbances—were frequently noted and monitored as part of trigger assessments.11,12 Integrating trigger tools into emergency department workflows can help detect ADRs earlier and more accurately. This approach can lead to better patient outcomes and a proactive safety culture. Ultimately, it may reduce preventable harm related to medication (Table 3).13,14

Limitations

Limitations of the study include a single-site setting and a small sample size of 300 patients, which may limit generalizability. The TTM process was labor-intensive and involved experienced reviewers, which brought variability. Moreover, some ADRs, particularly undocumented or subtle ones, were not caught. The large number of triggers evaluated may have decreased precision, and the study did not provide data on critical markers like naloxone or flumazenil administration.15

Conclusion

Emergency Severity Index-3 patients with MEWS scores of 0–3 were most susceptible to ADRs. The trigger tool was highly sensitive (83.8%) and had high NPV (92.3%), affirming its efficacy in excluding ADRs, although low specificity (48.7%) led to common false positives. Nevertheless, the tool markedly improved ADR detection—from 16.54 to 37.12%—highlighting its utility in ICU environments. Although positive tests need to be verified, negative tests are reliable, lowering missed ADRs and unwarranted interventions.

Clinical Relevance

Identification of ADRs is a serious issue in healthcare, usually resulting from medical treatment. It is difficult to distinguish between disease complications and ADRs caused by care. Trigger tools, first developed by Classen et al., provide a practical solution to the conventional monitoring approach. This work reaffirms the trigger tool's value in ICUs, and its acceptance among healthcare professionals implies its wider potential and routine incorporation into clinical practice.
