PET Imaging of Osteomyelitis with [68Ga]Ga-BP
Authors: Mukesh K. Pandey, Aditya Bansal, Shalini Sharma, Melissa Karau, Yash S. Raval, Stephen M. Broski, Robin Patel, Mark Nathan, Val J. Lowe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A low-cost, easy-to-adopt noninvasive imaging modality for differentiating bacterial infection from inflammation is desirable to accurately diagnose bacterial infections. Herein, a bacteria-specific PET probe, [68Ga]­Ga-BP, was developed and evaluated in rat models of foreign body-associated osteomyelitis induced by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae and was directly compared with sterile inflammation and uninstrumented uninfected controls. [68Ga]­Ga-BP-based imaging of MRSA-infected tibiae was also compared to [68Ga]­Ga-citrate and [18F]­FDG. After iron depletion, dynamic PET/CT imaging was performed at 15–120 min postinjection of radiotracers, followed by assessment of ex vivo biodistribution. [68Ga]­Ga-BP successfully detected tibial infection and demonstrated >6-fold higher uptake in infected tibiae with all three bacterial species versus contralateral tibiae via PET imaging. [68Ga]­Ga-BP outperformed [68Ga]­Ga-citrate and [18F]­FDG in identifying MRSA infection. [68Ga]­Ga-BP represents a promising, noninvasive PET imaging agent for direct bacterial detection and bacterial infection treatment monitoring.

Introduction

Osteomyelitis remains a challenging clinical problem affecting children and adults of all ages. , In children, hematogenous osteomyelitis is the most common type, where bacteria spread through the bloodstream to long bones from a distant infection site. In younger adults, osteomyelitis is usually caused by direct inoculation following trauma or surgery, whereas in older adults, it can be due to contiguous spread from adjacent soft tissue infection, including spread from decubitus ulceration (bedsores or pressure ulcers), or it can be a complication of arthroplasty or any other implanted skeletal hardware. , In addition, older adults experience osteomyelitis of the lower extremities due to nonhealing ulcers, most commonly related to diabetes mellitus and peripheral vascular disease. Regardless of etiology, delayed or inaccurate diagnosis contributes to morbidity, including chronic infection, sepsis, pathological fractures, and amputation. The most common pathogens associated with osteomyelitis are Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, with methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) being particularly challenging, followed by streptococci and enterococci, and Gram-negative bacteria, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Serratia marcescens. −

Conventional imaging modalities, including X-ray, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), excel at depicting anatomical changes such as breakdown and loss of the outer layer of the bone (cortical destruction), new bone formation in response to infection (periosteal reaction), dead bone fragment formation (sequestrum), and soft tissue involvement. All of the above-mentioned imaging modalities assess structural damage and edema rather than the presence of bacteria. Distinguishing active infection from post-treatment reparative changes, sterile inflammation, or Charcot neuroarthropathy can be difficult with conventional imaging.

There is an unmet clinical need for a noninvasive imaging modality that can be used for the assessment of active infection, which could lead to accurate diagnosis and identification of the exact site of infection, support targeted treatment, and monitor treatment efficacy.

Positron emission tomography (PET) using [18F]­fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]­FDG) has high sensitivity but like MRI does not reliably differentiate bacterial infection from noninfectious inflammatory processes.

Infection-specific radiotracers that directly target bacteria through their unique metabolic pathways or surface biomarkers are being explored in both the preclinical and clinical settings.

In terms of bacterial metabolic pathways, folate synthesis is a promising target; the agents [11C]­para-aminobenzoic acid and, [18F]­FPABA and analogs , are actively taken up by bacteria in which they accumulate. Another promising imaging agent is [18F]­FDS, which is specifically taken up by Enterobacterales (e.g., E. coli, K. pneumoniae) via a sorbitol-specific phosphoenolpyruvate:glycose phosphotransferase system. Other investigative imaging radiotracers include [18F]­fluoromannitol, radiolabeled antibiotics such as [18F]­fluoropropyl-trimethoprim, radiolabeled siderophores that utilize siderophore-specific uptake receptor systems, − and those targeting bacterial cell wall synthesis, which are in developmental stages.

Next-generation imaging radiotracers that feature low cost, simple radiosynthesis, easy workflow integration, and low regulatory complexity are needed. To facilitate this, a PET radioisotope 68Ga-based infection-imaging tracer, [68Ga]­Ga-BP, has been developed that exemplifies all of these characteristics. In this study, we tested in vitro uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in different bacterial species followed by assessment of its infection imaging potential in foreign body-associated osteomyelitis rat models with MRSA, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa by PET imaging and ex vivo biodistribution. To measure radiotracer specificity, the uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in infected tibiae was compared with uninfected instrumented (sterile inflammation) and uninstrumented uninfected tibiae (normal). The uptake profile of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was also compared with [18F]­FDG and [68Ga]­Ga-citrate in the MRSA osteomyelitis and instrumented uninfected tibial cohorts.

Results

Design and Radiosynthesis of [68Ga]­Ga-BP

We designed and synthesized [68Ga]­Ga-BP (Figure ), using [68Ga]­GaCl3 (pH 1.0) and phosphate buffer saline (PBS, 30×) at room temperature. Formation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was characterized by rad-TLC using 2.5 mM EDTA solution (pH 5.3–5.5). Rad-TLC showed conversion of [68Ga]­GaCl3 to [68Ga]­Ga-BP in >98% corrected yield with 0.53 ± 0.33 GBq/mmol (n = 15) molar activity and >98% radiochemical purity (Figure S1). To define the chemical structure of [68Ga]­Ga-BP, we synthesized Ga-BP, by mixing 0.031 mmol of GaCl3 and 2.0 mL of 30×-PBS solution at room temperature for 30 min and freeze-dried the final product to deliver Ga-BP as a white powder (>99% yield). The product was characterized using infrared and 31P NMR analysis. Two additional syntheses were performed using the aforementioned method with 0.046 and 0.903 mmol of GaCl3 (Figure S2).

Chemical structure of [68Ga]­Ga-BP.

Radiosynthesis of [68Ga]­Ga-Citrate and [18F]­FDG

[68Ga]­Ga-Citrate was synthesized by mixing [68Ga]­GaCl3 (pH 1.0) with 0.5 mL of sodium citrate (0.3 M) for 10 min, at pH 6.0–6.5, at room temperature. The formed [68Ga]­Ga-citrate was analyzed on rad-TLC using 2.5% acetic acid and 97.5% methanol as a mobile phase. [18F]­FDG obtained from the Mayo Clinic PET facility was utilized.

In Vitro Bacterial Uptake

Uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was tested using live and heat-killed MRSA, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, and S. marcescens. All studied live bacterial strains showed higher uptake of the [68Ga]­Ga-BP, with respect to their heat-killed counterparts. Heat-killed cohorts showed no or low uptake (Figure and Table S1). P. aeruginosa showed the highest uptake, followed by K. pneumoniae, S. marcescens, A. baumannii, and MRSA, with S. marcescens and A. baumannii showing similar uptakes.

Uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in live and heat-killed MRSA, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Serratia marcescens after 30 min of incubation at 37 °C. *p < 0.05 live versus heat-killed.

In Vivo Model

In vivo models of osteomyelitis in Wistar rats were generated by implanting K-wires and inoculating surgical implant sites with bacteria. Two controls were included to test the sensitivity and specificity of [68Ga]­Ga-BP for the detection of bacterial infection(i) Wistar rats with instrumented but uninfected tibiae (sterile inflammation model) and (ii) uninstrumented uninfected Wistar rats (normal). Details of the study design are outlined in Figure S3A. 14 days after placing and infecting the implant, surgical sites showed visible findings of infection in infected animals, whereas no findings of infection were observed in the inflammation-only control model (Figure S3B). Bacterial quantification in the excised K-wire and tibiae of infected animals showed MRSA, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa levels of ∼5 log10 colony forming units (CFU)/K-wire and 6–7 log10 CFU/g tibia, with no bacteria detected in the lungs, liver, kidneys, or urine (Table S2). The tibial bacterial burden for the foreign body-associated P. aeruginosa infection (1.33 ± 0.05 × 106 CFU/g, n = 4) was lower than for foreign body-associated MRSA infection (12.69 ± 4.47 × 106 CFU/g, n = 14) and foreign body-associated K. pneumoniae infection (9.01 ± 0.48 × 106 CFU/g, n = 4) (Table S2)

PET/CT Imaging and Ex Vivo Biodistribution

After 14 days of placing and infecting the implant, PET/CT imaging revealed high [68Ga]­Ga-BP uptake in infected tibiae independent of bacterial species (Figure A,B and Tables S3–S5). The developed tracer, [68Ga]­Ga-BP, started showing higher uptake at the site of the infection as early as 15 min and progressively increased up to 120 min, in comparison to the contralateral tibiae and the tibiae of uninstrumented uninfected animals (Figure A,B and Tables S3–S5). The SUVmean for MRSA, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa-infected tibiae was 3.48 ± 0.25 (n = 5), 1.89 ± 0.46 (n = 4), and 1.90 ± 0.30 (n = 4), respectively, at 120 min postinjection (Figure A,B and Tables S3–S5).

The contralateral tibiae of all infected groups showed gradual reduction in the retention of [68Ga]­Ga-BP over time; this difference was highest 120 min postinjection of the radiotracer, as compared to infected tibiae. Reduction in the retention of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the contralateral tibiae highlights the infection-specific change in the distribution profile of the [68Ga]­Ga-BP radiotracer (Figure A,B and Tables S3–S5). Animals in the MRSA-infected group exhibited higher uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP compared to the K. pneumoniae- and P. aeruginosa-infected cohorts at 30, 60, and 120 min post injection (Figure A,B and Table S6). The SUVmean ratio of MRSA-infected and the contralateral tibiae was 12.29 ± 7.84 (n = 4) at 120 min postinjection (Table S7), whereas the SUVmean ratios of K. pneumoniae- and P. aeruginosa-infected and contralateral tibiae at 120 min postinjection was 6.62 ± 2.43; (n = 4) and 7.16 ± 2.92; (n = 4), respectively (Table S7). When the infected tibiae were compared with instrumented uninfected tibiae, statistically higher retention of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was observed in the MRSA-infected tibiae at 30, 60, and 120 min postinjection (Figure A,B and Table S6). In terms of the SUVmean ratio of the infected tibiae over the contralateral tibiae, versus the instrumented uninfected tibiae over the contralateral tibiae, a statistically higher uptake was only observed in the MRSA-infected cohort (Table S7) at 120 min postinjection, a finding not observed in the K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa cohorts. Although there were trends of a higher SUVmean ratio of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa cohorts compared to instrumented uninfected cohorts at 15, 60, and 120 min postinjection, they did not meet statistical significance (Table S7).

In the case of the P. aeruginosa-infected tibiae, there was a statistically higher SUVmean of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the infected tibiae compared to the instrumented uninfected tibiae at 120 min postinjection (Table S6). However, with the K. pneumoniae-infected tibiae, there was a trend of higher SUVmean of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the K. pneumoniae-infected tibiae compared to the instrumented uninfected tibiae at 60 and 120 min postinjection, but the difference was not statistically significant (Table S6). In the instrumented uninfected model, the operated tibiae showed a statistically higher SUVmean of [68Ga]­Ga-BP than that of the contralateral or uninstrumented uninfected tibiae at 15, 30, and 60 min postinjection (Table S8). However, this difference or contrast was not as high as that observed in the MRSA-infected tibiae when compared with the contralateral or uninstrumented uninfected tibiae (Tables S3 and S8).

The ex vivo biodistribution analysis at 120 min postinjection showed a higher SUVmean of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the MRSA-infected tibiae (4.00 ± 1.12, n = 6) and instrumented uninfected tibiae (3.74 ± 1.42, n = 8) as compared to the uninstrumented uninfected tibiae (1.16 ± 0.51, n = 4) (Figure A and Table S9) and was found to be consistent with the PET image analysis; no significant difference was observed in the SUVmean of [68Ga]­Ga-BP between the MRSA-infected tibiae (4.00 ± 1.12, n = 6) and the instrumented uninfected tibiae (3.74 ± 1.42, n = 8) (Figure A and Table S9). It is imperative to point out that ex vivo SUVmean is based on the extracted tibiae only, whereas imaging SUVmean includes the tibiae and nearby infected muscle, which could attribute to the differences in SUVmean. However, the SUVmean ratio (operated/contralateral tibiae) of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was statistically higher in the MRSA-infected group (1.90 ± 0.31, n = 6) compared to that in the instrumented uninfected group (0.94 ± 0.32, n = 8) (Figure B and Table S10). A higher SUVmean ratio of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was also observed in the MRSA-infected group compared to the uninstrumented uninfected (1.00 ± 0.21, n = 4) and the P. aeruginosa -infected (1.24 ± 0.04, n = 4) animals (Figure B and Table S10). A statistically higher SUVmean ratio was observed for [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the K. pneumoniae-infected group (1.65 ± 0.46, n = 4) compared to the uninstrumented uninfected group (1.00 ± 0.21, n = 4) (Figure B and Table S10). Other than the site of infection, the whole body biodistribution of [68Ga]­Ga-BP remains similar in infected, instrumented uninfected, and uninstrumented uninfected groups within major uptakes in the blood, heart, lungs, spleen, kidney, and urine (Figure S4).

Discussion

Design, Synthesis, and Characterization of [68Ga]­Ga-BP

The radiotracer [68Ga]­Ga-BP is designed based on the bacterial need for iron (Fe). Fe is an essential element for both human and bacterial growth and survival. There are two ways bacteria acquire Fe: (1) direct intake through a membrane-bound transferrin receptor , and (2) through secretion and reabsorption of siderophores after Fe chelation. Due to the critical importance of the Fe in bacterial life cycles, Fe-targeted imaging has the potential to identify bacterial locations in the host. Due to the similar size and 3+ charge of Ga compared to Fe, it was hypothesized that 68Ga might serve as a surrogate for Fe and be taken up by bacteria in a similar fashion, using both membrane-bound transferrin receptors and siderophores. Recently, various siderophores, including ferrichrome, desferrioxamine, citrate, pyoverdine, enterobactin, and enterobactin analogs, were radiolabeled with either 67/68Ga or 89Zr and evaluated in preclinical animal models for infection imaging with mixed results, potentially due to the nature of the chelator or siderophores used, as they are non-natural and already complexed with 68Ga. As such, we envisioned and designed a novel molecular construct that both the bacteria and host utilize “68Ga+3” as a natural “Fe”, so that it can be effectively taken up by both the bacteria and host. While bacteria use Fe for growth and survival, the host uses Fe to fight against infection as it sends ferritin, transferrin, and lactoferrin to accumulate at the site of bacterial infection. This novel design (mimicking natural Fe) and molecular composition make this particular radiotracer, [68Ga]­Ga-BP, a unique and selective PET probe for live bacterial imaging. It is also important to recognize that gallium (Ga) has a similar size (atomic radius of Ga/Fe: 136 pm/126 pm) and charge (3+) as Fe, and therefore, 68Ga may serve as a natural surrogate for Fe. Radiosynthesis of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was achieved in 10 min with >98% yield and >98% radiochemical purity. Radiochemical purity was measured using rad-TLC as shown in Figure S1. The total time to synthesize [68Ga]­Ga-BP, including quality control analysis, was ∼30–35 min. Radiochemically characterizing the formation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP, was a daunting task due to the inorganic nature of both the starting material ([68Ga]­GaCl3) and product ([68Ga]­Ga-BP), which is novel. Translating this product into routine radiopharmaceutical manufacturing will need a robust, reliable, and rapid method of characterization. Various solutions, buffers, and conditions that could selectively differentiate [68Ga]­Ga-BP from [68Ga]­GaCl3 on a rad-TLC plate were explored, but these attempts failed until the molarity of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was reduced to a millimolar range and pH of the EDTA solution was optimized to a range of 5.3–5.5 to allow the best possible separation between [68Ga]­Ga-BP and [68Ga]­GaCl3. The developed rad-TLC method selectively moves [68Ga]­GaCl3 near to the solvent front (0.913 Rf) but keeps [68Ga]­Ga-BP at the origin (0.05 Rf) (Figure S1).

[68Ga]­Ga-BP can be produced using any small, automated synthesizer or by kit-based synthesis. Synthesis of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was tested using both cyclotron- and generator-produced [68Ga]­GaCl3. The short synthesis time and feasibility of using either cyclotron- or generator-produced [68Ga]­GaCl3 and the option to use either an automated synthesizer or simple kit-based synthesis enable [68Ga]­Ga-BP to be widely accepted and distributed, as needed. Prior to radiosynthesis of [68Ga]­Ga-BP, the nonradioactive version, “Ga-BP”, was synthesized and characterized by 31P-nuclear magnetic resonance and infrared spectroscopy (Figures S2, S7, and S8). Complexation of Ga with two phosphate moieties was established based on the 31P chemical shift. Typically, complexation of a Ga with a phosphate group causes a −5 ppm upfield shift on 31P NMR with respect to 85% phosphoric acid, due to more shielding. Complexation of additional Ga metals with a phosphate group causes further upfield shift (e.g., −7 to −8 ppm for two Ga, −9 to −10 ppm for three Ga, and −12 to −14 ppm for four Ga). However, here, there was only an approximately −2.5 ppm 31P NMR upfield shift, suggesting two phosphates per Ga. The synthesis was also performed with ∼29-fold higher quantity of GaCl3 with the same 2.0 mL of 30× PBS; with this, it was found that there was a 31P NMR shift at −8.296 ppm with respect to 85% phosphoric acid, corroborating a change in chemical species at higher gallium quantity, as reported previously. It should also be noted that the amount of 68Ga present during radiosynthesis is small, supporting the formation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP as a product. The chemical composition of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was further supported by the reported crystal structure of the gallium hydrogen phosphate hydrate, (Ga3(H3O)­H8(PO4)6.6H2O), where three Ga metals were complexed between six phosphates, supporting that two phosphate groups per gallium are a stable complex.

In Vitro and In Vivo Evaluation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP

The in vitro results suggest that bacterial viability is a critical determinant of radiotracer uptake. All live bacteria studied, independent of the cell wall structure (Gram-positive or Gram-negative), exhibited a high uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP, whereas heat-killed bacteria did not.

The difference between the uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in MRSA and P. aeruginosa could be due to the lower P. aeruginosa burden than that of MRSA in the tibiae. We also noticed a lower [68Ga]­Ga-BP uptake in the in vitro group of MRSA than in K. pneumoniae but a higher [68Ga]­Ga-BP uptake in the in vivo group of MRSA than in K. pneumoniae; this change in the uptake profile between in vitro and in vivo results could be attributed to a stronger systemic response in the case of more virulent MRSA than K. pneumoniae (Table S1). The lower uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the contralateral tibiae of the infected groups compared to the uninstrumented uninfected tibiae supports the notion that [68Ga]­Ga-BP is being used by the host to respond to infection, in addition to bacterial uptake, providing preferential accumulation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP at the site of infection. The observed preferential accumulation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the infected region, relative to the contralateral tibiae, aligns with the iron-like nature of [68Ga]­Ga-BP and the need for bacteria to acquire iron. Although complexation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP with transferrin, followed by interaction with transferrin receptors, could theoretically contribute to tracer uptake in osteomyelitis, gallium exhibits an approximately 300-fold lower binding affinity to transferrin compared with iron (ferric ions). ,,, Therefore, transferrin receptor-mediated uptake is likely to play only a minor role. In contrast, direct bacterial uptake mechanisms are more plausibly the predominant driver of [68Ga]­Ga-BP accumulation at the sites of infection.

Statistically significant higher uptakes of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the instrumented infected compared to the uninstrumented uninfected tibiae in all three infected groups are promising and show that [68Ga]­Ga-BP could be useful in the identification of MRSA, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae infection. Tracer uptake in the liver was indeed elevated in all surgical groups, including uninfected, MRSA-infected, K. pneumoniae-infected, and P. aeruginosa-infected subjects, relative to uninstrumented controls. This likely reflects a systemic response to surgical stress, which is known to increase hepatic iron sequestration in rats. Consequently, the observed tracer accumulation may primarily represent the physiological reaction to surgery. When [68Ga]­Ga-BP was compared with other clinically used infection-imaging radiotracers, including [18F]­FDG and [68Ga]­Ga-citrate, [68Ga]­Ga-BP showed higher uptake in the MRSA-infected tibiae than did [18F]­FDG or [68Ga]­Ga-citrate. The obtained results are promising and show that [68Ga]­Ga-BP could be clinically useful in detecting MRSA infection more than [18F]­FDG or [68Ga]­Ga-citrate.

In a study by Mäkinen et al., where Sprague–Dawley rats with the S. aureus-infected tibiae were used as an osteomyelitis model, the reported SUV ratios (operated/nonoperated sides) for [18F]­FDG (1.74 ± 0.37) based on 120 min dynamic imaging were similar to the ratios in the MRSA-infected tibiae in this study on [18F]­FDG PET imaging at 120 min postinjection (SUVmean ratio, 2.03 ± 0.31). In the same study, the uptake of [68Ga]­GaCl3 showed SUV ratios (operated/nonoperated sides) of 1.62 ± 0.28 based on 120 min dynamic imaging, which is lower than the SUVmean ratio observed here for [68Ga]­Ga-citrate (10.50 ± 4.70) and [68Ga]­Ga-BP (12.29 ± 7.84) at 120 min postinjection. Differences in the uptake of various 68Ga-based radiotracers highlight the importance of chemical composition of 68Ga radiotracers with more selective uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-citrate and [68Ga]­Ga-BP at infection sites compared to [68Ga]­GaCl3. Nonetheless, of the three radiotracers assessed, [68Ga]­Ga-BP showed the highest SUV ratios for the infected tibiae, supporting the notion that [68Ga]­Ga-BP could be a better “Fe” surrogate than others. The different chemical composition of 68Ga radiotracers with different in vivo behaviors in terms of SUVmean is consistent with observations by Lankinen et al., where a similar pattern was shown, albeit with SUVmax. Higher SUVmax was reported for [68Ga]­Ga-citrate (4.7 ± 1.5) than [68Ga]­GaCl3 (3.6 ± 1.4) in the tibiae infected with S. aureus, which corroborates observations here of differing in vivo behavior of [68Ga]­Ga-BP and [68Ga]­Ga-citrate.

In another study, a murine myositis model of infection with S. aureus and inflammation (heat-killed S. aureus), [18F]­fluoromannitol showed an SUV of ∼5 and ∼1.5, respectively, while [18F]­FDG showed an SUV of ∼12 and ∼11, respectively. With [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the results presented here, an SUVmean of 3.48 ± 0.25 (n = 5) was observed at MRSA-infected sites at 120 min postinjection, whereas instrumented uninfected sites showed an SUVmean of 1.22 ± 0.41 (n = 4). Although the model used by Simpson et al. is different from the one presented here, the SUVmean of [68Ga]­Ga-BP is comparable to that of [18F]­fluoromannitol, [68Ga]­Ga-BP was better in differentiating between infection and inflammation than [18F]­FDG.

Ordonez et al. tested [11C]­PABA as an infection-imaging probe for MRSA infections in a periprosthetic joint model in rabbits. The SUVmedian ratio for [11C]­PABA at the infection site was 3.17 at 30–60 min after tracer injection, with a bacterial burden of 7.74 ± 0.9 log10 CFU/metallic implant. In the current study, despite a lower bacterial burden in the excised bone (4.63 ± 1.29 log10 CFU/excised bone, n = 14) or K-wire (5.61 ± 0.41 log10 CFU/K-wire, n = 14) than that reported by Ordonez et al., at the infection site, the SUVmean ratios observed for [68Ga]­Ga-BP at 30 min (4.33 ± 0.14, n = 2), 60 min (4.86 ± 1.52, n = 4), and 120 min (12.29 ± 7.84, n = 4) postinjection were higher than the SUVmedian ratio reported for [11C]­PABA. Rua et al. investigated a fluorinated version of PABA, [18F]­FPABA, in a S. aureus myositis model and compared results with [18F]­FDS and [18F]­FDG. No uptake of [18F]­FDS was observed in S. aureus-infected and inflamed limbs at 120 min postinjection, while [18F]­FPABA showed an SUV ratio (infected/inflamed) of 3.1 ± 0.81 at 60 min postinjection, and no difference was observed between the uptake at infected and inflamed sites for [18F]­FDG at 60 min postinjection. In relation to the model presented here, there was a difference in the SUVmean of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in MRSA-infected and inflamed sites with increasing contrast over time. With [68Ga]­Ga-BP, at 60 min postinjection, there was an SUV ratio (infected/inflamed) of ∼2.11, which increased to ∼2.85 at 120 min postinjection. The difference observed in the uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP and [18F]­FPABA at 120 and 60 min, respectively, could be attributed to differences in the model used. Together, these results highlight the promising nature of [68Ga]­Ga-BP as compared to [68Ga]­GaCl3, [68Ga]­Ga-citrate, [18F]­FDG, [11C]­PABA, and [18F]­FPABA in detecting infection and in differentiating infection from the inflammation.

In summary, the present study showed that [68Ga]­Ga-BP is a superior noninvasive PET imaging radiotracer for infection imaging compared to previously studied radiotracers and offers a promising diagnostic tool for the diagnosis of MRSA-, P. aeruginosa-, and K. pneumoniae-associated infections.

Moreover, in immunocompromised hosts, chronic inflammation decreases red blood cell production, dysregulates iron homeostasis, and triggers iron sequestration. In this setting, [68Ga]­Ga-BP may perform better because of the absence of iron competition. Conversely, in states of iron overload, excess circulating or labile iron may competitively inhibit tracer binding, thereby reducing the performance of [68Ga]­Ga-BP. Notably, although additional 68Ga-labeled radiotracers for infection imaging have been described in prior reviews, there remains a critical need to further advance and translate PET-based imaging approaches for infection.

Conclusions

We have successfully developed and evaluated a bacteria-specific novel PET probe, [68Ga]­Ga-BP, capable of selectively detecting live bacterial infection in osteomyelitis. The developed radiotracer demonstrated robust performance across multiple bacterial strains, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species. In all tested osteomyelitis models, [68Ga]­Ga-BP exhibited significantly higher uptake (>6-fold) in the infected tibiae compared with the contralateral tibiae. Furthermore, in a head-to-head comparison, [68Ga]­Ga-BP outperformed [18F]­FDG and [68Ga]­Ga-citrate in detecting MRSA infection in the tibiae, underscoring its superior specificity for bacterial infection. These findings warrant further clinical validation of [68Ga]­Ga-BP as a targeted imaging agent for osteomyelitis.

Methods

Radiosynthesis of [68Ga]­Ga-BP

To synthesize [68Ga]­Ga-BP, 1.0 mL of [68Ga]­GaCl3 (pH 1.0) was mixed with 0.8 mL of phosphate buffer saline (PBS, 30×) and the reaction mixture was incubated for 10 min at room temperature (RT) with the final pH adjusted to 6.0–6.5. [68Ga]­Ga-BP, radiochemical yield, and radiochemical purity were analyzed by rad-TLC using 2.5 mM EDTA solution (pH 5.3–5.5). 2.5 mM EDTA (10 mL) rad-TLC mobile phase was prepared by mixing 0.25 mL of EDTA (0.1 M, pH 8.0) in 19.75 mL of water with a final pH of 2.5 mM EDTA solution of ∼5.3–5.5. The PBS stock (30×) was prepared by dissolving one tablet of PBS in 6.66 mL of deionized water. 0.1 M EDTA solution (1.0 mL) was prepared by dissolving 37 mg of EDTA in 0.8 mL of Na2CO3 (0.1 M) and 0.2 mL of H2O. The pH of the final 0.1 M EDTA solution was ∼8.0. After reaction completion, Rad-TLC clearly showed conversion of [68Ga]­GaCl3 to [68Ga]­Ga-BP in >98% yield (corrected) in >98% radiochemical purity (Figure S1) and a molar activity (Am) of 0.53 ± 0.33 GBq/mmol (n = 15). The obtained product was filtered through a Millex GV 0.22 μm before in vivo study. The molar activity (Am) of [68Ga]­Ga-BP was determined by dividing the measured 68Ga radioactivity (0.15 ± 0.09 GBq, n = 15) by the total amount of phosphates (0.295 mmol) present in the reaction mixture at the end of synthesis.

Synthesis of Ga-BP

To establish the chemical structure and identity of [68Ga]­Ga-BP, a nonradioactive version, Ga-BP, was synthesized using ingredients described above except that instead of using [68Ga]­GaCl3, nonradioactive GaCl3 was used. Briefly, 5.4 mg (0.031 mmol) of GaCl3 was dissolved in con. hydrochloric acid HCl and mixed with 2.0 mL of 30×-PBS and stirred for 30 min at room temperature and then freeze-dried to a white solid powder in >99% yield. 30×PBS contains 14.7 mg (0.108 mmol) of KH2PO4 and 85.1 mg of Na2HPO4 (0.60 mmol), 479.79 mg of NaCl (8.21 mmol), and 11.93 mg of KCl (0.16 mmol). The obtained product was subjected to infrared and 31P NMR analyses. The product and PBS were separately dissolved in water as saturated solutions and subjected to infrared analyses using a Thermo scientific, Nicolet-iS5, iD3-ATR-equipped spectrometer. IR (neat) Ga-BP: νmax 3312.37 (OH), 2367.70 (CO2), 2128.24,1637.84 (water bending), 1216.22 (O–P–O), 1080.36 (PO, weak); PBS: νmax 3351.24 (OH), 2358.69 (CO2), 2092.36, 1637.84 (water bending), 1078.67 (PO moderate), 991.95 (P–O); 31P NMR (23 °C, 202.4 MHz, D2O, H3PO4 ref std.), Ga-BP: δ −0.039; PBS: δ 2.529.

Additional synthesis was performed using 8.1 mg (0.046 mmol) and 159 mg (0.903 mmol) of GaCl3 dissolved in water and mixed with 2.0 mL of 30×-PBS and stirred for 30 min at room temperature and then freeze-dried to a white solid powder in >99% yield; the obtained products were analyzed with 31P NMR (Figure S2).

Radiosynthesis of [68Ga]­Ga-Citrate and [18F]­FDG

To synthesize [68Ga]­Ga-citrate, 0.5 mL of [68Ga]­GaCl3 (pH 1.0) was mixed with 0.5 mL of sodium citrate (0.3 M), and the reaction mixture was incubated for 10 min at RT. The pH of the final mixture was 6.0–6.5. The formation of [68Ga]­Ga-citrate was analyzed on a rad-TLC using 2.5% acetic acid + 97.5% methanol as the mobile phase. [18F]­FDG was obtained from the Mayo Clinic PET radiopharmaceutical facility.

In Vitro Bacterial Uptake

All bacterial studies were performed after approval from the Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC; ID: Bios00001090.02) at Mayo Clinic. Single colonies of bacteria (MRSA IDRL-6169, K. pneumoniae IDRL-8443, P. aeruginosa IDRL-7852, A. baumannii IDRL-11626, and S. marcescens IDRL-7521) were picked from blood agar plates (Trypticase Soy Agar II with 5% sheep blood, TSB-blood agar) to inoculate 10 mL of trypticase soy broth (TSB). After 11–12 h of incubation at 37 °C, 100 μL of TSB with growing bacteria was added to 25 mL of iron-deficient T-media (formulation shown in Table S17). The iron-deficient T-medium used for the culture and in vitro uptake study was obtained after triple-treating T-media with Chelex resin. The bacteria were allowed to grow in iron-deficient T-media at 37 °C for 6 h. One batch of bacteria was heat-killed by exposing the live bacterial culture at the end of 6 h to boiling water for 60 min.

After 6 h of culture, 25 mL of iron-deficient T-media with live and heat-killed bacteria was centrifuged at 4600g for 5 min at 4 °C and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in ∼700–800 μL of iron-deficient T-media. The in vitro uptake study was performed with ∼100 μL of live and heat-killed bacterial suspensions and 0.3–0.6 MBq [68Ga]­Ga-BP in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. After adding 0.3–0.6 MBq [68Ga]­Ga-BP (in ∼10 μL) to ∼100 μL of bacterial suspension, ∼110 μL of bacterial suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in a thermomixer with shaking (700 rpm). At the end of incubation, the bacteria were pelleted by centrifugation at 4600g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 0.5 mL of phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The resuspended pellet was centrifuged again, and the supernatant was removed; washing of the pellet by centrifugation and resuspension in PBS was performed two more times. After the final wash, radioactivity in the bacterial pellet was measured by a dose calibrator or γ-counter. Radioactivity of the bacterial suspension or bacterial pellet was decay-corrected to the time when incubation was started for calculation of uptake. The percentage uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-BP in the bacterial pellet was calculated by Uptakeof[Ga68]Ga‐BPinbacterialpellet(%)=decay corrected radioactivity in bacterial pellet after final washdecay corrected radioactivity in bacterial suspension at incubation start×100

After radioactivity in the bacterial pellet was measured, the pellet was resuspended in PBS and serial 1:10 dilutions were made and plated in TSB-blood agar plates to estimate CFUs in the uptake assay.

Animals

Wistar rats were purchased from Charles Rivers Laboratories, Wilmington, MA, and housed in an animal facility maintained by the Department of Comparative Medicine, Mayo Clinic. They were singly housed in a standard rat cage with a floor space of 128 in2 or Innocage IVC rat-caging system with a floor space of 141 in2 (Innovive, LLC.) with corncob bedding and maintained on a 14% protein rodent maintenance diet (Inotiv, Inc.) and regular water. The animals (n = 41) were divided into five groupsuninstrumented uninfected (n = 4), foreign body-associated MRSA-infected tibiae (n = 14), foreign body-associated P. aeruginosa tibiae (n = 4), foreign body-associated K. pneumoniae-infected tibiae (n = 4), and foreign body without infectionto assess sterile inflammation (n = 15).

In Vivo Model

All animal studies were performed in a BSL2+ laboratory after the approval by the Institutional Biosafety committee (ID: Bios00001090.02) and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol # A00006217–21-R24) of Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, USA. On the day of surgery, all animals other than the uninstrumented uninfected group were anesthetized with an anesthetic cocktail (ketamine, xylazine, and acepromazine) or isoflurane. When the anesthetic cocktail was used, 50 mg/kg ketamine, 10 mg/kg xylazine, and 1 mg/kg acepromazine were injected intraperitoneally. When isoflurane was used, 2–3% isoflurane in oxygen was used as an induction dose and 1–2% isoflurane in oxygen was used as a maintenance dose, with both administered through inhalation. Anesthetized animals were provided with a heat support and ophthalmic ointment. Reflexes and breathing were checked prior to and during surgery to ensure that animals were properly anesthetized.

Anesthetized animals were administered buprenorphine (1 mg/kg) subcutaneously and the left leg was shaved and cleansed with a povidone-iodine swabstick followed by an alcohol swabstick. Cleansing with povidone-iodine and alcohol was repeated thrice. After the final alcohol cleansing, the medial, proximal third of the left tibia was surgically exposed with a ∼2 cm incision made with a size 10 surgical blade, and a ∼1.0 mm hole was drilled into the medullary cavity. Following drilling of the hole, a 5 mm × 1 mm stainless-steel threaded Kirschner wire or K-wire (Zimmer) was implanted into the bone. In the foreign body osteomyelitis model group, 10 μL of arachidonic acid (0.5 μg/0.01 mL) and 50 μL of MRSA (n = 14), K. pneumoniae (n = 4), and P. aeruginosa (n = 4) containing 1.11 ± 0.08 × 108 CFU/mL MRSA (from nine preparations), 1.07 ± 0.06 × 108 CFU/mL K. pneumoniae (from three preparations), and 1.10 × 108 CFU/mL P. aeruginosa (from one preparation) was injected into the hole along with the wire. In the instrumented uninfected inflammation group (n = 15), only 10 μL of arachidonic acid (0.5 μg/0.01 mL) was injected along with the wire. After injections, holes were covered with dental gypsum. The muscle and fascia were closed with 3–0 Vicryl (Ethicon, Inc.,) using simple interrupted sutures. The skin was closed with sterile wound clips and VetBond (3 M Company). The wound was sprayed with AluShield aerosol bandage (Neogen Corporation) and Chew Guard (Summit Hill Laboratories). After bandaging the wound, warm saline was administered subcutaneously, and the animals were allowed to recover under heat support. Animals were continuously monitored until they were awake. After waking, the animals were transferred to cages with regular drinking water.

After surgery, 1 mg/kg buprenorphine was subcutaneously injected every 48 h until imaging at 14 days postsurgery. At 7 days postsurgery, rats were given iron-deficient food TD.80396 (Envigo RMS LLC) and iron-deficient deionized water obtained from a Barnstead Nanopure water purification system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). On the 14th day postsurgery, 4 h prior to radiotracer injection, the animals were deprived of food and provided with only iron-deficient deionized water. In the uninstrumented uninfected control group (n = 4), no surgery was performed, but 7 days before imaging, uninstrumented uninfected rats were given iron-deficient food and water as described above, and on the day of imaging, 4 h prior to radiotracer injection, they were deprived of food and provided with only iron-deficient deionized water.

PET/CT Imaging and Ex Vivo Biodistribution

The body weight, gender, and amount of radioactivity injected for [68Ga]­Ga-BP, [18F]­FDG, and [68Ga]­Ga-citrate in the different groups of Wistar rats used in this study are shown in Table S18. Biodistribution of [68Ga]­Ga-BP, [18F]­FDG, and [68Ga]­Ga-citrate was assessed in Wistar rats using an Inveon PET/CT scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions UNITED STATES, Inc., Molecular Imaging) on the 14th day postsurgery in foreign body osteomyelitis and sterile foreign body inflammation models, followed by ex vivo biodistribution. The PET scanner had an axial field of view of 12.7 cm and a spatial resolution of 1.4 mm. A noncontrast CT scan was performed at low magnification (width: 9.65 cm, height: 9.58 cm), 80 keV beam energy, 500 μA tube current with a transaxial CCD size of 4096 μm, transaxial field of view of 9.652 cm, axial CCD size of 3968 μm, axial field of view of 9.351 cm, and axial scan length of 9.5 cm, with a spatial resolution of 20 μm. PET images were acquired at a fixed bed position focused on achieving maximum coverage of both the hind limbs. Due to the limited 12.7 cm axial field of view (FOV) of the Inveon PET/CT scanner, whole-body imaging was not possible with Wistar rats at this fixed single bed position. Prior to radiotracer injection and imaging, the animals were anesthetized under 2–3% isoflurane in oxygen and maintained under 1–2% isoflurane in oxygen for tracer injection and imaging under heat support. Radiotracers were injected in anesthetized rat via tail veins. After injection, the animals were placed in a prone position on the scanner bed, and body temperature was maintained using a heat lamp. Animals underwent a 10 min static PET scan at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min postinjection, followed by a 7 min CT scan. Between the 60 and 120 min scans, the rats were allowed to recover from anesthesia in a heat-supported recovery chamber in room air. PET images were reconstructed by Inveon Acquisition Workspace software (version 2.1) using the 3DRP algorithm in a 128 × 128 matrix. All corrections (dead time, decay, attenuation) and normalizations were applied to all PET images for accurate quantification of PET data. CT data were reconstructed using the Feldkamp reconstruction algorithm.

The standardized uptake value (SUV) was assessed by drawing the region of interest based on CT over the maximum PET signal in the fusion images using PMOD software version 3.504 (PMOD Technologies LLC) and MIM 7.2.7 software (MIM Software Inc.). PET/CT images were visualized and are presented in the coronal planes.

Ex Vivo Biodistribution and Quantification of Bacteria

After final imaging at the 120 min time point, animals were euthanized, and the organs, tissues, and tibiae were harvested for ex vivo biodistribution assessment. The extent of infection in the harvested lungs, liver, kidneys, urine, K-wires, and tibiae in terms of CFUs was expressed as log10 CFU/K-wire, log10 CFU/excised bone, log10 CFU/g bone weight, or CFU/g bone weight, respectively, calculated using 1:10 serial dilution plating following tissue homogenization, pulverization of the tibiae, or sonication of K-wires. Negative results of the CFU quantification assay were interpreted to be below the limit of detection of the assay.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in at least triplicate, and data in graphs and tables in the manuscript and the Supporting Information are presented as average ± standard deviation. The SUVmean ratio was calculated for each animal by dividing averaged SUV values of the operated tibia by that of the contralateral tibia for the same animal. Standard deviations for SUV were calculated via the “STDEV” functions in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation). Standard deviations for SUVmean ratios were calculated by using the delta method. , Final data are presented as average ± standard deviation. A 2-tailed unpaired student t test was performed to compare uptake using Microsoft Excel. P values <0.05 were considered statistically different.
