A novel workflow for 3D imaging and spatial analysis of nerves in bone☆
Authors: Allison L Horenberg, Eric Z Zeng, Yunke Ren, Tanishk Sinha, Emith Uyanwatte, Melia Matthews, Erica L Scheller, Karl J Lewis, Arvind P Pathak, Warren L Grayson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeripheral sensory and sympathetic nerves innervating bony tissues are of interest for their role in modulating bone function and responding to physiologic stimuli. However, due to their filamentous structure and small diameter, peripheral nerves are difficult to visualize and characterize in dense tissues such as bone. Recent advances in imaging have resulted in novel techniques to reconstruct the 3D bone microenvironment following fluorescent staining, using methods such as whole-mount immunostaining, tissue clearing, and lightsheet microscopy (LSM). While these methods have significantly improved visualization of peripheral nerves within the bony skeleton, current practices in skeletal nerve segmentation rely on manual tracing methods that are both subjective and time consuming. Further, due to excess background signal and moderate off-target staining of skeletal nerves, traditional methods for nerve segmentation remain inadequate for effective quantification. Here, we describe a methods-focused workflow for 3D imaging and spatial analysis of nerves in bone that consists of combining LSM with nerve segmentation using the Ilastik® open-source software. First, LSM was used to acquire high-resolution 3D images of nerves in uninjured and injured murine calvaria. Next, we employed Ilastik® to generate accurate segmentations of peripheral nerves in bone. This approach reduced the need for manual segmentation and overcame the limitations of conventional segmentation methods. We then demonstrated the utility of this workflow as a broadly applicable tool, by performing Ilastik®-based segmentation for three distinct skeletal bone contexts (i.e. use cases) with varied bone samples, tissue clearing, processing protocols, inflammatory states, and imaging modalities. Collectively, this workflow enabled the characterization of spatial nerve patterning in bone and has the potential to provide novel insights into spatially regulated phenomena in bone homeostasis, injury, and disease.Keywords: Lightsheet microscopy, Segmentation, Nerve, Bone, Neuroskeletal, Calvarial mapping

Introduction

Peripheral nerves were first visualized in bone in 1945 (McCredie, 2007). Since then, the field has expanded the understanding of the role of skeletal nerves in mediating bone-related biological processes (Hassan et al., 2023). Peripheral nerves have the potential to regulate bone development (Tomlinson et al., 2016), homeostasis (Pei et al., 2023), responses to injury (Li et al., 2019), and aging (Pei et al., 2023). In particular, sensory and autonomic nerves may contribute both positively and negatively to bone formation, depending on environmental stimuli, injury type, and specific bone region (Hassan et al., 2023). While nerve inhibition and activation studies have shed light on the mechanisms underlying these phenomena, the unique neural signaling pathways and their cell-cell communications with other cell types in the bone microenvironment remain largely unknown.

A barrier that limits our capacity to study nerves and their spatial interactions with other cell types is their filamentous structure. Given their small diameters (∼4 μm) (Horenberg et al., 2025), it is difficult to effectively visualize, segment, and quantify nerve structures with current microscopy and analysis capabilities. Current methods for visualizing nerves in pre-clinical studies primarily include fluorescent imaging and histology (Ohana et al., 2014; Helmbrecht et al., 2023). Fluorescent imaging allows high-resolution (<1 μm) nerve visualization, but is often restricted to a small, 2D field of view (<1 mm) (Fujita et al., 2022).

Nerves have been mapped throughout the periosteum and bone marrow regions in long bones, flat bones, and calvarial sutures (Horenberg et al., 2025; Fujita et al., 2022; Lorenz et al., 2021; Kosaras et al., 2009). Nerves also traverse from the periosteum to the bone marrow via transcortical canals (Horenberg et al., 2025; Fujita et al., 2022). These complex patterns are especially difficult to capture with 2D microscopy, which limits our understanding of spatial patterning and cell-cell spatial interactions. Whole-mount immunostaining and light sheet microscopy (LSM) offer a dramatic improvement over 2D imaging methods by allowing reconstruction of large regions of the 3D tissue microenvironment with moderately fast imaging times (minutes-hours) (Horenberg et al., 2025; Rindone et al., 2021). We have previously used quantitative LSM to visualize nerves, blood vessels and osteoprogenitors throughout the intact calvarial bone (Horenberg et al., 2025; Rindone et al., 2021). Using this method, we were able to visualize unique distributions of nerves in the periosteum and dura mater of uninjured mice, that could not be visualized in calvarial cross-sections. While 3D imaging methods have revolutionized visualization of nerves in bone, clear segmentation and quantitative spatial analysis is required to effectively elucidate the unique interactions and phenomena that occur within the bone compartments. Currently, subjective and time consuming manual tracing is the predominant method employed. This limitation is compounded by low signal-to-noise ratios rendering nerve segmentation remain inadequate for effective quantification. Therefore, there is a critical need for high-resolution, 3D nerve visualization and characterization across large bone regions or throughout entire bones.

In this study, we illustrate how artificial intelligence (AI)-based tools can be integrated into workflows to enhance nerve segmentation and quantification in bone. We describe a 3D imaging and spatially quantitative analysis workflow that generates comprehensive, high-resolution images of skeletal nerves. Specifically, we used the Ilastik® open-source software to accurately segment nerves from low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) immunostained images acquired with LSM. This enabled us to circumvent the inclusion of excess background signal in LSM images that can be exacerbated in inflammatory conditions. To demonstrate feasibility of this new approach, we applied the segmentation workflow to three distinct skeletal bone contexts (i.e. “use cases”), including homeostatic and injury conditions. We initially used this workflow on calvarial defect samples to expand on our previous work in the intact calvaria (Horenberg et al., 2025). While calvarial defect samples have previously been effectively cleared and imaged using the quantitative LSM method (Rindone et al., 2021; Horenberg et al., 2025), long bone samples, however, are generally more challenging, due to thick cortical bone sections and larger auto fluorescent bone marrow regions which demonstrate high levels of erythrocytes and other immune cells. This enhanced background results in increased difficulty segmenting and analyzing neural structures. Therefore, we applied a gold standard analysis to all three use cases, demonstrating efficacy using multiple bones and imaging modalities.

Methods

Animal procedures

Calvarial animal experiments were approved by the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol No. MO21M146 and MO24M96). Animals were housed and cared for in Johns Hopkins' Research Animal Resources central housing facilities. Calvarial mice (n = 6) used in this study were C57BL/6J (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 000664).

Metatarsal animal experiments were approved by the Cornell University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol No. 2020-00466). Animals were housed and cared for in the Cornell CARE facility. Mice (n = 3) used in this study were Cre + mice from a cross between GCaMP6f (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 028865) and DMP1-Cre mouse lines (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 023047), both of which are on a C57BL/6 background.

Knee animal experiments were approved by the Washington University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Saint Louis, MO, United States; Protocol No. 22–0302). Animals were housed and cared for by central housing facilities on a 12-h light/dark cycle and fed ad libitum (PicoLab 5053, LabDiet). Pan-neuronal reporter mice (n = 3) were generated by crossing Baf53b-Cre (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 027826) and Ai9-flox/flox mouse line (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 007909).

Calvaria processing and imaging

For calvarial defect surgeries, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Following anesthesia, the paw-pitch test was performed to ensure complete sedation. Buprenorphine-ER (1 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously. To access the calvarial bone, mice were shaved, sterilized by alcohol and betadine, and draped under sterile conditions. Next, mice were fastened to a stereotaxic frame to stabilize the skull. A sagittal incision was made along the midline of the calvaria. Skin and connective tissue were removed from the parietal bones to ensure localization of the surgical site. A 4-mm defect was then created using a trephine drill bit centered within the right parietal bone. Special precaution was taken to avoid disturbing the underlying dura mater. Following defect creation, the skin incision was closed with 6-0 nylon monofilament sutures. Mice were monitored daily for up to seven days for any neurological deficit, infection, pain, or distress. In addition to uninjured controls, mice were euthanized at weeks 1, 2, and 4 post-surgery. Two mice were harvested for each experimental time point.

For calvarial harvest, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg). Following anesthesia, mice were injected subcutaneously with 200 U of heparin prior to perfusion. An incision was made at the xiphoid process to gain access to the abdomen. Both the skin and peritoneum were cut up to the rib cage. Following disruption of the pleural cavity, heparinized saline (10 U/mL in 1× PBS) was perfused via a 20G blunt needle into the heart via the left ventricle. Prior to perfusion, the right atrium was cut open to allow an open circulation. Perfusion was performed at a rate of 10 mL/min. After perfusion, the skin was removed to gain access to the calvarium. The calvarium was harvested while maintaining the periosteum and the dura mater. Calvaria were fixed in 4 % methanol-free paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. Following fixation, calvaria were washed three times for 1 h with PBS at RT.

To stain and image calvaria via LSM, we used a previously published protocol (Horenberg et al., 2025; Rindone et al., 2021). Briefly, samples were blocked overnight at 4 °C in a blocking buffer containing 10 % V/V normal donkey serum and wash buffer (0.1 M Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.05 % V/V Tween-20, 20 % V/V dimethylsulfoxide, pH 7.5). Primary nerve staining was performed with TUBB3 antibody (Abcam ab18207) at 1:100 dilution for 7 days at 4 °C in blocking buffer. Next, samples were stained with AF647 fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (Fisher Scientific A32795) at 1:100 for 7 days at 4 °C. Between each staining incubation, samples were washed five times for at least 1 h in wash buffer. Following staining and immediately prior to imaging, samples were cleared in a graded series of 2,2-thiodiethanol (TDE in TBS-Tween; 25 %, 50 %, 75 %, 90 %, 100 % × 2). Clearing steps were performed for a minimum of 2 h at RT or overnight at 4 °C. Samples were stored in 100 % TDE prior to imaging.

For lightsheet microscopy (LSM), we used a LaVision Biotec Ultramicroscope II that was aligned for the refractive index of TDE. Calvaria were mounted to a sample mount and immersed in a glass imaging cuvette with 100 % TDE. Samples were imaged using three separate tile acquisitions: a 3 × 1 tile with double-sided illumination, a 3 × 2 tile with left-sided illumination, and a 3 × 2 tile with right-sided illumination. Tiles were overlapped by 15 % to facilitate image stitching. The following hardware and settings were used for all scans: ×2.5 zoom with a ×2 dipping cap (×5 magnification, 1.3 μm x–y pixel size), 5.5 Megapixel sCMOS camera, 20 ms exposure time, 0.154 numerical aperture, and 2.5 μm z-step size. Channels were imaged using the 640 laser and 680/30 filter. Laser intensities were set and held constant for each scan. To process the raw calvarial image data for Imaris®, we converted LaVision Biotec raw OME-TIFF files to the Imaris® file format (.ims) using the Imaris® File Converter 9.5. Tiles were manually aligned and stitched using the Imaris® Stitcher 9.8, resulting in a final 3D image. A pre-defined volume-of-interest (VOI) was defined for the defect region and images were cropped. For all Imaris® VOI, TIFF z-stacks were exported into ImageJ.

Metatarsal processing and imaging

Following disruption of the pleural cavity and access to the heart as described above, mice were perfused through the left ventricle of the heart with zinc formalin (containing 3.7 % formaldehyde). After perfusion, skin was removed to gain access to the metatarsal bones. Third metatarsal bones were dissected and fixed overnight in zinc formalin at 4 °C. As long bones contain a larger bone marrow cavity with erythrocytes that may inhibit clear imaging, decalcification is required prior to whole mount staining. Decalcification occurred over 5 days in 7.5 pH buffered 20 % EDTA at 37 °C with daily solution changes and decolorized with 15 % hydrogen peroxide for less than 1 h.

To stain metatarsal samples, tissues were blocked overnight with 10 % Donkey Serum (Sigma D9663-10ML) and 3 % BSA in PBST. Primary antibodies were incubated at 1:100 dilution in a blocking buffer for 48 h. Primary antibody used was Goat Anti-Vesicular Acetylcholine Transporter Antibody (VAChT, Sigma ABN100). Samples were washed in PBST overnight. Secondary antibodies were then incubated at 1:200 dilution in PBST for 48 h. Secondary antibody used were Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-goat antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific A11055). Samples were washed in PBST overnight. Tissue clearing was then performed with dehydration in 2-ethoxyethanol for 4 h, followed by 3 changes of acetonitrile between 4 h or overnight at room temperature. Bones were then cleared in benzyl alcohol for 4 h before being placed in a final refractive index matching solution of benzyl benzoate for imaging.

Tissue-cleared third metatarsals were imaged using a Light-Sheet microscope (LaVision BioTec) at the Cornell Institute for Biotechnology Resource Center (Cornell BRC). Bones were mounted onto clear hematocrit tubes using cyanoacrylate adhesive, which were subsequently attached to a custom microscope mount. Samples were fully immersed in a refractive index matching solution composed primarily of benzyl benzoate with residual benzyl alcohol. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an Olympus 2× CDC objective (collar setting 3.5, DBE corrected for high refractive indices) at excitation wavelengths of 488 nm. Emission signals were collected using bandpass filters centered at 525 nm. Samples were illuminated sequentially by two orthogonally oriented light sheets (right and left directions), and images were acquired using dynamic horizontal focusing to combine both images into a single image, reducing scattering relative to single-sheet illumination. Both light sheets were precisely calibrated and aligned within the z-plane prior to imaging. Mosaic tiling was conducted to cover entire bones, using a 1 × 3 tile arrangement with 10 % overlap, 3.2× zoom, sheet NA of 1.20, and a sheet width of 40 %. Final pixel resolution was 0.94 × 0.94 × 4 μm. Image data were saved in TIF OME format (six raw data images per bone), stitched using BigStitcher in FIJI (NIH), and subsequently converted into Imaris® (Oxford Instruments) format for using the Imaris® image converter for further analysis.

Knee cross-section processing and imaging

Tissues were processed as described previously (Lorenz et al., 2021) with step-by-step protocol available here (Scheller et al., 2020). Following disruption of the pleural cavity and access to the heart as described above, mice were perfused through the left ventricle of the heart with 10 mL phosphate buffered saline followed by 10 mL 10 % neutral buffered formalin (NBF; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; 23–245,684). Following perfusion, skin was removed to gain access to the knee joints. Next knee joints were harvest, maintaining the surrounding muscle tissue. Collected tissues were post-fixed in 10 % NBF for 24-h at 4 °C. After fixation, tissues were washed for 2 h in diH2O. As with metatarsal bones, knee joints were fully decalcified in 14 % EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; E5134), pH 7.4 and equilibrated in 30 % sucrose solution before embedding in OCT mounting media (Fisher HealthCare 23–730-571, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Embedded tissues were cut at 50 μm on a cryostat (Leica, BuffaloGrove, IL, USA). For immunostaining, cut sections on ColorfrostPlus glass slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific 12-550-18) were incubated in DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min, and washed again before mounting with Fluoromount-G (ThermoFisher Scientific 00-4958-02). Tiled images were taken with a 10× objective on a Nikon (Tokyo, Japan) spinning disk confocal microscope (μm/px = 0.650, step size = 2.5 μm). Stitching of the nd2 multipoint files was done using the FIJI Stitching plugin “snake by rows.” Knee z-stacks were input directly into Ilastik® for analysis and subsequently analyzed by ImageJ as 3D Imaris® reconstruction was not necessary.

Image processing and analysis

All image processing and analysis were performed with Imaris® 9.10 and 10.0, Ilastik® (Berg et al., 2019), and ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012) software running on a Dell Precision 7820 Tower workstation. The workstation was equipped with a dual Intel Xeon Gold 6240 processor, 384 GB DDR4 SDRAM (2666 MHz speed), 512 GB and 1 TB SATA SSDs, NVIDIA Quadro RTX5000 graphics card (16 GB GDDR6 memory), and Windows 10 Pro for Workstations.

We segmented nerves in all images using Ilastik®, a freely available machine-learning based image segmentation tool (Berg et al., 2019). To train the Ilastik® pixel classifier, one or multiple z-plane slices had to be selected. Slices were selected based on clear visualization of true positive nerve signal with excess background to train the classifier. Depending on the extent of variability between sample conditions, biological replicates, or within z-slices of an individual sample, multiple z-slices may need to be selected, thus, providing the classifier with a sufficient spread of data variability. Given the fact that some samples may have varied regions of nerve visibility within one image, we first determined the number of slices per condition (e.g. Timepoint 1), replicate (e.g. Sample 1), and tissue section (e.g. Z-Slice 1) that were needed to accurately segment the nerves. In previous studies on uninjured mice, one z-slice per condition was sufficient to segment the nerves in the remaining replicates (Horenberg et al., 2025). In this study, due to the enhanced background and variability that occurred in defect samples, two slices from two distinct replicates per condition, which in this instance was timepoint, were needed to accurately segment the calvarial nerves. Thus, a total of six slices were used for Ilastik® training. In metatarsal and knee joint samples, only one training z-slice was required. In general, multiple iterations with varied training image numbers are required to evaluate the tracing efficacy and run time of the classifier. Subsequently, an idealized training image number can be selected that results in effective tracing without extensive run time.

All potential features were selected for Ilastik® training to account for changes in nerve intensity, shape, and texture. Next, slices were trained on the Ilastik® pixel classification algorithm by selecting true positive signal and background signal for each of the training slices. Following complete labeling of all z-slices using the training data and running the classifier, prediction masks containing binary segmentations were exported for each label. The remaining z-slices for each sample were segmented using Ilastik's batch segmentation option and the corresponding prediction masks generated. Following Ilastik® segmentation, binary segmented z-stacks were generated in ImageJ by Ilastik®. Together, each z-slice from one sample was recombined to generate a binary segmentation of the nerve channel, where only the true nerve signal was segmented. Following this step, VOI post-processing occurred in Imaris®. However, it should be noted that any quantification platform that is capable of processing large 3D image files can be used for post-processing. In this instance, the binary image was imported back into Imaris®, combined with the original TUBB3+ signal channel, and analyzed using the Imaris® absolute threshold surface segmentation workflow. This segmentation was then used to mask the original TUBB3+ signal to reduce background and non-specific signal, resulting in improved nerve visualizations. Contrast-to-noise ratios (CNRs) were calculated from 2D maximum intensity projections of the original signal in ImageJ using the following equation:

The average pixel intensities and their standard deviations were derived for both the region segmented by Ilastik (SSeg) and the remaining area (SBG). Resulting CNRs were calculated by subtracting the average intensity of the signal outside the segmentation from that of the segmented region. This value was divided by the standard deviation of the background (σBG) to calculate the CNR.

Radial nerve density maps were computed as described previously (Ren et al., 2024). Briefly, the binary representations of nerves were processed in ImageJ to derive the centerline for each via the “skeletonization” function; next the tagged skeleton (i.e. branch points) was generated via the “analyze skeleton” function; finally the Euclidean distance map (EDM) was generated via the “Distance map” function. The binary representation, tagged skeleton, and EDM images were input to a customized MATLAB® script to derive radial nerve density plots. Resulting radial density plots exhibited three concentric rings corresponding to the inner, middle, and outer regions of the defect area.

For gold standard analysis, maximum intensity projections were generated in ImageJ for each use case. Using the ImageJ Neuroanatomy plugin, true nerve signal was manually traced for each image. The resulting tracing was used to generate a skeletonized representation of the true nerve signal within each image. Next, maximum intensity projections of the Ilastik® segmentations were also generated in ImageJ and the overlap of the skeletonized signal with the Ilastik® segmentations was calculated.

Results

Quantitative LSM generates 3D visualizations of skeletal nerves

To visualize skeletal nerves in intact calvarial bones, we used a quantitative lightsheet imaging platform that combines whole mount immunostaining, tissue clearing, and lightsheet imaging, resulting in quantifiable 3D image reconstructions (Fig. 1A). Following transcardial perfusion to remove excess blood and heme, which interferes with fluorescence imaging, we isolated and fixed the bones of interest for immunostaining. Calvaria were isolated from C57BL/6J mice, stained with primary and secondary antibodies, and cleared with 2,2-thiodiethanol. This imaging workflow resulted in comprehensive and high-resolution images of TUBB3+ skeletal nerves in the uninjured murine calvaria (Fig. 1B). Further, we visualized TUBB3+ nerves in 4-mm critical-sized calvarial defects, where a significant portion of the parietal bone had been removed via calvarial defect surgery (Fig. 1C). Although the background was significantly enhanced in defect samples relative to that in uninjured samples, TUBB3+ nerves were still visible throughout the calvaria and the defect region.

In metatarsal samples stained for VAChT, autonomic nerves were clearly visualized throughout the entire tissue, with large populations of putative VAChT+ structures throughout the bone marrow region (Fig. 1D). Nerves were also seen traversing the transcortical canals, connecting the periosteum to the bone marrow regions.

In knee cross sections of Baf53b-Cre–R26R-tdTomato (Ai9) reporter mice (hereto referred to as Baf53b-tdTomato), which express tdTomato+ fluorescence in all nerves, tdTomato+ nerves were observed on the anterior and posterior periosteum with few nerves traveling throughout the cartilage and epiphyseal region (Fig. 1E).

Ilastik® successfully segmented nerves exhibiting low signal-to-noise using machine learning

As the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) of nerves in whole-mount images was generally low, we developed an Ilastik®-based workflow to effectively segment skeletal nerves while reducing inclusion of the background (Fig. 2A). Comparing the Ilastik® pipeline with Imaris® intensity-based segmentation, we demonstrated improved segmentation of nerve-like structures, with reduced segmentation of non-nerve background (Fig. 2B). To illustrate how slice selection and iterative training refines nerve segmentation, we compared one, two, and four z-slices as training data (Fig. 2C). Additional training slices were selected based on intra-sample variability and inter-replicate variability. Thus, the initial two slices were selected from the sample at different axial locations, while the four z-slices were selected from two distinct samples. In this example, additional training slices improved nerve segmentation by reducing background segmentation of non-nerve structures. While a plateau in segmentation improvement was not reached, only slight differences were found between two and four z-slice segmentations.

While processing time may vary based on workstation, we quantified training time, inference time, and memory usage for the four z-slice training samples. While the overall defect 3D nerve channels are 22–23 GB, the individual training slices are 19.5 MB. Following manual tracing of selected z-slices, training time was 5 h and used 4 % of system memory (∼16 GB). For batch analysis, the inference time was 4 min for ten additional z-slices, which used 1.5 % of system memory (∼6 GB).

To validate the ability of the Ilastik® pipeline to segment nerves at a range of CNRs, we applied Ilastik® segmentation to calvarial defect injuries ranging from one week to four weeks post injury. In these samples, nerves rapidly infiltrated after injury and then retracted over time (Fig. 3A). At early time points (week 1), the nerve signal demonstrated a higher CNR (week 1 = 1.059). At two- and four-weeks post injury, the nerves were still present throughout the defect region, however, their CNR (week 2 = 0.937 and week 4 = 0.913) was lower as compared to the earlier timepoints. Further, the overall maximum intensity of the nerves was also lower at late timepoints.

Given the high variability between conditions, replicates, and tissue sections, we used two z-slices from two samples from each timepoint. The two z-slices were selected from both the center and the edge of the defect region to account for the variability between the nerves throughout each individual sample. Following initial training of Ilastik®, batch analysis was applied to all remaining z-slices. Z-slices were reconstructed in ImageJ, which resulted in a binary segmentation for each sample (Fig. 3B). Samples were then imported back into Imaris® and analyzed using the 3D surface segmentation (Fig. 3C). This segmentation was also used to mask the original channel and remove un-segmented background from regions outside of the Ilastik® mask (Fig. 3D). Ultimately, these images exclusively showed the nerve signal without excessive background signal.

Using radial analysis, we identified nerves throughout the defect region in early timepoints (Week 1 and Week 2), without any clear geometric distribution (Fig. 3E). At later time points (Week 4), nerves were primarily observed in the top and bottom regions of the defect. This may be due to the close location of the coronal and lambdoid sutures, which demonstrated high densities of nerves compared to the sagittal suture (Horenberg et al., 2025).

Ilastik® accurately segments nerves in other bones and imaging modalities

To demonstrate the general capability of the segmentation workflow on other skeletal samples, we applied the Ilastik® to both metatarsal and knee joint datasets. For both samples, the nerve signal variability was substantially lower than in calvarial defect samples. Thus, only one training slice from each use case was necessary to effectively train the classifier. In metatarsal samples, VAChT nerves were clearly segmented in all regions with limited background inclusion (Fig. 4A). For knee joint samples, although the orientation of each sample is vastly different, the Ilastik® segmentation was remarkably effective with only one training z-slice (Fig. 4A). Further, bright muscle regions, which often appear at higher intensities than the nerve signals were not segmented, while nerves in muscle regions were segmented. Thus, the Ilastik® segmentation workflow enabled precise visualization of nerve structures and reduced the need for manual tracing for quantitative analysis.

Gold standard evaluation. A) Gold standard and Ilastik® overlay on TUBB3+, VAChT+, and Baf53b + nerves in calvarial defects, metatarsal bones, and knee joints, respectively. Scale bar is 100 μm. B) Percent overlap calculation, between gold standard and Ilastik® data.

To evaluate the capacity of the workflow to accurately segment a majority of labeled skeletal nerves, we generated gold standard datasets using manual tracing for all three use cases (Fig. 4A). Using the ImageJ® Neuroanatomy plug in, which can generate a manually traced dataset in 2D, we used maximum intensity projections of each original image to develop skeletonized images for each sample. Next, skeletons were overlaid onto the projected Ilastik® segmentations, and the percent overlap calculated for each sample (Fig. 4B). These data represent the true positive segmentations, from which the false negative rate could be extrapolated. In this instance, the percent overlap is equivalent to the recall of the system. Across all three use cases, the overlap was 80.45 ± 8.37 %.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced an Ilastik®-based nerve segmentation workflow for accurate and efficient segmentation of skeletal nerves. By combining multiple image analysis platforms including Imaris®, ImageJ, and Ilastik®, we were able to segment nerves in high background, low CNR images with excellent nerve coverage. We applied this workflow to three use cases, demonstrating its widespread usability in multiple mouse models, tissue clearing and processing steps, and imaging modalities. In all three cases, the Ilastik® segmentation workflow effectively segmented nerves, allowing investigations into their spatial distributions. Notably, our study did not evaluate the performance of Ilastik®’s embedded AI engine. Rather the focus was to illustrate how the tool could be harnessed for the showcased applications.

Studying nerve spatial distributions provides unique insights into the impact of injury, aging, and disease on the bone microenvironment. Using LSM, we could generate high-resolution 3D images of nerves in different murine bones. In a previous study, we investigated the impact of aging on calvarial neurovascular distributions using the lightsheet platform (Horenberg et al., 2025). Due to the high variability of nerve intensities with the animal's age, we used Ilastik® to generate binary nerve segmentations for subsequent quantitative analysis. In addition to improving the quality from the Imaris®-based intensity segmentation, the Ilastik® segmentations enabled the identification of age-associated nerve loss with independent patterning in the frontal and parietal bones. Thus, in this study, we aimed to expand the applications of the Ilastik® pipeline and demonstrate its potential for more generalized use. In calvarial bones with injuries, there was higher background signal compared to uninjured samples, likely due to enhanced inflammation and fibrotic response. Given this range of intensities and varied CNRs, standard intensity-based segmentation did not sufficiently identify all nerves at all timepoints without including additional background. Thus, we used Ilastik® to segment all nerves accurately and effectively in these defect injury sample. Using Ilastik®, we segmented nerves at multiple timepoints after injury from Week 1 to Week 4. Given that the CNRs for these timepoints were all equal to or less than one, this segmentation performs remarkably to segment exclusively the filamentous nerve structures. Few studies have investigated the distribution of nerves after calvarial defect injury using fluorescent imaging. Following sub-critical calvarial defect injury and without tissue clearing, Meyers et al., visualized a reinnervation of the defect region along the endocranial aspect that increased from day 3 to day 28 in confocal images (Meyers et al., 2020). Similarly, we qualitatively visualized a dense infiltration of nerves throughout the periosteal surface at early timepoints. The addition of tissue clearing and LSM enabled complete visualization of nerves throughout the entire defect region and can be applied in future studies to fully evaluate nerve infiltration and retraction patterns in calvarial defects.

To validate the use of Ilastik® to segment nerves in other bones and imaging modalities, we incorporated two additional use cases into our gold standard analysis. In metatarsal samples, we visualized dense VAChT signal throughout the bone marrow regions, which are known to express higher densities of autonomic nerves than the periosteum (Elefteriou et al., 2014). Using Ilastik® segmentation, we were able to effectively segment periosteal and bone marrow regions, allowing future generation of meaningful quantitative metrics between conditions. In knee joint sections, we visualized the nerve supply to the anterior and posterior aspect of the knee form the sciatic and femoral nerves. While recent studies have developed methods to visualize nerves in the joint in 3D (Ko et al., 2024), using 2D thick sagittal sections and Ilastik® segmentation, we showed that 2D cross-sections with Ilastik® segmentation could also be used for spatial analysis. While all use cases showed effective nerve visualization and segmentation, varying fixation, staining, and tissue clearing protocols were used for each tissue. Due to sample thickness and bone marrow content, whole mount immunostaining protocols may require solution and timeline adjustments to allow adequate antibody diffusion and subsequent signal visualization. For thin tissues such as the calvaria, decalcification is not required for signal visualization. Thus, more complex protocols that include decalcification and harsher tissue clearing can be applied to most bones to generate a standardized workflow, however, best practice requires evaluation of each processing step to evaluate need, impact to tissue architecture, and overall efficiency.

In all three use cases, we generated gold standard data sets to accurately represent the nerve signal in each sample. Comparing the Ilastik® segmentations to the gold standard data, we identified a strong overlap with a high percentage of true positive segmentation. Unsurprisingly, the knee joint segmentations were the least accurate, which was likely because the nerve structures were not always continuous filamentous structures in the cryosectioned samples. Occasionally, some nerves appeared more punctate and disconnected, likely because the full nerve was not contained in the bone cross section being imaged. This fact underscores the enhanced benefit that whole mount 3D imaging provides, allowing full, connected nerve structures to be visualized throughout the entire bone. Unfortunately, with our approach, we were unable to adequately measure the false positives in every imaged sample because overlaying the gold standard skeletonized structures with the Ilastik® segmented nerves results in pixels within the width of the segmented nerves being counted as false positives. This results in high but incorrect false positive values. Due to the inability to measure false positives, it is not possible to accurately calculate the precision and specificity of the segmented data. Thus, we recommend that subjective evaluation follows the training iterations to ensure excess false positive signal is not being segmented.

The Ilastik® segmentation platform uses machine learning to generate binary segmentations of skeletal nerves. For both fluorescent and non-fluorescent imaging modalities, machine learning techniques are most used for nerve segmentation. For non-fluorescent modalities, including CT, MRI, and ultrasound, multiple machine learning-based segmentation methods have been developed for central nervous system (CNS) (Ranjbarzadeh et al., 2022; Valošek et al., 2024) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) images (Buyukcelik et al., 2023; Balsiger et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2023). These methods are primarily designed for human patient images and tissues. In comparison, fluorescent imaging modalities have been more widely applied for nerve visualization in animals (Helmbrecht et al., 2023; Im et al., 2019). Although several machine learning nerve segmentation methods have been developed for the CNS (Giacopelli et al., 2023; Bao and Gong, 2024), many of these focus on segmenting neuronal nuclei in addition to their axons. Thus, for the PNS, segmentation methods must exclusively focus on segmenting nerve axons. In addition to machine learning methods, other methods exist to segment nerves, including manual tracing and intensity-based thresholding, as reviewed in Helmbrect et al (Helmbrecht et al., 2023). While these methods can be effective for segmenting nerves, they are limited by tracing time and excess background inclusion, respectively. Therefore, machine learning methods can eliminate the need for extensive user input and reduce the impact of background or non-specific staining.

Within the field of neuroskeletal imaging, there remains a challenge with distinguishing nerves from other filamentous structures such as autofluorescent extracellular matrix fibers, non-specific edge artifacts, and in some cases blood vessels. Thus, when implementing this workflow, it is crucial that true nerve structures are accurately discerned for segmentation training and subsequent analysis. To accurately identify nerves in bone, we highly recommend using multiple markers to co-stain them. For example, antibody-based markers, such as TUBB3, can be combined with transgenic mouse models, such as Baf53b-tdTomato mice. Careful pre-optimization of each antibody using positive control tissues (e.g. intact spinal column with both bone and DRG/spinal cord) and negative controls is essential. Collectively, co-localization of these markers (and even a lack of co-localization with vascular markers), as well as the resulting filamentous, thin (<5 μm) structures can provide enhanced confidence in the staining patterns.

While we demonstrated that the Ilastik®-based segmentation workflow could be applied to multiple imaging modalities, bones, and fluorescent signals, several limitations remain. Training data must contain a sufficient density of nerves within one z-slice and have clear fiber-like nerve morphologies. For samples with high variability among experimental conditions and biological replicates, several training slices must be used to effectively train the classifier, which can significantly slow the processing time. For this study, we used a high-performance computer (Dell Precision 7820 Tower workstation equipped with a dual Intel Xeon Gold 6240 processor and 384 GB DDR4 SDRAM (2666 MHz speed)) for classifier training and batch processing. Therefore, adaptation of this method on computers with lower processing power may significantly increase the processing time. Notably, both training and inference sessions on large datasets did not exceed 17 GB of RAM, thus, standard computers with 32 GB of RAM should be capable of effectively performing this workflow. Regardless, batch-processing does not require user input and can be run simultaneously with additional programs running in the background.

In addition, determination of true segmentation success (and minimum required training slices) remains subjective. Although, additional optimization of Ilastik® could be performed by adding training slices, one needs to find a balance between processing time and tracing efficacy. Upon effective tracing of most nerves, no further training data were needed. If additional training fails to effectively segment most of the nerves, it might be indicative that the CNR may be too low to effectively use this segmentation workflow. Overall, this method was widely applicable to fluorescent nerves in whole mount and sectioned samples for a wide range of applications and could be easily implemented exclusively using open-source software (excluding Imaris® visualization).
