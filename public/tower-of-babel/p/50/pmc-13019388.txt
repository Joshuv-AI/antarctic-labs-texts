Simulation of Full-Area Propped Fracture Propagation and Productivity in Shale Gas Reservoirs
Authors: Xuemeng Yu, Kai Chen, Cuicui Wang, Xu Zhu, Guiqi Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-viscosity slickwater is the dominant fracturing fluid for shale gas reservoir stimulation, characterized by superior capability in generating complex fracture networks yet inadequate proppant transport performance. This deficiency commonly leads to a limited propped fracture length and undesirable proppant placement profile. To address these challenges and enhance proppant placement quality as well as single-well gas productivity, a fully coupled numerical model integrating nonplanar 3D fracture propagation, proppant transport dynamics, and a black-oil model is developed using field geological and engineering parameters from actual horizontal wells. The impacts of key fracturing operation parameters on proppant transport behavior and well productivity are systematically investigated via numerical simulations. Results indicate that both proppant transport distance and gas production increase with rising injection rate, but the growth rate diminishes when the injection rate exceeds 16 m3/min. Similarly, increasing fracturing fluid viscosity improves proppant transport distance and gas production, while no further enhancement is observed once the viscosity surpasses 63 mPa·s. Smaller proppant particle size contributes to longer proppant transport distance but results in gradual degradation of well productivity. Under a constant total proppant volume, merely substituting a large fraction of small-sized proppants extends propped fracture length but fails to boost gas production. Optimally incorporating a moderate proportion of ultrafine proppants based on the original field scheme achieves superior proppant transport distance and productivity performance. Field tests validate that appropriately increasing fracturing fluid viscosity and ultrafine proppant dosage facilitates full-area proppant placement and contributes positively to gas production enhancement in shale gas reservoirs. The findings of this study provide significant guidance for optimizing injection rate design, fracturing fluid selection, and proppant type screening in shale gas hydraulic fracturing operations.

Introduction

At present, oil and gas resources remain the world’s primary energy sources. With the gradual depletion of conventional oil and gas resources, the exploration and development of unconventional oil and gas resources (such as shale gas, tight oil, coalbed methane, etc.) have become an important strategic direction for safeguarding energy security. , Unconventional resources, represented by the shale gas revolution, have become a significant contributor to the growth in global oil and gas production, driving the adjustment and transformation of the global energy structure. As a natural gas resource stored in shale formations, shale gas boasts advantages of long production life and extended operation cycles compared with conventional natural gas. Additionally, shale gas is widely distributed in downhole formations with large reservoir volumes and generally high gas content, enabling shale gas wells to maintain stable long-term production and thus demonstrating significant economic value and strategic importance. However, shale gas reservoirs exhibit poor physical properties characterized by ultralow porosity and ultralow permeability. To achieve commercial exploitation, large-scale full-well reservoir fracture network stimulation is essential to attain economic productivity. Therefore, horizontal well volume fracturing has emerged as one of the key technologies for the effective development of shale gas reservoirs. Its objective is to maximize the effective fracture stimulated volume, which is accomplished in two aspects: first, optimizing fracture spacing, fracture half-length, and other parameters to maximize the fracture sweep area and sweep volume, while enhancing fracture complexity through different well completion methods and technical measures; second, realizing the effective transportation and placement of proppants, optimizing proppant particle size and proportion to achieve the goal of full sand filling in fracture systems of varying scales, thereby maximizing the effective fracture stimulated volume.

The transport and distribution of proppants within complex fracture systems directly affect the stimulation effectiveness of hydraulic fracturing treatments. Accordingly, investigating proppant transport behavior and sand placement patterns within hydraulic fractures is of critical theoretical and engineering significance for the efficient development of shale gas reservoirs. Owing to the complex geological settings and the involvement of multiple intricate physical processes throughout fracturing operations, the quantitative characterization and real-time monitoring of proppant distribution remain technically challenging. To date, domestic and international researchers have primarily investigated proppant transport laws in fractures through laboratory physical experiments, − numerical simulations, − and the integrated coupling of these two approaches. , Research efforts have advanced from steady-state analyses of individual fractures to unsteady-state multiscale simulations of complex fracture networks, uncovering the underlying mechanisms and governing laws of key operational parameters, − and establishing a synergistic control theory incorporating fluid viscosity, pumping rate, proppant properties, and other critical factors. ,

Viscosity determines the shear stress and suspension capacity of fluids; it controls the transport distance and settling rate of proppants by altering the equilibrium relationship among drag force, buoyancy, and gravity acting on the proppants. , At high viscosity, the internal shear stress of the fluid is substantial, and the drag force exerted on proppants exceeds gravity, which inhibits proppant settling, facilitates the transportation of proppants into branch fractures, keeps the fracture walls open, reduces proppant retention due to collision, and mitigates particle bridging and plugging caused by rough fracture surfaces. At low viscosity, insufficient drag force leads to rapid proppant settling and sandbank formation, which are prone to proppant accumulation near the wellbore and subsequent sand plugging. The fluid injection rate controls the net pressure within fractures and the fracture propagation morphology, thereby indirectly determining the transport space of proppants. At high injection rates, the net pressure in fractures increases rapidly, overcoming the in situ stress difference of the reservoir, inducing the generation of a large number of secondary fractures and microfractures from the main fracture, forming a complex fracture network and providing more transport channels for proppants. At low injection rates, the insufficient net pressure in fractures generates only a single planar main fracture, which limits the proppant transport space and leads to easy centralized sedimentation. The injection rate directly affects the proppant transport capacity: at high rates, the fluid is in a turbulent state with enhanced drag force, driving long-distance proppant transport; however, excessively high rates will intensify the collision between proppants and fracture walls, causing particle breakage and energy loss and reducing the proppant packing density in the near-wellbore zone. At low rates, the fluid is in a laminar flow state with a weak drag force, and proppants are prone to sedimentation and accumulation, failing to migrate to the far-wellbore zone. Particle size determines the relative magnitudes of proppant gravity, inertia force, and fluid drag force while also affecting its adaptability to fracture scale, thus exerting dual control over migration paths and placement morphology. Coarse-grained proppants (e.g., 30/50 mesh resin-coated sand): gravity exceeds fluid drag force, resulting in high settling velocity, easy accumulation at the bottom of main fractures, and difficulty in entering narrow or microfractures; however, they can ensure the long-term high conductivity of main fractures. Fine-grained proppants (e.g., 70/140 mesh ceramic proppants): drag force surpasses gravity, leading to slow settling, easy migration into secondary and microfractures with fluid flow, and uniform filling of fracture networks; yet, they feature low particle strength and are prone to crushing under high pressure, which causes the attenuation of conductivity.

These three factors collectively govern the proppant transport distance, distribution uniformity, and sandbank morphology by balancing the drag force and gravitational force, as well as coupling the dynamic propagation of fracture networks with proppant transport. Fluid viscosity acts as the fundamental factor determining the minimum threshold for proppant-carrying capacity; in the absence of sufficient viscosity, long-distance proppant transport cannot be achieved even with high injection rates and small particle sizes. Injection rate serves as the driving force, controlling both the scale of the generated fracture network and the kinetic energy for proppant transport, which should be reasonably matched with fluid viscosity to prevent ineffective fluid injection. Particle size acts as the matching factor and must be optimized according to fracture width and transport distance requirements, thereby forming a synergistic placement pattern by “coarse proppants in main fractures to ensure conductivity and fine proppants in secondary and microfractures to enlarge the stimulated reservoir volume (SRV)”. Therefore, the synergistic interaction of these three factors represents the core principle for optimizing fracturing design. −

Hybrid fracturing, combined proppant placement, and high-efficiency variable-viscosity slickwater have become standard practices in global unconventional oil and gas stimulation. However, considerable heterogeneity exists across different shale formations, and the geological conditions in China are more complex than those in major shale gas-producing countries such as the United States. The development of shale gas fracturing in China started relatively late; theories regarding shale reservoir fracture behavior remain incomplete, and fundamental understanding is still insufficient in key aspects, including fracture propagation mechanisms, spatial distribution characteristics, proppant transport and placement laws within fractures, and the controlling factors of fracture conductivity. Furthermore, studies focusing on the influences of different fracturing operation parameters on proppant transport are limited, and conventional analytical methods seldom account for the impact of proppant transport on well productivity. These gaps represent the primary challenges currently encountered in the fracturing development of shale reservoirs in China.

Therefore, to address the aforementioned challenges, achieve full-area proppant placement in shale gas reservoirs, and improve proppant placement quality as well as single-well productivity, this study builds on existing experimental and numerical simulation research on hydraulic fracture propagation. Based on the nonplanar 3D fracture propagation model, proppant transport model, and black oil model and by incorporating geological and engineering parameters of an actual field horizontal well, a numerical model for fracture propagation and productivity analysis was established. This model is adopted to investigate the influence of different fracturing construction parameters on proppant transport and well productivity. This research aims to determine the optimal matching relationships among fracturing injection rate, fracturing fluid viscosity, and proppant assemblage suitable for shale gas reservoirs, to provide a solid theoretical foundation and technical support for the design optimization of fracturing treatments in shale gas wells.

Natural fractures are neglected in the model, and articular hydraulic fractures are represented as continuously distributed primary fractures. For most shale gas reservoirs in China, the minimum principal stress is horizontal and vertical fractures are predominantly generated during hydraulic fracturing. Therefore, the fractures are assumed to remain vertical while being able to propagate arbitrarily in the horizontal direction. Basic Governing Equations for the Numerical Model of Post-Fracturing Productivity

The black-oil model is currently the most well-developed, mature, and widely employed numerical model for reservoir simulation. It is capable of describing the multiphase flow of oil, gas, and water, where the gas phase can exist as both free gas and dissolved gas in the liquid phase. Therefore, to satisfy the requirements of post-fracturing productivity simulation, this study establishes a numerical productivity model based on the black-oil formulation, using the geological-engineering integrated fracturing optimization design software (FrSmart). The fundamental assumptions adopted in the black-oil model are as follows:

The reservoir contains at most three phases: oil, gas, and water, whose seepage behavior obeys Darcy’s law.

The oil and gas phases attain instantaneous phase equilibrium; oil and water are immiscible, and gas is insoluble in water.

If dissolved gas exists, then only the dissolution of gas into the oil phase is considered, while the volatilization of oil components into the gas phase is neglected.

Fluid flow within the reservoir is isothermal.

The reservoir rock and fluids are slightly compressible.

Effect of Injection Rate on Proppant Transport and Well Productivity

With the other basic parameters kept constant (low viscosity slickwater as the fracturing fluid and a blend of 70–140 mesh quartz sand and 40–70 mesh ceramsite as the proppant), numerical simulations were conducted to investigate the effects of different injection rates on proppant transport behavior and well productivity, respectively. The simulation results are shown in Figures , , and . It can be observed from the results that the proppant transport distance and well productivity exhibit a positive correlation with the increase in injection rate, demonstrating that a relatively high injection rate facilitates the long-distance transport of proppants by the fracturing fluid. Nevertheless, when the injection rate exceeds a critical threshold, the growth rates of both proppant transport distance and three-year cumulative gas production tend to decelerate, with the optimal injection rate determined to be 16 m3/min.

It can be observed from the results that proppant transport distance and gas production increase progressively with the rise in fracturing fluid viscosity, which demonstrates that elevated fracturing fluid viscosity facilitates the transport of proppants to the far end of fractures. Effect of Proppant Particle Size on Proppant Transport and Well Productivity

With the fracturing fluid viscosity and total proppant volume kept constant, numerical simulations were conducted to investigate the effects of five proppant types with distinct particle sizes on proppant transport and well productivity, namely, 40–70 mesh ceramsite, 40–70 mesh quartz sand, 70–140 mesh quartz sand, 200 mesh ultrafine proppant, and 270 mesh ultrafine proppant. The simulation results are shown in Figures , , and . It can be observed from the results that proppant transport distance increases with the decrease in proppant particle size, demonstrating that small-particle-size proppants are more prone to migrate to the far end of fractures, which facilitates the effective propping of the distal fracture zones. Nevertheless, owing to the lower fracture conductivity of small-particle-size proppants, well productivity exhibits a gradual decline as the proppant particle size decreases.

Field Application

To investigate the effect of fracturing fluid viscosity on full-area propped fracture stimulation, a field trial of medium viscosity slickwater sand-carrying fracturing was implemented in Well H52–5, a shale gas well in the southern Sichuan Basin, in March 2025, and the fracturing operation was successfully completed. The horizontal lateral of the well extends 1673 m, with a total of 23 fracturing stages designed. Stages 12 to 23 adopted full-process medium viscosity slickwater for sand transport, with an operational injection rate of 16 m3/min. The designed cluster spacing and stimulation scale were comparable to those of adjacent Well H52-4. As shown in Table , the test well exhibits superior performance in both daily gas production per kilometer and gas production rate per unit pressure drop relative to Well H52-4, which validates that the full-process medium viscosity slickwater sand-carrying fracturing technique exerts a favorable effect on enhancing well productivity.

The production enhancement effect of ultrafine proppants has also been preliminarily validated. Conclusions

Injection rate, fracturing fluid viscosity, and proppant particle size all exert certain influences on the proppant transport distance and well productivity. Within a reasonable range, increasing the injection rate and fracturing fluid viscosity can improve proppant transport distance and productivity. Nevertheless, the effect diminishes or plateaus once it exceeds the optimal limit. With a constant total proppant volume, smaller proppants can migrate farther but result in lower productivity. By incorporating ultrafine proppants into the original field application scheme, both transport distance and productivity can be improved simultaneously. Field applications confirm that appropriately increasing fracturing fluid viscosity and ultrafine proppant dosage is conducive to achieving full-area propped fractures and enhancing gas well performance.

In this paper, the quantitative response laws of fracturing parameters, proppant transport, and well productivity in shale gas reservoirs are clarified via numerical simulation. The regulatory mechanism of proppant particle size combination on fracture propping and gas production is revealed, and an innovative concept for the synergistic application of ultrafine proppants is proposed. The research findings not only enrich the theoretical system of volume fracturing in shale gas reservoirs and provide a scientific basis for the optimization of full-area propped fracturing technology, parameter design, and operational scheme formulation but also carry important theoretical significance and engineering guidance value for enhancing shale gas development efficiency and guaranteeing the large-scale and effective development of unconventional oil and gas resources.

In this work, a single-stage, single-cluster fracture model for horizontal wells is adopted, focusing only on typical operational parameters and proppant combinations. Natural fractures, formation heterogeneity, and complex fracture networks are not taken into account, leading to certain simplifications between simulation and actual field conditions. In future research, complex fracture network models can be established to perform refined multifield coupling simulations. The model can be further modified in combination with long-term field production performance, so as to continuously improve the theory and technical system of full-area propped fracturing.
