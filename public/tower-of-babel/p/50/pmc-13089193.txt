Number words recruit numerosity-related cortex in 3- to 5-year-old children
Authors: Alyssa J. Kersey, Lauren S. Aulet, Jessica F. Cantlon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Learning to map number words onto their ordinal and quantitative meanings is critical in the acquisition of formal mathematics. Previous neuroimaging work in adults suggests that the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) represents verbal, symbolic number (i.e., number words) as well as non-verbal, non-symbolic numerosity (i.e., number of items in an array), but it is unclear when this overlap emerges in development. If regions that represent number words rely on representations of numerosity, then there should be functional overlap between number word and numerosity representations in the brain, even in children as young as 3–5 years of age. Using fMRI, we compared neural responses to number word sequences in an auditory task with neural responses to numerosity in a visual task. Across multiple whole-brain analyses, we found that the bilateral IPS is involved in perception of number word sequences, even in very young children. Moreover, we found that child-to-adult neural synchrony during the auditory task was significantly correlated with children’s formal math ability. Lastly, we found significant overlap between auditory number word processing and visual numerosity processing, suggesting that even early in development, similar neural substrates are involved in both verbal, symbolic number, and non-verbal, non-symbolic numerosity.

Introduction

Preverbal and verbal number concepts develop predictably and tractably in children. Children have robust and abstract nonverbal representations of quantity (numerosities) beginning in infancy (Izard et al., 2009, Lipton and Spelke, 2003), but they do not begin to learn formal verbal counting until around 2.5 years (Wynn, 1992). A central focus of research in numerical cognition concerns what role nonverbal numerosity representations play in the acquisition of symbolic number (Cantlon et al., 2009, Carey et al., 2017, Odic et al., 2015, vanMarle et al., 2018). Theories of cognitive development predict that higher level cognition builds on basic, “core”, concepts (Gallistel, 1990, Spelke and Kinzler, 2007), and theories of concept representation suggest that symbolic concepts are grounded in basic perceptual and motor representations (Barsalou, 1999, Binder and Desai, 2011). In line with these theories, accumulating neural evidence suggests that the cultural acquisition of mathematics, including numerical symbols, builds on primitive numerosity representations in the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) (Ansari, 2008, Cantlon et al., 2006, Cantlon et al., 2009, Dehaene and Cohen, 2007, Nieder and Miller, 2004).

The IPS is a strong neural candidate for number word acquisition because numerosity representations are “native” to that region, as evidenced by neural tuning to numerosities in non-human animals, human adults, and children as young as three years old (Kersey and Cantlon, 2017a, Nieder, 2016, Piazza et al., 2004). In young children who are in the early stages of symbolic number acquisition, the IPS is the primary brain region that shows evidence of numerosity tuning. A causal role for the IPS in numerical and mathematical processing is suggested by data showing that patients with damage to IPS exhibit impaired calculation (e.g., Dehaene and Cohen, 1997; Takayama et al., 1994), data showing that children with dyscalculia have differential engagement and anatomy of IPS (e.g., Molko et al., 2003; Moscoso et al., 2025; Price et al., 2007), and data showing that transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to parietal cortex induces numerical processing deficits similar to dyscalculia (Cohen Kadosh et al., 2007).

There is also some evidence that the prefrontal cortex is involved in the acquisition of numerical symbols. For one, human and nonhuman primates show evidence of primitive representations of numerosities in this region (Jacob and Nieder, 2009, Viswanathan and Nieder, 2013), though in humans prefrontal regions do not consistently exhibit neural tuning to numerosity (Kersey and Cantlon, 2017a, Piazza et al., 2004). In nonhuman primates who have been trained to recognize numerical symbols, it is primarily prefrontal neurons, and to a lesser degree parietal neurons, that associate visual symbols with numerical values (Diester and Nieder, 2007, Nieder, 2009). In humans, neuroimaging studies reveal that older children who know how to count show stronger recruitment of prefrontal cortex for numerical symbols compared to adults (Ansari et al., 2005; Cantlon et al., 2009a; Rivera et al., 2005), though both the IPS and the prefrontal cortex respond selectively to numerical stimuli in children and adults (Arsalidou et al., 2017, Arsalidou and Taylor, 2011, Kersey and Cantlon, 2017b).

The IPS and prefrontal cortex represent both numerosities and symbolic numbers in proficient counters (Cantlon et al., 2009a; Holloway and Ansari, 2010; Kersey and Cantlon, 2017b; Lussier and Cantlon, 2017; Piazza et al., 2007). However, it is unclear whether children’s early acquisition of number symbols/words involves non-symbolic numerosity representations. One possibility is that early number word semantics are related to numerosity representations in the IPS and/or prefrontal cortex (Bugden et al., 2021). If this is the case, then even in very young children, sequences of number words should engage regions involved in the processing of numerosity. Alternatively, another possibility is that early number word semantics are primarily related to linguistic representations, and do not involve numerosity representations in IPS and prefrontal cortex. If this is the case, then sequences of number words should not activate regions that represent numerosity, and instead, should activate regions involved in sequence or linguistic processing more generally, such as the temporal lobe.

In the current study we evaluated the hypothesis that symbolic number representations involve primitive numerosity representations by testing 3- to 5-year-olds for neural overlap between neural representations of number word sequences (versus alphabet sequences), identified using a naturalistic listening task which required listening to number word sequences, and neural representations of numerosities, identified using a Numerosity Discrimination Task within the same children.

Methods

Participants

68 typically developing children (3.06 – 5.96 years) and 20 adults (20.2 – 31.6 years, mean age = 23.9 years, 10 women) participated in this study. All participants had normal or corrected to normal vision and no history of neurological impairments. Adult participants and the parents of all children gave informed written consent in accordance with the University of Rochester’s Research Subjects Review Board. Children were excluded from the analyses for failure to complete any functional scans (n = 5), for excessive motion (> 3 mm of volume-to-volume motion or > 5 mm of overall displacement, n = 19 children on one or more runs of the Numerosity Discrimination Task, n = 15 children from Number Word/Alphabet Task), for inattention or noncompliance during experimental tasks (n = 5 from Number Word/Alphabet Task) or for low accuracy on the Numerosity Discrimination Task (accuracy < 60%, n = 1 run from 5 children, n = 2 runs from 8 children). This resulted in a final sample of 45 children who successfully completed 1 or 2 runs of Numerosity Discrimination Task (19 girls, mean age = 4.7; n = 13 with 1 run, n = 32 with 2 runs), and 43 children who successfully completed the Number Word/Alphabet Task (23 girls, mean age = 4.6). The maximum amount of motion within a run in the data from the included children was less than 3 mm (average motion = 0.33 mm; range of average motion = 0.02– 2.60 mm; see Preprocessing for motion correction methods). fMRI session

Prior to scanning, children were familiarized with the MRI environment, practiced lying still in a mock scanner, and practiced the Numerosity Discrimination Task. Following the 30-min practice session, children proceeded to the actual MR scanner where their heads were secured with headphones, foam padding, and medical tape. Adults simply received verbal instructions about the tasks.

Stimuli were presented using MATLAB R2014B and the Psychophysics toolbox.

Number word/alphabet task

In the Number Word/Alphabet Task, child and adult participants were instructed to listen to pre-recorded audio tracks of a person saying number words or the alphabet (in order). Sequences were presented in 70-second blocks of 60 items presented at a rate of 1 item every 1.1–1.2 s. Fifteen blocks were presented throughout the experimental paradigm and were separated by 4-seconds of black screen. To keep the child participants engaged in the Number Word/Alphabet Task, visual clips from child-friendly cartoons were presented on the screen. The visual clips were identical for the number word and alphabet conditions. The original audio tracks from the cartoons were replaced with quieter, child-friendly instrumental music, which was matched across both the number word and alphabet sequences. Spoken word sequences (i.e., number words or alphabet) were layered on top of the music at a louder volume and were the prominent audio track. Thus, the main difference between the number word and alphabet conditions was the words in the sequences. Each scan began and ended with 12 s of black screen resulting in a total scan time of 18.9 min (567 volumes at TR = 2 s).

Each spoken sequence block included 60 words. The number word sequences were divided into small, medium, and large number ranges, with each set containing an equal number of spoken items. The small sequences (1−10) and medium sequences (11−20) both included 10 numbers, repeated 6 times. The large sequences covered broader number ranges (51–80, 61–90, and 71–100), repeated twice per block for a total of 60 items. Alphabet sequences also included 60 items and consisted of A to J (6 times) and A to Z (repeated up to 60 items). Sequence lengths and types were shuffled randomly across the session.

Numerosity discrimination paradigm

To localize the numerosity processing network, children and adults completed a numerosity discrimination paradigm. Two arrays of cookies were presented on either side of a fixation cross. Participants were instructed to press a button on the side with the most cookies. Comparisons were made across an easy ratio (0.25) or a difficult ratio (0.60). One run consisted of 16 blocks of 3 trials for a total of 48 trials. Trial order varied across participants. Arrays were presented for 2 s with a 2-second inter-trial interval. Each block ended with an additional 6 s of black screen time. The experiment began with 12-seconds of black screen, followed by another 2-seconds of a fixation cross, and ended with another 12-seconds of black screen. Total time for one run was 4.6 min (139 volumes at TR = 2 s).

Cookie arrays were presented on a black background in the center of the screen to the left and the right of a central fixation cross. Arrays consisted of 1–30 cookies. The correct answer (larger array) was presented equally on the left and right sides. To control for non-numerical factors, on half of the trials the size of the cookies was matched across arrays (491 pixels2) and on the other half, cumulative surface area of the cookies was matched (14726 px2). In addition, on half of the trials, the density of the array was larger for the correct answer, and on the other half, the density of the array was smaller for the correct answer.

MRI parameters

Whole-brain BOLD imaging was conducted on a 3-Tesla Siemens MAGNETOM Trio scanner with a 12-channel head coil at the Rochester Center for Brain Imaging. High-resolution structural T1 contrast images were acquired using a magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE) pulse sequence at the start of each session [TR = 2530 ms, TE = 3.44 ms flip angle = 7 °, FOV = 256 mm, matrix = 256 × 256, 192 or 176 (depending on head size) 1 × 1×1 mm sagittal left-to-right slices].

An echo-planar imaging pulse sequence was used for T2* contrast (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90 °, FOV = 256 mm, matrix 64 ×64, 30 axial oblique slices, parallel to the AC-PC plane, voxel size = 4 ×4×4 mm). For children, an additional sequence was simultaneously acquired with online, retrospective motion correction. Inclusion of data from children was determined by movement measured from the raw, uncorrected sequences, and analyses were conducted on the sequences acquired with online motion correction.

Cognitive assessments

After the primary imaging session, children completed a behavioral session to evaluate number and letter skills. Children’s number skills were evaluated using the Test of Early Mathematics Ability Third Edition (TEMA-3, Ginsburg and Baroody, 2003). We evaluated children’s letter skills using the Kaufman Test of Early Achievement, Letter and Word Recognition Subtest, which tests children’s knowledge of letter names and sounds (K-TEA; Kaufman and Kaufman, 2004).

Preprocessing

fMRI data were analyzed using BrainVoyager (Goebel et al., 2006) and in-house scripts drawing on the BVQX toolbox (http://support.brainvoyager.com/available-tools/52). The first 2 volumes of functional data in each run were discarded prior to analysis. Preprocessing consisted of slice scan time correction (cubic spline interpolation), motion correction with respect to the first volume in the first run, and linear trend removal in the temporal domain (cutoff: 2 cycles within the run). Functional data were registered to high-resolution anatomy on a participant-by-participant basis in native space. Echo-planar and anatomical volumes were then transformed into Talairach space (Talairach and Tournoux, 1988). Data were normalized into Talairach space by first aligning images with the stereotactic axes and then transforming them to the Talairach grid using a piecewise affine transformation based on manual identification of anatomical landmarks. Analyses were performed on preprocessed data in Talairach space. A Gaussian spatial filter was applied to each volume of functional data at 1.5 voxels (6 mm) FWHM. Average framewise displacement (FD, [Grill-Spector et al., 2008; Power et al., 2012]) was regressed for each child participant using their volume-by-volume realignment parameters. This controls for any increases in signal intensity due to volume-to-volume changes in motion.

Analyses of fMRI data: general linear models

Random effect analyses were used to analyze the group data. Functional data were analyzed using the general linear model. Experimental events were convolved with a standard dual gamma hemodynamic response function. All models included 6 regressors of no interest that corresponded to the motion parameters obtained during preprocessing, and the numerosity discrimination data included an additional regressor of no interest that corresponded to the button presses. The numerosity discrimination data were first modeled with one regressor corresponding to the blocks of numerosity discrimination trials, to observe the effect of numerosity discrimination compared to baseline, and were then modeled with two regressors of interest corresponding to the easy (0.25-ratio) and difficult (0.6-ratio) mini-blocks, to observe the signature of numerosity discrimination, ratio effects.

The Number Word/Alphabet Task data were modeled with 4 regressors of interest, including the main effect of number word sequences, main effect of alphabet sequences, and a parametric regressor for each sequence type to represent the ordinal positions of items in their sequences. The parametric regressor for number word sequences modeled each number word with a weight equivalent to its position in the memorized count list (e.g., “five” was given a weight of 5, “twenty” was given a weight of 20). Similarly, the parametric regressor for the alphabet modeled the position of the letter in the memorized sequence (e.g., “C” was given a weight of 3 and “Y” was given a weight of 25). The parametric regressor allows us to test where in the brain neural activity is modulated by the ordinal positions of items in sequences.

Analyses of fMRI data: intersubject correlations

Intersubject correlations (Hasson et al., 2004) were calculated across children, and between children and adults using in-house Matlab scripts. Intersubject correlations were performed by correlating the preprocessed neural timecourses between subjects. For each voxel, each child’s functional data was correlated with that of each child or each adult to produce an r-map for each child representing the timecourse similarity at each voxel to that of each other child or to each adult. Each r-map was then Fischer-z transformed and averaged to produce single maps of how similar each child was to other children or to adults. For correlations from children to each adult, this image, which can be thought of as how “adult-like” each child’s functional timecourse appears, is referred to as a map of “child-adult synchrony.” Whole-brain correlations between children’s child-adult synchrony and their number skills (TEMA-3) were calculated using custom scripts that draw on BVQX toolbox in MATLAB (Goebel et al., 2006). These intersubject correlation maps were previously analyzed as part of a larger study on gender differences and similarities in numerical processing in early childhood (Kersey et al., 2019).

Analyses of fMRI data: thresholding

For all whole-brain analyses, results were considered significant at voxel-wise p < 0.01 and cluster corrected to a family-wise p < 0.05 using the BrainVoyager statistical cluster threshold estimator plugin in order to account for multiple comparisons. Minimum t-values and r-values for each analysis are listed with the degrees of freedom in the corresponding figures.

Overall activation to number words and alphabet

Mean levels of activation were similar across number word and alphabet sequences in children and overlapped in visual and auditory cortices (see Fig. 1; t(42) ≥ 2.70, voxel-wise p < 0.01, cluster-corrected to p < 0.05). Ordinal representations of number words and alphabet

A whole-brain parametric regression identified regions in which neural activity was modulated by the ordinal positions of items in the number word and alphabet sequences (Fig. 2). Using a parametric regressor, each number word and each letter in the alphabet was weighted according to its position in the memorized sequence, and we tested where in the brain the parametric number word and alphabet regressors significantly predicted neural activity. The results showed that ordinal representations for both number word and alphabet sequences were present in bilateral inferior frontal gyri (IFG) and bilateral anterior temporal lobes (ATL). These areas, often implicated in language and sequence processing (Humphries et al., 2001, Uddén and Bahlmann, 2012), were sensitive to the ordinal structure of both sequence types, suggesting that they may support general cognitive mechanisms for processing well-learned, ordered lists, such as numbers and letters (t(42) ≥ 2.70, voxel-wise p < 0.01, cluster-corrected to p < 0.05, cluster thresholds = 102 voxels for number word sequences, 75 voxels for alphabet sequences).Fig. 2Children’s neural responses while listening to number word sequences (blue) and alphabet sequences (red). For number word sequences, the left and right IPS exhibited significant sensitivity to the ordinal positions of numbers, but not to alphabet sequences. Overall intersubject synchrony for number words and alphabet

We measured intersubject synchrony by calculating the intersubject correlation (ISC) for children’s neural activity separately during number word and alphabet sequences. Specifically, for each participant, we calculated their mean voxel-by-voxel correlation with other participants to determine which regions’ activity were significantly correlated across children. Then, we conducted a two-sample t-test between children’s ISC maps during number word sequences and their ISC maps during alphabet sequences, where positive values reflect greater correlation across children during number word sequences and negative values reflect greater correlation across children during alphabet sequences. Neural processing of numerosity

We tested for a main effect of numerical ratio in a 2 (age-group: children, adults) x 2 (ratio: difficult – 0.6 ratio, easy – 0.25 ratio) ANOVA on neural amplitudes from the numerosity discrimination task. A main effect of ratio (stronger activation for difficult trials) emerged in bilateral IPS, bilateral IFG, ACC, and bilateral occipital cortex (t(67) > 2.65, voxel-wise p < 0.01, cluster-corrected to p < 0.05, cluster threshold = 91 voxels). To examine whether individual differences in behavioral performance were related to neural ratio effects, we extracted parameter estimates from those IPS clusters identified in the whole-brain ratio effect analysis. In children, the neural ratio effect was related to task performance. The neural ratio effect in the IPS was positively correlated with children’s response times (RT ratio effect: RT for difficult trials – RT for easy trials; neural ratio effect: beta for difficult ratios – beta for easy ratios; Fig. 5B; right IPS: r(42) = 0.34, t(42) = 2.35, p = 0.024; left IPS: r(42) = 0.40, t(42) = 2.63, p = 0.012). Accuracy showed a similar pattern but a marginal effect (Accuracy ratio effect: accuracy for easy trials – accuracy for difficult trials; Fig. 5B; right IPS: r(43) = -0.19, t(43) = -1.26, p = 0.215; left IPS: r(43) = -0.28, t(43) = -1.95, p = 0.058). A similar relation between neural activity and performance emerged in the inferior frontal regions of the numerosity network (Response Time: left IFG: r(42) = 0.38, t(42) = 2.63, p = 0.012; right IFG: r(42) = 0.42, t(43) = 3.02, p = 0.004; ACC: r(42) = 0.18, t(42) = 1.20, p = 0.239; Accuracy: left IFG: r(43) = -0.32, t(43) = -2.20, p = 0.033; right IFG: r(43) = -0.26, t(43) = -1.80, p = 0.079; ACC: r(43) = 0.02, t(43) = 0.15, p = 0.878). This shows that behavioral performance on the numerosity discrimination task also relates to neural activity in the bilateral IFG. In sum, children and adults engage canonical number processing regions during numerosity comparisons.

Neural overlap between children’s numerosity and number word tasks

Finally, we compared children’s neural activity from the Numerosity Task to children’s neural activity from the Number Word/Alphabet Task. Specifically, we computed the conjunction (threshold t = 3) of the maps for children’s comparison between the Numerosity Task (Easy + Hard blocks > Baseline) and for the parametric effect of the Number Word blocks of the Number Word / Alphabet task (i.e., a map of regions where activity significantly scaled with the numerical value of the number words). This analysis yielded 615 significant voxels, comprised of 4 clusters: the anterior cingulate cortex, left inferior frontal gyrus, and left and right intraparietal sulci. That these tasks show significant overlap, and do so in regions commonly associated with numerical cognition, suggests that number words recruit numerosity-related cortex in young children, at very early stages of learning to count. Discussion

The present study investigated how 3- to 5-year-old children's brains process number word and alphabet sequences, and how these processes relate to their developing numerical skills. Number word and alphabet sequences showed ordinal representations in bilateral inferior frontal gyri and anterior temporal lobes, regions commonly implicated in language and sequence processing (Humphries et al., 2001, Price and Ansari, 2011, Uddén and Bahlmann, 2012, Zaleznik and Park, 2021). These areas may support general cognitive mechanisms for processing ordered lists regardless of whether they are numerical or linguistic in nature. However, the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), a region known for its role in numerical cognition, selectively processed the ordinal properties of number word sequences, but not alphabet sequences despite comparable auditory, sequential, familiarity, and attentional demands. In contrast, alphabet sequences uniquely engaged the right prefrontal cortex and the right angular gyrus, consistent with prior studies (Seghier, 2013), and suggesting distinct neural mechanisms for processing alphabetic versus numeric sequences in childhood. Crucially, we found that children's number skills, measured by the TEMA-3 mathematics assessment, were significantly correlated with the neural maturity of number word sequence processing in the left IPS. This relationship persisted even when controlling for letter skills, reinforcing the importance of the IPS in the development of numerical abilities. Finally, we observed significant overlap in neural activation between the Number Word/Alphabet Task and a separate Numerosity Task in both the IPS and IFG, suggesting shared neural loci for number word and numerosity processing. These findings support our hypothesis that symbolic number words and nonsymbolic quantity processing recruit overlapping parietal circuitry early in development.

These results place number word processing within a broader developmental framework in which symbolic numerical representations gradually become integrated with parietal quantity-processing systems (Kersey and Cantlon, 2017a, Kersey and Cantlon, 2017b). Our results demonstrate concurrent engagement of these regions for numerosities and number words in early childhood but do not by themselves adjudicate whether numerosity representations scaffold number word acquisition, whether symbolic number knowledge influences perceptual numerosity processing, or whether both emerge through coordinated development within a broader representational system. Importantly, however, these findings are situated within a broader developmental literature showing that numerosity sensitivity in the IPS emerges well before formal number word acquisition (Hyde et al., 2010, Libertus et al., 2009, Kersey and Cantlon, 2017a) and that homologous IPS regions represent numerosity in other primates (Kersey and Cantlon, 2017b, Nieder and Miller, 2004). Together with our results, this body of work substantially constrains plausible developmental trajectories and suggests that number word representations become integrated with an already functional quantity-processing system in the IPS.

Previous work demonstrated that numerosity representations and symbolic number word representations are linked by 8 years of age (Lussier and Cantlon, 2017), and that number words engage the IPS in school-age children (Bugden et al., 2021). Across studies, the IPS, the IFG, and the fronto-temporal language network have been identified as candidates for number word acquisition (Ansari et al., 2005; Cantlon et al., 2009a; Diester and Nieder, 2007; Lussier and Cantlon, 2017; Lyons and Ansari, 2009; Nieder, 2009; Rivera et al., 2005; Wilkey and Ansari, 2020). However, because previous studies only tested older children with extensive experience using number words, the developmental timing with which these systems become engaged has remained unclear (Bugden et al., 2021). Here, evidence that even 3- to 5-year-old children’s representations of number words evoked activation in the IPS, the IFG, and the ATL suggests that symbolic numerical representations emerge through early integration with an established system rather than only after prolonged mathematical learning.

The IFG and IPS regions are important because they overlapped in part with children’s activation to numerosities. Furthermore, there was evidence of overlap despite differences in the tasks and demands. In the Number Word/Alphabet Task, the numeric information was presented auditorily, and the task was passive listening. In contrast, in the Numerosity Task, numeric information was presented visually, and the task was active perceptual discrimination. Neural overlap thus emerged despite substantial differences in modality and task demands. Convergent recruitment of IPS and IFG across these very different tasks strengthens the interpretation that these regions participate in number-relevant processing rather than more general demands. These findings lend support to the hypothesis that the IPS plays a developmental role in number word semantics.

Some regions involved in numerosity processing did not overlap number word processing in children. For example, we observed activation in lateral occipital cortex during numerosity discrimination but not number word processing. Recent evidence suggests that numerosity information is represented not only in parietal regions but also along the ventral visual stream (Karami et al., 2025). This occipital activation may reflect early visual encoding of numerical quantity but, in light of our results, perhaps not broader number semantics in children.

Math skills were systematically related to neural activity in the left IPS. Children with stronger numerical concepts showed more adult-like patterns of number word sequence processing, even after controlling for letter skills. These findings suggest the IPS is involved in early representations of number words with the right IPS playing a strong role throughout development while the left IPS matures gradually in its relation to precise meanings of number words. This interpretation is consistent with previous work that shows early maturation of the right IPS for numerical processing and developmental change in the left IPS related to symbolic mathematics development (Ansari et al., 2006, Bugden et al., 2012, Cantlon et al., 2006, Cantlon and Li, 2013, Emerson and Cantlon, 2014, Holloway and Ansari, 2010, Hyde et al., 2010, Kersey and Cantlon, 2017a, Rosenberg-Lee et al., 2011, Vogel et al., 2015). One plausible account is that the left IPS becomes increasingly relevant as number words are integrated with language systems that are themselves left lateralized. Under this view, the left IPS may play a growing role in linking symbolic number representations to precise meanings, whereas the right IPS supports quantity processing more broadly across development (Dehaene et al., 1999). Our findings suggest this left-lateralized symbolic number system begins to emerge early in development, as children integrate linguistic number words with parietal magnitude representations. Overall the findings in the IPS are consistent with theories that symbolic mathematics is grounded in primitive, numerosity representations (e.g., Dehaene and Cohen, 2007) and suggest that the functional overlap seen in older children and adults reflects the origin rather than the outcome of long-term mathematical training (Cantlon et al., 2009a; Holloway and Ansari, 2010; Kersey and Cantlon, 2017a; Lussier and Cantlon, 2017; Piazza et al., 2007).

The IFG and the left ATL also represented number word sequences in children. Like the IPS, the IFG was recruited for both numerosity processing and for representing number word sequences. Unlike the IPS, the IFG also represented alphabet sequences. Representations of alphabet sequences suggest that this region may function in a domain-general manner by representing ordinal information of sequences (Wang et al., 2015). And, according to previous studies, the left ATL may function as part of a lexical-semantic network during the acquisition of word meanings (Binder et al., 2011, Hickok and Poeppel, 2007, Kotz et al., 2002).

The dissociation between number sequences and alphabet sequences in the IPS, and the specific relation between IPS activity and number skills controlling for letter skills indicates that the IPS has some degree of functional specificity for ‘number’ in early childhood. Alphabet sequences served as a closely matched control for modality, sequential, familiarity, and task demands. Our supplementary analyses further confirmed that IPS sensitivity to number words was not driven by differences in syllable count between the counting and alphabet sequences (see Figure S1). Future studies could test for other structural differences between sequences such as phoneme and morpheme composition.

The possibility remains that the IPS responds more generally to sequences beyond number words during development, just not the alphabet. Previous work suggests that parietal cortex represents patterns, sequences, and other ordinal information (Fias et al., 2007, Franklin and Jonides, 2009, Marshuetz et al., 2006, Matejko et al., 2019, Pariyadath et al., 2012, Turconi and Seron, 2002, Wang et al., 2015). For example, Pariyadath and colleagues found that the right parietal cortex exhibited significantly greater activation to ordered sequences of months or days (e.g., “Monday Tuesday Wednesday”), than to non-ordinal sequences (e.g., “Pear Peach Banana”). Future work could investigate whether the IPS is involved in sequence representations beyond counting sequences in children.

Finally, our intersubject correlation analyses converge with the other measures reported here to establish reliable and shared neural dynamics during number word processing. Within the same sample, intersubject synchrony in the IPS aligns with parametric sensitivity to number word ordinality, overlap with numerosity-related activation, and systematic relations to individual differences in children’s counting skills. Notably, this relation persisted after controlling for children’s letter skills, arguing against an interpretation of intersubject correlations based on nonspecific factors or noise. We have previously showed that individual variability in intersubject correlation is related to mathematics development in young children (Cantlon and Li, 2013, Kersey et al., 2019). Longitudinal, within-subject designs could extend this work by tracking how IPS synchrony evolves as children’s brains mature to identify precise functional causes of developmental change. Additional methods such as multivariate approaches or cross-decoding could inform whether number words and numerosities evoke shared representational codes or within these regions or instead recruit overlapping circuitry for distinct but related computations

Together, these findings show that even at the early stages of learning to count, children recruit numerosity-sensitive parietal systems when processing number words. Functional overlap between perceptual numerosity representations and symbolic number word representations in the IPS in the youngest counters (3- to 5-year-olds) is consistent with theoretical accounts of cultural recycling (e.g., Dehaene and Cohen, 2007) and symbol grounding (e.g., Barsalou, 1999) in the developing brain, which predict that the acquisition of formal mathematics builds on evolutionarily-primitive, perceptual numerosity representation in the IPS. These data provide a novel early childhood demonstration of integration between evolutionarily recent language systems and evolutionarily primitive perceptual systems in the representation of number words.
