Apoptotic BMSCs reduce grafted islets apoptosis through the holo-Lcn2/Slc22a17/Fe3+ axis
Authors: Cuinan Lu, Jiale Wang, Ying Wang, Jingwen Wang, Huanjing Bi, Xiaoyang Yu, Zuhan Chen, Boqing Dong, Ruiyang Ma, Xiaoming Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly apoptosis of grafted islets is one of the critical challenges that significantly impact the efficacy of islet transplantation. We employed Staurosporine to pre-induce apoptosis in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). The conditioned medium from apoptotic BMSCs was then used to pretreat β cells, which notably enhanced the suppression of β cell apoptosis. For in vivo experiments, co-transplantation of islets and apoptotic BMSCs under the renal capsule of diabetic rats inhibited islets apoptosis and resulted in better transplantation outcomes. Subsequently proteomic analysis revealed that the iron-loaded form of Lcn2 protein (holo-Lcn2) secreted by apoptotic BMSCs played a crucial role in exerting anti-apoptotic effects. Holo-Lcn2 binds to the Slc22a17 transporter on cell membrane, facilitating the transport of Fe3+ into cells. Inhibition of Fe3+ transport suppressed the anti-apoptotic effect of holo-Lcn2. Thus, we hypothesize that apoptotic BMSCs reduce grafted islets apoptosis through the holo-Lcn2/Slc22a17/Fe3+ axis. This study provides insights into the application of BMSCs-based acellular therapies in islet transplantation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05001-9.Keywords: T1DM, Islet transplantation, Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, holo-Lcn2, Slc22a17, Apoptosis

Introduction

Currently, the incidence and prevalence of type 1 diabetes are rising globally. The underlying cause is the autoimmune-mediated destruction of β cells, leading to an absolute lack of insulin [1, 2]. Insulin replacement therapy is the primary treatment for type 1 diabetes (T1DM), with the most recognized method being a multi-dose insulin regimen that simulates physiological insulin release [1, 3]. Ideally, patients should adjust their insulin intake based on carbohydrate consumption, but real-world insulin regimens are often challenging [1]. Islet transplantation helps diabetic patients restore normal islet function, thereby physiologically secreting insulin and glucagon, achieving long-term stable control of blood glucose and preventing the progression of complications. As a result, islet transplantation is regarded as one of the most effective treatments for insulin-dependent diabetes [4]. However, once the isolated and purified islets are transplanted, they inevitably face challenges such as hypoxia [5–8], IBMIR [7, 9], recurrent autoimmune reaction, and allograft immune rejection [9–11]. These factors contribute to islets apoptosis, which is considered the primary form of programmed cell death leading to islet loss. [6, 12] Thus, there is an urgent need for effective therapies to prevent grafts apoptosis and enhance grafts function.

Research indicates that MSCs from various sources can reduce islets apoptosis and improve the survival and function of grafted islets through several approaches, including co-transplantation or co-culture with MSCs [13–15], preconditioning islets with MSC-derived extracellular matrix (ECM) or active components in conditioned medium(CM) [16–20], and intravenous MSC infusion [21]. MSCs and their secreted active components reduce early grafts apoptosis by improving the microenvironment following islet transplantation, promoting angiogenesis, and alleviating hypoxia and inflammatory stress [22].

In recent years, numerous studies have revealed that preconditioning MSCs can enhance their protective functions, such as through chemical agents, hypoxia, and genetic modifications [23–28], particularly through damage pre-treatment [29, 30]. Apoptotic MSCs have been shown to promote the macrophage polarization to the anti-inflammatory/immunoregulatory M2 phenotype, inhibit dendritic cells (DCs) maturation, and upregulate regulatory T cells (Tregs) and regulatory B cells (Bregs) [31]. Apoptotic MSCs may also secrete more damage-protective factors in a damaged environment, thereby exerting superior therapeutic effects compared to viable MSCs in various disease models [32].

The use of apoptotic MSCs in disease treatment has gained recognition, but their biological role in islet transplantation remains unclear. In this study, we induced apoptosis in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) using Staurosporine (STS) and demonstrated that apoptotic BMSCs (BMSCs-STS) were more effective than viable BMSCs in preventing early apoptosis of grafted islets and improving grafts survival and function. Additionally, the conditioned medium of apoptotic BMSCs (BMSCs-STS CM) exhibited anti β cell apoptosis properties in vitro. Moreover, our proteomics results further revealed that the iron-loaded form of Lcn2 protein (holo-Lcn2) may be a key anti-apoptotic component in BMSCs-STS CM. Our further research suggests that holo-Lcn2 protects β cells from apoptosis by binding to the membrane receptor Slc22a17 and facilitating the transport of Fe3+ into the cells. Our study provides a novel approach to enhancing MSCs-based decellularized therapies and highlights holo-Lcn2 as a potential strategy for protecting grafted islets.

Methods

Animals and ethics statement

Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from the experimental animal center of Xi’an Jiaotong University, Xi’an, China, and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Rats were age-matched within experiments. All animal experiments conducted in this study complied with the Xi’an Jiaotong University Committee on Animal Care regulations. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. In this experiment, animals were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and euthanized by carbon dioxide exposure at the endpoint. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration for over 2 min.

Isolation, culture, and identification of islets

Male SD rats weighing 250–350 g were used as pancreatic islet donors. Islet cells were isolated using collagenase P (1 mg/mL, Roche, Switzerland) and purified by histopaque-1083 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) density gradient centrifugation. The islet cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin under standard culture conditions (5% CO2, 37 °C, and 95% humidity) overnight. The primary islets were identified by dithizone (DTZ, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) staining.

Isolation, culture, and identification of BMSCs

BMSCs were extracted from male SD rats weighing 60–80 g using the whole bone marrow adherent method. Briefly, after euthanizing the rats, they were soaked in 75% ethanol for 10 min. Then, the femurs and tibias were flushed with BMSC complete medium to harvest the bone marrow. The bone marrow was washed three times by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min. Afterward, the cells were resuspended in 5 mL of BMSC complete medium and seeded in T25 culture flasks (Corning, USA). The cells were cultured for 48 h under standard culture conditions, and then half of the medium was replaced. After an additional 24 h, the medium was completely replaced. When the cells reached approximately 90% confluence, they were passaged at a ratio of 1:2. In this experiment, BMSCs from passages 3 to 6 (P3-P6) were used.

To identify BMSCs, flow cytometry was used to analyze the surface markers. The cells were stained with FITC-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD29 and CD45, and PE-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD90 and CD11b/c (eBioscience, USA). Isotype IgG antibodies (eBioscience, USA) were used as negative controls. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a NovoCyte flow cytometer, and the results were analyzed using FlowJo software. Next, osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs was induced using the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation kit (HyCyte, China). Subsequently, the cells were assessed by Oil Red O staining (Solarbio, China) for adipogenesis and Alizarin Red staining (Solarbio, China) for osteogenesis.

Induction of BMSCs apoptosis

When the BMSCs reached 90% confluence, the conditioned medium was discarded, BMSCs were treated with 0.2 μM STS (MedChemExpress, China) for 2 h to induce apoptosis, obtaining apoptotic BMSCs (BMSCs-STS). According to the manufacturer's instructions, BMSCs were stained with Annexin V/7-AAD (Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit, Yeasen, China), and the apoptosis level of BMSCs was analyzed by flow cytometry. Each flow cytometry experiment included 10,000 events.

Preparation conditioned medium (CM) of BMSCs and BMSCs-STS

When the BMSCs reached 90% confluence, the conditioned medium was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS (Servicebio, China). The cells were then cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. Afterward, the conditioned medium was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was retained and filtered through a 0.22 µm sterile filter (Millipore, USA) to obtain the BMSCs CM. For the preparation of BMSCs-STS CM, the cells were pretreated with 0.2 μM STS for 2 h before collection, with the remaining steps being the same as for the BMSCs CM collection. Two kinds of collected CM were stored at -80 °C. For experimental applications, the CM was mixed with INS-1 cell culture medium at a ratio of 1:9 (v/v).

Cell culture, treatment, and siRNA transfection

The rat insulinoma cell line (INS-1) was purchased from the Cell Center of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences. The INS-1 cell line was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) under standard conditions. Cells were cultured and passaged in T25 flasks and used for experiments during the exponential growth phase.

In subsequent experiments, streptozotocin (STZ) was dissolved in citrate buffer and diluted to a final concentration of 3 mM in complete cell medium for treatment of INS-1 cells for 24 h. BMSCs CM, BMSCs-STS CM, Lcn2 protein (MedChemExpress, China), Lcn2 protein inhibitor ZINC00784494 (MedChemExpress, China) were dissolved and diluted in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), holo-Lcn2 protein and Fe3+ (Servicebio, China) were added to the complete cell culture medium in subsequent experimental steps.

The Slc22a17-specific siRNA and negative control (nc) siRNA used in the study were purchased from Tsingke Biotechnology Company (China), with the sequences shown in Table S2. When the INS-1 cells reached approximately 70% confluence, transfection was performed. Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Scientific,USA) was mixed with Slc22a17 siRNA or nc siRNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions, then added to RPMI 1640 medium. After 8 h, the medium was replaced with complete cell medium.

Diabetes and islet transplantation model

Male SD rats weighing 100–150 g were used to establish a diabetes model by a single intraperitoneal injection of 55 mg/kg STZ, which induces pancreatic islet cell destruction. Rats with consecutive non-fasting blood glucose measurements greater than 16.7 mM/L for three consecutive times were considered successfully modeled. These rats were used for subsequent experiments 7 days after STZ injection. The diabetic rats were randomly divided into four groups: (a) non-transplantation group; (b) islets transplantation group (Islets); (c) combined transplantation of BMSCs and islets group (Islets + BMSCs); and (d) combined transplantation of BMSCs-STS and islets group (Islets + BMSCs-STS). The number of transplanted BMSCs was 4 × 105. The number of grafted islets was 2000 IEQ.

Primary islets were isolated and cultured overnight, then co-transplanted with BMSCs or BMSCs-STS under the renal capsule of diabetic rats. Non-fasting blood glucose levels and body weights were measured on post operative days (PODs) 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 14. The grafts were removed on POD 7 and 14.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) in vivo

Diabetic rats and transplanted rats were fasted for 6 h, followed by an intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g/kg). Blood glucose levels were measured and recorded through tail vein blood sampling at 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after glucose injection.

Cell viability assay cell apoptosis analysis

The INS-1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well. After 12 h, the cells were treated with a medium containing BMSCs CM or BMSCs-STS CM for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with 3 mM STZ for an additional 24 h. Following the manufacturer's instructions, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was performed by adding CCK-8 solution to each well. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Finally, cell viability was calculated as a relative value compared to the control cells.

Islets were stained with Acridine Orange (AO) and Propidium Iodide (PI) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The viability of islets was assessed by quantifying the proportion of positive cells using the colocalization function of Image J software.

Apoptosis of INS-1 cells was measured using the Annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis Detection Kit (Yeasen, China), and detection was performed by flow cytometry following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each flow cytometry experiment included 10,000 events.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from INS-1 cells and islet cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Pierce, USA). The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Total proteins were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked at room temperature with 5% BSA (Gibco, USA) for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (listed in Table S1). After washing with TBS containing Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated at room temperature for 1 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (listed in Table S1). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence Western blotting detection kit (Tanon, China) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200, China). All results were derived from three independent biological replicates. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and the expression levels of each protein were normalized to GAPDH.

RNA preparation and RT-qPCR

BMSCs, INS-1 cells, and islets were harvested, and total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The total RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcriptase . Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions, using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, China) and the ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time (RT) PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The expression of each gene was normalized to GAPDH, and the relative expression levels of target genes were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Relevant primers were purchased from Tsingke Biotechnology Company, with the primer sequences listed in Table S2.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and analysis of grafted islets

On POD 7 and 14, kidneys containing the grafts were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices. After blocking with 3% BSA at room temperature for 30 min, the primary antibody (listed in Table S1) diluted in PBS was applied to the sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The sections were then incubated with secondary antibody (listed in Table S1) at room temperature for 50 min. Hematoxylin (Servicebio, China) was used to counterstain nuclei. Slides were imaged using a Nikon microscope system, and the area of positively stained cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining and analysis

As described previously, the grafts were stained for Bcl-2 and insulin, with antibodies listed in Table S1. Pancreatic tissues from rats were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h, followed by staining for insulin and Slc22a17, with antibodies listed in Table S1. Images were captured using a Leica DMR HC epifluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems). The area of fluorescence signals from positively stained cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay

TUNEL staining was performed using the Servicebio TUNEL detection kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss (Germany) fluorescence microscope. For quantitative analysis, five randomly selected fields per section were examined. TUNEL-positive cells and total cells in each field were quantified using ImageJ software. The apoptotic rate was subsequently calculated as the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells relative to the total cell count and subjected to statistical analysis.

Intracellular and extracellular iron determination

INS-1 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well into black 96-well plates with clear bottoms. When INS-1 cells reached approximately 70% confluence, they were transfected with Slc22a17 siRNA. After transfection, the cells were treated with medium containing BMSCs CM, BMSCs-STS CM, or other cytokines for 24 h, followed by treatment with 3 mM STZ for an additional 24 h. After the treatments, the cells were stained with the Fe3⁺ dye Calcein AM (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The stained cells were analyzed using a fluorescence microplate reader (BioTek, USA) to measure fluorescence intensity at an excitation wavelength of 480 nm and an emission wavelength of 510 nm. The final fluorescence intensity was calculated as the relative fluorescence intensity compared to control cells.

The concentration of Fe3⁺ in the BMSCs CM and BMSCs-STS CM was determined using ICP-MS (Agilent 7800, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentration of Lcn2 protein in BMSCs CM and BMSCs-STS CM was measured using a rat Lcn2 ELISA kit (Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Generation and purification of recombinant holo-Lcn2

The full-length Lcn2 cDNA was cloned into pGEX-4T-3 vector and expressed in the E. coli strain BL21 (DE3). BL21 (DE3) constitutively expresses the iron-loaded siderophore enterobactin, making the Lcn2 protein derived from this strain in its iron-loaded form (holo-Lcn2). [33, 34]The bacteria were induced with 0.4 mM IPTG, lysed thoroughly, and then centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and loaded onto a purification column. After elution, the holo-Lcn2 protein was obtained. The iron content of the eluted protein solution was quantified using Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS), confirming that the protein was in its iron-loaded form (holo-Lcn2).

Proteomics analysis

Using the method mentioned earlier, BMSCs CM and BMSCs-STS CM (n = 3) were collected. Equal amounts of protein, based on protein concentration, were taken. After digestion into peptides with trypsin (Promega, USA), the peptides were then separated using a NanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics, Germany), followed by mass spectrometry data acquisition on a timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany). Protein identification and quantification were carried out using DIA-NN (version 1.8.1). Proteins with a fold change ≥ 1.5 and a P ≤ 0.05 were considered differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the two CM groups. A heatmap of protein expression was generated using the heatmap package in R software, based on fold change values.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Inc., USA). All data are presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were tested using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s test where appropriate. A P value< 0.05 was considered to indicate significance.

Results

Isolation and characterization of BMSCs, and induction of apoptosis in BMSCs

After successfully isolating BMSCs using the whole bone marrow adherent method, P3-stage BMSCs exhibited a typical spindle-shaped morphology under a light microscope (Fig. S1B). The differentiation potential of BMSCs into osteoblasts and adipocytes was confirmed by Alizarin Red staining and Oil Red O staining, respectively (Fig. S1C, D). Flow cytometry was performed to characterize the surface markers of BMSCs, showing positive expression of CD90 and CD29 and negative expression of CD45 and CD11b/c (Fig. S1E).

To establish an apoptotic BMSCs model, we treated BMSCs with varying concentrations of STS and observed their morphology under a light microscope. As the STS concentration increase, the morphology of BMSCs gradually become more elongated. Notably, when the concentration reached 0.5 μM for 2 h, the cells struggled to maintain their typical spindle-shaped morphology (Fig. 1A). Therefore, we selected 0.2 μM STS for 2 h to treat BMSCs, which resulted in approximately 40% (41.47 ± 3.16%) of the cells exhibiting pre-apoptotic characteristics in vitro (Fig. 1B, C).Furthermore, analysis of the Bcl-2/Bax ratio at the protein and mRNA levels was conducted to evaluate the extent of apoptosis in BMSCs cultured in vitro (Fig. 1D–F).

BMSCs-STS co-transplanted with islet cells improved the therapeutic effects of islet transplantation

MSCs and islet co-transplantation can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of islet transplantation [35–37], and some studies have suggested that apoptotic BMSCs may have better therapeutic effects in other disease models [38–40]. To evaluate the in vivo effect of STS-induced apoptotic BMSCs co-transplanted with islets, and to verify whether BMSCs-STS can improve the survival of grafted islets compared to viable BMSCs, we designed the following experiment (Fig. 2A): Diabetic rats induced by STZ were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5):a non-transplanted group, only islets transplantation group (Islets), a co-transplantation group with islets and BMSCs (Islets + BMSCs), and a co-transplantation group with islets and BMSCs-STS (Islets + BMSCs-STS). The cells in each group were transplanted under the kidney capsule of diabetic rats, and the grafts were harvested for observation on POD 7 and POD 14.

We tested the random blood glucose and body weight of the rats at POD 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 14. The body weights of rats in all transplant groups gradually increased with no significant differences, while the non-transplanted rats showed slow weight gain or even weight loss (Fig. 2B, F). Following transplantation, only the rats in the Islets + BMSCs-STS group exhibited an immediate restoration of blood glucose levels to normal within 24 h, which subsequently stabilized. At this juncture, a significant difference in blood glucose levels was observed compared to the Islets + BMSCs group (P < 0.05). In the Islets + BMSCs group, blood glucose levels returned to normal by POD 3, whereas in the Islets group, normalization of blood glucose levels was achieved around POD 5 (Fig. 2C, G). However, some rats in the Islets group exhibited a rebound in blood glucose levels.

To further assess the function of the grafts, we performed an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) on all four groups of rats at POD 7 and 14. The area under the curve (AUC) of blood glucose levels was significantly reduced in all islet transplantation groups. Among them, the Islets + BMSCs-STS group showed the most significant improvement in glucose tolerance (Fig. 2D, E, H, I). Thus, co-transplantation of islets with BMSCs-STS significantly improves the therapeutic effects of islet transplantation.

BMSCs-STS co-transplanted with islet cells increased grafts survival and reduced grafts apoptosis

In order to further clarify whether BMSCs-STS can reduce early grafts apoptosis and improve grafts survival, we harvested the grafts at POD 7 and 14, then performed insulin immunohistochemical staining as well as double immunofluorescence staining for Bcl-2 and insulin. Insulin immunohistochemical staining revealed that the Islets + BMSCs-STS group had the largest insulin+ area among all transplant groups, indicating higher grafts survival and better insulin secretion function in the surviving grafts (Fig. 3A, B). Dual immunofluorescence staining for Bcl-2 and insulin, which can assess grafts apoptosis, revealed that compared to the Islets and Islets + BMSCs groups, the Islets + BMSCs-STS group had the largest Bcl-2+/Insulin+ staining area, suggesting a significant reduction in apoptosis in the Islets + BMSCs-STS group (Fig. 3C–E).The apoptosis in grafted islets was further assessed by TUNEL staining. Imaging analysis showed concordant results with the Bcl-2+/Insulin+ staining, indicating that the Islets + BMSCs-STS group possessed the lowest apoptotic level (Fig. 3F, G).These results collectively demonstrate that co-transplantation of BMSCs-STS can reduce grafts apoptosis and enhance islet grafts survival.

BMSCs-STS CM protects islets and β cells from cell apoptosis

For the purpose of assessing the anti-apoptotic function of BMSCs-STS in vitro, we collected BMSCs-STS CM and BMSCs CM and pretreated INS-1 cells and islets.

As previous experiments have shown, 3 mM STZ is the best concentration for treating INS-1 cells [28, 41]. Therefore, we first pretreated INS-1 cells with the two types of CM for 24 h, followed by a 24-h treatment with 3 mM STZ. INS-1 cell viability was then assessed using the CCK-8 assay. We found that BMSCs-STS CM pretreatment significantly enhanced the viability of INS-1 cells (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we used Western blotting, RT-qPCR, and flow cytometry to evaluate apoptosis levels in INS-1 cells. Annexin-V-APC/7AAD double staining flow cytometry results showed that, compared to the BMSCs CM-treated cells, the proportion of apoptotic cells in the BMSCs-STS CM-treated group was dramatically reduced (Fig. 4B, C). Furthermore, BMSCs-STS CM pretreatment significantly increased the expression of Bcl-2 protein and decreased the expression of Bax protein (Fig. 4D, E). These results confirm that BMSCs-STS CM protects INS-1 cells from apoptosis in vitro.

Next, we treated islets with the two types of CM for 24 h and collected cell samples. Consistent with the results in INS-1 cells, BMSCs-STS CM pretreatment significantly increased the expression of Bcl-2 protein and decreased the expression of Bax protein (Fig. 4F, G). We also performed AO/PI staining, and observed that both types of CM reduced the proportion of dead cells. However, BMSCs-STS CM notably reduced the intensity of PI staining, indicating better protection for islets (Fig. 4H, I). In summary, BMSCs-STS CM pretreatment can reduce β cell apoptosis in vitro.

Proteomics reveals beneficial protein factors in BMSCs-STS CM

In order to explore the potential mechanisms by which apoptotic BMSCs reduce β cell apoptosis both in vivo and in vitro, we performed proteomic sequencing on the BMSCs-STS CM and BMSCs CM (n = 3). The results revealed a total of 201 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) shared between the two CM groups (listed in Table S3), with 152 proteins upregulated and 49 proteins downregulated (Fig. 5A). Since MSCs primarily exert their biological effects through paracrine mechanisms [22, 42], we further analyzed the 201 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) to specifically focus on those localized extracellularly or identified as secreted proteins, using a threshold of fold change ≥ 1.5 and p ≤ 0.05 (Fig. 5B, C).We then searched for anti-apoptotic proteins in the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org) (listed in Table S4), and intersected them with the secreted proteins among the DEPs, resulting in a final set of 11 proteins (Fig. 5D). These proteins were: Slpi, Lcn2, Tnfrsf1b, Timp1, IL6, Ngf, Adm, CCL20, Osm, IL11, and Vcan.

Among them, Lcn2 exhibited the largest differential fold change. We pre-treated INS-1 cells with recombinant Lcn2 protein at concentrations of 1 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL, 100 ng/mL, and 500 ng/mL, followed by induction with 3 mM STZ. The activity of INS-1 cells was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Unfortunately, our experimental results indicated that pre-treatment with recombinant Lcn2 protein did not alleviate INS-1 cell apoptosis or enhance their activity (Fig. S2A). Additionally, through the literature review, we identified other secretory proteins with high differential fold changes that are known to protect cells from apoptosis, such as Lif and Psap [43–46]. However, pre-treatment with their recombinant proteins did not exhibit protective effects on INS-1 cells (Fig. S2B, C).

Interestingly, we found that previous studies have reported that iron-loaded Lcn2 (holo-Lcn2) could protect against apoptosis and ferroptosis in HeLa cells, primary murine tubular epithelial cells, and renal tumor tubular epithelial cells [47–49]. However, it remains unknown whether holo-Lcn2 can inhibit β cell apoptosis.

Holo-Lcn2 pre-treatment can inhibit apoptosis and ferroptosis in β cells

To verify whether holo-Lcn2 is an effective anti-apoptotic component in BMSCs-STS CM, we first measured the Lcn2 protein levels in both CM using an ELISA assay, and observed a significant increase in Lcn2 protein content in BMSCs-STS CM (Fig. 5E). Next, we used RT-qPCR to confirm that the mRNA expression of Lcn2 in BMSCs treated with 0.2 μM STS was higher than that in untreated BMSCs (Fig. 5G), indicating that STS treatment promotes Lcn2 secretion in BMSCs. Additionally, we measured the total iron levels in both CM using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and found that the total iron levels in BMSCs-STS CM were significantly higher than in BMSCs CM (Fig. 5F).

Next, we used the E. coli strain BL21 (DE3) to express and purify holo-Lcn2. INS-1 cells were then treated with 5 ng/mL of apo-Lcn2, holo-Lcn2, or Fe3+ (at the same molar concentration) for 24 h, followed by treatment with 3 mM STZ. Apoptosis levels in INS-1 cells were assessed using Western blotting and RT-qPCR. We observed that holo-Lcn2 increased the Bcl-2/Bax protein and mRNA ratios in INS-1 cells, thereby inhibiting cell apoptosis. However, Fe3+ and apo-Lcn2 treatments did not result in reduced apoptosis levels (Fig. 6A–C). These results indicate that only the iron-loaded form of Lcn2 (holo-Lcn2) can inhibit apoptosis in INS-1 cells.

Further, we used an Lcn2 protein inhibitor, ZINC00784494, which antagonizes Lcn2 by directly binding to the protein [50]. After adding the inhibitor to the BMSCs-STS CM and then pre-treating INS-1 cells, we assessed apoptosis again using Western blotting and RT-qPCR. We observed that the anti-apoptotic effect of the CM was antagonized upon the addition of ZINC00784494 (Fig. 6D–F).

Considering the addition of the iron-loaded form of the protein, and given that the above results demonstrate holo-Lcn2 alleviates INS-1 cell apoptosis, it raised the question of whether it might induce other forms of cell death, such as ferroptosis. To investigate this, we conducted Western blotting and RT-qPCR to examine ferroptosis-related markers, and found that treatment with 5 ng/mL holo-Lcn2 did not induce ferroptosis but rather alleviated ferroptosis levels in the cells (Fig. 6G–I).

To summarize, we have demonstrated that holo-Lcn2 is a key anti-apoptotic factor in BMSCs-STS CM. Pre-treatment with holo-Lcn2 can inhibit apoptosis in β cells and protect against ferroptosis-induced damage.

holo-Lcn2 inhibits apoptosis in β cells by transferring Fe.3+ into the cells via Slc22a17

With the aim of determining how holo-Lcn2 inhibits apoptosis in INS-1 cells, we used the STRING database to predict potential interacting proteins with Lcn2. We found that Slc22a17, as the membrane receptor of Lcn2, can bind to holo-Lcn2, internalize it into the cells, and simultaneously transfer Fe3+ into the cells, thereby inhibiting apoptosis. Based on this, we further predicted the underlying mechanisms and illustrated them in a schematic diagram. (Fig. Graphical Abstract).In summary, holo-Lcn2 inhibits apoptosis by increasing intracellular Fe3+ levels through the transfer of Fe3+ into the cells.

There is no previous literature reporting on the expression of Slc22a17 in islets or INS-1 cells. Therefore, we first extracted pancreatic tissue from rats and performed immunofluorescence staining, finding that insulin-secreting pancreatic β cells also express Slc22a17 (Fig. 7A). We then isolated islets and performed RT-qPCR experiments in both islets and INS-1 cells to verify the mRNA levels of Slc22a17 relative to GAPDH expression (Fig. 7B, C). These experiments confirmed the presence of Slc22a17 expression in β cells.

Next, we used a Fe3+ dye, Calcein-AM, which binds to Fe3+ and results in fluorescence quenching [48]. We stained INS-1 cells pre-treated with two types of CM, holo-Lcn2, apo-Lcn2, and Fe3+, and found that the Fe3+ content in cells treated with BMSCs-STS CM and holo-Lcn2 increased (Fig. 7D, E).

To further investigate the correlation between Fe3+ transfer and the alleviation of apoptosis, we conducted a rescue experiment. We designed three Slc22a17-siRNAs, with siSlc22a17-2 showing the most significant knockdown efficiency (Fig. S2D). We transfected INS-1 cells with siSlc22a17-2, knocked down Slc22a17, followed by pretreatment with holo-Lcn2. We subsequently assessed cell apoptosis levels and Slc22a17 expression. Our findings revealed that the siSlc22a17 group did not show an increase in the Bcl-2/Bax protein and mRNA ratio, failing to reverse the apoptosis in INS-1 cells (Fig. 7F–J), and in the meantime exhibited reduced intracellular the Fe3+ levels (Fig. 7K).

In conclusion, co-transplantation of BMSCs-STS with islets increased grafts survival and reduced grafts apoptosis. And, we demonstrated that only the iron-loaded form of Lcn2 can bind to the transporter protein Slc22a17, then transferring Fe3+ into the cells and reducing apoptosis in β cells.

Discussion

Clinical islet transplantation is considered one of the safest and least invasive transplant procedures, as it can normalize blood glucose control in T1DM patients. [51]However, due to mechanisms such as cell death, inflammatory responses, hypoxia before and after transplantation, host immune rejection, and the application of immunosuppressive treatments to counteract rejection, there is a persistent loss of functional islets [52]. Therefore, it is crucial to protect the grafted islets from the earliest stages of transplantation. MSCs and their derived exosomes have been shown to protect islets during isolation, culture, and co-transplantation [22]. MSC-based therapies have demonstrated promising outcomes in various diseases, with good safety and tolerance in allogeneic MSC transplantation [53, 54], but concerns about the safety of MSC-based treatments persist. The main issues include the potential for unwanted differentiation of transplanted MSCs and the risk of promoting tumor growth and metastasis [55]. Cultured and expanded BMSCs may undergo chromosomal abnormalities, which can lead to the development of malignant tumors in mice [56]. Additionally, injecting human MSCs into tumor-bearing mice has been shown to promote tumor growth and metastasis. [57] Given these risks, further research is needed to explore safer alternatives, such as acellular therapies based on MSCs or other methods to enhance the safety of MSC administration. Currently, the primary strategies for enhancing the safety of MSC-based therapies involve the utilization of MSC-derived EVs (including its exosomes and apoptotic bodies), as well as MSCs CM. Current studies have also indicated that MSCs inevitably undergo apoptosis after infusion into the body. Paola et al. developed a switch (iCasp9/AP1903) to induce MSC apoptosis, and apoptotic MSCs exhibited enhanced suppression of T cell responses and anti-inflammatory effects both in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, due to their shorter survival time in vivo, MSCs exhibit a reduced risk of carcinogenic transformation, thereby enhancing their safety profile. [58] Therefore, we propose that the utilization of apoptotic MSCs may serve as a strategy to enhance the safety of MSC infusion.

Various methods of inducing MSCs apoptosis have been shown to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of stem cells [38, 59]. In fact, MSCs apoptosis is essential for their immunosuppressive functions [40]. STS is an apoptosis inducer. In this study, BMSCs were treated with STS, inducing an apoptosis rate of approximately 40% (41.47 ± 3.16%). These BMSCs-STS were then co-transplanted with 2000 IEQ of islets under the renal capsule of diabetic rats. We observed that in the Islets + BMSCs-STS group, rat blood glucose levels returned to normal within 24 h, with a faster reduction in blood glucose compared to the Islets and Islets + BMSCs groups. Furthermore, co-transplantation of BMSCs-STS with islets reduced grafted islet apoptosis, improved grafts survival, and better preserved grafts function. MSCs can adapt to and respond to environmental cues by releasing growth factors, exosomes, and chemokines, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory, immune-regulatory, and tissue repair effects [60–62]. In the microenvironment, activation of cytotoxic T cells or NK cells, along with inflammatory cytokine stimulation, can induce MSCs apoptosis, transitioning them into an active form capable of exerting biological effects—this process is known as "licensing" [63–66]. We hypothesize that apoptotic BMSCs can protect grafted islets due to STS pretreatment, which can complete "licensing" process in advance, thereby priming BMSCs into an activated and prepared state. This enables them to exert protective effects from the initial stages of transplantation, coinciding with the phase of most severe islet apoptosis. In this experiment, we also observed that some rats in the Islets and Islets + BMSCs groups experienced a mild glucose rebound on POD 14, while no such phenomenon was observed in the Islets + BMSCs-STS group. This may be due to vascular reconstruction, which begins 2–4 days after islet transplantation and is mostly completed by 10–14 days [67]. Therefore, we hypothesize that the rats in the Islets and Islets + BMSCs groups may have failed to achieve effective graft vascularization, whereas the grafts in the Islets + BMSCs-STS group had already established robust vascular networks at this stage. This successful revascularization likely ensured adequate blood perfusion and oxygen delivery to the grafts, thereby preserving their functional integrity and contributing to long-term glycemic stability in the transplanted rats. The dose of BMSCs transplanted was 4 × 105, which is lower than the doses used in previous studies [13, 28, 68]. This suggests that BMSCs-STS is safer and more beneficial than non-treated BMSCs.

We collected CM from BMSCs-STS and BMSCs, which were subsequently used to treat β cells. Compared to BMSCs CM, BMSCs-STS CM provided stronger protection against STZ-induced apoptosis in INS-1 cells and also inhibited apoptosis in primary islets that were cultured for 24 h in vitro. To further understand how BMSCs-STS alleviates apoptosis in grafted islets and improves its survival, we performed proteomic analysis on both BMSCs-STS CM and BMSCs CM. This also aims to provide a theoretical basis for the development of subsequent acellular therapies. Lcn2, also known as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) in humans, is an innate immune protein. It can block bacterial iron uptake, exerting an antibacterial effect, while also modulating iron homeostasis. Lcn2 exists in two forms: iron-loaded (holo-Lcn2) and non-iron-loaded (apo-Lcn2). However, Lcn2 itself does not bind directly to iron; rather, it binds to iron carriers that have a high affinity for Fe3+ but do not bind Fe2+ [69]. Slc22a17, an atypical member of the Slc22 family of organic ion transporters, is a key membrane protein involved in Lcn2 receptor-mediated endocytosis. It can bind both forms of Lcn2 and facilitate Fe3+ transport [70]. Devireddy et al. found that when apo-Lcn2 binds to Slc22a17, it induces intracellular iron depletion, triggering cell apoptosis [48]. Yudan Chi et al. showed that inflammatory factors increase Lcn2 expression in cancer cells, which then compete with macrophages in cerebrospinal fluid to take up iron, ultimately stimulating cancer cell growth [71]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that a similar Lcn2-Slc22a17-mediated iron transport mechanism might exist in grafted islets, affecting cell apoptosis levels. As there are no prior reports on this mechanism in the pancreas or islets, we first demonstrated that Slc22a17 is present on the surface of rat β cells. Additionally, we synthesized holo-Lcn2 in vitro and pretreated INS-1 cells. We observed that pre-treatment rescued STZ-induced apoptosis and increased intracellular Fe3+ levels. However, when Slc22a17 was knocked down, Fe3+ levels did not increase, and the protective effect against apoptosis was lost. Therefore, holo-Lcn2 is an effective anti-apoptotic component in BMSCs-STS CM, and this anti-apoptotic process is Fe3+-dependent.

Interestingly, in some studies, holo-Lcn2 treatment has been shown to promote apoptosis [72, 73]. We speculate that this may be due to differences in the inflammatory state of the disease, baseline levels of Lcn2, and the concentration changes of Fe3+ induced by exogenous holo-Lcn2 [70, 72]. Iron is an essential trace element in the body, and both iron deficiency and overload can lead to programmed cell death. Iron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters are indispensable cofactors involved in many critical cellular functions, particularly in the mitochondrial respiratory chain [74]. Research by Bin Qi et al. shows that the α subunit of mitochondrial ATP synthase (ATP-1) can bind to the iron carrier Ent, promoting an increase in mitochondrial iron levels and further enhancing the activity of aconitase and succinate dehydrogenase (two mitochondrial Fe-S cluster enzymes). This suggests that the Ent-ATP-1 complex plays a role in supplying iron to Fe-S clusters and other iron-containing molecules in the mitochondria [75]. Proper function of the oxidative respiratory chain helps prevent mitochondrial ROS production, thus inhibiting mitochondrial apoptosis. This could explain why Fe3+ transfer suppresses β cell apoptosis, but it remains unclear whether Lcn2 is involved in the process of iron carrier-mediated Fe3+ transfer into the mitochondria.

Overall, our results suggest that co-transplantation of BMSCs-STS with islets improves transplantation outcomes in a diabetic rat renal capsule transplant model and inhibits grafted islets apoptosis. However, this study did not investigate whether BMSCs-STS participate in the chronic rejection response in allogeneic transplantation or provide further insights into its immunosuppressive efficacy. Additionally, the application of BMSCs-STS CM reduced STZ-induced apoptosis in INS-1 cells. Our proteomic analysis further revealed that holo-Lcn2 is an effective anti-apoptotic component in BMSCs-STS CM, and its anti-apoptotic function is Fe3+-dependent. We also confirmed the presence of the Lcn2 membrane receptor Slc22a17 in β cells. Another limitation of this study is that we did not trace the fate of Fe3+ after its transfer into the cytoplasm, so the specific mechanism by which Fe3+ entry inhibits INS-1 cell apoptosis remains unclear. In the future, further exploration of the potential immunomodulatory functions of BMSCs-STS could be pursued, alongside the application of holo-Lcn2 in MSC-based acellular therapies to enhance the clinical efficacy of islet transplantation. In summary, the application of BMSCs-STS and holo-Lcn2 provides valuable insights into reducing islet apoptosis and protecting grafted islets from other adverse effects.
