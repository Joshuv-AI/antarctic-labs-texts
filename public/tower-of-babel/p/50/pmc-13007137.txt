Spatial distribution of HIV prevalence and associated factors in Guinea: retrospective cross-sectional study using Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) data from 2012 and 2018
Authors: Ibrahima Balde, Almamy Amara Toure, Jessica Lee Abbate, Abdoulaye Sow, Mamadou Saliou Sow, Salifou Talassone Bangoura, Castro Gbêmêmali Hounmènou, Sidikiba Sidibé, Alioune Camara, Alexandre Delamou, Cheick Ahmed Ouattara, Sokhna Dieng, Abdoulaye Touré
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesIn Guinea, around 17 new cases of HIV occurred each day and it was responsible for 10 deaths a day in 2022. In addition to this burden, regional disparities have emerged over the years. This study aimed to describe and explain the uneven distribution of HIV infection in Guinea using spatial analysis.DesignThis is a retrospective cross-sectional secondary analysis using data from the 2012 and 2018 Guinea Demographic and Health Survey (DHS).SettingThis study was conducted in Guinea.Participants and methodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of data from 300 and 400 enumeration areas, respectively, included in the 2012 and 2018 DHS Program for participants aged 15 to 49 who underwent HIV testing. Spatial analysis methods, including Moran I, interpolation and Kulldorff’s scan statistic, were applied to examine variation and identify high-risk spatial clusters of HIV prevalence rate. The potential relationship between HIV status and socio-demographic, biological, behavioural and socio-environmental explanatory variables was explored using logistic regression at individual level.ResultsIn total, 7922 individuals in 2012 and 8539 in 2018 participated in the study. HIV prevalence rate in 2012 and 2018 was 1.9% and 1.5%, respectively. Across Guinea’s 33 prefectures, HIV prevalence rate varied from 0% to 3.9% in 2012 and from 0% to 3.5% in 2018. Spatial analysis identified four significant high-risk spatial clusters in 2012 and one high-risk cluster in 2018. The high-risk clusters in 2012 were in Kissidougou (relative risk (RR)=3.97; p value=0.037), Matam (RR=2.80; p value=0.019), Pita (RR=3.46; p value=0.035) and N’zerekore prefectures (RR=6.08; p value=0.027), the high-risk cluster in 2018 was located in Boffa prefecture (RR=3.95; p value=0.022). Factors significantly and positively associated with HIV infection in 2012 included age class 25–34 (aOR: 2.20; 95% CI 1.40 to 3.47), age class 35–49 (aOR: 2.43; 95% CI 1.51 to 3.92), number of HIV healthcare facilities>30 (aOR: 2.14; 95% CI 1.34 to 3.43). HIV infection was significantly lower in men (aOR: 0.52; 95% CI 0.35 to 0.77). In 2018, in addition to age groups 25–34 years (aOR=1.90; 95% CI 1.18 to 3.04) and 35–49 years (aOR=2.25; 95% CI 1.40 to 3.64), the Soussou ethnicity group (aOR=1.73; 95% CI 1.04 to 2.87) was also positively associated with HIV infection.ConclusionThis study describes the spatial distribution of HIV prevalence rate and identified high-risk clusters in Guinea. In addition, risk factors associated with HIV status were identified. The information can help prioritise surveillance and response efforts to control HIV in Guinea.Keywords: Prevalence, Risk Factors, INFECTIOUS DISEASES, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Introduction

Since its discovery in the USA in 1981, HIV infection has remained a major public health problem globally.1 2 In 2022, approximately 39 million people were living with HIV worldwide, with over 67% of them residing in sub-Saharan Africa.3 4 In Guinea, the prevalence of HIV infection has consistently been around 1.5% in the general population.5,8 According to the United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS), in 2022, Guinea reported approximately 17 new HIV cases and 10 deaths daily,9 largely due to insufficient access to prevention, care and treatment services.2 This burden is compounded by territorial disparities, with varying incidence and mortality rates across regions.7

To address these disparities, it is crucial to identify where and why the response to HIV is inadequate.10 Spatial analysis methods are powerful tools11 12 for examining spatial disparities and identifying factors associated with such heterogeneous distribution.13,17 Like many other sub-Saharan African countries, Guinea employs Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program methods to report population health data, including national HIV prevalence estimates.18 Despite this, spatial variation of health problems, including HIV, is rarely examined.18 19 Existing studies have identified sociodemographic, behavioural and biological factors as predictors of HIV prevalence,17 20 21 providing insights into transmission dynamics and planning interventions.1722,24 However, these studies have not fully explored the spatial distribution of environmental features such as the HIV health services, mining sites, sex work and urbanisation, which can influence HIV spread.10 25 26 Hence, there is a need for more information on spatial variation in HIV prevalence.

In recent years, several interventions in Guinea27 28 have focused on HIV prevention, screening and treatment, contributing to a decrease in HIV-related deaths.29 However, the number of new infections has remained stubbornly unchanged or even slightly increased, with significant regional variations.6,829 This is attributed to insufficient access to HIV services, low funding and lack of prioritisation of effective responses,27,29 highlighting the need to understand the factors driving geographic inequalities.

To date, few studies have investigated the spatial distribution of HIV prevalence and associated risk factors in Guinea. Therefore, this study aims to describe the spatial variations in HIV prevalence rate and to identify the risk factors of HIV infection in Guinea through secondary analysis of DHS data from 2012 and 2018.

Methods

Study design

This study is a retrospective observational analysis of HIV prevalence rate among 300 and 400 enumeration areas (EAs) in Guinea using the DHS conducted, respectively, in 2012 and 2018.

Study area

Guinea is a West African country located between 7°30′ and 12°30′ North latitude and 8° and 15° West longitude.30 It shares borders with Guinea Bissau and the Atlantic Ocean to the west, Senegal and Mali to the north, Ivory Coast to the east and Sierra Leone and Liberia to the south.30 The country covers 245 857 km2 in area and has a population of 13 261 638 people,30 with 70% of them residing in rural areas.31 Guinea is organised into eight administrative regions, which are further divided into prefectures, with the exception of Conakry region, which is instead subdivided into urban municipalities31 (table 1).

Participants and sampling

The DHS conducted in Guinea in 2012 and 2018 was conducted jointly by the national authorities and American consulting and technology services company (ICF International), using the standardised methodology of the DHS Program.7 8 They aimed to generate reliable and representative data, both at the prefectural, regional and national levels, as well as at other relevant levels of geographic disaggregation. To this end, the national territory was divided into eight study areas corresponding to the country’s seven administrative regions, plus the capital, Conakry. With the exception of the latter, which does not have a rural area, each area was stratified into two subunits: an urban stratum and a rural stratum. The sampling design was based on a two-stage probability method, stratified by area of residence.7 8 First, primary sampling units were randomly selected from the EAs corresponding to DHS clusters, defined during the last two General Population and Housing Censuses of Guinea.7 8 Second, a sample of households was drawn within each EA, based on updated household lists developed during the census preparatory phase.7 8 In this study, the population included in the analyses consisted of individuals from the sampled household of both sexes, aged 15 to 49, who had consented to an HIV serological test during the 2012 (7922) and 2018 (8539) DHS (as illustrated in figure 2).

Variables

The relevant variables for the analysis were selected from the DHS surveys in 2012 and 2018.

Socio-demographic: Age group, gender, residence, education level, marital status, ethnicity, religion, gender of the household head, age of the household head.

Socio-economic: Household wealth index based on household assets and services, occupation, employment in the last 12 months and access to media (newspaper, radio and television).

Behavioural: Engaging in sexual activity, using a condom during sexual intercourse within the last 12 months, age at the initiation of sexual activity and the number of sexual partners in the last 12 months.

Biological: Include the HIV status, existence of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), genital lesions within the past year and genital discharges within the past year.

Socio-environmental sites: Number of mining companies, number of sex worker sites and number of HIV prevention, care and treatment service sites. These data were obtained from the database of the Mining Promotion and Development Center, Ministry of Mines and Geology (CPDM/MMG) and the annual reports on the response to HIV/AIDS from the National Committee for the Fight against AIDS (CNLS) and the National Program for the Fight against AIDS and Viral Hepatitis (PNLSH).

Comprehensive information regarding these variables can be found in online supplemental table 1, which is accessible online.

Statistical analysis

This study reports descriptive statistics, mapping, spatial analysis and multivariable analysis for understanding the distribution of HIV prevalence rate across different spatial levels and for identifying risk factors for HIV infection in Guinea.

General considerations

In accordance with standard data management practices,34 variables missing responses from less than 5% of participants were retained for analysis, while those exceeding this threshold were excluded. No imputations were carried out.

The statistical analysis accounts for the complex sampling design employed in the DHS, including clustering, stratification and sampling weights to account for sampling design.20 35 36 By incorporating these elements, the method minimises potential biases in SE estimation and ensures more accurate CIs for the OR estimates.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.5.0). Spatial analysis was conducted using SaTScan software (V.10.1.3). For mapping, R statistical programming software (V.4.5.0) and Quantum Geographic Information System software (V.3.42.3) were used.

Descriptive analysis

Frequency tables for qualitative variables and means with SD and quartiles for quantitative variables were used to describe the variables used in analyses. Univariate analyses comparing HIV prevalence with quantitative variables were tested using Wilcoxon’s or t-tests (depending on data normality), while χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests (depending on sample sizes) were used for qualitative variables.

Spatial analysis

HIV prevalence rate

First, according to DHS methodology37 and UNAIDS/WHO guidelines,38 the raw prevalence rate of HIV infection was calculated in each of the 33 prefectures into the eight administrative regions as the ratio of the number of people who tested positive to the total number of people tested. These prevalence rate estimates, expressed as proportions, were then mapped for 2012 and 2018.

To construct these maps, we used the geographic coordinates of survey clusters (EAs) provided in the Global Positioning System (GPS) files from the DHS, which were linked to individual-level data. Official DHS shapefiles were employed to delineate administrative boundaries.

Subsequently, continuous mapping of HIV prevalence at the national level was carried out, without being constrained by administrative boundaries, using Gaussian kernel density estimate with adaptive bandwidths, implemented via the prevR package in R.39 This method enables the estimation of HIV prevalence rate at a subnational level by generating a smoothed surface based on observed data.40 The analysis relied on 300 EAs in 2012 and 400 EAs in 2018. These data points were interpolated to produce continuous HIV prevalence rate surfaces.

Spatial autocorrelation of HIV prevalence rate

Spatial autocorrelation of HIV prevalence rate was analysed using the Moran’s index (Moran I), which measures the spatial similarity between adjacent geographic units.41 This test relies on a neighbourhood matrix defining the spatial relationships between units.16 42 In this study, a k-nearest neighbour matrix was used, identifying, for each unit, its 10 nearest neighbours based on geographic distances.16 A significant Moran I test indicates that there is a presence of spatial autocorrelation.16

High-risk spatial clustering of HIV prevalence rate

Kulldorff’s spatial scan statistic was used to identify spatial clusters with high prevalence rate of HIV using DHS EAs as the spatial unit.16 43 This method pinpoints areas with numbers of HIV cases that differ from a uniform spatial distribution by systematically scanning circular windows of various sizes across the study area.16 Likelihood ratio tests determined the statistical significance of differences between clusters. The null hypothesis of a uniform spatial distribution of HIV cases was rejected if the p value≥0.05.16

All spatial analyses were based on the geographical coordinates (latitude and longitude) of the 300 EAs from the 2012 DHS and the 400 areas from the 2018 DHS, obtained using Geographic Information System and GPS technologies.36 These coordinates correspond to the centroids of the EAs.20 36 To protect respondent confidentiality, the GPS locations were randomly displaced: up to 2 km in urban areas and up to 5 km in rural areas, with a maximum displacement of up to 10 km applied to approximately 1% of rural areas. While this procedure introduces a degree of spatial inaccuracy into the analysis, it is necessary to reduce the risk of household identification.20

Multivariable analysis

For each of the DHS surveys conducted in 2012 and 2018, an individual-level multivariable analysis was conducted. In this study, the dependent variable was HIV status (positive or negative) among women and men who participated in the DHS in these 2 years.

Explanatory variables included socio-demographic, behavioural, economic, biological, health and socio-environmental characteristics, such as the presence of mining companies and HIV healthcare sites. Prior to multivariable modelling, variable selection was performed using simple univariable statistics to retain only variables independently associated with HIV status with a p value<0.05.

A weighted logistic regression was then conducted, taking into account the sampling weights and the structure of the complex sampling design. The final model was selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

Patient and public involvement

No patient was involved in this study.

Results

Description of participants

A total of 7922 in 2012 and 8539 in 2018 participants aged 15–49 years from both DHS series were analysed. Among them, 154 (1.9%) and 127 (1.5%) were HIV-positive in 2012 and 2018, respectively (online supplemental table 2).

The age group 35–49 was the most affected by HIV with 62 (40.3%) in 2012 and 54 (42.5%) in 2018. More than half of HIV-positive participants were women 108 (70.1%) in 2012 and 90 (70.9%) in 2018. Most of the participants were not enrolled in school during both DHS years. The majority were in a couple, with 113 (73%) in 2012 and 97 (76%) in 2018. In terms of place of residence, more than half of the HIV-infected participants in both DHS cycles came from urban areas. Although 114 (74%) in 2012 and 90 (71%) in 2018 had a liberal profession, the majority were employed in the past 12 months, with 123 (80%) in 2012 and 94 (74%) in 2018.

Roughly half of HIV-infected participants in both surveys were sexually inactive in the 4 weeks prior to the survey, with 74 (48%) in 2012 and 60 (47%) in 2018. In terms of household wealth, HIV-positive individuals were evenly distributed across classes.

Descriptive analysis showed that gender, age group, marital status, head of household’s gender, head of household’s age, occupation, employment in the last 12 months, sexuality activity, age of first sexual intercourse, number of mining companies in resident region, number of sex worker sites in resident region and number of HIV healthcare provision sites in resident region were significantly linked to HIV infection. Although type of residence is significantly linked to HIV status, it was not included as an independent variable in the multivariable analysis because it was already taken into account in the survey design.

Spatial variation of prevalence rate

In 2012, the raw prevalence rate of HIV infection varied from 0% in the prefectures Beyla, Dabola, Dubreka, Koubia and Yomou to 3.9% in the Kissidougou prefecture. The prefectures of Labe (3.7%), Coyah (3.6%), Koundara (3.3%), N'zerekore (3.1%), Conakry (2.9%), Kankan (2.6%), Macenta (2.2%), Siguiri (2.2%), Pita (2.2%), Telimele (2.2%) and Dinguiraye (2.1%) showed high proportions of HIV infection, followed by Boke (1.8%), Gueckedou (1.8%) and Kerouane (1.7%), which had slightly elevated prevalence rate (figure 3A).

In 2018, raw prevalence rate ranged from 0% in the Dabola, Kerouane and Kouroussa prefectures to 3.5% in Labe prefecture. Spatial autocorrelation

The estimated values of the Global Moran Index were 0.054 (p value: 0.01499) in 2012 and 0.046 (p value: 0.01199) in 2018. Moran’s spatial autocorrelation results were significant for the 2012 and 2018 data (online supplemental table 3). In addition, interpolation of the data using the Gaussian kernel revealed spatial heterogeneity of HIV prevalence rate in Guinea (0 to 4%), regardless of administrative boundaries. In 2012, the areas with the highest prevalence rate were concentrated in several prefectures, including Conakry, Coyah, Koundara, Dinguiraye and Gueckedou (figure 4A). In contrast, in 2018, the area with the highest prevalence rate was located mainly in the Boke and Boffa prefectures (figure 4B).

Discussion

The analysis highlighted significant spatial heterogeneity in HIV prevalence rate at both the prefectural and EAs levels in 2012 and 2018. Spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed an overall Moran’s index of I=0.054 (p value=0.01499) in 2012 and I=0.046 (p value=0.01199) in 2018, highlighting positive and statistically significant spatial autocorrelation. However, an overall and gradual decline in HIV prevalence rates was observed between 2012 and 2018 in all regions and prefectures of Guinea, reflecting a spatial decline in the epidemic.

In 2012, four (04) spatial clusters with high risk of HIV prevalence rate were identified in the prefectures of Kissidougou, Matam, Pita and N'zerekore, located, respectively, in the regions of Faranah, Conakry, Mamou and N'zerekore. In contrast, in 2018, only one high-risk cluster remained, located in the prefectures of Boffa and Boké, within the Boké region. Furthermore, HIV prevalence rate remained consistently higher among adults aged 35–49, followed by those aged 25–34 and among women. Finally, in 2018, significantly higher seroprevalence was also observed among the Soussou ethnic group.

The first cluster window in 2012 with a relative risk of 3.97 was located in Kissidougou, with a radius of 25.72 km, covering a substantial portion of the urban area. This could be attributed to a number of contextual, behavioural and geographical factors. Kissidougou hosted a large number of refugees and internally displaced persons during the civil wars in neighbouring countries (Liberia, Sierra Leone and Ivory Coast). These population movements, often in precarious situations and without access to adequate healthcare, promote HIV transmission, particularly due to sexual violence, sexually motivated economic exchanges or risky practices.44,46 Furthermore, this prefecture constitutes a transit and commercial exchange zone, located on a strategic road axis linking the centre of the country to the forest region of Guinea. This geographical position makes it a crossing point frequented by truckers, traders and migrants, which is often correlated with an increase in risky sexual behaviour, such as multiple partnerships or occasional recourse to sex work.47 48

A second significant cluster was identified in the municipality of Matam, located in the Conakry region, with a radius of 7.71 km. The risk of HIV infection within this cluster was 2.80 times higher compared to areas outside the cluster. This area is socially and economically characterised by the presence of several markets, bus stations and informal economic zones in the municipality, which experience a high daily mobility of the population. Such dynamics can facilitate economically motivated sexual practices, such as sex work, especially in the context of urban poverty. In addition, as observed in many urban settings, HIV-related stigma remains high, which could discourage people living with HIV or at risk of infection from getting tested or starting treatment. This, in turn, contributes to sustained viral transmission within the community.47

A third spatial cluster in 2012 was identified in the prefecture of Pita, covering a radius of 27.71 km and extending into adjacent areas of the Labe and Dalaba prefectures. This cluster exhibited a relative risk of 3.46, indicating a significantly higher concentration of cases compared with surrounding regions. This elevated risk may be explained by a combination of local, socio-cultural and economic factors. Pita is predominantly an agricultural and pastoral area, characterised by substantial population mobility, particularly related to transhumant livestock herding, trade activities and seasonal rural-to-urban migration. These patterns of temporary or prolonged migration often result in the separation of sexual partners and are frequently associated with increased high-risk sexual behaviours, such as multiple concurrent partnerships, engagement in sex work and low condom use.47 49 Finally, a fourth spatial cluster was identified in the urban areas of N'zerekore prefecture, with a radius of 14.70 km. This cluster presented a relative risk of 6.08, indicating a significantly higher concentration of cases than in the surrounding areas. Located in a strategic border zone between Guinea, Liberia and Côte d'Ivoire, this prefecture is characterised by intense cross-border mobility, particularly involving migrants, traders and sex workers. This mobility dynamic constitutes a factor conducive to HIV transmission, facilitating the circulation of the virus within already vulnerable populations. Furthermore, armed conflicts in neighbouring countries, particularly Liberia and Côte d'Ivoire, have generated significant refugee flows and internal displacement. These forced population movements have created conditions of great precariousness, marked by promiscuity, the disorganisation of health systems and increased exposure to sexual violence, all factors recognised to promote the spread of STIs, including HIV.44 47 48

The only high-risk HIV cluster identified in 2018 was located in the prefecture of Boffa and an adjacent area of Boke prefecture, within the Boke region, and covered a radius of 45 km. This aligns with previous research and sentinel surveillance reports from 2015, which found high prevalence rates among pregnant women and the 15–49 age group in the region.27 50 The increased operations and expansion of the Guinea Bauxite Company (CBG) in 2018 may have contributed to this higher observed HIV prevalence.2526 51,53 Additionally, the Boke region shares a border with the Tombali region in Guinea-Bissau, where HIV prevalence rate was above 5% in 2015.54 The region’s large fishing ports, such as Kassapo and Koukoude, which house over 2000 artisanal fishing boats, may also have contributed to the high prevalence rate. Studies have shown that fishermen are a vulnerable population for HIV infection.55,58 The influx of job seekers, the establishment of leisure sites and businesses, including the sex trade, may have further contributed to the spread of HIV in Boke.16 59

The shift in terms of quantity and locations of high-risk spatial clusters for HIV infection between 2012 and 2018 could be associated with better coordination among actors involved in the HIV/AIDS response, increased funding allocations and broader stakeholder involvement in interventions to combat the epidemic.27 These efforts have led to the implementation of enhanced prevention activities, notably through awareness campaigns, increased HIV testing, treatment of STIs, condom distribution, as well as improved availability of testing and treatment services, particularly through the increased involvement of community stakeholders.28 However, it is important to emphasise the inherent complexity of interpreting these results.57 Indeed, the apparent expansion of low-prevalence rate areas could also reflect variations in migration dynamics, including immigration and emigration patterns of people living with HIV.57 58 Nevertheless, these findings highlight the importance of sustainably strengthening HIV prevention services and epidemiological surveillance systems at national, regional and prefectural levels.

In both 2012 and 2018 surveys, a higher proportion of HIV infections was found among people aged 25 to 34 and 35 to 49 than in other age groups after having accounted for spatial sampling structure, a trend consistent with previous studies.17 20 60 61 This could be explained by greater exposure to behaviours promoting HIV transmission, including multiple sexual partners, extramarital relationships with divorced or widowed partners or increased vulnerability related to socio-environmental factors specific to this age group.62,64 HIV prevalence rate was also higher in females in both survey years, consistent with the findings of other studies.20 65 This could be explained by several biological, behavioural and social factors.66 67

The observed positive association between HIV infection and areas with more than 30 sites offering HIV prevention and care services is not surprising, particularly when access to healthcare is concentrated in central urban areas where other economic and social activities associated with greater HIV transmission risk are also concentrated. Problems with quality of care, stigma, the cost of treatment and frequent and detrimental disruptions in the supply of HIV resources are more pronounced in the interior of the country, prompting patients to migrate to areas where care is more accessible.68 69 These migration flows of men and women seeking healthcare may, through their new sexual partners, spread HIV within the community and alter the local epidemiology.59 This is particularly relevant in contexts where these migrants have multiple partners, engage in sex work or do not use condoms.

In 2018, in addition to the persistent association with the 25–34 and 35 to 49 age groups, the Soussou ethnic group was found to have higher levels of HIV seroprevalence. This could be explained by a combination of geographical (presence in urban areas with high prevalence), socio-behavioural (mobility, risky sexual behaviour), structural (social norms around sexuality and condom use) and cultural factors.70,72

Strengths and limitations

The present investigation has some constraints. As DHS survey data are cross-sectional, it is impossible to establish causal or temporal relationships. Furthermore, questionnaire-based surveys and respondent recall may introduce potential recall bias. However, the study is the first to use spatial analysis and the generalised linear model to explore the relationship between sociodemographic, behavioural, socio-environmental (mining companies, sites visited by sex workers) and HIV service delivery factors and HIV infection in Guinea.

For confidentiality purposes, DHS cluster coordinates are intentionally displaced: up to 2 km in urban areas, up to 5 km in rural areas and up to 10 km for approximately 1% of rural clusters. This displacement may affect geographic accuracy, particularly in densely populated or geographically small areas.

Conclusion

This study highlighted spatial variations in HIV infection prevalence rate across local and prefecture levels in Guinea in 2012 and in 2018. While some areas in the Kankan, Faranah and N'zerekore regions have seen a significant improvement in their epidemiological situation, others, particularly in the Boke region, have seen the emergence of high-risk hotspots, reflecting a reconfiguration of HIV prevalence clusters. These trends can be explained by disparities in access to and coverage of prevention, screening and treatment services, as well as by contextual factors such as population mobility, local socioeconomic dynamics and varying intensity of public health interventions.

The identification in 2018 of clusters with high prevalence rates, particularly in the prefectures of Boffa and Boke, Boke region, highlights the need to strengthen targeted interventions in areas with persistent or emerging risk. Furthermore, analysis of the factors associated with high HIV prevalence revealed associations with female gender, the 25–49 age group, membership of the Soussou ethnic group, and areas with more than 30 HIV prevention and care service sites, suggesting both increased vulnerability of certain groups and a localisation effect of services in areas with a high disease burden. These findings call for a reorientation of public policies to combat HIV in Guinea, favouring differentiated, evidence-based strategies rather than uniform approaches. Priorities should include geographically targeted interventions, strengthening primary healthcare, integrating HIV prevention and care services, and specific actions targeting the most exposed groups. Such an approach, combining territorial and social equity, is essential to consolidate gains, reduce persistent inequalities and accelerate the control of the epidemic at the national level.
