Biodiversity threats and socio-ecological dynamics in Chebera Churchura National Park: A One Health approach
Authors: Tadesse Guadu, Berhanu Abera, Kissanet Tesfay, Anteneh Berhane, Ararso Tafese, Tarekegn Solomon, Belete Debebe, Melaku Getachew, Agumas Shibabaw, Alfoalem Araba Abiye, Mukarim Abdurahman, Waktole Yadeta, Yordanos Tadesse, Teshale Aymeta, Theodore JD Knight-Jones, Siobhan M Mor, Seid Tiku Mereta, Mirgissa Kaba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChebera Churchura National Park (CCNP), a protected area in Ethiopia renowned for its diverse and thriving ecosystems, including nearly one-third of the nation's African elephant population and the largest concentration of African buffalo, grapples with pressing conservation challenges. We conducted a study in 2024 in the Konta and Dwauro Zones to assess the park's conservation status and ecosystem services using a One Health (OH) approach. The study utilized a mixed-method approach using the Rapid Assessment and Prioritization of Protected Area Management (RAPPAM) tool, field observations, document reviews, and key informant interviews across human and animal health, agriculture, local administration, and environmental sectors in five purposefully chosen kebeles bordering the park. Findings showed that the conservation status and ecosystem services of CCNP were inadequate, with key challenges including expansion to the park, deforestation, and illegal logging, leading to habitat loss, increased poaching of high-value species, and frequent regular human-wildlife conflicts. The problem is exacerbated by inadequate financial, human, and infrastructural resources for effective park management. Outbreaks of vector-borne diseases like malaria and trypanosomiasis in nearby communities and animals, respectively, and neglected tropical diseases such as schistosomiasis and onchocerciasis were found to prevail. Lack of collaboration among stakeholders and limited support for park management from the local government and the public at large were critical gaps. In conclusion, the study underscored the need for multisectoral collaborative engagements, strong partnerships, and improved governance and resources to enhance conservation strategies, improve community health, and ensure the sustainable management of CCNP. Cross-sectoral alliance is essential for good governance, optimizing resource allocation, and advancing effective conservation practices to address the multifaceted challenges confronting the park despite its biodiversity hotspot of global significance.Keywords: Biodiversity threats, Chebera Churchura, Ecosystem health, Multisectoral collaboration, National Park, One health approach, Protected areas

Introduction

Globally, protected areas (PAs) encompass 17 % of terrestrial and inland water ecosystems. Over the past decade, PAs have expanded by 21 million km2 worldwide. These are crucial for preserving biodiversity and maintaining ecosystem structure and functions, which are vital for human survival and well-being [1,2]. Ethiopia, distinguished by its ecological diversity and endemism, has about 104 PAs, covering approximately 200,074 km2 [2,3]. However, PAs in Ethiopia face threats from anthropogenic activities, including the expansion of agriculture and settlements, fuel wood collection, expansion of invasive species, and unlimited utilization of biodiversity from overgrazing, overharvesting, and uncontrolled hunting [4,5].

Chebera Churchura National Park (CCNP) was established in 2005 in the southwestern region of Ethiopia. It is a critical habitat for a diverse array of flora and fauna, including significant populations of African elephants (Loxodonta africana) and African buffalo (Syncerus caffer). The park's unique ecosystems, which encompass montane forests, grasslands, and wetlands, provide essential ecological services such as carbon sequestration, water regulation, and biodiversity conservation [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. However, despite its ecological significance, the CCNP faces numerous anthropogenic threats that jeopardize its biodiversity and the livelihoods of surrounding communities. The primary threats to CCNP include habitat degradation due to agricultural expansion, deforestation, and illegal logging [1,10]. As local populations grow, the demand for land and resources intensifies, leading to increased encroachment into park boundaries. This encroachment not only results in habitat loss but also heightens human-wildlife conflicts, as wildlife increasingly comes into contact with agricultural lands [11,12]. Such conflicts often manifest in crop damage and livestock predation, creating tensions between conservation efforts and local livelihoods [13,14]. Moreover, the park is experiencing common health challenges, including outbreaks of diseases such as malaria and schistosomiasis, which are exacerbated by environmental changes and inadequate health interventions [15,16]. The socio-economic dynamics surrounding CCNP further complicate conservation efforts. Many local communities rely on the park's resources for their livelihoods, engaging in activities such as livestock grazing, firewood collection, and subsistence farming [11]. However, these activities often conflict with conservation goals, leading to a cycle of resource depletion and increased human-wildlife interactions [17,18]. Literature indicated that understanding the socio-ecological dynamics is crucial for developing effective management strategies that balance conservation objectives with the needs of local communities [19]. The interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health underscores the necessity of adopting a One Health (OH) approach, which emphasizes the collaboration between health and conservation sectors to address these multifaceted challenges [11,[20], [21], [22]].

Despite the ecological significance of CCNP in harboring diverse flora and fauna and providing critical ecosystem services, there is limited research on the socio-ecological dynamics and biodiversity threats within the park. Existing studies predominantly concerned on issues related to biodiversity conservation, but fail to comprehensively integrate human health, environmental health, and biodiversity conservation within the broader OH framework. This gap hinders the development of holistic strategies to address anthropogenic pressures, biodiversity loss, and human-wildlife conflicts in the park. Findings from this study generate useful evidence that helps to improve conservation strategies, reduce human-wildlife conflicts, and promote sustainable management practices that safeguard critical habitats and species, including African elephants and buffalo.

Material and methods

Study settings and design

Chebera Churchura National Park (CCNP) is located in the Konta and Dwauro Zones of the South Western regional state of Ethiopia, about 427 km and 475 km southwest of Hawassa and Addis Ababa, respectively. It covers an area of 1410 km2 and lies between the coordinates 36° 27′00“- 36° 57’14”E and 6°56′05″-7° 08′02”N. The park is bordered by Konta Zone to the north, the Omo river to the south, Dawro Zone to the east and southeast, and the Agare High mountains and Ouma river to the west (Fig. 1).

Chebera Churchura National Park (in green color fill) and its buffer zone (in grey color fill), 2024. (For interpretation of the references to color in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

CCNP is renowned for its rich biodiversity, with African elephants representing a flagship species. The park features diverse habitats such as grasslands, woodlands, montane forests, and riverine areas. It is home to over 400 elephants, with some estimates suggesting numbers between 400 and 700 individuals. These elephants are distributed across various herds and are a central attraction of the park. In addition to elephants, the park hosts a variety of other large mammals, including lions, leopards, servals (Leptailurus serval), greater kudus, colobus monkeys, vervet monkeys, hippos, defassa waterbucks, and warthogs. The park also supports 237 bird species, with some endemic to Ethiopia, such as the white-winged cliff chat, banded barbet, wattled ibis, Ethiopian oriole, and thick-billed raven.

The human population in and around CCNP is relatively low. The surrounding communities primarily engage in agriculture and livestock rearing, with cattle, sheep, and pack animals like horses, mules, and donkeys. Each household typically owns an average of 9.37 livestock [23].

The study employed a convergent mixed-method design to assess the biodiversity threats and socio-ecological dynamics of the park, including the interactions among biodiversity, human health, and community dynamics.

Study population and sampling

This study included five kebeles within the Konta and Dawro Zones, namely Chebera, Delba, Koyisha, Chevera, and Seri-Shewa. The assessment employed a purposive sampling technique to select the kebeles, which were chosen based on diversity of fauna, flora, habitat (including genetic, species, community, and ecosystem variations), levels of human-wildlife conflict, and community engagement. A total of 12 key informants from the five kebeles participated in the interviews. Eight were from human health facilities, three were from veterinary clinics, and one was from the park office.

Data collection tools and sampling

We applied different data collection techniques, including the RAPPAM tool, key informant interviews, field observations, and document reviews in health facilities and veterinary clinics. The RAPPAM tool was designed to assess the management effectiveness of protected areas [24]. The rapid assessment questionnaire was used to collect evidence on different themes including, the current context of the park, design and planning, inputs, management process, and outcome (Table 1). Furthermore, the questionnaire incorporated a set of questions addressing system-level design issues, protected area policies, and the broader policy environment. This questionnaire was administered to key informants (park manager, selected park management team members, scouts, and security officers) by the researchers' team.

Key informant interviews were conducted with health and veterinary professionals from local health and veterinary posts, as well as with the park management staff, community leaders, scout members, and security officials. These interviews aimed to gather qualitative data on health issues predominant in the surrounding communities and their relationship with the park's biodiversity. This data was collected from the nearest health facilities and veterinary clinics to the selected kebeles. Reports on existing human and animal health facility, and management documents were reviewed, focusing on common infectious diseases of public health importance, to contextualize the current health challenges and management practices of the park.

An observation checklist was prepared and utilized to systematically record observed findings related to biodiversity and environmental health. Field observations were carried out in and around the park to assess the park conditions and human-animal interactions.

Data quality control

Before data collection, the interview guide was carefully pretested to validate its effectiveness and clarity. Training was provided for data collectors and supervisors. Orientation was given to local guides and scouts. To ensure the credibility of the peer debriefing process, it was done by research teams. Audio recordings were reviewed and cross-verified with their corresponding transcripts by the research teams.

To enhance the reliability of the findings, data from different sources (interviews, observations, and document reviews) were triangulated. This approach ensured that the assessment was based on a comprehensive understanding of the park's management effectiveness and the socio-ecological context.

Data management, measurement, and analysis

The quantitative data from the rapid assessment were compiled using Microsoft Excel 2013 and summarized into tables and graphs, allowing for a visual representation. The RAPPAM tool was used to assess the pressures and threats within the OH framework. One Health is a collaborative, multisectoral, and transdisciplinary approach that recognizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health. Pressures are forces or influences that can be exerted without clear intention, while threats are explicit statements or actions indicating potential harm or damage to the park. The degree of pressure and threat was characterized based on the three elements: probability, extent, and impact. Probability refers to the likelihood of the threat occurring in the future, ranging from very low to very high. To identify threats and pressures in the assessment process, an initial list of potential threats and pressures, along with their probability of occurrence across the entire protected area was recorded. Extent is the range across which the impact of the activity occurs, which was represented by “throughout”, “widespread”, “scattered”, and “localized”. Impact is the degree, either directly or indirectly, to which the pressure affects overall the protected area resources. The RAPPAM tool employs a rating scale to categorize pressures and threats to the park, assessing its severity and impact on a scale from 1 to 5, where 5 indicates severe impact and 1 indicates minimal impact. The magnitude or extent of the scale was as follows: 5-Throughout (>50 %), 4-Widespread (15–50 %), 3-Scattered (5–15 %), 2-Localized (2–5 %), and 1-Minimal. The degree of each pressure and threat was then calculated by multiplying its extent, impact, and severity of occurrence using numerical values. A degree of 1 to 3 was considered none, 4 to 9 mild, 10 to 27 moderate, 28 to 64 high, and 65 to 125 severe [25]. In scoring the various elements of management effectiveness (e.g., biological importance, planning, inputs, and processes), respondents replied to statements such as “The siting of the protected area is consistent with the protected area's objectives”. These were evaluated using a four-point scale, with a “yes,” “mostly yes,” “mostly no,” or “no” response, rating 4 to 1, respectively.

The qualitative data was transcribed, coded, and analyzed using thematic content analysis. Evidence obtained from interviews and field observation was categorized based on the common themes related to health, biodiversity, and socio-ecological dynamics.

Results

This study revealed that CCNP provides diverse biological and socioeconomic importance for the surrounding community. Despite having unexploited resources and opportunities, this study identified various gaps and challenges that need to be addressed by all stakeholders. Some of the key importance and critical gaps were summarized below.

Understanding socio-economic and biological importance of CCNP

Fig. 2 shows the socio-economic importance of a CCNP. Overall, the park holds great socio-economic importance, providing a significant source of employment and numerous job opportunities for local communities, supporting biodiversity, offering recreational benefits, and contributing to ecosystem services. It has high educational and scientific value, serving as a site for research, education, and knowledge dissemination, in addition to its recreational value, which attracts visitors for leisure and tourism activities.

Community perceptions to various socio-economic and ecological benefits obtained from CCNP in 2024.

The protected area plays a great role in ecosystem services, including water purification, climate regulation, and biodiversity support. Furthermore, the PA contains plant and animal species of significant social, cultural, and economical value, highlighting its botanical importance and its role in wildlife conservation.

The protected area also offers opportunities for community development through sustainable resource use, contributing to local growth even though nearby communities do not rely on its resources for subsistence, indicating that their basic needs are met through other means. Likewise, its cultural and spiritual significances, along with its aesthetic features, are valued for its beauty and uniqueness. These factors underscore the need for its sustainable management and long-term preservation.

The CCNP harbors a relatively high number of rare (goldfish), endangered (African elephants), and threatened (African buffalo, etc.) species with high levels of biodiversity for maintaining ecological balance and resilience, indicating its importance for conservation efforts. There is also a notable degree of endemism within the park, suggesting that it harbors species that are not found anywhere else, further emphasizing its ecological significance. CCNP plays a critical role in landscape functions, which may include water regulation, soil conservation, and habitat connectivity. Furthermore, the CCNP encompasses the full spectrum of plant and animal diversity, contributing significantly to the representativeness of the broader PA system. It sustains minimum viable populations of key species and maintains structural diversity aligned with historic norms. Additionally, the CCNP includes ecosystems with historically diminished ranges and preserves the full range of natural processes and disturbance regimes.

Challenges facing CCNP

Vulnerability of the park

The PA is highly vulnerable due to the high market value of its resources and easy access for illegal activities. Illegal hunting and human-wildlife conflicts are attributed to weak law enforcement, corruption, political instability, and civil unrest. The PA manager faces pressures to exploit resources, while challenges in recruiting and retaining staff further complicate sustainable management.

The assessment identified several threats to the PA system, including poaching, deforestation, human-wildlife conflict, invasive species, and climate change. Poaching and deforestation pose the most severe risks to biodiversity, while human-wildlife conflict and invasive species have moderate effects. Climate change emerges as growing concern.

Management efforts are hindered by insufficient resources, including staffing shortage, lack of vehicles, and limited fuel supplies. Weak policy enforcement and poor collaboration further exacerbate conservation challenges. Deforestation and agricultural expansion continue to threaten habitats due to increasing land-use demands. Additional threats include road construction and human activity, which facilitate the spread of invasive species and degrade ecosystems, affecting essential services like soil fertility and water purification. Tensions between local communities and conservation initiatives further complicate protective measures (Fig. 3). Despite challenges, the park's management planning is effective, supported by strong legal frameworks, community engagement, and well-defined biodiversity objectives. National parks, despite their own laws, usually face disagreements. These issues are managed according to the established social norms despite border disputes. However, due to absence of well-established long-term legally binding framework, unresolved disputes over land tenure and use rights create conflicts (Fig. 4), and inadequate staffing and financial resources weaken law enforcement efforts. Conflicts with the local community are resolved fairly and effectively, indicating a strong relationship between the PA management and local stakeholders. While the boundaries of the park are adequately demarcated to meet the objectives of the park, poor land use in surrounding areas limits effective management. In addition, the park is not linked to other areas of conserved or protected land, lacking ecological connectivity and biodiversity conservation efforts.

The assessment of input allocations for CCNP management revealed several challenges and strengths (Fig. 5). While data management and certain communication aspects are sufficient, staffing remains a significant issue due to skill gaps and poor retention. Infrastructure, including field equipment, visitor facilities, and maintenance remains inadequate, limiting operational effectiveness. Most critically, financial allocations are insufficient, posing a serious threat to the park's long-term sustainability and management efficiency. Addressing these deficits is essential for ensuring effective conservation and stability.

Management, system-level design, and policy environment of CCNP

CCNP benefited from a well-established management framework, including an updated written plan that aligns stakeholder actions with conservation objectives. A comprehensive inventory of natural and cultural resources facilitates informed decision-making. Efforts are made to address threats as proactive approach in identifying potential challenges and developing strategies to mitigate them, ensuring the sustainability of the management efforts. A detailed work plan identified specific targets for achieving management objectives, and ensures clear accountability. However, research and monitoring findings are not consistently integrated into management strategies, limiting adaptability and the use of new tools (Fig. 6).

Evaluation of management processes, decision-making, research, and outputs in Chebera Churchura National Park in 2024.

The park system (CCNP) effectively conserves key ecosystems, maintains natural processes, and systematically protects high-value sites. However, biodiversity protection for endemic species, and optimization of conservation efforts remain only partial. National protected area policies set clear objectives and restoration targets while supporting ongoing research. Weaknesses include gaps in biological diversity assessments, infrequent PA system evaluations, and the absence of effective training programs.

The policy framework supports enforcement and environmental protection goals, but critical issues such as funding shortages, limited interdepartmental communication, weak environmental education initiatives, and inadequate training for governmental employees hinder effective PA administration. Addressing these gaps is necessary for enhancing conservation outcomes and long-term sustainability.

Threats of zoonotic diseases

Ethiopia has adopted a One Health approach and framework to address public health challenges, particularly zoonotic diseases, by integrating human, animal, and environmental health sectors. However, key informant interviews with health professionals and health facility reports revealed that malaria is a top public health problem in the area, while schistosomiasis and onchocerciasis were the second and third top diseases reported by nearby health facilities. Human attack by buffalo have been reported from a few health facilities to injure humans. Malaria outbreaks have commonly occurred during the rainy season, with increased prevalence in 2024 compared to previous years. According to the health professionals' response, the occurrence of schistosomiasis might be attributed to river water contamination, since the river is also used as a source of water for households in the area. The health professionals also reported that these diseases are exacerbated by factors such as a lack of training for health professionals on these health threats, a lack of diagnostic tests, and inadequate access to preventive measures and treatment, which limit their capacity to address these issues. The KII with veterinarians and veterinary clinic reports revealed that trypanosomiasis is the most prevalent animal disease, followed by pasteurellosis, blackleg, brucellosis, and lumpy skin disease in the area. Tsetse flies are suspected to enhance the transmission of trypanosomiasis between wildlife and domestic animals. Brucellosis is suspected to be associated with high rates of cattle abortion in the area. These veterinarians stated that, although there are frequent interactions between humans, domestic animals, and wildlife in shared spaces such as hot springs in the park, which might likely increase the risk of zoonotic disease transmission, they reported limited collaboration among OH stakeholders. While some cooperation exists between park managers and the local community, there is a lack of integration with human health professionals, which is essential for effective zoonotic disease management. They further reported that resource constraints, including a lack of professionals, equipment, and drugs needed for handling, restraining, diagnosing, and treating wildlife, along with inadequate training and resources for zoonotic disease diagnosis and management, are affecting effective disease management in the area. Thus, Ethiopia needs more efforts to implement OH policy and integrate sectors to alleviate threats of zoonotic diseases.

Ecosystem diversity and observational findings

The OH policy and framework is important for maintaining ecosystem diversity and balancing human-wildlife-domestic animal interactions. CCNP hosts a variety of ecosystems due to its diverse topography and climatic conditions. The main ecosystem types include montane forests, grasslands, wetlands, savanna, rocky outcrops and cliffs, rivers and streams, as well as hot springs (Fig. 7). The Barbo River is a tributary of the Omo River that originates from the park, which plays a vital role in supporting the Koisha hydroelectric power project. The Barbo waterfall enhances the park's aesthetic appeal, while Bulo Lake further contributes to its scenic beauty.

The presence of under-construction lodging facilities and resorts near the Barbo waterfall indicates a growing interest in developing the tourism infrastructure within the park. This development could contribute to the local economy and provide employment opportunities for surrounding communities. However, it is essential to ensure that these facilities are constructed and operated sustainably and minimize their impact on the park's natural resources.

The park's biodiversity is evident in the presence of large mammals such as buffalo and elephants. While towers for scouts indicate efforts to monitor and protect the park's resources, further measures may be necessary to address environmental challenges and ensure the park's long-term sustainability.

The ongoing construction of the road connecting Jimma town to Chebera Churchura National Park is a positive development that could increase tourism and visitor numbers to the park. However, the current state of the road, characterized by poor conditions, may pose challenges for visitors, especially during the rainy season.

Human-wildlife-domestic animal interactions

The observation of domestic animals (cattle and goats) grazing within the park's boundaries and the presence of wildlife such as elephants near the hot spring highlighted the potential for human-wildlife conflicts. These interactions could have negative consequences for both wildlife and local communities. Strategies to mitigate these conflicts, such as establishing buffer zones and implementing effective wildlife management practices, were crucial for the long-term conservation of the park's biodiversity. Additionally, the presence of domestic dogs within the park poses a risk of disease transmission between wild and domestic animals.

The production of smoke by nearby settlements to avoid wildlife damage to agricultural products might also lead to fires and potential encroachment on park lands and reflected ongoing wildlife conflicts (Fig. 8). Hot springs, on the border between the park and the surrounding community, served as essential water sources for humans, domestic animals, and wildlife. This shared resource created further opportunities for conflict as domestic and wild animals gather at these sites. The community believed that the hot spring water is beneficial for their livestock, while wild animals also periodically use it for drinking (Fig. 9). This might create opportunities for disease transmission among domestic and wild animals and humans. Generally, each of these ecosystems plays a crucial role in maintaining the ecological balance and supports a wide range of wildlife, making the CCNP an important conservation area.

Threats drawn from observation

Since its establishment in 2005, CCNP has become recognized for its abundant biological diversity, playing a vital role in wildlife conservation and habitat preservation. However, the park faces increasing pressure from human activities, leading to significant changes in land cover. This observational study highlighted the following critical challenges both within and outside the park.

Agricultural expansion

The rising demand for agricultural land has led to encroachment on park boundaries, threatening natural ecosystems. The OH team observed fires in previously cleared dry forests and ongoing vegetation removal, both of which increase the risk of wildfires near the park.

Deforestation and resource use

Deforestation poses another serious threat to the park, driven primarily by the harvesting of timber and fuel wood. Population growth in areas surrounding the park has resulted in heightened demand for construction materials and energy. However, the lack of alternative energy sources and building materials forces local communities to rely on the park's forests. This dependency not only accelerates deforestation but also exposes individuals to the dangers of wildlife attacks when gathering resources covertly.

Urbanization and its impacts

Urban expansion near CCNP exerts additional pressure on park resources. The growth of nearby urban centers leads to habitat fragmentation through road construction and other infrastructure projects, increases resource competition, and introduces pollutants that can degrade ecosystems. As human settlements encroach on park boundaries, human-wildlife interactions become more frequent, raising the likelihood of conflicts that can harm both people and animals. While urbanization may boost tourism, it also risks overburdening the park's infrastructure if not carefully managed.

Plastic waste and lack of a buffer zone

The presence of plastic waste near the park's waterfall points to inadequate waste management practices both within and around the park. Additionally, the absence of a designated buffer zone leaves the park vulnerable to habitat degradation and further encroachment from human activities.

Impacts of tourism on park ecological condition

Tourism can bring significant benefits to local economies and conservation efforts; it can also have detrimental effects on the ecology of CCNP if not carefully managed. Some of the potential impacts of tourism on the ecology of the park include habitat disturbance (leading to wildlife displacement, habitat degradation, and noise pollution), pollution (due to waste generation, water pollution, and air pollution), pressure on resources (leading to overexploitation of natural resources and resource competition with local communities), spread of invasive species, and contribution to climate change due to carbon footprint. Sustainable tourism practices and appropriate regulation are key to minimizing these negative impacts while promoting conservation and ecological health. So, appropriate implementation of the OH policy and framework is crucial for maintaining ecosystem diversity and balancing human-wildlife-domestic animal interactions.

Discussion

This study assessed the conservation status and ecosystem services of the CCNP using a One Health approach and framework. Overall, limited cross-sectoral collaboration across One health domains proved highly relevant to the observed outcomes. The major findings of the study were human-wildlife conflict, poaching, deforestation, weak enforcement of regulations, inadequate staff and finance, lack of training and development of park staff, road and infrastructure development in the area, and outbreaks of malaria, schistosomiasis, and onchocerciasis. The evidence is crucial for improving conservation strategies, reducing human-wildlife conflicts, and promoting sustainable management practices that safeguard critical habitats and species in the park. The findings are timely for advancing OH policy in Ethiopia; where the approach is gaining strategic importance due to the country's vulnerability to zoonotic diseases, climate change, and rapid population growth, all of which are factors that intensify interactions between humans, animals, and the environment.

This study revealed the presence of a conflict between humans and wildlife as a result of habitat encroachment, reports on a spillover (from wildlife to domestic animals and humans), and a spillback (from humans and domestic animals to wildlife), which has a potential for zoonotic disease transmission. Intensive wildlife poaching is a pressure with a high impact. This study also demonstrated health facility reports of mortality of one to two individuals per year; more than ten people lost their lives in the last ten years due to elephants and buffalo attack. Another local study done in Wof-Washa Natural State Forest and Bale Mountains National Park of Ethiopia indicated human-wildlife conflict resulting in crop damage, predation of livestock, and human attacks [18,20,26]. Besides, studies conducted in India and a meta-analysis study also showed the existence of human-wildlife conflict in different areas [19,27,28]. The human-wildlife conflict results from habitat encroachment. This is caused by agricultural expansion, deforestation, lack of a buffer zone, and human settlement near parks. It could cause a negative perception of local communities in the park due to loss of crops, human attack, and livestock predation. This needs a physical barrier such as proper land use planning, management, and enforcement of buffer zone policies with appropriate compensation efforts for the loss the community encounters [29].

The findings of the health facility and observation indicated road construction and other infrastructure projects in the park connecting it to the nearby city of Jimma. Roads and infrastructures can lead to increased resource competition. Evidence showed that roads cause destruction of habitats, isolation of wildlife populations, wildlife mortality due to car accidents, noise and light disturbances [22,30,31]. Likewise, roads increase human-wildlife conflict [20]. The competition for resources exacerbates when infrastructure projects are increased in protected areas. However, the construction of roads and infrastructure is undeniable to the development of ecotourism. The road and infrastructure improve sustainability and attract visitors [32]. There is a need to balance road and infrastructure construction and ecosystem conservation efforts. Caution should be taken with environmental conservation goals while building infrastructure. There is a need for ecological protection, ensuring that tourism activities do not harm the fragile ecosystems of the protected areas [21,30,32].

In the present study, the health facility report revealed that malaria outbreaks were occurring in the area. It was reported that multiple water-holding areas used for road construction had resulted in malaria outbreaks in the local communities. A study conducted in the Amazon of Brazil showed the area with higher forest cover resulted in higher malaria incidence. Deforested areas resulted in higher malaria incidence rates compared with the forest cover [33]. Additionally, the findings of a study conducted in tropical forests indicated prevention of malaria in humans due to the presence of wild animals serving as dead-end hosts for malaria parasites. Moreover, the competition of malaria-causing mosquitoes with non-malaria-causing mosquito species reduces the risk of transmission [34]. However, different studies have demonstrated the presence of stagnant water as a risk factor for malaria outbreaks [8,35,36]. Therefore, actions should be taken to mitigate the accumulation of stagnant water for the construction of roads.

This study also confirmed that schistosomiasis is occurring in the area. Schistosomiasis is a neglected tropical disease. It occurs because rapid urbanization, poor sanitation, and the use of natural water sources accommodate immediate hosts like snails for daily activities [37,38]. Similarly, our study findings revealed an outbreak of onchocerciasis. Onchocerciasis is a disease targeted for elimination in 2030 in Ethiopia [39]. Despite the stated elimination goal, there was an onchocerciasis outbreak in Chebera Shasho Health Post. This calls for strengthening disease surveillance systems and public health interventions.

Our study from a veterinary clinic revealed a high prevalence of trypanosomiasis among domestic animals living near the park. African animal trypanosomiasis (AAT) is a major animal health problem occurring in 38 sub-Saharan African countries, including Ethiopia [40]. Strategies to control trypanosomiasis include vector control measures, giving chemotherapy, promoting good animal husbandry practices, and early diagnosis and treatment of animals [41,42]. These prevention and control measures should be taken to prevent and control the disease in the area.

Our observations showed deforestation for the harvesting of timber and fuel wood. We have found a lot of smoky areas around the park. Similarly, studies of Amazon-protected areas showed deforestation occurred due to the growing of new unofficial roads, land grabbing, and illegal cattle ranching [22,43,44]. Another study showed deforestation of protected areas occurring due to agricultural expansion, infrastructure development, illegal logging and timber harvesting, and geographic variability due to weak law enforcement [45].

This study showed high pressure, throughout magnitude, and high impact of weak enforcement of regulations. Weak enforcement exacerbates illegal logging and deforestation. Therefore, strengthening governance and legal frameworks is required [20,46]. However, our study findings showed the presence of effective communication and support of the local community for the protected area. Studies suggest community involvement and integrating local knowledge to improve conservation of the protected areas [47].

This study presented inadequate staffing and budget. There is also a lack of training and development opportunities for the protected area staff. Similarly, a study revealed the need for protected area staff training to raise their knowledge and skill in managing the protected area; lack of protected area staff capacity affects their ability to respond to emerging challenges such as climate change, illegal activities, and community conflicts [48].

Our field observation findings revealed the presence of plastic waste near the park's waterfall points due to inadequate waste management practices both within and around the park. A study showed 92.2 % of the waste in protected areas was plastic waste. This study states plastic pollution affects biodiversity as well as tourism and fisheries [22,49]. Another study conducted in Greece indicated that inadequate waste management, lack of awareness, and insufficient enforcement of environmental regulations are major contributors to plastic accumulation in a park [21,50]. Additionally, another study in areas of Carpathian protected areas showed that inadequate infrastructure for waste collection and disposal in these areas leads to dumping of plastic waste. Moreover, plastic waste often clogs watercourses, impacting both biodiversity and water quality [51].

Limitations of the study

This study was undertaken with the following limitations. First, due to resource limitations, the authors did not collect data on all bordering kebeles. However, we employed the RAPPAM methodology and tools in five purposively selected kebeles, which is the most thorough and effective approach to seeing the biodiversity and socio-ecological dynamics of the park in a holistic way. Secondly, the study was not complemented with quantitative data. But the qualitative data was collected and triangulated from multiple sources, including field observations, document reviews, and key informant interviews across human and animal health, agriculture, local administration, and environmental sectors among kebeles bordering the park.

Conclusion

This study showed that CCNP is rich in biodiversity and ecosystems. Nevertheless, the park is facing challenges from human-wildlife conflict, deforestation, weak enforcement of regulations, and the presence of plastic waste within and around the park. The park is undergoing road and infrastructure development, which poses additional environmental pressures. It also suffers from inadequate staffing, insufficient funding, and a lack of appropriate training for existing staff. Furthermore, nearby communities have experienced disease outbreaks, such as malaria, schistosomiasis, and onchocerciasis, emphasizing the need for an integrated prevention and control system.

Recommendations

Therefore, a collaborative and multi-sectorial effort through the OH approach is essential to address the challenges facing the park. This requires strengthening law enforcement and governance frameworks in partnership with federal and local agencies, as well as park management. Additionally, the park management should work on a comprehensive waste management system for the park, such as bans on single-use plastics, promoting recycling, and enhancing local waste management infrastructure. Infrastructure development within the park must balance ecological conservation by utilizing environmental impact assessments to guide planning and minimize harm. Health risks from stagnant water call for targeted interventions, including proper drainage systems of wetlands for mosquito/malaria control, to suppress the population of mosquito predators and competitors and are found to be counterproductive. Furthermore, allocating enough budget to the park, investing in capacity building, and providing appropriate training for park staff are crucial to ensuring sustainable management and protection efforts.
