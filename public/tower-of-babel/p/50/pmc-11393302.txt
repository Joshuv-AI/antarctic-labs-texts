Evaluating ExpandED: Evaluating the effectiveness of a serious game expansion pack in teaching health professional students about interprofessional care
Authors: Clare Fiala, Sowmithree Ragothaman, Gursukhmani Johl, Monica Sabbineni, Sarah Wojkowski, Teresa M. Chan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergency department (ED) is a challenging fast‐paced environment with high‐acuity, undifferentiated patients who often require extensive interdisciplinary care. This paper introduces ExpandED, an expansion pack to the serious board game GridlockED, designed to enhance players’ understanding of interprofessional collaboration in the ED and the diverse scope of practice of different ED professionals including physicians, residents, registered nurses, registered practical nurses, social workers, occupational therapists, and physiotherapists. This investigation evaluates the effectiveness of ExpandED as a teaching tool for medical and allied health professions students about interprofessional collaboration in the ED.MethodsA program evaluation harnessing a playtest framework was employed. Participants completed pre‐ and postgame surveys including quantitative measures (e.g., Likert scales) and qualitative free‐text feedback that focused on participant familiarity with ED functioning, valuation of interprofessional collaboration before and after playing, and feedback on game usability and effectiveness.ResultsRecruitment was open to students in all health care and allied health professional programs at the institution. Forty‐five participants were recruited from medical doctor, nursing, physiotherapy, and speech language pathology programs. ExpandED enhances participants' understanding of ED workflow (p < 0.001) and provides an enjoyable playing experience. However, participants’ valuation of interdisciplinary teamwork did not change significantly before and after game play (p = 0.17). Participants expressed satisfaction with the game's accuracy in simulating the ED environment and appreciated the opportunity to collaborate with peers from different disciplines. Challenges reported included some tension among players, potential biases, and limitations of fidelity to a real‐life ED.ConclusionsWhile this study has limitations regarding participant sampling and duration of gameplay sessions, it highlights the potential of ExpandED for teaching interprofessional collaboration in the ED. These findings will guide further development to optimize the expansion pack's effectiveness and its implementation into health care curricula.

INTRODUCTION

The emergency department (ED) is a fast‐paced, complex environment where individuals from various disciplines are required to work quickly and efficiently with each other. Junior learners have limited clinical exposure and may have little understanding of workflow prior to entering the ED; GridlockED was created in response to this need and allows junior learners to experience the stress and strain of overseeing an ED, via a serious game. 1

Historically, most EDs were staffed primarily by physicians and nurses. However, a modern‐day ED incorporates various other allied health professionals including nurse practitioners, physician assistants, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, and social workers who are instrumental in helping EDs cope with growing patient volumes and complexity, aging populations, and staffing shortages. 2 Collaboration between health care providers has shown to have multiple benefits, including reducing clinical error and improving overall continuity of care. 3 Of note, specialized interdisciplinary teams that can be composed of individuals such as physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and social workers are increasingly being utilized to help ED patients that face more complex barriers to discharge. They can perform specialized assessments and connect patients to resources, which other ED clinical staff may not have the time or training to do. 2 There has been limited formal scholarship about these teams. 4

Interprofessional education (IPE) is defined by the World Health Organization as occurring “when two or more professionals learn about, from and with each other to enable effective collaboration and improve health outcomes.” 5 While most health professions education is uniprofessional, IPE is valuable in providing students with the knowledge and skills to work together as a team to improve patient care and outcomes. 6 IPE is being implemented to some degree in health care education programs across Canada, but reviews by the Canadian Federation of Medical Students (CFMS) have found that there are limited opportunities in both formal and informal innovative approaches for students to collaborate meaningfully from across different health care specialties. 7 In particular, some institutions still adopt a didactic approach to IPE that is less effective at fostering a sense of interprofessional understanding than interactive methods like case and simulation‐based learning. 8 , 9 , 10 Similarly, the Liaison Committee for Medical Education (LCME) elevated the IPE standard to a core standard within medical doctorate programs in 2013, and several studies have highlighted gaps in IPE since that time. 4 , 11 , 12 Serious games (i.e., games that are not designed purely for entertainment) can potentially address this gap. In the health sciences, they have been gaining popularity as an accessible, safe, and engaging simulation‐based learning method outside the clinical setting. 13 , 14 , 15 While many such games have shown promise, the field remains underresearched.

GridlockED, a serious board game, was developed by a team at McMaster University in 2017. 1 Across an “8‐hour shift” (i.e., eight rounds of the game, each round representing 1 h of clinical care within the game), players deploy preassigned ED professionals (ED physicians, residents, nurses, consultants, and radiologists) across different acuity zones to treat patients presenting with a variety of illnesses. Players also learn about the individualized care required for each patient and the scope of practice of each ED professional. Every hour, more patients enter the department, and players must optimize the distribution of limited clinical resources while simultaneously responding to unexpected positive and negative events (by drawing “event” cards) just as they would in the real ED. Each ED professional has a limited number of actions that they can carry out per hour, forcing players to prioritize sicker patients and maximize efficiency like in an actual ED. The goal is to finish the “8‐hour shift” with less than three patient safety events and 500 points (which are allocated once patients leave the ED). The original GridlockED game generally takes about 1–2 h to play fully, depending on the experience of the players. GridlockED has been formally play tested with medical students, residents, and emergency department physicians and nurses, with the results published in multiple academic journals. 1 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 It has received positive feedback and acclaim from both players and educators and has been selected as a top innovation both nationally and internationally. 20

ExpandED is a continuation of the scholarship that began with GridlockED. 1 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 21 While the premise of the game remains the same, ExpandED was designed as an expansion pack to the original game which allows players much more control over which health care professionals they can hire (Figure 1). While each game of ExpandED starts with a skeleton crew of one ED doctor, one resident, one radiologist, and one consultant, the players also start with a certain number of points to hire the rest of their staff depending on the difficulty level they select. Unlike original GridlockED where players could only hire additional ED physicians and registered nurses, ExpandED allows players to hire a larger variety of health professionals with the personnel budget depending on the level of difficulty selected. Players can select from ED doctors, ED residents, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, registered nurses, registered practical nurses (RPNs; nurses without a Bachelors of Nursing degree), occupational and physical therapists (combined into the mobility team to simulate the broad scope of practice in the ED), and social workers. There are also 28 more patient cards and 20 more event cards that showcase how a diverse team can be instrumental in efficiently handling more complex patients such as those who have concomitant medical, mobility, and social issues. For example, a social worker can take care of gathering collateral history about a patient presenting with psychosis, freeing up more actions for a physician who would otherwise have to perform this. The expert mobility team (occupational therapy/physical therapy) can reduce nursing workload by performing a walk test and home safety assessment.

ExpandED is played using the original GridlockED game; however, there are 28 additional patient cards and 20 additional event cards. Players can also more specifically determine the composition of their health care team through a points budget that allows them to hire ED doctors, ED residents, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, registered nurses, registered practical nurses, occupational therapists, and physical therapists (combined into the mobility team to simulate the broad scope of practice in the ED) and social workers. The hiring budget, number of admit to no bed patients, and new patients change based on preselected difficulty level.

Points are roughly equivalent to pay level (e.g., more points are required to hire an ED physician compared to a physician assistant), with responsibilities generally mirroring scope of practice in real life. For example, RNs can perform nursing actions in the resuscitation zone whereas RPNs are restricted to lower acuity zones. Physician assistants are limited to the lower acuity zones and residents must be supervised by an ED physician while in the resuscitation zone. The objective of this investigation was to evaluate the effectiveness of ExpandED as a teaching tool for medical and allied health students in better understanding interprofessional collaboration in the ED, using a program evaluation and play test framework.

METHODS

We conducted a mixed‐methods, program evaluation study consisting of two surveys: (1) a presimulation survey administered directly prior to playing the game and (2) a postsimulation survey that participants responded to directly after completing the game (see Appendixes S1 and S2 in the supplemental digital content). We analyzed the qualitative data using thematic analysis 22 and quantitative data via simple descriptive statistics and comparative statistics (e.g., paired t test).

Study setting and population

The study took place at McMaster University. Participants were recruited by mass email to the McMaster Program for Interprofessional Practice, Education and Research (PIPER), which encompasses all McMaster students in medicine, nursing, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, midwifery, bachelor of health sciences, physician assistant program, and child life studies trainees. Specifically, the study population consisted of individuals who self‐identify eligibility based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) 18 years of age and older; (2) studying in Hamilton; and (3) belong to one of the following student groups: medical students, nursing students (enrolled in a degree to become an RN, RPN, or NP), allied health students including physician assistants, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, social work, and speech language pathology.

Study protocol

This study received a HiREB exemption (No. 15812) after a full review by the student research ethics board. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, with participants choosing to self‐identify as being part of one of the eligible groups and indicating their interest in participating. Each participant was guided through and signed a standardized online consent form that outlines the purpose, risks, and benefits of the study prior to participation. The study design was executed as described below.

Key outcome measures

Key outcome measures included change in pre‐ and postgame difference in familiarity with functioning of an ED; difference in valuation of importance of interprofessional collaboration in the ED; difference in valuation of important of each health care practitioner in the ED; and participants free‐text descriptions of the usability, engagement, effectiveness as a teaching tool, incorporation of IPE, strengths, and weaknesses of the game.

Study design

All participants completed two surveys in this program evaluation study: a presimulation survey administered directly prior to playing the game (Appendix S1) and a postsimulation survey that participants responded to directly after completing the game (Appendix S2). The presimulation survey included questions regarding participant demographics, their level of training, familiarity with ED workflow, and roles of different professionals in the ED. The postsimulation survey questionnaire asked participants to rate the game on usability, engagement, effectiveness as a teaching tool, and incorporation of IPE. Free‐text options were also included for participants to provide feedback on the general experience as well as strengths and areas for improvement.

Participants were grouped into cohorts of two to four people based on convenience sampling. While we always aimed to have mixed groups of students, due to scheduling restrictions, this was not always accomplished. However, since the study facilitators were MD students themselves, there was an opportunity to engage in thinking about IPE at every session, since there was always at least two types of learners (including the student investigators). They played three to four turns of the original GridlockED game to become familiar with the concept. They then proceeded to the “boardroom” phase of the ExpandED expansion pack, where they chose a playing mode and selected their staff members with the allocated “money” (points) they were provided in that playing mode. Participants then played three to four turns of the expansion pack, which also included new patient and event cards. The objective of the game remained the same as the original GridlockED, which was to survive eight turns while accumulating more than 500 points without more than three patient safety incidents. As participants only played half of the game due to time constraints, the goal was to survive four turns while amassing more than 250 points, with no more than one patient safety incident.

Using a postpositivist paradigm, we conducted a mixed‐methods study that used pre‐ and post–game play survey data to generate our evaluation data. Based on prior studies by our group, a sample size required to reach theoretical data sufficiency was determined to be 20, on the basis of anticipated limitations with recruitment, response rates, interest in the study, and the time commitment (2–3 h) being a possible deterrent or barrier to participation. As the study was qualitative in nature, it was difficult to set an a priori sample size as there was no set number of participants to guarantee whether “enough” variety or diversity in perspectives had been collected. Our final sample size was 45 participants, although two individuals did not complete the pregame survey.

Data collection and analysis

Study data were collected through pre‐ and postgame surveys with both quantitative and qualitative components. The quantitative piece consisted of determining study participants’ perspectives of familiarity with the ED, importance of interprofessional collaboration, and the perceived value of individual professionals providing care with a series of Likert scale and yes/no/maybe questions. Statistical analysis of these responses both before and after game play was conducted using Microsoft Excel using both simple descriptive statistics and comparative statistics with parametric assumptions. The qualitative portion included free‐text fields for participants to describe their exposure to interprofessional collaboration prior to playing the game and feedback regarding general game play experience. These free‐text fields were compiled and coded by two members of the research team using a thematic analysis approach. 22 Line‐by‐line coding was performed on all data, followed by coding to identify and explore key themes. Anonymized notes were also taken by investigators about participant comments to capture information to improve game play (e.g., wording of instructions, clarifying rules) but were not formally included in data analysis.

Conflict mitigation

The authors did not receive any funding or sponsors in the conduct and reporting of the study. All proceeds from GridlockED and ExpandED are used to fund research in emergency medicine at McMaster University and to further develop the games. The authors do not financially profit in any way.

RESULTS

Demographics

Of all participants in group testing, the majority of students were medical students (n = 21), then followed by physiotherapy students (n = 17), nursing (n = 4), and speech language pathology (n = 1) as shown in Figure 2. The majority of students were intended to graduate within the next 2 years. A strong majority of participants (79%) had the opportunity to have completed at least one rotation in the ED and felt that they were somewhat familiar with the functioning of an ED. Participants’ familiarity with the ED significantly increased (p < 0.001) following gameplay as shown in Figure 3.

Perceptions of interprofessional collaboration

Participants rated their valuation of the importance of interprofessional collaboration in the ED both before and after gameplay on a scale of 1–7. The valuation increased from 6.2 (pregame) to 6.4 (postgame; p = 0.17) and was not statistically significant. Although this finding was not statistically significant, the high valuation of interprofessional collaboration in the presimulation survey suggests that the participating students had preexisting positivity toward team‐based, interprofessional collaboration in the ED setting. Analysis of data collected through the pre‐ and postgame surveys revealed valuable insights into the utility and applicability of ExpandED in teaching interprofessional collaboration and ED dynamics to medical and allied health students.

Most participants had some exposure to interprofessional collaboration, mainly through mandated curricular events. Participants reported high satisfaction levels with ExpandED and reported increased familiarity with ED workflow and the roles of allied health professionals. Participants expressed concerns about the game's focus on task completion over patient details, suggesting improvements such as including patient case details on the back of cards to enhance learning outcomes. These findings lay the groundwork for a comprehensive discussion on ExpandEDs effectiveness as well as potential enhancements.

Pregame survey

The primary theme extracted from the pregame survey responses was that most students had some degree of exposure to interprofessional collaboration, although largely through mandated curricular events in their respective programs. Some examples of this included communication skills, team‐building sessions, code blue, and serious play workshops. Students who had participated in a clinical placement also identified “interprofessional team meetings,” “family meetings,” and “physician directed rounds” as sources of exposure. However, only three respondents identified having attended multiple events, with answers generally being limited to one or two examples. In particular, the most popular event recorded was a “case study” in which attendees worked through a patient scenario with student representation from different programs including medicine, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech language pathology and midwifery. Most participants attended small‐group simulation or discussion‐based IPE events that were interactive in nature, although two reported attending more didactic lecture‐based sessions. Overall, the data demonstrated that student engagement with IPE was limited and largely driven by program requirements. Similarly, many of the participants were early in their training, limiting their practical understanding of interprofessional collaboration in a health care setting. The use of a serious game was therefore a novel approach when compared to ongoing initiatives and may help in furthering both participants’ knowledge base and interest in IPE beyond mandated events.

Postgame survey

Responses were found to be centered around satisfaction, group dynamics, player roles, and attentiveness to individual patient details. Of 45 responses, 29 expressed a high level of satisfaction with their overall experience, calling the game “very fun,” “well developed,” and “unique.” Multiple participants, especially those with some clinical experience, commented positively on the general accuracy of the game in simulating the ED environment. Others found that they had developed a better understanding of the structure and workflow in an ED, and the value of interdisciplinary involvement in facilitating effective discharge planning (see Table 1 for comments). This indicates that ExpandED serves as an important addition to GridlockED in teaching learners about not only ED workflow, but specifically the roles filled by the diverse health professionals in providing patient care in the department. Indeed, participants reported a significant increase in familiarity with the ED pre and post gameplay (p < 0.001). However, their perception of the significance of interprofessional collaboration and teamwork remained largely consistent before and after playing the game (p = 0.17), suggesting a ceiling effect driven by high positive perceptions of the importance of interprofessional collaboration prior to this learning experience.

The role of effective collaboration and fostering a strong team dynamic was highlighted in multiple responses. Participants identified that learning to play the game together enhanced their gameplay experience, and that “hav[ing] other people's perspectives” was useful when making decisions on how to triage patients and utilize personnel accordingly. However, seven participants (16%) noted increased tension among group members associated with an increasing difficulty level of the game and found the process “hectic” and “high stress” and felt that their fellow team members were “anxious about time” and “critical of most choices.” While this may have been somewhat attributable to external factors (e.g., limited time/space to run the actual event), it is reflective of the challenges of working together in a fast‐paced environment with patients who may be complex and evolving in their presentation.

In general, responses indicated that the scope of responsibilities assigned to each professional was accurate and appreciated the wide variety of personnel to choose from compared to GridlockED. Some participants felt that there was redundancy in allowing for multiple professionals to complete the same task. While this was more a practical choice on the team's part to better integrate the expansion pack cards with the existing card deck, it highlights an important aspect of tasks in real‐life health care environments being highly specific to each provider without much crossover. Respondents wished to see occupational therapists, physiotherapists, and social workers take on a more prominent role too.

The last theme highlighted a lack of attentiveness to patient details due to the objective of checking off tasks for each patient. Participants felt that the game could “devolve into matching colors” rather than developing an understanding of the actions taking place, especially when playing more advanced modes with higher patient volumes to manage. Others indicated that having dedicated time to read each patient case in detail prior to initiating decision making on how to use their personnel would be beneficial for their learning.

DISCUSSION

This mixed‐methods program evaluation study shows that the ExpandED extension is a promising expansion to the existing GridlockED game. The satisfaction data from our study suggest that the ExpandED expansion pack can be useful in highlighting aspects of interprofessional collaboration in the setting of a serious analog game, it is imperative that individuals develop effective communication and delegation skills to foster a strong team environment while maintaining patient safety. Although the groups within this study population were a convenience sampling of participants and only played together once, ExpandED may serve as an even more effective tool for groups who collaborate on an ongoing basis to learn how to build group dynamics and navigate conflict in a low‐stakes, simulated setting. Compared to the original GridlockED study 1 and our first program evaluation study of GridlockED, 17 our present findings continue to show continued positive learner experiences with regards to how the game represents the complexity of emergency medicine and ED‐based care.

With regard to roles and responsibilities of characters represented in ExpandED, our participants clearly felt that the ExpandED version of the game provided a broader representation of the health care providers who work in the ED. Interestingly, one area where there was particular overlap was between physician assistants and residents; many participants felt that physician assistants being more expensive (a choice made based on the differences in hiring between the two professions in real life) did not incentivize their utilization within the game. Moving forward, it would be important to have input from physician assistants themselves to better define their scope of practice within the ED context. The team's initial thought process when creating patient presentations was that these physician assistants are not always involved in ED patient care; however, another important future consideration would be to receive more diverse stakeholder feedback and ensure that professionals are adequately differentiated from each other. The learner participants flagged that it may be helpful to create more patients who would require social work, occupational therapy, and physical therapy intervention. The team is seriously considering this feedback though we are cognizant to anchor this to the reality of ED care rather than the care of admitted patients who are boarded in the ED as there is not yet room for them in the inpatient units.

Similar feedback was also received in previous iterations of the game. 1 , 16 Bearing in mind that our primary goal was not to teach specific approaches to patient presentations, the importance of using other more traditional methods to teach about case‐specific interprofessional care (e.g., case rounds, interprofessional rounds, chart review) to elucidate the importance of interprofessional health care services in the ED cannot be understated. With regard to design considerations to help facilitate this, one suggestion would be to include placing patient cards face down on the board with case details listed on the back, thus requiring players to read each patient card before proceeding with the game.

LIMITATIONS

There are various limitations owing to participant sampling and the qualitative nature of this study. Although recruitment was done through email via the McMaster Interprofessional Education Portal (reaching all McMaster medicine, nursing, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, midwifery, bachelor of health sciences, physician assistants, child life studies students) to be accessible to students across all disciplines, the final study sample was significantly skewed toward medical students and physiotherapy students, which impacts the generalizability of findings. In addition, participants only played the game for one session lasting approximately 2 h; as such, it is difficult to make judgments on whether this significantly enhanced their knowledge of interprofessional collaboration given limited exposure. Multiple sessions with the same participants would be effective in assessing whether their understanding and comfort working with multidisciplinary teams grows over time. Lastly, the study team was composed entirely of medical students, which invited potential bias during the gameplay sessions and analysis of survey data. While efforts were made to offset this by having different combinations of the study team running sessions together, in addition to utilizing multiple reviewers and following a systematic thematic coding approach, it is possible that participants’ comments may have been influenced by subjective differences in experience.

CONCLUSIONS

Despite the limitations described, this investigation represents an important first step in showing the utility of ExpandED as an interactive tool to teach junior learners from a variety of health care disciplines about the value of interdisciplinary collaboration in a modern ED. Moreover, participants provided a significant amount of verbal and written feedback about potential improvements to the expansion pack to increase playability, fidelity, and pedagogical value. These insights will be used to further refine and develop the expansion pack before it is available publicly. Future investigations could include studying ExpandED with fully qualified ED health care workers who work regularly in the ED as well as adding further important professionals such as respiratory therapists or other members of the ED support/administrative team.
