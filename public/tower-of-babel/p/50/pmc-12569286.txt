Genome-wide identification, phylogenetic analysis and expression pattern study of trihelix transcription factors in Pisum sativum L.
Authors: Xiaozong Wu, Zhiwen Zhu, Zhihou Chen, Xiaodan Xie, Zhilian Liu, Lei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrihelix transcription factors have essential functions in plant development, growth, and coping with environmental stresses. Peas (Pisum sativum) are an important economic crop, rich in protein themselves, and their stems and leaves also serve as high-quality green manure and forage. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the trihelix gene family in the pea, identifying a total of 56 PsTrihelix genes. These genes exhibited significant diversity in structure and were classified into six subfamilies based on their homology with Arabidopsis thaliana. Additionally, we found that trihelix genes in the pea were most densely distributed on chromosome 6, with segmental duplication events further confirming the importance of chromosome 6. In terms of gene structure, most PsTrihelix genes contained few introns, although two genes contained a higher number of introns, and the relationship between motif number and exon number was complex. Through evolutionary and collinearity analysis with other plants, we found that pea and tomato exhibit the highest number of collinear Trihelix genes, indicating a closer evolutionary relationship between the two species. Regarding functional prediction, trihelix genes were highly expressed in pea leaf and bean tissues and exhibited significant expression changes under specific stresses and hormone treatments, suggesting their important roles in plant growth, development, and adaptation strategies. Particularly, the PsTrihelix55 showed significant upregulation under multiple stress treatments, making it a promising candidate gene for further study. These results offer valuable perspectives on the role and evolutionary history of the trihelix gene family in peas, and lay the groundwork for subsequent studies in this field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21438-z.Keywords: Pea, Trihelix transcription factor, Evolution, Genome-wide identificationSubject terms: Functional genomics, Bioinformatics

Introduction

The pea (Pisum sativum L., 2n = 2x = 14) is a leguminous crop that serves as an important source of dietary protein and is highly valued for its nutritional and agricultural significance1. Additionally, peas are utilized as fodder and green manure crops. Due to its genetic characteristics and ease of reproduction, the pea has emerged as a significant model plant for genetic and plant biology research2,3. Transcription factors are a class of proteins that regulate the transcription process of genes, thereby influencing gene expression. They activate or repress transcription by binding to specific DNA sequences, including promoters, enhancers, and repressors4,5. Trihelix transcription factors constitute a distinct class of transcription factors named after their characteristic triple-helix structure6. They contain one or more highly conserved trihelix domains, also known as GT domains. The trihelix transcription factor is categorized into five subfamilies, namely GT-1, GT-2, GT-γ, SH4, and SIP1. The typical structure of this family is characterized by three α-helices that are organized into a stable triple-helix bundle7. The central helix (H2) is usually the longest, while the two flanking helices (H1 and H3) are shorter. This structure enables trihelix transcription factors to bind to DNA and participate in transcriptional regulation. The typical length of the trihelix domain is 70–80 amino acids. The DNA binding mechanism of trihelix transcription factor members is unique, with the highly conserved motif in the H2 helix recognizing and binding to specific bases in the DNA sequence. Although the H1 and H3 helices do not directly exhibit sequence specificity in DNA binding, they stabilize the overall triple-helix bundle structure and interact with other proteins8–10.

Trihelix genes have been extensively identified across a wide range of plant species, including Oryza sativa11, Arabidopsis thaliana12, Solanum lycopersicum13, Glycine max14, Triticum aestivum15, Malus pumila16, Zea mays17, Salix matsudana18, Phyllostachys edulis19, Sorghum bicolor20 and Fagopyrum tataricum21. These studies have revealed that trihelix transcription factors in plants not only regulate the expression of light-responsive genes but also play crucial roles in plant growth and development. Additionally, they are widely involved in plant responses to abiotic stresses such as high salt concentrations, drought, and cold. In Arabidopsis, the trihelix transcription factor GT-2-like 1 (GTL1) regulates the expression of genes related to salicylic acid (SA) synthesis and transport, thereby positively regulating immune responses induced by bacteria22. Another Arabidopsis trihelix transcription factor, AtGT-3b, induces the expression of the calmodulin gene, SCaM-4, in soybeans through interactions with other transcription factors in response to pathogen infection and salt stress23. Under high salinity conditions, the Arabidopsis GT-4 protein binds to the GT element within the promoter of the Cor15 gene and interacts with the TEM2 transcription factor. This interaction collectively upregulates the expression of the Cor15 gene, thereby enhancing the salt tolerance of transgenic Arabidopsis24. The soybean trihelix transcription factor GmGT-2 regulates the expression of auxin-responsive genes by modulating the expression of the Aux28 gene from the auxin/indole-3-acetic acid (Aux/IAA) family. The expression level of the GmGT-2 gene and the binding ability of the GmGT-2 protein to GT elements are both regulated by light25. These findings underscore the multifaceted roles of trihelix transcription factors in both plant development and stress responses, thereby illuminating their potential for enhancing crop resilience and guiding future breeding programs aimed at stress tolerance.

In this study, we utilized bioinformatics analysis to identify trihelix transcription factors in the pea cultivar Zhongwan 6 from publicly available genomic databases. We conducted a comprehensive genomic analysis to explore the structural characteristics, evolutionary relationships, motif composition, and chromosomal localization of the trihelix family in peas. Subsequently, we analyzed the expression patterns of trihelix transcription factors in different pea organs and their expression levels under stress conditions and hormone responses using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). These results provide valuable insights into the role and evolutionary history of the trihelix transcription factor family in peas and further reveal the mechanisms by which this family regulates pea growth, development, and environmental responses. This research also holds significant application value for the breeding of high-quality new pea varieties.

Materials and methods

Gene identification

The complete genome sequence of Pisum sativum was downloaded from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/), whereas the whole-genome sequence of Arabidopsis thaliana was downloaded from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) web-site (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The selected genome version was CAAS_Psat_ZW6_1.0. This genome integrates multiple assembly strategies, demonstrating excellent accuracy and completeness, as well as precise annotation26. The BLASTp method27 was employed to identify protein sequences in the downloaded genome, and the hidden Markov model (HMM) for the MYB/SANT-like domain was obtained. Initially, all trihelix genes in the pea were screened using the protein sequences of Arabidopsis trihelix proteins as references (with a score value ≥ 100 and an e-value ≤ 1e-10). Subsequently, the HMM of MYB/SANT-like (PF13837) was downloaded from the Pfam database28 (https://pfam.xfam.org/) and aligned using HMMER 3.029 (e-value < 0.01, domain coverage ≥ 60%) to remove redundancy (http://plants.ensembl.org/hmmer/index.html). The selected trihelix genes were confirmed using the SMART tool (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/). Through this identification process, 56 trihelix genes were identified. To analyze the structural features of the trihelix genes, the ExPasy tool (https://www.expasy.org/) was employed to determine their physicochemical properties, including sequence length, isoelectric point (pI), and molecular weight (MW). Additionally, the WoLF PSORT tool (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) was used to predict the subcellular localization of the selected trihelix genes.

Trihelix gene structure, chromosomal distribution, and gene duplication events

The Gene Structure Display Server (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) was utilized to analyze the distribution structure of introns and exons in the genes. The MEME Suite30 online program (https://nbcr.net/meme/) was employed to identify motifs distributed within the genes, with parameters set to ten motifs and an optimal width of six to two hundred residues. The Circos program31 was used to map the trihelix genes onto different chromosomes, while the MCScanX toolkit32 was utilized to analyze gene duplication events in the PsTrihelix gene family. After identification by MCScanX, whole-genome visualization validation was performed via Circos. Manual inspection of gene spacing was conducted to exclude tandem repeats. For each predicted segmental duplication event, structural genomic features were analyzed, including conservation of intron-exon architecture and traces of transposable element insertions, to filter retrotransposition events.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of the PsTrihelix gene family

Using Arabidopsis trihelix genes as homologous sequences, the evolutionary tree of the 56 PsTrihelix genes was constructed using GeneDoc 2.7.0 and MEGA7.0 software33, with parameters set to a bootstrap value of 1000, the genus R11 assignment, and the BLOSUM62 cost matrix. The PsTrihelix genes were classified into five subfamilies based on this analysis. Similarly, evolutionary trees were constructed for the pea in comparison with Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, Fagopyrum tataricum, Zea mays, Oryza sativa and Sorghum bicolor using the same methods.

Plant materials, growth conditions, and different abiotic stresses in the pea

Peas are widely cultivated in China, and, for this experiment, we used the Zhongwan 6 variety, provided by Hebei Agricultural University’s Li Long. Peas were grown in a growth chamber with a substrate mixture of soil and vermiculite (1:1) in 10 × 10 cm pots. The temperature in the growth chamber was set to 25 °C for 16 h during the day and 20 °C for 8 h at night, and the relative humidity was 75%. Samples were collected when the peas reached 45 days of age. Five uniform plants were selected for sampling, with each plant divided into five parts: roots, stems, leaves, peas, and pods. Fruit were sampled when the peas began to form seeds, with samples collected at weekly intervals for a total of five weeks: on days 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35. Each sample was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction.

Twenty representative trihelix genes were selected to investigate their expression patterns under various stress and hormone treatments. Seedlings were subjected to the following stress treatments: salt (5% NaCl), drought (30% PEG 6000), heat (40 °C), and cold (4 °C). Samples were collected at 0, 2, and 12 h after treatment. Similarly, seedlings were subjected to hormone treatments: 250 µmol/L ABA, 250 µmol/L IAA, 250 µmol/L GA, 500 µmol/L JA, and 500 µmol/L SA. Samples were collected at 0, 1, 4, and 12 h after treatment. Each treatment was performed with five replicates.

Total RNA extraction, cDNA reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the collected samples using an RNA extraction kit (Vazyme) purchased from Novozymes. The RNA concentration was normalized to 1 mg for reverse transcription to construct cDNA libraries for subsequent quantification. Beacon Designer 7 was used to design quantitative primers to detect the expression levels of trihelix genes in different samples (Table. S5). At each time point/for each organization, three biological replicates were set up. The stable expressed gene of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) in pea was selected as the control and used as the internal reference for gene expression levels. The expression levels of the trihelix genes in different samples were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, and the condition |ΔΔCt| ≥ 1 was set34.

Results

The identification of trihelix genes in the pea

BLASTp was performed using Arabidopsis trihelix protein sequences as queries to identify putative trihelix genes in the pea genome. Candidate genes were further screened using the HMMER 3.0 program with the MYB/SANT-like domain (PF13837) HMM profile as the query. The selected trihelix genes were confirmed using the SMART tool. A total of 56 trihelix proteins were identified in the pea. They were named Trihelix-1 to Trihelix-56. These trihelix proteins exhibited uneven distribution across seven pea chromosomes. The lengths of coding sequences for trihelix genes ranged from 333 to 2994 bp, while the corresponding trihelix proteins exhibited lengths varying from 111 amino acids (Trihelix21) to 998 amino acids (Trihelix5). The molecular masses of these proteins spanned from 13.10 to 113.38 kDa, and their isoelectric points ranged from 4.50 to 9.85. Subcellular localization analysis revealed that these trihelix proteins were predominantly localized in the nucleus, with a smaller proportion distributed in the cytoplasm, chloroplasts, and mitochondria, with counts of 43, 22, and 13, respectively (Table. S1).

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of PsTrihelix genes via multiple sequence alignment

To further elucidate the evolutionary relationships within the trihelix gene family, an unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed MEGA 7.0 software, based on homologous sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana. As shown in the Fig. 1, all trihelix transcription factors were classified into six subfamilies (GT-1, GT-2, SH4, GT-γ, SIP1and UC), with the number of trihelix genes in the pea being twice that found in Arabidopsis (Fig. 1). This result is consistent with previous analyses of trihelix gene numbers, suggesting that the pea may have more trihelix genes due to tandem duplication events during evolution. Interestingly, the number of trihelix genes in subfamily GT-2 was twice that in Arabidopsis. The numbers of trihelix genes in subfamilies SIP, SH4, and GT-1 were consistent between the pea and Arabidopsis, while the highest number of trihelix genes (25) in the pea was from the subfamily GT-γ: eight times that in Arabidopsis. Most genes were clustered into different subfamilies, indicating high homology between trihelix proteins in the pea and Arabidopsis. However, PsTrihelix25 and PsTrihelix31 do not belong to any of the five main subfamilies. Through manual inspection and literature review, we found that Kuzmitskaya et al.16 classified the apple trihelix transcription factor family into six subfamilies in their study. The MdTH17 and MdTH35 from the GT-δ subfamily exhibited conserved motifs and gene structures nearly identical to PsTrihelix25 and PsTrihelix31 in this study. Similar observations have been reported in other plant species. Therefore, based on the analysis of the conservative structure, cross-species homology, and phylogenetic analysis, PsTrihelix25 and PsTrihelix31 should be classified as members of the GT-δ subfamily of the trihelix transcription factor family. This subfamily is absent in Arabidopsis thaliana but is retained through gene duplication and functional differentiation in species such as apple and pea, and may be involved in lineage-specific biological processes. This discovery in peas also reflects the complexity of the evolution of the plant transcription factor family.

Conserved motif identification and gene structure analysis of PsTrihelix genes

We utilized cDNA and genomic DNA sequence information and employed the Gene Structure Display Server to construct a schematic representation of the structures of the PsTrihelix genes, aiming to better understand their structural evolution. The PsTrihelix gene family exhibited structural diversity, and, based on sequence differences, we classified the 56 trihelix genes into six subfamilies using a single unrooted phylogenetic tree. The exon number in trihelix genes varied from one to seventeen, with seven genes being intronless. The majority of these genes contained a limited number of introns. Members within the same subfamily tended to have similar exon-intron distribution patterns, with SIP1 subfamily members consistently containing fewer than two introns, indicating relative conservation within this subfamily during evolution. Interestingly, PsTrihelix25 and PsTrihelix31 had the highest number of exons (17), suggesting functional diversity. Additionally, we analyzed ten conserved motifs in trihelix transcription factors using the MEME Suite online tool and named them motif 1-motif 10. As shown, some motifs were highly conserved within subfamilies. The subfamily GT-γ contained the highest number of motifs, with most genes containing motif 6 and motif 7, indicating the functional importance of these motifs within the trihelix gene family. The distribution of motifs at the N-terminus was variable, while motif 6 was consistently located at the C-terminus. Subfamilies GT-1, GT-2, SH4, GT-δ, and SIP1 generally contained motif 6 and motif 4 (Fig. 2).

Chromosomal distribution and gene duplication of PsTrihelix genes

Through analysis of the sequencing information of the pea genome, we revealed the chromosomal distribution of the 56 trihelix genes (Fig. 3A). These genes were randomly distributed across seven chromosomes, with a notably higher density observed in specific regions of chromosome 6. Specifically, chromosome 4 harbored the fewest genes, with three present, while chromosome 6 contained the highest number, with fifteen genes. Interestingly, both chromosome 1 and chromosome 3 contained eight genes each. This uneven distribution suggests genetic variations during evolution.

(A). Physical distribution of PsTrihelix genes among 7 chromosomes. (B). Schematic representation of the fragment duplication pattern of the PsTrihelix genes. The red lines indicate the duplicated segments of the PsTrihelix genes.

To elucidate the genome structure and evolutionary mechanisms, we analyzed the arrangement of genes on chromosomes to gain insights into genomic duplication events and the expansion of gene families. We identified nine pairs of trihelix genes that underwent segmental duplication. As depicted, a total of 18 homologous genes were identified, accounting for approximately 32.1% of the total, with no tandem duplication events detected. These segmental duplication events were unevenly distributed across six chromosomes (excluding chromosome 2). The highest number of segmental duplications was observed on LG6 (five), while the lowest numbers were found on LG4 and LG5, with one each. Despite PsTrihelix7 and PsTrihelix53 being located on different chromosomes, they shared the closest relationship with PsTrihelix34. PsTrihelix46 and PsTrihelix41, originating from the same chromosome, formed a segmental duplication pair. All segmental duplications originated from the same subfamily, with the GT-2 subfamily having the highest number of segmental duplications, while the SIP2 subfamily contained three pairs of segmental duplicate genes, and GT-γ contained two pairs of segmental duplicate genes (Fig. 3B, Table. S2).

The evolutionary analysis of PsTrihelix genes and trihelix genes from other species

To elucidate the evolutionary history and relationships among genomes of different species, we performed inter-species collinearity analysis with Pisum sativum using representative species from both dicotyledonous (A. thaliana, S. lycopersicum, and F. tataricum) and monocotyledonous plants (O. sativa, Z. mays and S. bicolor). As depicted in the figure, the highest number of collinear genes was observed between PsTrihelix genes in the pea and tomato, with 30 genes identified. In contrast, the lowest number of genes collinear with pea genes was found in rice and maize, with 5 genes identified. Compared to monocotyledonous plants, the pea exhibited a higher number of collinear genes with other dicotyledonous plants, approximately four to six times more. Interestingly, we found genes collinear with PsTrihelix8, PsTrihelix13, PsTrihelix29, and PsTrihelix52 in all the monocotyledonous plants investigated. Additionally, PsTrihelix8 was collinearly distributed with AT1G61215.1/Zm00001d006606_T001/OQU90106/Os07t0565300-01/Solyc02g078810.3.1, suggesting the conservation of these genes during evolution (Fig. 4, Table. S3).

Expression patterns of the PsTrihelix gene in different developmental stages and different organs of pea plants

We investigated the expression profiles of trihelix genes in different developmental stages and different organs of pea plants using qRT-PCR technology35,36. We examined the expression patterns of trihelix genes across various developmental stages of peas to elucidate their regulatory mechanisms in seed development. The PsTrihelix gene shows significant differential expression at different stages of seed development. Most PsTrihelix genes showed upregulation followed by downregulation, with the greatest number of genes (nine) upregulated at 14 days. In contrast, one gene showed high expression levels at day 35, suggesting that trihelix genes primarily regulate early growth and development in the pea (Fig. 6). We also examined the expression levels of PsTrihelix genes in pods, where most genes exhibited a continuous increase in expression levels. At day 35, the greatest number of trihelix genes (nine) exhibited high expression levels in pods (PsTrihelix24, PsTrihelix25, PsTrihelix26, PsTrihelix29, PsTrihelix31, PsTrihelix36, PsTrihelix40, PsTrihelix45 and PsTrihelix53) (Fig. 6). Notably, during different growth stages, the expression levels of the PsTrihelix gene in pea fruits and bean pods show completely opposite trends (Table S6A).

As depicted in the figure, most genes exhibited expression across different tissues, with significant variations in expression levels. Particularly, trihelix genes showed high expression levels in leaves and bean tissues, while they exhibited low expression levels in pods. These genes were classified into five subfamilies, with all genes showing the lowest expression levels in roots. Seven genes (PsTrihelix26, PsTrihelix36, PsTrihelix38, PsTrihelix39, PsTrihelix41, PsTrihelix54 and PsTrihelix55) showed the highest expression levels in pea fruit, while four genes (PsTrihelix24, PsTrihelix31, PsTrihelix40 and PsTrihelix53) showed the highest expression levels in leaf. Three genes (PsTrihelix9, PsTrihelix23, PsTrihelix29) showed the highest expression levels in stem and root tissues, and one gene (PsTrihelix32 and PsTrihelix45) showed the highest expression levels in pods (Fig. 6 and Table S6B).

Expression patterns of PsTrihelix genes under different treatment conditions

Previous studies have indicated the involvement of trihelix genes in plant stress responses. To validate this hypothesis, we subjected pea seedlings to four types of stress treatments (NaCl, drought, heat, and cold) to determine which trihelix genes were involved in stress responses. Overall, under different stress handling conditions, the expression levels of most genes have undergone significant changes. Particularly, under heat treatment, most genes exhibited continuous upregulation, with PsTrihelix55 showing the highest expression level, especially in roots. However, some genes (PsTrihelix3 and PsTrihelix32) showed initial upregulation followed by downregulation, while PsTrihelix31 exhibited continuous downregulation. Under cold stress, PsTrihelix26, PsTrihelix29, and PsTrihelix31 exhibited opposite expression patterns. Most of the genes reached their maximum expression levels 12 h after treatment. Interestingly, some genes, such as PsTrihelix3, PsTrihelix9, PsTrihelix13, PsTrihelix24, PsTrihelix36, PsTrihelix40, PsTrihelix54, and PsTrihelix55, exhibited similar expression patterns in response to both cold and heat stress (Fig. 7 and Table S6C).

Additionally, we investigated the influence of hormones on the expression of trihelix genes by treating pea seedlings with five hormones (Indole-3-acetic acid, IAA; jasmonic acid, JA; salicylic acid, SA; gibberellin, GA; and abscisic acid, ABA). The results indicated that under hormone treatment, nearly all genes exhibited significant differential expression, with the majority of them showing upregulated expression. PsTrihelix3 exhibited the highest expression level under GA, IAA, and ABA treatments, while PsTrihelix45 showed the highest expression level under SA treatment. PsTrihelix31 exhibited the highest expression level under JA treatment. However, some genes showed significant downregulation, such as PsTrihelix54, which exhibited low expression levels under all five hormone treatments, suggesting that PsTrihelix54 may not be influenced by hormones. PsTrihelix55 showed down regulation under ABA treatment, while PsTrihelix25, PsTrihelix41, PsTrihelix47, and PsTrihelix54 exhibited negative regulation under SA treatment (Fig. 8 and Table S6D).

Discussion

PsTrihelix gene structure and evolutionary analyses

With the continuous development of bioinformatics, the genome of the pea has been sequenced, providing a foundation for comprehensive analysis of the trihelix gene family in peas26. In the study, a total of 56 PsTrihelix genes were identified, all of which exhibited significant structural differences, indicating the high complexity of the trihelix family. This explains the differences in the length of the encoded trihelix proteins, ranging from 111 amino acids (Trihelix21) to 998 amino acids (Trihelix5), as well as the variation in molecular mass (from 13.1 to 113.38 kDa). Based on homology with Arabidopsis, the PsTrihelix genes can be classified into five main subfamilies (GT-1, GT-2, SH4, GT-γ, and SIP1), each containing a homologous trihelix gene from Arabidopsis. This suggests that most trihelix genes in peas have been conserved during evolution, with no apparent loss of genes. Additionally, the number of trihelix genes in peas is twice that in Arabidopsis, which we speculate may be due to the larger genome of peas compared to Arabidopsis, possibly resulting from gene family expansion events. We also found that two PsTrihelix genes were not classified into any of these five subfamilies when compared with the homologous sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana. After analysis, it was discovered that these two genes shared a large number of similarities with the genes in the GT-δ subfamily of the apple trihelix family. Therefore, PsTrihelix25 and PsTrihelix31 were classified into the GT-δ subfamily.

The 56 trihelix genes in the pea are randomly distributed across seven chromosomes, with chromosome 6 exhibiting a notably high density of trihelix genes (15). Under specific environmental conditions or biological processes, trihelix genes may exhibit higher adaptability and functional diversity, thus being preserved in specific regions. During gene duplication events, we identified nine pairs of trihelix genes undergoing segmental duplication. Consistent with previous speculation, chromosome 6 had the highest number of segmental duplications (five pairs) on LG6, further validating its importance. Among these, the GT-2 subfamily harbored the highest number of segmental duplications. Moreover, the occurrence of segmental duplications within the same subfamily aligned with the results of gene expansion.

Previous studies have shown that trihelix genes generally contain fewer introns. In our analysis of PsTrihelix gene structure and evolution, most genes did indeed contain a small number of introns (less than three). However, PsTrihelix25 and PsTrihelix31 contained a higher number of introns (16). Contradictorily, these two genes had the fewest motifs (motif 4 and motif 6), suggesting the conservation of these two motifs. Meanwhile, the results indicated a complex relationship between the number of exons and motifs. However, even a small number of motif sequences can play crucial regulatory roles.

For evolutionary and interspecies collinearity analysis, we selected representative species from both dicotyledonous plants (Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, and Fagopyrum tataricum) and monocotyledonous plants (Zea mays, Sorghum bicolor, and Oryza sativa) to compare with Pisum sativum. The analysis revealed that these seven species were divided into five subfamilies, with different subfamilies exhibiting similar motif structures. The GT-2 subfamily possessed the most motifs, indicating structural and functional diversity. There were differences in phylogenetic relationships among different subfamilies and species. The SH4 subfamily in the pea clustered with that found in tomato, while the GT-γ subfamily showed the closest relationship with that found in sorghum.

During collinearity analysis, it was observed that the pea shared more collinear genes with other dicots compared to monocots, suggesting a closer evolutionary relationship with other dicots. As a dicotyledonous plant, peas share more common evolutionary features and genomic compositions with other dicots. Furthermore, collinear genes such as PsTrihelix8, PsTrihelix13, PsTrihelix29, and PsTrihelix52 were identified across monocots and dicots, indicating their conservation throughout evolution.

Expression patterns and functional prediction of PsTrihelix genes

Plant gene expression analysis is crucial for identifying genes involved in stress responses, signaling pathways, and plant interactions, providing valuable data for studying plant growth, development, and adaptation strategies37. We selected 15 representative genes from the six subfamilies to explore their expression patterns in specific tissues and during different fruit developmental stages. The results revealed that trihelix genes exhibited high expression levels in leaves and cotyledons while showing low expression levels in pods. This expression pattern may be related to the specific physiological and functional roles of these tissues. Leaves are the main organs for photosynthesis in plants, converting light energy into chemical energy and synthesizing organic compounds. The high expression levels of trihelix genes in leaves may be involved in the regulation of photosynthesis, leaf development, and leaf specialization. Conversely, the low expression levels in pods may be associated with the specific physiological processes and functions of pods. PsTrihelix26, PsTrihelix36, PsTrihelix38, PsTrihelix39, PsTrihelix41, PsTrihelix54, and PsTrihelix55 showed the highest expression levels in pods, suggesting their involvement in pea growth and development.

Previous studies have found a specific gene in the GT-1 subfamily in Arabidopsis (At5g63420) and rice (LOC_Os02g33610), which was expressed in the vegetative tissues of Arabidopsis and was particularly highly expressed in seeds38. PsTrihelix55 belongs to the GT-1 subfamily, and, as a homologous gene, we speculate that it may have similar functions. PsTrihelix24, PsTrihelix31, PsTrihelix40, and PsTrihelix53 showed the highest expression levels in leaf tissues. A chimeric gene was identified in the SIP1 subfamily, which was present in both Arabidopsis (At3g10030) and rice (Os04g33300), and its putative parent kinase genes were also present in these two species (At3g18680 and Os01g73450)39,40. PsTrihelix24 and PsTrihelix53, as homologous genes highly expressed in leaves, may have similar functions.

Furthermore, we treated peas with various stresses (NaCl, drought, heat, and cold) and hormones (IAA, JA, SA, GA, and ABA) to gain deeper insights into pea adaptation strategies. Overall, most genes showed significant changes in expression levels under different stress treatments. PsTrihelix3, PsTrihelix9, PsTrihelix13, PsTrihelix24, PsTrihelix36, PsTrihelix40, PsTrihelix54 and PsTrihelix55 exhibited similar expression patterns under cold and heat stress, suggesting they play important roles in plant stress responses and adaptation mechanisms. In Arabidopsis, GT-3a and GT-3b belong to the GT-1 subfamily. The biological role of GT-3b has been elucidated under salt and pathogen stress. GT-3b exhibits high expression levels in response to 150 mM NaCl and infection by Pseudomonas syringae41,42. In poplar, PtaGTL1 functions as a Cat-CaM binding protein, modulating stomatal density to enhance drought tolerance43. Interestingly, PsTrihelix55, as a homologous gene, showed significant upregulation under all four stress treatments, revealing its importance in the stress response. In cotton, the trihelix family SH4 subfamily regulatory genes responded to drought, low temperature, high salt, and ABA treatments44. PsTrihelix47, belonging to the SH4 subfamily, also showed significant expression under stress treatments, indicating its involvement in regulation. OsGTγ−2 in rice directly binds the salt-induced GT-1 motif (GAAAAA) in the OsRAV2 promoter. Its expression is induced by high salt and ABA. Overexpression of OsGTγ−2 significantly enhances seed germination, seedling growth, and survival under salt stress; conversely, knockout mutants exhibit salt-sensitive phenotypes. These results demonstrate that OsGTγ−2 acts as a positive regulator in rice salt stress response45,46. PsTrihelix9, PsTrihelix32, and PsTrihelix39, as homologous genes, also participate in regulating stress responses. Additionally, we found that these genes are involved in ABA regulation, showing significant upregulation under hormone treatments, further validating the function of the GT-γ subfamily. AtGTL1, a member of the GT-2 subfamily in Arabidopsis, is involved in the AtMPK4 signaling cascade and coordinates salicylic acid metabolism, conferring immunity against Pseudomonas syringae47. However, under SA treatment, PsTrihelix41, belonging to the same family, exhibited a downregulation trend, suggesting its potential negative regulation of plant disease resistance, but further verification is required. Yu et al. cloned the cold-induced gene SlCIGT from wild tomato in the GT-1 subfamily, which is constitutively expressed in various tissues and induced by ABA and various abiotic stresses. Overexpression of SlCIGT enhances tomato resistance to cold and drought, and reduces sensitivity to ABA. SlCIGT may interact with SnRK1 to enhance tomato resistance to abiotic stresses48. PsTrihelix55 exhibited downregulation under ABA treatment and upregulation under SA treatment. Additionally, PsTrihelix55 showed significant upregulation under all four stress conditions. It can be considered as an important candidate gene for further research.

In conclusion, the expression patterns and functional predictions of PsTrihelix genes suggest that these genes are likely involved in regulating pea growth, development, and stress adaptation mechanisms, making them promising candidates for further research.

Zhongwan 6 is a widely cultivated pea variety in China, with a complete and accurate genome. During its cultivation, it often receives cold stress during the seedling stage, and heat stress in the later growth stage. In this study, PsTrihelix 55 was found to show an increasing trend in expression levels with the growth and development period under heat stress. PsTrihelix 26 had a higher expression level under cold stress in the early growth stage, and its expression level gradually decreased as the growth and development progressed. Analyzing these two genes may be involved in regulating the maturity of peas and their responses to cold/heat stress. In the future, an overexpression vector can be constructed to verify its function, study its regulatory mechanism, and provide a theoretical basis for molecular breeding of high-quality and resilient pea varieties suitable for field cultivation in China.

Conclusion

In this study, a total of 56 PsTrihelix genes were identified and named Trihelix-1 to Trihelix-56. Their gene structures, conserved motifs, evolutionary relationships, chromosomal distributions, gene duplications, and expression patterns were explored. An important candidate gene, PsTrihelix55, was identified. These results contribute to a better understanding of the function and evolutionary mechanism of the pea trihelix gene family, providing important references for further research on pea growth, development, and stress responses. The comprehensive analysis of the pea trihelix transcription factor family, combined with the expression patterns of the family genes under non-biological stress and hormone responses, provides important data support for molecular breeding of new high-quality and highly stress-resistant pea varieties.
