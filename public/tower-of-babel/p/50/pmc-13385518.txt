Gonococcal Peritonitis Complicated by Intraperitoneal Abscess and Pleural Empyema: A Case Report
Authors: Hanne Rheder Ellegaard Mikkelsen, Filip Lyng Lindgren, Thomas Vemmelund Rose, Hans Linde Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis report presents a unique case of gonococcal peritonitis complicated by an intraperitoneal abscess and pleural effusion in a 52‐year‐old woman with liver cirrhosis. No bacteriologically confirmed genital infection was identified, and the route of infection could not be established. This rare presentation of gonococcal peritonitis was successfully managed with repeated drainage procedures and 6 weeks of targeted antimicrobial therapy.Keywords: abdominal abscess, alcoholic liver cirrhosis, bacterial peritonitis, gonorrhea, Neisseria gonorrhoeae , pleural empyema

Key Clinical Message

Gonococcal peritonitis may occur without genital symptoms and should be considered in atypical presentations. In complicated cases with abscess formation or empyema, source control is essential for successful management.

Introduction

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is one of the most frequent bacterial infections in patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites, occurring in 10%–30% of cases [1, 2]. It is defined as an infection of ascitic fluid without an evident intra‐abdominal source, typically diagnosed by the presence of more than 250 neutrophils/μL in ascitic fluid and/or a positive culture, and is most commonly caused by enteric bacteria such as Escherichia coli [3, 4].

Neisseria gonorrhoeae , first isolated by Albert Neisser in 1879, is a Gram‐negative diplococcus and an obligate human pathogen that primarily colonizes the mucosal surfaces of the urogenital tract, pharynx, rectum, and conjunctiva, causing the sexually transmitted infection gonorrhea [5]. N. gonorrhoeae has rarely been reported as a cause of peritonitis in patients with liver cirrhosis [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Here, we present the first Danish case of gonococcal peritonitis complicated by an intraperitoneal abscess and pleural effusion.

Case History/Examination

A 52‐year‐old woman with alcoholic liver cirrhosis, esophageal varices, and chronic pancreatitis was admitted with increasing abdominal distension and right upper quadrant tenderness. She was afebrile on admission, with a blood pressure of 96/64 mmHg, heart rate of 90 beats per minute, and respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute. Examination revealed a distended abdomen with prominent venous patterns and right upper quadrant tenderness. She reported no gynecological or urinary symptoms. Laboratory tests showed a C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 293 mg/L, a white blood cell count (WBC) of 56.5 × 109/L, serum albumin of 16 g/L, bilirubin of 10 μmol/L, an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.6, and a serum creatinine level of 109 μmol/L.

Diagnostic paracentesis drained 2 L of cloudy yellow fluid. Empirical piperacillin/tazobactam 4 g IV every 6 h was initiated, but was switched the following day to cefotaxime 2 g IV every 12 h on suspicion of SBP [14]. Ascitic fluid analysis demonstrated an elevated WBC count (20,640 × 106/L), predominantly polymorphonuclear cells (15,230 × 106/L), with a protein concentration of 26 g/L. Ascitic fluid albumin and glucose measurements were unavailable, and the serum–ascites albumin gradient could therefore not be calculated. A chest X‐ray revealed a large right‐sided pleural effusion, which was drained.

Four days later, the patient developed fever. A contrast‐enhanced CT scan revealed pleural empyema and a 35 mm intraperitoneal abscess, both of which were drained (Figure 1). Following identification of the abscess, antibiotic therapy was broadened to piperacillin/tazobactam. However, ascitic fluid culture subsequently grew N. gonorrhoeae . On day five, antibiotic treatment was changed to ceftriaxone 2 g once daily combined with oral metronidazole 500 mg every 12 h to ensure anaerobic coverage. Pleural fluid was also positive for N. gonorrhoeae by PCR (see below).

Computed tomography (CT) scans of the thorax and abdomen in coronal, axial, and sagittal planes, demonstrating intra‐abdominal abscess (indicated by red arrows) and pleural empyema (indicated by green arrows).

Despite treatment, the patient's condition worsened, with progression of liver failure and an INR of 2.3 despite vitamin‐K administration. On day 16, antibiotic therapy was switched back to cefotaxime 1 g every 8 h based on the treating physician's clinical assessment. Repeated drain dislodgements and challenges with abscess drainage further prolonged hospitalization. During admission, the patient was diagnosed with diabetes mellitus, likely as a complication of chronic pancreatitis, and experienced a decline in hemoglobin levels. However, gastroscopy revealed no active bleeding from known esophageal varices.

A follow‐up CT scan on day 22 showed regression of the abscess, pleural fluid, and ascites. The patient received intravenous antibiotics for 4 weeks, followed by oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg every 12 h for 14 days. Screening for viral hepatitis and HIV was negative. As she was in a stable relationship, her partner was advised to undergo testing for gonorrhea. At 6‐month follow‐up, she had not required re‐hospitalization and reported cessation of alcohol overuse.

Investigations

Ascitic fluid culture grew N. gonorrhoeae , semi‐quantified at 102–103 CFU/mL on standard solid media. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed according to EUCAST breakpoints (version 13.0), and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using Etest (BioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France). The N. gonorrhoeae isolate tested negative for beta‐lactamase and was reported susceptible with low MIC to ceftriaxone (MIC: 0.003 μg/mL), cefotaxime (MIC: 0.002 μg/mL), ciprofloxacin (MIC: 0.002 μg/mL), and azithromycin (MIC: 0.500 μg/mL). Cultures of pleural fluid and abscess aspirate were negative, but N. gonorrhoeae was detected in both samples by PCR (GeneXpert, Xpert CT/NG kit). PCR tests for N. gonorrhoeae in the pharynx and rectum were negative, and blood cultures taken at admission remained negative. The patient declined urethral and cervical swab sampling. The result of the patient's partner's testing was not available (if performed).

Discussion

This report presents a rare and unusual case of gonococcal peritonitis, complicated by intraperitoneal abscess and pleural effusion. The clinical course was protracted, requiring multiple drainage procedures, and was further complicated by impaired liver function and a new diagnosis of diabetes secondary to chronic pancreatitis. Baseline Child–Pugh and MELD scores before the acute infection were not available. Although these scores could have been estimated during the infectious episode, they would primarily have reflected acute‐on‐chronic liver failure rather than baseline liver disease severity. Notably, the diagnosis was established in the absence of genital symptoms, based on cultivation of N. gonorrhoeae from ascitic fluid. Although the bacterial burden in ascitic fluid was relatively low (102–103 CFU/mL), contamination was considered unlikely given the marked neutrophilic inflammation of the ascitic fluid and independent detection of N. gonorrhoeae in pleural fluid and abscess material by PCR. The patient was treated with antibiotics for a total of 6 weeks.

SBP is typically diagnosed by the presence of > 250 neutrophils/μL in ascitic fluid and/or positive culture, in the absence of an identifiable intra‐abdominal source requiring surgical intervention. It primarily affects patients with cirrhotic ascites and is most often caused by enteric bacteria such as E. coli . While prompt diagnosis and treatment are essential, the causative agent remains unidentified in up to 60% of cases [3]. In Denmark, empirical therapy includes cefotaxime 2 g every 12 h, alternatively ciprofloxacin [14], which also covers N. gonorrhoeae . However, international literature recommends administering cefotaxime three times daily, which supports a more favorable pharmacokinetic profile with increased time‐dependent bacterial killing [15].

N. gonorrhoeae is a common sexually transmitted pathogen. In Denmark, the number of diagnosed gonorrhea cases has risen steadily over the past decade with more than 4000 cases in 2023 [16]. Similar trends have been observed across Europe, with the gonorrhea notification rate increasing by 321% between 2014 and 2023. In fact, the 2023 notification rate for the EU/EEA is the highest recorded since the initiation of European surveillance of sexually transmitted infections in 2009 [17]. Men who have sex with men (MSM) accounted for more than half of the reported cases in 2023, while relatively few cases were reported among women aged 45 years and older [17]. In contrast, the United States reported a 7.2% decrease in gonorrhea cases from 2022 to 2023 [18], although the infection remains a significant public health concern. In the present case, no information was available regarding the patient's or her partner's sexual behavior.

Gonococcal peritonitis is exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of cases documented in the literature to date [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. In all previously reported cases, the patients were women presenting with symptoms of acute abdomen, and in two cases, the patients had underlying portal hypertension and liver cirrhosis, respectively [8, 10]. Our case had a disseminated gonococcal infection without verified genital involvement, consistent with findings from a recent review by Al‐Ali et al. (2024), which identified that only one‐fourth of cases (n = 3/12) had N. gonorrhoeae detected at the vulvovaginal or cervical site [9]. Patients described by Al‐Ali et al. originated from the United States, Japan, France, the United Kingdom, Kuwait, the Netherlands, Italy, Spain, and Malta. Furthermore, the complications of intraperitoneal abscess and pleural effusion, as seen in our case, are exceptionally rare manifestations of gonococcal infection [19].

Treatment regimens in previously reported cases varied, with ceftriaxone being the most commonly used antibiotic; however, the duration of antibiotic therapy differed, with a maximum duration of 14 days reported. In contrast, our patient received 4 weeks of intravenous anti‐gonococcal therapy with piperacillin‐tazobactam, ceftriaxone, and cefotaxime, followed by 2 weeks of oral ciprofloxacin. The prolonged 6‐week treatment duration was guided by the presence of pleural empyema, intra‐abdominal abscess formation, and repeated challenges with source control. Importantly, the N. gonorrhoeae isolate in our case was fully susceptible to all tested antibiotics. However, successful clinical resolution was achieved not by antimicrobial therapy alone, but also by repeated drainage of the pleural empyema and intra‐abdominal abscesses, highlighting the essential role of source control in managing complicated gonococcal infections.

The distinction between spontaneous and secondary bacterial peritonitis warrants consideration in this case. Although no intestinal perforation, tubo‐ovarian abscess or other surgically correctable intra‐abdominal source was identified, the presence of a localized intraperitoneal abscess raises the possibility of secondary peritonitis. Furthermore, the markedly elevated ascitic fluid LDH concentration also favored secondary peritonitis according to conventional diagnostic criteria. Interpretation was further limited because ascitic fluid glucose and albumin were not measured. Nevertheless, a definitive distinction between spontaneous and secondary bacterial peritonitis could not be made based on the available clinical data.

The pathogenesis and route of entry of gonococcal peritonitis in our case remained unknown. Although an ascending genital tract route has been proposed [8], no clinical or radiological evidence of pelvic inflammatory disease or Fitz‐Hugh‐Curtis syndrome was identified in our patient, and cervical sampling was not performed because the patient declined testing. Consequently, the route of infection could not be established. Alternatively, transient bacteraemia from an unrecognized mucosal site may have resulted in secondary seeding of the peritoneum. Further studies are needed to clarify transmission pathways and risk factors, including the role of asymptomatic extra‐genital infections.

Conclusion

This case highlights N. gonorrhoeae as a rare but important cause of peritonitis, reported exclusively in women to date and typically presenting with acute abdominal pain. Unlike previously described cases, in which antibiotic treatment durations were limited to a maximum of 14 days, our patient required 6 weeks of antimicrobial therapy due to the presence of an intra‐abdominal abscess and pleural empyema requiring repeated drainage. As gonococcal infection may remain undiagnosed without targeted testing, particularly in the absence of genital symptoms, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in selected cases. The pathogenesis of gonococcal peritonitis remains incompletely understood and warrants further investigation.
