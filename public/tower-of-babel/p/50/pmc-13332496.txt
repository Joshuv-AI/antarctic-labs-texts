A hidden threat in the apical shadow: a case report regarding Loeffler endocarditis mimicking NSTEMI

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLoeffler endocarditis (LE) is a rare but potentially life-threatening cardiac manifestation of hyper-eosinophilic syndrome (HES). Introduction

Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by prolonged eosinophilia (>1.5 × 10^9/L) with evidence of eosinophil-mediated organ damage. Among the potential targets, the heart is notably the most critical one due to the high risk of morbidity and mortality associated with its involvement. Cardiac damage in hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) evolves through three pathological stages: An initial necrotic stage with eosinophilic infiltration and myocardial injury, a thrombotic stage characterized by mural thrombus formation, and a final fibrotic stage resulting in restrictive cardiomyopathy.1 Loeffler endocarditis (LE) refers to the cardiac manifestation of hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) that typically involves endomyocardial fibrosis and thrombus formation, particularly at the apex of the ventricles.

Diagnosis of Loeffler endocarditis (LE) is complex, often requiring a combination of imaging modalities, laboratory tests, and exclusion of secondary causes of eosinophilia. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) has emerged as a key non-invasive tool for identifying myocardial fibrosis and thrombi. Treatment typically involves corticosteroids to reduce eosinophilic activity and anticoagulation to manage thromboembolic risk. However, the prognosis remains guarded, particularly in patients with delayed diagnosis or complications such as heart failure.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old male patient with a known history of chronic coronary syndrome and idiopathic hypereosinophilia since December 2023 presented to the emergency department with exertional chest and axillary pain lasting approximately one hour, followed by persistent dyspnoea. The patient did not have any history of asthma, rhinitis, eczema, or other allergic or atopic conditions. His cardiovascular history included a non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) in January 2024, treated with percutaneous coronary intervention and drug-eluting stent (DES) implantation to the mid-left anterior descending artery (LAD).

On 31 January 2025, the patient came to the emergency department reporting a single episode of precordial pain that occurred during exertion and persisted for about an hour at high intensity, subsequently regressed at rest. Since then, he reports more dyspnoea than usual without chest pain. Vital parameters were normal in apyretic, normocardiac, and normotensive patients. At physical examination, the only pathological finding was bibasal crackles in the lungs.

At admission, his electrocardiogram (ECG) (Figure 1) showed new repolarization abnormalities in leads V3-V6 and inferior leads, with ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion (Figure 1) which were not present at the previously available electrocardiographic check. Blood tests revealed persistent hypereosinophilia [3.93 ×109/L—normal value (n.v.) <0.7 × 109/L], elevated high sensitive troponin T (0h: 49 ng/L, 1h: 40 ng/L—n.v. <14 ng/L), and elevated NT-proBNP (3096 ng/L—n.v. <900 ng/L).

ECG (31.01.2025) with suspicious findings for acute ischaemia with diffuse repolarization alterations in a symptomatic patient.

Echocardiography showed thickening of the left ventricular apex (max. thickness 14 mm) with systolic obliteration, confirmed after contrast administration, showing no evident endocavitary thrombotic formation, in the presence of preserved global systolic function [biplane ejection fraction (EF) 60%, global longitudinal strain (GLS) −17%] and grade I diastolic dysfunction.

The clinical picture initially raised the suspicion of a new acute coronary syndrome, NSTEMI. A coronary angiography was promptly performed on 4 February 2025 and revealed biventricular coronary artery disease with non-haemodynamically significant stenoses of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and right coronary artery (RCA), both evaluated by fractional flow reserve (FFR=0.84). Additionally, a functional endotype microvascular dysfunction was documented in the LAD territory (Coronary flow reserve, CFR=2.1; index of microcirculatory resistance IMR=14), as well as a myocardial bridge on the mid-distal LAD.

Given that coronary findings could not fully explain the clinical and biochemical profile, further cardiac imaging was pursued. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram confirmed apical thickening and systolic obliteration. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) (Figure 2), however, provided definitive diagnostic clarity by showing diffuse subendocardial fibrosis and an intracavitary thrombus at the apex—hallmark features of Loeffler endocarditis.

(D) Two-chamber projection.

Further investigations ruled out secondary causes of eosinophilia: negative autoimmune and viral panels, negative FIP1L1-PDGFRA mutation by FISH/NGS, and positive Toxocara serology (indicating past exposure). Bone marrow biopsy and T-cell receptor gene rearrangement suggested a possible lymphocyte-variant HES (L-HES), although no definitive evidence of a neoplastic clone was found.

Treatment was initiated with high-dose corticosteroids (prednisone 1 mg/kg/day) and unfractionated heparin (Liquemin), subsequently transitioned to warfarin (Sintrom). The dual antiplatelet therapy was discontinued. Steroid therapy led to a rapid and marked reduction in eosinophil count. For microcirculatory dysfunction, given the likely secondary nature of the infiltrative process of this functional alteration, it was indicated to consider a clinical re-evaluation following the introduction of systemic therapy if symptoms persist. The patient was stabilized and transferred to internal medicine for continued care.

Discussion

This case exemplifies the insidious and potentially severe nature of LE in the context of HES. Our patient’s diagnosis was complicated by overlapping cardiovascular risk factors, including prior coronary artery disease and previous NSTEMI. However, persistent eosinophilia and the characteristic findings on CRM pointed clearly towards a diagnosis of LE.

In accordance with the recommended etiologic evaluation of eosinophilia, a systematic and stepwise diagnostic approach was undertaken. First, secondary causes of eosinophilia were excluded through an extensive work-up, including a review of possible drug reactions and screening for autoimmune diseases with Antinuclear antibodies (ANA), extractable nuclear antigen antibodies (ENA), anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies (anti-dsDNA), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), rheumatoid factor (RF), and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (anti-CCP), which were negative, and complement components C3c and C4 showed no evidence of consumption. We excluded haematologic and solid neoplasms, respectively, with osteomedullary evaluation and positron emission tomography (PET-CT) scan. Viral serologies performed showed hepatitis B virus (HBV) from previous contact, hepatitis C virus (HCV), and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) negative. Parasitic infections were ruled out with serologies for Strongyloides, Toxocara, and filarial disease, with only a positive Toxocara immunoglobulin G (IgG) indicating past exposure without active infection.

Second, a myeloproliferative variant of HES was excluded based on the absence of FIP1L1-PDGFRA rearrangement on FISH/NGS analysis, along with negative testing for other pathogenic mutations typically associated with myeloid neoplasms (absence of rearrangements involving PGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2 (9p24) or ETV6). Bone marrow biopsy showed normocelled haematopoietic tissue with significant eosinophilia with no features suggestive of a clonal myeloid process.

Third, the hypothesis of a lymphocytic-variant HES (L-HES) was supported by immunophenotypic abnormalities of circulating T cells, T-cell receptor monoclonality, and significantly elevated interleukin-5 (IL-5) levels—together fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for L-HES.

LE often masquerades as other cardiovascular conditions, and its recognition requires a high index of suspicion, especially in patients with idiopathic eosinophilia. The hallmark of LE lies in the pathologic interplay between eosinophil infiltration, endomyocardial injury, thrombus formation, and subsequent fibrosis. In our patient, echocardiography alone was insufficient to detect the thrombus, reinforcing the utility of CMR in delineating both fibrosis and thrombotic involvement.

Regarding the temporal relationship, the patient had documented persistent eosinophilia for over one year before the onset of cardiac symptoms. The evolution from chronic eosinophilia to the appearance of new apical thickening, elevated troponin, and progressive subendocardial involvement on CMR strongly suggests that sustained eosinophil-mediated inflammation preceded and ultimately triggered the cardiac manifestations. This chronology is consistent with the natural progression of untreated or insufficiently controlled HES.

Therapeutically, the combination of corticosteroids and anticoagulation remains the cornerstone of management.1 Corticosteroids rapidly suppress eosinophilic activation, mitigating further cardiac injury, while anticoagulation prevents embolic complications. In this case, vitamin K antagonists were preferred over direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). Although comparative data are lacking, many clinicians opt for warfarin in eosinophilia-associated thrombosis due to the limited evidence on the safety and efficacy of DOACs in this specific context.1

Subsequent haematologic follow-up in May 2025 further clarified the underlying pathology. A comprehensive diagnostic reassessment confirmed the absence of myeloid neoplasms or aberrant T-cell clones by cytogenetics and flow cytometry. However, molecular evidence of T-cell receptor monoclonality and elevated IL-5 levels strongly supported the diagnosis of L-HES. This form of HES is driven by clonal or reactive T cells that promote eosinophilia via IL-5 overproduction and is often less responsive to corticosteroids alone.

As part of a steroid-sparing strategy, the patient was started on Mepolizumab (anti-IL-5 monoclonal antibody) at 300 mg subcutaneously every four weeks beginning 4 April 2025. At follow-up on May 2, the patient reported significant clinical improvement. Eosinophil counts remained suppressed, and repeat CMR demonstrated resolution of the apical obliteration and subendocardial deposits, though mild basal inferolateral fibrosis persisted (Figure 4).

(B) Follow-up cardiac MRI (four-chamber view, LGE sequence) demonstrating regression of the prior subendocardial enhancement and disappearance of the apical thrombus, though mild basal inferolateral fibrosis persisted. Together, these findings indicate a marked structural recovery following corticosteroid therapy and targeted anti–IL-5 treatment.

Our experience aligns with previous case series demonstrating favourable haematologic and cardiac response to early steroid therapy. Despite this, the risk of long-term complications such as progressive fibrosis, heart failure, and arrhythmic events persists, particularly due to myocardial scarring, which can act as a substrate for ventricular arrhythmias, mandating close follow-up and consideration of adjunctive therapies such as Mepolizumab in cases with suspected L-HES. Moreover, these patients are at risk of developing restrictive cardiomyopathy in the long term and require serial clinical and imaging follow-up.

Myocardial injury in Loeffler endocarditis occurs through the release of toxic substances from degranulating eosinophils that infiltrate the endocardium and myocardium. These toxins, including major basic protein and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin, directly damage cardiac myocytes and endothelial cells, which can lead to thrombosis, fibrosis, and eventual myocardial infarction. The process involves three stages: a necrotic phase, followed by a thrombotic stage with thrombus formation on the damaged endocardium, and a final fibrotic stage. The main pathophysiological mechanisms are represented by eosinophil degranulation, direct toxic damage, reactive oxygen species, which cause further cellular damage, thrombus formation, and fibrosis. Indeed, eosinophilic cationic proteins can bind to thrombomodulin on endothelial cells, impairing anticoagulant activity and promoting thrombus formation on the endocardium. Eosinophils also activate platelets, which aggregate to form thrombi within the heart chambers and on the endocardium. The chronic inflammation and damage from toxins and thrombi eventually lead to fibrosis, a process of scar tissue formation that stiffens the heart walls and valves. This can result in restrictive cardiomyopathy.

Conclusion

Loeffler endocarditis, though rare, should be considered in patients with hypereosinophilia and cardiac symptoms. Its recognition can be particularly challenging in individuals with coexisting cardiovascular conditions, such as coronary artery disease. In patients who already carry a diagnosis of chronic coronary syndrome, differentiating between ischaemic and eosinophilic cardiac pathology demands a high level of clinical suspicion and a multidisciplinary approach. CMR plays a crucial role in achieving diagnostic clarity when echocardiographic findings are inconclusive.

In addition, patients with Loeffler endocarditis are at significant long-term risk of developing restrictive cardiomyopathy due to progressive endomyocardial fibrosis. For this reason, serial clinical evaluation and periodic imaging follow-up—particularly with echocardiography and CMR—are essential to monitor ventricular stiffness, the evolution of residual fibrotic tissue, and the potential recurrence of eosinophil-mediated cardiac injury.2

Early initiation of corticosteroids and appropriate anticoagulation can significantly improve outcomes. Long-term monitoring and individualized therapy, including targeted immunomodulation in cases such as lymphocytic HES, remain essential due to the disease's potentially progressive and relapsing nature.3
