Replication of the Association between Retinal Aging Clock Susceptibility Genes and Retinal Age Gap in an Asian Population: The Nagahama Study
Authors: Yoshifumi Komatsu, Takuro Kamei, Kazuya Morino, Shin-ya Nakao, Masahiro Akada, Keina Sado, Yuta Doi, Tomotaro Tanaka, Masayuki Hata, Yasuharu Tabara, Fumihiko Matsuda, Akitaka Tsujikawa, Masahiro Miyake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo replicate previously reported genetic associations for the retinal aging clock—quantified as the retinal age gap—in an East Asian population and to assess cross-ancestry consistency of effect directions and sizes.DesignGenetic association study with candidate-single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) replication embedded in a population-based cohort.ParticipantsIndividuals who participated in both first and second visits of the Nagahama Study with gradable fundus photographs and genome-wide genotype data.MethodsA deep learning model fine-tuned on an independent subset (i.e., data from participants who attended only one of the 2 visits of the Nagahama Study) was used to estimate retinal age from color fundus photographs. We tested 13 previously reported lead variants; 7 were evaluable in our dataset (directly or as available leads). Linear regression models adjusted for standard covariates estimated SNP effects on the retinal age gap at the first visit. Statistical significance was set as Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.0071 (0.05/7). Secondary measures included directional concordance and effect-size correlation with discovery estimates.Main Outcome MeasuresAssociation of previously reported retinal aging clock SNPs with the retinal age gap; number of loci meeting replication criteria; directional concordance; cross-study effect-size correlation.ResultsAge-prediction accuracy in the genetic cohort showed mean absolute error = 3.00 (first visit) and 3.42 years (second visit). Of 13 variants to be replicated, 7 were testable. The lead variant rs17713879 (SH3YL1) replicated after multiple-testing correction (β = –0.41 years per effect allele, standard error = 0.094, P = 9.8 × 10-6). After harmonizing effect directions, all 7 of 7 testable variants showed directional concordance with discovery findings (binomial P = 0.0078) and a strong cross-study effect-size correlation (r = 0.93).ConclusionsIn an East Asian population, we replicate the SH3YL1 association with the retinal age gap and observe consistent direction and magnitude across all testable discovery variants, supporting a partially shared genetic architecture of retinal aging across ancestries. Larger transethnic meta-analyses and functional follow-up are warranted to refine causal biology.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Retinal age gap, Retinal aging clock, Deep learning, Nagahama Study

The field of geroscience is currently garnering significant attention.1 It has long been recognized that an individual's chronological age does not necessarily correlate with their physiological aging process. This concept was further underscored by the study of genetically determined progeroid syndromes, such as Werner syndrome,2 which prompted a paradigm shift toward understanding aging not merely as a consequence of time, but as a potentially modifiable, disease-like condition. This shift created a demand for objective methods to define and quantify aging, leading to the development of the "biological age" concept. For instance, leukocyte telomere length3 or DNA methylation4 are well-known molecular marker of cellular aging. However, its assessment, along with other biomarkers, often involves complex laboratory procedures and invasive sampling like blood draws, limiting its widespread application in large-scale population studies.

Recent advancements in deep learning have revolutionized the analysis of retinal fundus images, expanding their application from age estimation5 to risk stratification for systemic diseases, including cardiovascular events,5,6 stroke,7 and chronic kidney disease.8,9 Among these applications, models that predict an individual's age with high precision (mean absolute error [MAE] ≈ 3.55 years) have led to the identification of a novel biomarker. The discrepancy between the predicted retinal age and chronological age, initially dismissed as mere prediction error, was redefined as the "retinal age gap" by Zhu et al.10 Their report, which demonstrated a significant association between this gap and all-cause mortality, prompted a profound reassessment of its clinical significance. An elevated retinal age gap is hypothesized to reflect subtle microvascular alterations, such as arteriolar narrowing and increased vascular tortuosity. These features, in turn, may be indicative of underlying systemic pathologies like hypertension, chronic inflammation, or oxidative stress. Thus, the retinal age gap is emerging as a novel biomarker of systemic health, reflecting a biological aging process distinct from chronological age.11, 12, 13, 14

The first genome-wide association study on this trait was reported in 2023.15 This study identified single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) at 12 genetic loci that achieved genome-wide significance. Notably, the identified loci included ARMS2, the most potent genetic risk factor for age-related macular degeneration.16, 17, 18 Furthermore, pathway analyses revealed a significant enrichment for genetic associations with ocular phenotypes, including macular thickness, age-related macular degeneration, and refractive error.15 These findings suggest that the genes linked to the retinal age gap likely play a pivotal role in the aging processes of ocular tissues. However, this landmark study was conducted using data from the UK Biobank, a cohort of predominantly European ancestry. Given that the genetic architecture, particularly the allele frequencies of disease-associated SNPs, is known to vary across different ancestral populations, the generalizability and reproducibility of these findings in East Asian populations remain to be determined.

Therefore, the present study aimed to conduct the replication analysis of the genetic loci previously associated with the retinal age gap in an East Asian population, by utilizing genomic and fundus imaging data from the Nagahama Study, a large-scale, community-based cohort in Japan.

Methods

The ethics committee of Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine and Nagahama Municipal Review Board approved the protocols of this study. All participants were fully informed of the purpose and procedures of this study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures of our study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The Nagahama Study

In the present study, we included the first visit (2008–2010) and the second visit (2013–2015) participants of the Nagahama Prospective Cohort for Comprehensive Human Bioscience (Nagahama Study), started from 2008. A total of 6070 individuals who participated in both the first visit and the second visit were included in this study. Although the details of the Nagahama Study are presented elsewhere,19, 20, 21, 22, 23 we briefly explain here. The Nagahama Study is a community-based cohort conducted in Nagahama City, a rural city with 126 000 inhabitants in Shiga Prefecture, Japan. None of the subjects had serious diseases at enrollment, were able to live independently, and participated in this study voluntarily. Subjects underwent standardized ophthalmic examinations, including color fundus photograph (CR-DG10; Canon). A part of the participants was genome scanned.

Genotyping

For the current study, we used genome-wide dataset of the Nagahama Study revision 12. In the revision 12 dataset, genome-wide data were obtained from a series of BeadChip DNA arrays, namely, Infinium Omni2.5, Infinium Omni2.5-8, Infinium Omni2.5S-8, Infinium Omni5-4, Infinium CoreExome-12, Infinium CoreExome-24, and Infinium Asian Screening Array-24 (Illumina). They were categorized to 4 genotyping array sets (5M, 2.5M, CoreExome, and Asian Screening Array) according to the types of genotyping array platforms. Quality control filters were used to remove poorly preformed samples and SNP markers. Subjects with a call rate <0.9 were excluded from further analysis. Single nucleotide polymorphisms with a call rate <0.99, allele frequency <0.01, and deviation from the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P < 1.0 × 10–6) were also excluded. Finally, revision 12 dataset consisted of a total of 6020 participants, including 454 participants classified into 5M group, 786 into 2.5M group, 1331 into CoreExome group, and 3449 into Asian Screening Array group. Genomic imputation were performed for each group using Michigan Imputation Server (https://imputationserver.sph.umich.edu/index.html#!pages/home) using the East Asian population of the 1000 Genomes Project dataset (phase3 v5 release) as a reference for each group. After genotype imputation, we performed the second quality control; individuals with a call rate <90%, SNPs with a call rate <90%, minor allele frequency <1%, significant deviation (P < 1.0 × 10-5) from the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, or imputation quality r2 ≤ 0.5 in each dataset were excluded. After the second quality control, SNPs included in both datasets were used for the analysis. Among the remaining participants, individuals estimated to have first-degree or second-degree kinship within this population (Pi-hat >0.35, PLINK ver. 1.90 [https://www.cog-genomics.org/prink/1.9/]) were excluded from the dataset. Final dataset included 6 537 098 SNPs from 5271 participants.

Evaluation of Retinal Age Gap

Although details of retinal age gap evaluation are described in our previous report,24 we explain it briefly here.

First, we fine-tuned an existing retinal age prediction model to perform retinal age prediction. This existing retinal age prediction model was created by the Japanese Ophthalmological Society, the National Institute of Informatics, and the Japan Ocular Imaging Registry and is publicly available on their website (http://www.joir.jp/data/en_index.html). This model was fine-tuned using 2261 fundus images from 2261 participants of the Nagahama Study. These images were selected by the following criteria: (1) individuals who participated in either of the first visit or second visit only and (2) no past history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, stroke, or heart disease. These images were divided into training dataset and validation dataset by 4:1. The details of the training process are explained in our previous report.

The fine-tuned model was applied to individuals who participated in both the first visit and the second visit and were aged <70 years at the second visit. The predicted age is referred to as retinal age hereafter (in previous reports, it was also referred to as “eyeAge”). These analyses were performed using PLINK (version 1.9; https://www.cog-genomics.org/plink2). P values of <0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. Discussion

In the present study, we sought to elucidate the genetic architecture of the retinal age gap in an East Asian population by replicating loci first identified in a European-ancestry genome-wide association study. Our primary finding is the successful replication of a significant association for the susceptibility SNP rs17713879, at the SH3YL1 gene, from among the 12 previously reported loci. This provides the evidence in an Asian population for this association, suggesting that at least part of the genetic basis for the retinal age gap is shared across different ancestries.

Over the past decade, deep learning has become a key technology in medical imaging.25,26 Its capabilities extend beyond surpassing human accuracy in classification tasks to also quantifying complex features in images that are challenging for human to assess.27 Certain measurements derived from this technology, termed "digital biomarkers," have been shown to be valuable for predicting patient prognosis.28,29 The ocular fundus is an especially valuable source for these biomarkers, as it provides a unique, noninvasive view of the body's small blood vessels. A prominent example is the "retinal age gap," the difference between an artificial intelligence-predicted age and a person's actual age. This biomarker has gained significant attention in health and aging research because of its strong association with mortality and cardiovascular disease.10,30 Previous research on this topic has primarily used data from European populations, such as the UK Biobank. Our work, therefore, represents a study to investigate this important biomarker in an Asian cohort.

The finding that our model's performance (MAE = 3.00 years) was slightly less accurate than the benchmark of the public pretrained model (MAE = 2.39 years) that we utilized for transfer learning is an expected outcome that reflects the different characteristics of the training and evaluation data. The public pretrained model was developed using a "super-normal" cohort of ophthalmologically and systemically healthy individuals selected from the Japan Ocular Imaging Registry, a large national database. In contrast, our community-based cohort represents an actual population that includes participants with systemic conditions like hypertension. It is therefore reasonable to conclude that this greater phenotypic heterogeneity in our cohort influenced the model's predictive accuracy.

The primary finding of our study is a successful replication of the strong association between the SNP rs17713879 at the SH3YL1 gene and the retinal age gap in an East Asian population. Notably, the effect size per risk allele in our cohort was 0.41 years, which is highly comparable to the 0.38 years reported in the original European-ancestry study.15 As previous research was limited to European populations, our finding strongly suggests that this locus is a robust, transethnic susceptibility factor for the biological aging of the human eye. The biological function of SH3YL1 may provide an intriguing context for this genetic association. Previous in vitro studies have implicated SH3YL1 in inflammatory processes mediated by oxidative stress; for instance, its expression is upregulated in aortic endothelial cells and peritoneal mesothelial cells in response to inflammatory stimuli. This leads to the hypothesis that retinal microangiopathy induced by oxidative stress may be a key driver of an increased retinal age gap. Consequently, the retinal age gap holds potential as a future biomarker for other diseases in which oxidative stress plays a key pathogenic role.

This study has several limitations. First, our replication analysis was likely underpowered to detect associations for loci with small effect sizes. The fact that we only replicated the 1 locus with the largest reported effect suggests our sample size was insufficient to validate the associations for the remaining 11 loci. A larger-scale meta-analysis, potentially through an international consortium, will be necessary to definitively assess these loci in Asian populations. Second, the precision of our phenotype is a limitation. The deep learning model used to calculate the retinal age gap has an inherent measurement error (MAE = 3.00 years). This noise in the phenotypic data may have attenuated the signal from genetic factors. Developing and utilizing more accurate retinal age models in the future could enhance the power to identify novel genetic associations. Third, our study was based on a single Japanese cohort, warranting caution when generalizing these findings to East Asian populations. Follow-up studies in other Asian cohorts with different genetic and environmental backgrounds are essential to confirm the universality of our findings. The last limitation is that retinal aging clocks and the retinal age gap implicitly assume a concordance between retinal and systemic aging, particularly vascular aging. This assumption may not hold in conditions that selectively affect retinal circulation or structure independent of systemic aging, such as carotid artery occlusive disease, diabetic retinopathy, retinal vascular occlusive diseases, or following local ocular treatments including anti-VEGF therapy or laser photocoagulation. In addition, various ocular diseases, including glaucoma, retinal detachment, uveitis, and drug-induced retinal toxicity, may influence the predictive performance of retinal aging models. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting retinal age gap as a biomarker of biological aging in disease-specific or treatment-exposed populations. To overcome these limitations, future studies should prioritize the harmonization of retinal age phenotypes across cohorts to enable sufficiently powered meta-analyses for variants with modest effect sizes. In addition, incorporating multimodal and longitudinal retinal imaging may improve phenotypic precision and reduce measurement error. Finally, applying identical analytical pipelines to independent Asian cohorts will be essential to evaluate the generalizability of these findings across populations.

In conclusion, this study successfully validated a susceptibility locus for the retinal age gap, a key aging biomarker derived from fundus photographs, in an Asian population. We demonstrated that the locus at SH3YL1 is a robust, transethnic factor in ocular aging, providing insight into the genetic underpinnings of this digital biomarker. We expect that our findings will contribute to the broader understanding of the biological mechanisms of aging and advance the rapidly progressing field of geroscience.
