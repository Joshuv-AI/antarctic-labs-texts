Principal Component‐Based Comprehensive Physical Health Score Associates With Long‐Term Death in Patients With Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Guangyong Chen, Jialing Lou, Jianing Wang, Wei Xie, Rui Zhang, Changhao Zhang, Yuxin Zhu, Fan Wu, Junhe Huang, Yihan Shao, Yiyun Weng, Suwen Huang, Dehao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrincipal component analysis (PCA) integrates multiple clinical indicators into a single score, providing a holistic assessment. Existing clinical indicators often fail to fully reflect health conditions. To address this gap, our study constructs a comprehensive physical health score (CPHS) based on PCA, incorporating 42 clinical and laboratory indicators, and explores its association with long‐term death after acute ischemic stroke.MethodsWe collected 42 indicators across 9 categories from 1017 patients with thrombolysis‐treated acute ischemic stroke in the training cohort and 316 in the testing cohort. The outcome was 1‐year death. CPHS was constructed using PCA and Cox regression in the training cohort and validated in the testing cohort. Hierarchical clustering was performed on the basis of PCA results to define subgroups and assess the predictive value of CPHS.ResultsFifteen principal components from PCA were analyzed using Cox regression, and CPHS was significantly associated with 1‐year death (P<0.001). To better define the characteristics, hierarchical clustering on the 8 most important principal components identified 5 clusters: healthy, potential kidney dysfunction, liver and biliary dysfunction, metabolic abnormalities with high inflammation, and poor health with high disease risk. Cox regression showed that, compared with cluster 1, the other 4 clusters were significantly associated with 1‐year mortality, with mortality risk decreasing from cluster 5 to cluster 1.ConclusionsCPHS reliably predicted 1‐year death after acute ischemic stroke. Additionally, CPHS was able to identify markers of liver and kidney dysfunction, cardiovascular abnormalities, metabolic dysfunction, and high inflammation levels, all of which were associated with increased death after stroke.

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS), with low‐ and middle‐income countries bearing the greatest burden, is a significant subtype of stroke and ranks as the second‐leading cause of both disability and death globally. 1 , 2 Despite significant advancements in acute treatment, such as intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, the long‐term prognosis for patients with stroke remains a major challenge. 3

Currently, stroke prognosis is often assessed using traditional clinical markers such as age, sex, medical history, and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). While these markers provide some prognostic value, they are typically limited to specific aspects of health and do not fully reflect the overall health status of a patient. These single indicators, although helpful in acute assessments, are insufficient in predicting long‐term outcomes such as poststroke death. As such, there is a growing need for a more comprehensive and accurate tool that integrates various clinical and laboratory parameters to provide a holistic assessment of health and long‐term prognosis in patients with AIS.

This study focuses on a cohort of patients with AIS who have received thrombolysis treatment. Intravenous thrombolysis has become a standard acute treatment for ischemic stroke, particularly for patients within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. While intravenous thrombolysis significantly improves neurological recovery during the acute phase, the long‐term prognosis of these patients is still influenced by multiple factors. 4 In our study, the choice of this patient population is critical for exploring how comprehensive health status, encompassing inflammation, metabolism, and liver and kidney function, among other factors, affects long‐term death following stroke.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a widely used dimensionality reduction technique in medical research. 5 PCA works by transforming multiple correlated variables into a smaller set of uncorrelated principal components (PCs), thereby reducing the complexity of data while retaining the maximum variance. 6 Therefore, in clinical data analysis, PCA is widely used to extract insights from high‐dimensional data. 7 Unlike traditional single‐indicator methods, PCA offers a more holistic view of a patient's health, making it an ideal tool for stroke prognosis and long‐term outcome prediction.

While existing stroke assessment methods have some value in the acute phase, they generally have several limitations. First, traditional clinical indicators such as NIHSS, age, and sex are typically used in isolation, focusing on 1 dimension of health and failing to consider the overall clinical picture. Second, these indicators often fail to predict long‐term death accurately, particularly when accounting for complex factors such as inflammation, metabolic abnormalities, and liver or kidney dysfunction. To address these limitations, we introduce a comprehensive physical health score (CPHS), constructed using PCA to integrate 42 clinical and laboratory indicators. This scoring system aims to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of health in patients with stroke and explore its relationship with 1‐year poststroke death. The CPHS offers an innovative approach by incorporating multidimensional health data, which can provide a deeper understanding of patients' health risks beyond traditional indicators. Unlike commonly used neurological deficit scores such as the NIHSS score, which primarily assess neurological recovery at different stages of the disease, our CPHS places emphasis on comprehensively evaluating the overall health status of patients.

In this study, 42 clinical and laboratory indicators were selected across 9 categories: demographic characteristics, cardiovascular health, hematologic function, inflammatory markers, liver function, kidney function, metabolic indicators, lipid metabolism, and thyroid function. These indicators were chosen on the basis of their clinical relevance and ability to comprehensively reflect different aspects of health in patients with stroke. Previous studies indicate that demographic factors are important for stroke prognosis. High body mass index increases stroke risk by contributing to hypertension and insulin resistance. 8 , 9 Smoking and alcohol use also raise ischemic stroke risk by promoting atherosclerosis and vascular damage. 10 Cardiovascular indicators significantly impact stroke outcomes as well. For example, elevated blood pressure is a major risk factor for stroke, affecting severity and recovery. 11 , 12 Inflammatory markers are associated with more severe stroke and poorer recovery. High CRP (C‐reactive protein) levels, for instance, are linked to worse outcomes through vascular inflammation. 13 Metabolic factors are key in stroke risk assessment. High fasting glucose levels increase the risk of both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes by promoting atherosclerosis. 14 , 15 High levels of low‐density lipoprotein also worsen outcomes by accelerating artery damage. 16 , 17 Similarly, liver and kidney function indicators are critical in evaluating patients with stroke. Impaired liver function has been shown to worsen poststroke complications by affecting clotting mechanisms, 18 while kidney dysfunction has been associated with higher mortality rates due to its impact on fluid and electrolyte balance. 19 The selection of these indicators ensures a thorough representation of the multidimensional health status of patients with stroke, forming the basis for constructing the CPHS.

The primary objective of this study is to construct the CPHS using PCA and explore its relationship with 1‐year death in patients with ischemic stroke. We hypothesize that the CPHS, as a comprehensive scoring system, will offer a more accurate prediction of long‐term death compared with traditional single‐indicator assessments. Specifically, we expect that higher CPHS scores will be associated with an increased risk of death, while lower scores will correlate with better survival outcomes.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

The study design outlined the methodology used in this research (Figure 1). A retrospective study was conducted, encompassing consecutive hospitalizations of patients with AIS who received thrombolytic therapy. The study was carried out at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University for the training cohort from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022, and at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University for the testing cohort from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adult patients diagnosed with AIS on the basis of neurological signs, clinical symptoms, and neuroimaging tests, including computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, according to the World Health Organization criteria 20 ; and (2) patients with complete baseline data. Patients with incomplete follow‐up data were excluded. It is noteworthy that our study included patients with additional comorbidities (such as malignancy, hepatic or renal dysfunction, and respiratory diseases [eg, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchial asthma, pneumonia]) if they met the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, a total of 1017 patients in the training cohort and 316 patients in the testing cohort were included in the analysis (Figure S1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. KY2025‐R250), as well as the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. YJ2022022). The requirement for informed consent was waived by both ethics committees due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical characteristics were collected through a comprehensive review of electronic medical records. A wide array of patient data were obtained, including demographic information, medical history, and clinical parameters. The data set comprised 42 variables spanning several categories. These categories were shown as below. Demographic and lifestyle characteristics were included, such as age, sex, body mass index, cancer history, smoking status, and drinking status. Cardiovascular health indicators, such as heart rate, systolic pressure, diastolic pressure, left ventricular ejection fraction, previous stroke history, and heart disease, were also included. Hematological function indicators, such as red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelet count, were recorded. Inflammatory‐related indicators, including CRP, d‐dimer, white blood cell count, lymphocyte percentage, and neutrophil percentage, were also gathered. Liver function indicators such as alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, total protein, albumin, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, and indirect bilirubin were assessed. Kidney function indicators, specifically urea, creatinine, uric acid, sodium, and chlorine, were included in the data set. Additionally, glycemic metabolism indicators, including glucose and hemoglobin A1c, were recorded. Lipid metabolism indicators such as triglycerides, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol were also part of the data collection. Finally, thyroid function indicators, including triiodothyronine and thyroid‐stimulating hormone, were considered. NIHSS score at admission was recorded to evaluate the severity of the stroke. Blood samples were collected within the first 24 hours after admission for routine laboratory tests. Baseline clinical evaluations were performed by a trained clinician within 24 hours of admission. Follow‐up data were collected through telephone interviews conducted by professional clinicians within 1 year of the initial admission.

Clinical Outcomes

The primary outcome was all‐cause death within 1 year.

PCA and CPHS

As a commonly used exploratory tool in data analysis, PCA can be used to reduce dimensionality and compress data by omitting contributions from higher PCs that explain smaller amounts of the overall variance, although this approach may sometimes result in loss of useful information. 7 In this study, we applied PCA for dimensionality reduction of the data. On the basis of the results of PCA, the PCs with significant contributions were selected. These components were then analyzed using Cox regression to construct CPHS.

Cluster Analysis

Agglomerative hierarchical clustering, an unsupervised machine learning technique, was carried out to identify the clusters of indicators, ensuring that the levels of indicators in 1 cluster were more similar than those in other clusters. This bottom‐up algorithm initiates with each sample considered as an individual cluster and progressively merges similar clusters on the basis of a proximity distance matrix until only a single cluster remains. The selection of the final clusters was guided by subject information, existing research, and clinical relevance. Hierarchical clustering algorithms inherently construct a hierarchy of input data items, which obviates the need to predefine the number of clusters. On the basis of the results of PCA, we subsequently selected the top 8 PCs with the highest contribution to CPHS for hierarchical clustering analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All 42 clinical variables included in the PCA were subjected to multiple imputations for missing data using a random forest method, provided that the conditions for using multiple imputation were met. The training, testing, and validation cohorts were imputed separately to avoid information leakage. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. For nonnormally distributed data, nonparametric tests were applied. Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]), while categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test.

After presenting the baseline characteristics of the training and testing sets, PCA was performed on the training set. PCA was fitted exclusively on the Z score–standardized predictors in the training set (mean, 0±1). The resulting loading matrix was then applied to the testing and validation cohorts to derive their PC scores and compute the CPHS, ensuring a consistent definition of the score across all cohorts. This procedure reduced the original 42 clinical variables spanning 9 categories into 15 PCs. These PCs were then included in a Cox regression model, and a formula for CPHS was constructed on the basis of the resulting β values. This formula was subsequently validated in the testing set and was further confirmed in the validation cohort.

In the testing set, receiver operating characteristic curves were used to determine the cutoff value for CPHS. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to explore the relationship between the binary CPHS and baseline NIHSS with 1‐year death following stroke. Additionally, the relationship between continuous CPHS and 1‐year death was examined, and subgroup analyses were performed on the basis of the TOAST (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) classification.

Using the top 8 PCs contributing most to CPHS from PCA, hierarchical clustering analysis was performed with both the training and testing sets combined, resulting in 5 clusters. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the correlations among the 8 PCs. Continuous variables across the 5 clusters were compared using 1‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate, and categorical variables using χ2 tests. The relationships between CPHS and clusters, as well as NIHSS score and clusters, were visualized using ridgeline plots. Scatter plots of grouped linear regression were used to display the relationship between individual CPHS and NIHSS within each cluster.

Finally, Cox regression was used to investigate the association between the 4 clusters with distinct characteristics and 1‐year death. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to visualize the relationship between the 5 clusters and 1‐year death, with P values calculated using the log‐rank test.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R 4.3.0 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and P<0.05 (2‐tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Cohorts

The baseline characteristics of 1017 patients from the training cohort and 316 patients from the testing cohort demonstrated substantial similarity across most evaluated parameters (Table S1). Both cohorts shared comparable demographic profiles, with nearly identical male predominance (66.67% versus 66.77%) and similar distributions in lifestyle factors including smoking (36.97% versus 37.97%) and drinking (32.25% versus 28.16%). Clinical severity as measured by NIHSS score at admission showed remarkable consistency between cohorts (median score, 6.00 in both groups) but with slightly wider distribution in the training cohort (IQR, 4.00–12.00) versus testing cohort (IQR, 4.00–10.75). Laboratory profiles revealed parallel patterns in key metabolic markers including comparable glucose levels (median, 5.60 versus 5.44 mmol/L) and hemoglobin A1c (median, 5.90% versus 5.90%), as well as similar renal function parameters (urea: median, 5.20 versus 5.18 mmol/L; creatinine: median, 70.00 versus 68.00 μmol/L). While the testing cohort exhibited moderately higher age (median, 72.00 versus 67.00 years) and systolic blood pressure (median, 158.50 versus mean, 153.46 mm Hg), the overall clinical and biochemical profiles between cohorts maintained strong concordance, supporting the validity of cross‐cohort comparisons in subsequent analyses. The mortality rate at 1 year was 12.19% in the training cohort and 7.28% in the testing cohort, with a total of 124 deaths in the training cohort and 23 in the testing cohort.

Additionally, Table S2 presented a comparison of the baseline characteristics between patients who survived and those who did not survive at 1 year. In Table S3, we also summarized the causes of death for nonsurvivors, providing insight into the primary factors contributing to death in this patient population.

PCA and CPHS Construction

Dimensionality reduction analysis using PCA was performed on 42 clinical and laboratory variables in the training cohort. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.637, which was greater than the recommended threshold of 0.6, indicating that the data were suitable for PCA. Additionally, the data passed the Bartlett's test of sphericity (P<0.001), further confirming the appropriateness of PCA for the data set. The analysis yielded >20 PCs, of which the first 15 PCs with eigenvalues >1 were retained for further analysis.

The eigenvalue spectrum showed a clear inflection point after the first few components, with PC1 demonstrating the strongest explanatory power (eigenvalue, 4.65; 11.06% variance explained). Subsequent PCs exhibited progressively decreasing contributions: PC2 (eigenvalue, 3.16; 7.52%), PC3 (eigenvalue, 2.71; 6.46%), PC4 (eigenvalue, 2.42; 5.75%), and PC5 (eigenvalue, 1.91; 4.54%). The remaining components (PC6–PC15) showed eigenvalues ranging from 1.66 to 1.01, each contributing between 3.95% and 2.40% of the explained variance (Figure S2). Collectively, these 15 components accounted for 65.68% of the total variance in the data set, providing a comprehensive yet parsimonious representation of the original 42 variables.

Through Cox regression analysis of the 15 PCs against 1‐year death, we derived β coefficients for CPHS construction (Table S4). The final CPHS formula was calculated as: CPHS=−0.334×PC1+0.183×PC2+0.437×PC3+0.179×PC4+0.030×PC5−0.147×PC6−0.045×PC7+0.119×PC8+0.027×PC9+0.193×PC10−0.018×PC11−0.089×PC12+0.049×PC13−0.249×PC14−0.160×PC15

Notably, PC3 (β=0.437) and PC10 (β=0.193) showed the strongest positive associations with mortality risk, while PC1 (β=−0.334) and PC14 (β=−0.249) demonstrated inverse relationships. Additionally, PC2 (β=0.183), PC4 (β=0.179), PC15 (β=−0.160), and PC6 (β=−0.147) also contributed substantially to the CPHS, collectively capturing the multidimensional nature of physiological dysregulation in patients with stroke. This weighted integration effectively transformed complex clinical measurements into a single comprehensive health metric for risk stratification.

Association Between CPHS and 1‐Year Death

In the testing cohort, CPHS showed strong prognostic value for 1‐year all‐cause death in patients with AIS. Receiver operating characteristic analysis yielded an area under the curve of 0.839 with a 95% CI of 0.778 to 0.901, indicating excellent discriminatory ability (Figure 2A). The optimal cutoff value of 0.553, determined by maximizing Youden's index, effectively stratified patients by mortality risk.

C, Kaplan–Meier survival estimates curve for the risk of 1‐year death according to NIHSS. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with CPHS ≥0.553 had a significantly higher 1‐year mortality rate compared with those with lower scores (P<0.0001, log‐rank test; Figure 2B). For comparison, NIHSS score was also used to stratify patients, with NIHSS score ≥10 defined as the high‐risk group and NIHSS <10 as the low‐risk group on the basis of previous prognostic studies. 21 Patients in the NIHSS‐high group exhibited significantly worse survival than those in the NIHSS‐low group (P<0.0001; Figure 2C). These results preliminarily suggested that CPHS had a good predictive value for stroke prognosis.

Cox regression analysis further supported the prognostic utility of CPHS. As shown in Figure 3A, each unit increase in CPHS nearly doubled the risk of death within 1 year (hazard ratio [HR], 1.992 [95% CI, 1.538–2.579]; P<0.001). Subgroup analysis by TOAST classification showed significant associations in both large‐artery atherosclerosis (LAA; HR, 2.214 [95% CI, 1.125–4.359]; P=0.021) and non‐LAA (HR, 1.873 [95% CI, 1.413–2.484]; P<0.001) populations, with no significant interaction observed between CPHS and TOAST subtype (P for interaction=0.654).

Forest plot of the association between CPHS and 1‐year death in the testing cohort.

A, Forest plot of the association between continuous CPHS and death within 1 year. B, Forest plot of the association between dichotomous CPHS and death within 1 year. CPHS indicates comprehensive physical health score; HR, hazard ratio; LAA, large‐artery atherosclerosis; and TOAST, Trial of Org 10 172 in Acute Stroke Treatment.

When CPHS was dichotomized at the 0.553 threshold, the association with death remained robust. In the non‐LAA subgroup, patients with low CPHS had a significantly lower risk of 1‐year death compared with the high CPHS group (HR, 0.081 [95% CI, 0.023–0.281]). In contrast, this association was not statistically significant in the LAA group (HR, 0.244 [95% CI, 0.049–1.209]; Figure 3B). Again, no significant interaction was observed between CPHS and TOAST subtype in the dichotomized analysis (P for interaction=0.285). Together, these findings underscored CPHS as a robust, multidimensional predictor of long‐term death in patients with AIS.

To further assess the external validity of CPHS, we even introduced a validation cohort of 394 patients with AIS who received thrombolytic therapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023, with inclusion and exclusion criteria consistent with the previous cohorts. Table S5 presented the baseline characteristics of this cohort, including demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables for both survivors and nonsurvivors. Figure S3 showed the forest plot of the association between CPHS and 1‐year death in the validation cohort, confirming CPHS as a strong predictor of death (HR, 2.136 [95% CI, 1.708–2.673]; P<0.001). Notably, the CPHS still remained predictive across subgroups, with particularly high risk in patients with LAA (HR, 1.872 [95% CI, 1.438–2.437]; P<0.001) and patients without LAA (HR, 3.208 [95% CI, 1.956–5.262]; P<0.001), strongly demonstrating its robustness and broad applicability. These results were also consistent with those we obtained previously.

Hierarchical Clustering Based on Key PCs

To explore the heterogeneity in physical health profiles, hierarchical clustering was performed on the basis of the top 8 principal components (PC3, PC1, PC14, PC10, PC2, PC4, PC15, and PC6), which contributed most substantially to CPHS. Each of these PCs represented distinct physiological domains: PC3 reflected inflammatory status; PC1 was associated with hematological function; PC14 captured a combination of inflammation, cardiovascular, and glycemic metrics; and PC10 was linked to hepatic function and lifestyle, while others represented renal (PC4), metabolic (PC15), and mixed cardiovascular–metabolic features (PC6) (Table S6).

A total of 1333 patients from both cohorts were included in the clustering analysis. The resulting 5‐cluster solution was visualized using a correlation heatmap of component scores; a 3‐dimensional scatterplot based on PC1, PC3, and survival time; and a series of boxplots demonstrating component‐level score distributions across clusters (Figure 4A through 4C). According to the boxplot analysis, there were statistically significant differences among the 5 clusters in PcaScore1 (P<0.001), PcaScore2 (P<0.001), PcaScore3 (P<0.001), PcaScore4 (P<0.001), PcaScore6 (P<0.001), and PcaScore15 (P<0.001). PcaScore14 also differed significantly among clusters (P=0.008), whereas PcaScore10 did not reach statistical significance (P=0.159), suggesting that most key PCs played a meaningful role in differentiating the identified clusters.

Each cluster was then characterized on the basis of its component score profile and the clinical interpretation of contributing indicators. Cluster 1 was defined as “healthy individuals,” who demonstrated generally normal physiological and laboratory parameters across systems. Although PcaScore1 was relatively elevated, suggesting higher hematological function, all related indicators, including red blood cell count, hemoglobin, and hematocrit, remained within normal clinical ranges, reflecting a robust but nonpathological hematologic profile. Cluster 2 was characterized as “individuals with potential kidney dysfunction.” While other systems showed no major abnormalities, these patients had notably low levels of urea, creatinine, and uric acid, some falling below the normal reference range, indicating early or subclinical renal impairment. Cluster 3 corresponded to “individuals with liver and biliary dysfunction,” with distinct elevations in total, direct, and indirect bilirubin, often exceeding normal limits, pointing to abnormal hepatic or biliary function. Cluster 4 represented “individuals with metabolic abnormalities and high inflammation levels,” exhibiting elevated glucose and triglyceride levels indicative of both glycemic and lipid dysregulation. In addition, all inflammatory markers, including CRP, white blood cell count, and neutrophil percentage, were markedly elevated, suggesting active systemic inflammation. Finally, cluster 5 was defined as “individuals with poor health and elevated disease risk,” presenting with significantly impaired renal function, severe inflammation, metabolic disturbances, and poor cardiovascular and cerebrovascular parameters. This group exhibited globally deranged physiology, consistent with a high‐risk, high‐mortality patient profile.

Baseline characteristics across the 5 clusters, summarized in Table S7, revealed clear stratification in age, sex, body mass index, cardiovascular markers, laboratory indicators, and stroke severity. Most variables demonstrated significant intercluster differences (P<0.001), validating the robustness of the clustering solution, and highlighting its potential utility in patient stratification on the basis of multidimensional health status.

CPHS and NIHSS Score Distribution Across Clusters

To further examine the relationship between overall health status and stroke severity, we analyzed the distribution of CPHS and NIHSS scores across the 5 identified clusters. As shown in Figure 5A, CPHS scores progressively increased from cluster 1 to cluster 5, reflecting worsening physiological status along the clustering gradient. Specifically, cluster 1 had the lowest median CPHS score (−0.82 [IQR, −1.33 to −0.21]), followed by cluster 2 (median, −0.18 [IQR, −0.71 to 0.48]), cluster 3 (median, 0.06 [IQR, −0.75 to 0.77]), cluster 4 (median, 0.71 [IQR, −0.05 to 1.67]), and cluster 5, which exhibited the highest CPHS score (median, 2.11 [IQR, 1.46–2.66]).

CPHS and NIHSS distribution characteristics of 5 clusters.

A, Ridgeline plots of CPHS distribution binned by clusters. B, Ridgeline plots of NIHSS distribution binned by clusters. C, Scatter plots and linear regression of NIHSS vs CPHS for each cluster. CPHS indicates comprehensive physical health score; and NIHSS, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale.

A similar gradient was observed in baseline stroke severity, as measured by NIHSS score (Figure 5B). The median NIHSS scores were 5.00 (IQR, 3.00–9.00) in cluster 1, 6.00 (IQR, 3.00–11.00) in cluster 2, 7.00 (IQR, 4.00–11.75) in cluster 3, 10.00 (IQR, 5.00–14.00) in cluster 4, and 9.00 (IQR, 6.00–16.50) in cluster 5. These results indicated that patients in higher‐risk clusters not only had worse systemic health profiles but also tended to present with more severe strokes at baseline.

To assess the relationship between CPHS and NIHSS scores at the individual level within each cluster, scatterplot analysis with linear regression was performed (Figure 5C). CPHS was positively correlated with NIHSS score in cluster 1 (slope, 0.057; R 2=0.087; P<0.001), cluster 2 (slope, 0.066; R 2=0.162; P<0.001), cluster 3 (slope, 0.060; R 2=0.128; P<0.001), and cluster 4 (slope, 0.078; R 2=0.164; P<0.001), suggesting that worse physical health status as captured by CPHS was associated with higher stroke severity. Interestingly, in cluster 5, while the trend direction was positive (slope, 0.210), the correlation did not reach statistical significance (R 2=0.024; P=0.246). This is likely due to the small sample size (n=57), which may have reduced statistical power, weakening the ability to detect a significant relationship between CPHS and NIHSS scores despite the positive trend.

These findings demonstrated that CPHS and NIHSS scores not only showed a consistent gradient across data‐driven clusters but were also moderately correlated within most subgroups, reinforcing the value of CPHS.

Mortality Risk Stratification by Clusters

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significant differences in 1‐year death among the 5 clusters (log‐rank P<0.0001; Figure 6). Cluster 1 had the lowest mortality rate, while cluster 5 showed the highest, with a rapid early decline in survival. Cluster 4 also had notably poor survival. Clusters 2 and 3 showed an intermediate mortality rate, with slightly worse outcomes in cluster 3. To be specific, the 1‐year mortality rates for clusters 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 were 3.42%, 8.30%, 12.71%, 22.64%, and 43.86%, respectively. The number at risk declined more rapidly in high‐risk clusters, confirming the association between poorer baseline health and higher mortality rate.

Clinical Utility of the 5 Clusters

The 5 clusters identified in this study served as valuable tools for pathway prioritization in stroke care (Figure 7). Rather than replacing organ‐specific diagnoses, these clusters helped organize multisystem health signals, guiding clinicians in developing personalized follow‐up and secondary prevention strategies. Each cluster reflected a unique health profile that informed targeted interventions, ensuring that patients received the most appropriate care on the basis of their systemic health status. This approach was in line with current international standards for secondary prevention, 22 offering a more comprehensive and individualized framework for stroke management.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a novel PCA‐based CPHS to quantify systemic health status in patients with AIS and demonstrated its strong predictive value for 1‐year death. Unlike traditional stroke scales, CPHS captured multidimensional physiological dysfunction by integrating a wide range of clinical and biochemical parameters. Notably, CPHS also identified key health phenotypes, such as renal and hepatic dysfunction, cardiovascular impairment, metabolic abnormalities, and elevated inflammatory states, that were strongly associated with poststroke death. In addition, the use of 2 completely independent cohorts from different time periods enhanced the model's robustness and external validity, making it more applicable and useful in dynamic health care settings. The purpose of the testing cohort was to assess the model's ability to predict outcomes in future patient populations, not just within the scope of the training data. To our knowledge, this is the first study to construct a data‐driven health score and stratify patients with AIS into biologically distinct clusters, offering complementary prognostic insight beyond neurological severity alone.

Given the complexity of stroke pathophysiology, PCA provides a distinct advantage over traditional multivariate regression models, which typically model the relationship between specific predictors and death using linear equations. While standard regression methods, such as logistic regression, can be effective, they often struggle with multicollinearity when predictors are highly correlated, a common issue in clinical data. 23 PCA, on the other hand, addresses this by transforming correlated variables into uncorrelated PCs, improving both model stability and predictive accuracy. 24 Additionally, while regression models typically assume linear relationships, PCA excels at capturing complex, nonlinear interactions between diverse clinical and biochemical factors, providing a more comprehensive view of how various physiological systems contribute to stroke outcomes. 25 , 26 Given that CPHS integrates diverse dimensions of health status, including both neurological and systemic factors, PCA‐based modeling allows for more nuanced and holistic risk stratification.

Previous prognostic models for ischemic stroke have primarily relied on neurological scores such as the NIHSS, or focused on individual biomarkers of inflammation, metabolism, or organ dysfunction. For instance, the triglyceride–glucose body mass index has been widely studied as an integrated indicator of insulin resistance and is associated with increased stroke severity and adverse outcomes. 27 , 28 Similarly, inflammatory indices such as the systemic immune–inflammation index and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio have demonstrated strong associations with early death and poor functional recovery. 29 , 30 However, while they reflect certain systemic processes, they remain confined to limited subsets and lack coverage of broader physiological dysfunctions. In parallel, frailty scales such as the Clinical Frailty Scale or Frailty Index have been adopted to capture overall physiological vulnerability. 31 These tools typically combine assessments of comorbidity, mobility, nutritional status, and cognitive impairment, and have shown predictive value in stroke populations. 32 However, they rely on subjective clinical evaluation, are susceptible to observer variability and rarely incorporate biochemical indicators, limiting their reproducibility and precision. 33 The Charlson comorbidity index is widely used to predict long‐term death, focusing on the burden of comorbidities such as heart disease, diabetes, and cancer. 34 It provides a robust measure of chronic health conditions and has been valuable in predicting long‐term outcomes in various populations, including stroke patients. 35 However, the Charlson comorbidity index has limitations: it primarily considers comorbidities and does not account for acute changes in a patient's physiological status following a stroke, nor can it capture dynamic changes over time. 36 Instead, the CPHS integrates a wide range of clinical and biochemical indicators to provide a real‐time and holistic assessment of stroke risk. Thus, unlike the Charlson comorbidity index, CPHS can be modified through interventions, allowing clinicians to track improvements in patient health and observe real‐time risk reduction.

In contrast, PCA, a powerful dimensionality reduction tool, has been applied in various medical fields to extract key prognostic indicators from large data sets. For example, in a study on AIS, PCA was used to integrate blood microRNA data, successfully distinguishing patients with stroke from healthy controls. The identified microRNA patterns were found to correlate with stroke type and severity, providing valuable prognostic information beyond traditional clinical assessments. 37 Similarly, another study used PCA to analyze intracranial hemodynamic parameters and cerebral blood flow velocity data, enabling the differentiation between ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage subtypes. This multidimensional approach allowed for stroke subtype classification without the need for imaging, offering a novel tool for early stroke diagnosis and risk stratification. 38 However, unlike these single‐domain analyses, our study uses PCA to integrate a broader spectrum of physiological dysfunctions by incorporating 42 routine clinical and laboratory indicators across 9 domains. This multidimensional approach allows for a more comprehensive stroke risk stratification, capturing both neurological and systemic factors that contribute to stroke prognosis. By transcending the limitations of unidimensional or inflammatory‐based models, the CPHS offers a novel and more holistic framework for personalized risk assessment in AIS.

Through PCA cluster analysis, patients with stroke were classified into 5 groups (clusters 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5), showing significant mortality differences. Cluster 5 had the highest mortality rate, with patients displaying severe inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and cardiovascular dysfunction, while cluster 1 had the lowest mortality rate. The biological markers assessed revealed key factors driving these differences, primarily inflammation, metabolic imbalance, and vascular dysfunction.

In cluster 5, elevated inflammatory markers like CRP and white blood cell count indicate systemic inflammation, which activates endothelial cells through NF‐κB (nuclear factor κB) and mitogen‐activated protein kinase pathways. 39 This leads to increased vascular permeability and blood–brain barrier disruption, allowing immune cells to infiltrate the brain and aggravate neuronal damage. 39 Oxidative stress markers, such as malondialdehyde, exacerbate neuronal injury by inducing lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, which leads to cell apoptosis. 40 Animal models have shown that oxidative stress severely impairs mitochondrial function in neurons, contributing to a higher mortality rate. 41 Metabolic dysregulation in cluster 5, with elevated glucose and hemoglobin A1c, accelerates the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which bind to the receptor for AGEs on endothelial cells and neurons, triggering inflammation and oxidative stress. 42 This results in endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis, reducing cerebral blood flow. 43 Furthermore, hyperlipidemia activates TLR4 (toll‐like receptor 4) signaling, increasing inflammation and exacerbating neuroinflammation. 44 Animal studies have demonstrated that inhibiting the receptor for AGE and TLR4 reduces neuroinflammation and improves recovery outcomes. 45

Cluster 2 shows mild metabolic disturbances, with moderate elevations in glucose and lipid levels. These abnormalities lead to increased oxidative stress, which damages endothelial cells and promotes atherosclerosis, impairing cerebral perfusion. 46 Mildly elevated CRP levels suggest a moderate inflammatory response, contributing to vascular dysfunction. 47 Previous studies have confirmed that even moderate metabolic disturbances lead to endothelial cell apoptosis and thus hinder recovery, explaining the intermediate mortality rate in cluster 2. 48

Cluster 3 exhibits more pronounced lipid dysregulation, with elevated cholesterol and triglycerides, as well as higher levels of total and direct bilirubin. Oxidized low‐density lipoprotein activates the NF‐κB pathway, triggering the production of proinflammatory cytokines that aggravate endothelial damage and neuroinflammation. 46 This lipid‐induced inflammation reduces cerebral blood flow and worsens stroke outcomes. 46 Elevated triglycerides also increase PAI‐1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1) levels, which inhibits fibrinolysis and promotes thrombosis, further increasing stroke severity. 49 Studies have confirmed that inhibiting PAI‐1 improves stroke recovery by reducing thrombotic events. 50

Cluster 4, with moderate dyslipidemia and mild hypertension, shows endothelial damage, vascular stiffness, and impaired blood flow. 51 Hypertension increases vascular stress, while lipid dysregulation promotes endothelial dysfunction. 52 These combined factors lead to increased thrombotic events and worse stroke recovery, contributing to a higher mortality rate than cluster 2. 53

In conclusion, the mortality differences among the clusters are driven by complex interactions between inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and cardiovascular dysfunction. These factors act through key molecular pathways like NF‐κB, receptor for AGE, TLR4, and PAI‐1, which contribute to endothelial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and impaired stroke recovery. Animal models further support these findings, highlighting the importance of targeting these pathways for improving stroke outcomes. Monitoring these biomarkers in clinical practice can help identify high‐risk patients with stroke and guide personalized treatment strategies.

A key strength of this study lies in the development of a novel CPHS based on PCA, which effectively overcomes the limitations of single‐indicator approaches by integrating multidimensional clinical and biochemical parameters to comprehensively reflect patients' systemic health. The CPHS demonstrated strong predictive performance across multiple centers, enhancing its external validity and supporting its generalizability. Importantly, all 42 input variables were derived from routine admission tests, underscoring the model's feasibility and scalability in real‐world clinical settings. Furthermore, the clustering analysis based on CPHS components revealed distinct physiological subtypes among patients with ischemic stroke, enabling more personalized risk stratification and management. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the sample was externally validated, it primarily included patients from a single regional hospital network, which may limit broader applicability. Additionally, the study's applicability to diverse populations could be further strengthened by incorporating multiethnic cohorts. Future studies should consider validating the CPHS across different ethnic groups to assess its generalizability and robustness in varied populations. Second, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias and residual confounding. Finally, while the study identified key dimensions of physiological dysfunction associated with stroke prognosis, the underlying biological mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Future studies incorporating genomic or metabolomic profiling may provide further insights into the pathophysiological pathways linking systemic health to stroke outcomes.

This study highlights the clinical utility of CPHS as a novel, data‐driven tool to quantify systemic physiological health in patients with AIS. By capturing multidimensional biological disruptions, CPHS provides valuable prognostic information beyond conventional neurological assessments, and offers a promising basis for individualized risk stratification and decision making. Looking forward, future research should focus on validating CPHS in larger, multiethnic, and multicenter cohorts to enhance its generalizability. Integration with advanced modalities such as imaging data, electronic health records, and machine learning algorithms may further refine its predictive performance. Moreover, translating CPHS into clinical workflows could support personalized care planning, enabling clinicians to tailor poststroke interventions based on each patient's holistic physiological profile.

Conclusions

This study is the first to construct CPHS using PCA and explore its impact on long‐term outcomes in AIS patients. Higher levels of CPHS were significantly associated with an increased 1‐year all‐cause death following stroke. Furthermore, CPHS identified markers of liver and kidney dysfunction, cardiovascular abnormalities, metabolic dysfunction, and high inflammation, all of which contribute to increased death after stroke. These findings provide additional prognostic information for clinicians, aiding in the treatment and rehabilitation planning for patients with ischemic stroke.
