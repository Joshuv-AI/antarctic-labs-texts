Characteristics of studies falsely excluded during single-reviewer abstract screening: a meta-epidemiological analysis
Authors: Lisa Affengruber, Robert Emprechtinger, Emma Persad, Jos Kleijnen, Gerald Gartlehner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbstract screening is a crucial step in systematic reviews, yet it can be prone to errors, leading to the exclusion of potentially relevant studies. Understanding the characteristics associated with falsely excluded studies can inform strategies to improve screening processes and minimize errors.MethodsWe conducted a meta-epidemiological analysis of a dataset from a methods study, comprising 924 inclusion and exclusion decisions for 73 studies. We examined the study, abstract, journal, and reviewer characteristics associated with the falsely excluded abstracts. The characteristics examined included the study design, publication year, risk of bias, abstract content, country of conduct, journal indexing, SCImago Journal & Country Rank, and reviewer characteristics.ResultsAbstract decisions referring to nonrandomized studies of interventions (NRSI) constituted the highest proportion of false exclusions (19.6%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 12.7%–28.8%) compared to nonrandomized controlled trials (NRCTs) (6.7%; 95% CI, 1.2%–29.8%), randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (9.3%; 95% CI, 7.5%–11.7%), and interrupted time series (ITS) (12.7%; 95% CI, 7.0%–21.8%). Abstracts that insufficiently reported study information (18.4%; 95% CI, 14.6%–22.8%) and study design (15.4%; 95% CI, 10.3%–20.6%) were more often falsely excluded by reviewers than abstracts sufficiently reporting study information (6.0%; 95% CI, 4.4%–8.3%) and study design (9.4%; 95% CI, 7.3%–11.5%).ConclusionsTo reduce false exclusion decisions in abstracts, study authors should implement clear, structured, and informative abstract reporting, explicitly mentioning the study design and key details. These findings highlight the need for adherence to current reporting standards, reviewer training, and reviewer support (e.g., meetings, discussions), to improve the accuracy of systematic reviews.Trial registrationhttps://osf.io/7xhkj.

Background

As the need for rapid evidence syntheses continues to grow, research is increasingly focused on exploring rapid review methods. To address research questions in a timely manner, rapid reviews often only use one reviewer to screen the literature, which has been shown to reduce screening time by approximately 60% compared to dual independent screening [1, 2].

However, the single-reviewer screening process is error-prone and may falsely exclude relevant studies. A crowd-based randomized controlled trial (RCT) found that single-reviewer abstract screening missed 13% of relevant studies on average [3]. Similarly, the authors of recent retrospective observational study reported that single-reviewer abstract screening missed 9% (95% confidence interval [CI], 6%–12%) [2] to 11% (95% CI, 7%–14%) [4] of relevant studies. In a systematic review on single- versus dual-reviewer screening, the median proportion of missed studies was 5% (95% CI, 0%–58%) [5]. Comparatively, dual screening missed only 3% of relevant studies on average [3]. To avoid the risk of false exclusions, dual-reviewer screening is regarded as the international standard for systematic reviews [6–8].

The characteristics of studies falsely excluded during literature screening are poorly researched [9]. A recent retrospective observational study found that the most common exclusion criteria for falsely excluded studies were ineligible population (59%), ineligible exposure (58%), and ineligible outcome (35%) [2]. However, the influence that specific study types, abstract formats, publication journals, and reviewer experience level have on the risk of false exclusion is largely unknown [10]. To clarify this, we conducted a systematic review to identify the characteristics of falsely excluded studies [9]. We identified three methods studies, two case studies, and one case series for inclusion [11–13]. Two studies reported that up to 8% of studies were falsely excluded due to uninformative titles and abstracts [11, 12], and one study showed that 11% of eligible non-English studies were falsely excluded [14]. Due to the limited evidence identified, we could not draw any firm conclusions about the characteristics that might predict a higher risk of false exclusion [9].

Recognizing the characteristics that might lead to the false exclusion of studies or publications can benefit researchers conducting evidence synthesis. This awareness could enable them to approach the screening process with increased caution, especially when they encounter studies presenting potential risk factors for erroneous exclusions.

The objective of our methods study was to identify characteristics on a study, abstract, journal, and reviewer level that are associated with false exclusion of studies during single-reviewer title and abstract screening.

Methods

For this methods study, we use the term “abstract decisions leading to false exclusion” as our dataset is based on abstract-level decisions. However, when discussing broader implications, we may refer to “falsely excluded studies” to emphasize the impact on the overall systematic review process. We registered our study protocol at the Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/7xhkj. We followed the EQUATOR guideline for the reporting of meta-epidemiological methodology research [15].

Data sources

We utilized a database provided by Gartlehner et al. (2020) of a crowd-based, parallel-group RCT that assessed the accuracy of single-reviewer abstract screening [3]. This database included data from two published systematic reviews:

Pharmacological review: This systematic review focused on comparing pharmacological versus nonpharmacological interventions for depression [16]. The review included only RCTs.Public health review: This review examined environmental interventions aimed at reducing the consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages [17]. The review included interrupted time series (ITS), nonrandomized controlled trials (NRCTs), nonrandomized studies of interventions (NRSIs), and RCTs. ITS were defined as quasi-experimental studies that evaluated the effect of an intervention by analysing outcome trends over multiple time points before and after the intervention. NRCTs included experimental studies in which investigators actively allocated an intervention but without randomization, whereas RCTs used random allocation to intervention or control groups. NRSIs, in contrast, included observational designs (e.g., cohort, cross-sectional) where the exposure or intervention was not allocated by the investigators.

The database comprised 24,942 inclusion and exclusion decisions for abstracts made by 280 reviewers, covering 80 eligible primary studies. We excluded seven primary studies, corresponding to 76 abstract decisions and 260 reviewers, because they were companion publications to included studies, leaving 73 primary studies that met the inclusion criteria of the respective reviews. A total of 260 reviewers made 924 screening decisions, of which 826 were correct inclusion decisions and 98 were false exclusion decisions.

Data collection

For the 73 studies described above, we extracted the available information on study-, abstract-, and journal-level characteristics on each reference from EndNote and the full texts; missing information was retrieved through internet searches. One investigator extracted the information, which a second investigator checked for completeness and accuracy. Additionally, we utilized the information on reviewer characteristics collected in the study by Gartlehner et al. [3]. The authors [3] surveyed the reviewers on various characteristics, including their profession, expertise, experience, and native speaker status. Additionally, several measures, such as the time taken per decision and the number of assessments completed on a given day, were recorded during the screening process. The data were collected in a pilot-tested Excel spreadsheet. Appendix 1 presents the extracted study-, abstract-, journal- and reviewer-level items.

Data analysis

In our analysis, we examined 924 inclusion and exclusion decisions on abstracts to understand the characteristics of frequently falsely excluded abstracts and associated studies.

We conducted our data analysis using R version 4.3.2, employing both exploratory and descriptive statistical methods, to gain comprehensive insights into our dataset.

First, we performed an exploratory data analysis, which provided visual insights into the data’s distribution, patterns, outliers, and anomalies [18]. Following visual inspection, we excluded unrealistic outliers from the dataset to ensure the robustness of our analysis. For continuous data, we calculated medians and quartiles to provide measures of central tendency and dispersion. For categorical variables, we performed cross-tabulations and calculated proportions with 95% CIs.

In this paper, we present all relevant plots, including histograms, density plots, and bar plots, to illustrate the variables’ characteristics and distributions. While histograms use discrete bars to represent the data frequency, density plots provide a continuous curve that represents the estimated data density at each variable value, which can be thought of as a smoothed version of the histogram. This density is estimated using the kernel density function. A key property of density plots is that the total area under the curve (AUC) sums to 1 [19]. Additionally, we present a Sankey diagram—a flow diagram that visualizes the flow of data between variables—with the width of each arrow proportional to the quantity of the data points. The Sankey provides a clear visual representation of the variables’ interconnections and relative proportions, enhancing the understanding of the data structure [20].

Deviations from the study protocol

Our study protocol (https://osf.io/7xhkj) aimed to identify predictors for falsely excluding eligible studies during single-reviewer title and abstract screening. Initially, we planned to build a random forest model to identify the predictors for false exclusion. However, due to the highly imbalanced data, with only 10.6% of the abstract decisions leading to false exclusion, the model exhibited low precision for the false exclusion class (0.20) and an AUC of 63%. Despite attempts to mitigate the imbalance through various techniques [21, 22], our dataset did not demonstrate improved precision for the false exclusion class. Consequently, we shifted our approach and focused on conducting a meta-epidemiological exploratory data analysis to identify characteristics associated with abstract decisions leading to false exclusion.

We observed that false exclusions often occurred when the study information (population, intervention, or comparison) was not sufficiently described in the abstracts (e.g., a study evaluating a food intervention did not mention that beverages were also assessed), highlighting the subjective nature of the screening process. To account for this variability, we introduced a new variable “Abstract provides sufficient study information (yes/no).”

Study characteristics

The proportions of false exclusions resulting from abstract-screening decisions varied across study designs. Decisions involving NRSIs (n = 92) had the highest proportion of false exclusions at 19.6% (n = 18; 95% CI, 12.7%–28.8%). For decisions involving ITS studies (n = 79), the proportion of false exclusions was 12.7% (n = 10; 95% CI, 7.0%–21.8%). RCTs (n = 738) had a lower proportion of false exclusions at 9.3% (n = 69; 95% CI, 7.5%–11.7%), and NRCTs (n = 15) the lowest proportion at 6.7% (n = 1; 95% CI, 1.2%–29.8%) (see Fig. 2).

Abstract characteristics

Reviewers made false exclusion decisions in 11.3% (95% CI, 9.2%–13.8%) of cases (n = 83 decisions) when an abstract was structured, compared to 7.9% (95% CI, 4.9%–12.7%) of cases (n = 15) when an abstract was unstructured. Similarly, the proportion of false exclusion decisions was comparable for abstracts with and without reported study sample sizes and study aims: 10.4% (n = 82; 95% CI, 8.4%–12.7%) vs. 11.9% (n = 16; 95% CI, 7.5%–18.5%) for sample sizes, and 11.1%, (n = 62, 95% CI, 8.9%–13.9%) vs. 9.6% (n = 30; 95% CI, 6.8%–13.3%) for aims.

The proportion of decisions leading to false exclusion was higher when the study design was not reported in the abstract (15.4%; n = 29; 95% CI, 11.0%–21.3%), compared to when the study design was reported (9.4%; n = 69; 95% CI, 7.5%–11.7%). Abstracts that provided sufficient study information (as described in Deviations from the study protocol) were falsely excluded by reviewers less often (6.0%; n = 35; 95% CI, 4.4%–8.3%) compared to those that insufficiently described the study information (18.4%; n = 63; 95% CI, 14.6%–22.8%).

The Sankey diagram illustrates the flow in absolute numbers between the variables presented above for abstract decisions leading to false exclusions and correct inclusions (see Figs. 4 and 5). Abstracts leading to false exclusions compared to correct inclusions had lower proportions of reported study designs and study information and higher proportions of NRSIs and high risk of bias studies (see also Table 1).

Native speaker status

Among the 260 reviewers, 26.9% (n = 70) were native English speakers. The proportion of reviewers who falsely excluded at least one abstract was 25.7% (18/70) for native English speakers and 30.5% for nonnative English speakers (58/190) (see Table 1).

Professional background and expertise

Out of 141 reviewers with available data, most (39.0%, n = 55) identified as researchers. The proportion of reviewers who falsely excluded at least one abstract was similar across different professions (see Table 1) compared to those who correctly included all abstracts. Most reviewers self-rated their experience with systematic reviews as “Very good” and “Good,” while topic expertise was mostly self-rated as “Average” and “Below average.”

The median time for correct inclusions was 27.0 sec compared to 28.0 sec for false exclusions.

Discussion

Our dataset revealed that abstracts referring to nonrandomized studies of interventions were more prone to false exclusion. Abstracts that provided insufficient study information and did not report the study design were more often falsely excluded by reviewers than abstracts sufficiently reporting the study information and design. The analysis of screening decisions and reviewer characteristics within our dataset offers insights into the title and abstract screening process and the factors contributing to false exclusions.

While studies focusing on falsely excluded studies during the abstract screening process are limited, evidence supports the notion that RCTs generally exhibit better reporting than observational studies [23]. This may be less a consequence of the study design itself and more a surrogate of reporting quality, as RCTs are more consistently required to adhere to standards such as the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement. Observational studies (e.g., cohort or cross-sectional studies) often lack detailed or clear reporting due to their complex methodology and fail to clearly articulate their methods, making it challenging for reviewers to discern key aspects from the abstract alone [24].

Additionally, evidence from a systematic review [9] and a study by Hartley and Benjamin [25] showed that inadequate reporting, such as not reporting the study design in abstracts, can lead to reduced visibility and potentially contribute to false exclusions during screening. Poorly written abstracts lacking clarity or important details may fail to convey the relevance or significance of the study [26, 27], leading to their exclusion during screening.

Our study emphasizes the critical role of clear and informative abstracts in facilitating accurate screening decisions. Authors and journals should prioritize the provision of comprehensive abstracts that succinctly convey key study details. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for observational studies [28] and the CONSORT statement for RCTs [29] both recommend structured abstracts for clear visibility, including key aspects of study design, methods, and results. This structured and transparent reporting more easily enables readers to understand and evaluate a study’s methods. Therefore, the endorsement and enforcement of reporting guidelines by journals can enhance the transparency and quality of published studies, ultimately improving the screening process and minimizing the risk of excluding relevant studies.

By adhering to reporting guidelines, clear and comprehensive abstracts enable reviewers to quickly assess a study’s relevance, thereby reducing the risk of false exclusions. Our findings indicate that falsely excluded abstracts often lacked sufficient reporting of the study design and general study information. This underscores the need for reviewers to exercise caution and only exclude studies if they are certain. In cases where there is uncertainty due to missing information in the abstract, it may be prudent to order the full paper for a more thorough evaluation.

The approach chosen for screening abstracts can depend significantly on the review format and decision-makers’ needs. For instance, Cochrane reviews or systematic reviews for guideline development should aim to include all available evidence and, therefore, ordering the full paper when a reviewer is uncertain should be the goal. In contrast, for rapid reviews, it might be acceptable to exclude studies unless the information to include them is readily available. Another critical aspect is the inclusion and exclusion criteria form used during abstract screening. To ensure comprehensive and accurate screening, it is essential to include all various terminologies that might be used by the study authors. Less-experienced reviewers may not be familiar with or recognize the diverse terminology used in different studies [30]. By incorporating a wide range of terms, the eligibility criteria form can help mitigate this issue and ensure that relevant studies are not overlooked. Similarly, it is important to mention all relevant criteria of interest. By explicitly listing, for example, all interventions, the eligibility criteria form can guide reviewers in identifying pertinent studies, thereby enhancing the systematic review’s reliability and comprehensiveness. Implementing reviewer support, such as through clear screening forms, piloting, training, discussions, and standardized inclusion/exclusion criteria, can help mitigate false exclusion decisions. Additionally, limiting abstract screening to a maximum of three hours per day can help prevent reviewer fatigue and potentially reduce the risk of false exclusions [30].

Our study has limitations. Our dataset was small and focused on specific research topics, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other research topics. Additionally, while we measured the time per decision, we could not control for external activities by the reviewers, which may have affected the accuracy of the time measurements. Further, our analysis may not have accounted for all factors that could influence the risk of false exclusions.

To gain a broader understanding of the characteristics for false exclusion during screening, replicating the study using a larger dataset encompassing a wider range of study characteristics, research topics, and publication sources would be valuable. Ongoing research and collaboration within the scientific community can contribute to a better understanding of the characteristics of studies falsely excluded during abstract screening and help refine screening processes to minimize the risk of excluding relevant studies. Considering the potential for false exclusions, exploring and evaluating alternative retrieval strategies to identify studies that might have been falsely excluded during screening is imperative. Research investigating various methods, such as artificial intelligence for identifying falsely excluded studies, or conventional methods, such as reference list checking and citation tracking, can provide insights into the effectiveness of these approaches in mitigating the risk of excluding valuable studies.

Conclusion

To reduce the occurrence of falsely excluded abstracts, it is crucial for study authors to implement clear, structured, and informative abstract reporting. To facilitate accurate screening decisions, authors should adhere to reporting standards. Determining the screening approach is a crucial choice: either excluding studies lacking information in the abstract or ordering full papers when uncertain. The former approach, while efficient, risks omitting relevant studies, whereas the latter ensures thoroughness but significantly increases the workload. This decision should be guided by the review’s purpose and available resources. These findings underscore the multifaceted nature of false exclusions during abstract screening, suggesting the need for adherence to current reporting standards, reviewer training, and support (e.g., meetings, discussions) to improve the accuracy of systematic reviews.
