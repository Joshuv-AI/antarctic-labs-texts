Acute Hemorrhagic Leukoencephalitis With Active Hepatitis B Virus Infection
Authors: Shivam Mirg, Jasmine Parihar, Manjari Tripathi, Deepti Vibha, Rajesh Kumar Singh, Arunmozhimaran Elavarasi, Animesh Das, Ajay Garg, Megha Brijwal, Mehar Chand Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
CNS involvement in hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is rare. We report a 27-year-old woman with HBV infection presenting with headaches and seizures, whose imaging and brain biopsy confirmed acute hemorrhagic leukoencephalitis. She responded well to steroid and tenofovir therapy.

Case Presentation

A 27-year-old woman presented with a 20-day history of a persistent, left-sided, throbbing headache (VAS score 8/10), associated with vomiting and photophobia. One week later, she experienced an unprovoked tonic-clonic seizure with tongue biting. Her neurologic examination was notable for agitation and irritability but otherwise unremarkable. Six months earlier, she had a history of postpartum urosepsis complicated by acute kidney injury requiring dialysis and blood transfusion. She recovered within 15 days to asymptomatic state with normal renal functions.

MRI of the brain showed multiple hemorrhagic, ring-enhancing lesions with associated perilesional edema, affecting the bilateral hemispheres (Figure 1, A–C, F). Susceptibility-weighted imaging revealed microhemorrhages within the lesion cores, and a subset of these lesions demonstrated peripheral diffusion restriction (Figure 1, D and E). ^18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose PET (^18 F-FDG PET) showed increased metabolic activity at the brain lesion sites, with no abnormal uptake elsewhere. CSF analysis showed normal opening pressure, a mild pleocytosis of 10 cells/µL (all lymphocytes), elevated protein (115 mg/dL; normal <45 mg/dL), and normal glucose (63 mg/dL; corresponding blood glucose 114 mg/dL). The CSF infection panel was negative for fungal, bacterial, parasitic, and tuberculosis infections, and no atypical cells were identified. Patient tested negative for John Cunningham (JC) virus in CSF, neuromyelitis optica antibodies, and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies in serum and CSF. Autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antigen and extractable nuclear antigens, were also negative.

The patient tested positive for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBeAg), and had elevated serum hepatitis B DNA titers (7.74 × 103 International Units per milliliter [IU/mL]). Hepatitis B virus (HBV) DNA was also detected in the CSF. Notably, her antenatal screening 6 months prior had been negative for HBsAg. Owing to the uncertain etiology of the brain lesions, a brain biopsy was performed, revealing areas of hemorrhage and necrosis with infiltrating histiocytes, without granulomas or organisms (Figure 2). HBV DNA was detected in the biopsy sample, confirming viral involvement.

The patient was treated with IV corticosteroids, followed by an oral taper over 2 months. She was also started on tenofovir 300 mg daily, which has been continued. Within 2 weeks of therapy initiation, her symptoms had completely resolved.

At 1-year follow-up, she remained asymptomatic. Serial MRI scans at 3 and 6 months demonstrated a marked reduction in the size of previous lesions, resolution of contrast enhancement, and no new lesions (Figure 1, G–L).

Discussion

Acute hemorrhagic leukoencephalitis (AHLE) is an uncommon and severe demyelinating disease characterized by a rapid, progressive inflammation of the white matter. While its precise etiology remains unclear, evidence suggests that infectious agents and vaccinations may serve as potential triggers, identified in nearly 35% of cases.1 HBV infection has been associated with several autoimmune conditions, including autoimmune hepatitis, systemic lupus erythematosis (SLE), aplastic anemia, antiphospholipid syndrome, polyarteritis nodosa, rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes, thyroid disorders, and uveitis, although causality remains unproven.1 While not strongly associated, several CNS autoimmune disorders have occasionally been reported in the setting of HBV infection. These include multiple sclerosis,2 acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM),3 recurrent transverse myelitis,3 anti-CASPR2 myeloencephalitis,4 and CLIPPERS (chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement responsive to steroids).5

In this case, we observed an immune-mediated CNS reaction to recent HBV infection, presenting as an AHLE-like demyelinating episode. The detection of HBV DNA in both CSF and brain biopsy samples, along with recent HBV seroconversion, suggests a possible immunologic response triggered by HBV. This infection was likely acquired through blood transfusion or hemodialysis 6 months prior, with no other identifiable triggers upon comprehensive evaluation.

Diagnosing AHLE involves neuroimaging, CSF analysis, and histopathology, which help exclude mimics such as other demyelinating disorders: ADEM, MOG-associated disorders, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, tumefactive multiple sclerosis, and neurosarcoidosis.6 Other differentials include CNS lymphoma, herpes simplex encephalitis, acute necrotizing encephalopathy, and CNS vasculitis.7 In this patient, these differentials were excluded through a combination of imaging, antibody testing, CSF findings, and histopathologic examination. It is sometimes challenging to differentiate AHLE from ADEM because these conditions may represent a spectrum of similar pathologies. Pathologic findings in AHLE often include perivascular demyelination, lymphocytic infiltration, perivascular petechial hemorrhages, vasculitis, and fibrinoid necrosis of the vessel wall, all of which were observed in our patient.7

This case is unique as it demonstrates a strong association between active HBV infection and AHLE. Potential mechanisms underlying HBV-associated autoimmunity include molecular mimicry between HBV antigens and myelin basic protein, immune complex formation, apoptosis with exposure of intracellular antigens, and epitope modification.8 HBV has been shown to infect various cell types beyond hepatocytes raising the possibility that the virus could access the CNS through infected peripheral blood mononuclear cells.9 Although it is unclear whether the presence of HBV DNA in the CNS reflects active viral replication or merely local protein expression, the detection of viral components introduces the intriguing possibility of direct viral involvement in the pathogenesis of AHLE, rather than it being solely a consequence of a systemic autoimmune response.

This case also underscores the potential for a favorable response to corticosteroid therapy in AHLE. It also reinforces the importance of considering steroids despite delayed presentation. In addition, tenofovir was used to control HBV replication. At present, there are no established guidelines for the management of HBV-related extrahepatic disorders. Antiviral therapy with nucleos(t)ide analogs may help improve milder symptoms; however, more severe neurologic manifestations often necessitate the use of immunomodulatory agents for effective control.10 As per American Association for the Study of Liver and European Association for the Study of Liver guidelines, in noncirrhotic HBeAg-positive patients like ours, sustained HBeAg seroconversion is a key indicator for safely discontinuing nucleos(t)ide analog (NUC) therapy. Treatment may be stopped if seroconversion to anti-HBe is achieved, HBV DNA is undetectable, and alanine aminotransferase levels are normal after at least 12 months of consolidation therapy.11,12 This strategy is planned for our patient, with viral markers monitored every 3 months. Glucocorticoids are the most frequently used treatment (97%), with other therapies such as plasmapheresis (26%), IV immunoglobulins (12%), rituximab, and cyclophosphamide used variably.6 The prognosis of AHLE is generally poor, with mortality rates reaching up to 46%.6 Delayed diagnosis, a fulminant disease course, and limited effective treatment options contribute to this high mortality rate. In this case, however, the patient achieved a favorable outcome with early intervention, including steroids and antiviral therapy. At the 10-month follow-up, she was asymptomatic, with MRI indicating significant resolution of lesions. This positive outcome is likely due to the early diagnosis, prompt therapeutic intervention, and effective management of the HBV infection, which was a probable trigger.

In summary, clinicians should consider HBV as a potential trigger in cases of AHLE, and a thorough diagnostic workup for HBV should be included in these cases. Histopathologic evaluation should be pursued when feasible to exclude other mimics. Treating identified triggers, such as HBV, may improve outcomes in AHLE.
