Geographical dataset of questing nymphal density for three key tick vector species of Lyme disease
Authors: Yinsheng Zhang, Yifan Sun, Jinchen Wang, Xiaolong Wu, Luqi Wang, Xin Yang, Yiyang Guo, Ruying Fang, Linxuan Miao, Man Yang, Bingjie Peng, Sophie O Vanwambeke, Sen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNymphal ticks of Ixodes pacificus, Ixodes scapularis, Ixodes ricinus, and Ixodes persulcatus are primary vectors of Lyme disease, which affects both animal and human health. Understanding their population dynamic is therefore critical for public health risk assessment. Here we present a focused dataset of questing nymph density, comprising 3579 records from 136 publications from 1980 to 2024. The dataset includes three tick species that represent major Lyme disease risk in the Northern Hemisphere. Records were compiled through systematic literature review and preserved original density measurements with their respective units (e.g., nymphs/m², nymphs/100 m²). Each record contains detailed metadata including tick species, geographical coordinates, temporal resolution and collection methods. This resource provides transparent and structured tick surveillance data essential for ecological modelling, disease risk prediction, and public health risk mapping.

Background & Summary

Ticks pose a growing public health concern in temperate regions of the Northern Hemispher1–3. The local risk of contracting Lyme disease directly correlates with the population density of questing ticks4. Among developmental stages, nymphs are expected to transmit the majority of human infections, including Borrelia burgdorferi s.l. (pathogenic agent of Lyme disease)5,6. This is primarily due to their (1) small size, which makes them difficult to detect; (2) high abundance relative to adults; (3) questing height on vegetation, which may increase encounter rates with humans compared to larvae; and (4) critical epidemiological role in transmitting pathogens that are not vertically transmitted. Therefore, accurate data on nymphal tick density are essential for effective public health risk assessment7.

The ecology of questing ticks is complex, influenced by host populations, weather, climate, and local environmental conditions8. While regional studies have provided valuable insights into local population dynamics, the absence of a unified, large-scale dataset limits our ability to identify broad patterns and risk factors9. Previous studies have primarily focused on specific regions with diverse objectives, different collection methods and reporting standards, leading to substantial data heterogeneity10–13. The most critical variation concerns the metrics used to quantify tick abundance. Specifically, many studies report density based on collection effort (e.g., nymphs per hour) or as simple counts14,15, rather than area-based metrics (e.g., nymphs per square meter or per hectare). Additionally, some studies do not differentiate between life stages in their reporting, thereby obscuring the specific risk assessment posed by nymphs. These inconsistencies produce fragmented and incomparable data, which prevents comprehensive understanding of tick bite hazard and infection risk. Moreover, these limitations impede robust and comprehensive analyses between regions, making it difficult to predict tick-borne disease risk, identify emerging hotspots, or define distribution boundaries. A transparent, structured dataset focusing on comparable metrics is therefore essential for advancing ecological modelling and developing targeted public health strategies.

To address this bottleneck, we present a geographical dataset that systematically compiles field data on questing nymph density for three key species: Ixodes scapularis, Ixodes pacificus, and Ixodes ricinus9,16–24. For Ixodes persulcatus, the overwhelming majority of ticks collected were represented by adults25, largely because adults display markedly higher questing aggressiveness than nymphs, and it is difficult to collect nymphs by flagging methods26–28. Therefore, Ixodes persulcatus were not included in this dataset. The distributions of I. pacificus, I. scapularis and I. ricinus include much of the distribution of Borrelia burgdorferi s.l., and their population densities are sufficiently investigated in the literature to make a systematic compilation of questing tick data feasible. The dataset includes data reported specifically for the nymphal life stage, preserves density values with their original units as published in the literature, and documents the specific spatial and temporal resolution available for each record (e.g. coordinate precision and temporal aggregation level). This unified, publicly available dataset enables analysis of distribution-wide patterns in nymphal tick abundance and reveals existing heterogeneity in sampling methods and spatiotemporal recording scales, underscoring the importance of transparent sampling protocols and standardized records for future research.

Methods

Literature search and screening

We conducted a systematic literature search, screening, and data extraction following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (Fig. 1)29. Our dataset comprises English-language publications retrieved from Web of Science and PubMed databases, covering the period from 1980 to 2024. Our search strategy identified studies containing quantitative field data on three tick vector species: Ixodes scapularis, Ixodes pacificus and Ixodes ricinus. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we included historical and taxonomic synonyms: Ixodes dammini for I. scapularis; Acarus ricinus for I. ricinus. We combined species names with tick density terms (“abundance”, “density”, “population dynamics”). The detailed search queries for each database are presented in Table 1.

Our literature search commenced on August 19, 2024, and was completed by September 2024, yielding 6942 publications. All retrieved records were initially imported into EndNote X9 to remove duplicates before screening. Three reviewers (Y Z, Y S, and J W) screened titles and abstracts during the primary screening stage, excluded non-research publications (reviews, editorials, books) and studies unrelated to ticks. Nine reviewers (Y Z, X W, L W, X Y, Y G, R F, M Y, B P, and L M) independently conducted full-text screening, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. This process identified 136 publications meeting the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction and standardization

We extracted data from all studies that satisfied the inclusion criteria into a standardized template. The initial data extraction was performed manually by one reviewer (Y Z). The extracted content included three main categories: 1) geographical metadata, such as latitude, longitude, country, and site descriptions; 2) sampling method, including collection methods and sampling periods; and 3) the density measurements, for which we recorded the exact numerical values and their original units in the density_unit column. To systematically address the significant heterogeneity in how temporal data were reported (e.g., single collection events vs. monthly or seasonal aggregates), we developed a standardized temporal classification protocol and stored the resulting category for each record in the temporal_resolution field. The specific categories and definitions for this field are detailed in the Data Records section. For data not available in tables, we used WebPlotDigitizer (https://wpd.starrydata2.org/) to extract values from figures or contacted authors directly to obtain the original data. Following extraction and standardization, the entire dataset was independently cross-checked by two additional reviewers (Y S and S L) against the original publications to resolve any discrepancies through discussion and ensure the accuracy of the final data.

Geographical positioning

We employed a systematic approach to extract geographic coordinates from original publications reporting nymphal density. Whenever available, we extracted geocoordinates directly from the text, tables, or supplementary materials and classified their precision as “Extracted point/area”. For locations requiring geocoding, including those where original publications only provided coordinate ranges, lacked explicit coordinates but contained clear descriptions of sampling areas (e.g., a named park, forest, or village), or described larger administrative units such as provinces/states, counties, or municipalities, we first searched for the locality through GeoNames (https://www.geonames.org/) to obtain standardized coordinates. When localities could not be found in GeoNames, we referred to the original text descriptions and any accompanying maps to locate the sampling area in Google Maps, cross-validating these sources to extract the centroid coordinates with decimal precision appropriate to the spatial scale of the record. These geocoded records were classified as either “Geocoded area” (for specific named locations like parks, forests, or villages) or “Polygon” (for larger or less precisely defined administrative units where the geometric centroid of the specified region was used as the representative coordinate)30. All coordinates were standardized to a number of decimal places consistent with the spatial precision of the respective record. In addition, each record was assigned a coordinate_uncertainty category (Precise, Approximate, Moderate, or Coarse) based on the geographic specificity reported in the source literature, to provide a transparent assessment of spatial reliability (see Data records below). For each coordinates, we extracted elevation values from the Global Multi-resolution Terrain Elevation Data 2010 (GMTED2010) Digital Elevation Model at a spatial resolution of 1 km31. Elevation extraction was performed using ArcMap 10.8, with coordinates matched to the nearest grid cell centroid. The extracted elevation values were stored in the elevation/m_1000m field to distinguish them from elevation values directly reported in the source literature (elevation/m field). Two researchers (Y S, S O V) independently cross-checked all extracted records and geocoded locations against the original publications to ensure accuracy.

Data Records

The data are provided as two Comma-Separated Values (CSV) files and available at Figshare32. The main file “Tick_density_raw.csv” contains the nymphal density records, and the file “Tick_density_metadata.csv” explains the full description for all columns. The final dataset consists of 3579 individual records of nymphal tick density (Fig. 2). An “individual record” represents a single row in the dataset, defined as a measurement of nymphal density extracted at the finest temporal resolution available in the source literature. When raw data were unavailable, we used the temporal resolution as reported in the original publications to preserve data precision without aggregating or interpolating measurements, with daily observations being the finest temporal resolution included. When multiple samples were reported from the same place and month, individual events are distinguished by a unique collection identifier (col_id). To address variation in temporal data reporting, we introduced a “temporal_resolution” column (formatted as reporting_interval/aggregation_type). This field reflects the temporal precision of each record. Technical Validation

We implemented a rigorous, multi-step validation process. Overall, primary data were extracted and comprehensively verified to eliminate redundancies and confirm accuracy. The geocoding stage incorporated an independent cross-validation process. To maintain consistency and reliability, all team members adhered to standardized inclusion criteria throughout data compilation.

The cross-validation process examined three primary domains of the data. First, for data value accuracy, extracted nymphal density values (density_mean, SD, collection_method, etc.) and their original units were compared against the source publications to ensure numerical accuracy. Second, for geospatial validity, the process involved plausibility checks of explicit coordinates using satellite imagery and verification of geocoded locations against the source text. All geocoded records were reviewed by two additional researchers (J W and X W) to confirm the accuracy of the final coordinates, precision labels, and coordinate uncertainty classifications. Finally, the temporal_resolution (reporting_interval/aggregation_type) field was rigorously reviewed to confirm that the assigned reporting_interval and aggregation_type classifications consistently and accurately adhered to our established protocol (Data records). Any discrepancies identified during this phase were resolved by consensus discussion among all researchers, and standardized inclusion criteria were strictly adhered to throughout the process33.

Usage Notes

This dataset represents a specialized resource with applications in disease ecology, public health, and environmental science. The data facilitate continental and regional scale meta-analyses identifying environmental and climatic drivers of nymphal tick abundance. These comprehensive density data support the development and validation of species distribution models. Furthermore, the dataset establishes a historical baseline for assessing temporal population changes. It enables generation of high-resolution risk maps for nymphal tick hazards, which can support risk assessment and future surveillance efforts in high-density areas.

Density values require standardization to a common unit (e.g., nymphs per 100 m²) using the corresponding density_unit column before comparison. The dataset contains significant spatio-temporal heterogeneity, systematically documented through temporal_resolution (reporting_interval/aggregation_type) and coordinate_resolution fields. Grouped analyses that account for this structural heterogeneity are essential for valid comparisons and robust conclusions in modeling and risk assessment applications. Given the spatial heterogeneity documented in the coordinate_uncertainty field, we recommend that users incorporate habitat or land-cover information using buffer-based extraction methods (e.g., with buffer sizes appropriate to each record’s uncertainty level) rather than point-based assignments. Standardized land-cover products, such as CORINE Land Cover or the National Land Cover Database, can be employed to address specific research objectives. Questing nymphal density serves as a critical indicator of human disease risk rather than a measure of absolute tick population size, as the data captured only the actively host-seeking fraction at sampling time. Because ticks remain off-host for most of their life cycle—questing, digesting, molting, or in diapause—and the data did not include host-attached ticks, these measurements provide only a partial representation of population dynamics. In addition, field-based ecological studies introduce additional sampling biases that merit consideration. Most studies are conducted during favorable weather conditions at accessible locations, and the collection methods (e.g., cloth dragging, flagging) of questing ticks are known to be sub-optimal with highly variable efficiency34. These values should therefore be interpreted only as an indicator of questing activity. Variation in the details of sampling site selection and elaboration of collection protocols likely affect compatibility between studies. This highlights the need for standardized sampling protocols to improve comparability across future surveillance efforts.

Users should be aware that the study’s objective for questing tick density data could be different. For instance, studies aiming to investigate pathogen prevalence may preferentially sample in “good tick habitat” to maximize collection. In contrast, some records may be derived from studies designed to capture ecological heterogeneity (e.g., sampling across both good and poor habitats within a single forest) or from random sampling designs, which may report relatively lower abundances than those from optimal habitats. Users should therefore carefully consider the potential bias, consult the study_obj column, or carefully reference the sampling and habitat details from the original publications to ensure the research questions and spatial scales in the data match their own study goals. Despite these limitations, this dataset constitutes the first large-scale synthesis of tick density data, as opposed to presence-only or distribution data. It provides a critical resource for advancing beyond studies limited to species distribution, offering a foundation for modeling abundance dynamics and quantifying disease risk across continents. As our systematic search was completed before September 2024, relevant studies published thereafter were not included. For example, recent large-scale study on I. ricinus density across Germany could not be incorporated within the current search timeframe34,35. We anticipate that this dataset will be iteratively updated to incorporate newly published data, thereby expanding its spatial and temporal coverage over time.
