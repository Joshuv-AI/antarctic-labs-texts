Evaluation and Mitigation of Time‐Dependent Confounding Effects in Conventional Exposure‐Response Analyses for Oncology Drugs
Authors: Xuefen Yin, Ye Xiong, Youwei Bi, Xin Wei, Hong Zhao, Elimika Pfuma Fletcher, Rajanikanth Madabushi, Amal Ayyoub, Hao Zhu, Stephan Schmidt, Jiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTConventional exposure–response (E–R) analyses, such as logistic regression and time‐to‐event analysis using summary exposure metrics, are often conducted in oncology using data from the pivotal trial(s) with a single dose level to support dosing decisions. However, these E–R analyses, affected by multiple confounding factors, may mischaracterize true E–R relationships, potentially limiting their utility in dosing decisions. This study investigates potential mischaracterization in such analyses influenced by the following time‐dependent confounding factors: exposure accumulation, dose modification patterns, and event onset time. We used a simulation‐based approach to evaluate two E–R scenarios: ER1, where event time generated with a Weibull distribution is not affected by drug exposure, and ER2, where the response is driven by drug exposure via a joint PK‐tumor size model. Our analyses indicate that when using time‐dependent exposure metrics (e.g., average concentration till event/censoring), exposure accumulation tends to induce an inverse E–R trend, while dose modifications (interruptions/reductions) likely induce a positive E–R trend. Simulations suggest that employing static exposure metrics (e.g., first‐cycle or steady‐state) minimizes these biases. Additionally, adopting an Emax model aligned with the underground truth in ER2 in the E–R analyses could reduce bias. When significant dose modifications are present, including relevant data from a dose‐range study and employing modified methods for time‐dependent exposure derivation may help mitigate bias. Overall, using multiple exposure metrics (including static ones) to assess E–R consistency and interpreting the potential causal effects with totality of evidence (including dose–response results) should be implemented to better inform dosing decisions.Keywords: bias control, dose modification, exposure–response, oncology dosing, PK accumulation, time‐dependent confounding factors

Introduction

Optimizing dosage in oncology treatment, as emphasized by recent efforts led by the FDA, underscores the importance of dosage selection to achieve a favorable profile with the efficacy and safety [1, 2]. Central to achieving this objective is a thorough understanding of the underlying exposure–response (E–R) relationships that determine the benefit–risk profile of the drug and can subsequently be used to optimize respective dosing regimens [3, 4, 5]. Given the importance of the E–R relationship, robust assessment and accurate interpretation are vital for efficient drug development and regulatory decision‐making.

In the clinical evaluation of oncology products, dose–response and/or E–R analyses are commonly used to characterize the relationship between drug exposure and treatment outcomes, such as overall response rate (ORR), progression‐free survival (PFS) for efficacy, and adverse events (AEs) of concern for safety. Conventional E–R analysis methods, for example, logistic regression and time‐to‐event analysis (typically Kaplan–Meier [KM] plots, Cox proportional‐hazards [Cox PH] models), generally utilize summary static exposure metrics (e.g., Cavg1C and CavgSS, representing average concentration over the first dosing cycle/interval or at steady state under the nominal dose, respectively) or time‐dependent exposures (also known as time‐averaged/aggregated exposures, e.g., CavgTE, representing average concentration until event/censoring) [6, 7]. Although longitudinal modeling can capture dynamic effects of time‐varying exposures, models using static and time‐aggregated summaries are often chosen for simplicity. However, challenges in correctly characterizing the E–R relationship arise from cancer heterogeneity, high variability in drug response among patients, and the lack of data from other dose levels other than the one evaluated in the pivotal trial (commonly the maximum tolerated dose based on the early dose escalation trial). Additionally, time‐dependent factors (e.g., changes in disease status, dose modifications due to adverse events) and their impacts on drug pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) further complicate E–R analyses and potentially lead to incorrect interpretations [8]. A well‐known example is nivolumab [9], for which responders exhibit declining clearance (and thus rising concentrations) as their disease status improves with time, while nonresponders maintain higher clearance and lower exposure, resulting in a falsely steeper slope for efficacy in E–R analyses using later‐stage or steady‐state PK metrics.

Efforts have been made by multiple groups to ameliorate confounding in E–R analysis resulting from time‐dependent exposure due to clearance change, derivation of exposure up to an event/censoring, both of which are particularly relevant to biologics with long dosing intervals. It suggests that conducting an adequate randomized dose‐range study and using time‐independent exposure would help reduce the risk of mischaracterizing the E–R relationship [7, 10]. Our study continued such efforts by systematically investigating the source, direction, and magnitude of bias introduced by time‐dependent exposure metrics, extending to clinical scenarios of exposure accumulation and/or dose modification (interruptions/reductions) that frequently occur with small molecule oncology drugs. In addition, the quantitative framework developed in this study enables exploration of potential bias‐mitigation strategies, which may facilitate an improved interpretation of E–R findings in clinical studies and improve drug development outcomes. The model and script files are available on the linked GitHub repository (https://github.com/XuefenYin/Time‐Dependent‐Confounding‐in‐ER‐Analysis.git).

Methods

Software

Simulations were conducted in R (version 4.4.0) using the mrgsolve package (E–R datasets generation, version 1.4.1), the glm function for logistic regression, the stan package for Emax model in logistic regression [11], and the survival package (version 3.5‐5) for KM plots and Cox PH models.

The Programming Structure for Data Simulation

To generate E–R datasets, we used a composite model with three components (Figure 1): drug, disease, and trial. The drug component consisted of a two‐compartment PK model mimicking an actual clinical case. PK parameters were subsequently adjusted to generate three scenarios representing drugs with short (PK1), moderate (PK2), and long (PK3) half‐lives. To simulate clinical outcome independent of drug exposure (i.e., ER1), the Weibull model was used to generate time‐to‐event datasets for two onset timings: EO1 (fast, 50% of events by ~110 days) and EO2 (slow, 50% of events by ~240 days). For exposure‐driven treatment response (i.e., ER2), a tumor size model with a sigmoidal Emax drug effect on tumor shrinkage was used to target a response/progression profile commonly seen in oncology clinical trials. It assumed zero‐order tumor growth with natural exponential shrinkage [12]. Representative efficacy endpoints ORR and PFS were derived to align with RECIST criteria: ORR was defined as the proportion of subjects with > 30% tumor reduction reflecting complete/partial response; PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to disease progression (> 20% tumor increase from baseline). For subjects with a PFS event, their last observation time was set at the PFS time point. The trial component incorporates key clinical factors: starting dose(s), sample size, dropout rate, and dosing profiles (i.e., constant, dynamic, and empirical dosing). The constant dosing (DH1) simulated a fixed dose throughout the treatment without any dose modification (interruptions/reductions). The dynamic dosing (DH2) employed a CP_AE model, where plasma concentrations (CPs) drive adverse events (AEs) and subsequent dose reduction, resulting in different degrees and patterns of dose modification based on the starting dose (Data S1 Section S1, Figure S1 for details). In contrast, the empirical dosing (DH3) used actual dosing records borrowed from a clinical trial for a drug with significant dose modifications, with up to 600 days follow‐up, where the dose pattern remained the same across different starting dose groups, mimicking scenarios where dose modifications occur similarly across multiple dose levels (e.g., purely due to patient compliance or gastrointestinal events that may not be sensitive to dose). Table S1 lists the model parameters, and Figure S2 illustrates the initial PK parameter distributions and dose modification patterns from the original dataset.

With the established drug‐disease‐trial modeling platform, virtual trials with different sample sizes (20–1000 per arm), dose levels (single vs. multiple dose levels), and ranges (up to 20‐fold) were simulated for various scenarios to evaluate their impact on E–R relationship characterization (see Section S2 for details). For example, for a scenario where clinical events are not affected by the drug exposures (ER1, e.g., no drug effect or effect plateaued), the PK dataset was created by administering a drug with a moderate length of half‐life (PK2) once daily at starting doses of 200 and 100 mg under dynamic dosing, with 500 subjects per dose arm. The time‐to‐event (TTE) dataset comprised 1000 observations: events based on a Weibull distribution plus 25% random dropout. The simulated TTE data were then merged with sampled subjects from the PK dataset, where the end time point for each individual was set to either censoring/event time or the last dosing time, whichever occurred first. For a scenario where clinical events are related to the drug (ER2), plasma concentration‐time profiles were linked to the tumor size model, and ORR and PFS event times were subsequently derived per the aforementioned criteria that formed the analysis dataset. In our simulation setting, intrinsic and extrinsic factors affecting either PK or response (efficacy or safety) were not incorporated in order to focus on the key issue of dose modification (interruptions/reductions). Refer to Table S2 for the simulated trials list and Figures S3–S8 for typical PK and PK‐PD profiles in ER1 and ER2. For ER2 with EC50 of 75 ng/mL, with PK2QD treatment, ORR plateaus at around 100 mg dose. The expected true E–R response with different exposure metrics for ORR and PFS event rates and onset times is summarized in (Section S3, Figures S9 and S10).

Investigation of Mischaracterization: Bias and Type I Error Rate

This section aims to identify sources of bias for conventional E–R analyses and their relationships with the PK properties of drugs, dose modification patterns, and event onset time. In ER1, a clinical outcome that may represent any efficacy or safety event not affected by the drug exposure was simulated based on different Weibull distributions to account for a fast or slow onset scenario; while in ER2, ORR and PFS driven by drug treatment were used as endpoints. In each simulated trial, both static and time‐dependent exposure metrics were calculated.

In ER1, for constant dosing without any dose modification (DH1), four PK‐dosing profiles were evaluated with a 60 mg dose, achieving steady state by Day 2 (PK1QD) or Day 5 (PK2QD) with daily dosing, or by 10 weeks with every‐two‐week (PK3Q2W) or every‐four‐week dosing (PK3Q4W). For empirical dosing (DH3), three PK‐dosing profiles (PK1QD, PK2QD, and PK3QD) were examined using a starting dose of 60 mg, followed by reductions to 40 and 20 mg. Each simulated trial was repeated 1000 times. Additionally, PK2QD with either constant or empirical dosing was used to evaluate the impact of sample sizes (20, 50, 100, 250, 500, and 1000) and random dropout rates (25% or 80%) on false E–R findings under both fast and slow event onset scenarios, with 500 replications per condition (see Table S3). Typical logistic regression and KM plots were generated to illustrate the direction of bias. Statistical significance was determined by applying a Wald test (p < 0.05) in logistic regression and Cox PH models, assuming a linear relationship between the PK metric and the log‐odds or log‐hazard of the event. A false E–R relationship was identified if the Type I error rate was significantly inflated (e.g., occurring in at least 10% of replications).

In ER2, E–R datasets were generated by incorporating the PK2 drug into the model, accounting for both constant (DH1) and dynamic dosing (DH2), with daily starting doses of 0, 60, 100, 200, and 500 mg to 1000 patients per simulation. For dynamic dosing, the percentage of patients with dose reductions increases with the starting dose level, with approximately 20%, 30%, 52%, and 75% at doses of 60, 100, 200, and 500 mg, respectively (Figure S11). Logistic regression and the Emax model in logistic regression visualized E–R relationships for ORR, which were compared with true E–R profiles (calculation strategies in Section S3) to identify bias, while KM plots visualized E–R relationships for PFS.

Modified CavgTE (mCavgTE) Derivation

We introduced a modified method to derive CavgTE (mCavgTE) for patients without an event when the majority of events happen early (e.g., ORR). This is an alternative to the conventional approach, which calculates CavgTE for non‐responders up to the last outcome assessment. In contrast, mCavgTE reassigns the last assessment day for patients without an event to either the last observed event onset day in the trial (based on the overall ORR distribution after outlier removal) or the patient's actual last assessment day—whichever occurs earlier. This is intended to reduce confounding effects related to time, for example, increased risk of dose modifications over time for non‐responders with long follow‐up. We compared the two methods for both ER1 and ER2 under dynamic dosing (DH2), where the last outcome assessment times in ER1 were randomly sampled with replacement from ER2 to match the timing and frequency of observed ORR events and censoring in ER2 (see Table S2 and Section S4 for details).

Results

The Source, Direction, and Degree of Mischaracterization

Clinical Outcome Unrelated to Drug Exposure (ER1)

For scenarios without significant dose modification (DH1), using time‐dependent exposure metrics like CavgTE tends to yield a false inverse E–R slope in logistic regression (Figure 2a,b), especially when the event occurs early relative to exposure accumulation. In a long accumulation phase (red line in Figure 2e), responders experience events before reaching steady‐state, artificially linking many early events to lower CavgTE and creating a strong inverse E–R trend that deviates from the ground truth. In contrast, faster attainment of steady state (Figure 2f) with minimal accumulation is associated with a lower degree and likelihood of this bias. Similar results were observed for time‐to‐event analyses where lower concentrations are falsely linked to earlier events and shorter survival, particularly for events with fast onset relative to exposure accumulation (Figures S12 and S13).

Typical plots for conventional Exposure–Response analysis using CavgTE in a scenario where drug exposure does not affect the response; CavgTE, Average concentration till event or censoring day; DH1, Constant dosing; DH3, Empirical dosing; EO1, Fast event onset; EO2, Slow event onset; ER1, Scenarios where drug exposure does not affect the response; PK2QD, Plasma concentrations reach to steady state at Day 5 with daily dosing; PK3Q2W, Plasma concentrations reach to steady state at Week 10 administered bi‐weekly; the gray bars represent the cumulative percentage of events over time, and the green line tracks the percentage of patients who experienced an event before their CavgTE reached steady state.

For scenarios with significant dose modifications (DH3), using time‐dependent exposure metrics for logistic regression resulted in a false positive E–R slope (Figure 2c,d). Under fast event onset EO1 (Figure 2g), many patients experience events early while still receiving relatively higher doses, leading to higher concentrations coinciding with events. Conversely, most patients without an event are more likely to undergo dose reductions over time until the end of treatment, resulting in lower concentrations as time goes on. The observed positive E–R trend is therefore a mischaracterization rather than a causal relationship. Under slow event onset EO2 (Figure 2h), more patients with events also have undergone dose reductions by the time the event occurs, which led to a lesser degree of positive bias. For time‐to‐event analyses, higher concentrations coincide with earlier events and shorter progression‐free survival—with greater bias for fast onset than slow onset (Figures S14 and S15).

For both scenarios with/without significant dose modifications, using static exposure metrics (e.g., Cavg1C and CavgSS: from the first dosing interval or steady state under the nominal dose) helps avoid these biases (Figure 3 and Figures S12–S15), as they are unaffected by subsequent exposure accumulation or dose modification. Moreover, in cases of late event onset, E–R analyses based on logistic regression tend to be less confounded as compared to that based on the Cox PH analysis. Regarding Type I error (defined as detecting a significant E–R slope with p < 0.05), Type I error rates remained below 10% with static exposure metrics. In contrast, time‐dependent exposure metrics led to inflated Type I error rates (exceeding 10%) in most cases—particularly with a large sample size where bias can be robustly detected. This inflation was more pronounced when event onset is rapid. Similar trends were observed for both logistic regression and Cox PH regression (Figures S16–S19) with greater inflation seen for Cox PH regression—even at a sample size as low as 20 under empirical dosing.

Clinical Outcome Caused by Drug Exposure (ER2)

For scenarios without significant dose modification (DH1), evaluating the ORR E–R relationship using logistic regression with CavgTE is likely to introduce a shallower E–R slope compared to the true E–R relationship (Figure S20) and will produce a false inverse relationship when the clinical outcome plateaus at the studied doses (e.g., at 200 and 500 mg), consistent with ER1 scenarios (Figure 4a). Including data from a placebo control group (i.e., placebo add‐on to background therapy) and fitting with an Emax model significantly reduced these biases (Figure 4b and Figure S21). We assume that the drugs show additive drug effects, with no synergism or antagonism. When PFS is the endpoint, KM curves stratified by exposure quartiles could not suggest an exposure effect even at a lower dose (i.e., 20 mg) with steady state Cavg around EC50 in our simulation (Figure 4e,f and Figure S22).

Typical plots for conventional Exposure–Response analysis using CavgTE in scenarios where response is driven by the drug exposure. For scenarios with significant dose modifications (DH2), evaluating the ORR E–R relationship using logistic regression with CavgTE is likely to result in a steeper positive slope, particularly at high doses where efficacy plateaus (Figure 4c and Figure S23). Adding a placebo control group and fitting with an Emax model significantly reduced these biases (Figure 4d and Figure S24). When PFS is the endpoint, consistent with the results for scenarios without significant dose modification, KM curves stratified by different exposure quantiles could not differentiate drug effects even at doses with steady state Cavg around EC50 in our simulation (Figure 4g and Figure S25). In contrast, at high starting doses, the high concentration tertile showed the shortest survival opposite to the underground truth that drug exposure improves progression‐free survival, due to the confounding from a high percentage of dose reductions in patients with slower disease progression (Figure 4h and Figure S25). Exposure metrics from the first dosing interval are less subjective to bias from time‐dependent confounding (Figures S20–S25). These findings mirror those in ER1.

CavgTE vs. mCavgTE

Switching to mCavgTE reduced bias in scenarios with a high percentage of patients requiring dose reduction. In ER2, logistic regression using mCavgTE showed no obvious bias (Figure 5a–c, and Figure S26). In ER1 where response is unrelated to drug exposure, the mitigation for bias becomes evident for the scenarios when there is a significant dose modification (Figure 5d–f, Figures S27 and S28, Table S4). This improvement is demonstrated in both the bias in odds ratio (Figure 5h,i and Figure S29) and the Type I error rate (Figure S30).

Influence of Dose Levels and Range on Mischaracterization

It is expected that adding additional dose levels would expand the exposure range and thereby reduce bias. When using time‐dependent exposure metrics, the dose range needed for mitigating mischaracterization was examined. Figure 6 shows the impact of the dose range on odds ratio bias in ER1 using violin plots. For constant dosing, a 3‐fold dose range appears to mitigate bias due to exposure accumulation (Figure 6a–d, Figures S31 and S32). For empirical dosing, a 6‐fold range for fast onset and > 12‐fold range for slow onset would be required to reduce bias effectively (Figure 6e–h and Figures S33–S36). Unlike bias reduction for odds ratio, including additional dose levels is less effective in reducing the Type I error rate likely due to the large sample size of 1000 virtual patients, which ensures robust detection of a biased shallow slope (Tables S5 and S6; Figures S37 and S38). For dynamic dosing, where the dose reduction relates to the drug exposure, adding a lower starting dose shifted odds and hazard ratios toward 1, notably showing in the case with mild exposure accumulation using CavgTE (Figure 6j, Figures S39 and S40). In the ER2 scenario with dynamic dosing, adding a lower dose of 100 mg (the dose at the linear PK range) may sufficiently reduce bias for ORR (Figure 7a–c, Figure S41), whereas even adding a dose as low as 60 mg is still unable to show the right order of KM curves based on the exposure quantiles (Figure 7d–f, Figure S42).

Discussion

In this study, we used simulation approaches to identify the sources, directions, and magnitudes of mischaracterization in conventional E–R analyses for common oncology outcomes (e.g., AE, ORR, and PFS), particularly those arising from time‐dependent confounding factors such as exposure accumulation and dose modification (interruptions/reductions). Our results indicate that using time‐dependent exposure metrics without careful evaluation may mischaracterize E–R relationships: exposure accumulation tends to produce a falsely inversed E–R trend, while significant dose modifications may lead to a biased positive relationship. In contrast, exposure metrics from the first dosing interval or steady state under initial dose effectively mitigate bias across all simulated trials. However, static exposure metrics may offer limited insight into E–R relationships in certain situations, particularly when dynamic dosing (e.g., dose titration/interruption/significant reduction) and various response onset times should be accounted for. Consequently, time‐dependent exposure metrics for comparison should be considered. To mitigate the risk of mischaracterization, we investigated the use of an Emax model, modified methods for time‐dependent exposure derivation, and including data from a wide dose range, all of which show promise for improving the accuracy and reliability of E–R relationship assessment.

The interplay between exposure accumulation and dose modifications determines bias direction. As shown in Figure 6j for ER1 with dynamic dosing, a low starting dose (e.g., 60 mg) with a low dose reduction rate (~20%) renders exposure accumulation the dominant factor, likely producing a falsely inverse E–R trend. At high starting doses (e.g., 500 mg with a ~75% dose reduction), dose modifications dominate, shifting the bias toward a falsely positive E–R slope. This is also illustrated in the ER1 empirical dosing scenario; for instance, the positive bias in the odds ratio is smaller in Figure 6f (high exposure accumulation) than in Figure 6e (mild accumulation), where the positively biased slope from significant dose modifications (comparable for both scenarios due to the empirical dosing) is partially offset by the inverse bias originated from substantial exposure accumulation, yielding a more balanced effect for the PK3 situation in Figure 6f.

Adopting a more appropriate model structure could reduce bias by accurately reflecting the underlying PD relationship, particularly at an exposure range lacking sufficient data. Since our underlying drug effect follows an Emax sigmoidal profile in the ER2 simulation, a logistic regression including data from the placebo control and integrating an Emax profile is more likely to capture the true drug effect on ORR and identify the plateau effect of high doses compared to a conventional logistic regression. Specifically, the inclusion of a placebo control group enhances the estimation of the lower end of the E–R curve, while the Emax model captures the plateau at higher exposures—where generalized linear models tend to overestimate marginal effects.

The modified approach for deriving CavgTE may significantly reduce the confounding effect from dose modifications in logistic regression, particularly in scenarios with a high percentage of dose reduction (> 50%). The conventional CavgTE derives the aggregate average concentration till the last clinical outcome assessment for patients without an event, which is more affected by dose reductions; thereby, it tends to yield a falsely steeper positive E–R slope. To address this issue, mCavgTE adjusts the duration based on the overall ORR distribution, that is, the last event onset day after outlier removal. This adjustment shifts exposure metrics calculation for patients without an event from the end of the study to earlier time points, where relatively fewer dose modifications occur; thereby potentially reducing bias (Figure S43). Simulation results confirm that mCavgTE offers a more accurate representation of the true E–R relationship. Similarly, mCavgOED and mCavg1WOED—modified average concentrations on the event day or 1 week prior—show improvements over their typical counterparts (Table S4, Figures S26–S30). Although this approach does not provide a definitive solution due to the inconsistent biased trends (Figure 5d–f), it represents a promising strategy for bias mitigation, warranting further evaluation and refinement.

ER data from a wider dose range may be needed to characterize the underlying exposure–response relationship when there are significant dose modifications. For instance, in ER2 dynamic dosing, a 5‐fold dose range is needed to avoid evident bias when the high starting dose is 500 mg, while a 2‐fold dose range appears adequate when the high starting dose is 200 mg (Figure 7). Thus, a small dose range may be insufficient for effective mitigation of mischaracterization if significant dose modifications exist. In the worst‐case scenario, when the drug exposure does not affect response and dose modification profile (i.e., ER1 with empirical dosing), a 6‐fold dose range for early event onset and > 12‐fold dose range for slow event onset may be needed to mitigate ER bias. In these cases, the consistency between E–R and dose–response relationships should be carefully assessed, and a holistic approach integrating the totality of evidence to inform causal relationship is needed to inform dosing decisions.

Our approach to derive CavgTE for long dosing intervals (i.e., 2 or 4 weeks) differs from that of Wiens et al. [7]. We define the duration as at least one dosing interval, preventing the positive slope observed by Wiens et al. from linking early events occurring around Cmax in the first dosing period to high concentrations. Lin et al. [6] derived cumulative concentration for censored patients from the end of treatment (EoT) to, for example, EoT + 14 days, where no drug was administered during the safety follow‐up. Their drug exhibits mild drug accumulation with daily dosing without dose modification. Since drug exposure increases the risk of AEs (i.e., a true positive ER relationship is assumed in the simulation), CavgEoT for censored patients (no AE event) is generally lower than CavgTE for patients with AEs. Shifting time points of censored patients later without dosing further lowers their exposures and artificially steepens this trend. Their results reflect a similar situation in our evaluation for the CavgTE approach (particularly in an ER2 scenario with significant dose modification) where typical CavgTE artificially steepens the positive ER trend. Our mCavgTE approach potentially mitigates these biases.

Although potential mischaracterization of the ER relationship exists with conventional analyses, understanding the potential biases could still support regulatory decisions in certain situations. For example, at the review of pembrolizumab 400 mg Q6W dosing, modeling and simulation predicted that switching to this regimen would result in a 34% lower Cmin,ss compared to 200 mg Q3W [13]. To evaluate potential efficacy loss due to the lower Ctrough, we leveraged the potentially biased ER relationship for worst‐case prediction based on a clinical trial for non‐small cell lung cancer where 2 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg were evaluated. Our mechanistic understanding of PK and E–R informs the bias direction and allows us to estimate the efficacy impact of switching to Q6W in the worst‐case scenario. The potential efficacy loss associated with the lower Ctrough of 400 mg Q6W versus 200 mg Q3W is projected to be minimal even at the worst‐case scenario with the potential biases and unlikely to be clinically meaningful, supporting the approval of the Q6W regimen.

Our simulation‐based study has several limitations. First, the simulation was focused on a large sample size to ensure robust bias detection and did not incorporate other clinically relevant factors such as tumor baseline characteristics and time‐dependent clearance. It was restricted to conventional E–R analysis methods that focus solely on the first event, ignoring recurrent events. Second, the bias mitigation strategies we proposed are better suited for early‐onset events (e.g., AEs, ORR) but may not work for events with wide onset times (e.g., PFS, overall survival), which are more prone to time‐dependent confounding factors like accumulative exposure changes or significant dosing modifications. Third, time‐to‐event analyses (e.g., KM and Cox PH) are more appropriate when event time is of interest, but KM plots show directional errors under dynamic dosing with high dose reduction (Figure 4h), and even a 3‐fold dose range could not correct it in our simulated scenarios (Figure 7d–f). Moreover, Cox PH is more sensitive to dose modifications than logistic regression, resulting in greater E–R distortion (Figure 3 and Figures S35 and S36). In those cases, advanced methods like PKPD modeling may better capture time‐dependent changes in both exposure and disease status, thereby potentially reducing confounding.

In summary, one major challenge in conventional E–R analyses is the interpretation of E–R relationships based on time‐dependent exposure metrics, which may be mischaracterized, especially for drugs with fast event onsets. To mitigate these biases, we recommend conducting E–R analyses using multiple exposure metrics, including static ones, to assess consistency. It is also advisable to explore a model structure that better reflects the underlying PD relationship and to switch to a modified method for deriving time‐dependent exposure when significant dose modifications occur. Furthermore, including additional relevant data from multiple dose levels, particularly with a wide exposure range, will help reduce bias, and evaluating the consistency between dose–response and E–R relationships will enhance the interpretation of the causal relationship and support dosing decisions. For future work, it is warranted to explore more advanced E–R analysis methods—such as longitudinal modeling—to better support clinical decision‐making, particularly in early drug development when data are limited.
