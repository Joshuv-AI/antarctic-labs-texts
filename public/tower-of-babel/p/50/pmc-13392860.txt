Enhancing venous thromboembolism risk prediction after immunotherapy for lung cancer
Authors: Shengyuan Wang, Siwan Wen, Mengmeng Zhao, Wanzhen Li, Minglei Yang, Chang Chen, Xianghuai Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAssessing venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk after immunotherapy remains important for lung cancer management. We analyzed 2,300 patients receiving first-line immunotherapy from two centers, randomly assigned to training (70%), validation (15%), and internal test (15%) sets, and included 491 patients from an independent external center for external validation. Five feature-selection methods and five machine-learning algorithms were compared to develop a 6-month VTE prediction model. The Lasso-logistic model showed the best performance, with areas under the curve of 0.692 and 0.728 in the internal and external test sets, respectively, outperforming Khorana, Padua, PROTECHT, ONKOTEV, and COMPASS-CAT scores (all p < 0.05). The high-risk group had a higher cumulative VTE incidence than the low-risk group (12.3% vs. 4.8%, p = 0.006). Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis identified D-dimer and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status as the most influential predictors, supporting individualized thromboprophylaxis decisions.Keywords: clinical model, venous thromboembolism, immunotherapy, lung cancer

Introduction

Compared with patients without cancer, patients with cancer are reported to have a 6-fold greater risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE; including pulmonary embolism (PE) and deep vein thrombosis (DVT)) risk, and a 2.2-fold greater risk of VTE-associated mortality.1 Furthermore, cancer types can be further divided into three groups according to VTE risk, and lung cancer was found to be associated with an intermediate risk of VTE.2 In terms of PE, which is a leading cause of mortality in patients with cancer, lung cancer has a significantly higher risk than other solid cancers do.3 Hence, recent guidelines have recommended utilizing the Khorana score as an effective tool to identify patients with cancer with high VTE risk who would benefit from pharmacological thromboprophylaxis after systemic treatment.4,5,6

However, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), a novel class of drugs that target the protein programmed cell death 1 receptor and its ligand PD-L1, have changed the therapeutic landscape for patients with lung cancer and are now considered standard therapy for patients with advanced or metastatic disease. Previous studies have shown that the inflammatory mechanisms and immune responses that occur during immunotherapy may increase thrombosis risk.7,8,9 Furthermore, compared with the treatment course prior to ICIs, the risk of developing VTE has been reported to be more than 4-fold greater after starting ICIs,9 and the occurrence of VTE is significantly associated with worse oncological outcomes,10 demonstrating the importance of developing a VTE risk quantification tool to guide thromboprophylaxis and further reduce VTE-associated mortality in these patients. However, even though the clinical benefit of the Khorana score in guiding prophylactic anticoagulation for patients with cancer receiving chemotherapy has been acknowledged clinically, its ability to predict VTE in patients with lung cancer treated with ICIs remains limited.10,11,12,13

Thus, our study utilized a broad range of readily available patient-, tumor-, and treatment-related features to develop and validate models to predict the occurrence of VTE in patients with lung cancer treated with immunotherapy, and the performance of several machine learning methods was compared. On the basis of our study, a reliable and effective method was proposed to evaluate the risk of VTE among patients with lung cancer treated with ICIs, which might aid in the selection of candidates who would benefit from chemoprophylaxis.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

As illustrated in Figure 1A, a total of 2,300 patients with lung cancer, consisting of 1,392 patients from Shanghai Tongji Hospital and 908 patients from Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, were included in this study for model development. The 6-month cumulative incidence risk of VTE was 6.46%, which was significantly greater than the incidence of death (4.51%; p < 0.001) for patients from Shanghai Tongji Hospital (Figure 1B); similar trends were also observed for patients from Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (6.14% vs. 2.02%; p < 0.001, Figure 1C). After data splitting, 1,610 patients were randomly allocated to the training set, 345 to the validation set, and 345 to the internal test set at a ratio of 70:15:15; the demographic characteristics of different sets were described in Table S1. The validation and internal test sets had comparable characteristics; however, the internal test cohort included significantly more patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score<2 (p = 0.032), fewer patients with tumors on the left side (p = 0.026), and fewer patients with a blood urea nitrogen concentration >8.2 mmol/L (p = 0.046).

Predictor selection and identification of the final model

As summarized in Table 1, 18 features for which p was <0.050 were selected by the chi-squared test and student’s t test; 8 features for which p was <0.100 were selected by multivariate logistic regression analysis, since inadequate variables with p < 0.050; 20 features for which p was <0.050 according to univariate fine gray analysis were selected, and 22 features were selected by Lasso. The experimental results for the machine learning algorithms and feature set combinations on different sets are shown in Figures 2A–2C. Finally, the XGBoost with all the features (area under the curve [AUC], 0.682), random forest (RF) with all features (AUC, 0.689), and logistic with Lasso-selected features (Lasso-Logistic, AUC, 0.692) performed the best on the internal test set (Figure 2C). Hence, a stacked model was established on the basis of these three models, and comparisons of the predictive performance of the stacked model with that of the other three models in different sets are illustrated in Figures 2D–2F. Accordingly, the Lasso-Logistic model performed better than the stacked model both on the validation (0.702 vs. 0.689, Figure 2E) and internal test sets (0.692 vs. 0.687, Figure 2F), with no significant difference (p = 0.451 and p = 0.803, respectively). The performance metrics were further analyzed on the basis of the Youden index derived from the training set, and the Lasso-Logistic model maintained the highest specificity (0.682, 0.663, 0.646), positive predictive value (PPV) (0.187, 0.159, 0.160), and accuracy (0.710, 0.665, and 0.632) on the training (Figure 2G), validation (Figure 2H) and internal test sets (Figure 2I), respectively, compared with the stacked model, RF with all features model, and XGBoost with all features, even though the sensitivity was slightly lower (0.738, 0.667, and 0.618, respectively). Therefore, the Lasso-Logistic model was selected as the final model.

Comparisons between the Lasso-Logistic model and existing VTE-risk score systems

The ability of the Lasso-Logistic model to predict VTE risk was compared with that of existing VTE-risk scores. Compared with all five existing VTE risk score systems, the Lasso-Logistic model exhibited significantly improved performance, with the highest AUCs in the internal test sets (p < 0.050 for all; Figure 3A). On the basis of the cutoff value of 0.0896 derived from the training set, the Lasso-Logistic model classified patients into high risk (value > 0.0896) and low risk groups (value ≤ 0.0896), and the cumulative incidence of VTE within 6-month of initiating ICI treatment exhibited a significantly distribution between the high and low risk groups in the internal test sets (p = 0.006; Figure 3B), indicating the better discrimination performance of the Lasso-Logistic model.

Subgroup analysis was subsequently performed. With respect to DVT, the Lasso-Logistic model outperformed the existing VTE-risk score systems on the internal test set (p < 0.050 for all, Figure 3C), and the 6-month cumulative incidence of DVT in the high-risk group was significantly greater than that in the low-risk group (p = 0.027; Figure 3D). With respect to PE, the Lasso-Logistic model still performed better, but the difference was significant only upon comparison with the Padua score (p < 0.050, Figure 3E). The cumulative incidence of PE did not significantly differ between the two risk groups (p = 0.093; Figure 3F), which might be due to the small number of PE events.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) suggested that the Lasso-Logistic model achieved better performance overall than the existing VTE-risk models did on the internal test set (Figure S1A). Net reclassification improvement (NRI) analysis of the internal test set revealed that applying the Lasso-Logistic model instead of the Khorana, Padua, PROTECHT, ONKOTEV and COMPASS-CAT scores improved VTE risk prediction by 33.9% (95% confidence interval (CI), −21.2%–89.0%), 43.8% (95% CI, −11.3%–99.0%), 32.7% (95% CI, −22.4%–87.8%), 49.0% (95% CI, −4.9%–1.00%), and 52.0% (95% CI, −1.9%–1.00%), respectively, indicating its superiority of it in individual VTE risk stratification.

Performance validation on the independent external test set

A total of 491 patients from Ningbo No. 2 Hospital were included as an independent external test set, with detailed baseline characteristics described in Table S2. When compared with the extensively validated VTE risk score systems, the Lasso-Logistic model achieved an AUC of 0.728 (95% CI, 0.643–0.804), which was significantly higher than that achieved by the other VTE risk score systems (p < 0.050 for all; Figure 4A). Based on the cutoff value of 0.0896, the high-risk group showed a cumulative occurrence rate of 8.8% for VTE after immunotherapy, which was significantly higher than that of the low-risk group (p = 0.001; Figure 4B). Subgroup analyses revealed that the Lasso-Logistic model consistently performed better than the existing models for DVT prediction in the internal test set (p < 0.050 for all; Figure 4C), effectively stratifying patients on the basis of the incidence of DVT at 6 months (p = 0.005; Figure 4D). With respect to PE prediction, the AUC of the Lasso-Logistic model was again the greatest, although the difference was statistically significant upon comparison with the Padua score only (p < 0.050; Figure 4E). The cumulative PE incidence also differed significantly between the high- and low-risk groups (p = 0.033; Figure 4F).

DCA demonstrated that compared with the existing models, the Lasso-Logistic model provided a greater net clinical benefit on the external test set (Figure S1B). Moreover, NRI analysis revealed that comparing with the Khorana, Padua, PROTECHT, ONKOTEV, and COMPASS-CAT scores, the Lasso-Logistic model improved VTE risk reclassification by 57.4% (95% CI: 25.1%–89.7%), 71.5% (95% CI: 39.7%–100.0%), 57.5% (95% CI: 25.3%–89.7%), 70.0% (95% CI: 37.9%–100%), and 59.5% (95% CI: 27.2%–91.7%), respectively.

Further, sensitivity analyses within the external test set revealed that the Lasso-Logistic model maintained robust discriminative performance and significantly stratified the 6-month VTE cumulative incidence regardless of prior VTE history or baseline anticoagulation status (Figures S2A–S2F).

Explanation analysis of the Lasso-Logistic model

The contributions of selected features incorporated into the model were evaluated based on their Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values and are shown in descending order in Figure 5. Among the 22 features in the training set, D-dimer level (mean SHAP value of 0.0267) and ECOG performance status (mean SHAP value of 0.0232) had the greatest impact on VTE risk prediction. Moreover, SHAP analysis revealed that the D-dimer level and ECOG status were robust predictors of VTE and the two most important features across different datasets. Meanwhile, sex showed a mean SHAP value ranging from 0.0059 to 0.0068 across all datasets, acting as the eleventh factor influencing the model output.

Analysis of the association between VTE risk and overall survival

The prognostic implications of the two VTE risk groups identified by the Lasso-Logistic model were further investigated in a development cohort consisting of 2,300 patients. The 6-month overall survival (OS) rate of the high-risk group was 91.9% (89.9%–93.9%), which was significantly lower than that of the low-risk group (96.9%, 95% CI, 95.9%–97.9%; p < 0.001; Figure 6A). According to the subgroup analysis of different tumor stages, the difference in the 6-month OS rate did not significantly differ between the high-risk and low-risk group did not differ significantly for stage I (p = 0.342, Figure 6B) and stage III patients (p = 0.085; Figure 6C), but did significantly differ for stage IV patients (p < 0.001; Figure 6D). The prognostic implications were not determined for stage II patients, since all of these patients were classified as having low VTE risk. Moreover, among patients treated with ICIs alone, the high-risk group also had a significantly lower 6-month OS rate than the low-risk group did (p < 0.001; Figure 6E); a similar result was also observed for patients treated with ICIs in addition to chemotherapy (p < 0.001; Figure 6F).

Discussion

In this multicenter, retrospective study of 2,791 patients with lung cancer treated with ICIs, we established an interpretable machine learning model for determining 6-month VTE risk after immunotherapy. The best risk stratification performance of the established machine learning model was verified by comparing Khorana (for patients with cancer receiving chemotherapy), Padua (for inpatients), and other previously validated scoring systems for cancer-associated VTE risk evaluation (namely, PROTECHT, ONKOTEV, and COMPASS-CAT score). Such a model might have future utility in personalized VTE r-isk classification, whereby only those at greatest risk have the most active measurements of prophylactic anticoagulation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study on VTE risk assessment in patients with lung cancer after ICI treatment with a large population.

Large studies have demonstrated that VTE is generally related to increased health care costs, reduced quality of life, poor prognosis, and thrombotic complications and is the second most important cause of death in patients with cancer. Current clinical guidelines recommend the use of the Khorana score to evaluate VTE risk and guide anticoagulation treatment in ambulatory patients with cancer receiving chemotherapy; patients who are at high risk for VTE have a score≥3.14 However, novel monoclonal antibodies targeting PD-1, PD-L1, or CTLA-4 have demonstrated effects that are superior to those of chemotherapeutic drugs in many settings. On the basis of the close relationship among immune system activation, inflammation, and thrombosis, several studies have investigated the incidence of ICI-associated VTE and have reported that the cumulative incidence of VTE is approximately 12.9%, and that VTE is associated with increased mortality after immunotherapy,10,11,13 indicating the increased risk of VTE after immunotherapy initiation. However, the predictive ability of the Khorana score was limited in these patients.12,13 Additional risk models for cancer-associated VTE, such as PROTECHT (which includes gemcitabine and platinum-based chemotherapy),15 ONKOTEV (which includes previous incidence of VTE, metastatic disease, and vascular or lymphatic compression),16 and COMPASS-CAT (which includes cancer and treatment-related factors),17 were recently developed but failed upon application to patients with lung cancer with ICI-related VTE. Although risk factors for ICI-associated VTE have been previously explored, a specific assessment tool for stratifying the risk of VTE in patients with lung cancer receiving ICIs is still lacking. Hence, our study was designed and conducted to establish an ICI-relative VTE risk stratification tool for these patients.

Machine learning is a powerful computational method for addressing complex and extensive data because it can handle highly variable datasets, understand the complex relationships among variables in a flexible way, and be trained. Many machine learning algorithms are currently being employed in the field of precision medicine. Hence, different machine learning algorithms were adopted in our study to construct the most intelligent model. In addition, the number of features to incorporate into a model during development remains unclear, since including a large number of features may limit the clinical use of the model, and including noncausal features may reduce the accuracy of the prediction. In this context, we compared different combinations of machine learning algorithms and feature sets and selected the Lasso-Logistic model as the final model because it had the best predictive performance. Furthermore, the Lasso-Logistic model outperformed each of the previously reported and validated VTE-risk prediction scores in determining VTE risk after immunotherapy. The percentage of patients classified as high-risk for VTE by the Lasso-Logistic model exceeded 10%, which was significantly greater than that of low-risk patients. The Lasso-Logistic model was also superior for determining PE and DVT risk, indicating the feasibility of the final model in various clinical scenarios. Positive associations between high VTE risk and adverse oncological outcomes were identified, regardless of the tumor stage or whether combined treatment with chemotherapy was administered. The aforementioned findings clarify the universal clinical application and advantages of our final model.

Machine learning has been described as a “black-box” with little explanation of how predictions are derived, which can lead clinicians to refuse to use it because they hesitate to make medical decisions based on opaque information. The SHAP method was utilized to explain the “black-box” of the machine learning model by ranking the importance of selected features to describe the overall functionality of the model and providing details regarding a certain prediction was made for an individual case by inputting the specific data. As noted, the D-dimer level and ECOG status were the two most important features incorporated into the final model. D-dimer levels have been widely used during screening tests to identify patients who have signs or symptoms suggestive of VTE, as D-dimer is a marker of endogenous fibrinolysis.18 ECOG status is an essential denominator of cancer patient independence and symptom burden, and patients with an ECOG score ≥1 have restricted physical activity, which promotes the development of VTE. The correlation between ECOG status and VTE risk was also reported in a previously published study.19,20,21,22 Several risk factors reported previously,23 including body mass index (BMI), history of VTE, ongoing hormonal treatment, and central venous catheter (CVC), have been proven to be consistently associated with an increased risk of VTE among patients with lung cancer treated with ICIs in our study. The features found to be significantly associated with decreased VTE risk in our study included diabetes and existing VTE at the initiation of ICI treatment, as reported in previous studies.24 Another negative feature identified in our study is coronary heart disease (CHD), and the relationship between VTE and CHD is still controversial.25,26 Epidemiologically, the paradoxical inverse association between VTE risk and comorbidities such as CHD and severe diabetes highlights a classic treatment paradox, driven by confounding by indication. Under current guideline-directed medical therapy, patients with these high-risk conditions are nearly universally maintained on long-term cardiovascular prophylaxis, predominantly statins and antiplatelet agents. Landmark randomized controlled trials have definitively established that these medications exert potent pleiotropic, anti-inflammatory, and antithrombotic effects, which substantially mitigate VTE incidence.27,28 Consequently, the protective effect observed in our model does not reflect an intrinsic biological mechanism of the underlying morbidities. In summary, the results from the machine learning model analysis were consistent with those from traditional statistical analyses, which supported the validity of our findings.

Limitations of the study

There are some limitations of this study that should be acknowledged. First, despite outperforming existing scoring systems, the predictive performance of the LASSO-logistic model did not surpass 0.70 in the internal test set, indicating merely moderate discriminative capacity. This limitation is likely attributable to the exclusive reliance on single-dimensional variables in the established model. A recent study reported that the ONCOTHROMB score consisting of three clinical and nine genetic variables could improve the AUC to 0.781 for cancer-associated VTE risk prediction,29 indicating the importance of multidimensional factors in evaluating ICI-associated VTE risk. The incorporation of multiomics data such as specific genetic susceptibility variants or transcriptomic signatures alongside dynamic coagulation biomarkers may further improve the predictive performance of the model. Second, the Lasso-Logistic model was not applied to predict the risk of arterial thromboembolism event because of the low incidence. Compared with DVT, ATE may occur in the brain or heart and is associated with much higher mortality. Thus, constructing a highly sensitive model for predicting ATE risk is of great importance for reducing mortality associated with this severe complication. Third, retrospective observational studies are inherently susceptible to selection bias. As such, prospective validation of the Lasso-Logistic model is necessary to confirm its performance. Fourth, despite the positive results from the NRI analysis and DCA, further research is still needed to assess the benefit of thromboprophylaxis in patients at high risk identified by the Lasso-Logistic model.

In conclusion, our study established a novel, reliable, effective, and interpretable model for VTE risk in patients with lung cancer receiving immunotherapy. With the inclusion of clinically accessible variables, the clinical model was able to discriminate patients with different risks of VTE, and thus might help physicians select candidates who might benefit from pharmacological thromboprophylaxis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethical statement

Our multicenter, retrospective study followed the ethics standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013), and was approved by the Institutional Ethics-Committee of all involved centers (Approval Number, K23-392, SBKT-2023-323, PJ-NBEY-KY-2026-112-01), with Ethics Approval Letter provided as Data S1. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective observational design.

Patients selection

Consecutive patients with histologically confirmed primary lung cancer who received first-line ICIs as a first-line treatment between January 2018 and December 2023 were identified from the intelligent databases of Shanghai Tongji Hospital and Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital were included in this study for internal validation. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ≥18 years of age; (2) treatment with at least one dose of an approved ICIs (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab or ipilimumab) for anticancer therapy; and (3) at least one vascular ultrasound or computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) examination after the first ICI administration. Patients who met any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) had an active infection; (2) were enrolled in randomized controlled trials and were unblinded; (3) received irregular immunotherapy without medical guidance; (4) did not have a complete laboratory index within one month prior to immunotherapy; and (5) did not have complete follow-up records for 6 months after the last dose of the ICI. Notably, patients receiving ICIs in open label or single -arm interventional trials were not excluded. Furthermore, patients with a history of VTE or who received continuous anticoagulation therapy were also not excluded, as these patients represent the real-life setting for evaluating the risk of VTE among lung cancer patients who received immunotherapy. Meanwhile, patients from Ningbo No.2 Hospital were also enrolled based on the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, and served as an independent external validation cohort. Patients demographic and clinical details were obtained from the electronic medical record systems of the participating centers, and presented in Table S1 and S2.

Method details

Study procedures and outcome measure

Among the patients in the internal validation cohort, 70% were randomly selected as the training set, 15% were selected as the internal validation set for hyperparameter optimization to avoid overfitting, and 15% were selected as an independent internal test set. The primary study outcome was occurrence of new VTE, including objectively confirmed symptomatic or incidental pulmonary embolism (PE), distal or proximal deep vein thrombosis (DVT), noncatheter-related upper limb DVT, and symptomatic catheter-related upper limb DVT assessed at least 3 days after the start of ICI therapy (index date). Asymptomatic upper limb DVT related to indwelling venous catheters and splanchnic vein thrombosis were not considered events. Deaths during the observation period were also recorded. The observation period in this study ranged from the index date to a maximum of 6 months after the last dose of the ICI, until the occurrence of VTE, death, or censoring because of curative surgery (only for patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy), the initiation of anticoagulation treatment for other reasons, or loss to follow-up. All patients were followed-up until July 31, 2024, and all VTE diagnoses were confirmed by radiological imaging.

Model development

The medical records were reviewed, and the following potential predictor variables were selected and recorded: (1) Clinical variables: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoke history, hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ECOG status, and malignant tumor history; (2) Variables incorporated into existed score system for VTE: TEs history prior to ICIs start, existed TEs at the first treatment, central venous catheter (CVC), reduced mobility (reduced mobility refers to Bedrest with bathroom privileges [either due to patient’s limitations or on physicians order] for at least 3 days), already known thrombophilia condition (carriage of defects of antithrombin, protein C or S, factor V Leiden, G20210A prothrombin mutation, antiphospholipid syndrome), trauma and/or surgery within 1 month prior to first ICIs, and ongoing hormonal treatment; (3) Tumor-related characteristic: tumor location, histology, vascular or lymphatic invasion, pathological TNM stage, and metastasis; (4) Treatment-related variables: ICIs agent, duration of immunotherapy, and concomitant therapy during ICIs; (5) Laboratory index within 1 month prior to ICIs: platelet count (×109/L), hemoglobin level (g/L), leukocyte count (×109/L), percentage of lymphocyte (%), hematocrit level (L/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, IU/L), aspartate transaminase (AST, IU/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, IU/L), blood urea nitrogen (mmol/L), creatinine (μmol/L), albumin (g/L), total bilirubin (μmol/L), sodium (mmol/L), prothrombin time (PT, second), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT, second), thrombin time (TT, second), fibrinogen (g/L), and D-Dimer level (ng/mL).The potential values of all included predictor variables are presented in Table S3.

To meet the preprocessing requirements for the machine learning algorithms, the continuous laboratory index was converted into categorical variables with cutoff values on the lower and upper normal limits. Furthermore, Khorana, Padua prediction,30 PROTECHT,15 ONKOTEV,16 and COMPASS-CAT scores17,31 were calculated. The features mentioned above were used to develop prediction models. Missing data among all variables ranged from 0% to 1.7% and which were handled by the median imputation method. A combination of 5 features selection methods (all features, chi-squared test for categorical variable data and Student’s t test for continuous variables, logistic regression analysis, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator [Lasso], and Fine-Gray analysis) and 5 machining learning algorithms (eXtreme gradient boosting [XGBoost], RandomForest [RF], support vector machine [SVM], light gradient boosting machine [L-GBM], and logistic regression) were applied to develop the predictive model. Hyperparameter optimization was performed via a grid-search and manual fine-tuning with 5-fold cross-validation. The hyperparameters of the final selected models are described in Table S4. The performance of each feature-algorithm combination was evaluated using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC) and compared by DeLong’s test. Stacked prediction models were then explored by averaging the predictions of the 3 algorithms with the highest AUC in the test set for the outcome being predicted. When the stacked prediction model was superior, it was selected as the final model for predicting that particular outcome. Otherwise, the single algorithm with the highest AUC was selected as the final model.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Model evaluation and risk group classification

The performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy, were evaluated based on the Youden index identified from the training set. The Youden’s index was also used to classify the patient population into high (outcomes might occur within 6 months after the first fraction of immunotherapy) and low (outcomes might not occur within 6 months after the first fraction of immunotherapy) risk groups, since most guidelines recommend basing the decision on the initiation of primary thromboprophylaxis on 6-month risk estimates.14 The time of the first occurrence of VTE was measured from the index date, and the cumulative incidence of VTE was estimated 6 months after treatment initiation with sub-distribution hazard model (Fine & Gray model), in which death was treated as a competing event, and then compared with Gray’s test. The discrimination performance of the final model was compared with that of established VTE-risk models based on the AUC values, while the net clinical benefits were evaluated by net reclassification improvement (NRI) analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA). Furthermore, the 6-month overall survival (OS) of both risk groups was evaluated by constructing Kaplan-Meier curves and compared by the log rank test.

Model explanation analysis

To facilitate the clinical application of the final model, the Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) method, a unified approach for explaining the outcome of any machine learning model, was used to examine the importance of the predictors and provide consistent attribution values for each feature incorporated into the prediction model.32

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the mean (standard deviation, SD), and categorical variables are presented as the frequency as percentages. The patients’ baseline characteristics were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables when appropriate, and independent t-tests were used for continuous variables. Statistical analyses were performed by SPSS software (version 23.0) and R (version 3.6.2), with a two-sided p value < 0.050 indicating a significant difference.
