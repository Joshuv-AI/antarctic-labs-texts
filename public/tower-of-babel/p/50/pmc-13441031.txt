Economic impact of potassium citrate in urolithiasis: A narrative review and economic evidence synthesis
Authors: Arianna Pischetola, Ali Talyshinskii, Wissam Kamal, Bhaskar K. Somani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Recurrent urolithiasis causes substantial healthcare and economic burden due to high recurrence rates and need for repeated interventions. Potassium citrate effectively prevents stone recurrence, yet its cost-effectiveness compared with nontreatment remains unclear. We aimed to evaluate the economic impact of potassium citrate therapy in the prevention and management of urolithiasis.Materials and Methods:A structured narrative review was conducted to evaluate the economic impact of potassium citrate therapy in urolithiasis. Databases were searched for studies assessing costs, cost-effectiveness, or healthcare resource utilization related to potassium citrate. Due to substantial heterogeneity in analytic perspective, outcomes, time horizons, and healthcare system context, findings were synthesized narratively rather than quantitatively.Results:The economic evidence base was limited but consistent in demonstrating a meaningful reduction in stone recurrence with potassium citrate therapy. Two decision-analytic economic evaluations showed that potassium citrate substantially lowers recurrence rates compared with conservative management, translating into reduced downstream healthcare utilization. Economic outcomes were most favorable in patients with high baseline recurrence risk and in healthcare systems with lower medication acquisition costs. Across models, recurrence risk, drug pricing, and treatment adherence emerged as clear and reproducible drivers of economic value. No studies incorporated quality-adjusted life years or health utility outcomes.Conclusion:Potassium citrate is a clinically effective and economically reasonable preventive therapy for recurrent urolithiasis, particularly in high-risk and hypocitraturic patients. When targeted to appropriate populations, it reduces recurrence and can offset downstream treatment costs. The current evidence supports potassium citrate as a key component of preventive stone management.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, urolithiasis accounted for an estimated 106 million incident cases and 694,000 disability-adjusted life years in 2021, with incident cases increasing by 26.7% between 2000 and 2021 despite a 17.5% decline in the age-standardized incidence rate over the same period.[12] Despite widespread awareness of metabolic contributors, preventive management remains inconsistent, and many patients relapse within a few years of their initial episode.[3] Potassium citrate has long been used to counter the biochemical abnormalities that fuel stone formation and is more than a generic alkalinizing agent, it directly addresses the physiology that underpins stone recurrence.[45] Metabolic studies consistently show higher urinary citrate, a pH increase and a fall in calcium oxalate supersaturation once potassium citrate is introduced, shifts that translate into a significantly less lithogenic urinary environment.[6] These mechanistic advantages explain why hypocitraturia, present in almost half of recurrent calcium stone formers, remains one of the most actionable metabolic defects in urolithiasis.[7] However, although potassium citrate is widely used in the prevention of recurrent urolithiasis, its economic value remains insufficiently defined. The available health-economic evaluations are few and marked by substantial heterogeneity in analytic design, including differences in cost-effectiveness frameworks, outcome measures, time horizons, and healthcare system perspectives. In this context, a structured narrative economic review is warranted. The aim of this study is to critically synthesize and contextualize the existing economic evidence on potassium citrate therapy, with the objective of identifying the cost drivers, assumptions, and healthcare settings in which its use is likely to be economically justified in the prevention and management of urolithiasis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was designed as a structured narrative economic review aimed at critically synthesizing the health-economic evidence on potassium citrate therapy in urolithiasis. A literature search was conducted in November 2025 using MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, and the Cochrane Library, using the following terms: “potassium citrate,” “citrate therapy,” “urolithiasis,” “nephrolithiasis,” “kidney stones,” “stone recurrence,” “recurrence prevention,” “cost,” “cost-effectiveness,” “economic evaluation,” and “healthcare utilization.” Studies were considered relevant if they examined potassium citrate in the prevention or management of urolithiasis and reported economic outcomes, cost-related endpoints, or healthcare resource utilization data relevant to economic interpretation. Formal economic evaluations were prioritized as the core evidence base. Given the narrative nature of the review, the limited number of formal economic studies, and the substantial heterogeneity across analytic perspectives, outcome measures, time horizons, and healthcare settings, a quantitative synthesis was not considered appropriate.

RESULTS

Evidence base and scope of the economic evidence

The economic literature evaluating potassium citrate therapy in urolithiasis is limited and consists primarily of decision-analytic modeling studies. Two formal health-economic evaluations specifically assessing potassium citrate were identified, both estimating costs and outcomes under predefined clinical and healthcare system assumptions.[89] These studies were retained as the core economic evidence because they explicitly model potassium citrate as a preventative strategy with quantified cost and outcome consequences, whereas the broader utilization literature was used only to contextualize healthcare resource use and between-system cost differences. Hence, Lotan et al. adopted a system-level approach with annualized costs across multiple countries, comparing conservative dietary management with empiric and directed medical therapy following comprehensive metabolic evaluation.[8] Bolaños-Díaz et al. used a patient-level perspective over a 3-year horizon, comparing potassium citrate plus dietary therapy with dietary therapy alone in recurrent calcium oxalate stone formers, reporting outcomes as cost per avoided recurrence[9] [Table 1].

Economic modeling approaches and quantitative drivers of cost-effectiveness

The international decision-tree model by Lotan et al.[8] adopts a healthcare system perspective with costs annualized on a per-patient basis and applied across ten countries. For recurrent calcium oxalate stone formers, the model assumes a recurrence rate of 0.30 stones per patient per year under conservative dietary management, compared with 0.06 stones per patient per year with empiric medical therapy and 0.084 stones per patient per year with directed therapy following comprehensive metabolic evaluation, corresponding to an assumed overall risk reduction of approximately 80% with pharmacological treatment. Despite reduction in recurrence, conservative therapy remained the least costly strategy in nine of ten countries. Annual per-patient costs for recurrent stone formers ranged from US$54–234 for conservative management, compared with US$221–612 for empiric therapy and US$289–887 for directed therapy, depending on country. Empiric therapy was economically favored only in the United Kingdom, where annual drug costs were estimated at US$29 per patient, compared with US$508 in the United States and over US$400 in several European countries.[8] Sensitivity analyses in the Lotan model further quantified the recurrence thresholds required for empiric therapy to achieve cost equivalence with conservative management. In most healthcare systems, recurrence rates exceeding one stone per patient per year were required. In contrast, in settings with low medication costs, cost equivalence was achieved at much lower recurrence thresholds, specifically 0.12 stones per patient per year in the UK and 0.67 stones per patient per year in Switzerland.[8]

In contrast, the patient-level cost-effectiveness analysis by Bolaños-Díaz et al. evaluates potassium citrate plus dietary therapy against dietary therapy alone over a 3-year horizon, adopting a direct medical cost perspective. Using recurrence data from a randomized controlled trial, this model reports the recurrence rates of 12% with potassium citrate versus 84% with diet alone. Over 3 years, total costs were estimated at US$1,556 for potassium citrate therapy and US$759 for dietary management alone, yielding cost-effectiveness ratios of US$360 per avoided recurrence for potassium citrate and US$3,795 per avoided recurrence for diet alone. The resulting incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was US$193 per additional avoided recurrence.[9] Sensitivity analyses incorporating treatment adherence (89% for potassium citrate and 95% for diet) demonstrated that the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio remained below US$220 per avoided recurrence under conservative assumptions, indicating robustness within the modeled outpatient setting.[9]

DISCUSSION

Medication acquisition cost represents the dominant recurrent cost input in the economic models of potassium citrate therapy because treatment is continuous, whereas stone-related healthcare utilization occurs intermittently. These estimates align with the central structural tension in preventive stone pharmacotherapy: even when recurrence reduction is large, the economics can be unfavorable if medication pricing is high relative to the expected downstream costs avoided.[10] In Lotan’s model, only 15% of recurrent stone formers were assumed to have a symptomatic episode annually and 55% of symptomatic episodes required surgical intervention, assumptions that substantially dampen the downstream cost savings attributable to prevention, thereby increasing the recurrence threshold needed for cost neutrality.[8] Healthcare system structure determines which cost centers dominate and therefore whether the conclusions generalize across countries. Cross-country differences in drug and procedural pricing help explain why preventive therapy appears more economically favorable in some healthcare systems than in others.[11]

Healthcare system context and geographic variability

Cross-country differences in the economic performance regardless of treatment in urolithiasis are primarily driven by how long-term preventive medications are priced, reimbursed, and accessed within the different healthcare systems. Similar variability in potassium citrate pricing has been reported in national formularies and claims-based analyses, reflecting differences in generic availability, pharmaceutical price regulation, and patient cost-sharing mechanisms.[12] The economic value of potassium citrate is further shaped by how recurrent stone episodes are managed within a given healthcare system and which downstream resources are displaced by effective prevention. Preventive therapy is most likely to generate economic benefit in settings where recurrent stones commonly result in emergency department (ED) presentations, advanced imaging, hospitalization, and procedural intervention. In such contexts, potassium citrate may offer greater economic value by preventing episodes that would otherwise trigger high-intensity care pathways.[8] Conversely, in systems where most recurrences are managed in the outpatient settings with limited imaging and low procedural rates, the economic impact of prevention is attenuated, even when clinical effectiveness is substantial.[9] These structural differences limit the transferability of economic evaluations of potassium citrate across the healthcare systems. Patient-level analyses directly link avoided recurrences to reductions in outpatient visits, diagnostic testing, and acute treatment costs within a defined care pathway, whereas system-level models distribute recurrence-related costs across more heterogeneous populations and therefore may show a lower apparent economic return of long-term preventive therapy.

Clinical and resource-use inputs informing economic assumptions

Across the economic models identified, the primary clinical input is the degree to which potassium citrate reduces recurrent stone events over time. The recurrence reductions used in economic modeling are broadly consistent with key controlled and long-term clinical studies in calcium stone formers. In the randomized double-blind trial by Barceló et al., potassium citrate reduced new stone formation in idiopathic hypocitraturic calcium nephrolithiasis, and treatment discontinuation due to adverse effects was uncommon (two withdrawals in the potassium citrate arm and one in placebo).[8] Longitudinal experience also supports sustained benefit during continued therapy. Pak et al. evaluated long-term potassium citrate treatment in calcium nephrolithiasis, forming part of the clinical basis often cited for durable preventive effect assumptions in models.[13] A related alkalinizing regimen (potassium-magnesium citrate) demonstrated substantial recurrence risk reduction over up to 3 years in recurrent calcium oxalate stone formers, reinforcing the plausibility of large effect sizes in selected recurrent populations.[14] Importantly, for economic interpretation, these efficacy estimates are most applicable to biochemically selected or high-risk recurrent cohorts (e.g., hypocitraturia), which aligns with the observation that cost-effectiveness improves as baseline recurrence risk rises.

A second critical input is real-world adherence and tolerability, which determines the “realized effectiveness” that economic models implicitly assume. Even when trial discontinuation rates are low, long-term adherence in routine care can be materially lower, and this has direct economic consequences because potassium citrate’s cost accrues continuously while benefits depend on sustained use. In a study explicitly examining secondary prevention adherence, Golomb et al. reported long-term adherence patterns and reasons for nonadherence to alkali citrate regimens among stone formers, highlighting adherence as a limiting factor for the effectiveness of chemoprevention outside trial settings.[15] As potassium citrate incurs ongoing cost while its preventive benefit depends on sustained use, even modest reductions in long-term adherence may materially erode real-world cost-effectiveness, particularly in lower-risk patients.

At the same time, the efficacy inputs used in economic models require cautious interpretation. The large recurrence differences applied in earlier analyses, particularly the 84% versus 12% recurrence rates reported by Bolaños-Díaz et al., may reflect selected recurrent stone formers at relatively high baseline risk rather than the broader population of all stone patients.[9] Similarly, the approximate 80% recurrence reduction assumed in the Lotan model is most plausible in metabolically selected or highly recurrent cohorts who are adherent to treatment. If such effect sizes are extrapolated to lower-risk or less well-characterized populations, the economic benefit of potassium citrate may be overstated. Accordingly, the existing economic conclusions are likely to be most robust in hypocitraturic and high-recurrence patients, whereas their generalizability to unselected stone populations remains uncertain.

Finally, economic models depend on how clinical events translate into healthcare utilization, because not every recurrence triggers the high-cost pathways that prevention is intended to avert. Utilization studies show that urolithiasis generates substantial and increased demand and imaging use, which helps parameterize the downstream costs potentially displaced by prevention.[89] In the United States, Fwu et al. found ED visit rates for urolithiasis nearly doubled between 1992 and 2009, with computed tomography (CT) use increasing markedly during ED encounters over the same period.[16] At the same time, imaging strategy influences resource intensity. Randomized evidence indicates that an ultrasound-first approach can reduce cumulative radiation exposure without worsening major clinical outcomes in suspected nephrolithiasis, while European guidance continues to position ultrasound as a primary diagnostic tool in stone evaluation, with CT reserved for defined indications.[1718]

Limitations

This narrative economic review is limited by the scope and heterogeneity of the available evidence. Formal health-economic evaluations of potassium citrate are scarce, with only a small number of decision-analytic models directly assessing cost-effectiveness; consequently, conclusions rely largely on modelled assumptions rather than empirical economic data.

The identified evaluations vary widely in analytic perspective, time horizon, outcome definitions, and cost components. The outcomes were reported almost exclusively as cost per avoided recurrence, with no incorporation of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) or broader health utility measures, limiting comparability with other preventive or chronic interventions. Indirect costs, productivity losses, and patient-borne expenses were inconsistently included, potentially underestimating the total economic burden of recurrent stone disease. The scarcity of formal economic studies may also reflect publication bias, with unfavorable or inconclusive economic evaluations of potassium citrate in higher-drug-cost settings being less likely to be published.

A further limitation is that the two formal economic evaluations included in this review were published in 2005 and 2011, and their cost inputs may not fully reflect current acquisition costs, procedural pricing, imaging pathways, or reimbursement structures. Contemporary publicly accessible pricing suggests that generic availability has reduced the likelihood that potassium citrate is uniformly cost-prohibitive in some settings, particularly where lower-cost generic formulations are available. However, current pricing still demonstrates substantial variation by market and formulation, with relatively low alkalinizing therapy prices in the United Kingdom, lower but variable generic pricing in the United States, and higher official prices for some modified-release potassium citrate-containing products in Saudi Arabia and parts of Europe. Taken together, these contemporary data suggest that drug acquisition cost remains a major determinant of cost-effectiveness rather than a resolved issue. Accordingly, the direction of the earlier models remains informative, but the magnitude and transferability of their conclusions should be interpreted cautiously and updated locally where possible [Table 2].

The outcomes were reported almost exclusively as cost per avoided recurrence, with no incorporation of QALYs or broader health utility measures.[19] This substantially limits comparability with other preventive or chronic interventions and reduces the usefulness of the available evidence for reimbursement, formulary, and health policy decision-making. Finally, this review did not aim to pool clinical outcomes or derive unified economic effect estimates. Long-term adherence and persistence with potassium citrate therapy are incompletely captured in existing economic models, which may overestimate realized effectiveness and economic benefit in routine practice. Accordingly, conclusions should be interpreted as context-dependent and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive estimates applicable across all the settings.

Future perspective

Future research on the economic role of potassium citrate should shift from broad, population-level assessments toward a more clinically grounded understanding of where preventive therapy truly adds value. Rather than treating stone formers as a homogeneous group, future work should focus on identifying patient profiles, such as those with frequent recurrence or correctable metabolic abnormalities, in whom long-term citrate therapy is most likely to meaningfully reduce both clinical events and healthcare utilization.

Equally important is the need to align economic evaluations with real-world stone care pathways. How recurrent stones are evaluated, monitored, and treated in routine practice, including decisions around imaging, emergency presentation, and intervention, largely determines whether prevention translates into measurable economic benefit. Future studies that better reflect contemporary clinical workflows may provide more actionable insights for clinicians and health systems.

Greater emphasis should be placed on how access, affordability, and long-term treatment persistence influence the real-world impact of potassium citrate. Understanding how preventive therapy is adopted, sustained, or discontinued in everyday practice may be as important as its biological efficacy in determining its economic value across different healthcare settings. While there exist many studies on cost in the surgical arena,[2021] more needs to be done in the prevention of kidney stone disease. A particularly important next step would be a prospective cost-effectiveness study embedded within a metabolically characterized recurrent stone cohort, ideally linked to a randomized or otherwise well-controlled comparative treatment design. Such a study should stratify patients by recurrence risk and metabolic abnormality, while capturing recurrence outcomes, treatment adherence, adverse effects, healthcare utilization, and validated health utility measures over time. This would allow potassium citrate to be evaluated not only in terms of cost per avoided recurrence, but also in terms of QALY-based outcomes and real-world value in clearly defined patient subgroups.

CONCLUSION

Despite limited economic studies, the available evidence supports potassium citrate as a high-value preventive therapy in recurrent urolithiasis. Its economic benefit is greatest in patients with hypocitraturia and high recurrence risk, where preventing stone events reduces downstream healthcare use. Variability in cost-effectiveness largely reflects drug pricing and healthcare system differences rather than uncertainty in clinical efficacy. In practice, potassium citrate should be considered an important component of targeted stone prevention, particularly in hypocitraturic patients and those at high risk of recurrence, where it may offer both clinical benefit and favorable economic value.
