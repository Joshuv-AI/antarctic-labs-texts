Background

Researchers and policymakers increasingly recognise public involvement in research as essential to ensuring that studies respond to lived realities, foster accountability, and strengthen the relevance and impact of findings [1–3]. Yet, meaningful patient and public involvement (PPI) remains under-realised in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [4]. In LMIC research contexts, hierarchical structures, limited resources and systemic inequities often constrain opportunities for meaningful participation, leaving the voices of those living with disease underrepresented [5–7]. A systematic review of PPI in health research across LMICs reported that although discussions about the concept are increasing, few studies describe detailed mechanisms or demonstrate active participation [4].

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), including asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), clearly illustrate this gap. These conditions impose a major health burden [8, 9]. In 2019, CRD ranked as the third leading cause of death worldwide and caused approximately four million deaths, a number that continues to rise [8]. LMICs, including Malaysia, shoulder a disproportionate share of this burden, driven by enduring social, environmental and systemic inequities that hinder early diagnosis, guideline-based care and patient-centred support [10–13].

Although researchers have increasingly documented the quantitative burden of CRD [14], evidence remains limited on how individuals living with CRD in LMICs experience and make sense of everyday challenges [15]; how they navigate healthcare systems, cope with limitations, and envision change. Participatory research methods, particularly visual, narrative-based approaches such as Photovoice [16], offer promising tools to foreground marginalised experiences, identify local priorities, and catalyse community-led advocacy and policy action [16, 17]. Photovoice typically involves participants taking photographs, discussing their meaning through facilitated dialogue, and sharing curated images with wider audiences to stimulate reflection and action [16]. These methods shift the locus of knowledge production, positioning people with lived experience as co-researchers rather than passive subjects [18]. Within this context, there is a need for participatory approaches that foreground patients lived experiences and foster co-produced solutions.

We used Photovoice as a participatory method to explore the lived experience of people with CRD in Klang, Malaysia. Through this approach, participants actively shared their realities and priorities, guiding the researchers’ understanding of CRD beyond clinical perspectives. The paper focuses on how meaningful public involvement, through collaborations with patients, carers, and community partners, shaped every stage of the research process, from design and data interpretation to dissemination and policy dialogue. By embedding PPI throughout the research cycle, we amplified lived experiences, addressed the gap between patient realities and local policy decisions in LMICs, and co-created pathways to improve CRD care. This case study reports PPI processes and their influence on study conduct, interpretation, and dissemination; Photovoice thematic findings are reported elsewhere.

Methods and processes

Study design and setting

We adopted a participatory visual research approach using an adapted Photovoice design, implemented between December 2023 and October 2024 across five public primary care clinics in Klang, with key PPI-related activities took place between February to June 2025. The clinics were purposively selected to reflect variation in catchment area (urban and suburban) and patient demographics, supporting maximum variation sampling and transferability of lessons across clinic contexts.

Klang is a densely populated industrial-residential district in Selangor, located on the Straits of Malacca and home to major port infrastructure along one of the world’s busiest shipping corridors (See Fig. 1 for map). Air quality is frequently affected by industrial emissions, open burning, and seasonal transboundary haze.

Participants and recruitment

For the study, healthcare professionals at participating clinics identified eligible adults (≥ 18 years) with self-reported chronic respiratory disease or respiratory symptoms. We purposively sampled participants for initial interviews using a maximum variation strategy (age, gender, CRD type, and ethnicity). Eligible individuals received study information from clinic staff and either contacted the research team directly or consented to follow-up, to minimise coercion. Written consent was obtained prior to interviews; participants were reimbursed (about GBP 20) for travel or data costs. All interview participants were invited to take part in subsequent Photovoice phases, with the photo-taking commitment explained after the initial interview. Basic demographics (age, gender, ethnicity, diagnosis) were collected to describe the sample and support reflexive interpretation.

The final sample comprised 14 interviewed participants, with a mean age of 53.93 years (SD 14.31). Eight were men, six were of Malay ethnicity, and seven had asthma.

Ethical approval and consent

We received ethical approval from the Medical Research and Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health, Malaysia (ID: NMRR ID-23-02011-WZO (IIR)) and the Edinburgh Medical School Research Ethics Committee (Ref: EMREC-RESPIRE-23-03). Sponsorship approval was obtained from the Academic and Clinical Central Office for Research & Development at the University of Edinburgh (ACCORD) (ID: AC23004). All participants provided informed consent.

Adapted photovoice process

We implemented four iterative stages as illustrated in Fig. 2. Participants took photographs, joined individual Photovoice interviews, and contributed to a final photo exhibition involving multi-level stakeholders.

Steps used in this study

Interview guides were semi-structured and developed using Photovoice principles (including prompts to describe the photograph, its meaning, and desired change), with input from PPI members to ensure cultural appropriateness and plain-language phrasing. Guides were piloted and refined before data collection. Interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred language (Malay or English) by trained facilitators. Two semi-structured guides were used: an initial one-to-one orientation or rapport-building session and a subsequent individual photo-interview after the photo-taking period.

Rapport building and orientation: We conducted introductory sessions to build trust, explain study aims, and familiarise participants with photography ethics and consent. Unlike the original group-based model by Wang and Burris [16], our adaptation incorporated preliminary one-on-one sessions to build rapport [17].

Embedding public involvement

Public involvement was embedded throughout the study, aligned with the GRIPP2 Short Form (GRIPP2-SF) framework (see Appendix 1). Patients, carers, and community representatives contributed to four main phases of the research: design, recruitment, analysis, and dissemination.

Two PPI members (patient and carer: Fig. 4) from the RESPIRE Malaysia network reviewed all patient-facing materials for clarity and cultural sensitivity. Their feedback led to a revised recruitment strategy: instead of direct researcher contact, treating physicians first introduced the study to potential participants, followed by a researcher follow-up via text or voice message. This adaptation addressed concerns about phone scams and helped to build early trust.

Discussion with PPI

PPI advice guided recruitment to ensure representation across gender, ethnicity, and socioeconomic background. We communicated in participants’ preferred languages (Malay, Mandarin, or Tamil), to promote inclusivity, as Malaysia is a multiethnic and multilingual society.

During the analysis phase, three PPI members and two Photovoice participants joined the researchers in reviewing emergent themes. Their perspectives enriched the interpretation by reframing researcher assumptions, for example, redefining ‘loss of mobility’ as ‘loss of identity’, highlighting the emotional and social dimensions of illness experience.

Three study participants served as community curators, co-designing the Living with CRD photobook (Fig. 5) and co-facilitating the public photo exhibitions. Together with district health officers and policymakers, they co-produced a policy brief (Fig. 5), translating lived experiences into actionable recommendations. Data analysis

We analysed PPI reflections, discussions and facilitator notes (using a thematic approach) to identify enablers, challenges, and perceived impacts, drawing on Photovoice materials only as illustrative examples.

PPI impact and reflections

PPI shaped every stage of the study and served as the driving force of participatory research in an LMIC context. Embedding PPI throughout the design, recruitment, analysis, and dissemination phases transformed a traditional Photovoice project into a co-produced, culturally grounded, and policy-relevant process.

This case study focuses on the PPI processes and their influence on study conduct, interpretation, and dissemination. The full Photovoice report (participant characteristics and thematic findings from photographs and interviews) will be published separately; here we use illustrative examples only to support reflections on involvement, power-sharing, and impact.

Building trust and redefining relationships

Trust formed the foundation of this Photovoice journey. During the design stage, PPI members highlighted that many Malaysians are cautious about unsolicited phone calls due to the prevalence of scams. Acting on their advice, researchers adopted a physician-mediated recruitment strategy, in which familiar HCP first introduced the study to potential participants before follow-up contact was made by the researchers. When approached by trusted HCPs, study participants felt ‘safe’ and ‘respected’.

From the researchers’ perspective, facilitation required sustained coordination: aligning clinic workflows for recruitment, scheduling interviews around participants’ symptoms and caregiving responsibilities, and arranging transport or logistics for exhibitions and stakeholder dialogues. To support feasibility, we designated primary facilitators (HS, FA) to maintain relationships and continuity, documented decisions in facilitator notes, and held regular debriefs to adapt processes in real time.

We also attended to power dynamics explicitly throughout the PPI activities. Ground rules were agreed at the start of group dialogues and co-curation sessions (e.g., contributors could pause or step out at any time; no clinical judgement of images or stories; respectful listening). Contributors were encouraged to lead with their narratives and to decide which photographs and captions could be shared publicly. Using preferred languages, reimbursing time and expenses, and keeping participation clearly separate from clinical care helped reduce perceived hierarchy and create safer spaces for dissent and critique. As facilitators, we adopted a deliberately non-expert stance (e.g., asking open questions, avoiding interpretation and actively inviting quieter voices) to reduce researcher dominance. We also held brief reflexive debriefs after sessions to notice when hierarchy may have surfaced and to adjust subsequent facilitation (e.g., turn-taking, pacing, and prompts) to support more equal participation.

This simple adaptation greatly improved participation rates and reduced refusals, showing how involvement can directly strengthen both the ethical (e.g., respect for persons, voluntary participation and trust-building) and practical dimensions of recruitment. By enabling participants to make informed choices within an environment of trust, this process helped rebalance the typically hierarchical relationship between researchers and communities in LMIC contexts [4].

Over time, this trust grew into shared ownership of the study. Participants were no longer mere subjects of research but collaborators whose experiential knowledge informed decisions at every stage. Involving HCPs also bridged professional and community perspectives, cultivating mutual respect and a collective sense of purpose.

Co-analysis and shared interpretation

Meaningful involvement extended into data analysis. PPI members and study brought emotional and cultural insights that complemented academic interpretation, reframing ‘loss of mobility’ as ‘loss of identity’. This interpretive shift revealed deeper psychosocial dimensions of living with CRD that might otherwise have been overlooked.

This co-analysis exemplifies the shift from tokenistic involvement to valued team membership [18]. By incorporating lived-experience perspectives, the analysis became more authentic, empathetic, and locally relevant. It also built analytical capacity among community members and reflexivity among researchers, with each learning to see illness and care through the other’s lens.

Dissemination as empowerment and feedback

Dissemination was designed as a feedback loop connecting communities, HCPs, and policymakers. Participants co-curated public exhibitions, selected photographs, and crafted captions that reflected their lived realities. These exhibitions generated dialogue and co-developed recommendations, resulting in a joint policy brief. Through this, participants and the public found a platform for advocacy and visibility; dissemination became an act of empowerment [6].

To preserve community voices during engagement with clinicians and policymakers, we used participant-led storytelling: photographs were displayed with participant-written captions, and participants spoke first during exhibition walk-throughs and dialogues. Researchers acted as facilitators rather than spokespersons, ensuring that professional reflections responded to participant priorities (e.g., exposure to haze, and psychosocial impacts). The policy brief was co-drafted with community curators, retaining their language and framing, and was shared with participants for final approval before dissemination.

Participants described dissemination as both an opportunity to contribute to change and a space where day-to-day struggles could be voiced and validated.

One participant, a 45-year-old educator, remarked:

I’m happy to participate in this project. I hope this sharing could help improve the health system and make the community more aware.

Another participant, a 42-year-old housewife, shared during the dissemination session, tears welling in her eyes:

Even climbing the stairs leaves me breathless. My husband jokes about it, and it makes me sad, because it’s not funny when it’s my daily struggle.

In this project, we treated empowerment as more than expressions of satisfaction. We observed empowerment through participants’ increased willingness to share experiences publicly, take on active roles (e.g., co-curating the photobook, facilitating exhibition discussions), and contribute to collective advocacy via the co-authored policy brief. Several participants expressed interest in staying connected through a community CRD network proposed during the policy workshop, suggesting potential for sustained engagement beyond the immediate study.

Broader implications of participatory approaches

The project generated relational and emotional transformations across all stakeholders. HCPs reported renewed empathy after listening to patient narratives, while participants gained new skills and confidence, later co-facilitating subsequent sessions. This two-way capacity building illustrated how participatory research can strengthen not only individual empowerment but also entire systems by fostering humility, reciprocity, and sustained collaboration. These insights reinforce findings from recent LMIC literature, which show that PPI is both feasible and valuable when supported by cultural sensitivity, institutional collaboration, and trust-based facilitation [3, 7].

Strengthening institutional acknowledgement of PPI roles, remuneration for public partners, and capacity-building for both researchers and lay collaborators is critical to achieving parity.

Conclusion

By embedding public involvement throughout this Photovoice project, people living with CRD and their carers became agents of change, shaping data analysis, advocacy, and policy dialogue. Through their active participation, PPI partners redefined relationships between researchers, communities, and clinicians, illustrating how co-production strengthens both evidence and empathy.

In LMIC contexts, where hierarchies and resource constraints often limit expression, this experience demonstrates that, with trust, skilled facilitation, and institutional partnership, co-production is both achievable and transformative.
