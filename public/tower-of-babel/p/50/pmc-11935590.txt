Unraveling the Complexities for Antiplatelet Strategies Among Patients With Acute Coronary Syndromes Undergoing Percutaneous Coronary Intervention
Authors: Yasar Sattar, Islam Y Elgendy
Published: 2024

Because of the important role of the P2Y12 receptor in platelet activation, aggregation, and procoagulant activity, 1 dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and an oral P2Y12 inhibitor is considered the cornerstone therapy among patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). 2 , 3 In 2001, the CURE (Clopidogrel in Unstable Angina to Prevent Recurrent Events) trial established the role of a strategy of aspirin with clopidogrel for 12 months to prevent recurrent ischemic events. 4 Subsequently, the high‐potency P2Y12 inhibitors (ie, ticagrelor or prasugrel), which are characterized by more prompt and potent platelet inhibition, on a background of aspirin for 12 months demonstrated superior efficacy compared with clopidogrel and aspirin for 12 months in TRITON‐TIMI 38 (Trial to Assess Improvement in Therapeutic Outcomes by Optimizing Platelet Inhibition With Prasugrel—Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 38) and PLATO (Platelet Inhibition and Patient Outcomes) trials, respectively, but with an expense of increased bleeding events. 5 , 6 Accordingly, the 2021 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association coronary artery disease revascularization guidelines and the 2023 European Society of Cardiology ACS guidelines recommend a default strategy of DAPT, preferentially with a high‐potency P2Y12 inhibitor for 12 months among patients with ACS undergoing PCI. 2 , 3

Notably, the risk of recurrent ischemic events and bleeding decreases over time but not evenly. Among patients with ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction undergoing primary PCI, the bleeding rates exceeded those for recurrent ischemic events within 30 days. However, the risk of recurrent ischemic events exceeded bleeding events beyond 30 days. 7 Nevertheless, early major bleeding after PCI for ACS is strongly linked with mortality, 8 highlighting the importance of trade‐off strategies to minimize bleeding events, especially among patients who are at high bleeding risk without increasing the ischemic risk. During the past decade, several advancements in secondary preventative therapies, such as potent lipid‐lowering strategies and enhancement in PCI techniques (ie, intracoronary imaging, newer‐generation drug‐eluting stents with thinner struts, and biocompatible polymers) have led to a reduction in ischemic risk. Still, the bleeding risk has remained largely unchanged. Because the intensity of P2Y inhibitor and duration of DAPT are linked with the bleeding risk and associated mortality, alternative strategies to mitigate the bleeding risk have been the subject of numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) during the past decade. These strategies include: (1) de‐escalation (ie, switching from a high‐potency P2Y12 inhibitor to clopidogrel, usually 3–6 months from PCI); (2) down‐adjustment (ie, reducing the dose of high‐potency P2Y12 inhibitor); or (3) shorter DAPT for 1 to 6 months instead of standard 12 months. The purpose of de‐escalation and down‐adjustment is to achieve greater platelet inhibition by using a high‐potency P2Y12 inhibitor early after PCI when patients are at higher risk for recurrent ischemic events, followed by a reduction of P2Y12 inhibitor potency (switching to clopidogrel or reduced‐dose prasugrel or ticagrelor) to reduce late bleeding risk while maintaining the duration of DAPT. While a strategy of shorter DAPT limits the duration of exposure to 2 agents to reduce bleeding risk. 9 Importantly, most of the individual RCTs did not directly compare one bleeding‐reducing strategy to another but rather focused on comparing one of these strategies with standard DAPT for 12 months.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Ullah et al present the results of a network meta‐analysis comparing the safety and efficacy of different antiplatelet strategies among patients with ACS who underwent PCI. 10 The analysis included 32 RCTs with 103 459 patients. Compared with a default strategy of DAPT with aspirin and clopidogrel for 12 months, a strategy of de‐escalation from ticagrelor to clopidogrel at 3 months for a total of 12 months and DAPT with aspirin and prasugrel for 12 months were associated with a lower risk of major adverse cardiac events, while a strategy of DAPT with aspirin and clopidogrel for 1 month followed by aspirin monotherapy was associated with the highest risk major adverse cardiac events. These findings were mostly consistent in several subgroup analyses, including the clinical presentation (ie, ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction versus non−ST‐segment elevation ACS). Not surprisingly, a strategy of DAPT for 12 months with aspirin and a high‐potency P2Y12 inhibitor was associated with a higher risk of major bleeding compared with DAPT with aspirin and clopidogrel, and a strategy of DAPT with aspirin and clopidogrel for 1 month was associated with lower bleeding. However, a strategy of de‐escalation from ticagrelor to clopidogrel at 3 months was not associated with a lower risk of major bleeding. In terms of net clinical benefit, a strategy of de‐escalation from ticagrelor to clopidogrel at 3 months for a total of 12 months was the most effective in reducing the risk of major adverse cardiac events without increasing the risk of the major bleeding or the individual ischemic end points.

Acknowledging the limitations inherent to trial‐level network meta‐analyses, the analysis by Ullah et al represents the most comprehensive analysis to date, comparing the different antiplatelet strategies exclusively among patients with ACS undergoing PCI. 10 However, some issues deserve further consideration. There was heterogeneity between the RCTs in the P2Y12 inhibitor regimen, doses, drug‐eluting stent platforms, and ethnicity of the included patients (ie, some exclusively enrolled only Asians). There were also variations in the definition of major adverse cardiac events and the criteria used to define major bleeding. Some tested strategies (ie, down‐adjustment to low‐dose prasugrel) were represented by a small number of RCTs and conducted exclusively in Asia. In addition, most of the RCTs included patients at low ischemic risk; thus, the findings might not be generalizable to those with high ischemic risk or those with recurrent events. Finally, the lack of patient‐level data precluded further analyses to identify subgroups that could derive the most net clinical benefit. Nevertheless, the authors complemented their analysis with several subgroup and meta‐regression analyses, demonstrating that the findings were mostly consistent within the subgroups. Notwithstanding these issues, the authors should be applauded for their work, which contributes to the existing body of literature regarding the prospects of antiplatelet modulation strategies among patients with ACS undergoing PCI.

Although adopting a standard approach for a wide spectrum of patients with ACS undergoing PCI is attractive, the choice is often more “complex” and can be optimized by a personalized approach that takes into consideration: (1) identifying the appropriate DAPT duration for each patient; and (2) selection of the appropriate P2Y12 inhibitor (Figure). Patients at high bleeding risk might benefit from an abbreviated DAPT duration (ie, 1–3 months of DAPT followed by P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy for 12 months). 11 , 12 There is compelling evidence to support the safety of abbreviated DAPT to reduce bleeding risk without increasing the risk of ischemic events. In a patient‐level meta‐analysis of the TICO (Ticagrelor Monotherapy After 3 Months in the Patients Treated With New Generation Sirolimus‐eluting Stent for Acute Coronary Syndrome) and the T‐PASS (Ticagrelor Monotherapy in Patients Treated With New‐generation Drug‐eluting Stents for Acute Coronary Syndrome) trials showed that ticagrelor monotherapy after abbreviated DAPT (ie, 1–3 months) was associated with less major bleeding without an increase in ischemic events compared with 12‐month DAPT with aspirin and ticagrelor. 13 Ticagrelor monotherapy (as the P2Y12 inhibitor agent), after abbreviated DAPT, appears to be the superior choice, as opposed to clopidogrel. In a patient‐level meta‐analysis of 6 RCTs, ticagrelor monotherapy after abbreviated DAPT, but not clopidogrel, was noninferior to standard DAPT (ie, 12 months) for the composite of death, myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke. However, both ticagrelor and clopidogrel monotherapy were superior to standard DAPT for major bleeding. 14 Notably, the exact timing for omitting aspirin remains unclear, particularly in patients with high bleeding risk, but the current evidence supports at least 1 month. By contrast, patients with high ischemic risk (eg, recurrent events, complex PCI, diabetes) might benefit from extended DAPT (ie, beyond 12 months) if they have no overt bleeding while on DAPT. 15

The appropriate P2Y12 inhibitor for each patient can be selected using platelet function or genetic testing. While de‐escalation and down‐adjustment can be offered unselectively (ie, to any patient), up to 30% of patients exhibit high on‐treatment platelet reactivity on clopidogrel. 16 One network meta‐analysis of 15 RCTs demonstrated that a guided de‐escalation strategy was associated with reduced ischemic events. 17 By contrast, another network meta‐analysis of 19 RCTs showed that an unguided de‐escalation strategy was associated with a lower incidence of ischemic events than a guided de‐escalation strategy (either platelet function or genetic testing). 18 Since the comparison between guided and unguided de‐escalation strategies in these analyses was based on indirect comparisons, adequately powered RCTs are needed to better understand the role of guided de‐escalation. Moreover, the practicality of a guided selection strategy for P2Y12 inhibitors might be limited by the availability and cost of these tests, although rapid assays are available.

In conclusion, the meta‐analysis by Ullah et al is an important contribution to unraveling the complexities of the various antiplatelet modulation strategies among patients with ACS undergoing PCI. Their findings support that a strategy of de‐escalation from ticagrelor to clopidogrel at 3 months for 12 months might be the default strategy. While 12 months of DAPT remains the standard of care for patients with ACS, we should take into consideration identifying those at high risk of bleeding or ischemic risk who might be candidates for shorter or extended periods of DAPT.
