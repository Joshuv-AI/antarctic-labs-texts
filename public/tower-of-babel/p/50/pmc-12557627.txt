Diagnostic accuracy of computer-aided detection (CAD)-derived solid component proportion and mean CT attenuation for predicting invasiveness in pulmonary part-solid nodules: a retrospective study
Authors: Xiaodong Wang, Jing Xue, Guizhou Gao, Lin Qu, Haihua Zhang, Ramsy Abdelghani, Tao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate preoperative identification of invasiveness in pulmonary part-solid nodules (PSNs) is critical to guide extent of resection and operative timing, yet reliable, automated imaging markers are limited. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of volumetric proportion and mean computed tomography (CT) value of solid components for the invasiveness of pulmonary PSNs using computer-aided detection (CAD).MethodsThis retrospective study included 313 patients with 324 PSNs who underwent thin-section CT (TSCT) before surgery and had postoperative pathologic diagnoses of atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH), adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), or invasive adenocarcinoma (IA). The proportion of solid components at CT values of −300 Hounsfield units (HU) and −400 HU and the mean CT values of the solid components were automatically computed. The diagnostic accuracy was evaluated with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The tumor size was stratified, and the optimal predictive indicators within each stratum were evaluated.ResultsAmong 313 patients (324 PSNs; median age 58 years), 4 AAH, 88 AIS, 69 MIA, and 163 IA were identified. The mean CT value of the solid component had an AUC of 0.795 (threshold −144.5 HU), with sensitivity 0.799, and specificity 0.696. Tumor size had an AUC of 0.784 (threshold 1.65 cm, sensitivity 0.595, specificity 0.826), and solid component size had an AUC of 0.759 (threshold 0.95 cm, sensitivity 0.804, specificity 0.509). Analysis of solid component proportion demonstrated AUCs of 0.795 and 0.783 at −300 and −400 HU, respectively, with corresponding optimal thresholds of 16.2% (sensitivity 0.706, specificity 0.814) and 31.3% (sensitivity 0.687, specificity 0.807). In the stratified analysis, the mean CT value and solid component proportion showed high AUCs in all tumor size strata.ConclusionsCAD-derived volumetric solid proportion and mean CT attenuation show promise for predicting PSN invasiveness in this single-center retrospective cohort; prospective multicenter validation with standardized protocols is warranted.

Introduction

Pulmonary part-solid nodules (PSNs) are increasingly detected on chest computed tomography (CT) scans (1-5). PSNs, including pre-invasive lesions, are particularly susceptible to progressing to invasive adenocarcinoma (IA) (6). For individuals diagnosed with preinvasive lesions in the form of PSNs, sublobar resection may be a suitable option. Meanwhile, for those with lesions of IA presenting as PSNs, lobectomy may be more suitable (7). Consequently, accurate prediction of PSN invasiveness can guide resection strategies.

Previous studies have indicated that the size of the solid component is more closely associated with tumor invasion and prognosis than overall tumor size (8-15). In one study, a strong positive linear relationship was observed between the size of the solid components on CT scans and the size of the pathological invasion (16), which is consistent with the recommendation from the eighth edition of the Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) classification of lung cancer that the size of the solid component in PSNs should be used for T staging (17). Several studies (18-23) have attempted to employ computational volumetry analyses to calculate the proportion of solid volume (PSV) in PSNs, and the results of these studies have indicated that this approach is feasible. However, more clinical information is needed to evaluate the efficacy of volumetric analysis.

Beyond determining the extent of resection, preoperative classification of PSNs also influences whether patients should undergo surveillance versus surgery. Consensus recommendations emphasize conservative follow-up for appropriately selected subsolid nodules to avoid overtreatment, while acknowledging that the presence and growth of a solid component increase the likelihood of invasiveness and the need for intervention (6). In surgical candidates, the decision between sublobar resection and lobectomy is often informed by the suspected invasive potential; consolidation-to-tumor ratio thresholds have been used to guide this decision-making, but remain imperfect at the individual-nodule level (12). These realities create a clear clinical need for practical, reproducible imaging biomarkers that can stratify PSNs by invasive risk to optimize timing and extent of treatment while minimizing unnecessary procedures.

Existing prediction approaches range from rudimentary size-based criteria to sophisticated computational methods. The employment of volumetry and computer-aided detection (CAD) facilitates the automated segmentation of PSNs and the quantification of solid components, encompassing three-dimensional solid proportion (SP) and attenuation-derived metrics. These metrics have been associated with prognosis and histologic aggressiveness (18,24). Recent advancements in radiomics and radiogenomics have enabled the use of high-dimensional image features and machine learning algorithms to develop models that predict invasiveness. However, the clinical translation of these models is hindered by variability in acquisition and segmentation processes, limited external validation, and challenges related to interpretability. In recent years, multimodal models that integrate CT-derived features with molecular markers (e.g., DNA methylation) have reported enhanced discrimination for invasive disease. However, these models may necessitate specialised assays and currently provide limited, size-stratified evidence for differentiating invasive from preinvasive lesions in routine clinical practice (25). These discrepancies are the motivation for the current focus on standardized, reproducible, CAD-derived volumetric markers as practical diagnostic imaging biomarkers for PSN risk stratification.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the ability of PSV to distinguish IA from preinvasive lesions in PSNs. We also determined the optimal cutoff value in the mean CT Hounsfield units (HU) of the solid component to more accurately predict invasiveness. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1475/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (No. 202307-13). Due to the retrospective nature of this study, using anonymized imaging data and medical records, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Patients

We retrospectively collected CT images and pathology reports from consecutively hospitalized patients with PSNs from July 1 to December 31, 2021. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) presence of PSNs detected on thin-section CT (TSCT); (II) pathologically confirmed atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH), adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), or IA. Clinicopathological information, including sex, age, PSV of the PSN, mean CT HUs of the solid component, nodule location, surgery type, and pathological type, was obtained from the medical records.

Postoperative specimens were classified as AAH, AIS, MIA, or IA according to 2021 World Health Organization (WHO)/International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) criteria by two thoracic pathologists (disagreements resolved by consensus), blinded to CAD outputs and quantitative CT measurements.

CT acquisition

TSCT was performed before PSN resection with dual-source CT scanners (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The scanning area extended from the apex to the base of the lung. The CT scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, automatic control; slice thickness, 0.6 mm; pitch 1.2; and rotation time, 0.5 s. The image matrix was 512×512, the lung window was reconstructed with the lung algorithm, and the mediastinal window was reconstructed with the standard algorithm. After the scanning was completed, multiplanar reconstruction was performed in the axial and coronal positions with a slice thickness of 5 mm and a slice distance of 5 mm. The pulmonary window was reconstructed with a window width of 1,200 HU and a window height of −600 HU, whereas the mediastinal window was reconstructed with a window width of 370 HU and a window height of 70 HU. For axial reorganization, a slice thickness of 1 mm and a slice spacing of 1 mm were used to allow for the observation of image details. CT images were reconstructed for all patients using a high-frequency algorithm, with a section thickness of 0.6 mm. The target reconstruction included the following parameters: a field of view (FOV) of 200 mm; a slice thickness of 0.6 mm; a slice distance of 0 mm; a window width of 1,200 HU; and a window position of −600 HU.

Software analysis

All nodules were examined by a radiologist using the Dr. Wise AI-Assisted Diagnosis System for Pulmonary Nodules (version 1.0.0.0; Deepwise, Beijing, China). The software can separate ground-glass and solid components (if any) from a target node, thereby allowing the user to select different CT value thresholds for each component. The software can also automatically calculate the volume proportion of the solid or ground glass components. The software also provides various measurements including the size of the entire nodule and its solid component. In this study, we used two different thresholds for the solid component (−400 and −300 HU). All CAD-derived segmentations were examined by a thoracic radiologist; manual corrections were applied only if gross segmentation errors were identified.

Statistical analyses

First, the patient and tumor characteristics were compared between the IAs and pre-IA groups. Age, tumor size, PSV of PSNs, and solid component CT HUs were compared via the Student’s t-test or the Welch’s t-test. Second, the sex distribution and frequency of pathological invasiveness were compared via the chi-squared test. Third, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff value in the solid volume proportion for predicting invasiveness. The Youden index was then calculated. Finally, ROC analysis and calculation of the Youden index for the mean CT HUs of the solid component were also conducted. We did not have a separate validation cohort; performance estimates are apparent and require external validation. For each biomarker, the Youden index identified the optimal cutoff; sensitivity and specificity were computed at this cutoff. Areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence interval (CIs) were compared using DeLong’s test.

All statistical analyses were performed with R version 3.5.3 (The Foundation for Statistical Computing; http://www.r-project.org) for Windows and SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P<0.05.

Sample size and precision

As an observational retrospective study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Precision for diagnostic performance was quantified by 95% CIs around AUCs and classification metrics.

In this study, of the 1,614 patients initially examined, 564 were classified as having PSNs, and 318 patients with 329 pulmonary nodules underwent surgery. After applying exclusion criteria, this study examined 324 nodules from 313 patients (97 males and 216 females). ROC curve analysis

The ROC analysis and comparative results are provided in Tables S1,S2.

ROC curve analysis was conducted to predict invasiveness based on the following six variables: tumor size, solid component size (SS), CT value of the ground glass region (Vctg), CT value of the solid region (Vcts), SP at −300 HU, and SP at −400 HU. The SP was defined as the percentage of voxels with CT attenuation values exceeding the specified Hounsfield unit thresholds (Figure 2). The analysis yielded the following AUC values with 95% CIs and optimal thresholds: Vctg: 0.623 (0.563–0.684; threshold =−556.0 HU), Vcts: 0.795 (0.746–0.844; threshold =−144.5 HU), tumor size: 0.784 (0.735–0.833; threshold =1.65 cm), SS: 0.759 (0.707–0.812; threshold =0.95 cm), SP−300: 0.795 (0.742–0.842; threshold =16.2%), SP−400: 0.783 (0.732–0.833; threshold =31.3%). Using DeLong’s test for pairwise comparisons, no statistically significant differences were found among tumor size, SS, SP−300, SP−400, or Vcts. However, Vctg showed significant discrepancies compared to all other variables (P<0.001 for comparisons with tumor size, SS, SP at −300 HU, SP at −400 HU, and Vcts).

ROC curve analysis for tumor size stratification

The AUC values for tumor size, SS, mean Vctg, mean Vcts, SP at −300 HU, and SP at −400 HU in predicting the invasiveness of pulmonary PSNs were suboptimal, with all values below 0.8. This finding suggests that these factors may not be optimal predictive indicators. To identify more valuable predictive markers within specific categories, we conducted stratified analysis. Tumor size was stratified into three categories: ≤1 cm, >1–2 cm, and >2–3 cm. In the first level (0–1), there were 65 (20.1%) nodes that contained AAH, AIS, or MIA while 10 (3.1%) nodes had IA. In the second level (>1–2), there were 81 (25.0%) nodes with AAH, AIS, or MIA and 97 (29.9%) nodes with IA. In the third level (>2–3), there were 15 (4.6%) nodes with AAH, AIS, or MIA and 56 (17.3%) nodes with IA.

ROC curve analysis within the 0–1 stratum

In Figure 3, the SS, Vctg, Vcts, SP at −300 HU, and SP at −400 HU are depicted in the five ROC curves along with their AUC values. In the 0–1 stratum, the AUC for SS, Vctg, SP at −300 HU, and SP at −400 HU were low, suggesting a lack of significant predictive value. Among the five curves, Vcts achieved the highest AUC (0.746; 95% CI: 0.592–0.901). Using −183.5 HU as the cutoff yielded high sensitivity (0.90) with moderate specificity (0.585), indicating that the mean CT value of the solid component may serve as a sensitivity‑oriented predictor of invasiveness in tumors ≤1 cm.

ROC curve analysis within the >1–2 stratum

Among the five ROC curves, Vcts yielded an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.745–0.876) with a threshold of −82.5 HU with sensitivity 0.660 and specificity 0.864, which was the highest among the variables (Figure 4). Furthermore, SP at −300 HU and SP at −400 HU demonstrated strong predictive value. SP at −300 HU showed an AUC of 0.799 (95% CI: 0.731–0.867; threshold =16.87%) with sensitivity 0.753 and specificity 0.790, while SP at −400 HU yielded an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.705–0.848; threshold =30.95%) with sensitivity 0.722 and specificity 0.778.

Discussion

As the solid component is typically indicative of invasiveness, for part-solid lung cancers, the eighth edition of the Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) classification for lung cancer recommends that the size of the solid component be considered in determining the extent of the tumor (T) (17). Lee et al. (26) found a significant correlation between the maximum diameter of subsolid nodules on CT scans and tumor size on pathology reports (r=0.82–0.87 for 3D measurement and 0.72–0.88 for 2D measurement; P<0.0001). Recently, due to the rapid development of CAD for nodule detection via automated segmentation, volumetric analysis has become more widely used. However, in volumetric analysis, it is crucial to validate a substantial amount of data to identify the diagnostically valuable indicators and their corresponding optimal cutoff values.

In our retrospective study, TSCT scans and CAD were used to identify pulmonary nodules, and software was used to automatically segment the nodules. We analyzed the predictive value of two-dimensional indices, including the tumor size of the lung nodule and the maximum diameter of the solid component, as well as 3D indices of the fractional volume of the solid component at −300 HU and the fractional volume of the solid component at −400 HU. We also analyzed the CT values of the ground-glass section and the CT values of the solid section for the aggressiveness of the pulmonary nodule. We found that the size of the solid component and volume proportions of the solid component at −300 and −400 HU, as well as the CT value of the solid component, were predictive factors for assessing the invasiveness of pulmonary nodules.

Our study confirms that quantitative CT parameters—particularly SS, volumetric proportions at specific attenuation thresholds, and mean CT attenuation—provide valuable predictors of invasiveness in PSNs. The strong performance of these parameters (AUCs consistently >0.78) aligns with prior studies (24-27) and highlights their biological relevance: denser solid components reflect increased cellular density and architectural distortion characteristics of invasive pathology (25,26). In particular, Yang et al. (25) identified the proportion of solid components and maximum CT value as independent predictors of invasiveness and showed that integrating these CT metrics with DNA methylation features further improved discrimination (AUC 0.90 in the training set and 0.87 in the validation set). Notably, the volumetric proportion at −400 HU emerged as a powerful predictor for larger nodules (>2–3 cm, AUC =0.905), suggesting its utility in preoperative stratification of these lesions in our study.

The study has several limitations. First, due to the retrospective design and inclusion of consecutive surgical patients admitted to a single centre, there is likely to be selection bias, and the findings may not be generalisable to screening cohorts or non-surgical populations. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size highlights the need for further prospective large-scale trials to confirm the predictive performance of our findings. Second, all scans were performed on a single CT platform (SOMATOM Definition Flash), which may limit reproducibility across scanners and protocols. Standardization and multicenter validation across vendors are needed. Third, our volumetrics relied on a single CAD tool (Dr. Wise AI-Assisted Diagnosis System, version 1.0.0.0). Algorithmic differences between software or versions may influence SP and mean CT values; future work should compare measurements across platforms. Fourth, we did not assess interobserver reproducibility of segmentation or attenuation metrics; future studies should quantify inter-reader and inter-software agreement. Fifth, this retrospective study did not assess the reproducibility of segmentation or attenuation metrics between observers. Future studies should quantify inter-reader and inter-software agreement. Finally, we did not have a separate validation cohort; performance estimates are apparent and require external validation.

Conclusions

CAD-derived volumetric SP and mean CT attenuation show promise for predicting PSN invasiveness in this single-center retrospective cohort. Prospective, multicenter validation with standardized protocols is warranted.
