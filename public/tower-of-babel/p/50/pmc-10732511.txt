Axillary Treatment and Chronic Breast Cancer–Related Lymphedema: Implications for Prospective Surveillance and Intervention From a Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: John Boyages, Frank A. Vicini, Behnaz Azimi Manavi, Richelle L. Gaw, Louise A. Koelmeyer, Sheila H. Ridner, Chirag Shah
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Study finds that it is essential to screen for lymphedema. Early detection and intervention improves outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Rates of chronic breast cancer–related lymphedema (cBCRL) range from 5% with sentinel node biopsy (SNB) alone to 20%-50% with axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) and regional node irradiation (RNI) as detected by perometry.1,2 The extent of treatment to the axilla is often considered the most important risk factor for BCRL, with other factors being obesity, barriers to lymphatic screening from distance or cost, and the use of taxane chemotherapy.3,4 One compounding factor is that patients with larger tumors who undergo a mastectomy often require an ALND, have more positive nodes, and are more likely to require taxane chemotherapy and postmastectomy RNI. Therefore, understanding the risk of cBCRL on the basis of these treatment-related factors is essential as they may not be independent but rather additive.

cBCRL can significantly affect the quality of life and lead to pain, recurrent infections, limited arm function, and costly resource-intensive therapies such as complex decongestive physiotherapy (CDP).5 Identifying patients with subclinical BCRL (sBCRL), before lymphedema is clinically visible, potentially allows for early intervention and cBCRL prevention.6 Short-course compression therapy as treatment of sBCRL has been shown to improve outcomes and may prevent the need for CDP.7-12 This secondary analysis expands on previously reported risk factors from the PREVENT study.4 Four axillary treatment groups were based on surgery and radiation to the axilla as previously defined.1 The current study, to our knowledge, the largest prospective study conducted to date, explores progression to cBCRL with or without early intervention. Patients were carefully measured by trained research staff in 13 centers over 3 years. The risk of cBCRL was categorized as <5% (low), 5%-10% (intermediate), or >10% (high), and the study outcomes suggest that a risk-based lymphedema screening, early intervention, and treatment program are optimal.

METHODS

Study Design

This secondary analysis includes data from a multicenter, international, unblinded randomized controlled trial (RCT) that compared bioimpedance spectroscopy (BIS) and circumference measurements (using tape measurement [TM]) for sBCRL surveillance among patients with newly diagnosed breast cancer. The protocol specified a secondary analysis of predictive factors of lymphedema progression (page 25, Study Protocol). The trial was conducted at four sites in Australia and nine in the United States. Detailed design and inclusion criteria are published elsewhere and are included in the study protocol (Protocol, online only).13 Presurgical inclusion criteria included women over 18 months with histologically confirmed breast cancer (invasive or ductal carcinoma in situ [DCIS]). Exclusion criteria have been published elsewhere.14 Patients who developed cBCRL, irrespective of the timing (during or between screening visits), were included in this secondary analysis if they underwent axillary treatment (surgery with or without radiation) and had complete data available (Data Supplement).

Procedures

Those assigned to the BIS or TM group were assessed by trained research staff using an L-Dex U400 (ImpediMed Limited, Australia) and reported in L-Dex units or with a nonflexible Gulick-II tape/marked board, measured twice at 10-cm increments from the wrist to 50 cm. Arm volume was calculated by a truncated cone formula using the average of the two assessments.15 An L-Dex unit change ≥6.5 or TM volume change ≥5% and <10% above pre-surgical baselines triggered sBCRL and initiated a 4-week, 12-hour per day compression intervention of a 23-32 mmHg compression sleeve and gauntlet. Postoperative BIS or TM assessments for all end points were scheduled at 3- and 6-month follow-up visits and then 6-monthly until 36 months. cBCRL was defined as the need for CDP using the same criterion for both screening methods (TM ≥10% relative volume increase of the at-risk arm compared with the control arm).16

Outcomes

The final results of the primary outcome have been published.9 This analysis was performed after study completion (December 31, 2020) on patients categorized into four groups by the extent of axillary treatment: SNB (n = 651), SNB + RNI (n = 58), ALND (n = 85), or ALND + RNI (n = 87). Patients with no nodal treatment (eg, DCIS without SNB; n = 25) and 12 patients with incomplete axillary or radiation details were excluded; none developed cBCRL. Radiation location was categorized as yes/no for the following: breast/chest wall; supraclavicular fossa (SCF), infraclavicular fossa, and internal mammary chain (IMC); and axilla level 1, axilla level 2, and axilla level 3. Patients in the SNB alone (group 1) and ALND alone (group 3) might have received radiation to the breast or chest wall but not regional nodes. Various patient, tumor, and treatment factors were explored within each axillary treatment group, along with previously identified patient risk factors (BMI, rurality) and chemotherapy use (categorized as no chemotherapy, taxane-based, or non–taxane-based). BMI at initial assessment was categorized as normal (or underweight) at <25, overweight (≥25-30), or obese (>30).17 This study categorized patient residence as rural or nonrural (including city/urban and suburban combined).

Statistical Analysis

All cases of cBCRL were included in this analysis irrespective of the method of sBCRL detection (TM or BIS) or timing (detected at scheduled visits with intervention, as in our primary analysis [n = 30], or progressed between visits without intervention [n = 39]). As this is a study of risk factors, all patients who developed cBCRL were included, irrespective of how they were screened (TM or BIS). Categorical variables were summarized using frequency distributions and compared using chi-square tests of independence. The median and IQR summarized continuous study variables; group comparisons were conducted using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Medians were compared using nonparametric two-sample tests to delineate samples. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of surgery. Analyses were conducted in SPSS (Version 29·0 2022, IBM, Armonk, New York), MedCalc (Version 11·6 2011, Medcalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium), and Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington). Kaplan-Meier estimates of cBCRL were calculated18 and compared using the log-rank test.19 Statistical significance was established as a maximum two-sided type I error of 0.05. To develop a proposed sBCRL screening program, crude rates of cBCRL were documented as low (≤5%, green), intermediate (>5%-10%, orange), or high (>10%, red).

RESULTS

Overall, 881 patients enrolled between June 24, 2014, and September 11, 2018, met the criteria for this secondary analysis. Most patients underwent SNB alone as axillary treatment (n = 651; 73.9%). Fifty-eight patients (6.6%) had SNB + RNI, 85 (9.6%) ALND, and 87 (9.9%) ALND + RNI. The total ALND rate was 19.5% (Table 1). Most patients were overweight or obese (total 73.2%), and this did not vary by axillary treatment group (P = .394; Appendix Table A1, online only). Most patients lived in city or suburban locations (76.7%). Patients having more treatment to the axilla tended to be younger with higher-stage tumors (P < .001; Appendix Table A1). The median follow-up was 32.8 months (IQR, 21-34.3).

Patients being screened for sBCRL using BIS had significantly lower rates of cBCRL. For TM, 43 of 438 (9.8%) developed cBCRL versus 26 of 443 (5.9%) for BIS (P = .03). The corresponding 2-year actuarial rates were 8.3% (95% CI, 5.6 to 11.0) for TM versus 3.6% (95% CI, 1.8 to 5.4) for BIS (P = .001). The corresponding 3-year rates were 10.3% (95% CI, 7.2 to 13.4) versus 6.4% (95% CI, 3.9 to 8.9; P = .03; Fig 1A).

The rates of sBCRL were 20.3% for SNB, 21.1% for SNB + RNI, 28.6% for ALND, and 50.6% for ALND + RNI. Progression to cBCRL after intervention was 1.9%, 1.8%, 8.6%, and 13.9%, respectively (P < .001). The 3-year actuarial risk of cBCRL, regardless of intervention, was 4.4% (95% CI, 2.7 to 6.1), 4.2% (95% CI, 0 to 9.8), 25.8% (95% CI, 15.8 to 35.8), and 26.0% (95% CI, 15.4 to 36.7), respectively (P < .001; Fig 1B). Figure 2 shows the rates of cBCRL by various patient, tumor, and treatment factors. Low rates of cBCRL (shown in green when <5%) were seen with lower BMI (BMI <25), even for those undergoing ALND. IMC irradiation or mastectomy was not a cBCRL risk factor for any group. Rural patients had higher rates of cBCRL, independent of BMI, for all subgroups (Fig 2).

For SNB alone, cBCRL risk increased with BMI: 1.2% for BMI <25 versus 6.3% for BMI ≥30 (P = .03). For patients having a SNB, RNI (SNB, 4.1% v SNB + RNI, 3.4%), or receipt of a taxane (no chemotherapy, 3.9% v taxane, 4.4%), the risk of cBCRL was low. For patients treated with SNB + RNI, taxane use (5.7% v 0%) or SCF radiation (5.0% v 0%) was associated with an intermediate risk of cBCRL (Fig 2) and radiation fractionation (standard v hypofractionation) was not associated with cBCRL risk.

For patients treated with ALND alone, with BMI ≥25<30 (34.6%) or BMI ≥30 (29.7%), the rate of cBCRL was high compared with normal-weight patients (4.5%). Similarly, chemotherapy use (taxanes [33.3%] or nontaxane [33.3%]) was associated with higher rates of cBCRL than with no chemotherapy (13.9%). Among patients treated with ALND + RNI, most received SCF radiation (94.9%) and taxanes (89.7%), so no specific additional risk factors emerged for cBCRL.

At 1 year after an ALND, the actuarial risk of cBCRL was 7.8% (95% CI, 1.8 to 13.8) for SCF radiation versus 0% without SCF radiation (log-rank; P = .524). At 2 years, the corresponding rates were 18.6% (95% CI, 9.4 to 27.8) versus 0% (log-rank; P = .312).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this secondary analysis of the largest RCT assessing prospective screening and early intervention for sBCRL examined established risk factors for cBCRL by the extent of axillary treatment. We used the previously published risk stratification model from a prospective nonrandomized study, which assessed the risk of BCRL on the basis of four axillary treatment groups and examined our previously determined risk factors for cBCRL for each group (Fig 2) 1,3 In this study, interventions driven by the detection of extracellular fluid change, rather than whole arm volume change, appear to benefit patients significantly but have been debated in other studies.16,20-22 The results demonstrate that patients undergoing SNB with or without RT have low rates of cBCRL, but factors such as increasing BMI, SCF radiation, or use of a taxane increase risk. Patients treated with ALND with or without RT had high rates of cBCRL, particularly with increasing BMI, SCF radiation, or taxanes, irrespective of mastectomy or breast conservation treatment. Together, the results demonstrated that the most significant cBCRL risk factor is the extent of axillary management, particularly surgery. On the basis of these results, we have proposed a pragmatic lymphedema screening and intervention program that increases in frequency as the risk of cBCRL increases (categorized as <5% [low], 5%-10% [intermediate], or >10% [high]) on the basis of various patient, tumor, and treatment factors with associated treatment pathways (Fig 3).

As in our previous study, rurality appeared to be an independent patient factor associated with cBCRL, possibly because of more rural dwellers being in the group that progressed to cBCRL without receiving any intervention.4 This suggests that more frequent monitoring of these individuals may be needed than was offered in the parent study. Developing cost-effective models of care for rural patients at risk for lymphedema remains a critical health challenge. BIS screening would allow a simple, rapid, and objective measure for more remote communities.

With the ongoing de-escalation of breast cancer treatment, questions about optimal locoregional treatment inevitably arise. While decisions are sometimes more straightforward in the context of breast-conserving radiation, dilemmas occur after a mastectomy when patients have a pathologic complete remission after neoadjuvant chemotherapy or limited nodal involvement after SNB or ALND. In addition, when immediate flap- or implant-based reconstruction has taken place, there is often a reluctance to add radiation, particularly when the risk of locoregional recurrence is low or intermediate. The AMAROS trial found that ALND can be omitted if radiation is given after a positive SNB with lower rates of cBCRL (arm circumference increase ≥10% [12.6% for ALND v 5.3% for RNI]).23 SCF recurrence was 11-20 per 1,000 without RT versus 1-15 per 1,000 with RT in older studies where taxane chemotherapy was not routine.24,25 In addition, the relative contribution of radiation to different nodal basins to the reduction in distant metastases is unknown. However, radiation of the first-echelon axillary or IMC nodes is likely more critical than the second-echelon SCF nodes.

To our knowledge, while this study represents the largest RCT that compares BCRL diagnostics and includes an intervention for sBCRL, limitations exist. The follow-up is relatively short at around 3 years; however, many studies demonstrate that most cBCRL is present within 3 years.1 Consistent with modern practice, the ALND rate for the study was 19.5% as many patients now receive RNI after a positive SNB.26 Screening for sBCRL by BIS was significantly associated with less cBCRL, suggesting that early detection may prevent progression to cBCRL. BIS was also previously shown to be associated with fewer false-positive triggers for sBCRL than volumes derived using TM. Because of the small number of events in some of the subgroups, the proposed guidelines for lymphedema screening (by BIS, TM, or perometry), education, and early intervention have been derived not only from our trial but also from other prospective studies and our experience with lymphatic screening implementation over the past 15 years.6 They are documented for debate, discussion, and guidance.

How do the results of this study affect clinical practice? Previous results from this RCT demonstrated that using BIS as part of prospective sBCRL surveillance, coupled with an early compression sleeve and gauntlet intervention, significantly reduced cBCRL (7.9% v 19.2%; P = .016) as compared with TM screening. The study design of the primary analysis focused on patients eligible for screening and excluded 39 patients who developed cBCRL between visits and were therefore ineligible for intervention (BIS [n = 19] or TM [n = 20]). Some patients will develop rapid-onset cBCRL, usually during adjuvant taxane–based chemotherapy after axillary dissection, even before radiation commences. From a clinical perspective, total rates of cBCRL, as used in this study, are required to make informed decisions about the treatment extent and associated rates of cBCRL. Using all cases, irrespective of whether intervention was initiated, the risk of cBCRL was 9.8% for TM and 5.9% for BIS (P = .028).

Many centers cannot access sophisticated equipment such as bioimpedance or perometry and need to rely on patient symptoms or less expensive measurement techniques, including circumferential TMs.26 Our early intervention protocols apply, irrespective of the monitoring method for sBCRL. Other studies have compared L-Dex or volume measures in the context of established cBCRL and treatment with a compression garment over 12 months.21 These studies cannot be directly compared with our study, which monitored for sBCRL over 3 years in an international setting, with active intervention if sBCRL was detected. At this point of time in lymphatic research, it is time to translate our findings into practice using an implementation approach as previously published.27 Ultimately, for patient care, it is vitally important to screen for sBCRL to ensure early detection and intervention to attempt to prevent large volume changes. We defined cBCRL as a 10% or more relative volume change between affected and unaffected arms in both arms of the study. Volume change affects a patient's quality of life, such as the need to wear a long sleeve or cope with recurrent cellulitis.

However, questions remain about integrating lymphatic screening into a follow-up program and whether patients with a SNB should have less frequent screening than patients with an ALND. Our study demonstrates that screening and early intervention using L-Dex or TM worked across 13 clinical settings in the United States and Australia. These methods are totally different. One measures interstitial fluid change and the other volume change through methods such as perometry and water displacement. Our rates of cBCRL are low, not only because of regular screening but also because of less use of ALND and undoubtedly because of increased awareness in and education of the patients about early detection of sBCRL. Developing a lymphedema screening program using the constraints of space and resources is vitally more important than trying to compare previously validated measuring techniques.

Finally, for ideal patient care, it is crucial to provide evidence-based risk reduction education. There are too many myths and variations in information about BCRL that have not been informed by clinical evidence. It is vital that lymphatic screening and pragmatically delivered education, including symptoms and arm care, become standard although axillary treatments have decreased in intensity over time. We particularly need to educate and screen those at higher risk more closely than those at very low risk to empower patients and improve outcomes.28 The present study suggests that for patients undergoing SNB, regular lymphatic screening (eg, 3-6 monthly) is essential for those who are overweight or obese or receive SCF irradiation or a taxane. Patients who have had an ALND with or without radiation should be advised to have 3-monthly lymphatic screening.

As part of this risk-benefit discussion, understanding estimates of cBCRL shown in Figure 2 and communicating them to the patient is essential to shared decision making. Given that most cases of cBCRL present within 3 years, it would be sensible to adjust screening schedules on the basis of risk and reduce the frequency after a 3-year follow-up. On March 24, 2023, the National Cancer Control Network updated its survivorship guidelines to include the fact “that early detection/diagnosis and early referral are key for optimal lymphedema management because stages 0 and I are reversible, whereas stages II and III are less responsive to treatment. Therefore, survivors at risk for lymphedema should be regularly screened for lymphedema by symptom assessment, clinical examination, and, if available, BIS”.29
