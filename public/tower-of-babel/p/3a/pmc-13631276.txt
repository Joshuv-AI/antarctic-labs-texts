A trait database for the characterization of diatom communities in disconnected pools
Authors: Guillermo Quevedo-Ortiz, Fernanda Gonzalez-Saldias, Julie Crabot, Frédéric Rimet, Joan Gomà, Núria Bonada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrait-based approaches are increasingly used in ecology to understand biodiversity responses to environmental changes. However, information on diatom traits, particularly in temporary aquatic systems, remains limited. Here, we present DIATPOOL, the first database focused on diatom traits from disconnected pools. DIATPOOL includes 17 morphological, ecological, and physiological traits, derived from literature and expert knowledge, including size, shape, apical constriction, dispersal capacity, ecological guilds, colony formation, or habitat tolerance. The database also includes an initial inventory of diatom species, their relative abundances, and environmental characteristics of disconnected pools sampled across the Iberian Peninsula. DIATPOOL provides an open and standardized resource to explore diatom biodiversity and functional traits in temporary and intermittent aquatic systems, offering a valuable framework for ecological, taxonomic, and bioassessment studies in these transitional habitats.

Background & Summary

Over the last decades, trait-based studies have gained considerable attention in ecology due to their ability to predict biodiversity responses to environmental conditions1–4. Traits, whether used individually or in combination, allow for a straightforward characterization of community assembly under different environmental scenarios5–7. For example, they are useful in anticipating community shifts in response to global change drivers such as eutrophication or deforestation8–10. In general terms, traits can be defined as any heritable morphological, ecological, physiological, or phenological characteristic measurable at the individual level11. This definition encompasses a wide variety of characteristics related to species morphology, such as size, physiology, behavior (e.g., mobility), and life history traits (e.g., spore formation)12–15. The use of traits requires a detailed and careful preliminary characterization for each group of organisms13,16. Although several trait databases are already available and cover a wide range of organisms, including fish17, macroinvertebrates4, spiders18, and crustaceans19, there is still a need to create, complete, and expand trait information for many other organism groups.

Diatoms are photoautotrophic unicellular microalgae, solitary or colonial, that inhabit most aquatic and moist terrestrial environments20,21. They represent an extremely diverse group, with estimates ranging between 100.000 and 200.000 species22,23, of which only about 64.000 have been formally described24,25. Currently, several trait databases classify diatoms according to life-forms, size classes, ecological guilds, or indicator values, among others14,26,27. Their effectiveness as predictors of environmental conditions has been widely demonstrated across studies28–30. For example, it is known that small, mobile, and low-profile species are more abundant during the summer in alpine rivers31. It has also been observed that adhesion strategies appear to confer a greater competitive advantage under more unstable environmental conditions, compared to diatoms of lower adhesion capacity (e.g., motile and planktonic)32. Additionally, small diatom species tend to disperse more successfully over long distances, primarily due to wind transport33,34. Furthermore, mobile taxa with weak substrate attachment can disperse farther than those that are strongly attached or planktonic35. However, collecting information on traits for small and diverse organisms such as diatoms is a highly complex task. The lack of data for rare or infrequent taxa, the existence of species complexes, and information based on researchers’ expertise pose significant challenges for characterizing these traits14,36. Moreover, given that diatoms are good bioindicators, it is crucial to compile these traits, as they can offer valuable insights into the use of diatoms in biomonitoring14,27,37.

Temporary rivers, also known as Intermittent Rivers and Ephemeral Streams (IRES), are highly dynamic fluvial ecosystems that can harbor a rich diversity of diatoms38. These systems are characterized by alternating wet and dry phases39,40. While in the wet phase water flows between different sections of the river, the dry phase is characterized by a complete loss of flow. During the latter, it is common to find disconnected pools, which may either reconnect or dry out depending on environmental conditions41. Pools therefore constitute transitional habitats between aquatic and terrestrial environments, with a strong capacity to host unique diatom communities38,40,42,43. Despite this, no database currently exists that specifically addresses the diversity of diatom traits in disconnected temporary pools. This is especially important considering that temporary rivers are more prevalent globally than perennial rivers44, and that flow intermittence is projected to increase45, leading to a greater occurrence of disconnected pools. To address this gap, we present DIATPOOL, a database focused on the traits of diatoms from disconnected pools of temporary rivers. While information on certain traits (e.g., guilds, indicator values, and coloniality) can be found online, other relevant data remains scattered, incomplete, or unpublished. In DIATPOOL, we have compiled, updated, and organized traits related to the potential adaptation of diatoms to the environmental conditions of disconnected pools. Additionally, we propose new traits that may be useful for characterizing such habitats. Our goal is therefore to provide a unique resource that facilitates the integration of traits related to flow intermittence in ecological research and highlights the importance of these transitional habitats for diatom diversity.

Methods

Diatom species in DIATPOOL

The DIATPOOL database is associated with an initial list of diatom species characteristic of disconnected pools (see the taxonomy section in the DIATPOOL matrix, available in Data Records), which is expected to grow as more pools are sampled. This inventory of diatoms was obtained from successive samplings of benthic diatoms conducted in the Mediterranean region of the Iberian Peninsula (Fig. 1). Between May and July 2023 and 2024, a total of 66 disconnected pools with diverse characteristics (size, geology, degree of anthropogenic influence, climate, etc.) were sampled (Metadata_pool, available in Data Records). All pools were in an arheic state, i.e., completely disconnected, without flow but with surface water39,46. In cases where there was more than one (within 200 m), the most representative one was always selected, recording the number of other pools and the distance to the nearest one. Samples were collected by scraping seven submerged stones (approx. 15 cm² each) with a toothbrush and subsequently preserved in 96% ethanol. In addition, physicochemical variables were recorded in situ in each pool using a multiparameter probe (YSI Professional Plus Multiparameter Probe), and a water sample was collected for nutrient analysis in the laboratory (Metadata matrix, available in Data Records).

Diversity of disconnected pools sampled. (a) Map of the Iberian Peninsula with the locations of the pools (red circles); (b) Riera de Llogari (Catalonia); (c) Río Bergantes (Valencia); (d) Arroyo de Valdeazores (Andalusia); (e) Rambla del Garruchal (Murcia); (f) Río Gibranzos (Extremadura).

All diatom samples were treated in a 33% hydrogen peroxide bath at 90 °C. Drops of hydrochloric acid were added to remove potential calcium carbonate inclusions. The material was left to settle for 48 hours, followed by successive rinses with distilled water, removing the supernatant each time. A portion of the resulting material was placed onto glass slides and allowed to dry for 24 hours. The dry slides were then mounted with Naphrax© resin. Samples were examined under a Zeiss Polyvar microscope at 1000 × magnification using differential interference contrast and a digital camera. All samples were taxonomically identified to the species level using online publications and the main diatom monographs47–52. The taxonomic list consisted of a total of 385 species, including varieties. Additionally, a total of 400 valves were counted for each sample, and the relative abundance was calculated (Abundance Matrix, available in Data Records). Once the matrix was obtained, each species was assigned the traits described below, based on information from published sources, our own data, image interpretation, and expert evaluation.

Below, we describe the main traits selected for the construction of DIATPOOL. These traits are expected to reflect adaptations to the hydrological, physicochemical, and geomorphological conditions characteristic of disconnected pools41. Some of them have been used to characterize lentic ecosystems, such as wetlands, ponds or lakes, whereas others are typically used in lotic ecosystems53. Although other traits, such as physiological27, transcriptomic, or metabolomic ones54, could provide valuable insights into the adaptations of diatoms to these environments, they were not included in the current version of the database due to the limited availability of such information. Nevertheless, DIATPOOL can serve as a solid foundation that may be expanded in the future as more data on these and other types of traits become available.

Size [morphological]

This trait classifies diatom species according to cell size. Given the high dynamism of temporary rivers, a higher presence of larger species is likely to be observed in disconnected pools due to their lentic conditions. Cell length, width, and thickness (µm) values were obtained from published literature14,42,48–51,55–86. For this reason, we propose five alternative morphological forms.

Length and width (µm): Corresponds to the average length and width of the valve, calculated as (minimum value + maximum value)/2. Most reference values for each diatom species considered in DIATPOOL (see below) were obtained from previously compiled data14,87, primarily based on Krammer and Lange-Bertalot48–51. We also updated some values and added others for new species. (Continuous variable).

Classical group [morphological]

This trait corresponds to the traditional classification of diatoms into centric and pennate forms83,94–96. Although this division is not phylogenetic (e.g., centric diatoms do not form a monophyletic group), it provides valuable information on morphology and ecology. Considering that disconnected pools originate from temporary rivers (lotic systems) but function as small ponds or lakes (lentic systems), this trait can be highly informative (particularly in multi-habitat samples, e.g., benthic, planktonic, or periphytic samples). (Categorical binary variables, mutually exclusive).

Centric: Species with radial symmetry, typically circular, oval, triradiate, triangular, quadrate, or polygonal in shape (rarely elongated). They are usually associated with lentic environments and exhibit a primarily planktonic lifestyle. Sexual reproduction is morphologically oogamous in most genera53.

Centric: Species with radial symmetry, typically circular, oval, triradiate, triangular, quadrate, or polygonal in shape (rarely elongated). They are usually associated with lentic environments and exhibit a primarily planktonic lifestyle. Sexual reproduction is morphologically oogamous in most genera53.

Pennate: Species that display bilateral symmetry around a sternum, most often elongated in shape. They are generally associated with benthic habitats and may or may not have the ability to move through a raphe (a slit-like structure). Sexual reproduction is morphologically isogamous in most genera53.

Raphe group [morphological]

This trait classifies diatom species according to the presence and type of raphe, a structure that enables cell motility89. Since diatoms have been shown to actively move toward areas of higher moisture97, this trait may represent an adaptive advantage in disconnected pools, where partial drying is frequent. Instead, centrals could dominate larger disconnected pools, as they are usually found in lentic waters such as lakes or ponds98,99. We defined five main categories according to the published literature83,96,100–104. (Categorical binary variables, mutually exclusive).

Centric: Radially symmetric species that completely lack a raphe. They lack significant motility. Although centric diatoms, like araphid species, do not possess a raphe, their circular morphology is highly distinctive in planktonic environments (e.g., Aulacoseira, Cyclotella).

Shape [morphological]

This trait considers the overall shape of diatom valves. It is a non-phylogenetic trait aimed at identifying morphological similarities that may reflect convergent adaptations to the specific conditions of disconnected pools. For example, some shapes may be advantageous for moving through sediments, while others, such as curved or hook-shaped forms, may facilitate passive dispersal between pools38,97. The classification was established based on the main forms described in the original species descriptions and referenced in various available monographs42,52,56,59,73,77,80,106. Although the rhomboid shape was used in several guides, it was not included because it is often similar to the lanceolate shape. Each species was assigned a percentage of membership to its main shape, ensuring the total sum reached 100%. This means that diatoms with basic forms are composed of 100% of a single form (e.g., 100% circular), whereas others are composed of a combination of basic forms (e.g., 70% curved and 30% lanceolate). Constriction [morphological]

This trait classifies diatom species based on the degree of apical constriction. Similar to the shape trait, it is not a phylogenetically-related trait but a functional criterion aimed at detecting potential convergent adaptations to highly dynamic environments such as disconnected pools. Apical constriction may influence both attachment and the ability of cells to move among sediment microparticles in search of more humid microhabitats. To code this trait, the apical poles of all identified species were analyzed using original species descriptions and the main available monographs42,52,56,59,73,77,80,106. Figure 2c shows a diagram of the constriction degree and their representation in diatoms from some disconnected pools. This trait characterizes ecological guilds, which are groups of taxa that share similar traits and exploit similar resources108. We adopted the guilds proposed by Rimet and Nicolosi Gelis14,87, modified from Passy26, due to their robustness and wide use in ecological studies14,26,42,47–51,62,71,72,82,89,109–152. Teratological form [physiological]

This trait includes diatom taxa prone to exhibiting teratological forms, which are morphological anomalies reflecting sublethal responses to environmental stress. Based mainly on Falasco and collaborators, we included the occurrence of deformities to assess potential adaptive strategies of species under such dynamic environmental conditions152. Information was also compiled from specialized online resources on teratologies (e.g., La Tératothèque from IRAE372) through detailed analysis of diatom images. Technical Validation

DIATPOOL consists of 17 traits obtained both from external sources and from information generated specifically for this study.

On the one hand, 8 traits were obtained and harmonized from previously published literature. This includes Classical group94, Rafe group89, Morphology group83, Ecological guilds, If colonial, Solitary cells/Colonial type14, Teratological form371, as well as Ecological indicator values27. The use of these previously published traits ensures compliance with basic quality standards and provides valuable information for applications in disconnected pools. Furthermore, all these traits have been referenced by other studies, supporting their reliability as they have been previously tested and validated by members of the scientific community. Similarly, 4 traits were adapted for straightforward use and applicability in disconnected pools: Chloroplast number, Chloroplast shape, Dispersal capacity, and Habitat substrate. All of these traits were derived and subsequently adapted from the results presented by Nicolosi Gelis87.

On the other hand, 5 traits were specifically coded for this work: Size, Shape, Constriction, Habitat presence and Habitat tolerance. The design of these traits required the analysis of various diatom monographs, scientific articles and online resources. The different categories defined for Size, for instance, were derived from length and width measurements published both in original species descriptions and in other available references14. The coding of Shape and apical Constriction for each species required additional validation efforts by the authors. It was necessary to establish a consensus on the percentage of each trait category for all species. To address such variability, several diatom monographs and original species descriptions were reviewed52,56,73. Finally, the values for Habitat_presence and Habitat_tolerance were obtained after analyzing and comparing various previously published species lists from the main environments considered (aquatic, terrestrial, and aerial).

As mentioned in previously, the development of DIATPOOL required the incorporation of an initial taxonomic list of diatoms from disconnected pools. It was necessary to update and standardize numerous species names according to the most recent nomenclature (except for cases of taxonomic uncertainty) (AlgaeBase96). This included, for example, elevating some varieties to species rank (e.g., Meridion circulare var. constrictum to Meridion constrictum) and incorporating newly described species (e.g., Sellaphora temporalis). To ensure data comparability and transparency, DIATPOOL includes, at the end, part of the synonym considered during the compilation process. For all species, trait information was added whenever possible. It was necessary to add or update trait data both for species already included and for those absent from the original databases. This characterization was carried out using previously reviewed and published references, both for species and traits, which are recorded in the corresponding bibliographic columns of DIATPOOL. When the required references were not available, each species was carefully evaluated by experts to ensure accurate characterization. In cases where original or estimated information was provided (e.g., inferred from related species), it was explicitly indicated with notes such as “Present study” or “This information is considered an estimate based on expert judgment and the collective knowledge of the group.” All these efforts aimed to strengthen the transparency and credibility of the data. Nevertheless, important limitations remain in this study and should be carefully considered. First, we confirmed the existence of uneven information availability among taxa, which creates knowledge gaps and, in some cases, necessitates the use of expert-based annotations. Furthermore, the evolving nature of taxonomic uncertainty hinders a fully stable characterization of traits. These limitations should be understood within the context of each habitat, where optimal trait values may vary substantially, highlighting the need for further research tailored to specific environmental conditions. Therefore, DIATPOOL should be regarded as a dynamic resource, subject to continuous revision and the incorporation of new information.

The database of relative abundances (Abundance_diatom) and the habitat characterization database (Metadata_pool) are also made available to the scientific community. Both datasets have been thoroughly reviewed and optimized by the authors for application in various ecological analyses. Since our sampling was conducted in a standardized manner, the information provided by both matrices is ready to be used in a wide range of ecological studies.

To further validate DIATPOOL, a combined RLQ and fourth-corner analysis40 was carried out to relate diatom trait profiles in disconnected pools to environmental conditions. This analysis is a multivariate ordination technique capable of integrating three tables and detecting co-structures among a trait matrix (DIATPOOL), an environmental matrix (Metadata_pool), and a species matrix (Abundance_diatom)378,379. To facilitate data analysis and visualization, only a subset of traits and environmental variables were used (other combinations could reveal additional patterns related to diatoms in disconnected pools). Using 9999 random permutations and adjusted p-values, we identified significantly positive relationships (red cells), negative relationships (blue cells), and non-significant relationships (gray cells) between traits and environmental variables (Fig. 3). For example, in our size category, a positive relationship was observed between pool depth and very small/small diatoms, whereas pH and NPDOC showed a negative relationship. Conversely, both pH and oxygen concentration were positively associated with diatoms lacking a raphe (centric and araphid species). Additionally, higher chloride concentrations appeared positively related to strictly aquatic species, suggesting greater salinity tolerance in these taxa. These results highlight the value of DIATPOOL as a useful tool for ecological studies of diatoms in these habitats, enabling the analysis of distribution patterns and environmental adaptation.
