Real-time automated billing for tobacco treatment: performance evaluation of the CigStopper machine learning framework
Authors: Derek J Baughman, Layth Qassem, Lina Sulieman, Michael E Matheny, Scott D Nelson, Edward Anselm, Hilary A Tindle, Daniel Fabbri, Peter J Embi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate CigStopper, a machine learning algorithm designed to predict billing eligibility for tobacco cessation counseling (CPT 99406/99407), addressing persistent underbilling and documentation gaps in health systems.Materials and MethodsWe trained CigStopper on a 40,000-note corpus comprising real-world de-identified clinical notes, synthetically generated notes, and a blended dataset. Notes were categorized by billing eligibility and smoking documentation. Random Forest models were trained and evaluated using both flat multiclass and hierarchical classification approaches. Performance was assessed on a 20% holdout set of real notes using standard metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1).ResultsModels trained on real or blended datasets achieved high performance for billing eligibility (F1 ≥ 0.97) and 99406 prediction (F1 ≥ 0.90). Prediction for intensive counseling (99407) remained limited (F1 ≤ 0.56). Synthetic-only training resulted in overfitting, with poor generalization to real-world data. Hierarchical classification improved eligibility detection and CPT code prediction compared with flat multiclass models.DiscussionFindings demonstrate that blended datasets mitigate class imbalance and improve generalizability, while hierarchical architectures enhance performance on billing tasks. Persistent gaps in 99407 prediction were related to low training volume, likely reflecting documentation and coding culture rather than model limitations, underscoring systemic issues in clinical note content.ConclusionCigStopper demonstrates feasibility as a scalable NLP-based billing validation tool. By automating tobacco cessation CPT coding, the algorithm can improve data integrity, reduce missed reimbursement, and support health systems in aligning clinical care with financial and population health priorities.

1. Background

Physician delivery of tobacco cessation services is poor relative to other preventive services.1 There is a well-established relationship between smoking and adverse health, as well as the substantial economic burden associated with tobacco-related disease.2,3 Although there has been notable progress in reducing the prevalence of smoking in the United States to 11.6%, over 28.8 million adults continue to smoke cigarettes regularly.4 The barriers to the routine provision of these services have been studied, 5,6 but there has been little attention given to reimbursement.

Clinical practice guidelines recommend clinicians screen for tobacco use at every visit and provide brief counseling to all tobacco users, regardless of readiness to quit. The American Lung Association (ALA) publishes billing guidelines for practitioners on how to properly offer and document this standard of care.7 Two CPT codes, 99406 and 99407, are utilized for tobacco use counseling sessions lasting 3-10 minutes and more than 10 minutes, respectively. According to Medicare, these codes are reimbursable eight times within a 12-month period; other payers typically adhere to these guidelines.8

Despite the established standard of care—advising a tobacco user to quit9 and screening for tobacco use every visit10—it is not usually captured in claims data. A recent report identified an unexpectedly low rate of billing (0.3%) for tobacco cessation interventions in an eight-hospital health delivery system in Pennsylvania and Maryland (Wellspan Health).11 The model presumed all insurance contracts were fee-for-service and that a cessation intervention could be provided at every office, suggesting numerous missed opportunities and a substantial impact on health system revenue. Similarly, an analysis of Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC) data from January 2021 to December 2023 similarly indicated a 1% billing rate for these codes among potentially eligible encounters over the past three years, corresponding to a significant annual revenue.12 Across published studies, billing rates for 99406/99407 typically range from <1% to 5% of eligible encounters, reflecting substantial variation across health systems and care settingsThis pattern of billing has been observed in a wide variety of settings, including academic medical centers13,14 and rural health clinics nationwide.15

Improving the accuracy of medical coding and billing data for tobacco cessation counseling can help identify and treat patients who want to quit smoking. When tobacco-treatment encounters are consistently documented and billed, health systems gain the ability to monitor cessation service delivery, identify gaps in care, and sustainably fund treatment programs—mechanisms that can expand access to counseling and increase the likelihood that smokers receive evidence-based support.16 Previous studies have explored the use of electronic health records (EHR) data to support smoking cessation efforts. A Cochrane review by Boyle et al. examined the use of electronic health records EHRs to support smoking cessation, providing valuable insights into the potential and challenges of leveraging EHR data for this purpose.17 Automating CPT codes for validating tobacco cessation care has 5 s beyond streamlining administrative billing processes. These include improving clinical workflows, enhancing decision support systems, and strengthening population health management—supporting providers, administrators, and researchers. Natural language processing (NLP) methods for CPT code automation have been demonstrated previously.18 Prior studies have demonstrated that NLP can accurately recover procedure and counseling codes from free-text notes; random forest models have shown particular promise for this purpose.19 Since billing-relevant documentation often exists in unstructured clinical text but is not reflected in structured claims data, NLP is essential for detecting eligibility. Expanding these methods across the CPT code suite has important implications for improving data quality, expanding visibility into cessation care delivery, and reducing reliance on incomplete structured fields for quality reporting.

The impact of missed opportunities in tobacco cessation care is significant, especially since the literature indicates that clinician tobacco cessation services increase quitting rates substantially.3 *Although the literature has thoroughly explored the barriers to implementing tobacco cessation systemically,20,21 there is limited study on scalable tools to improve documentation and billing alignment. High-quality data is needed for the accuracy and reliability of AI tools in healthcare.22 Because tobacco cessation care documentation often exists in free-text rather than structured fields, unstructured clinical notes offer the best practical source for training machine learning models for identifying and validating eligible billable services.

Building from these insights, we previously developed CigStopper, an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven tool for improving tobacco cessation care billing and data quality.23 The tool employs an application programming interface (API)-based implementation that provides a real-time, EHR-agnostic approach to identifying tobacco-treatment interventions in clinical notes.24

This study aims to further develop and evaluate CigStopper, a machine learning framework for automating CPT billing validation for tobacco cessation counseling based on EHR documentation. By leveraging real and synthetic clinical notes, the project assessed whether blended data can improve generalizability and address class imbalance in supervised learning tasks. The specific objectives were: (1) train a CPT code classification model guided by ALA documentation criteria; (2) compare model performance across three training conditions—real, synthetic, and blended corpora; and (3) validate model performance on a withheld real-world test set from Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC), focusing on eligibility determination and precise CPT code prediction. This framework was evaluated using hierarchical versus multiclass classification architectures to inform scalable implementation strategies for AI-assisted billing in clinical workflows.

2. Methods

2.1 Adaptation of prior methodology

In our previous work during the 2024-2025 AMIA AI Showcase, we developed a clinical decision support (CDS) algorithm to serve as the CigStopper framework for predicting billing codes for tobacco cessation treatments.18 In that work, we used a synthetic note corpus (generated by a large language model to mimic clinician documentation) categorized based on the eligibility for CPT codes (99406 or 99407). The corpus also included notes that were not coded for 99406 or 99407, with two subsets: notes that mentioned smoking, and notes that did not. We trained the algorithm across five conventional machine learning classifiers capable of rapid, low-resource execution: Naive Bayes, Decision Trees, Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression, and Multilayer Perceptron. Outpatient tobacco cessation encounter notes were used as features for unsupervised training. Transformer-based models (e.g, ClinicalBERT) were considered; however, their substantially higher computational cost and inference latency were incompatible with our goal of deploying CigStopper as a real-time, EHR-integrated billing-support tool. Aligning with AMIA Stage III AI Showcase’s ‘health impact analysis’ focus, we evaluated algorithm prediction accuracy for known billed note classes (eligible for 99406, eligible for 99407, non-eligible mentions smoking, non-eligible does not mention smoking) to characterize the model’s potential impact on healthcare quality and operational performance. The study was exempt from full review by VUMC’s institutional review board (IRB #232175).

2.2 Inclusion/exclusion criteria

In alignment with the ALA’s and CMS’s guidelines for tobacco cessation billing (Table 1),7,8 outpatient tobacco cessation encounters were identified from VUMC’s synthetic derivative (VUMC SD), a de-identified clinical data repository containing clinical notes transformed to remove personal identifiers while preserving documentation structure. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 years or older seen from the years 2015 to 2023 with documentation of current tobacco or nicotine use (potentially billable encounters per CPT codes 99406 or 99407 definitions, see Table 1). Exclusion criteria were patients younger than 18 years, those enrolled in hospice or palliative care, and encounters occurring in inpatient units, emergency departments, or clinical departments that do not routinely provide tobacco cessation counseling.

2.3 Data classification

Clinical notes extracted were categorized into four mutually exclusive classes based on billing claims and smoking documentation (Table 2). This categorization facilitated model training to differentiate between encounters billable for counseling services (positive examples), notes that reference smoking but do not meet billing criteria (near-positive comparators), and notes without relevant content (negative controls). These distinctions allowed for models to learn discrete features–narrative eligible counseling services versus clinically related but non-billable documentation.

2.4 Data sources and cleaning

A total of 31,980 clinical notes were extracted from VUMC’s synthetic derivative (“SD”)—a multi-specialty, de-identified copy of ambulatory medical notes. Notes were first grouped into four predefined documentation/billing categories (Table 2). Data cleaning of the VUMC SD corpus commenced in two manual stages ensure billing relevance and sufficient clinical content.

First, irrelevant notes were defined as those written by either non-clinicians or not applicable for ambulatory billing (Figure 1a). Examples include notes written by social workers, nutritionists, speech-language pathologists, procedure notes, ICU attendings, other inpatient specialists, physical therapists, pharmacists, etc. Second, we removed notes with fewer than 500 characters. In our dataset, notes with <500 characters were overwhelmingly administrative or fragmented narrative (e.g, nursing triage notes, patient portal message exchanges, etc.) and rarely contained documentation relevant to tobacco cessation counseling. Although minimum-length standards have not been established, we chose a pragmatic filter based on prior studies reporting average progress note length (∼ 3000 character counts).25 Corresponding to ∼500 words, this threshold serves as a proxy of sufficiency to establish clinician-written narrative in progress notes suitable for our NLP classification tasks.26

2.5 Data generation and corpus composition

Three distinct corpora were developed to evaluate model performance across real, synthetic, and blended documentation sources: VUMC_SD (real notes only–unbalanced), Blend_40K (both real and synthetic notes–balanced), GPT_Synth (synthetic notes only–unbalanced). Each corpus was constructed with the aforementioned four structured class labels—corresponding to CPT billing eligibility and smoking status (Table 2)—and used for independent model training. A summary of the clinical note extraction, filtering, and corpus construction pipeline is shown in Figure 1a, and the distribution of note classesaccross training corpora is shown in Figure 1b.

The VUMC_SD corpus consisted of 17,106 de-identified outpatient notes from the VUMC Synthetic Derivative (VUMC_SD) that passed manual relevance and length filters (see Section 2.4). This corpus represents authentic clinical documentation patterns, with natural variation in how tobacco use and counseling are recorded across specialties. It served as the benchmark for model performance on real-world data, but retained inherent class imbalance based on data availability.

The Blend_40K corpus merged all 17,106 notes from VUMC_SD corpus with a new sample of synthetic notes to create a balanced dataset of 40,000 total notes—10,000 per class. To generate the additional synthetic notes, we fine-tuned a GPT4o mini model on a previously clinician-validated synthetic corpus.18 This fine-tuned GPT4o model generated an extra 22,924 notes to complement the 17,106 SD notes. A clinician team reviewed random samples of GPT4o-generated notes to confirm their clinical plausibility, consistency with assigned class labels, and alignment with tobacco-cessation counseling documentation patterns. This blended dataset was designed to mitigate class imbalance while preserving real-world clinical language. The strategy of combining synthetic billing-aligned phrasing and “real” documentation variability was to facilitate model learning and evaluate generalizability across documentation styles.

The GPT_Synth corpus consisted of 22,924 notes generated using a fine-tuned GPT-4o model trained on a previously clinician-validated synthetic dataset. Four types of notes were generated to align with one of the four predefined categories (Table 2), incorporating language elements consistent with ALA billing guidelines.7 This corpus served as a control to test generalizability of synthetically trained models on real-world data.

2.6 Model training

To facilitate ML training on clinical narrative, notes were first tokenized and vectorized with SpaCy27 and then lemmatized with Sci-kit learn.28 The lemmatized text was fed to the machine learning models (decision tree, naïve bases, RF, logistic regression, and multi-layer perception). RF was chosen as the sole classifier for comparing the three datasets due to its superior performance in capturing nuanced billing requirements. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using GridSearchCV from the Scikit-learn library to identify optimal model training settings.24

To evaluate classification performance across different data sources and architectures, we trained and tested three model variants: CigStopper _SD, CigStopper_Blend_40K, and CigStopper_Synth, each corresponding to one of the three training corpora (Figure 1b). Figure 2 provides an overview of the study design, illustrating the three training datasets and the shared holdout test set used for model evaluation.

Project overview—comparative analysis of the CigStopper algorithm across training data types. Diagram summarizing the study design: Three separate ML models (all random forest) corresponding to the three training corpora—(1) VUMC real notes (17,106), (2) GPT4o-generated synthetic notes (22,924), and (3) the 40,000 blend—were tested on the same holdout 20% real-world set (n = 4268). Standard ML metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1) were used for model comparison.

For each three model variants, we compared two architecture approaches in the classification model: hierarchical (cascading) versus flat multiclass. For the cascading classification, the process was divided into two hierarchical steps: (1) determining eligibility, i.e., whether the encounter was billable, and (2) differentiating between CPT codes 99406 and 99407. Effectively, this was two separate RF models. The cascade was designed to reduce class imbalance and improve recall for low-frequency codes. For the flat multiclass model, a separate triplet of CigStopper model variants were trained as a single-step, four-class multiclass model. The three CigStopper algorithms were similarly trained across the 4 note classes, allowing for a direct prediction of CPT codes 99406, 99407, or non-eligible status without requiring an initial eligibility determination.

2.7 Statistical considerations

A comprehensive set of standard ML metrics compared performance of the CigStopper model variants. This included accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scores. Separate metrics were calculated for eligibility (correctly identifying an encounter as billable) and CPT code classification (distinguishing between 99406 and 99407). The cascading hierarchical classification was compared to traditional multiclassification (simultaneous eligibility and code prediction).

3. Results

3.1 Feature importance

Across all three models—trained on real-world notes (VUMC_SD), blended data (Blend_40K), and synthetic notes (GPT_Synth)—terms appearing consistently among the top-ranked features included “smoking_cessation,” “smoking,” “quit,” and “varenicline.”

Patterns in the real notes appeared to connotate clinically pragmatic language, while synthetic datasets emphasized guideline-adherent phrasing. The VUMC_SD model prioritized shorthand clinical documentation such as “ppd” (packs per day), “smoke,” and “cigarette.” The Blend_40K model elevated structured terms like “tobacco_use” and “nicotine,”. The GPT_Synth model included more generic terms like “health,” “support,” “provide,” and “minute” (See appendix 1 for the full list of terms across data sets).

3.2 Model validation performance

On the validation set (Figure 3), model performance across the three CigStopper algorithms demonstrated very high scores for eligibility classification (F1 > 0.99). For CPT code classification, F1 scores were ≥ 0.94 for the 99406 CPT code prediction. CPT prediction scores were lower for 99407, but CigStopper_Blend_40K performed better than the CigStopper_SD (F1 = 0.588 vs 0.377). The CigStopper_Synth’s F1 scores were consistently ≥0.98 on the synthetic training set.

4. Discussion

This preliminary “real world” evaluation demonstrates the CigStopper algorithm effectively identifies tobacco cessation counseling encounters eligible for CPT billing. The model consistently achieved high accuracy for eligibility classification (F1 > 0.97) and for CPT 99406 prediction (F1 ≥ 0.90) across the blended and synthetic derivative datasets (Blend_40K and VUMC_SD). The performance for CPT 99407 remained consistently low across training sets, likely due to low training volume and documentation heterogeneity (discussed below). Training on the Blend_40K dataset resulted in a modest improvement in billing eligibility prediction compared to models trained only on VUMC_SD (i.e., “real” notes). These findings support augmenting model training data with synthetically generated, billing guideline-aligned documentation to help mitigate class imbalance. This proof-of-concept highlights the value of automating CPT validation to correct misalignment between care delivered and claims submitted—improving data quality.

The three CigStopper algorithms—real-world notes (VUMC_SD), synthetic-only (GPT_Synth), and a blended dataset (Blend_40K)—provided comparative insight into model generalizability. Although models trained solely on real-world notes (VUMC_SD) performed best on the 99407 class, blending real and synthetic notes improved 99406 classification and increased robustness across non-eligible classes. Models trained on synthetic-only corpora (GPT_Synth), while useful for class balance, were not surprisingly prone to overfitting and lacked real-world generalization. These findings are consistent with the literature, supporting a hybrid training approach29,30 that marries authentic clinical documentation with structured guideline-conformant language to capture both language variability and billing specificity.

Our findings highlight important considerations for model performance in predicting CPT codes from narrative text. First, clinical data quality and clinical documentation practices have a clear impact on predictive accuracy. While the blended and synthetic note corpora provided stronger generalization eligibility detection and common 99406 documentation cases, 99407 predictions remained sensitive to training note structure and documentation quality. For example, an important source of classification error involved time-based documentation for 99407-labeled notes. Some of these notes, reimbursed for intensive counseling (CPT 99407), qualified through brief statements like “greater than 10 minutes spent on smoking cessation” (see appendix 3) but lacked the granular clinical descriptors emphasized in synthetic training. In practice, clinicians may simply document time without specific counseling elements, and the note still qualifies for 99407 reimbursement under time-based billing. Due to the relatively low value of add-on CPT codes,31 reimbursement can still ensue despite lacking the full recommended clinical documentation content guidelines.7 This divergence between minimal billing-compliant documentation and ML-recognizable features highlights a systemic training obstacle in documentation culture—a divergence that emphasizes the misalignment of billing requirements and data-driven public health specificity. This is likely why CigStopper’s billing prediction thresholds on the 99407 holdout set (real-world test) were poor, classifying most as 99406 rather than 99407 (Figure 3b). Consequently, 99407 prediction performance in our study likely reflects both class imbalance and clinical documentation gaps, not necessarily model failure.

Second, classification architecture also influences model performance. Hierarchical (cascading) models outperformed flat multiclass configurations in eligibility detection and both billing code tests. The stepwise structure, binarily dividing eligibility versus non-eligibility, poses less statistical risk for false negatives and true positives. The first “sensitive” model (billing eligibility) filters only likely eligible notes to the second “specific” model (CPT prediction). This is apparent in the comparatively higher performance of the hierarchical model (Table 3). However, multiclass models offer simplified deployment and may still be appropriate for use cases prioritizing technical efficiency over marginal gains in accuracy. Thus, either methodology provides a foundation for broadening automated billing research.

Overall, CigStopper demonstrates significant potential for improving billing accuracy and revenue recovery in tobacco cessation counseling. The literature supports two key points: (1) the relevance of CPT codes (99406 and 99407) and their alignment with improved patient outcomes32 and (2) the lack of CPT coding is a data quality issue that underrepresents the true number of smokers in the USA, creating a barrier to care and negatively impacting patient outcomes.33 CigStopper’s functionality targets these points respectively by increasing clinician-prompted quit attempts and automating and then verifying cessation services that are documented but not billed. Regarding the health impact focus, CigStopper’s metrics indicate strong performance on real-world data for eligibility prediction. Effectiveness in distinguishing between note classes is particularly important for eligible and non-eligible encounters, in which the model excelled in. The nuances between moderate counseling (99406) and intensive counseling (99407) highlight the need to retain physicians “in the loop,” and our future studies aim to preserve this aspect (discussed below). Nonetheless, with proper training volume, annotated data, and prediction thresholds, CigStopper’s real-world potential to reduce clinician’s administrative burden and enhance health system’s financial sustainability is high.34 Future CigStopper versions could also incorporate transformer-based language models as compute-efficient variants become more practical for real-time clinical deployment.

From a health system perspective, CigStopper addresses both revenue and care quality gaps. Tobacco cessation interventions are a recognized standard of care, yet recent studies reveal that 2% of potentially eligible visits are billed for cessation counseling.11 Our team replicated these studies at VUMC and found similar patterns of potentially eligible, unbilled encounters, representing both under-captured clinical work and missed revenue.12 These unbilled encounters suggest that either the cessation treatment or counseling was neglected, or the clinical documentation contains insufficient content (including coding/billing) that fails to qualify for billing claims submission. Beyond the loss of revenue, underreporting may conceal valid clinical effort–thereby impairing the accuracy of public health cessation services data. In other words, without billing and coding integrity, it is impossible to discern whether the care occurred but was unrecorded or if the recommended care is not being provided when it should be.

Although CPT codes for tobacco-cessation counseling do not directly affect patient outcomes, accurate documentation and billing create the operational foundation for delivering evidence-based cessation.35 Inconsistent documentation and coding of cessation treatment hinder timely counseling and resource allocation to cessation programs. This data is crucial for health systems to identify opportunities and gaps in guideline care.

Ultimately, real-time billing automation can establish a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop for patients and their clinicians. By validating the clinical care delivered with CPT codes, qualifying revenue can be reinvested into more effective smoking cessation interventions, affirming the important work of clinicians. When tobacco-treatment encounters are consistently coded, health systems can (1) leverage EHR data to gain visibility on treatment delivery patterns to (2) promote cessation rates, and (3) improve their financial capacity to reinvest revenue into expanding intervention infrastructure.36 Without this revenue stream, expanding or even maintaining the operationalization of identifying and treating every tobacco user at every visit is a significant barrier.37

Future studies are underway, including a randomized pilot to measure the tool’s impact on reimbursement rates and patient outcomes. With new IRBs enhancing analysis capabilities, additional recapture analysis on unbilled encounters will further showcase CigStopper’s value as a data quality intervention. Our goal is to support clinicians in retaining control over final billing decisions (e.g, ensuring that erroneous prompts can be unobtrusively overridden). A real-time clinical pilot will enable the quantification of changes in reimbursement rates and patient outcomes, allowing for direct comparison of claim submission, net revenue, and establishing causal insights into the role of AI-assisted clinical decision support in increasing smoking cessation rates.

4.1. Limitations

First, due to IRB constraints, we could not annotate the VUMC_SD notes for detailed supervised learning and thus were reliant on unsupervised training (unlike our prior work demonstrating high performance on labeled synthetic notes18). Second, we had insufficient 99407 note volume to properly train this note class, causing low performance as discussed. Third, we could not perform a recapture analysis—potential billing opportunities on notes containing qualifying ALA billing language—due to pending IRB access for VUMC published notes. Fourth, the single health system (VUMC) and synthetic data limits generalizability; future studies should incorporate multiple institutional sources of data.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of a scalable machine-learning solution for automating tobacco cessation billing that addresses gaps in data quality, care delivery visibility, and revenue capture. High F1-scores in billing eligibility classification support the use of blended datasets during training to mitigate class imbalance and improve model generalization. While nuanced code prediction for intensive counseling (CPT 99407) remains a limitation, this reflects systemic billing practices rather than model failure. By aligning NLP-driven billing validation with CPT billing and documentation guidelines, CigStopper has the potential to enhance claims data integrity and support the validation of preventive care. Future randomized trials will evaluate the tool’s prospective effects on reimbursement and clinical outcomes to inform billing applications in other value-based care contexts.
