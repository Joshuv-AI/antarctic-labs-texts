Antimicrobial stewardship in UK special care baby units: gaps in research and future directions
Authors: Amedine Duret, Frances Davies, Felicity Fitzgerald, Mark Gilchrist, Charlene Rodrigues, Jayanta Banerjee, Vijaykumar Shivamurthappa, Aisleen Bennett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite a significant decline in the incidence of neonatal sepsis, the clinical imperative to avoid missing life-threatening infection continues to drive widespread antibiotic overprescription. Antimicrobial stewardship is universally recognized and explicitly mandated within the UK 2024–2029 National Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance as a critical mechanism to combat resistance and prevent iatrogenic harms related to infant dysbiosis. However, a major misalignment exists within the current evidence base: most of the neonatal stewardship research is conducted in highly resourced, tertiary Neonatal Intensive Care Units (Level 3), even though most postnatal inpatient care occurs in Special Care Baby Units (Level 1) and Local Neonatal Units (Level 2). This Viewpoint explores the operational and structural barriers that limit the transferability of intensive-care stewardship models to lower-acuity settings, identifies key research gaps around baseline prescribing practices and multidisciplinary collaboration, and argues for the inclusion of Level 1 and 2 populations in future validation studies of emerging diagnostic tools for neonatal sepsis.

Towards evidence-based neonatal antimicrobial stewardship

Rates of neonatal sepsis have been declining in the UK over the past 20 years,1,2 yet the fear of missing a case remains high, fostering widespread antimicrobial overprescription: in high-resource neonatal units, 58 neonates receive antibiotics for every one case of culture-confirmed sepsis.3,4 Infection and neonatal healthcare professionals are thus focused on achieving appropriate use of antimicrobials in neonates in the context of increasing antimicrobial resistance (AMR),3 the impact of antibiotic use on neonates’ microbiomes5 and complications possibly associated with early prolonged antibiotic exposure such as necrotizing enterocolitis or growth faltering.6 Neonatal antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programmes could be a solution to optimize antimicrobial use within this population. At a system level within the UK National Health Service, the 2024–2029 UK National Action Plan establishes AMS as a central priority for healthcare delivery, recognizing its critical role in tackling the growing threat of AMR.7 Recent NICE guidance recommending switching from intravenous to oral antibiotic use in suspected early-onset neonatal sepsis illustrates how this priority is being translated into clinical practice.8 Preterm neonates are a small but unique patient population due to their vulnerability to severe bacterial infections and prolonged hospital admissions. Aggregated AMS evaluation on a hospital-level does not always accurately reflect the impact of an intervention on this patient group.9

Research on AMS interventions in neonatal units has expanded within the last few decades,4,10 and several systematic reviews have been published since 2020.4,9–11 Individual interventions studied range from (i) clinical decision support tools for initiation of antibiotics (Kaiser-Permanente calculator,12,13 48-hour observation of clinically healthy neonates with risk factors for early-onset sepsis),14 (ii) guidance for cessation of antimicrobials (stop orders,15,16 C-reactive protein17 or procalcitonin18,19 guidance), and (iii) clinical AMS rounds20 which incorporate education and training.20–22 Most studies demonstrate a reduction in antibiotic initiation and/or duration after AMS programme implementation,4,9,10 and reassuringly, no increased morbidity or mortality signal has emerged from the minority of studies powered for safety endpoints.19,23,24 Systematic reviews of neonatal AMS intervention also showed that the design and quality of individual studies skewed towards small, single-centre retrospective studies with significant risks of bias, which may help explain why large-scale recommendations of specific AMS interventions have not followed from this increased reporting.4,9–11 Although most studies in the reviews originated from the global North, India and China, high-quality multi-centre studies of AMS interventions in the neonatal population are also being carried out in sub-Saharan Africa (NeoAMS in South Africa25 and Neotree in Zimbabwe and Malawi).26 Of note, no UK studies featured in any of these reviews.

One key difficulty in achieving effective AMS in neonates is the lack of high-accuracy diagnostics for neonatal infections. Traditional biomarkers such as C-reactive protein and procalcitonin are less reliable in this population,27 and low blood culture volumes pose a challenge in confirming bacteraemia episodes.28 Furthermore, the signs and symptoms of both early- and late-onset sepsis are often non-specific, and as a neonate’s physiological status can deteriorate rapidly with potentially fatal consequences, clinicians often prescribe early, fixed courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics. This reflects the limitations of current diagnostic and prognostic tools, which remain insufficiently precise to reliably guide decisions on when to initiate or discontinue treatment. Better diagnostics would allow for tighter adherence to the ‘Start Smart then Focus’ model of antimicrobial prescribing.29 Research into this includes leveraging machine-learning to utilize routinely collected clinical parameters to model the risk of early- or late-onset sepsis and guide clinicians’ decisions,30–34 or translating novel biomarkers onto diagnostic tests, for example host-based RNA signatures of neonatal sepsis.35,36

Where do special care baby units fit into the current research landscape?

One striking observation in the literature on neonatal AMS is the mismatch between the populations studied and the settings where most neonatal care occurs. In the UK, neonatal postnatal care is organized into a tiered system: Level 1 Special Care Units provide basic support primarily for term infants, Level 2 Local Neonatal Units offer high-dependency care for term or preterm infants, and Level 3 Neonatal Intensive Care Units deliver intensive care for critically ill or very/extremely premature infants, who typically have lower median birthweights and gestational ages.37 Although the majority of units are Level 1 or 2 (113 units at these levels compared with 43 Level 3 units in England), research into neonatal AMS is conducted disproportionately in Level 3 settings.9,10 One systematic review found that only 20% of studies on neonatal AMS included any non-NICU patients.10 It is true that Level 3 units face a unique AMS challenge: because of their patients’ high vulnerability and long stays, antibiotic use in these settings is substantial, with neonates born before 32 weeks’ gestation receiving a median of 7 days of antibiotics during their admission.38 A recent national survey of UK practice revealed marked variation in the choice of empirical antibiotics for late-onset sepsis in Level 3 units,39 echoing findings from almost a decade ago in Australia and New Zealand.40 This highlights the ongoing need for greater guideline harmonization and stronger evidence to support neonatal intensive care units in safely using narrow and broader-spectrum regimens to suit their population risk.

The imbalance between most neonates being cared for in Level 1 and 2 units and the research emphasis on Level 3 units suggests that important, context-specific opportunities for AMS in lower-acuity settings may be under-recognized and underexplored. Although AMS should be embedded in routine care, its prioritization may be stronger in neonatal intensive care units in tertiary centres with close links to paediatric infection teams (infectious diseases, microbiology, infection pharmacy services). In contrast, smaller Level 1 or 2 units may have less consistent access to such expertise and face competing service priorities, which can limit the emphasis placed on stewardship. Crucially, meeting the National Action Plan’s statutory goals of a 5% reduction in antibiotic use and no rise in Gram-negative bloodstream infections by 20297 will depend on actively engaging healthcare providers with the highest patient volumes in antimicrobial stewardship (AMS). This, in turn, requires extending AMS efforts beyond Level 3 units to also include lower-acuity units.

Research directions

Given the limited volume of UK-based research into neonatal antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) interventions and the underrepresentation of Level 1 and 2 units within this evidence base, we have identified several priority questions to be addressed in future research.
