Synergistic effects of outdoor nighttime light, air pollution, and PM2.5 components on multimorbidity risk of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and liver diseases: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yongbin Wang, Yifang Liang, Ziyue Liang, Siyu Qing, Renfang Zhang, Chunjie Xu, Fei Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension, dyslipidemia, and liver diseases often coexist as multimorbidity, yet its assocaition with environmental drivers like nighttime light (NL) and air pollution remain underexplored. This study investigates the independent and joint effects of NL, air pollutants, and PM2.5 components on multimorbidity risk.MethodsBased on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2015–2020), we analyzed 11,216 adults aged 45 years and older who did not have multiple chronic conditions at baseline. We adjusted the covariates, including age, gender, education level, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, and sleep state. We employed Cox models that incorporated various combinations of these covariates to estimate the hazard ratios (HR). The exposure variables included NL, sulfur dioxide (SO₂), carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O₃), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and particulate matter (PM₁, PM2.5, PM₁₀), along with components of PM2.5 (black carbon [BC], organic matter [OM], nitrate [NO₃⁻], sulfate [SO42-], and ammonium [NH4+]). Dose-response relationships and synergistic effects were quantified using restricted cubic spline functions and interaction analyses.ResultsDuring a median follow-up period of five years, 1,504 participants (13.4%) developed multiple conditions. A significant dose-response relationship (P < 0.05) was observed between the risks of multiple diseases and NL, as well as several air pollutants, including PM1, PM2.5, PM10, NO₂, SO₂, CO and components of PM2.5 (BC, OM, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, and NH₄⁺), with the exception of O₃. The relative excess risk in interaction (RERI) for NL and PM₁ was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.01–0.46), for PM2.5 it was 0.26 (95% CI: 0.03–0.48), for O₃ it was 0.35 (95% CI: 0.11–0.58), and for ammonium (NH₄⁺) it was 0.25 (95% CI: 0.03–0.47), indicating synergistic risk. The most pronounced interactions were observed between NL and BC (HR= 1.85, 95% CI: 1.57–2.18) as well as NO₂ (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.39–1.93). Concurrent exposure to high levels of NL and moderate levels of PM₂.₅ increased the risk by 61% (HR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.29–1.99), while high levels of NL combined with black carbon resulted in an 85% increase in risk (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.57–2.18).ConclusionsNL and air pollutants independently and synergistically elevate multimorbidity risk, with PM2.5 components (e.g., BC, NO3-) and O₃ exhibiting pronounced interactions. Policy interventions targeting light pollution reduction and air quality improvement are urgently needed to mitigate environmental health risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25157-y.

Introduction

Hypertension, dyslipidemia, and chronic liver diseases represent major global health challenges, contributing to escalating morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burdens [1, 2]. The coexistence of two or more of these conditions—termed multimorbidity—is increasingly prevalent, particularly among aging populations, amplifying risks of complications such as cardiovascular events and metabolic dysfunction [3]. Age-related physiological decline, chronic diseases, and cumulative environmental stressors collectively elevate the risk of multimorbidity in middle-aged and older adults, underscoring the urgency of targeted research for this vulnerable population [4]. Despite growing recognition, preventive strategies remain inadequate, and only a minority of affected individuals receive optimal care.

Hypertension and dyslipidemia share pathophysiological pathways, including endothelial dysfunction and systemic inflammation, which synergistically elevate cardiovascular risks [1]. Concurrently, chronic liver diseases exacerbate metabolic dysregulation through mechanisms such as insulin resistance and lipid metabolism disturbances [5]. The interplay between these conditions underscores the need to identify modifiable environmental drivers.

Nighttime light (NL) has been identified as an emerging environmental risk factor in recent years. Accumulated evidence implicates disrupted circadian rhythms—often caused by artificial NL—as a critical factor in metabolic and cardiovascular disorders [6]. NL suppresses melatonin secretion, impairing sleep regulation, glucose homeostasis, and antioxidative defense, while also promoting hepatic steatosis and inflammation [7, 8]. Furthermore, air pollutants, including fine particulate matter (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and ozone (O3), induce oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and endothelial damage, pathways that may converge to accelerate multimorbidity [9, 10]. Notably, PM2.5 components such as black carbon (BC), sulfate (SO42-), and nitrate (NO3-) exhibit distinct toxicities, yet their combined effects with NL remain uncharacterized [11], This study encompasses a vast area of China, extending from the eastern coast to the central and western inland regions (Figure S1). The variations in urbanization and industrial structures across different regions result in diverse nighttime light distributions, providing a unique setting for investigating the standalone effects of nighttime lights and their interactions with pollutants [12]. In the eastern regions, intense nighttime illumination and active nocturnal activities interact complexly with industrial and transportation pollutants, thereby accelerating the formation of secondary pollutants and altering human exposure patterns. In contrast, the central and western regions exhibit lower nighttime light intensity and quieter nocturnal activities, which interact with domestic and agricultural pollutants, resulting in distinct patterns. To elucidate these effects, this study employs controlled experiments and multivariate models to quantify the contributions of nighttime illumination, pollutants, and their interactions. The analyses account for regional variations and validate the specificity of these interactions. Overall, nighttime light exerts direct effects and engages in complex interactions with pollutants, influencing the study systems through both environmental and human activity dimensions. The broad regional coverage of this study facilitates a comprehensive analysis of these impacts and mechanisms. Future research should focus on representative regions and pollutants to further explore these interactions.

While traditional risk factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol use, sedentary lifestyles) have been extensively studied [13, 14], the role of environmental exposures, particularly NL and air pollution, in driving multimorbidity remains inadequately explored. This knowledge gap hinders the development of holistic prevention strategies. Existing studies predominantly focus on single diseases or isolated exposures. For instance, PM2.5 has been linked to hypertension incidence, while NL correlates with metabolic syndrome. However, real-world exposures involve simultaneous interactions between multiple pollutants and circadian disruptors. Mechanistically, NL may exacerbate pollutant toxicity by impairing immune function or amplifying systemic inflammation, yet such synergies are poorly understood. Additionally, critical questions persist: Do specific PM2.5 components disproportionately contribute to multimorbidity? How do NL and air pollution jointly influence disease progression? Addressing these gaps is vital for designing targeted interventions.

Nighttime light, as an emerging environmental exposure factor, has only recently garnered attention in research—primarily over the past decade. Early studies predominantly focused on the associations between individual environmental factors, such as air pollutants, and health outcomes. Furthermore, assessing the combined exposure to nighttime light and air pollution necessitates the integration of multi-source data, which includes remote sensing imagery (nighttime light), environmental monitoring (pollutants), and population cohort data (individual health). The technical barriers to data acquisition and spatial matching are significant, and discrepancies in the spatiotemporal resolution of different datasets can easily lead to exposure misclassification, thereby complicating the research.

This study addresses the existing gap in the literature through three primary contributions: (1) We utilize nighttime light data from Defense Meteorological Satellite Program Operational Linescan System (DMSP-OLS)/Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (NPP-VIIRS) and air pollutant data from Tracking Air Pollution in China (TAP)/China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) by aligning the spatiotemporal resolution at the prefecture-level cities as the administrative unit. The nighttime light and pollutant data are averaged over the two years preceding the follow-up, which minimizes data matching errors; (2) We employ Cox models to analyze the independent effects separately and combine likelihood ratio tests with relative excess risk interactions (RERI) and attributable proportions (AP) indicators to evaluate interactive effects. Additionally, we illustrate the combined effects through a 3 × 3 exposure surface map, systematically revealing their synergistic interactions; (3) By utilizing prospective data from the CHARLS cohort of middle-aged and elderly adults, we circumvent the limitations associated with causal inference in cross-sectional studies, thereby providing robust evidence for the association between combined exposure and the risk of multimorbidity.

We hypothesize that (1) NL, air pollutants, and PM2.5 components independently elevate multimorbidity risk; (2) specific PM2.5 components, such as NH4+ and NO3-, are key drivers of risk; and (3) their co-exposure induces additive or multiplicative interactions, magnifying health impacts. By employing Cox models, restricted cubic splines, and interaction analyses, we quantify dose-response relationships and identify high-risk exposure combinations. Our findings hold significant public health implications. Urbanization and industrialization in China have intensified NL and air pollution, yet policies often address these exposures in isolation. This study provides evidence to advocate for integrated regulations targeting light pollution reduction, air quality improvement, and PM2.5 component control. Furthermore, it highlights the necessity of circadian rhythm preservation in chronic disease management, offering a framework for global contexts facing similar environmental challenges. By elucidating the interplay between multifactorial exposures and multimorbidity, this work advances the paradigm from single-disease models to a holistic understanding of environmental health risks.

Methods

Study population

The study population were derived from the CHARLS [15], which was initiated in 2011, conducted across five follow-up waves, and concluded in 2020. The cohort included approximately 17,000 participants in 2011, with additional participants selected in subsequent waves. The study collected data on health, medical information, occupation, elder support, and economic status, etc. All data from this study are publicly available at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/.

Our analyses utilized three waves of CHARLS data (2015, 2018 and 2020), encompassing 11,216 eligible participants, with 2015 designated as the baseline. We established the following exclusion criteria: participants younger than 45 years, missing age data, incomplete follow-up, absence dates of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and liver disease (excluding fatty liver, liver tumor or cancer), and the presence of multimorbidity at baseline (Figure S2). The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of NL, air pollutants, and PM2.5 components

A portion of the data NL data comes from the DMSP-OLS, which has been accumulating NL data over a long period of time, which can be used to analyse anthropogenic changes in the Earth’s surface over time [16]. Long-term trends in human activity on the Earth’s surface, however this data is no longer publicly available after 2013. Another portion of the nighttime light data comes from the NPP-VIIRS, which refers to the Visible Light The Suomi NPP satellite was successfully launched on 28 October 2011 and operates in a sun-synchronous orbit, providing a stable observation platform for the VIIRS, the data provided are highly accurate and can provide accurate information for scientific research and practical applications, and the satellite began collecting nighttime light data in 2012 until today [16]. The imagery obtained from DMSP-OLS features a spatial resolution nearing 1 km, with pixel values expressed through grayscale intensity levels ranging between 0 and 63. In contrast, the NPP-VIIRS imagery, which boasts a spatial resolution of around 500 m, is capable of detecting subtle reflective signals emanating from the Earth’s surface. To ensure consistency in image formats, this research conducted a DN value correlation analysis on shared regions between the two datasets from the years 2012 and 2013, allowing for the calibration of NPP-VIIRS imagery to align with the DMSP-OLS format. As a result of this data processing sequence, a comprehensive long-term series of nighttime light imagery for the study area spanning from 1992 to 2023 was created, and the average DN value of nighttime light exposure was determined for each participant’s city of residence, focusing on prefecture-level administrative divisions.

Air pollution exposures were from two datasets: TAP and CHAP (accessible at http://tapdata.org.cn). Air pollution exposures include PM1, PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, CO, and PM2.5 components (including black carbon [BC], ammonia [NH4+], organics [OM], sulphate [SO42−], nitrate [NO3−]). The pollution levels were predicted using a sophisticated methodology based on space-time extremely randomized trees models. These models integrated a comprehensive set of predictors, including meteorological variables, land use characteristics, pollutant emissions data, population density, and other relevant spatial and temporal factors [17–23]. Daily estimates were generated at a fine spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km for PM1, PM2.5, PM10, and O3, and at a coarser resolution of 10 km × 10 km for NO2, SO2, and CO. The resulting datasets demonstrated high accuracy, with cross-validation root mean square errors ranging from 0.80 to 0.92. To protect participant confidentiality, locations in the CHARLS database were geocoded only to the prefecture-level city. Therefore, air pollution exposure was averaged at the prefecture-level city to align with the survey data, meaning all participants residing in the same prefecture-level city were assigned the same air pollution exposure value. Following established practices in the literature [24], we calculated the annual average pollutants levels prior to the baseline survey as an indicator of exposure.

Distribution of NL and pollutants and geographical exposure maps for NL and various air pollutants are shown in Figure S3 and Figure S4. In this study, the year 2015 was established as the baseline year. The emergence of chronic diseases, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and liver diseases, is primarily attributed to long-term cumulative environmental exposure, rather than being directly induced by short-term exposure. The average exposure levels from 2013 to 2014 are indicative of the recent stable pre-baseline exposure levels. By designating 2015 as the baseline year, this study effectively mitigates random fluctuations that may be associated with single-year exposure data and encompasses the critical exposure window prior to the onset of disease.

Covariates

The selection of covariates was based on potential confounders associated with NL, air pollutants, PM2.5 components and multimorbidity [25–27]. This process generally included sociodemographic characteristics, as well as lifestyle and behavioral factors. Data were collected through interviews utilizing standardized questionnaires that captured socio-demographic information, including age (categorized as middle-aged, age < 60, and older, age ≥ 60), Gender (male and female), marital status (married or partnered versus other marital statuses), and educational attainment (less than high school, high school, university, or above). Lifestyle and behavioral factors were documented through the interviews and included smoking status (categorized as non-smoker and smoker), alcohol use (categorized as non-drinker and drinker), and sleep patterns, which were classified into four categories based on frequency: little or no sleep (< 1 day), some or little sleep (1–2 days), occasional or moderate sleep (3–4 days), and most (or all) sleep (5–7 days).

Outcome

According to the diagnostic criteria for hypertension established by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Society of Hypertension (ISH) [28, 29], hypertension is diagnosed when blood pressure is measured three times on non-consecutive days without the use of antihypertensive medication, with systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Alternatively, patients with a previous history of hypertension who are currently on antihypertensive medication should be diagnosed with hypertension even if their blood pressure is less than 140/90 mmHg. When systolic and diastolic blood pressures fall into different categories, the diagnosis should be based on the higher category.

According to the Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management (2023) [30], the diagnostic criteria for dyslipidemia in adults are as follows: total cholesterol (TC) levels of ≥ 6.2 mmol/L; low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels of ≥ 4.1 mmol/L; triglycerides (TG) levels of ≥ 2.3 mmol/L; and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels of < 1.0 mmol/L. Dyslipidemia is diagnosed when at least one of the aforementioned fasting venous plasma test criteria is met.

The WHO classifies chronic liver diseases in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th edition (ICD-11), This classification includes chronic liver disease associated with viral hepatitis, autoimmune chronic liver disease, metabolic chronic liver disease, and chronic liver disease due to inherited metabolic disorders, among others.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or as percentages. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, while continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. We employed Cox regression for variable selection and assessed multicollinearity by evaluating the variance inflation factors (VIF). A VIF value exceeding 10 generally indicated severe multicollinearity among variables, prompting us to consider model optimization for those variables accordingly (Table S1). In constructing a Cox model for RCS mapping, we adjusted for covariates such as age, gender, marital status, educational attainment, smoking status, drinking status, and sleep state to control for potential confounding factors. Simultaneously, we incorporated air pollutant variables that exhibited no multicollinearity with the core study factors (VIF<10). This approach ensured that no severe linear confounding existed among the pollutant variables or between the pollutants and core variables. Subsequently, Cox models were constructed specifically for each core pollutant to prevent overcorrection (i.e., excessive adjustment) that could compromise the authenticity and accuracy of the findings. This further mitigated the confounding effects of these environmental factors on the study conclusions, thereby enabling a more precise exploration of the relationships among the core variables.

In this study, we employed the Cox model to evaluate the associations between NL, pollutants, PM2.5 components and the risk of multimorbidity. Utilizing this model, we calculated the corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to quantify the relative risk associated with these exposures and multimorbidity. To ensure the model’s validity and applicability, we rigorously tested the proportional hazards assumption. Through the weighted Schönfeld residual test (all P > 0.05), indicating that the risk ratios between the exposures and the risk of multimorbidity remained constant throughout the follow-up period [31]. Then, we constructed both single-exposure and multiple-exposure models based on existing research [32, 33]. Three stepwise-adjusted Cox models were constructed: Model 1 did not include any covariates; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, marital status, and education level; and Model 3 further controlled for smoking, alcohol consumption, and sleep status.

We employed a restricted cubic spline (RCS) approach to investigate the dose-response relationship between multiple exposures and the risk of multimorbidity. In constructing the RCS-Cox model, we utilized the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to establish curve nodes at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles [34]. This approach not only accommodated the non-linear effects of the target variables but also adjusted for other covariates such as age, gender, and smoking status.

To evaluate the combined effects of NL and air pollutants, we will analyze this issue from multiple perspectives. Our investigation will focus on the multi-factorial interaction effects between these substances. Interaction effects denote the non-independent influences that arise from the interactions of variables, encompassing a broad concept that includes specific forms such as multiplicative and additive effects [35], The interaction between multiplicative and additive effects elucidates the nature of effect superposition from a statistical modeling perspective. The multiplicative model emphasizes the amplification or attenuation of effects, whereas the additive model focuses on the accumulation of effects. The distinction between ‘multiplicative and additive interactions’ pertains to statistical modeling, clarifying how effects are aggregated; multiplicative models emphasize the amplification or attenuation of effects, while additive models focus on the accumulation of effects. The combined effects underscore the overall impact of co-exposure to multiple variables, offering an interpretation that aligns more closely with real-world exposure scenarios. To evaluate the potential interactive effects of NL and air pollutants (including PM2.5 components) on the risk of multimorbidity, we categorized exposure levels into high and low groups based on the median, Using the low-level air pollutant group and the NL group as reference categories, we calculated and reported HRs along with their 95% CIs for various combinations of exposure levels. Furthermore, we assessed the presence of multiplicative interactions by comparing models with and without interaction terms through likelihood ratio tests. For the analysis of additive interactions, we computed RERI and AP [36, 37]. These analyses deepen our understanding of the interactions between different exposure factors and their implications for the risk of multimorbidity.

In addition, we applied multivariable Cox regression to assess the joint effects of NL exposure and air pollutant concentrations. Joint effects refer to the combined influence exerted by two or more variables (or factors) when they coexist and interact simultaneously on research outcomes, such as health status, ecological changes, and social phenomena. This concept emphasizes the examination of overall outcomes in scenarios where multiple factors coexist, rather than merely summing the effects of individual variables. We categorized both NL and air pollutant concentrations into three classes: low, medium and high. Using the combination of low NL exposure and low air pollutant concentrations as the reference group, we further investigated the joint effect of these two factors on the risk of multimorbidity.

To assess the robustness of the model results, we conducted two sensitivity analyses by varying the number and location of nodes in the RCS model and fitted the Cox model using NL, air pollutants, and PM2.5 components as continuous variables, with adjustment for confounding factors. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). The Cox model was constructed utilizing the ‘cph()’ function from the ‘rms’ package (version 7.7.0). Additionally, the ‘rcs()’ function from the same package was employed to generate the RCS curves, while visualizations were created using the ‘ggplot2’ package (version 3.5.1).

Participants with multimorbidity demonstrated systematically higher exposure levels to air pollutants, including PM1​, PM2.5​, PM10​, SO2, NO2​, CO, BC, NH4+​, OM, SO42−​, and NO3−​, compared to their non-multimorbidity counterparts. Furthermore, individuals with multimorbidity were characterized by lower educational attainment and shorter self-reported sleep duration.

Independent effects of NL, air pollution, and PM2.5 components on multimorbidity

In this study, the dose-response relationship between NL, air pollutants (excluding O₃), and PM2.5 components and the risk of multiple coexisting diseases was found to be significant (Table S2, Fig. 1 and Figure S5). Specifically, for each interquartile range (IQR) increase in NL, air pollutants (excluding O₃), and PM2.5 components, the risk of multiple coexisting conditions increased by 7% to 23%. Notably, PM₁₀ exhibited the strongest association with the risk of multiple coexisting diseases among all pollutants and PM2.5 components, with the hazard ratio for NO3- exceeding 1.20 per IQR increase. Within the studied exposure range, fine particulate matter (PM₁, PM2.5), CO, NH4+, NO3-, and NL displayed linear dose-response trends with the risk of multiple coexisting diseases, indicating a monotonically increasing risk as exposure levels rose. In contrast, coarse particulate matter (PM₁₀), O₃, SO₂, OM, SO42-, and BC showed non-linear associations with the risk of multiple coexisting diseases, which may reflect threshold or saturation effects (Fig. 2).

Interactive effects of NL, air pollution, and PM2.5 components on multimorbidity

As shown in (Table S3, Fig. 3) interaction analyses revealed significant additive and multiplicative interactions between NL and specific air pollutants or PM2.5 components. Notably, additive interactions were observed for NL combined with PM₁ (RERI = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.01–0.46), PM2.5 (RERI = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.03–0.48), O₃ (RERI = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.11–0.58), and NH4+ (RERI = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.03–0.47), indicating synergistic effects exceeding individual risks. Multiplicative interactions were prominent for NL with NO₂ (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.39–1.93), SO2 (HR = 1.20, 95% CI:1.14–1.25), O₃ (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.15–1.59), and BC (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.57–2.18). For instance, co-exposure to high NL and PM₁ increased multimorbidity risk by 72% (HR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.46–2.03), with 14% of this risk attributable to their interaction (AP = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.02–0.26). Similarly, the combination of high NL and BC demonstrated the strongest multiplicative effect (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.57–2.18), highlighting the amplified risk from joint NL and specific pollutants or PM2.5 components.

Joint effects of NL, air pollution, and PM2.5 components on multimorbidity

Joint exposure analyses (Table S4, Fig. 4) revealed notable patterns in the associations between night light (NL) and air pollutants concerning multimorbidity risk. Several combinations exhibited significant elevations in risk, particularly those involving PM2.5 and its components. For instance, high NL combined with medium PM2.5 resulted in a substantial hazard ratio (HR) of 1.61 (95% CI: 1.29–1.99), whereas medium NL with high PM2.5 produced an HR of 1.60 (95% CI: 1.31–1.95). Additionally, combinations of medium NL with elevated levels of PM2.5 components such as NH4+ (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.13–1.66), NO3- (HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.19–1.71), BC (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.04–1.48), OM (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.07–1.55), and SO42- (HR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.10–1.60) exhibited significant increases in risk. These findings suggest that the interplay between NL and PM2.5, along with its components, may have a pronounced impact on multimorbidity risk.

Discussion

This study investigates the relationship between NL, air pollutants, PM2.5 components and the multimorbidity risk of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and liver diseases. Our findings reveal a significant dose-response relationship between NL and the risk of multimorbidity, with a HR of 1.06 (95% CI: 1.01–1.12) per IQR increase. This outcome aligns with prior research on the health impacts of NL exposure, yet further reveals a robust association between NL and multiple coexisting conditions. Previous studies have indicated that elevated levels of NL may disrupt the body’s normal circadian rhythms and impact the homeostasis of endocrine and metabolic systems [8]. NL inhibits melatonin secretion, which is essential for sleep regulation and involved in various physiological processes [38]. It has been well documented that melatonin exerts a protective effect on the liver by remodeling gut microbiota and activating intestinal FXR/FGF-15 axis [39]. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to elevated levels of NL may heighten the risk of liver disease [40]. This may elucidate the close association of NL with multimorbidity observed in this study. We observed that, with the exception of O₃, air pollutants exhibited a dose-response relationship with multiple diseases, increasing the risk of illness by 7% to 23%. This finding is consistent with previous research results, PM2.5, NO2, and BC are all linked to increased all-cause mortality and the risk of cerebrovascular disease. Additionally, BC is specifically associated with both all-cause mortality and cerebrovascular mortality. Notably, even when average PM2.5 exposure levels are below the EU limit of 25 µg/m³, each 5 µg/m³ increase in concentration corresponds to a 12.8% rise in the risk of cerebrovascular disease mortality [41].

Interaction analyses revealed significant additive interactions between NL and air pollutants, including PM1, PM2.5, CO, O3, OM, and NH4+. The risk estimates for RERI ranged from 0.21 to 0.35, while the risk estimates for the AP ranged from 0.13 to 0.26. The RERI value quantifies the difference between the actual multi-risk and the sum of risks from individual exposures. The observed 21% to 35% excess risk indicates the additional harm stemming from these additive interactions, serving as a quantitative indicator of their effects. Furthermore, the AP suggests that 13% to 26% of the overall multimorbidity incidence risk attributable to the combined effects of air pollutants and NL can be exclusively ascribed to this additive interaction. Additionally, multiplicative interactions were observed between NL and NO2, O3, SO2, and BC, with HR ranging from 1.20 to 1.85. These findings suggest that multiple environmental exposures synergistically amplify the risk of multimorbidity. For instance, simultaneous exposure to high levels of NL and PM1 was associated with a 72% increase in multimorbidity risk, with 14% of this increase attributable to the interaction. This indicates that the association between NL and multimorbidity is more pronounced in areas with elevated PM2.5 levels, while the relationship between PM2.5 and multimorbidity is stronger in regions with higher NL levels. Furthermore, Existing studies have shown that there is a complex interaction mechanism between luminous exposure and the effects of air pollutants on depression [42]. On the one hand, noctilucent can directly inhibit melatonin secretion [43], while PM2.5 and O3 air pollutants damage pineal cells through oxidative stress, further aggravating the decline of melatonin levels [44], PM2.5 component-mediated oxidative stress has also been shown to have a strong pathogenic risk [45]. As a key hormone in regulating circadian rhythm, melatonin has both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties and can protect hippocampal neurons by activating the PKM2/NRF2/GPX4 signaling pathway [46], Once its secretion is insufficient, it may lead to dysfunction of serotonergic system, and then induce depressive symptoms [47], this makes it easy to become a potential confounding factor in the study of the association between luminous and depression. In order to accurately analyze the interaction mechanism between luminous exposure and air pollutants on depression, future research needs to rely on high-precision exposure monitoring technology, strictly control confounding factors in research design, and at the same time, combine basic experiments to deeply explore the synergistic damage mechanism at the cellular and molecular levels, thus providing scientific basis for the formulation of intervention strategies. Controlling concentrations of air pollutants such as PM1 and PM2.5 to levels recommended by the WHO, while simultaneously reducing NL to the lowest quartile, yields significant health benefits. For various diseases, incidence rates exhibit marked reductions, which can be partly attributed to the elimination of their additive interactions. Regarding multimorbidity, the incidence risks associated with the interactions between PM1, PM2.5, CO, O3, OM and NH4+, and NL would all decrease. Concurrently, the multiplicative interactions between NL and NO₂, O₃, SO₂, and BC would also diminish the associated incidence risks as exposure levels decrease. Overall, the incidence risks arising from these interactions would decline to a certain extent, potentially leading to a considerable reduction in new cases. The benefits are particularly pronounced in areas of high exposure and among specific age groups.

Joint effect analysis indicated that NL exhibited a significant joint effect with all pollutants (P < 0.05). However, the specific combinations of NL and pollutants concentrations that produced a joint effect varied. For instance, in the low, medium, and high concentration groups, NL demonstrated a joint effect with PM1 (HR from 0.44 to 1.39). In contrast, a significant joint effect (HR from 1.28 to 1.35) was observed between high concentrations of NL and high concentrations of pollutants (PM10, NO2, SO2, O3) when compared to the low concentration group. The combined effect of high NL and elevated levels of pollutants (PM10, NO2, SO2, O3) was significant (HR from 1.28 to 1.35). It is important to note that air pollutants can impair immune cells, thus reducing the functionality of the immune system and potentially leading to the onset of various diseases and health issues [48]. The association of multiple air pollutants with the risk of multimorbidity identified in this study may partly result from the interference of these pollutants with the immune system, subsequently increasing the risk of disease. Furthermore, some researchers have established a link between ambient air pollutants and acute myocardial infarction [49]. The coexistence of multiple diseases often poses a greater danger than the presence of individual diseases. Studies have demonstrated that air pollutants are linked to multimorbidity, contributing to both the onset and progression of diseases, as well as increasing the rate of transition between various diseases [50]. The pollutants examined in this study, including PM2.5 and NO₂, have been shown to contribute to an increased risk of diseases associated with NL-causing illnesses [51]. This finding further underscores the significant role that air pollutants play in the development of chronic diseases and offers additional insights into the understanding of the risk associated with multimorbidity.

NL and air pollutants exhibit a spatial correlation, with urban centers typically experiencing both intense NL and elevated concentrations of air pollution. This phenomenon may arise from shared factors such as transportation emissions and industrial activities—motor vehicle exhaust serves as a source of air pollution, while headlights contribute to night-time illumination. Furthermore, industrial processes not only emit pollutants but also necessitate nocturnal lighting. This association may intensify their combined health impacts: nocturnal illumination disrupts melatonin secretion, potentially weakening the body’s antioxidant defenses and exacerbating oxidative stress damage induced by air pollutants. Conversely, respiratory inflammation caused by air pollution may impair the body’s adaptive response to light stimulation, indicating that the health risks from combined exposure are not merely additive. Clarifying the patterns of association between these factors will facilitate the development of coordinated prevention and control strategies. For instance, simultaneously optimizing lighting schemes and emission reduction measures in areas with high pollution and elevated light exposure could more effectively mitigate the risks of multiple coexisting diseases.

This study is distinctive in its approach. Unlike many studies that concentrate solely on the effects of air pollutants on a singular disease, it examines the risk of multimorbidity while considering the cumulative effects of various environmental exposures. This shift in perspective may arise from differences in study design and focus. The inclusion of NL in this research is justified by its prevalence in contemporary living environments and its potential impact on human physiological rhythms. However, this study also has several limitations. First, ecological exposure assessment at the city level may introduce misclassification bias, as individual-level exposure variations were not captured. Second, this study may be subject to residual confounding factors that influence the results. Although BMI is a known risk factor for metabolic disorders and liver disease, it was not included in the analysis. Furthermore, physical activity levels were not considered; areas with high pollution and intense nighttime illumination may reduce outdoor activity, indirectly exacerbating health burdens. Additionally, critical dietary intake details, such as sodium and trans fatty acid consumption—both directly linked to hypertension and dyslipidemia—were not captured. The impact of blue light from electronic devices in indoor lighting was also overlooked, despite its synergistic interference with melatonin secretion and metabolic regulation alongside outdoor nocturnal illumination. These uncontrolled variables may distort associations with metabolic outcomes, highlighting the necessity of incorporating objective measurement indicators in future research. Third, the use of satellite-derived NL data, while validated, may not fully reflect personal light exposure patterns. Fourth, the CHARLS cohort’s focus on adults aged ≥ 45 years limits generalizability to younger populations. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference, necessitating mechanistic studies to validate biological pathways. Future research should integrate personalized exposure monitoring and longitudinal biomarker data to address these gaps.

Conclusion

This national prospective cohort study demonstrates that NL exposure, ambient air pollutants, and PM2.5 components act both independently and synergistically to elevate the risk of multimorbidity (hypertension, dyslipidemia, and liver disease) and reveal robust dose-response relationships among middle-aged and older adults in China. The findings underscore the need for integrated public health policies targeting environmental hazards. Prioritizing light pollution reduction, stringent air quality regulations, and science-based strategies to mitigate PM2.5 component emissions (e.g., BC, NO3-) could substantially lower multimorbidity burden in aging populations. However, this study has limitations, including city-level exposure aggregation and unmeasured confounders like dietary patterns. Future research should employ personalized exposure monitoring and mechanistic studies to validate causal pathways. By addressing these gaps, policymakers and researchers can design targeted interventions to curb the dual threats of light pollution and air pollution in rapidly urbanizing societies. Effective measures to mitigate light pollution include the implementation of dimmable smart lighting systems, the use of shielding devices to direct light downward, and the promotion of community awareness campaigns regarding the impacts of light pollution. These strategies not only reduce light pollution but also enhance energy efficiency and lower municipal costs. However, the complete elimination of nighttime illumination may result in unintended consequences. While it may improve wildlife behavior and human health, it could also exacerbate safety concerns in high-crime areas and disrupt the nocturnal routines of individuals who rely on artificial lighting. Therefore, policy formulation must carefully balance the benefits of reducing light pollution with the potential adverse effects of diminishing nighttime illumination.
