Worsening Kidney Function and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Type 2 Diabetes and Atherosclerotic Disease
Authors: Abhinav Sharma, Amir Razaghizad, Susanna R Stevens, Yuliya Lokhnygina, George L Bakris, Jennifer B Green, Darren K McGuire, Renato D Lopes, John B Buse, Paul W Armstrong, Eric D Peterson, Rury R Holman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe bidirectional association between worsening kidney function (WKF) and cardiovascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes (T2D) patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is unclear.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the bidirectional association between: 1) nonfatal cardiovascular events and WKF; and 2) severe hypoglycemia and WKF.MethodsWe analyzed data from TECOS (Trial Evaluating Cardiovascular Outcomes with Sitagliptin), enrolling 14,671 patients with T2D and ASCVD randomized to sitagliptin or placebo. The primary outcome was a 4-component major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE4) comprising nonfatal myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular death, or unstable angina hospitalization. We also examined hospitalization for heart failure (hHF). WKF was defined as a glomerular filtration rate decline of ≥40% (per the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula) or initiation of renal replacement therapy. Cox proportional hazards models assessed associations between WKF and subsequent MACE4 and hHF.ResultsOver a median 3-year follow-up, 1,030 (7.0%) experienced WKF. Overall, WKF risk increased after nonfatal myocardial infarction, stroke, or unstable angina (adjusted HR [HRadj]: 2.0; 95% CI: 1.6-2.5) and was higher after hHF (HRadj: 6.1; 95% CI: 4.8-7.9). Median estimated glomerular filtration rate declines before MACE4 and hHF were −3.8% (IQR: –17.4, 9.4) and −9.2% (IQR: –22.7, 6.6), respectively. WKF was associated with increased risks of MACE4 (HRadj: 1.9; 95% CI: 1.5-2.4), hHF (HRadj: 3.2; 95% CI: 2.2-4.6), and severe hypoglycemia (HRadj: 2.8; 95% CI: 1.8-4.4).ConclusionsIn patients with T2D and ASCVD, WKF is common and linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes and severe hypoglycemia, highlighting the need for early cardiorenal therapies.Key words: cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, type 2 diabetes

Among patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D), chronic kidney disease is a major source of morbidity and premature death.1,2 More than 660,000 patients in the United States require intervention for end-stage kidney disease, with over 500,000 needing dialysis and nearly 200,000 requiring kidney transplantation.3 Along with hypertension, T2D is one of the principal drivers for the development of chronic kidney disease.4,5 Effective management strategies, including tight glycemic control, blood pressure management, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system blockade, have been demonstrated to slow the onset and progression of chronic kidney disease in patients with T2D.6 However, despite these interventions, the prevalence of chronic kidney disease and end-stage kidney disease among patients with T2D has remained unchanged.7

Chronic kidney disease is a strong marker for adverse clinical outcomes, as patients with even mild chronic kidney disease are at increased risk of premature mortality.8 Among patients with T2D and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), the presence of chronic kidney disease as a comorbidity is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular (CV) outcomes and CV-specific mortality.9, 10, 11 While the association between established chronic kidney disease and an increased risk of CV outcomes and mortality has been described, the time-dependent and longitudinal association between worsening kidney function (WKF), as often experienced by patients with T2D throughout their disease trajectory, and CV events has not been extensively explored. The development of WKF complicates the management of patients with T2D who have comorbidities such as heart failure given the need to initiate and titrate therapies that may impact kidney function.12, 13, 14, 15

Hence, the relationship between nonfatal CV outcomes and subsequent WKF warrants further exploration given the high risk of CV outcomes among patients with T2D.16 In addition to CV events, severe hypoglycemic events (SHEs) among patients with T2D are associated with an increased risk of subsequent CV events,17 as well as nonfatal CV events being associated with an increased risk of subsequent SHEs.18 However, a potential association between SHEs and WKF remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to address: 1) the time-dependent association between nonfatal CV outcomes and WKF; and 2) WKF and nonfatal CV events. By modeling these associations bidirectionally, we assessed whether WKF predicts later CV events or SHEs, and whether these events, in turn, predict later WKF. To address this knowledge gap, we used data from TECOS (Trial Evaluating Cardiovascular Outcomes with Sitagliptin).

Methods

Study population

The primary results of the TECOS study have been published previously.19 In brief, TECOS was a double-blind, multinational, placebo-controlled CV safety study evaluating the long-term effects of adding the dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor sitagliptin to usual care in patients with T2D and an established history of ASCVD. The TECOS study randomized 14,671 patients to receive either sitagliptin or placebo in addition to their existing glucose-lowering therapy. Eligible patients had hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) values of 6.5% to 8.0% (48-64 mmol/mol) on treatment with stable doses of 1 or 2 oral glucose-lowering agents (metformin, pioglitazone, or sulfonylurea) or stable treatment with insulin with or without metformin. Eligible patients were ≥50 years of age with T2D and established ASCVD, which included any of the following: history of major coronary artery disease, ischemic cerebrovascular disease, or atherosclerotic peripheral arterial disease. Patients were excluded if they had 2 or more episodes of severe hypoglycemia in the previous year or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 at baseline. The study protocol was approved by the Duke University Health System Institutional Review Board and the ethics committee of the University of Oxford Diabetes Trials Unit, as well as by the ethics committees of all participating TECOS sites. All trial participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. An independent clinical events committee adjudicated endpoint events using standardized definitions. The median follow-up was 3.0 years. Sitagliptin had no clinically significant impact on CV or kidney outcomes, irrespective of baseline eGFR.10

Outcomes of interest

The primary CV outcome was a 4-point major adverse CV event (MACE4) composite of CV death, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), nonfatal stroke, or hospitalization for unstable angina (hUA). We also examined hospitalization for heart failure (hHF) and severe hypoglycemia. WKF was defined as an eGFR decline ≥40%, per previous trials,20, 21, 22, 23 or as the initiation of renal-replacement therapy. SHEs were defined, per protocol, as episodes in which a participant was sufficiently disoriented or incapacitated to require assistance from another individual or medical personnel.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were structured to evaluate bidirectional associations between WKF and CV outcomes, including SHEs, within a time-to-event framework. Only incident events occurring after study baseline were included in time-to-event models, irrespective of prior disease history.

Descriptive analyses

Baseline demographics of patients who did or did not experience WKF are described with median (IQR), mean (SD), or number (percentage). Changes in eGFR from baseline to just prior to a CV outcome or death were assessed, with a median of 6 measurements per patient (IQR: 4-8).

Baseline predictor analyses

Univariable Cox models evaluated associations between each baseline characteristic and development of WKF. Variables demonstrating nonlinear relationships were modeled using piecewise linear splines. In the multivariable model, categorical HbA1c and eGFR were not included because the continuous values were included. Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio was not included because the majority of patients had missing values, but albuminuria, which was included, was correlated and had less missing. Prior vascular disease and coronary artery disease were composite variables, so the components were included instead. Prior percutaneous coronary intervention was not included because it correlated with coronary occlusion. Cerebrovascular disease was included because it was more strongly associated with WKF than were its components stroke, transient ischemic attack, or 50% stenosis in carotid artery. Diastolic blood pressure was not included because of its correlation with systolic blood pressure. Medications were not included in the multivariable model. Subsequent multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for randomized treatment, age, sex, race, ethnicity, duration of T2D, qualifying HbA1c (%), baseline eGFR, prior history of coronary artery disease, prior MI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, carotid stenosis ≥50%, atrial flutter or atrial fibrillation, amputation, diabetic neuropathy, foot ulcers, NYHA heart failure class, systolic and diastolic blood pressures, height, body mass index, heart rate, smoking status, and baseline insulin use.

Evaluation of eGFR changes

For each clinical endpoint (MACE4, CV death, or hHF), the percentage change in eGFR from baseline to the measurement immediately preceding event onset was summarized using medians and IQRs.

Bidirectional time-to-event analyses

Two parallel sets of Cox regression models were constructed to assess the bidirectional associations between WKF and CV events, estimating cause-specific hazards in the presence of competing events. In the first set, incident WKF was modeled as a time-dependent exposure predicting the risk of subsequent CV outcomes (MACE4, hHF) and SHEs. WKF status switched from 0 to 1 at the time of its occurrence, provided the outcome of interest had not yet occurred. In the second set, incident CV events (nonfatal MI or stroke; composite of nonfatal MI, stroke, hUA, or hHF) and SHEs were modeled as time-dependent predictors of subsequent WKF. To adjust for potential differences in baseline hazards, both sets of models were stratified by geographic region and treatment arm and adjusted for the same baseline covariates listed above. Proportional hazards were assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals, a supremum test, and treatment-by-time interactions; no meaningful violations were detected. Medications and adjustment variables highly correlated with other variables were excluded from multivariable models to avoid collinearity.

Joint modeling of eGFR trajectories and cardiovascular outcomes

As another approach to bidirectional analyses, joint modeling was used to analyze longitudinal eGFR data and its association with CV endpoints. This method involves simultaneous fitting of 2 statistical models, combining information from longitudinal and time-to-event data. Repeated measures linear regression mixed modeling was used to model the trajectory of postbaseline eGFR over time as a function of sitagliptin or placebo, adjusting for baseline eGFR. Random effects included random intercepts and slopes. Additionally, for each CV endpoint, a parametric proportional hazards regression model with a piecewise-exponential baseline hazard assessed the association between current eGFR values, eGFR trajectory slopes, and CV events, adjusting for age. Shared parameters linked time-to-event and longitudinal models using current eGFR values and slopes. This approach captured the association between kidney function trajectories and CV outcomes.

Missing data

Missing baseline covariates were imputed via multiple imputation (100 data sets) using SAS PROC MI; results from univariable and multivariable analyses of multiple data sets were consolidated with PROC MIANALYZE.

All analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (www.sas.com). Joint models were fit using the SAS %JM macro (https://www.jm-macro.com/).

Results

Baseline demographics

In TECOS, 1,030 (7.0%) participants developed WKF. At baseline, those who developed WKF compared with those who did not (93.0%, n = 13,641) were older (66.0 vs 65.4 years of age; P < 0.03), had a longer duration of diabetes (11.0 vs 10.0 years; P < 0.05), had lower renal function as assessed by eGFR (73.0 vs 75.0 mL/min/1.73 m2; P < 0.001), and had a slightly higher urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (2.2 vs 1.2 g/mol creatinine; P < 0.001) (Table 1). Regarding medications, participants who developed WKF were more likely to be on an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi)/angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) (81.9% vs 78.5%; P = 0.01), diuretic (51.5% vs 40.2%; P < 0.001), or calcium-channel blocker (36.8% vs 33.6%; P = 0.04). For baseline glucose-lowering therapies, patients who developed WKF were more likely to be on insulin (26.3% vs 23.0%; P = 0.015) (Table 1).

Kidney function prior to cardiovascular events

The median change in eGFR from baseline to the onset of a CV or mortality event varied based on the clinical event (Supplemental Table 1). The median change in eGFR prior to the primary TECOS MACE4 outcome was −3.8% (IQR: −17.4, 9.4). In comparison, the median change in eGFR prior to all-cause death was −7.2% (IQR: −26.0, 10.9); for CV death or hHF, −7.2% (IQR: −21.8, 9.3); and for hHF, −9.2% (IQR: −22.7, 6.6).

Cardiovascular events and SHEs as predictors of WKF

The overall pattern of bidirectional associations between worsening kidney function and cardiovascular outcomes is summarized in the Central Illustration. Adjusted analyses indicated that the risk of subsequent WKF was higher among participants who experienced a nonfatal CV event or hUA (adjusted HR [HRadj]: 2.0; 95% CI: 1.6-2.5), nonfatal CV event (HRadj: 2.0; 95% CI: 1.5-2.6), or hHF (HRadj: 6.1; 95% CI: 4.8-7.9) (Figure 1A, Supplemental Figure 1A). There was also a greater risk of WKF after experiencing an SHE (HRadj: 1.7; 95% CI: 1.1-2.6) (Figure 2B).

WKF as a predictor of cardiovascular events and SHEs

In unadjusted and adjusted analyses, there was an association between WKF and CV and mortality outcomes (Figure 1B, Supplemental Figure 1B). In adjusted analyses, WKF was associated with a higher risk of subsequent MACE4 (HRadj: 1.9; 95% CI: 1.5-2.4), CV death (HRadj: 2.8; 95% CI: 2.1-3.6), and hHF (HRadj: 3.2; 95% CI: 2.2-4.6). After experiencing WKF, the risk of subsequent SHEs was greater in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses (HRadj: 2.8; 95% CI: 1.8-4.4) (Figure 2A, Supplemental Figure 2A).

Longitudinal kidney function and cardiovascular outcomes

The relationship between eGFR and the hazard of event occurrence varied by clinical endpoint (Supplemental Table 2). For MACE4, the rate of eGFR decline per month (ie, slope) showed no significant association with risk (HR: 0.8; 95% CI: 0.4-1.5). For hHF, each unit increase in the rate of eGFR decline per month was associated with a significant increase in the risk of subsequent events (HR: 3.5; 95% CI: 1.9-6.4). Each unit increase in the rate of eGFR decline per month was also associated with increased risk for hHF or CV death (HR: 2.6; 95% CI: 1.7-4.0). Additionally, each unit increase in the rate of eGFR decline per month was associated with higher risks of CV death (HR: 2.9; 95% CI: 1.9-4.4), all-cause death (HR: 2.5; 95% CI: 1.6-3.7), and severe hypoglycemia (HR: 4.4; 95% CI: 2.6-7.6).

Discussion

Kidney function is a strong predictor of adverse outcomes in T2D; however, data specifically addressing the impact of longitudinal WKF on CV outcomes in people with T2D and established ASCVD remain limited. Our post hoc analysis of TECOS provides novel insights by demonstrating two critical bidirectional relationships: first, nonfatal CV events markedly increase the risk of WKF, while in turn, WKF is associated with a significantly elevated risk of subsequent CV events, including hHF and all-cause death. Second, WKF significantly increased the risk of SHEs, and in turn, nonfatal SHEs significantly increased the risk of WKF. We additionally found that a more rapid decline in eGFR strongly increases the risk for hHF, CV mortality, and SHEs. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring kidney function after nonfatal CV events and suggest that therapies such as sodium glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, which were not available during TECOS, could play a crucial role in preventing WKF and reducing associated CV risks following nonfatal CV events.

The association of baseline variables—such as age, eGFR, albuminuria, prior MI, prior stroke, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation—with the risk of future WKF aligns with previous research findings. In a cohort of 25,029 patients in the Italian Association of Diabetologists network of patients with normoalbuminuric T2D and eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2, the variables age, sex, body mass index, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol were most associated with a decline in kidney function.24,25 In the Verona Diabetes Study, among patients with T2D with eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 at baseline, degree of albuminuria at baseline was most associated with a decline in eGFR after 10 years of follow-up.26 Increasing albuminuria, even in the normal range and among patients without T2D, is strongly associated with subsequent declining kidney function and adverse CV outcomes.27, 28, 29, 30

In our study, WKF was associated with a significantly increased risk of subsequent CV events and all-cause death, suggesting that prevention of kidney damage remains an important public health strategy in patients with T2D. The median change in eGFR prior to a nonfatal CV or mortality event suggests that even minor declines in kidney function may identify patients who are at risk of adverse CV outcomes. This is further supported by our joint modeling results which showed that even a one-unit increase in the slope of eGFR decline was associated with a large increased risk of hHF, all-cause death, and CV death. Current clinical practice guidelines recommend tight glycemic and blood pressure control in addition to renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system blockade with ACEi/ARBs to slow the progression of diabetic kidney disease.6,31,32 Yet our results suggest that despite a >80% use of ACEi/ARBs, 7.0% of participants developed WKF within a median follow-up of 3.0 years. Our findings emphasize the need to initiate additional strategies to improve kidney outcomes, including the use of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with T2D who have established CV disease or CV risk factors.

In a meta-analysis33 assessing the impact of 3 SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin,34 canagliflozin,20,35 and dapagliflozin21) and the risk of kidney outcomes among 38,723 randomized patients with T2D at high CV risk, the medications substantially reduced the risk of dialysis, transplantation, or death due to kidney disease (relative risk [RR]: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.52-0.86), end-stage kidney disease (RR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.53-0.81), and acute kidney injury (RR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.66-0.85) compared to placebo. In a similar meta-analysis36 of 7 trials evaluating the impact of the GLP-1 receptor agonists lixisenatide,37 liraglutide,38 semaglutide,39 exenatide,40 albiglutide,41 dulaglutide,42 and oral semaglutide43 on renal outcomes among 56,004 patients with T2D over a median follow-up of 3.2 years, the medications reduced the composite risk of development of new-onset macroalbuminuria, decline in eGFR rate, progression to end-stage kidney disease, or death attributable to kidney causes by 17% (HR: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.78-0.89). This result appeared to be primarily driven by a reduction in the risk of macroalbuminuria development, and the benefit based on decline in eGFR was not significantly reduced (HR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.73-1.03). Despite this evidence of benefit, use of these therapies in clinical practice remains low.44,45 Our results suggest that WKF should be considered a marker for increased risk of subsequent CV and mortality outcomes, and initiation of therapies (such as SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists) that modify this risk should be prioritized among affected patients regardless of prior CV risk.46 Given the high risk of kidney outcomes in T2D, studies should investigate the use of multidisciplinary and synchronous health delivery models to address the interrelated CV, renal, and metabolic risks in this population.47

Our analysis demonstrated that nonfatal CV outcomes are associated with an increased risk of subsequent WKF, highlighting the need for close kidney monitoring after a nonfatal CV outcome. Patients who experience a nonfatal hHF outcome appear to have the largest magnitude of risk for subsequent WKF. Although recurrent CV outcomes are common in the early and late periods following a nonfatal CV outcome among patients with T2D,45 to our knowledge, this analysis is among the first to demonstrate increased risk of WKF following nonfatal CV events in patients with T2D and established ASCVD.

The development of WKF following a nonfatal CV outcome is likely multifactorial, including hemodynamic changes during the CV outcome, investigations requiring the use of nephrotoxic agents, and the titration of therapies that impact kidney function. The development of WKF following a nonfatal CV event clinically complicates the management of patients with T2D. For instance, among heart failure patients, WKF often requires withdrawal of evidence-based renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system blockade.14,48 These results suggest the need to evaluate renoprotective strategies following nonfatal CV events. Although SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce the risk of kidney endpoints in stable patients with T2D and CV disease or CV risk factors, there is limited evidence to support the initiation of these therapies during or shortly after a nonfatal CV event as a renoprotective strategy. As a result, future studies should focus on the integration of newer therapies, such as SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, into treatment protocols for patients with T2D and ASCVD, especially following nonfatal CV events. Given the strong association between WKF, SHEs, and adverse CV outcomes observed in this analysis, prospective randomized trials could assess whether initiating these agents immediately after a CV event can reduce WKF and subsequent CV risk.

Hypoglycemia among patients with T2D is a marker of increased subsequent CV risk.18,49,50 We have demonstrated that the development of WKF was independently associated with subsequent SHEs; furthermore, SHEs were associated with an increased risk of subsequent WKF. This bidirectional association may be related to greater reliance upon glucose-lowering therapies such as insulin or sulfonylureas and the cessation of metformin in the setting of a worsening eGFR.51 However, these findings may alternatively lend support to the “frail phenotype” hypothesis.18 These results highlight the need for vigilant monitoring for hypoglycemia following development of WKF, and perhaps careful monitoring of kidney function in patients who experience an SHE. Additionally, the complex U-shaped relationship between glycated hemoglobin and CV events suggests that targeting an HbA1c of 7.0% may decrease the risk of adverse CV outcomes in high-risk individuals with T2D.52

The joint modeling analysis that was conducted revealed a significant association between the rate of eGFR decline and hHF, all-cause death, and CV death, with no clear association with MACE4. These results are in alignment with existing literature, which has documented a strong association between renal dysfunction, as indicated by albuminuria or eGFR, and an elevated risk of recurrent and incident hHF, particularly in patients with T2D.53 Furthermore, given the relative paucity of high-quality studies establishing a connection between renal function and atherosclerotic processes, the increased rates of all-cause death and CV mortality in subjects with a more rapid eGFR decline could potentially be attributed to the adverse sequelae associated with hHF in T2D. This interpretation lends support to the concept of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Syndrome, recently categorized by the American Heart Association as a multifaceted disorder characterized by CV risk factors, chronic kidney disease, and pre-existing metabolic disturbances, which collectively precipitate an accelerated decline in the health of affected individuals.54 Crucially, these findings highlight the need for implementing multidisciplinary and synchronous health care delivery models to enhance the management of patients with such complex health profiles.47

Strengths and limitations

Our analysis is supported by a large sample size, including nearly 45,000 patient-years of observation and central adjudication of all endpoints using standardized criteria. However, these results are subject to the limitations of post hoc analyses. As with most clinical trials, the TECOS population may not accurately reflect the broader population of patients with T2D, which could limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, sufficient urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio data were unavailable for inclusion, and prior changes in eGFR for those who experienced CV events before the first follow-up measure were unknown. The relatively short follow-up period (median of 3.0 years) further restricts generalizability, as renal function typically declines over many years. While patients with higher baseline eGFR may have greater opportunities for decline, the high-risk population in TECOS provides a robust number of events to evaluate the relationship between WKF, CV events, and mortality in a contemporary patient cohort.

Conclusions

Patients with T2D are at high risk for chronic kidney disease. Our analysis has demonstrated that WKF was associated with both nonfatal CV outcomes and SHEs in a bidirectional manner among patients with T2D with established ASCVD in TECOS. Patients who develop WKF are at higher risk of subsequent CV events and death; conversely, patients who experience a nonfatal CV event are at higher risk of WKF. Patients who experience hHF appear to be at the highest risk for subsequent WKF. Furthermore, WKF appears to be a marker of subsequent SHEs, and SHEs may be predictive of WKF. Our results highlight the need for close kidney function monitoring following a CV outcome. Further research and enhanced implementation are needed to optimize initiation of therapies (such as SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists) among appropriate patients to reduce the risk of CV events and mortality outcomes following the development of WKF. Furthermore, research is needed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of initiating and optimizing renoprotective strategies following a nonfatal CV event.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: WKF and CV events are closely linked, with each condition increasing the risk of the other. This bidirectional relationship highlights shared pathophysiological pathways, including hemodynamic stress and neurohormonal activation.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 1: Our findings suggest that patients with T2D and established CV disease should be monitored for both kidney and CV deterioration after either type of event. Incorporating kidney surveillance into CV follow-up, and vice versa, may improve early detection of patients at heightened risk.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 2: Future research should test whether interventions such as SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists, initiated promptly after kidney or CV events, can disrupt this cycle of reciprocal risk. Evaluating multidisciplinary models of cardio-renal-metabolic care could help translate these observations into improved outcomes in real-world practice.
