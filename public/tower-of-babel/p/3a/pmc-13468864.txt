Experimental and data-driven modeling of multistage freeze desalination for high-salinity reverse osmosis brine treatment
Authors: Amira Nemmour, Aman Al Khatib, Symeon Savvopoulos, Khadije El Kadi, Isam Janajreh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates multistage freeze desalination (MSFD) for the treatment of reverse osmosis (RO) brine, targeting high-purity water production with improved overall recovery efficiency. Experiments were conducted using indirect batch freeze crystallization of seawater samples at − 15 °C, examining the effects of freezing configuration (i.e., top-bottom, and bottom-up). A wide range of initial salinities (i.e., 2, 4, 8, 17.5, 35, 70, and 120 g/L NaCl) was evaluated to determine their impact on water recovery and the minimum number of stages required to achieve potable water quality. Directional melting was employed to resolve salinity gradients across 32 discrete layers, enabling detailed characterization of salt diffusion and brine entrapment during freezing. Based on the experimental data, a predictive mathematical framework was developed to optimize the MSFD process performance. A three-dimensional response surface model, fitted using a second-order polynomial and artificial neural network (ANN), successfully correlated initial salinity, crystallinity, and accumulated salinity, achieving an excellent coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9978). Top-down freezing exhibits the lowest salinity accumulation and thus the highest desalination efficiency, outperforming bottom-up freezing. Results also show that increasing initial salinity necessitates additional crystallization stages, with water recovery decreasing sharply from 39.3% to 5.28% (top-bottom) and from 40% to 1.3% (bottom-up) as salinity increased from 8 to 120 g/L to reach potable water quality (~ 0.95 g/L). The top-bottom configuration consistently outperformed the bottom-up arrangement, producing lower cumulative salinity and more uniform gradients. To further enhance recovery, the concentrated brine rejected from the MSFD process was examined through thermodynamic modeling of eutectic freeze crystallization (EFC), allowing prediction of phase behavior, salt precipitation, and maximizing water recovery. The integrated MSFD–EFC system achieved total water recoveries of 92.52% (top-bottom) and 81.72% (bottom-up). These findings demonstrate that MSFD–EFC coupling enables progressive purification with high yield and minimal waste, offering a sustainable near-zero-liquid-discharge solution for high-salinity brine treatment.

Introduction

Freeze desalination (FD) is a promising water desalination technology that relies on the natural exclusion of salts and dissolved impurities during the crystallization of ice. When saline water freezes, the ice lattice rejects most solutes forming pure ice crystals and a concentrated brine phase (Rahman et al. 2007). Compared with conventional desalination technologies such as reverse osmosis (RO) and multistage flash distillation (MSF), FD offers several unique advantages. Its operation at subzero temperatures largely eliminates scaling and corrosion problems common in thermal systems (Kalista et al. 2018; Najim 2022). It also demonstrates the ability to process high-salinity brines that cannot be effectively treated with RO membranes (Arhin et al. 2016; Davenport et al. 2018; El Kadi et al. 2021; Randall & Nathoo 2015). These advantages make FD particularly attractive for sustainable desalination in resource-limited regions and for industrial wastewater treatment.

The limitations of FD are best understood in the context of zero-liquid-discharge (ZLD) brine management, where the objective is to recover nearly all influent water while leaving a minimal, disposable solid residue. Conventional ZLD trains rely on thermal brine concentrators and crystallizers such as evaporation ponds and multi-effect or mechanical-vapor-compression evaporators, which achieve very high recovery but require substantial amounts of energy to drive phase change, making them costly in terms of operational expenses and environmental impact (More & Maree 2025; Zhao et al. 2024). Membrane-based alternatives reduce this energy penalty: high-pressure reverse osmosis can extend recovery from hypersaline streams more efficiently than thermal evaporation, but its operating pressure and the risk of scaling place an upper bound on the salinity it can handle (Davenport et al. 2018). Freeze crystallization occupies a distinct position in this landscape. Because the latent heat of fusion of water is roughly one-seventh of its latent heat of vaporization, FD and eutectic freeze crystallization can approach near-zero-waste recovery of both potable water and solid salts from RO retentate at markedly lower energy demand than evaporative crystallizers, while avoiding the membrane scaling and pressure ceilings that constrain RO (Lewis et al. 2010; Randall et al. 2011; Randall & Nathoo 2015). The principal trade-offs are process complexity and the operational difficulty of efficient ice–brine separation and continuous crystallizer operation, which have historically limited large-scale adoption (Kalista et al. 2018). Multistage freeze desalination addresses these constraints directly by partitioning the separation across successive cycles, offering a route to ZLD-relevant recovery and salt rejection with energy intensity comparable to RO. Despite FD advantages, a single freeze–melt cycle is often insufficient to meet potable water standards (Shi et al. 2025). The World Health Organization (WHO 1963) specifies that drinking water should contain no more than 500 mg/L of total dissolved solids (TDS), equivalent to roughly 0.05 wt. %. However, typical FD processes achieve desalination efficiencies of only 30–70%, depending on the crystallizer design, ice recovery efficiency, and operating conditions (Zhang et al. 2023). As a result, multistage freeze desalination (MSFD) has been developed to progressively reduce brine salinity over successive cycles, improving both water recovery rate and water quality (Randall et al. 2011).

The concept of multistage freezing was first established by Griffiths (1988), where it revealed that after four consecutive crystallization stages, the purity of the recovered ice could exceed 99.99%. Later studies provided further insight into the influence of stage number and operational conditions on the process performance. For example, Eghtesad et al. (2020) studied a three-stage indirect FD system and reported that when the applied negative heat flux was maintained above 750 W/m2, ice production increased in later stages due to the progressive reduction in brine salinity. Badawy (2022) conducted sequential freezing–separation–melting experiments over eight stages and achieved 98.5% salt removal efficiency. However, water recovery dropped to about 35% by the end of the process, highlighting the trade-off between purity and yield. Similarly, Salakhi et al. (2022) investigated a continuous falling-film FD system and found that increasing the Reynolds number of the refrigerant enhanced water recovery, showing the importance of fluid dynamics and heat transfer in the overall process. Zhang et al. (2021a, b) further advanced the theoretical basis of MSFD by proposing a five-stage directional freezing model that linked salinity, crystallinity, and the number of stages. Previous studies establish both promise and limitations of MSFD. On one hand, they demonstrate that high salt rejection and potable-grade water quality are achievable with wisely controlled multistage operation (Al-Mutaz 2020; Thabit et al. 2022). On the other hand, they reveal challenges in balancing recovery and efficiency as higher crystallinity in each stage increases ice yield but also results in more salt presence, reducing the purity, whereas limiting/lowering crystallinity improves desalination efficiency but reduces water recovery. This trade-off underscores the necessity of quantitative models and optimization strategies that can determine the most effective number of stages for given water quality targets. This work highlights the potential of MSFD to achieve very high levels of seawater desalination efficiency with relatively low energy input. Artificial intelligence (AI), especially artificial neural networks (ANNs) and related machine learning methods, is increasingly used to improve water treatment and desalination by modeling complex, nonlinear processes, optimizing operation, and cutting energy and cost. Reviews show ANN models have been widely developed for membrane processes such as microfiltration, ultrafiltration, nanofiltration, reverse osmosis, membrane bioreactors, and membrane distillation, supporting simulation, optimization, and process control across lab, pilot, and full-scale plants (Ibrahim et al. 2024; Jawad et al. 2021). In RO desalination, ANNs can outperform traditional response surface models by accurately predicting performance over wider salinity ranges and enabling determination of optimal operating conditions for both brackish and seawater (Khayet et al. 2011; Taloba 2022). Beyond RO, AI tools including ANNs, random forests, and other machine learning techniques have been applied to crystallization and freeze-based desalination, hazardous waste freeze crystallization, and brine management to predict treatment efficiency, identify key operating variables, and reduce experimental workload (Savvopoulos et al. 2024). Broader reviews emphasize that AI in desalination and water treatment can enhance water quality, improve energy efficiency (with reported energy reductions up to ~ 50% in some AI-optimized desalination systems), enable predictive maintenance, and support integration with renewable-energy-driven systems, while also highlighting persisting challenges such as data quality, reproducibility, and limited full-scale implementation (Alam et al. 2022; Ibrahim et al. 2024). Beyond desalination, ANN-based frameworks have also been applied to real-time flow monitoring and demand prediction in water distribution networks, underscoring the growing role of data-driven models across the water sector (Ladino-Moreno 2025).

Building on this foundation, this work introduces a three-dimensional surface model that integrates several parameters including initial salinity, crystallinity, and accumulated salinity, to predict the outcomes of MSFD. In contrast to earlier research that primarily focused on case-specific experiments or simplified analytical relations, the proposed model provides a more general optimization framework, which enables systematic evaluation of stage requirements under a wide range of initial conditions, allowing the process to be tailored for different applications ranging from potable water production to industrial water reuse.

The primary objective of this study is to investigate the feasibility and optimization of multistage freeze desalination (MSFD) as an energy-efficient method for treating reverse osmosis (RO) brine. The research aims to evaluate how operational parameters, such as initial salinity, freezing configuration (top-bottom vs. bottom-up), and crystallization conditions, affect the purification efficiency and water recovery. Additionally, a predictive mathematical model capable of correlating key process variables, including initial salinity, crystallinity percentage, and accumulated salinity, is developed to support process optimization and scalability. In addition, the integration of eutectic freeze crystallization (EFC) with MSFD was evaluated through thermodynamic modelling as a complementary treatment for the concentrated brine, enabling the prediction of both salt crystallization and water recovery toward near-zero-liquid-discharge performance. By combining experimental data with modelling and simulation, this work provides quantitative performance evaluation and design-oriented insights for the development of sustainable and high-efficiency freeze desalination systems suitable for high-salinity waste streams.

Methodology

Experimental procedure

MSFD experiments using two freezing configurations are investigated in this work, namely bottom-up and top-down, and were selected to isolate the principal diffusion-controlled mechanism governing indirect freeze crystallization, namely the relative orientation of the gravity vector and the heat-extraction direction. The bottom-up case represents freezing in which buoyancy-driven brine settling and heat extraction oppose each other, while the top-bottom case represents freezing in which gravity assists brine drainage away from the ice front. Together, these two modes span the practically relevant range of gravity-opposed and gravity-assisted indirect FD geometries reported in the literature (El Kadi et al. 2021; Kalista et al. 2018; Najim 2022).

First, experiments using an indirect batch crystallizer in the bottom-up cooling configuration under four different subcooling temperatures (i.e., − 10, − 15, − 35 and − 40 °C) were conducted. Brine solutions of 35 g/L NaCl were prepared by dissolving reagent-grade sodium chloride in demineralized water at room temperature under magnetic stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. Considering the method described by Zhang et al. (2021a, b) as also illustrated in Fig. 1a, the setup included a freeze dryer (Virtis Advantage XL-50) serving as a temperature-controlled cold shelf and running at atmospheric pressure, a heated plate for controlled melting, and a conductivity meter (HACH HQ40D) for salinity measurement. During solidification, the sidewalls of the crystallizer were thermally insulated to ensure that heat transfer was unidirectional along the vertical axis. To perform top-down freezing configuration, the crystallizer was placed beneath the cold shelf. This allowed ice to grow downward from the cooled surface, while the container’s sidewalls were insulated to ensure unidirectional heat extraction.Fig. 1Experimental procedure for bottom-up and top-down brine freezing-melting experiments processes

Experimental procedure for bottom-up and top-down brine freezing-melting experiments processes

Directional melting was used as a spatial probe of the frozen column. After complete solidification, the ice was melted progressively from the surface that had been in contact with the cold source, and 32 successive meltwater fractions of equal volume were collected sequentially using a calibrated 30-mL syringe. Each fraction corresponds to a discrete layer along the original freezing direction, so its measured salinity reports the local solute concentration that was trapped at that position during ice growth. Three quantitative diagnostics are then extracted from the 32-layer dataset: the layer-wise (instantaneous) salinity, which reflects the local interfacial rejection efficiency; the cumulative (progressive) salinity, defined as the mass-weighted average of all previously melted layers at a given crystallinity, which represents the salinity of the recovered ice batch; and the deviation of the cumulative profile from the ideal solute-rejection limit, which quantifies the extent of brine entrapment caused by morphological instability of the advancing front. The 32-layer resolution is sufficient to capture the steep initial rejection regime, the intermediate diffusion-limited regime, and the final concentrated-brine regime within the same sample.

Subsequent experiments using a subcooling temperature of −15 °C were selected to investigate the effects freezing configurations (top-bottom and bottom-up) with varying initial salinities of 2, 4, 8, 17.5, 35, 70, and 120 g/L. These salinity levels were selected to represent the changing feed conditions encountered during a MSFD cascade rather than a single fixed feed concentration. At the assumed crystallinity/water-recovery level, the melted ice recovered from one stage has a reduced salinity and may serve as the feed to a subsequent purification stage, while the rejected liquid brine becomes progressively more concentrated and requires further treatment. Therefore, the selected salinities capture both the dilution pathway toward potable-water quality and the concentration pathway toward high-salinity brine management. Specifically, 2–8 g/L represents dilute intermediate streams approaching the final purification stages, 17.5–35 g/L brackets diluted seawater and standard seawater feed, 70 g/L corresponds to typical seawater RO concentrate, and 120 g/L represents the concentrate of RO brine level feed going through MSFD. This range ensures that the experimental dataset and the ANN surface model remain valid across the operating envelope encountered during MSFD operation. Each condition was repeated three times to reduce experimental uncertainty and improve statistical robustness. Another set of experiments using the top-down configuration was performed using the same freeze dryer experimental apparatus. The effect of shelf temperature (i.e., −15, −20, and −35 °C) on salinity and ion distribution has been investigated.

Experimental dataset and mathematical model

The experimental dataset obtained represents salinity values for each meltwater fraction as a function of crystallinity. Due to the sensitivity of the collected and melted samples caused by brine entrapment, the raw salinity values were aggregated into a progressive salinity, defined as the cumulative average of all previous layers at a given crystallinity level. This approach produced smoother data trends and facilitated the comparison between different setups, such as initial salinity or temperature, configuration, and subcooling temperature. The accumulated salinity versus crystallinity plots were then extended into three dimensions (3-D) corresponding to different initial salinity, that is, the dependent accumulative salinity versus the two independent parameters, initial salinity, and crystallinity. This enables the construction of a 3D dataset with initial salinity on the x-axis, crystallinity percentage on the y-axis, and accumulated salinity on the z-axis. The ANN framework was selected due to its superior capability in capturing the strongly nonlinear relationship between accumulated salinity and crystallinity observed in the experimental data. This choice is also physically consistent with the underlying freeze desalination mechanisms. During the initial stages of ice formation, a sharp decrease in accumulated salinity is observed as salts are efficiently rejected from the advancing ice front. As crystallinity increases, however, solute entrapment within the ice matrix becomes increasingly significant due to morphological instabilities and reduced interfacial rejection efficiency. This transition results in a nonlinear increase in accumulated salinity with further ice growth. Although higher-order polynomial regression can reproduce these trends, such models exhibited marginal improvements in the coefficient of determination (R2) at the expense of increased model complexity and a higher risk of overfitting, as reflected in the RMSE analysis. In contrast, the ANN-based model achieves an optimal balance between predictive accuracy and robustness, providing a reliable and physically interpretable representation of the experimental system without introducing unnecessary mathematical complexity. Early stopping on a held-out validation subset was applied during training to prevent overfitting; the reported metrics reflect performance on this validation partition rather than in-sample fit.

Dealing with RO brine

Figure 3 schematically illustrates the integrated multistage freeze desalination (MSFD) and eutectic freeze crystallization (EFC) process developed for the treatment of reverse osmosis (RO) brine. The process begins with RO brine entering the first freeze crystallization stage, where ice is selectively formed and physically separated from the residual brine. After each stage, the melted ice is analyzed to verify compliance with potable water salinity limits. If the quality criterion is satisfied, the meltwater is collected as freshwater; otherwise, the partially concentrated brine is forwarded to subsequent freezing stages for further purification. This staged freezing sequence continues until the brine reaches a highly concentrated condition. The final MSFD stage produces the last recoverable fraction of ice, after which the remaining hypersaline residual brine is directed to an EFC unit for further processing. The thermodynamic behavior of the hypersaline brine is modelled using OLI thermodynamic software, an electrolyte-based modelling platform capable of predicting phase equilibria, salt precipitation, and crystallization behavior in complex aqueous systems. The model accounts for the simultaneous crystallization of ice and dissolved salts, enabling separation of solid salt phases while leaving a minimal volume of concentrated liquid brine (Lewis et al. 2010). The integrated MSFD–EFC schematic highlights the progressive purification achieved through successive freeze–melt cycles, resulting in substantial freshwater recovery while minimizing residual waste streams. Overall, the process demonstrates the potential of coupling MSFD with EFC as a sustainable near-zero-liquid-discharge (near-ZLD) strategy for the treatment and valorization of high-salinity brines.Fig. 3Schematic of multistage freeze desalination (MSFD) process with eutectic freeze crystallization (EFC) of RO for enhanced water recovery

Results and discussion

Subcooling temperature effect

Figure 4 (left) illustrates the variation of accumulative salinity with crystallinity (%) for a 35 g/L NaCl brine solution subjected to different shelf (subcooling) temperatures of − 10, − 15, − 25, and − 40 °C. A general upward trend is observed across all cases, indicating that salinity in the remaining brine increases as crystallization progresses due to the continuous rejection of salt ions from the forming ice. Among the tested temperatures, − 15 °C exhibits the lowest accumulative salinity throughout the freezing process, suggesting superior salt exclusion efficiency and enhanced ice purity. In contrast, results corresponding to − 25 °C and − 40 °C show higher accumulative salinity values, particularly at higher crystallinity levels, which implies that excessive subcooling promotes rapid ice formation that dominates ion diffusion and thereby their entrapment. The − 10 °C case also shows a less efficient performance, likely due to insufficient subcooling and slower crystallization, resulting in reduced salt rejection. The error bars indicate good experimental reproducibility, with relatively small variations across triplicate runs. The results identify − 15 °C as the optimal subcooling temperature, balancing the freezing rate and solute rejection, and yielding the most favorable desalination performance among the tested conditions.Fig. 4Accumulative salinity gradient at different subcooling temperatures for 35g/L salinity (left), and at different initial salinities at subcooling temperature of − 15 °C (right)

Accumulative salinity gradient at different subcooling temperatures for 35g/L salinity (left), and at different initial salinities at subcooling temperature of − 15 °C (right)

To investigate the influence of initial salinity concentration, the experiments were carried out at seven initial salinity levels: 120, 70, 35, 17.5, 8, 4 and 2 g/L. Figure 4 (right) reports the cumulative salinity from the successive crystallized layers at different initial salinities. The accumulated salinity curves provided smoother and more interpretable profiles, showing a steady rise in salinity with increasing crystallinity. Notably, the experiments confirmed expected behavior that at approximately 50% crystallinity, the accumulated salinity is reduced to about half of the initial concentration. These findings indicate that experiments conducted at progressively lower initial salinities can be interpreted as equivalent to sequential stages in MSFD conducted at a fixed 50% crystallinity value, since each 50% freezing step produces ice with half the salinity of the feed. The results further revealed differences in the sharpness of the salinity reduction curves at different initial salinity concentrations. At higher initial salinities (e.g., 120 and 70 g/L), the decrease was more pronounced in early stages, while at lower salinities, the curve was flatter, reflecting a weaker driving force for salt rejection. This trend is consistent with previous studies which have reported stronger exclusion efficiency when the salinity gradient between ice and brine is larger. The experimental findings established a strong dataset that captures the essential dynamics of salt rejection under bottom-up freezing conditions.

Freezing configuration effect

Figure 5 compares the accumulative salinity gradients under bottom-up and top-down freezing configurations at a subcooling temperature of − 15 °C for brine solutions with varying initial salinities (2, 4, 8, 17.5, 35, 70, and 120 g/L). In both configurations, accumulative salinity increases with crystallinity, reflecting the progressive rejection of salts during ice formation. However, the extent and rate of salinity increase differ between configurations and initial salinities. In the bottom-up configuration (Fig. 5 (left)), the accumulative salinity rises more steeply particularly for high initial salinities (35–120 g/L). This behavior indicates less efficient salt exclusion due to the opposing direction of gravity and heat transfer. As ice grows upward, denser brine tends to sink toward the freezing front causing ion entrapment and higher residual salinity in the ice. Conversely, in the top-down configuration (Fig. 5 (right)), the salinity profiles are generally lower and more stable across the crystallization range. The downward growth of ice allows gravity to assist brine drainage away from the freezing interface, improving salt rejection and resulting in purer ice layers. The difference is more pronounced at higher salinities, where buoyancy-driven segregation becomes more significant. The results demonstrate that top-down freezing yields better desalination performance than bottom-up freezing, producing lower accumulative salinity and a more uniform salinity gradient across all tested concentrations. Mathematical modeling results

The accumulated salinity–crystallinity curves for each initial salinity were interpreted as contour slices of a larger dataset. By introducing initial salinity as a third dimension, the experimental data were represented as a smooth surface learned with ANN (Fig. 6). A compact feed-forward network (two hidden layers of 64 ReLU units; inputs Min-Max scaled; Adam optimizer with early stopping) was trained on the 32 × 7 grid of measurements. In the subcooling experimental data, the model accurately interpolates over the experimental domain, with R2 = 0.9978 and RMSE = 1.163 g/L on the measured grid. This ANN surface restores non-linear trends and replaces the quadratic approximation with a more accurate interpolator to predict salinity and crystallinity values. However, it should be noted that the model is currently limited to the specific experimental conditions employed in this study: bottom-up freezing at −15 °C with NaCl as the representative salt. Variations in temperature, freezing rate, or the presence of mixed salts could alter the rejection characteristics, and further validation under such conditions is required. Nonetheless, the present surface model provides a useful framework for evaluating and optimizing MSFD processes. Dealing with RO brine for enhanced water recovery

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the treatment of reverse osmosis (RO) brine was carried out using an integrated multistage freeze desalination (MSFD) and eutectic freeze crystallization (EFC) process. The RO brine underwent successive freezing stages, where ice was formed and separated at each stage, and only ice meeting potable salinity criteria after melting was collected as freshwater. The remaining brine was progressively concentrated and forwarded to subsequent stages until reaching extreme salinity. The final highly concentrated brine was processed using EFC, which was modelled using OLI software to predict simultaneous ice and solid salt crystallization behavior. It should be emphasized that, consistent with the NaCl-based scope of this study, the brine forwarded to the EFC stage is a concentrated NaCl–H₂O stream fully described by its salinity. The reported recoveries therefore correspond to this binary system; the sensitivity of EFC performance to multicomponent composition and impurity accumulation in real RO concentrates is a subject for future work.

Table 3 presents the results for top-bottom and bottom-up of MSFD for both configurations when applied to RO brine. In the top-bottom configuration, the process begins with an initial salinity of 70 g/L and progressively increases after each stage as water is extracted, and the remaining brine becomes more concentrated. The salinity rises from 78.64 g/L in the second stage to 205.54 g/L in the final stage, with a corresponding decline in water production from 11.13% in the first stage to 2.92% in the fourth. For the bottom-up configuration, although the process begins with the same initial salinity, it extends over eight stages. The total potable water recovery is 16.08%, which is significantly lower than in the top-bottom system. The results show that the top-bottom configuration remains more effective, producing nearly double the total potable water compared to the bottom-up setup. The decline in performance with increasing stages in the bottom-up system suggests that salt accumulation and reduced freezing efficiency limit water recovery at higher salinities. These results delimit the practical scalability window of standalone MSFD. Up to approximately 35 g/L, the process delivers acceptable recovery (19–39%) within a moderate number of stages (Table 2) and is viable as a single-technology route. Above 70 g/L, the number of stages required to reach potable quality rises steeply while the marginal recovery per stage becomes small, rendering standalone MSFD impractical. In this regime, integration with EFC is essential: routing the residual hypersaline brine to the EFC unit restores the total water recovery from 31.82% (MSFD-only, top-bottom) to 92.52%, and from 16.08% to 81.72% for the bottom-up case (Table 4), while additionally recovering solid salts toward near-zero-liquid-discharge. The MSFD–EFC coupling, rather than the scaling of MSFD alone, therefore constitutes the scalable architecture for high-salinity RO concentrate and industrial brine streams. Conclusion

This study demonstrated the potential of multistage freeze desalination (MSFD) as a promising crystallization-based technique for purifying high-salinity brine from reverse osmosis (RO) systems. By extending the experimental salinity-crystallinity results into a three-dimensional model incorporating initial salinity, crystallinity, and accumulated salinity, an artificial neural network (ANN)–based predictive surface was developed to capture the non-linear dynamics of the process and support multistage optimization. The developed framework identifies the optimum crystallinity and number of stages required for maximum ice recovery while producing potable-quality water. The findings demonstrate that higher initial salinity requires a greater number of crystallization stages, while water recovery progressively decreases with increasing salt concentrations. Experimental results further revealed that desalination performance strongly depends on both initial salinity and freezing configuration. As salinity increased from 8 to 120 g/L, water recovery declined from 39.3% to 5.28% for the top-bottom configuration and from 40% to 1.3% for the bottom-up configuration to achieve potable water quality (~ 0.95 g/L TDS). This trend occurs because higher salinity lowers the freezing point and increases brine viscosity, reducing ice crystal mobility and salt exclusion efficiency.

The present study uses synthetic NaCl-based solutions as a representative model for seawater RO brine. This choice is justified by dedicated ion chromatography measurements on real Arabian Gulf seawater and RO brine samples (Zhang et al. 2025), which confirm that Na⁺ and Cl⁻ together account for approximately 87% of the total ionic mass of the feed streams investigated, consistent with the IAPSO standard seawater reference composition (Millero et al. 2008). Real industrial brines additionally contain Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, SO₄2⁻, K⁺, and HCO₃⁻. Because divalent ions exhibit lower ice–liquid partition coefficients than Na⁺ and Cl⁻, their presence is expected to improve rather than degrade the per-stage salt rejection efficiency, while sparingly soluble species (e.g., CaSO₄·2H₂O, CaCO₃) may co-precipitate at high crystallinities and provide additional mineral recovery opportunities in the EFC stage. These effects are expected to modify, but not fundamentally alter, the salinity–crystallinity relationships and MSFD stage-number predictions reported here. Validation of the MSFD–EFC framework using real RO concentrate, with full multicomponent ion tracking through the OLI MSE thermodynamic database, is therefore identified as a priority next step.

Further work is needed to strengthen the practical applicability of the proposed MSFD–EFC framework. In particular, future studies should quantify the specific energy consumption (SEC) of the process under thermally integrated conditions, including internal heat recovery between the feed, recovered ice, and concentrated brine streams, as well as the possible use of waste-cold sources such as LNG regasification, industrial refrigeration systems, or other low-temperature waste streams. Such analysis would enable a direct techno-economic comparison with benchmark brine-treatment technologies such as RO, MSF/MED, and conventional crystallization. In addition, variable crystallinity strategies, where different stages are operated at optimized crystallinity levels rather than fixed values, should be explored using the developed 3D surface model. Hybrid modeling approaches that couple the ANN surface with thermodynamic and transport-based formulations could also provide deeper insight into the interplay between salt rejection, heat transfer, and brine entrapment. Finally, pilot-scale experiments using real seawater RO concentrate would be essential to validate the process under practical operating conditions and assess its real-world feasibility. Overall, the proposed framework provides a practical basis for adaptive, modular freeze-based desalination systems, while highlighting the need for future energy analysis and pilot-scale validation before real-world implementation.
