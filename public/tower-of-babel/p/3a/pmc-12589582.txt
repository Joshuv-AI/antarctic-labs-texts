Microbiological profile and 30-day mortality outcomes in febrile neutropenia among solid malignancy patients: a multicentre retrospective study from resource-limited setting
Authors: Chinna Babu Dracham, Sweta Bandanatham, Venkata Ravi Kiran Dumpala, Praveena Dasari, Sudhakar Kotlapati, Vijayalakshmi Juturu, Lokeswari Annam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFebrile neutropenia (FN) is a life-threatening complication in patients undergoing cancer chemotherapy. This multicentric retrospective study aimed to evaluate the microbiological profile and factors affecting 30-day mortality in FN patients with solid malignancies. FN defined as single episode of oral temperature of > 101 °F (≥ 38.3 °C), with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 500cells/mm3 or ANC which is expected to decrease to < 500cells/mm3 over next 48 h. All episodes of FN data that occurred between May 2021 and April 2023 were collected from three tertiary-care cancer centres and analysed. Among 710 patients who received chemotherapy over two years, 70 (9.8%) experienced FN. Antibiotic therapy was administered according to published guidelines. Of the 106 different cultures analysed, microbiological isolates were obtained from 23 (21.6%) cultures. Gram-negative bacteria were predominant (60.8%), followed by Gram-positive bacteria (30.43%). On the last day of follow-up, 52 (74.28%) patients were alive and 18 (25.71%) were dead. The 30-day mortality was significantly higher in patients with ANC < 500 cells/mm3 at the time of presentation (p = 0.001), who had positive cultures (p = 0.001), who developed FN on first-line chemotherapy (p = 0.020) and patients who did not receive prophylactic G-CSF support (p = 0.001). FN is a serious complication in patients undergoing chemotherapy for solid malignancies. This study identifies microbiological patterns and factors associated with higher 30-day mortality in FN among solid malignancy patients, suggesting areas for improved early recognition and tailored management.Keywords: Febrile neutropenia, Solid malignancy, Mortality, Microbiological profile and clinical outcomesSubject terms: Cancer, Cancer, Microbiology, Oncology

Introduction

Febrile neutropenia (FN) represents a critical complication in the treatment of patients undergoing cytotoxic chemotherapy for various malignancies. FN is defined as a single oral temperature of ≥ 38.3 °C (101 °F) or two consecutive readings of ≥ 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) over a period of one hour and absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of < 500 cells/mm3 or/and a predicted decline of ANC to ≤ 500 cells/mm3 over the next 48 h. FN is a direct consequence of chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression, where the depletion of neutrophils occurs, which is the body’s first line of defence against bacterial and fungal infections. This depletion of ANC makes patients susceptible to microbial invasion1–3.

The clinical significance of FN lies in its potential to rapidly escalate into life-threatening sepsis with or without shock if not promptly recognised and treated. Patients with FN often require urgent medical attention, hospitalisation, haemodynamic stabilisation and initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics to manage suspected infections until microbiological identification of the causative agent is obtained. Delays in appropriate treatment can lead to significant morbidity and mortality, making FN a major concern in oncology practice2,3. The incidence varies depending on the type of cancer, chemotherapy regimens, drug doses, and patient-specific factors such as age, comorbidities and baseline white blood cell (WBC) count. Haematological malignancies such as acute leukaemia and aggressive lymphoma, carry a higher risk of FN-related mortality due to more profound and prolonged neutropenia4–7. Many investigators recognise that gram-negative bacterial infections are associated with the highest FN-related mortality in haematological malignancies, specifically Pseudomonas aeruginosa, owing to multi–drug resistance. Clinical management approaches have changed from the previous “one-size-fits-all” in-hospital approach in haematological malignancies to better risk-adapted approaches taking into consideration patient-related, disease-related and treatment-related factors8,9. In contrast, FN in solid malignancies has different microbiological profile and low mortality rate compared to haematological malignancies. The literature on FN-related mortality and factors affecting it in solid malignancies is sparse.

Febrile neutropenia represents a complex clinical scenario requiring interdisciplinary collaboration and vigilant management to mitigate the associated risk and improve patient outcomes. Managing FN is challenging due to the necessity for swift diagnosis, infectious risk assessment, thorough workup, prompt initiation of empirical antibiotics, and the need to escalate or de-escalate antibiotic therapy while balancing concerns about antibiotic resistance. Preventive strategies include prophylactic antibiotics and growth factors (granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF)) (to boost neutrophil production), which play a crucial role in reducing FN severity in high-risk patients5,6. Keeping all these in mind, a clinical audit was conducted at multiple centres with the aim of evaluating the clinical profiles of patients, microbiological patterns, FN-related mortality, and factors associated with poor survival outcomes in the solid malignancies.

Materials and methods

This multicentric retrospective observational cross-sectional study was conducted at three state-level cancer care centres (Government General/Cancer Hospitals at Kurnool and Kadapa, and NRI Medical College and General Hospital, Mangalagiri). The medical records of patients treated with chemotherapy for solid malignancies at all three centres from May 2021 to April 2023 were analysed. The diagnosis of febrile neutropenia (FN) after chemotherapy was based on the standard definition. Patients with incomplete medical records or insufficient data were excluded from analysis.

All FN-diagnosed patients were examined clinically and investigated with appropriate laboratory tests, including complete blood picture, kidney function test, liver function test, serum electrolytes, chest X-ray, serum procalcitonin (if indicated), cultures, and sensitivity profiles from blood (collected from a peripheral vein), urine, and pus (if indicated from wounds). High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest was performed for clinically suspected cases of fungal infections.

Blood sample collection for culture and sensitivity

Samples were collected from peripheral venous access or existing central lines at the onset of fever. The peripheral venous site was cleansed with an antiseptic-soaked cotton swab in a centrifugal manner (from centre to periphery in a circular shape). Culture bottle caps were cleaned with alcohol swabs and made dry. Venipuncture was done with a sterile needle, and approximately 8–10 ml of blood was collected for each aerobic and anaerobic bottle.

All culture specimens were processed according to standard aseptic techniques upon suspicion of infection. The recommended techniques and stains were applied for isolation and identification of microorganisms. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines or institutional protocols. The results were interpreted based on established breakpoints. In patients with persistent fever, blood cultures were repeated every 48 h.

Once the patients were evaluated and diagnosed with FN based on the above definition, all of them were admitted immediately and started on empirical antibiotics with cefoperazone/sulbactam. If the patients had the following signs and conditions, vancomycin was added to above empirical antibiotics: (i) skin infections, (ii) pneumonia, (iii) gram-positive culture isolates, (iv) hypotension, and (v) venous catheter infection. Empirical antifungal drugs (amphotericin B) were administered to patients with suspected fungal infections of the lung and paranasal sinuses and/or identified by microscopy. Antibiotic drugs and dose schedules were modified according to culture and sensitivity reports.

Septic shock

Shock in FN is a life-threatening condition characterised by persistently low blood pressure with evidence of organ dysfunction, which leads to multiple organ dysfunction (MODS), acute kidney injury (AKI) and death of a patient.

Pitt’s bacteraemia score

This score was calculated from multiple parameters: fever (temperature readings), hypotension, mental scale, mechanical ventilation and cardiac arrest. All these parameters were taken into consideration within 48 h before or on the day of first positive blood culture and highest score was recorded for each parameter, and total points were calculated.

Demographic and clinical parameters, including age, sex, site of solid malignancy, stage, comorbidity status, duration of fever, chemotherapy regimen with drugs, dates of febrile neutropenia documentation, previous episodes of FN, absolute neutrophil count (ANC) at FN diagnosis, Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC) score at FN diagnosis, presence of septic shock, Pitt’s bacteraemia score, microbiological profiles (blood, urine, stool and wound swab cultures) and survival outcome at 30 days post FN, were entered into a structured proforma. Survival status (30-day mortality) was entered as in medical records.

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical profiles, microbiological patterns, antibiotic susceptibilities, and 30-day mortality rates post-FN in patients who underwent chemotherapy for solid malignancies. The primary outcome of this retrospective study was to measure/document the microbiological profile during FN episode, including the types of organisms isolated and their frequency. Secondary outcomes were antibiotic susceptibility of isolated organisms and mortality outcomes within 30 days of FN diagnosis, focusing on the association between risk factors and 30-day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Retrospective data were collected and maintained in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office Professional Plus 2010 version [14.0.7155.5001] SP2 MSO [14.0.7153.5000] developed for Microsoft Corporation by Impressa Systems, Santa Rosa, CA, USA). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences v23). Descriptive statistics were used to quantify demographic and clinical parameters of the study population. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile range (IQR), and the categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used to compare between the different variable groups. Survival analysis (30-day mortality) was performed using the life table method and computed with Kaplan-Meier analysis. The univariate influence of various clinical variables on survival outcomes (30-day mortality) was analysed using Pearson’s test. The survival parameter (30-day mortality) was determined as the time interval from the point of diagnosis of febrile neutropenia to the next follow-up of 30 days or date of death. Independent clinical variables for survival outcomes were analysed using the Cox proportional hazard model (multivariate analysis). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical considerations

The study (multicentric, retrospective, observational, cross-sectional study) was approved by the local Institutional Ethics Committee of all the participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from patients/carers prior to the FN treatment.

Ethical approval.

Retrospective clinical analysis and all authors have followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for all human experimental investigations. Approved from Institutional Ethics Committees of their Institutes.

Consent to participate.

Consent from individual patients for publication was not required as study was retrospective observation analysis.

Results

In all three institutes, a total of 710 patients with solid tumours received cytotoxic chemotherapy during the study period. Of the 710 patients, 70 (9.8%) were identified as having febrile neutropenia (FN) according to the definition. All patients were aged > 18 years. The median age of patients was 50 years (interquartile range (IQR): 42–57). Most of the patients were females (65.71%). The median duration of fever was six days (IQR: 2–16). Different chemotherapy doses and schedules were followed for each variant of solid malignancy (disease status), performance status and comorbid condition. Chronic illnesses were present in 23 patients (32.8%). Previous episodes of FN were found in 13 patients (18.5%). The highest number of FN episodes was found in gastrointestinal cancers (25.71%), followed by gynaecological cancers (21.4%). The paclitaxel/docetaxel + carboplatin regimen was the regimen found to have higher FN episodes (18.5%), followed by capecitabine + oxaliplatin (15.7%) and/or etoposide + cisplatin/carboplatin (15.7%). On the day of documentation of FN or when patients came to emergency with febrile symptoms, median total leukocyte count (TLC) was 900 cells/mm3 (IQR: 300–18000). The most common sites of infection were the gastrointestinal (47.73%), respiratory (38.6%), urinary tract (9.04%) and soft tissue (4.55%). Microbial profile and sensitivity data

A total of 106 samples were analysed for culture, with only 23 (21.6%) microbiological isolates identified in the cultures. The remaining samples were sterile (78.3%). Gram-negative bacteria (60.8%) were the most isolated organisms, followed by gram-positive bacteria (30.43%). Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were the most common gram-negative bacterial isolates. Staphylococcus and Enterococcus faecalis were the most common isolates among the gram-positive bacteria. Among Gram-positive cocci, Staphylococcus species accounted for 85% of the isolates (Table 2).

FN treatment

Sixty-four per cent of the patients treated with first-line empirical antibiotics (cefoperazone/sulbactam) showed clinical improvement. In the remaining patients, antibiotics were changed to second-line drugs with meropenem or imipenem/cilastatin or piperacillin/tazobactam and amikacin. Clinical improvement was recorded in 30% of patients treated with these second-line antibiotics. The empirical antibiotic regimen was changed to definitive therapy in 28 patients (40%) after clinical, imaging and microbiological results. Third-line drugs were administered to patients who did not respond to either antibiotic. The median time to defervescence was three days. Vancomycin was added in patients with persistent fever and organ dysfunction. Antifungal drugs were added in therapeutic doses in four patients (5.7%), out of which Amphotericin B was given to three patients and Itraconazole to one patient.

Clinical outcomes

The follow-up data for all patients was entered until day 150. The 30-day follow-up from the date of FN presentation was considered for the calculation of clinical outcomes. On the last day of follow-up, 52 (74.28%) patients were alive and 18 (25.71%) were dead [Figure 1]. The impact of various clinical variables on 30-day mortality was determined using univariate analysis. Clinical variables included age (≤ 50 vs. >50 years), sex, shock at the time of FN presentation, comorbidity status, duration of fever (≤ 6 days vs. >6 days), ANC (ANC ≥ 500 vs. <500 cells/mm3), chemotherapy (first line vs. multiple lines), granulocyte- Colony stimulating factor usage (G-CSF), culture (microbial positivity vs. negativity) and Pitt’s bacteraemia score (< 4 vs. ≥4). The 30-day mortality showed a trend towards significance in patients > 50 years of age compared with those aged ≤ 50 years (35.48% vs. 7.94%, p = 0.06) [Figure 2]. Similarly, a trend towards significance in mortality was observed in patients who presented with shock (30.76% vs. 22.72, p = 0.064). Patients who presented with FN after first-line chemotherapy (34.8% vs. 11.1%, p = 0.020 [Figure 3]), ANC of < 500 cells/mm3 at the time of FN presentation (66.6% vs. 17.3%, p = 0.001 [Figure 4]) and patients with positive microbial flora on cultures (39.1% vs. 19.1%, p = 0.001 [Figure 5]) had significantly higher mortality. The 30-day mortality was significantly less in patients who had taken G-CSF prophylactically in comparison with patients who did not (22% vs. 50%, p = 0.001 [Figure 6]) with the univariate analysis; however, this was not sustained in multivariate analysis. Higher mortality was observed in patients with a Pitt’s bacteraemia score ≥ 4 compared to < 4 (46.1% vs. 13.63, p = 0.002). Patients who had duration of fever of more than six days and had comorbidity at baseline did not show significant effect on FN-related mortality (Table 4). On multivariate analysis, none of these clinical factors showed significance (Table 5).

Discussion

Febrile neutropenia is an oncologic emergency that is less common in patients with solid malignancies than haematological malignancies. We performed an institutional analysis of the clinical and microbiological profiles of the patients with FN only solid malignancies. The key factor in solid malignancy management is gaining maximum pharmacological effect with anti-cancer drugs while minimising adverse events. According to the common terminology criteria for adverse events (CTCAE), the adverse events range from grade 0 to grade V (death), whereas the chemotherapy-induced neutropenia ranges from grade 0 to grade IV depending on absolute neutrophil count10. Recent developments in the field of antibiotics have revolutionised the management of neutropenia and cancer care. Data suggests that the incidence of febrile neutropenia in solid malignancy ranges from 6 to 21%11–14. We observed a similar incidence of FN (7.8%).

Recent studies have shown isolation rates of approximately 26–30% for microbiological organisms. Gram-positive bacteria contribute 54%, gram-negative bacteria contribute 35%, and fungal infections contribute 3.2%15–17. The present study identified a notable microbiologic flora in 21.6% of FN cases, of which gram-negative bacteria were predominant (60.8%), followed by gram-positive bacteria (30.43%) and fungi (0.9%). This finding is in contrast with earlier reports indicating a higher prevalence of gram-negative bacteria due to uncommon use of central venous catheters. According to Zuckermann J et al., there was a 52.8% prevalence of gram-positive bacteria and 47.2% prevalence of gram-negative bacteria in the 2001–2003 period. However, between 2004 and 2005, 38.1% were gram-positive and 61.9% were gram-negative bacteria9. This bacterial shift towards gram-negative isolates, including multidrug-resistant strains like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and oxacillin-resistant Staphylococcus suggests evolving antimicrobial resistance patterns and treatment challenges8,18,19. Gram-negative bacterial infections in FN patients had a worse prognosis than gram-positive bacteria, with mortality rates of 18% and 5%, respectively20. This shift may be influenced by changes in empirical antibiotic therapy protocols, prolonged empirical antibiotic usage, decreased usage of prophylactic antibiotics like fluoroquinolones, reduced usage of central lines and an increased adherence to protocol21,22. Antibiotic sensitivity patterns in the present study revealed a high level of resistance to cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, amikacin and cefoperazone/sulbactam in gram-negative isolates. Higher sensitivity of isolates to piperacillin/tazobactam, meropenem and colistin indicates the need for revision of antibiotic policy in our centres. The isolates of Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus Aureus (MRSA) were low in present study. All Staphylococcal and Enterococcal isolates were sensitive to vancomycin, which is comparable with other centres in India23,24.

Yadegarynia D et al. studied the spectrum of bacterial infections in cancer patients and reported most common sites of infections included respiratory tract (38%), blood (35%) and urinary tract (11%)25. In the present study, most common sites of infection were the gastrointestinal tract (47.7%), respiratory tract (38.6%), urinary tract (9%) and wound sites (4.5%). In gram-negative bacterial infections, the most common agents reported are Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae25,26, and we found these isolates to be the most common gram-negative organisms in our study as well.

According to Nesher L et al.27, periodic epidemiological surveillance on microbiological patterns, both globally and at the institutional level, should be emphasised to effectively guide empirical antibiotic therapy. These surveillances are crucial for adapting treatment strategies to emerging antibiotic resistance patterns. Such adaptation is essential to optimise patient outcomes and to minimise the further development of antimicrobial resistance. Most of the studies report FN-related mortality rates for both solid and haematological malignancies in the range of 17–34%28–32. Our study found an FN mortality rate of 25.7% in a patient cohort with solid malignancies alone, and the prevalence was similar to other studies reported from around the world29,30.

Prophylactic administration of granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) reduces the incidence and severity of neutropenia and its related infections and shortens its duration. However, the chemotherapy-induced neutropenia remains a dose-limiting factor in cancer management33,34. Evidence-based guidelines from different societies (NCCN, ASCO and ESMO) recommend use of G-CSFs, such as filgrastim and pegfilgrastim, in the primary prophylaxis for patients who are at high risk (≥ 20%) and intermediate risk (10–20%; with ≥ 1 FN risk factors) for FN and receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy35,36. Mahtani RL et al. evaluated the incidence of FN in breast cancer patients treated with pegfilgrastim and placebo and showed a significantly higher incidence in placebo arm (4.1% vs. 7.8%)37. Another systematic review of 13 studies (eight retrospective, one prospective, one phase I, and three randomised trials), investigated the efficacy, effectiveness and safety of pegfilgrastim in preventing FN in solid malignancies and lymphomas. This review reported that the incidence of FN in patients treated with placebo was significantly higher than that in patients treated with pegfilgrastim (21% vs. 11%)11. Two randomised control trials comparing the FN-related mortality data between pegfilgrastim and placebo reported numerically higher numbers in the placebo arm but were statistically insignificant38,39. The present study showed significantly higher mortality in patients who did not receive G-CSF (p = 0.001), but our study was retrospective in nature, and only 12 FN patients did not receive G-CSF.

Several studies have contributed valuable insights into various prognostic factors affecting outcomes in FN. According to Philip ML et al., the incidence of FN varies with each cycle of chemotherapy and decreases with increasing chemotherapy cycles. The study reported FN in 36.6% of the patients in the first cycle, 22.7% in the second; and 18.1% in the sixth cycle40. Similar results are reported by Salako O et al., with an incidence of 14.2% after the first cycle and 4.9% after the last cycle of chemotherapy41. The same study also showed continuously decreasing magnitude of FN episodes with successive chemotherapy lines and a higher rate of FN for first cycle (43 patients; 61.4%) than in successive multiple lines (27 patients; 38.5%). Our study also showed statistically significant higher mortality in patients receiving first-line chemotherapy (34.8% vs. 11.1%, p = 0.02).

A retrospective study by Moreira-Pinto J et al.42 reported the effect of ANC < 100/mcL on FN mortality in patients with solid malignancies who were undergoing intravenous chemotherapy. The mortality rate was higher in patients with ANC < 100/mcL in comparison with patients with ANC 101–1000/mcL (p = 0.04). On multivariate analysis, ANC < 100/mcL was associated with poor survival (p = 0.015). In our study, patients with ANC < 500/mcL had significantly higher mortality compared with patients ANC ≥ 500/mcL (66.6% vs. 17.2%; p = 0.001). Ghosh et al.32 studied the effect of Total Leucocyte Count (TLC) < 200/mcL on FN mortality. They found that nadir TLC < 200/mcL and abnormal chest radiographs were associated with significantly a high mortality rate. Darmon M et al.43 studied the impact of FN on short-term mortality in both solid and haematological malignancies and highlighted that age, respiratory failure, renal failure, and coma were significant predictors of 30-day mortality. Hosiriluck N et al.44 evaluated the FN-related mortality rates in both haematological cancers (66.1%) and solid malignancies (33.9%) and reported a significantly higher 30-day mortality in patients with positive cultures, patients admitted with septic shock and abnormal liver enzymes. On multivariate analysis with other clinical parameters such as age > 60 years, gender, comorbidities, type of malignancies and MASCC score did not significantly influence 30-day mortality. The current study showed significantly higher mortality in patients with culture positivity (39.1% vs. 19.1%; p = 0.001). Haemodynamic instability with shock and age > 50 years did not seem to influence mortality. All these findings collectively emphasise the critical role of infections, laboratory parameters and organ dysfunction in predicting mortality of FN patients and highlight the need for personalised care and careful monitoring to improve outcomes for these patients.

Febrile neutropenia is more common and serious in haematological malignancies than solid tumours. Although the duration of neutropenia was significantly longer in haematological malignancies, there is no significant difference in the mortality rate or survival outcomes36–46. Similar results were reported by many studies from all over the world47–51, with the presence of medical comorbidities, MASCC score and positive culture with a predominance of gram-negative infections showing higher mortality, but not described specifically to solid malignancies, whereas our study reported solid malignancy FN-related microbiological profiles and clinical outcomes; every institute must have a regular revision of antibiotic policy based on periodic assessment of the clinical and microbiological profile in FN to combat antibiotic resistance. Therefore, improving outcomes in FN requires early detection and prompt treatment. Using the right antibiotics early, based on local resistance patterns, is important to prevent resistance. Standard guidelines by NCCN, ASCO and ESMO for FN management are crucial in updating the microbiological profile shifts, developing antibiotic regimens & supportive protocols, and potentially improving patient outcomes. Future research should focus on faster infection detection methods, personalised therapies based on infection type, and immunomodulatory treatments to enhance patient recovery.

Limitations

This study has limitations, including its retrospective observational study design, which leads to selection bias, which limits the quality and comprehensive evaluation of clinical variables affecting outcomes and, therefore, restrains the generalisability of our findings. The data collection from pre-existing documents introduces the possibility of information missing and documentation errors, which further hinders the applicability of our findings. We did not report the automated drug sensitivity and antibiotic resistance, which are strong predictive factors in FN, and this happened due to logistical reasons and lack of access to automated culture testing methods. Despite conducting the study in a resource-constrained setting, we did not directly evaluate the impact of limited resources and cost-effectiveness on clinical outcomes.

Another significant limitation of this study is missing essential information to calculate the SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) score and APACHE (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation) score. The SOFA score is commonly used in medical ICUs (Intensive Care Units) to assess the severity of critical illness by measuring the performance status of six organ systems – respiratory, cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, coagulation and central nervous system. The APACHE score is used to predict the mortality in ICU setting and is calculated from acute physiological values of patient within 24 h of admission (this score is calculated from 12 physiological variables), age score and chronic previous health condition score. These two scores help in predicting the prognosis, making the necessary level of diagnostic and therapeutic interventions possible, and monitoring the response to therapy. Without this essential information, our understanding of influence of individual variables on clinical outcomes and treatment effectiveness is limited. Because of these limitations, none of the individual factors may be significant on multivariate analysis.

Unlike many other studies that included both haematological and solid malignancies, we exclusively studied patients with solid malignancies from multiple centres. The present study has estimated the effect of individual factors on FN-related mortality and inform clinical management strategies for FN in solid malignancies, particularly in resource-limited settings. Future prospective studies and collaborative research efforts will be crucial to validate and expand upon our findings in diverse patient populations.

Conclusion

In summary, bacterial infections are prevalent in febrile neutropenia among patients with solid malignancies, posing a significant clinical challenge. Predictors of mortality include profound neutropenia (ANC < 500cells/mm3), culture positivity, patients who developed FN during the first-line chemotherapy, Pitt’s bacteraemia score ≥ 4, and patients who did not receive prophylactic G-CSF. This study identifies microbiological patterns and factors associated with higher 30-day mortality in FN among solid malignancy patients, suggesting areas for improved early recognition and tailored management. A predominance of gram-negative bacterial isolates was observed in this study, aligning with emerging trends in FN microbiology.
