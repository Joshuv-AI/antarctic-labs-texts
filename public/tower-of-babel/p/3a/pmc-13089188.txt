Effects of dietary allicin supplementation on nutrient digestion and gastrointestinal health of Guizhou black goats
Authors: Chao Yuan, Xiaomin Wu, Yang Yang, Yong Han, Chaozhi Su, Pramote Paengkoum, Xin Wang, Jiafu Zhao, Shengyong Lu, Haolin Chen, Defeng Wang, Wen Xiao, Yong Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to reveal the effects of allicin on nutrient digestion, gastrointestinal enzyme activity, rumen fermentation parameters, gastrointestinal morphology, intestinal barrier function, and gastrointestinal microbial ecology of Guizhou black goats. Thirty-two male Guizhou black goats, each aged five months, with an initial body weight of 18.28 ± 0.41 kg, were divided into one of four groups in a completely randomized design: control (CON, without allicin), low allicin (L; 0.5 g/d per head), medium allicin (M; 0.75 g/d per head), and high allicin (H; 1 g/d per head), respectively. Each group consisted of eight replicates with one growing goat per replicate. The experiment lasted for 75 d, including a 15-d acclimation period and a 60-d experimental period. The results showed that the apparent digestibility of crude protein (CP), dry matter (DM), and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) was highest in the M group, being significantly higher than those in the CON group (P < 0.05). Moreover, ruminal cellulase and cellobiase activities, jejunal trypsin activity, cecal cellulase activity, ruminal total volatile fatty acids (TVFA), acetate, propionate, and butyrate concentrations were all highest in the M group (P < 0.05). In contrast, ruminal ammoniacal nitrogen (NH3–N) concentration was the opposite (P = 0.015). As allicin inclusion increased, the jejunal trypsin activity and ruminal butyrate concentration exhibited linear responses (P < 0.05), while the apparent digestibility of DM and NDF, rumen TVFA, acetate, and propionate concentrations, as well as cellulase and cellobiase activities were affected quadratically (P < 0.05). In the jejunal mucosa, the protein expression levels and the relative mRNA expression levels of claudin 1, claudin 4, and ZO-1 were higher in the M group than in the CON group (P < 0.05). As allicin supplementation increased, claudin 1, claudin 4, and ZO-1 protein expressions exhibited both linear and quadratic responses (P < 0.001), whereas the relative mRNA expression levels were modulated solely by the quadratic term (P < 0.05). Moreover, the L and M groups had significantly greater papillae height and muscle layer thickness than the CON group (P < 0.05), and papillae width increased in a quadratic manner (P = 0.029). However, the M group showed increased villus density compared with the CON group (P = 0.026), with significant quadratic effects on papillae density and villus height (P < 0.05). Importantly, allicin also improved the gastrointestinal microecological balance by reconstructing the gastrointestinal microbial composition. In conclusion, allicin improves gastrointestinal health by regulating gastrointestinal microbial composition, promoting nutrient absorption and tissue development, enhancing rumen fermentation and gastrointestinal enzyme activities, and strengthening the intestinal barrier. The optimal supplementation level of allicin is 0.75 g/d per head.

Introduction

Ruminants are the cornerstone of global agriculture, contributing significantly to the human food supply through the production of meat, milk, and other byproducts. Since 2006, the European Union has banned the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in livestock (Kobayashi et al., 2016). In 2020, China introduced a new era in livestock farming by officially banning the use of antibiotics in animal feed (http://www.moa.gov.cn, No. 194). Plant-derived additives, such as allicin from garlic, have emerged as promising options due to their antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects (Nan et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2020).

Allicin (diallyl thiosulfate) is the primary active compound in garlic, whereas other organosulfur compounds it produces—including γ-glutamyl-S-allyl-L-cysteine, diallyl sulfide, ajoene, and S-allyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide—serve as the main pharmacological agents, collectively known as allium compounds (Chen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2015; Suleria et al., 2015). In ruminants, allicin has shown potential benefits. Supplementation at 2.0 g/d per head in Dorper crossbred ewes improved feed digestibility and reduced methane emissions (L/kg body weight [BW]0.75), which was suggested may have been achieved by altering rumen microbial composition (Ma et al., 2016). Additionally, it was further confirmed by Liu et al. (2024) that supplementing the diet with 30.5 mg/kg allicin has the potential to reshape rumen microbial composition and reduce the abundance of methane-producing bacteria. Notably, their study also demonstrated that allicin improved immunity and antioxidant capacity, and reduced damage to rumen epithelial cell junctions caused by high-grain diets, without affecting average daily gain (ADG) and rumen fermentation parameters in goats. On the contrary, Tang et al. (2024) showed that dietary supplementation of 750 mg/d per head of allicin could significantly increase the ADG of goats. In the early stage, the research showed that supplementation with 0.75 g/d per head of allicin improved slaughter performance, growth performance, antioxidant capacity, immune function, and economic returns in Guizhou black goats (Yuan et al., 2025).

Although extensive research has been carried out on the effects of allicin in monogastric animals, studies on its impact on ruminants remain scarce (Ding et al., 2023). Furthermore, the effects of allicin on ruminant gastrointestinal health are still largely unknown. Therefore, the present study hypothesizes that allicin enhances nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation, gastrointestinal development, and intestinal barrier function in Guizhou black goats by reshaping the gastrointestinal microbiota, exhibiting a quadratic dose–response relationship with maximal benefits at a dosage of 0.75 g/d per head. The main objective of this study is to evaluate the dose-dependent effects of allicin supplementation on gastrointestinal health in Guizhou black goats by testing three concentrations (0.5, 0.75, and 1 g/d per head). Specific objectives include assessing nutrient digestibility and rumen fermentation parameters, examining gastrointestinal morphology and enzyme activity, evaluating intestinal barrier function, and analyzing microbial composition to elucidate the mechanisms of allicin. This research aims to provide insights into allicin as a novel feed additive for improving ruminant productivity and sustainability.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animals were treated carefully following animal welfare guidelines and under strict regulatory supervision by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou University, Guiyang, Guizhou, China (EAE-GZU-2022-E065). All experimental animals were euthanized at the end of the study, following the ARRIVE guidelines for animal research.

Animals, materials, experimental design, diet, and management

Allicin (C6H10S2O, 98% pure) was procured from Weifang Zhongtian Feed Technology Co., Ltd., Weifang, Shandong, China. Thirty-two male Guizhou black goats, weighing 18.28 ± 0.41 kg at five months of age, were divided into one of four groups in a completely randomized design: control (CON; without allicin), low allicin (L; 0.5 g/d per head), medium allicin (M; 0.75 g/d per head), and high allicin (H; 1 g/d per head), respectively. Each group consisted of eight replicates (n = 8) with one growing goat per replicate. All groups were fed the same basal diet, which was formulated according to the nutrient requirements recommended by the NRC (2007). Each goat in this experiment was raised individually in a fully automatic metabolic cage system, which consisted of 44 metabolic cages (2 m2). Moreover, this fully automatic system was also equipped with automatic weighing, automatic water supply, temperature detection, humidity detection, hydrogen sulfide detection, and safety sensor detection equipment. The experiment lasted 75 d. Prior to the experiment, all goats were acclimatized to the experimental environment for 15 d, where the temperature was controlled at 24 ± 2 °C and the relative humidity at (75 ± 5)%. During this period, they were fed the same basal diet as used in the trial (Table 1) and had free access to water via automatic drinkers. The formal experimental period was 60 d. Feeding times were set at 08:30 and 16:30 daily. Feeding times during the experiment were synchronized with the natural light cycle to eliminate circadian bias.

Chemical analysis and apparent digestibility

Approximately 100 g of the basal diet was sampled weekly and mixed at the end of the feeding trial. The digestibility test lasted for 12 d, consisting of a 7-d adaptation period followed by 5 d of total fecal collection. From d 70 to 75, prior to the morning feeding, total feces excreted by each goat were collected, weighed, and recorded daily. Twenty percent of the fresh feces were mixed with 10% (v/v) dilute sulfuric acid (10 mL per 100 g of fresh feces, pH = 3.22) to stabilize nitrogen, and stored at −20 °C. All samples were dried in a vacuum oven at 65 °C for 72 h, then ground and passed through a 2-mm mesh sieve.

The contents of dry matter (DM, method 934.01), ether extract (EE, method 920.39), crude protein (CP, method 954.01), ash (method 942.05), acid detergent fiber (ADF, method 973.18), and minerals (method 985.01) were determined according to AOAC (2005). The organic matter (OM) content in the diet was calculated according to the following equation: OM = DM – ash. Metabolizable energy (ME) was calculated according to NRC (2007). Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) was analyzed following the method of Van Soest et al. (1991) with sodium sulfite added and without thermostable α-amylase, and the NDF results were expressed as ash-free values. Apparent nutrient digestibility was calculated using acid-insoluble ash (AIA) as an endogenous indicator based on the procedure described by Van Keulen and Young (1977). Accordingly, the AIA content in feed and feces was analyzed, and nutrient apparent digestibility was calculated as follows (Li et al., 2022):

Apparent nutrient digestibility (%) = 1 – (AIA content in diet/AIA content in feces × Nutrient content in feces/Nutrient content in diet) × 100.

Fatty acids from feedstuffs were prepared according to Sukhija and Palmquist (1988) by the one-step extraction transesterification procedure with toluene and nonadecanoic acid (19:0, 1 mg/mL) as internal standard. The FAME were analysed by gas chromatography (GC) using an Agilent 7890B (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a flame-ionization detector and a DB-5 capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the chromatographic conditions described by Santos-Silva et al. (2023). Moreover, amino acid content in the extracted sample was according to Long et al. (2022), and each amino acid's concentration was quantified with an amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Rumen fermentation parameters

On the final experimental day, six goats from each treatment group were randomly chosen and transported 38 km to a commercial abattoir. All goats were rested for 16 h before slaughter, with free access to water but no feed, following standard procedures. According to animal welfare requirements (euthanasia), all goats were electroshocked (220 V, 1.4 A), bled, and their viscera, forelimb knee joints, and hindlimb toe joints were removed (Abdullah et al., 2019). The rumen sample of each goat was filtered through gauze to separate the rumen fluid and rumen contents. The pH of freshly collected rumen fluid was measured immediately on-site. After pH analysis of the rumen fluid, it was temporarily stored in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C). The rumen fluid was then transported from the commercial abattoir to the laboratory, with the transport time kept within 1 h. Upon arrival, ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N) concentration was analyzed immediately. The volatile fatty acids (VFA) concentrations in the rumen fluid were quantified using gas chromatography (GC-MS; HP8890/7693A, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The sample preparation protocol for chromatographic analysis was modified from the method described by Han et al. (2018). The GC was equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Rumen contents were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

The gas chromatography procedure was as follows: nitrogen (N2) served as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The split ratio was 10:1, the injection volume was 1 μL, and the injection port temperature was set at 250 °C. The ion source temperature was 300 °C, and the interface temperature was 250 °C. The column temperature was programmed as follows: the initial temperature was set at 90 °C, then increased to 120 °C at 10 °C/min, raised to 150 °C at 5 °C/min, and finally ramped to 250 °C at 25 °C/min and held for 2 min, with a total run time of 15 min.

Gastrointestinal tract histomorphology

Rumen and jejunum tissue samples (1 cm2) were collected from each goat after slaughter. The samples were washed with pre-cooled phosphate buffer (pH = 7.1), placed in embedding cassettes, and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. All sample trimming, embedding, sectioning, and hematoxylin and eosin staining were completed by Wuhan Servicebio Technology, Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China. The direction of shearing the sample by rotary microtome (Leica RM2245, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Nussloch, Germany) was longitudinal. Moreover, the final sections of the samples were analyzed by panoramic scanning (model Panoramic desk 1000, 3DHistech Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) according to the method described by Abdelsattar et al. (2022). CaseViewer2.4 scanning and browsing software (version 2.4, 3DHistech Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) was used to analyze the morphological structure in five fields of view per slice at high magnification (rumen tissue: 10 × imaging; jejunum tissue: 50 × imaging). The average value for each parameter was subsequently calculated. The morphological parameters of jejunum included crypt depth, crypt density, villus height, villus density, and villus width. The morphological parameters of rumen included rumen epithelial thickness, papillae height, papillae width, papillae density, and muscle layer thickness.

Gastrointestinal enzyme activities

The activities of cellulase, cellobiase, and α-amylase in the rumen contents and jejunal mucosa were determined using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Intestinal barrier

RNA quality assessment, cDNA transcription, and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) procedures followed the methodology outlined by Long et al. (2022). GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene, and relative mRNA expression levels of claudin 1, claudin 4, occludin, and ZO-1 were quantified using the 2–ΔΔCt method as described by Livak and Schmittgen (2001). Gastrointestinal microbial profile

Samples from rumen fluid, jejunal mucosa, and cecal contents were collected, snap-frozen, and stored at −80 °C. DNA was extracted from these samples using the MagPure Soil DNA LQ Kit (D6356-02, Magen Biotech Co., Ltd., Hilden, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). Mechanical disruption was performed using bead-beating on a PowerLyzer homogenizer (Mo Bio Laboratories, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 320 × g for 30 s, followed by a 30 s pause, and then repeated once. DNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR using primers (343F: 5′-TACGRAGCAGCAG-3′; 798R: 5′-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3′) in a 25 μL reaction. The reverse primer, containing the sample barcode, was used, and both primers were ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters.

Amplicon quality was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Quantification of PCR products was conducted using the Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit (Q32854, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), followed by purification with Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). Product concentrations were normalized in preparation for high-throughput sequencing. Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), utilizing paired-end reads of 250 bp in length.

Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were initially processed using Cutadapt to identify and remove adapter sequences. Subsequent quality control was performed using QIIME2 (version 2020.11, QIIME2 Development Team, San Diego, CA, USA) in conjunction with the DADA2 pipeline, which enabled filtering, denoising, merging of paired-end reads, and chimera removal, following the protocols established by Callahan et al. (2016) and Bolyen et al. (2019). Representative sequences and an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) abundance table were then generated. QIIME2 was employed to select a representative read for each ASV, which was subsequently annotated by aligning against the Silva reference database (version 138, Silva GmbH, Bayreuth, Bavaria, Germany) using the default parameters of the q2-feature-classifier plugin. For α-diversity analysis, the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indexes were calculated as described by Chao and Bunge (2002) and Hill et al. (2003). Phylogenetic trees were constructed, and unweighted UniFrac-based principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was conducted using the UniFrac distance matrices generated within QIIME2. Sequencing and bioinformatic analyses of the 16S rRNA gene amplicons were conducted by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

Raw data were initially organized using Excel 2023. Normality of data distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk procedure of SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA), and skewed data sets were log-transformed. All subsequent statistical analyses, including ANOVA and post hoc tests, were performed on the transformed data to ensure the validity of P-values. Independence of observations was assumed based on the experimental design, which involved random assignment of goats to treatments and individual housing to minimize inter-animal interactions. Levene's test was used to assess homogeneity of variance. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and orthogonal polynomial contrasts (linear and quadratic) were used to investigate the effects of allicin supplementation, with nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation parameters, gastrointestinal enzyme activities, gastrointestinal morphology, gene expression levels, and microbial composition serving as dependent variables. Duncan's post hoc test was applied to determine statistically significant differences among group means. The data were analyzed using the following linear model:Yij=μ+τi+εij,where Yij is the observed value for the j replicate in treatment i; μ is overall mean; τi is fixed effect of treatment i (denoted an unknown parameter); εij is random error; assumed to follow a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance σ2.

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted on the cloud platform of Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China; https://cloud.oebiotech.com/) to examine the relationships between all statistically significant rumen-related parameters (e.g., apparent digestibility, enzyme activity, and morphology) and the top 15 microorganisms. The same analytical procedure was applied to the jejunum and cecum. Data expressed as percentages (e.g., apparent digestibility) were log-transformed prior to analysis to improve normality. Results are presented as correlation matrices, with statistical significance defined at P < 0.05.

Data are expressed as means and standard error of the mean (SEM), with P < 0.05 considered indicative of statistical significance.

The relative abundance of Bacteroides, Bacteroidales_RF16_group, and Clostridia_UCG-014 was also negatively correlated with α-amylase activity (P < 0.05).

This study further analyzed the microbiome phenotypes of the rumen, jejunum, and cecum, focusing on potentially pathogenic, Gram-negative bacteria, and Gram-positive bacteria. In the rumen (Fig. 7A), the M group exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance of Gram-positive bacteria compared with the other groups (P < 0.001), while the relative abundance of Gram-negative bacteria was significantly lower (P < 0.001). In the jejunum (Fig. 7B), the potentially pathogenic bacteria in the M and H groups were significantly higher than those in the CON and L groups (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the relative abundance of Gram-positive bacteria was greater in the CON group than in any allicin-supplemented group (P < 0.05), whereas Gram-negative bacteria showed the opposite pattern (P < 0.05). The CON group showed higher potential pathogenicity in the cecum compared with all other groups (P < 0.05). Discussion

Ammonia nitrogen serves as the most crucial nitrogen source for ruminants, reflecting the dynamic balance between protein degradation and microbial protein synthesis in the rumen. Produced through microbial decomposition of nitrogenous substances, it functions as an intermediate utilized in the formation of microbial protein. The ideal concentration of NH3–N lies between 0.39 and 29 mg/dL (Ran et al., 2024). The latest research demonstrates that allicin effectively inhibits ruminal urease activity and enhances the potential for urea nitrogen utilization, thereby promoting its conversion into microbial biomass (Zhang et al., 2025a). In this study, the M group exhibited the lowest NH3–N levels, indicating that supplementing with 0.75 g/d per head of allicin could effectively facilitate ruminal protein degradation and enhance nitrogen utilization efficiency. This study aligned the findings of Liu et al. (2024) and Tang et al. (2024), suggesting that allicin could effectively elevate NH3–N levels in the rumen of goats. Volatile fatty acids in the rumen are the most important energy source for ruminants, reflecting the efficiency of carbohydrate digestion by microorganisms. Generally, VFA levels show a positive correlation with substrate digestibility, providing approximately 70% of the energy required by ruminants (Cabezas-Garcia et al., 2017). In this study, the M group exhibited a significantly higher TVFA level than the other groups, consistent with the findings of Liu et al. (2024). This suggests that dietary supplementation with 0.75 g/d per head of allicin might provide more energy to goats and indirectly reflect improved digestive efficiency. Moreover, the higher DMD and NDFD in the M group in this study may offer preliminary support for this speculation.

Unexpectedly, this study revealed that acetate, propionate, and butyrate levels were significantly higher in the M group than in the CON group, suggesting that allicin supplementation may have altered rumen fermentation by reshaping the microbial community in goats. The increased propionate concentration in the M group may be attributed to a shift in rumen hydrogen metabolism. Studies have shown that dietary allicin can inhibit methanogenesis by reducing the abundance of methanogens in the rumen. This shift allows more hydrogen to be redirected toward propionate synthesis, leading to its accumulation and an overall increase in propionate concentration (Ma et al., 2016; Martin and Chaudhry, 2024; Tang et al., 2024). Unfortunately, the current study did not further investigate the effect of allicin on methane production and requires further validation. Additionally, the elevated butyrate concentration observed in the M group may result from the fact that approximately 28% acetate is not absorbed in its original form. This residual acetate may have been converted into butyrate by microorganisms via the acetyl-CoA or butyryl-CoA or acetate CoA-transferase pathway (e.g., butyryl-CoA + acetate → butyrate + acetyl-CoA), rather than by synthesizing acetoacetyl-CoA from two acetyl-CoA molecules (Diez-Gonzalez et al., 1999; Hackmann and Firkins, 2015; Kristensen, 2001). Unfortunately, the current study did not directly trace the metabolic flux and requires further validation using techniques like isotope labeling. However, the H group failed to improve propionate levels, as the pungent nature of allicin itself led to a direct reduction in the dry matter intake (DMI) of goats (Yuan et al., 2025).

It has been reported that approximately 50%–85% of VFA are directly absorbed through the rumen epithelium (Aschenbach et al., 2011). However, propionate and butyrate are generally considered more beneficial for supporting the growth and development of animals (Long et al., 2024). Butyrate is the primary energy source for the rumen epithelium. It maintains epithelial health by stimulating epithelial cell proliferation and metabolism, inhibiting apoptosis, and promoting papilla development and wound healing (Liu et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2021). Zhuang et al. (2024) demonstrated that activating the “propionate metabolism” and “butyrate metabolism” pathways in the rumen contributes to both structural development and the maintenance of rumen health. In this study, allicin supplementation improved rumen papilla height, width, density, and muscle layer thickness in goats. These effects are attributed to elevated concentrations of TVFA, particularly propionate and butyrate, which promote rumen development and health. Undoubtedly, these improvements will further improve nutrient digestibility.

As the predominant microbial phyla in the rumen, Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes play crucial roles in the breakdown of starch and fiber and are the primary producers of acetate, propionate, and butyrate (Zhang et al., 2025b). However, the results of this study indicated that dietary allicin supplementation in the diet of Guizhou black goats improved the equilibrium between the two dominant phyla, which may help explain the higher VFA levels detected in the M group.

Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, a major bacterial genus in this study, exhibited its highest relative abundance in the M group. This genus is recognized for its ability to produce carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), including oligosaccharidases, hemicellulases, and cellulases, which play a crucial role in the decomposition and absorption of carbohydrates and proteins (Zhu et al., 2021). Although the regulatory role of F082 remains unclear, relevant studies have shown a significant positive correlation between F082 relative abundance and the concentration of VFA in the rumen, particularly propionate (Mei et al., 2023). Prevotella is widely recognized as a key genus involved in the breakdown of cellulose, hemicellulose, starch, and protein (Bowen et al., 2018). Zhou et al. (2022) believed that Prevotella was positively correlated with the molar proportion of acetate and negatively correlated with the molar proportion of butyrate. In this study, dietary allicin significantly increased the relative abundance of F082. However, Prevotella relative abundance was highest in the L group and lowest in the M group. Additionally, this study demonstrated that allicin significantly decreased the relative abundance of the Lachnospiraceae_ND3007_group within the Lachnospiraceae family, which is known for its role in butyrate production (Meehan and Beiko, 2014). Notably, as a dominant genus in this study, Muribaculaceae was most abundant in the H group and least in the M group. Currently, it is predominantly studied in mice; it harbors enzymes that convert complex polysaccharides and fibers into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Ormerod et al., 2016). Nonetheless, its role in other mammals is poorly understood and requires further investigation. In general, this study demonstrated that supplementing with 0.75 g/d per head of allicin may promote fiber degradation and enhance ruminal TVFA levels by increasing the relative abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group and F082.

Although LEfSe biomarkers reveal progressive microbial shifts with increasing allicin doses, rumen papillae density exhibits a quadratic response, reaching its maximum at the medium dose. This discrepancy reflects both methodological and biological differences. Methodologically, ANOVA captures non-linear host responses, whereas LEfSe identifies taxa that discriminate between treatment groups. Biologically, moderate SCFA production driven by Firmicutes enrichment promotes papillae growth through cyclin D1 (Malhi et al., 2013), while high allicin doses induce acidification and inhibition (Shen et al., 2019). Allicin's dose-dependent microbiota modulation supports this (Li et al., 2023), as seen in ruminant studies (Rabee et al., 2025). Future metabolomic analyses could directly link microbial shifts to host tissue responses.

On the one hand, Proteobacteria are Gram-negative bacteria that mainly include pathogenic species such as Salmonella and Escherichia coli. When their abundance in the rumen exceeds 19%, it suggests that the rumen microecosystem is in a sub-healthy state (Auffret et al., 2017). In this study, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria in L and M groups was significantly higher than in CON and H groups, but remained below the threshold value (< 19%) associated with rumen subhealth. This suggests that allicin does not impair the stability of the rumen microbial system in Guizhou black goats. On the other hand, a puzzling finding in this study was the substantial intergroup variation in allicin's effects on the rumen microbiota. For example, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group showed a non-linear response—decreasing, then increasing, and decreasing again—as allicin supplementation increased. While the present data reveal the occurrence of this phenomenon, its mechanistic basis is not yet clear and should be explored in greater depth in future studies.

Moreover, the increased cellulase and β-cellobiase activities observed in the L and M groups in this study provide additional support for this conclusion. Notably, Liu et al. (2024) confirmed that allicin repairs high-sugar diet-induced epithelial injury and structural disruption in the goat rumen, highlighting its key role in maintaining epithelial integrity and overall rumen health. Taken together, the incorporation of allicin in the diets of Guizhou black goats can improve rumen fermentation patterns and enzyme activities through the reshaping of rumen microbial composition. These changes increase VFA concentrations and energy availability, support rumen development and health, and ultimately enhance nutrient digestibility. Nevertheless, in light of this study's limitations, it is crucial to investigate further the molecular mechanisms through which allicin exerts its effects on rumen health.

The integrity of a healthy intestinal barrier is crucial for preventing the translocation of bacteria, pathogens, and harmful or allergenic substances from the gut into the bloodstream (Wang et al., 2021). An impaired intestinal barrier can cause the epithelial lining to become more permeable (Ma et al., 2023). This study found that dietary allicin significantly increased the expression levels of claudin 1, ZO-1, and claudin 4 proteins in the jejunum, with gene expression of these proteins being most significantly elevated in the M group. These findings suggest that allicin may enhance intestinal barrier function by upregulating both the protein and relative mRNA expression of key markers associated with intestinal permeability. Actinobacteriota have been shown to play a role in immune regulation and vitamin synthesis, thereby promoting gut development, barrier maturation, and nutrient absorption (Khalil et al., 2022; Sanjorjo et al., 2023). Moreover, it has been demonstrated that intestinal bacteria, particularly Gram-negative bacteria species, activate intestinal dendritic cells, which subsequently stimulate immunoglobulin A (IgA) secretion from mucosal plasma cells—an essential mechanism for establishing and maintaining the intestinal barrier (Du et al., 2023). In the present study, the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota and the level of Gram-negative bacteria were significantly increased in the M and H groups, which means that allicin can further promote intestinal development and health. However, it is important to note that these taxa may harbor opportunistic pathogens, such as certain species within Actinobacteriota (e.g., Corynebacterium) or Gram-negative bacteria groups (e.g., Escherichia-Shigella and Acinetobacter), which could proliferate and cause issues like inflammation or infection in susceptible animals (Fan et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2022). Therefore, in dietary interventions like allicin supplementation, ongoing monitoring of microbial composition through techniques such as 16S rRNA sequencing, metagenomics, or host immune markers (e.g., inflammatory cytokines) is recommended to detect potential dysbiosis early and ensure sustained benefits without adverse effects (Lichtman et al., 2015).

Muribaculaceae (formerly known as S24-7) has been reported as a promising candidate for next-generation probiotics due to its ability to produce SCFAs by fermenting both endogenous polysaccharides (mucin glycans) and exogenous sources (dietary fiber), which in turn supply energy to epithelial cells and strengthen intestinal barrier function (Lagkouvardos et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2024). Moreover, the relative abundance of Treponema was significantly positively correlated with SCFAs levels (Angelakis et al., 2019). In contrast, Campylobacter (a member of the phylum Campylobacterota) induces inflammation by releasing proinflammatory cytokines (such as interleukin-8 [IL-8]) and cleaving occludin via the serine protease HtrA, thereby disrupting tight junctions and increasing intestinal permeability (Bücker et al., 2018). Prevotella may also compromise intestinal barrier integrity indirectly by promoting intestinal inflammation (Iljazovic et al., 2021). In this study, the relative abundances of Muribaculaceae, Campylobacter, and Prevotella were significantly increased in M and H groups compared with those in group CON. Based on the findings of this study, when Muribaculaceae is present in absolute high abundance, its influence on the intestinal environment is more dominant than that of Campylobacter and Prevotella. In addition, relevant studies demonstrate that Bacteroides, Alistipes, Prevotella, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003, and Treponema can influence intestinal barrier integrity, but their effects are highly variable and species-specific. Different species within the same genus may exert entirely contrasting effects (Liu et al., 2023; Nie et al., 2024; Parker et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). Regrettably, this study does not include further analysis or exploration of this aspect.

In this study, the results indicated that the abundance of Muribaculaceae, Prevotella, and Bacteroides was higher in M and H groups in the jejunum, suggesting increased production of SCFAs, which are beneficial for nutrient absorption and intestinal epithelial development. As expected, in the jejunum, the M group had the greatest villus height, and the M and H groups had the highest villus density. These morphological characteristics are considered favorable for nutrient absorption (Li et al., 2021). Notably, dietary allicin increased trypsin activity, further confirming its potential to enhance the digestion and absorption of CP. Moreover, the Christensenellaceae_R-7_group has been reported to contribute to SCFA production, while also exerting anti-inflammatory effects and promoting intestinal health (Wang et al., 2023a,b). Unexpectedly, in this study, both Christensenellaceae_R-7_group and colony relative abundance were significantly lower in the M and H groups than in the CON group, while Proteobacteria, Campilobacterota, and Campylobacter relative abundance showed an opposite increase. This may help explain the elevated potential pathogenicity seen in the jejunum of the M and L groups. Additionally, the observed increase in potential pathogenicity may also be closely linked to the dual (both beneficial and detrimental) effects of Bacteroides, Alistipes, Prevotella, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003, and Treponema relative abundance. These results appear to contradict the known physiological characteristics and potential beneficial effects of allicin.

However, not all Gram-negative bacteria taxa are pathogenic; many are commensal or beneficial, producing SCFAs that energize enterocytes, reduce inflammation, and fortify epithelial integrity. For instance, LEfSe analysis highlights Muribaculaceae, Bacteroides, and Prevotella relative abundance—mostly Gram-negative bacteria—as biomarkers in the M and H groups, which ferment carbohydrates to produce SCFAs like butyrate, fueling epithelial cells and reinforcing barrier integrity via histone deacetylation and G-protein-coupled receptor activation (He et al., 2023). Similarly, certain Proteobacteria (e.g., non-pathogenic Succinivibrionaceae) contribute to SCFA production and immune modulation, distinguishing them from opportunistic pathogens like Escherichia coli (Belanche et al., 2021). Mechanistically, these Gram-negative bacteria commensals activate dendritic cells to stimulate IgA secretion, bolstering mucosal defense without compromising permeability (Ma et al., 2022). Thus, allicin's selective modulation favors protective over pathogenic Gram-negative bacteria populations, harmonizing microbiota shifts with barrier enhancement in Guizhou black goats.

Notably, while this study demonstrates short-term benefits of allicin supplementation, potential long-term safety issues should be considered, particularly at high doses. Allicin exhibits dose-dependent cytotoxicity; low to moderate doses are generally safe, but high doses may induce hepatotoxicity, oxidative stress, hemolysis, or gastrointestinal disturbances in animals, as evidenced by repeated-dose toxicity studies in rodents and other species (El-Saadony et al., 2024; El-Saber Batiha et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2022). In ruminants like goats, chronic high-dose exposure could exacerbate these risks, potentially leading to cellular damage or altered microbial stability over time. Therefore, future research should evaluate long-term effects through extended feeding trials and monitor biomarkers of toxicity to ensure safe application in livestock diets.

The cecum, as the second-largest site of microbial fermentation in ruminants following the rumen, plays a vital role in residual fermentation, reabsorption of nutrients, immune system support, and sustaining microecological balance. In the present study, dietary allicin increased cellulase activity and the relative abundance of Firmicutes, suggesting that allicin may enhance fiber degradation and fermentation in the cecum, thereby improving the production of SCFAs and increasing the energy supply for goats. Desulfobacterota is reported to utilize organic substrates or hydrogen (H2) as electron donors to reduce sulfate to hydrogen sulfide (H2S). When present in excess, H2S is toxic to the mucosa and disrupts mitochondrial function and cellular signaling (Munteanu et al., 2024). This study found that the abundance of Desulfobacterota was significantly reduced in the M group compared with the CON group. Similarly, all allicin-treated groups showed a reduction in Proteobacteria abundance, suggesting that allicin may contribute to improved hindgut health.

The relative abundance of [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group was significantly increased in M and H groups. This genus is known to be involved in bile acid metabolism and the regulation of lipid balance. A high relative abundance of [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group can enhance sphingosine conversion, support host lipid homeostasis, and alleviate dyslipidemia (Guo et al., 2023). In addition, this genus is capable of producing SCFAs, which contribute to improve nutrient absorption, energy metabolism, and barrier repair in the intestinal epithelium (Mukherjee et al., 2020). Notably, equipped with a highly specialized CAZyme system, Monoglobus effectively degrades pectin, the primary structural polysaccharide in plant cell walls. The resulting metabolites serve as substrates for other gut microbes, thereby indirectly facilitating SCFA synthesis. This cross-microbial collaboration is beneficial to maintaining intestinal microecological homeostasis, regulating immune responses, and inhibiting the growth of pathogens, thereby helping to reduce inflammation and strengthen barrier function (Kim et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2024). Furthermore, Clostridia UCG-014 is a butyrate-producing bacterium that synthesizes SCFAs, particularly butyrate, by degrading non-fermentable carbohydrates. It also stimulates intestinal immune cells to promote IgA secretion, thereby enhancing mucosal immune barrier function and reducing the production of inflammatory factors (Dai et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2020). Therefore, in this study, allicin may promote the production of SCFAs by increasing the relative abundance of Monoglobus, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, and Clostridia_UCG-014, thereby promoting intestinal development, enhancing barrier function, and contributing to the maintenance of intestinal health.

Conclusion

Under the conditions of this study, the results showed that allicin can alter rumen fermentation patterns and improve gastrointestinal morphological parameters by enhancing the activity of gastrointestinal enzymes and restructuring the microbial composition of the gastrointestinal tract in Guizhou black goats. These changes collectively promote nutrient utilization and absorption, ultimately improving nutrient digestion. Additionally, allicin enhances gastrointestinal barrier function and promotes gastrointestinal health by improving the balance between beneficial and harmful microorganisms in the gastrointestinal tract. Finally, based on the results of this study, optimal improvement was observed when allicin was supplemented at a rate of 0.75 g/d per head in the diet of Guizhou black goats. For practical feed formulation, a safety margin of 0.5 to 0.8 g/d per head is recommended to account for variations in animal responses, environmental factors, and formulation precision. Although this study is the first to reveal the improvement effects of allicin on the gastrointestinal health of Guizhou black goats, it is not without limitations. Future research should unravel the molecular mechanisms of allicin in the goat gastrointestinal tract, with particular focus on the rumen and small intestine.
