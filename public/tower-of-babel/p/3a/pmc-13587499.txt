PD-L1 and PD-L2 regulate in cell-autonomous and N-glycosylation-dependent manners the pro-tumorigenic characteristics and functions of human cancer-associated fibroblasts
Authors: Alaa Abu Raiya, Tsipi Meshel, Iris Kamer, Christina Lipin, Raneen Tarabe, Amit Kessel, Tamar Horvitz, Linor Rubinstein-Achiasaf, Dina Morein, Jair Bar, Adit Ben-Baruch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumor progression is regulated by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNFα, IL-1β, IFNγ) and acquired immunity (such as inhibitory immune checkpoints: PD-L1, PD-L2). Here, we determined the interactions between these elements by analyzing the impact of pro-inflammatory cytokines on the proportions of PD-L1 + PD-L2-expressing CAFs; the cell-autonomous regulation of pro-tumorigenic characteristics and functions of CAFs by PD-L1/PD-L2; and the control of PD-L1/PD-L2 activities in CAFs by their N-glycosylation.MethodsCAFs were obtained from breast and lung cancer patients (BC-CAFs, Lung-CAFs). PD-L1/PD-L2 surface and whole-cell expression were determined by flow cytometry and Western blot, respectively. CAFs were transduced to express WT-PD-L1, WT-PD-L2, WT-PD-L1 + WT-PD-L2, PD-L1/PD-L2 N-glycosylation mutants or vector controls, followed by determination of the pro-tumorigenic factors osteopontin (qRT-PCR) and CXCL8 (IL-8)/CCL2 (MCP-1)(ELISA). The effects of factors produced by CAFs expressing WT-PD-L1/WT-PD-L2/their N-glycosylation mutants on tumor cell migration were analyzed by wound-healing assays.ResultsTNFα + IL-1β potently increased the expression of CXCL8, whereas IFNγ stimulation up-regulated the proportions of BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs expressing PD-L1 + PD-L2 at cell surface. Combined TNFα + IL-1β + IFNγ stimulation has prominently increased intracellular pools of N-glycosylated PD-L1 and PD-L2, in BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs. WT-PD-L1 induced in cell-autonomous manners pro-tumorigenic characteristics and functions in BC-CAFs: elevated the expression of osteopontin, CXCL8 and CCL2 and induced the production of factors that promoted tumor cell migration. WT-PD-L2 also acted in a cell-autonomous manner in BC-CAFs, generally down-regulating these phenotypes and activities. When co-expressed in BC-CAFs, the cancer-supporting effects of WT-PD-L1 dominated the anti-tumorigenic impacts of WT-PD-L2. The cell-autonomous functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2 were regulated, in context- and function-dependent manners, by their N-glycosylation sites, primarily N35/N192 in PD-L1 and N37/N163 in PD-L2.ConclusionsChronic inflammation can up-regulate the proportions of PD-L1 + PD-L2 expressing CAFs. PD-L1 and PD-L2 act in cell-autonomous manners to control the pro-tumorigenic characteristics and functions of CAFs, with dominance of the tumor-promoting effects of PD-L1 over the tumor-inhibiting roles of PD-L2. The cell-intrinsic functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in CAFs depended on their N-glycosylation. These observations emphasize the need to consider the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in stromal cells when designing treatments with immune checkpoint blockades in patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01531-3.

Introduction

Immunotherapies have opened a new era in cancer treatment, primarily targeting the axis of the inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules PD-L1/PD-1 and CTLA-4. Immune checkpoint blockades (ICBs) have given rise to good response rates in a certain percentage of the patients in melanoma and have been introduced to other types of cancer (Park and Skalhegg 2025; Lin et al. 2025), including breast cancer (Jin et al. 2024) and lung adenocarcinoma (Zhou et al. 2024). However, eventually only a limited number of patients benefit from ICB treatments, raising the need to better understand the regulation and activity of inhibitory immune checkpoints in cancer (Park and Skalhegg 2025; Lin et al. 2025).

In this respect, unlike PD-L1 that is routinely targeted in the clinic, therapies aimed at inhibition of the second PD-1 ligand, PD-L2, are not in use; PD-L2 also has been much less frequently addressed in research. PD-L2 has a higher affinity to PD-1 than PD-L1, and is generally connected to poor prognosis in cancer due to its inhibitory effects on immune activities (Yang et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023). However, the expression of PD-L2 in clinical samples is usually ignored prior to patient stratification for therapy, and PD-L1 expression is generally determined only in tumor cells and immune cells. The dual expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in the same sample is also not considered when therapy protocols are designed.

These limitations suggest that studies addressing the expression and functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2 need to be extended to other cell types that are found at the tumor microenvironment (TME), including stromal cells and particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (Lan et al. 2025; Guo and Xu 2024). CAFs were identified as a heterogenous cell population in tumors, often gaining metastasis-promoting effects through direct interactions with the cancer cells or via the release of soluble factors that eventually facilitate tumor cell invasion and dissemination (Lan et al. 2025; Guo and Xu 2024). However, the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 by CAFs is minimally considered when stratifying patients for treatment with ICBs targeting the PD-1-relevant axis (Doroshow et al. 2021).

Another level of activity of PD-L1 and PD-L2 that is not well-addressed is their ability to act in cell-autonomous manners, regulating pro-metastatic functions of cells that express them. Such cell-intrinsic PD-L1 activities were described in cancer cells in several papers (Deng et al. 2023), including our studies showing that breast tumor cell-expressed PD-L1 leads through STAT1/STAT3 activation to increased cancer cell invasion, proliferation and release of pro-metastatic factors in vitro (Erlichman et al. 2022, 2023); also, we found that PD-L1 expression in breast cancer cells has led to elevated tumor growth and metastasis in vivo, in cell-intrinsic and T cell-independent manners (Erlichman et al. 2022). Our findings also indicated that PD-L1 cell-autonomous pro-metastatic effects depend on its glycosylation at all four N-glycosylation sites (N35, N192, N200, N219) (Benicky et al. 2021); in vivo, breast tumor cells that expressed the N35A-PD-L1 and N219A-PD-L1 mutants demonstrated reduced tumor growth and metastatic abilities compared to their WT-PD-L1 counterpart (Erlichman et al. 2023).

In parallel, recent findings described the ability of PD-L2 to promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in tumor cells, and to reduce the binding affinity of cetuximab to epidermal growth factor receptors (EGFR) (Ren et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2021). These PD-L2 functions demonstrate intrinsic pro-tumorigenic effects for PD-L2 in cancer cells; the activity of PD-L2 at the cetuximab level depended on its N-glycosylation, with no indication of specific sites involved (Xu et al. 2021) (PD-L2 N-glycosylation sites are N37, N64, N157, N163, N189; (Wang et al. 2017)).

Although cell-intrinsic functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2 were reported in cancer cells, they have not been well-investigated in CAFs. Several studies have identified the expression of mainly PD-L1 but also of PD-L2 by CAFs, including breast cancer and lung adenocarcinoma; however, a general conclusion was not reached about their connection to immune-suppression and to patient survival (Yoshikawa et al. 2021; Costa et al. 2018; Heenatigala Palliyage et al. 2023; Teramoto et al. 2019; Cao et al. 2025). Moreover, many regulatory aspects of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in human CAFs remain unsolved: Does the pro-inflammatory nature of the TME (Xie et al. 2025) regulate the incidence of PD-L1 + PD-L2-expressing CAFs and PD-L1/PD-L2 N-glycosylation? Do PD-L1 and PD-L2 exert cell-autonomous functions in CAFs, and of what type? If so, are these functions regulated by PD-L1 and PD-L2 N-glycosylation?

To answer these questions, the current study was aimed at getting improved insights into the regulation and functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2, each alone and together, in CAFs. We began this study by determining the way pro-inflammatory cytokines regulate CAF functions and the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 by CAFs, at the cell membrane and intracellularly. Specifically, our study included CAFs that were obtained from breast cancer patients (BC-CAFs) and lung adenocarcinoma patients (Lung-CAFs), stimulated by pro-metastatic factors acting in chronic tumor inflammation (Xie et al. 2025): tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) (Wang et al. 2022; Qodir et al. 2025; Shi and Hu 2023) and interleukin 1β (IL-1β) (Malkova et al. 2023; Tulotta and Ottewell 2018; Garon et al. 2020). In view of the fact that TNFα and IL-1β are both expressed in breast and lung tumors (Soria et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2021), the CAFs were stimulated by the two cytokines together. In parallel, we stimulated the cells by interferon γ (IFNγ), a cytokine whose expression was noted in many cancer types, including breast cancer (Garcia-Tunon et al. 2007; Lee et al. 2020) and lung cancer (Gao et al. 2018; Karachaliou et al. 2018). IFNγ is of particular interest in the context of this study, in view of its ability to induce anti-tumor immune activities under certain conditions, but to have pro-inflammatory functions and to up-regulate PD-L1 expression and thus induce immune suppression, in others (Liu 2025; Boehmer and Zanoni 2025; Mandai et al. 2016). Then, our analyses were extended to determine whether PD-L1 and PD-L2 regulate in cell-autonomous manners the pro-metastatic functions of BC-CAFs, and if these functions are controlled by N-linked glycosylation of PD-L1 or PD-L2.

Our findings indicate that pro-inflammatory cytokines up-regulate the ability of BC-CAFs to express tumor-promoting molecules: TNFα + IL-1β stimulation has led to increased production of the inflammatory, angiogenic and pro-metastatic chemokine CXCL8 (Xavier et al. 2025; Mishra et al. 2021), and IFNγ stimulation has given rise to elevated proportions of BC-CAFs as well as Lung-CAFs expressing PD-L1 + PD-L2 at the cell membrane. In both BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs, the intracellular content of N-glycosylated PD-L1 and PD-L2 was highly elevated by combined stimulation by TNFα + IL-1β + IFNγ. PD-L1 and PD-L2 were found to have cell-autonomous functions, however regulating pro-metastatic functions of BC-CAFs in opposing manners: elevated by PD-L1 and down-regulated by PD-L2. In BC-CAFs expressing both PD-L1 and PD-L2, the tumor-supporting functions of PD-L1 dominated the anti-tumor activities of PD-L1. These regulatory events by PD-L1 and PD-L2 depended on their N-glycosylation, in cell-, target- and glycosylation site-specific manners.

The observations made in this study have potential clinical implications by demonstrating active roles for CAF-expressed PD-L1 and PD-L2 in regulating tumor-supporting phenotypes and functions of CAFs. They provide evidence for another cellular layer that needs to be considered when the status of inhibitory immune checkpoints is determined prior to patient treatment with ICBs. We suggest that improved prediction of ICB success in therapy would require determination of PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression in CAFs, in parallel to their analysis in cancer cells and immune cells, especially when the tumors exhibit characteristics of chronic inflammation.

Methods

Cell cultures

In this study we used two breast patient-derived CAF isolates (BC-CAFs) that were described in our published report, in which the fibroblastic origin of the cells was validated (Katanov et al. 2015). Briefly, BC-CAF-1 cells (Lab designation: HK) were derived from lung metastasis of a breast cancer patient. BC-CAF-2 cells (ZB) were obtained from a primary breast tumor. BC-CAF-1 cells were cultured in growth medium containing DMEM medium (4.5 g/liter glucose), supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin (PSA), 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% minimum essential medium nonessential amino acids (MEM-NEAA) and 60 µM β-mercaptoethanol (2ME). BC-CAF-2 cells were grown in the same medium, without MEM-NEAA and 2ME. All materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO) or from Biological Industries (Beit Ha'emek, Israel).

Four lung patient-derived CAFs (Lung-CAFs) were isolated in a similar manner from primary tumors of patients. Lung-CAF-1 (HERI) and Lung-CAF-2 (SABREH) were obtained from non-smokers; Lung-CAF-3 (SHRA) and Lung-CAF-4 (HASAV) were derived from smokers. Validation of the fibroblastic source of the four Lung-CAF isolates is demonstrated in Figure S1. The cells were grown in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine, 1% PSA and 1% MEM-NEAA.

All patients provided written informed consent for the use of tumor specimens for research. This study was approved by the Sheba Medical Center Ethics Committee (approvals #2019-SMC and #8990–11-SMC).

MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cells (obtained from ATCC) were grown in DMEM medium, supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine and 1% PSA (all materials as above).

Cell stimulation by cytokines

BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs were stimulated for 96 h with recombinant human cytokines (all from PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ) including: TNFα (50 ng/ml; #300-01A), IL-1β (0.5 ng/ml; #200-01B) and/or IFNγ (20 ng/ml; #300–02) in growth medium. Cytokine concentrations were selected based on published reports and on titration analyses performed in the lab. In all procedures, control cells were treated with the vehicle of the cytokines (0.1% bovine serum albumin diluted in double distilled water).

Generating cells expressing WT-PD-L1/WT-PD-L2 or their glycosylation-deficient mutants

BC-CAFs that over-express WT-PD-L1/WT-PD-L2 or their glycosylation-deficient mutants were generated in parallel to control vector cells (“Vector” cells) that underwent a similar procedure with a sham vector. The cells have undergone retroviral infections with constructs that were generated using the primers detailed in Table S1 (for PD-L1) and Table S2 (for PD-L2). PD-L1 constructs were expressed on pQCXIN vector (https://www.addgene.org #631,514), and PD-L2 constructs on pQCXIH vector (https://www.addgene.org #631,516). The integrity of all constructs and the successful incorporation of the desired mutations were confirmed by DNA sequencing. The infection stage was performed as described before (Erlichman et al. 2022, 2023).

In this study, constructs of WT-PD-L1 and five PD-L1 N-glycosylation-deficient mutants were generated: four single-amino acid mutants (N35A, N192A, N200A, N219A; (Benicky et al. 2021)) and a variant carrying mutations at all four sites (Nx4). They were generated as previously described (Erlichman et al. 2022, 2023), with a change from the pQCXIP vector to pQCXIN in the current study.

In parallel, constructs of WT-PD-L2 and six PD-L2 N-glycosylation-deficient mutants were generated: five single-amino acid mutants (N37A, N64A, N157A, N163A, N189A; (Wang et al. 2017)) and a variant carrying mutations at all five sites (Nx5). To generate the constructs, the PDL2 gene (NM_025239.4) was cloned from MDA-MB-231 cells via PCR amplification using cDNA synthesized from total RNA.

ELISA analyses

BC-CAFs that were exposed to cytokines or vehicle were plated in equal numbers for 96 h, and PD-L1/PD-L2-transduced BC-CAFs for 48 h. Then, cell supernatants were collected, clarified by centrifugation, and used for determination of human CXCL8 and human CCL2 levels by ELISA. Samples were analyzed in each experiment in duplicates, and in series of dilutions, used to calculate protein concentration based on standard curves with recombinant human chemokines, at linear range of absorption. The following antibodies and recombinant proteins were used (all from PeproTech); CXCL8—Coating antibodies: #500-P28; Detecting antibodies: #500-P28BT; CXCL8: #200–08. CCL2—Coating antibodies: #500-P34; Detecting antibodies: #500-P34BT; CCL2: #300–04. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin (#016–030–084, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA) and substrate TMB Soluble Reagent (High Sensitivity) (#TM4500, ScyTek Laboratories, Logan, UT) were added; the reaction was stopped by addition of 0.18 M H2SO4 and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Flow cytometry analyses

To determine PD-L1 cell surface expression, CAFs were stained with mouse anti-human PD-L1 antibody (#14–5983-82; eBioscience, San Diego, CA) or its isotype control (#400102; BioLegend, San Diego, CA), followed by FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (#115–095-003; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). The membrane expression of PD-L2 was determined by APC-conjugated mouse anti-human PD-L2 (#345508; BioLegend), using APC-conjugated isotype as control (#400122; BioLegend). To determine the incidence of cells co-expressing PD-L1 + PD-L2, the cells were stained first for PD-L1 and then for PD-L2.

In separate analyses, vimentin expression was determined by rat anti-human antibodies (#MAB2105, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) and parallel staining was performed with its isotype control (#400501; BioLegend). This step was followed by FITC-conjugated goat anti-rat antibodies (#112–095-003; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories).

In all experiments, the cells were incubated with the above antibodies/isotype controls for 30–45 min (based on previous kinetics analyses). Samples were collected by flow cytometer S100EXi (Stratagim, San Jose, CA) and were analyzed by the Flow Jo V10.0 software. Flow cytometry analyses were performed on single viable cells, selected based on live/dead cell gating (Fig. S2). Isotype-stained cells were used to set axes, to determine percent positive PD-L1 + PD-L2 (Fig. S3). The proportion of PD-L1 + PD-L2 cells in isotype-stained cells (Fig. S3; minimal percentage) was subtracted from the values obtained in antibody-stained cells (Fig. S3). The net value of PD-L1 + PD-L2-expressing cells is presented in the relevant Figures of the paper.

Western blot (WB) analyses

CAFs were lysed in RIPA buffer, and WB analyses were performed using rabbit antibodies to the following proteins, all from Cell Signaling Technology (CST; Danvers, MA), unless otherwise indicated: Human PD-L1 (#51296S); human PD-L2 (#82723S); P-p65 (#3033S); T-p65 (#8242S); P-STAT1 (#9167S); T-STAT1 (#9172S); P-STAT3 (#9134T), T-STAT3 (#4904T). Calnexin (#2679S) and GAPDH (#ab9485; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) served as loading controls. The membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (#111–035-003; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). In parallel, detection of α smooth muscle actin (αSMA) was performed using anti-αSMA antibodies (#ab7817-100; Abcam), and membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (#115–035-003; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). Then, proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence analysis (#WBLUR0500; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and visualized with Amersham ImageQuant 800 (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK).

When indicated, in experiments involving cytokine-stimulated cells, the cells were treated by kifunensine (50 μM; #K1140; Sigma-Aldrich) and/or swainsonine (50 μM; #S8195; Sigma-Aldrich); the inhibitors were added two hours before cytokine stimulation and maintained throughout the stimulation period (for 96 h). In cells transduced by WT-PD-L1, WT-PD-L2 or both together, the inhibitors were added during cell growth for 48 h. Then, regular WB procedures were performed.

Quantitative real-time PCR analyses (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRI-Reagent (#T9424, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1-Bromo-3-chloropropane (#B62404, Sigma-Aldrich). qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (#95047, Quantabio, Beverly, MA) containing the MMLV reverse transcriptase and ribonuclease inhibitor protein was used to generate first-strand cDNA from RNA samples. cDNA targets were quantified by CFX Connect Real Time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) in Hard-Shell PCR 96-well, thin wall plates (#HSP9601, Bio-Rad). iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (#1725124, Bio-Rad) was used to detect transcripts, according to manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used for detection of osteopontin mRNA were as follows (5'−3'): Sense: AGGCATCACCTGTGCCATAC; Anti-sense: TGGTTTCTTCAGAGGACACAGC. Data were normalized to the housekeeping gene ribosomal protein S9 (RS9). Dissociation curves for each primer set indicated a single product, and “no-template” controls were negative after 40 cycles. Quantification was performed by the ΔΔCq technique and normalized to RS9 in the same sample; then, the average of osteopontin expression levels in Vector cells were given the value of 1 and the levels of osteopontin levels in cells expressing WT-PD-L1, WT-PD-L2 and WT-PD-L1 + WT-PD-L2 were normalized to this value. In experiments with PD-L1 and PD-L2 N-glycosylation mutants, values obtained in the mutants were calculated relatively to the average value in cells expressing the corresponding WT proteins (after normalization of values in cells transduced with the WT proteins to those of vector-infected cells).

Tumor cell migration

Tumor cell migration in the presence of conditioned media (CM) of CAFs was determined in wound-healing assays using the IncuCyte® Live-Cell Imaging system. To this end, CAFs were cultured for 48 h in growth medium containing 0.5% FBS, to generate CM. CM were collected and concentrated fivefold using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units with a 3 kDa molecular weight (MW) cutoff (Merck Millipore, #UFC900324).

In parallel, MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells were seeded into IncuCyte® ImageLock 96-well plates (#4379, Essen BioScience, Ann Arbor, MI) for 48 h. Then, uniform scratches were generated in confluent cell monolayers, followed by the addition of concentrated CM derived from CAFs. Cell migration and wound closure were monitored for 72 h using the IncuCyte® system, with images acquired from two fields per well, every hour throughout the experiment.

Relative wound density was quantified using the integrated IncuCyte® analysis software. Briefly, wound regions were automatically identified, and analysis parameters were optimized to distinguish cells from background and determine cell confluency in both wounded and non-wounded regions. Wound confluency measurements obtained from the two imaged fields were averaged to generate a single value for each well. Data were exported and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. Results are presented as average ± SD of wells from the same experimental condition.

Computational prediction of structures and membrane orientations of PD-L1 and PD-L2

PD-L1 and PD-L2 structures were predicted with the artificial intelligence software AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al. 2024). The two structures are embedded inside a lipid bilayer, with their N-termini, containing the two Ig-like domains, protruding to the extracellular side of the membrane, and their C-termini protruding to the cytoplasmic side. The exact orientations of the two proteins inside the bilayer were predicted by the PPM web server (Lomize et al. 2012).

Statistical analyses

In each experiment type, the data are from a representative experiment of at least three independent biological repeats. When appropriate, averages ± standard deviations (SD) are presented. Statistical analyses were performed by two-tailed unpaired Student's t-tests. Values of p ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Pro-inflammatory cytokines regulate the pro-metastatic properties of BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs

In this study we explored two isolates of CAFs from breast patient tumors, termed BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2, whose fibroblastic phenotype was validated in our published study (Katanov et al. 2015). First, we determined the impact of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNFα + IL-1β and IFNγ on the production of the pro-metastatic chemokine CXCL8. The data of Fig. 1A demonstrate that stimulation of both BC-CAF isolates by TNFα + IL-1β has led to robust increase in the production of CXCL8, whereas IFNγ did not affect the ability of the two BC-CAFs isolates to express the chemokine (Fig. 1A1, A2).Fig. 1Pro-inflammatory cytokines up-regulate the release of CXCL8 by BC-CAFs and lead to an intensified CAF phenotype in BC-CAFs. Following up on previous studies demonstrating that pro-inflammatory cytokines can modify the morphology of stromal cells (Liubomirski et al. 2019; Rubinstein-Achiasaf et al. 2021), we stimulated BC-CAFs by TNFα + IL-1β, IFNγ alone and the three cytokines together and evaluated the cellular structures of BC-CAF. As illustrated in Figs. 1B1 and 1B2, stimulation of both BC-CAF isolates with TNFα + IL-1β has given rise to a more elongated morphology of the cells and formation of cell bundles. Stimulation by IFNγ has not led to notable alterations in CAF morphologies; yet, following exposure to combined stimulation with TNFα + IL-1β + IFNγ, BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2 cells acquired an intensified CAF phenotype, characterized by extremely narrow and elongated cell extensions, accompanied by relatively small cell bodies (Fig. 1B1, B2).

Next, we determined the proportion of BC-CAFs expressing PD-L1 + PD-L2 at the cell membrane, following stimulation by the pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 2A1, A2; See gating strategies in Fig. S2, and more details on staining by isotypes/antibodies in Fig. S3). These analyses demonstrated that in vehicle-treated cells, a substantial proportion of BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2 expressed PD-L2 endogenously, and only a minor subset expressed basal levels of PD-L1. Stimulation by TNFα + IL-1β increased only to a small degree the proportions of PD-L1 + PD-L2-expressing BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2. However, IFNγ stimulation has led to a robust increase in the PD-L1 + PD-L2-expressing subset in both BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2 through up-regulation of mainly PD-L1 but also PD-L2; the effects of IFNγ were further increased in BC-CAF-2 by concomitant exposure to TNFα + IL-1β (Fig. 2A1, A2).

The findings obtained with BC-CAFs led us to determine whether these effects could be generalized to CAFs derived from other tumor types. To determine this aspect, similar analyses were performed in four different CAF isolates obtained from lung adenocarcinoma patients (Lung-CAFs); their fibroblastic phenotype was validated as indicated in Figure S1. These studies demonstrated that in general, the pro-inflammatory cytokines had similar effects in Lung-CAFs as in BC-CAFs.

Specifically, all four Lung-CAF isolates responded vigorously to the combined treatment of TNFα + IL-1β + IFNγ, giving rise to an intensified CAF phenotype, manifested by elongated cell extensions and confined cell bodies (Fig. S4). In parallel, we noted that similar to BC-CAFs (Fig. 2A1, A2), Lung-CAFs constitutively expressed more PD-L2 than PD-L1; TNFα + IL-1β stimulation has given rise to only a modest increase or did not affect the levels of PD-L1 + PD-L2-exressing Lung-CAFs (Fig. 2B1-B4). As in BC-CAFs (Fig. 2A1, A2), IFNγ has greatly increased in the four Lung-CAFs isolates the proportions of cells expressing PD-L1 + PD-L2 at the cell membrane (Fig. 2B1-B4); this elevation resulted mainly from increased expression of PD-L1 but to some extent also of PD-L2 (Fig. 2B1-B4). Of note, the proportions of PD-L1 + PD-L2-expressing Lung-CAFs were elevated following TNFα + IL-1β + IFNγ stimulation, as in BC-CAFs (Fig. 2A1, A2) but they were lower than after Lung-CAF stimulation with IFNγ alone (Fig. 2B1-B4).

Together, our findings illustrate the ability of pro-inflammatory cytokines to reshape CAFs into an intensified fibroblast phenotype and in parallel to elevate the expression of pro-tumorigenic molecules (CXCL8; PD-L1 + PD-L2) by CAFs, in a generalized manner that accounts for BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs, alike.

PD-L1 and PD-L2 are N-glycosylated in BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs, and their intracellular pools are tightly regulated by pro-inflammatory stimulation

In view of previous findings demonstrating that N-glycosylation of PD-L1 and PD-L2 regulates their functions (Erlichman et al. 2023; Benicky et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2017), we determined whether PD-L1 and PD-L2 are N-glycosylated in BC-CAFs, and if their intracellular pools are regulated by stimulation with pro-inflammatory cytokines.

To address these aspects, we have analyzed the whole-cell content (containing mainly intracellular proteins) of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2 by WB analyses (Fig. 3A1, 3A2, respectively). Generally, in line with the regulation of PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression at the cell membrane, TNFα + IL-1β stimulation has only moderately increased (or did not elevate), the intracellular levels of PD-L1 and PD-L2, whereas IFNγ stimulation has given rise to more pronounced elevation in the intracellular amount of PD-L1 and PD-L2 (Fig. 3A). The elevated levels of intracellular PD-L1 and PD-L2 obtained by cytokine stimulation have enabled us to clearly assess the MW of the two proteins. The predicted MWs of mature PD-L1 and PD-L2, without post-translational modifications (PTMs) are ~ 33 kDa for PD-L1 (UniProt: https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/Q9NZQ7/entry) and ~ 30 kDa for PD-L2 (UniProt: https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/A4GW21/entry). However, the data in Figs. 3A1 and A2 indicate that the MWs of endogenous PD-L1, as well as endogenous PD-L2 are dispersed in a large range of MWs in both BC-CAFs, each reaching > 40 kDa (Fig. 3A1, A2).

This extended dispersion of PD-L1 and PD-L2 MWs testifies for PTM processes taking place in the proteins, possibly their N-glycosylation. Indeed, treating the cells with swainsonine ("S" in the Figure) and particularly with kifunensine ("K"), inhibitors of α-mannosidases that participate in the N-glycosylation process (Li et al. 2021), has markedly reduced the MW of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in the two BC-CAF isolates (Fig. 3A1, A2). Of interest is the fact that we could identify a high MW form of PD-L2 (over 53 kDa) that was resistant to the treatment with kifunensine and/or swainsonine, indicating that it carries a different PTM than N-glycosylation.

PD-L1 and PD-L2 regulate in cell-autonomous manners the pro-metastatic characteristics and functions of BC-CAFs

Importantly, cell-intrinsic roles of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in CAFs are not well-identified.

The findings of Figs. 5A-5C demonstrate the effects of WT-PD-L1, WT-PD-L2 and WT-PD-L1 +WT-PD-L2 on the expression of osteopontin (at the mRNA, due to insufficiently reliable tools for protein analysis), CXCL8 and CCL2 (analyzed by ELISA). As a general observation, the expression of WT-PD-L1 has given rise to elevated levels of the three pro-metastatic molecules in both BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2: Osteopontin, CXCL8 and CCL2 (Fig. 5A-5C). These findings suggest that PD-L1 acts in a cell-autonomous manner to promote the pro-tumoral potential of BC-CAFs. This possibility was supported by the fact that CM of BC-CAF-2 cells significantly elevated the migration of breast tumor cells in wound-healing assays, compared to CM obtained from vector-transduced cells (Fig. 5D1), indicating that PD-L1 expression in BC-CAFs promotes their ability to produce factors elevating tumor cell motility.

In parallel, PD-L2 was found to also act in a cell-autonomous manner, however generally leading to an opposite effect than PD-L1, namely down-regulating osteopontin, CXCL8 and CCL2 by the cells (Fig. 5A2, B1, B2, C1). PD-L2 expression has led to reduced expression of the tumor-promoting molecules in both CAF subtypes, with two exceptions: it elevated osteopontin expression in BC-CAF-1 cells (Fig. 5A1) and no effect was noted for PD-L2 expression on CCL2 production in BC-CAF-2 (Fig. 5C2).

Strongly supporting the roles of WT-PD-L2 as a tumor-inhibitory entity in the CAFs was revealed by follow up analyses demonstrating that CM derived from BC-CAF-2 expressing WT-PD-L2 have led to a significantly lower migration of breast tumor cells in the wound-healing migration assay, compared to CM from Vector control cells (Fig. 5D2).

In view of our findings demonstrating the presence of a prominent sub-population of BC-CAFs expressing both PD-L1 and PD-L2 following cytokine stimulation (Fig. 2), we next determined the way the combined expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 affects the ability of CAFs to express these same cancer-promoting molecules. This analysis provided evidence for the dominance of tumor-promoting functions of PD-L1 over the cancer-inhibitory functions of PD-L2 in both BC-CAF isolates (Fig. 5A-5C). This was illustrated by elevated expression of osteopontin, CXCL8 and CCL2 by BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2 upon co-expression of PD-L1 + PD-L2 (Fig. 5A-5C).

Together, our findings demonstrate that PD-L1 and PD-L2 regulate in cell-autonomous manners pro-tumoral characteristics and functions in BC-CAFs, but do so in an opposite manner: PD-L1 up-regulates these functions whereas PD-L2 generally leads to their down-regulation. However, in a situation of PD-L1 + PD-L2 co-expression, as may by the case when CAFs are exposed to a pro-inflammatory TME, the pro-metastatic functions of PD-L1 dominate the outcome, leading to increased production of osteopontin, CXCL8 and CCL2 by the CAFs.

PD-L1 and PD-L2 N-glycosylation regulates their ability to affect the pro-tumorigenic characteristics and functions of BC-CAFs

The above findings have led us to inquire into the possibility that the differences between PD-L1 and PD-L2 may be attributed to different N-glycosylation features of the two molecules. This possibility is supported by the fact that PD-L1 carries only one N-glycosylation site (N35) at its extracellular IgV-like domain, whereas PD-L2 has two N-glycosylation sites at its IgV-like region (N37, N64) (Fig. 5E). Moreover, AlphaFold3-predicted structures, which are nearly identical to the crystal structures (PD-L1: IgV-like and IgC-like; PD-L2: IgV-like only) (Fig. S6), demonstrated folding differences between PD-L1 and PD-L2 at their IgV-like domains when analyzed at their PD-1-bound state (Fig. S7), further emphasizing the differences between the PD-L1 and PD-L2 molecules.

In view of these differences between PD-L1 and PD-L2, and published studies demonstrating roles for PD-L1 and PD-L2 N-glycosylation in controlling their cell-intrinsic functions in cancer cells, we next determined the roles of N-glycosylation in regulating the cell-autonomous functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in BC-CAFs. To this end, we expressed PD-L1 variants that were mutated at each single N-glycosylation site (N35A-PD-L1, N192A-PD-L1, N200A-PD-L1, N219A-PD-L1) or at all four sites together (Nx4-PD-L1), in BC-CAF-1 and BC-CAF-2, and determined the ability of these mutants to regulate PD-L1-induced tumor-promoting characteristics and functions, compared to the transduced WT-PD-L1. In parallel, PD-L2 variants mutated at each single N-glycosylation site (N37A-PD-L2, N64A-PD-L2, N157A-PD-L2, N163A-PD-L2, N189A-PD-L2) and a mutant of all five sites together (Nx5-PD-L2) were expressed in both BC-CAFs, followed by determination of BC-CAF activities, compared to WT-PD-L2.

The impairment of all four N-glycosylation sites in Nx4-PD-L1 was validated by the fact that this variant demonstrated only one band in the WB analysis, at the ~ 33 kDa range (Fig. 6A1, A2), which is the predicted MW of PD-L1 prior to PTMs.

The findings of Figs. 7B1 and 7B2 indicate that the activities of WT-PD-L2 in BC-CAFs are strictly regulated by all five N-glycosylation sites. Specifically, the down-regulatory effects of WT-PD-L2 on CCL2 production in BC-CAF-1 (Figs. 5C1 and 7B1) and on osteopontin expression in BC-CAF-2 (Figs. 5A2 and 7B2) were completely abrogated in all five N-glycosylation mutants of PD-L2 compared to WT-PD-L2 (Fig. 7B1 and B2, respectively). The ability of WT-PD-L2 to induce osteopontin expression in BC-CAF-1, which was noted as an exception from the general trend of WT-PD-L2 down-regulating effects (Figs. 5A1 and 7B1) was also totally abrogated in all five PD-L2 N-glycosylation mutants compared to WT-PD-L2 (Fig. 7B1). Of interest in this context are our previously shown findings, in which WT-PD-L2 did not affect CCL2 production by BC-CAF-2 (Figs. 5C2 and 7B2); in this case, the production of CCL2 was impaired in all five N-glycosylation mutants compared to WT-PD-L2, suggesting key roles for PD-L2 N-glycosylation in dictating the basal levels of CCL2 expression in these cells.

Breast tumor cell migration assays, performed with CM of BC-CAFs, further supported the tumor-suppressing functions of PD-L2 in BC-CAFs. Here, we have compared the functions of CM derived from WT-PD-L2-expressing BC-CAF-2 cells to CM collected from the N37A-PD-L2 and N163A-PD-L2 mutants (corresponding in tertiary structure to the location of the N35 and N192 sites of PD-L1 (Fig. 5E)). While the CM of WT-PD-L2-expressing BC-CAF-2 cells reduced tumor cell migration (Figs. 5D2 and 7B2, S8B), CM obtained from the N37A-PD-L2 mutant lacked this ability and CM from N163A-PD-L2 even reversed it (Fig. 7B2, S8B). These observations indicate that the ability of WT-PD-L2 to down-regulate the tumor-promoting functions of BC-CAFs depends on its N-glycosylation.

Overall, our findings indicate that PD-L1 and PD-L2 regulate the progression-supporting characteristics and functions of BC-CAFs in cell-autonomous manners that depend on their N-glycosylation. Moreover, N-glycosylation fine-tunes the cell-intrinsic effects of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in target-specific and N-glycosylation site-specific ways. Many of the functions were similarly-regulated in the two BC-CAF isolates, with some exceptions that reflect the existence of cell-specific regulation that can also take place.

Discussion

Immunotherapies directed to the PD-L1/PD-1 axis have greatly improved therapy outcomes in melanoma patients and are now being evaluated in many other cancer types including breast cancer and lung cancer (Park and Skalhegg 2025; Lin et al. 2025; Jin et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024). Although these treatments provide major breakthroughs in cancer treatment, often they are not effective enough (Park and Skalhegg 2025; Lin et al. 2025), raising the need to better identify the patients who may benefit the most from such therapies.

One of the major parameters used for patient stratification for PD-L1/PD-1 targeted therapy is the extent of PD-L1 expression in the cancer cells and in adjacent immune cells (Doroshow et al. 2021). Other TME players that were almost totally ignored in this respect are CAFs, whose expression of PD-L1 is minimally considered. The high-affinity binding of PD-1 to PD-L2 is also disregarded (Yang et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023), as is the potential regulation of PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression by chronic inflammatory processes that support tumor growth and metastasis (Xie et al. 2025).

In this study, we addressed these different aspects by building on published studies demonstrating the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in CAFs of breast cancer and lung cancer patients (Yoshikawa et al. 2021; Costa et al. 2018; Heenatigala Palliyage et al. 2023; Teramoto et al. 2019; Cao et al. 2025). In this setting, we have used CAFs that were isolated from breast cancer and lung cancer patients and determined the regulation of PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression by pro-inflammatory cytokines. We then took a yet unexplored research direction, and asked whether CAF-expressed PD-L1 and PD-L2, each alone and together, can modulate the pro-tumorigenic potential of CAFs by cell-intrinsic effects, and in which direction. We also determined if such cell-autonomous functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2 are controlled by their N-glycosylation state.

Using these experimental systems, our study provides several novel observations:

In the course of cancer inflammation, TNFα and IL-1β have many tumor-promoting activities, acting alone and together on tumor cells as well as on stromal cells (Wang et al. 2022); Malkova et al. 2023; Liubomirski et al. 2019; Rubinstein-Achiasaf et al. 2021). Several studies have reported that these two cytokines can induce the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in different cell types (Antonangeli et al. 2020; Baram et al. 2022; Behm et al. 2020). In parallel to TNFα and IL-1β, IFNγ has dual roles in regulating immune activities in tumors, enabling tumor cell killing on one hand but on the other hand issuing inflammatory processes and up-regulating PD-L1 expression (Liu 2025; Boehmer and Zanoni 2025; Mandai et al. 2016). While the roles of IFNγ in promoting PD-L1 expression are well-established, only few reports documented its ability to up-regulate PD-L2 expression (Cao et al. 2025; Garcia-Diaz et al. 2017).

To take forward the analysis of the regulation of pro-tumorigenic molecules in CAFs, in this study we investigated the impact of integrative activity of pro-inflammatory cytokines on the expression of pro-tumorigenic molecules by CAFs. Our data demonstrated the ability of TNFα + IL-1β to increase the production of the pro-metastatic chemokine CXCL8 by BC-CAFs. In parallel, a generally-shared phenotype was revealed for CAFs from different sources—from breast cancer and lung cancer alike—in which IFNγ stimulation has given rise to elevated proportion of cells expressing PD-L1 + PD-L2. A most prominent effect was noted to TNFα + IL-1β + IFNγ stimulation, reshaping the CAFs into an intensified fibroblast phenotype, with high proportions of cells co-expressing PD-L1 and PD-L2 at their cell surface.

Thus, our observations suggest that pro-inflammatory cytokines that are present in tumors can influence the tumor-promoting interactions of CAFs with other TME elements. Such potential effects on the TME may be the result of cooperative activities of TNFα + IL-1β—that can up-regulate the expression of the pro-metastatic chemokine CXCL8—with IFNγ-induced elevation of CAFs expressing inhibitory immune checkpoints, hence potentially leading to immune suppression.

A closer look at the effects of the cytokines indicated that IFNγ was a major inducer of mainly PD-L1 but also of PD-L2 expression, and that its cooperative activities with TNFα + IL-1β has further elevated the proportions of PD-L1 + PD-L2-expressing cells in BC-CAFs, but not in Lung CAFs. However, in both BC-CAFs and Lung-CAFs, the combined TNFα + IL-1β + IFNγ stimulus has prominently increased the intracellular levels of PD-L1 and PD-L2, revealing a complex regulatory mode of their trafficking to the cell membrane. These findings also suggest that the two molecules may have intracellular roles, agreeing with findings demonstrating regulatory roles for PD-L1 which is located in the nucleus in tumor cells (Gao et al. 2020; Ying et al. 2021).

Published studies have demonstrated the expression of PD-L1 or PD-L2 in CAFs from several tumor types, including breast cancer and lung cancer; however, these studies have not led to definitive conclusions on the connection of CAF-expressed PD-L1 and PD-L2 to T cell suppression or to patient survival (Yoshikawa et al. 2021; Costa et al. 2018; Heenatigala Palliyage et al. 2023; Teramoto et al. 2019; Cao et al. 2025). Moreover, they did not address the possibility that PD-L1 and PD-L2 act not only in the immune context but also in cell-intrinsic-dependent manners, controlling the ability of CAFs to express pro-metastatic factors.

In this study, we demonstrate in designated cell systems that PD-L1 and PD-L2 regulate in cell-autonomous manners the tumor-supporting functions of BC-CAFs; these observations are complemented by published studies on their cell-intrinsic functions in cancer cells (Deng et al. 2023; Erlichman et al. 2022, 2023; Ren et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2021). Moreover, we show that PD-L1 and PD-L2 do so in opposing manners: whereas PD-L1 up-regulates the expression of tumor-promoting molecules by BC-CAFs, including factors that promote tumor cell migration, PD-L2 generally leads to their down-regulation.

Our observations regarding the cell-autonomous impacts of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in CAFs suggest that these two molecules can regulate tumor progression in levels extending beyond immune-suppression. Moreover, our investigation points at the ability of PD-L2 to down-regulate the intrinsic ability of CAFs to exert progression-supporting phenotypes and functions if acting alone. However, analyses of BC-CAFs expressing both molecules together demonstrated clear dominance of PD-L1 over PD-L2, resulting in net effect of a tumor-promoting phenotype in the CAFs. Based on our findings in the first part of the study, this observation may have major relevance to the clinical setting, in which chronic inflammation can potentially lead to increased proportions of CAFs co-expressing PD-L1 + PD-L2 and thus potentiate processes leading to tumor progression.

In efforts to identify the molecular mechanisms contributing to the contrasting effects of PD-L1 and PD-L2, we have analyzed their ability to regulate the activation of key relevant transcription factors in BC-CAFs (Fig. S5B); in these studies, we analyzed p65—which is strongly activated by the pro-inflammatory cytokines used in this study (TNFα and IL-1β)—as well as STAT1 and STAT3 that were identified in our previous research to mediate the cell-intrinsic functions of PD-L1 in breast tumor cells (Erlichman et al. 2023). Out of these three components, STAT1 was activated by both PD-L1 and PD-L2, whereas p65 and STAT3 activation was not affected (Fig. S5B). However, we could not validate a direct regulatory role for STAT1 because siRNA to STAT1 did not provide results that were reliable enough. Our findings call for an extensive investigation which is beyond the scope of this research, in order to identify the molecular mechanisms regulating the cell-intrinsic functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in CAFs.

In coming to understand the opposing roles of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in regulating tumor-supporting characteristics and functions of BC-CAFs, we were aware of two past observations:

PD-L1 and PD-L2 share the ability to bind PD-1, but otherwise they differ in several functional and structural features: PD-L2 has a higher affinity to PD-1 than PD-L1; the amino acid homology of PD-L1 and PD-L2 is not high (particularly at their intracellular domain); PD-L1 and PD-L2 differ in their folding upon interaction with PD-1, primarily at the IgV-like domain; the two molecules carry a different number of N-glycosylation sites and at different positions (Fig. 5E, S6, S7) (Benicky et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2017; Philips et al. 2020).N-glycosylation is essential for PD-L1 to exert signaling events that are required for its cell-autonomous functions in breast cancer cells: Our published study on PD-L1 intrinsic activities in breast cancer cells demonstrated that PD-L1 N-glycosylation leads to STAT1/STAT3 activation, thereof giving rise to the cell-intrinsic pro-metastatic functions of PD-L1 in vitro (Erlichman et al. 2023).

These two lines of evidence suggest that PD-L1 and PD-L2 diverge in signaling, hence affecting differently the characteristics and functions of the cells, and that these differences may be connected to their N-glycosylation patterns. Indeed, in the current study we have demonstrated that N-glycosylation plays key roles in controlling the functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2: (1) For PD-L1: As a general observation, we found that the ability of PD-L1 to promote pro-tumorigenic phenotypes and functions in BC-CAFs depended on N-glycosylation, but in a fine-tuned manner: PD-L1-induced osteopontin expression was fully dependent on all four PD-L1 N-glycosylation sites, whereas the induction of CXCL8 and CCL2 required the N-glycosylation of the N35 and N192 residues only (and only in one of the BC-CAFs). These two sites also were necessary for PD-L1 to induce the production of factors supporting tumor cell migration. (2) For PD-L2: As noted above, the general effect of WT-PD-L2 was down-regulation of the pro-metastatic functions of BC-CAFs, and this activity was tightly regulated by all five N-glycosylation sites. Specifically, the N37 and N163 sites were also involved in regulating the ability of BC-CAFs to release factors that down-regulated tumor cell migration.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that N-glycosylation regulates the cell-autonomous functions of PD-L1 and PD-L2, however it does so in context-dependent manners: (1) Target-dependent: Whereas osteopontin was regulated by PD-L1 N-glycosylation in BC-CAF-1, CXCL8 and CCL2 were not. These findings suggest that osteopontin is found under a specific PD-L1-induced control mechanism which is not necessarily shared with other proteins, such as inflammatory chemokines. (2) Cell-dependent: PD-L1-induced expression of CXCL8 and CCL2 in BC-CAF-2 were regulated by PD-L1 glycosylation, while they were not similarly controlled in BC-CAF-1 cells, illustrating differences in control of the same target in divergent cellular contexts. (3) N-glycosylation site-dependent: The study of PD-L1 has demonstrated that N-glycosylation at all potential sites is not always required for PD-L1 to induce pro-metastatic functions in CAFs. This was observed in BC-CAF-2, where N-glycosylation at all four sites was necessary for osteopontin induction by PD-L1, but only two of the sites (N35, N192) regulated PD-L1-induced CXCL8 and CCL2 production.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that improved therapy with ICBs would require determination of PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression in CAFs, in addition to the current analyses of PD-L1 expression only in tumor cells and immune cells. Moreover, they raise the need to consider not only the immune-relevant functions of these molecules, but also their cell-autonomous effects and their N-glycosylation status in the cancer cells and in other cell types. The equilibrium between PD-L1 and PD-L2 should also be considered because the two molecules may act in opposing manners, with dominance of one over the other in different conditions.

Thus, the observations obtained in this study emphasize the complexity of the PD-L1-PD-L2/PD-1 axis in cancer progression, and the need to better identify the patterns of their expression and N-glycosylation in different cell types of patient tumors, including stromal cells, cancer cells, and immune cells.
