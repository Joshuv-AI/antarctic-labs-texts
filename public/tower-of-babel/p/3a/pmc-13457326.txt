Application of selective outflow control technique-assisted laparoscopic hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma in the right posterior superior area: a case series study (with video)
Authors: Qingyun Xie, Buzhe Zhang, Yu Chuan Tan, Benz KOH, Haoze Wu, Chao Zhang, Yunshi Cai, Fengwei Gao, Hong Wu, Kunlin Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic liver resection (LLR) for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) involving the right posterosuperior area (segments 7 and 8) remains technically demanding because of the deep anatomical location of these lesions and their close relationship to the right hepatic vein (RHV) and inferior vena cava (IVC). This study evaluated the safety, technical feasibility, and perioperative outcomes of a novel stratified selective outflow control (SOC) technique developed to optimize vascular management during complex laparoscopic hepatectomy.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed of 22 consecutive patients who underwent SOC-assisted LLR for HCC located in the right posterosuperior area between January 2024 and January 2026. Preoperative three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction and territory-based simulation were used to guide individualized operative planning. According to the extent of venous involvement, the SOC strategy was stratified into two anatomical approaches: the RHV Occlusion method, applied to tumors involving the RHV while sparing the IVC wall, and the Total Outflow Occlusion method, reserved for tumors involving the IVC or the RHV–IVC confluence.ResultsAll procedures were completed laparoscopically without conversion to open surgery. The median operative duration was 233.0 min, and the median estimated blood loss was 200 mL. R0 resection was achieved in all patients, with a median surgical margin of 1.5 cm. Minor postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade I–II) occurred in 59.1% of patients. Major complications (grade ≥ III) were limited to 9.9% and consisted of 2 cases of grade IIIa symptomatic pleural effusion requiring percutaneous drainage, both of which resolved successfully. No perioperative mortalities or 30-day readmissions occurred.ConclusionsSOC-assisted LLR represents a safe, technically feasible, and reproducible strategy for HCC in the right posterosuperior segments. Laparoscopic liver resection (LLR) has become an increasingly established component of minimally invasive surgery for hepatic malignancies and other liver tumors [1, 2]. Despite substantial advances in laparoscopic techniques and perioperative management, anatomical resection of lesions located in the right posterosuperior segments (segments 7 and 8) remains among the most technically demanding procedures in hepatobiliary surgery [3, 4]. The complexity of these resections is primarily attributable to the deep anatomical location of the posterosuperior segments, the narrow and restricted operative field, and the intimate relationship of these lesions with the right hepatic vein (RHV) and retrohepatic inferior vena cava (IVC). Consequently, uncontrolled hemorrhage arising from major hepatic venous structures continues to represent a major intraoperative challenge and a critical determinant of operative safety. Although the Pringle maneuver provides effective inflow control, it does not adequately address venous backflow from the hepatic veins, which remains highly susceptible to fluctuations in central venous pressure (CVP) [5, 6]. Several techniques have therefore been introduced to facilitate hepatic outflow management during LLR, including the liver hanging maneuver and extrahepatic control of the middle or left hepatic veins (LHV). However, these methods were developed primarily for left-sided hepatectomy or general hepatic suspension and do not specifically address the technical complexities associated with direct control of the RHV during resection of tumors located in the right posterosuperior area [7–10]. Furthermore, a standardized and reproducible strategy integrating selective RHV occlusion with total hepatic outflow control for HCC involving this anatomically complex region has not yet been fully established (Fig. 1).

Diagrammatic representation of the surgical workflow for LLR using the total hepatic outflow occlusion method. LLR laparoscopic liver resection

To address this limitation, we developed a structured SOC technique specifically designed for laparoscopic management of HCC located in the right posterosuperior segments. For tumors involving the major hepatic outflow tract between the RHV and the IVC, extended hepatectomy combined with resection of the involved venous structures is performed on the basis of detailed preoperative three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction and territory-based watershed analysis, thereby enabling precise operative planning and individualized vascular assessment. In cases involving RHV invasion, an extrahepatic approach is used to establish hanging-assisted RHV occlusion before parenchymal transection (Fig. 2A). Conversely, when the tumor extends to the IVC (Fig. 1A), a total hepatic outflow occlusion strategy is implemented, with local IVC resection and vascular reconstruction performed as required. This laparoscopic SOC framework provides reliable intraoperative vascular control, facilitates precise extended resection in anatomically challenging cases, and demonstrates favorable technical reproducibility with potential applicability in broader hepatobiliary surgical practice. The present study systematically evaluated the application, technical feasibility, and short-term perioperative outcomes of SOC-assisted laparoscopic hepatectomy for HCC located in the right posterosuperior area.

Materials and methods

This study was reported in accordance with the PROCESS criteria.

Patient selection

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) solitary or multifocal HCC confined to segments 7 and 8, demonstrating close anatomical proximity, marked external compression, or direct invasion of the main RHV or the RHV–IVC confluence; (2) preoperative 3D reconstruction confirming the anatomical feasibility of establishing a retrohepatic tunnel for implementation of the SOC technique; specifically, the indications for the two SOC strategies were strictly stratified according to the extent of venous involvement, with the RHV Occlusion strategy applied to tumors compressing or invading the main RHV while sparing the IVC wall, whereas the Total Outflow Occlusion strategy was reserved exclusively for tumors extending to the RHV root or directly involving the IVC wall; (3) adequate hepatic functional reserve, defined as Child–Pugh class A or early class B (score < 7); and (4) indocyanine green retention rate at 15 min (ICG-R15) < 15%.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) extreme spatial restriction caused by very large tumors or severe IVC compression precluding safe establishment of a retrohepatic tunnel; (2) inadequate hepatic reserve, including Child-Pugh class C or late class B disease; and (3) severe cardiopulmonary comorbidity contraindicating general anesthesia, clinically significant ascites or pleural effusion, active hemorrhage, or coagulation dysfunction.

Preoperative preparations

Preoperative evaluation included thin-slice contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) with a slice thickness of ≤ 1 mm, followed by 3D liver reconstruction using the SurgiPlan Abdomen 200 system (UNITED IMAGING, Wuhan, China). This approach enabled precise anatomical delineation of the ductal and vascular structures within the right posterosuperior segments and clearly defined the spatial relationships among the tumor, RHV, and retrohepatic IVC. In addition, 3D reconstruction facilitated identification of the RHV drainage territory and assisted in planning the optimal trajectory for retrohepatic tunnel creation and vascular sling placement during SOC implementation (Fig. 1A). To minimize venous backflow during parenchymal transection, multiple intraoperative anesthetic strategies were used to maintain a low central venous pressure (CVP). These measures included deepened general anesthesia, combined epidural blockade, restrictive fluid administration, pharmacologic modulation with vasoactive agents such as nitroglycerin and diuretics including furosemide, and placement of the patient in the reverse Trendelenburg position. Collectively, these interventions maintained CVP within a target range of 0–5 cm H2O (1 cm H2O = 0.098 kPa), thereby optimizing exposure of the right posterosuperior bare area and reducing hemorrhage related to hepatic venous backflow during transection.

Surgical procedure

Total hepatic outflow occlusion method (Video S1 and Supplementary Video)

Step 1: Liver mobilization and retrohepatic tunnel establishment.

Complete mobilization of the liver, including the caudate lobe, is a critical prerequisite for adequate tumor exposure and safe establishment of the retrohepatic tunnel. This process involves systematic division of the ligamentum teres, falciform ligament, coronary ligaments, bilateral triangular ligaments, caval ligaments, hepatogastric ligament, and hepatorenal ligament. During mobilization, the short segmental hepatic veins draining the posterior aspect of the caudate lobe are meticulously identified, isolated, and divided. To facilitate laparoscopic clockwise rotation of the right hepatic lobe around the longitudinal axis of the IVC, a gauze-assisted medial rotation maneuver is employed. In this technique, the assistant applies controlled frictional traction to the hepatic surface using both ends of a gauze strip, thereby enabling gradual and stable mobilization of the right liver through coordinated incremental “inching” movements.

Step 2: Placement of hepatic outflow occlusion devices.

An occlusion sling is passed from right to left through the retrohepatic space surrounding the suprahepatic IVC. A 5-mm subxiphoid incision is subsequently created to externalize the sling extracorporeally. A temporary plastic occlusion tube is then advanced into the abdominal cavity along the established sling pathway (Fig. 1B). To ensure complete hepatic outflow control, the right adrenal vein is carefully divided. Using the same technique, an additional temporary occlusion tube is inserted through a separate external incision to facilitate comprehensive vascular exclusion of the retrohepatic outflow tract (Fig. 1C).

Step 3: Tumor exposure, vascular exclusion, and specimen resection.

Following intraoperative ultrasonographic assessment of the spatial relationship between the tumor and the RHV, parenchymal transection is initiated using an ultrasonic dissector. As the RHV trunk becomes exposed, venous tributaries located along its left or ventral aspect are carefully dissected and controlled (Fig. 1D). After complete tumor exposure, the extracorporeal IVC occlusion device is tightened to establish total hepatic outflow occlusion, and the tumor-involved segment of the IVC wall is sharply excised using laparoscopic scissors (Fig. 1E). The RHV root is subsequently transected, permitting en bloc removal of the tumor specimen (Fig. 1F). The procedure is completed by reconstruction of the IVC defect using continuous vascular suturing (Fig. 1G), meticulous hemostasis of the transection surface (Fig. 1H), and sequential release of all inflow and outflow occlusion devices. In cases involving tumor thrombus extension into the RHV–IVC confluence, direct venotomy followed by thrombectomy is performed under total hepatic outflow occlusion (see Supplementary Video).

RHV occlusion method (Video S2)

Step 1: Extrahepatic mobilization and RHV encirclement.

For tumors involving the RHV (Fig. 2A), the procedure begins with mobilization of the right hepatic ligaments and the right caval ligament. When exposure of the retrohepatic tunnel between the caval fossa and the RHV root is insufficient, selected short hepatic veins are divided to enlarge the operative corridor. Once adequate retrohepatic space has been established, a vascular sling is passed around the RHV through the retrohepatic tunnel. This pre-positioned sling provides stable vascular traction and enables immediate RHV occlusion or transection when required during parenchymal dissection (Fig. 2B, C).

Step 2: Parenchymal transection and selective venous outflow control.

Parenchymal transection is performed along the left or ventral plane of the RHV. To improve anatomical precision, indocyanine green (ICG)-guided fluorescence navigation using either positive or negative staining techniques is employed to delineate the intersegmental boundaries (Fig. 2D). If substantial venous backflow hemorrhage from the RHV occurs because of elevated central venous pressure, the pre-suspended RHV root can be immediately tightened or transected using a linear stapler or Hem-o-lock clips to achieve rapid and effective hemostatic control (Fig. 2E).

Step 3: Glissonian pedicle division and specimen retrieval.

Following the creation of sufficient operative space through progressive parenchymal splitting, the Glissonian pedicle supplying the target segment or lobe is identified, ligated, and divided. The operation is completed by finalization of the parenchymal transection and en bloc removal of the specimen containing the tumor (Fig. 2F–G).

Postoperative considerations

Postoperative management was directed toward maintaining hemodynamic stability and closely monitoring recovery of hepatic function. Because the SOC technique involves temporary occlusion of major hepatic outflow structures, particular attention was focused on the early identification of venous congestion or localized outflow impairment within the remnant liver. Standard postoperative management included early ambulation, multimodal analgesic protocols, and routine thromboprophylaxis. Laboratory indices, including serum transaminases, bilirubin concentrations, albumin levels, and coagulation parameters, were assessed daily during the first postoperative week to facilitate timely detection of post-hepatectomy liver failure [11].

Perioperative data collection

Collected perioperative variables included patient demographic characteristics, preoperative laboratory findings, tumor-related characteristics, and operative parameters. Postoperative outcome measures included postoperative complications, duration of postoperative hospitalization, 30-day readmission, 90-day mortality, and recovery of hepatic function on postoperative day 3, as assessed by alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, albumin, and total bilirubin levels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables with normal and non-normal distributions are reported as means with standard deviations and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), respectively. For subgroup comparisons between the RHV Occlusion group and the Total Outflow Occlusion group, continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study consisted of a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained surgical database including consecutive patients who underwent SOC-assisted laparoscopic hepatectomy at our institution during the study period. A total of 22 patients with HCC located in the right posterosuperior area (Couinaud segments 7 and 8) were included between January 2024 and January 2026 on the basis of pathological confirmation or established clinical diagnostic criteria. All patients underwent LLR using the SOC technique. The original patient-level data are provided in Supplementary Table 1. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Written informed consent for publication of the study and accompanying images was obtained from all patients. Surgical management strategies were determined through the multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion at the authors’ institution. Copies of the written informed consent documents are available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics of the 22 included patients are summarized in Table 1. The cohort demonstrated a high prevalence of chronic hepatitis B infection (90.9%) and concurrent liver cirrhosis (50.0%). Most patients presented with relatively early-stage disease, with 81.8% classified as Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage A and 50.0% categorized as China Liver Cancer (CNLC) stage Ia. Despite the predominance of early-stage disease, a substantial proportion of patients harbored relatively large tumors, with 40.9% demonstrating a maximum tumor diameter between 5 and 10 cm.

Perioperative outcomes

Perioperative outcomes are presented in Table 2. The median operation duration was 233.0 min (IQR, 200.0–273.5 min). With respect to the SOC strategy employed, the RHV Occlusion method was applied in 17 patients (77.3%), whereas the Total Outflow Occlusion method was utilized in 5 patients (22.7%). Vascular wall repair was required in 4 cases (18.2%). Intraoperative blood loss was effectively controlled, with 54.5% of patients experiencing estimated blood loss of < 200 mL, whereas the intraoperative blood transfusion rate was 18.2%. Complete oncological resection was achieved in all patients, with a 100% R0 resection rate and a median surgical margin of 1.5 cm (range, 0.8–2.0 cm).

Histopathological analysis demonstrated that 72.7% of tumors were moderately differentiated, whereas microvascular invasion (M1 or M2 grade) was identified in 45.5% of specimens. The median postoperative hospital stay was 7.0 days (IQR, 6.0–8.7 days). On postoperative day 3, the median total bilirubin level was 25.5 μmol/L and the median albumin level was 34.1 g/L. Minor postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade 1–2) occurred in 59.1% of patients, including pleural effusion (50.0%) and transient liver insufficiency (31.8%). Major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) were observed in 9.9% of patients and consisted of grade IIIa symptomatic pleural effusions requiring percutaneous drainage. No 30-day readmissions or 90-day mortalities were recorded, supporting the short-term safety and technical feasibility of the SOC-assisted approach for HCC located in the right posterior superior area.

Subgroup analysis of the SOC strategies

A subgroup analysis was conducted to compare perioperative characteristics and clinical outcomes between the RHV Occlusion group (n = 17) and the Total Outflow Occlusion group (n = 5). As summarized in Supplementary Table 2, no statistically significant differences were identified between the two groups with respect to baseline demographic characteristics, preoperative laboratory findings, tumor-related variables, or the prevalence of pre-existing cirrhosis (all P > 0.05). Regarding operative outcomes, the median operative duration was comparable between the RHV Occlusion and Total Outflow Occlusion groups (236.0 vs. 200.0 min, P = 0.692). Both approaches achieved a 100% R0 resection rate and demonstrated comparable median surgical margins (1.5 vs. 1.5 cm, P = 0.530). However, the Total Outflow Occlusion group demonstrated a significantly higher frequency of vascular wall repair than the RHV Occlusion group (80.0% vs. 0%, P < 0.001). Postoperatively, the median total bilirubin level on postoperative day 3 was significantly higher in the Total Outflow Occlusion group than in the RHV Occlusion group (28.5 vs. 23.8 μmol/L, P = 0.011), reflecting the transient hepatic functional impact associated with complete vascular exclusion. Nevertheless, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding median postoperative hospital stay (9.0 vs. 7.0 days, P = 0.202) or the incidence of major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III: 0% vs. 11.8%, P = 1). No 30-day readmissions or 90-day mortalities occurred in either subgroup.

Discussion

Since its introduction in the early 1990s, LLR has evolved substantially, progressing from limited non-anatomical wedge resections to increasingly sophisticated anatomical hepatectomy procedures. Nevertheless, laparoscopic resection of tumors located in the right posterior superior segments (segments 7 and 8) continues to be regarded as one of the most technically demanding procedures in minimally invasive hepatobiliary surgery [12]. The complexity of these resections is attributable to the deep anatomical location of the posterosuperior segments, the restricted operative workspace, and the substantial difficulty associated with achieving adequate surgical exposure. Furthermore, the close anatomical relationship between these tumors and the major hepatic veins and retrohepatic IVC significantly increases operative risk. Intraoperative injury to these major outflow structures may result in massive hemorrhage or potentially fatal air embolism [13]. In the present study, the perioperative outcomes of 22 patients undergoing SOC-assisted laparoscopic hepatectomy were evaluated to clarify the technical feasibility and short-term safety profile of this approach for HCC involving major hepatic venous outflow structures.

Conventional laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy generally relies on a hepatic vein-guided transection strategy combined with maintenance of low CVP to reduce venous bleeding during parenchymal dissection [6, 14]. In contrast, the SOC technique emphasizes proactive and anatomically tailored vascular control. Integration of thin-slice contrast-enhanced CT with 3D reconstruction and territory-based watershed analysis enables precise delineation of portal inflow territories and hepatic venous drainage patterns. Consequently, retrohepatic tunnel creation is transformed from a predominantly experience-dependent maneuver into a preoperatively planned and anatomically guided procedure, permitting strategic placement of vascular slings before parenchymal transection. A principal advantage of the SOC framework lies in its stratified and individualized design, which incorporates two distinct approaches according to the extent of venous involvement. The RHV Occlusion method represents a relatively less invasive strategy that avoids circumferential IVC dissection, thereby reducing surgical trauma and minimizing systemic hemodynamic disturbance. This approach is particularly suitable for tumors causing compression or localized invasion of the main RHV while sparing the IVC wall. In contrast, the Total Outflow Occlusion method is reserved for more advanced tumors involving the IVC wall or the RHV–IVC confluence. Although technically more complex and associated with greater transient circulatory and hepatic functional impact, this approach is essential for achieving complete oncological resection in anatomically advanced cases. By establishing complete vascular exclusion of the retrohepatic operative field, the technique provides a bloodless surgical plane that facilitates safe sharp excision and precise vascular reconstruction of the involved IVC wall.

For HCC located adjacent to or directly invading major hepatic venous structures, conventional operative strategies frequently avoid vascular resection because of concerns regarding uncontrollable hemorrhage or postoperative venous outflow obstruction in the remnant liver. Consequently, resections are often performed with narrow or even zero surgical margins [15–17]. Under guidance from 3D hepatic venous watershed analysis, the SOC technique enables controlled sharp excision of the involved RHV or IVC wall under conditions of total vascular exclusion. This strategy permits en bloc incorporation of the invaded venous structures into the surgical specimen, thereby facilitating the achievement of an R0 resection.

Historically, preservation of the RHV trunk has been considered critical for maintaining venous drainage of the right anterior sector [18]. However, contemporary hemodynamic and anatomical investigations have demonstrated the existence of important compensatory venous collateral pathways within the hepatic venous system. Intervenous communications between the RHV, middle hepatic vein (MHV), and short hepatic veins have been documented, including through RHV venographic studies [19]. Following RHV obstruction, these collateral channels may progressively enlarge, redirecting venous drainage from segment 8 toward the MHV or directly into the IVC. This adaptive compensatory mechanism may help prevent extensive congestive injury within the remnant liver [20, 21]. Current reports describing laparoscopic hepatic outflow control have focused predominantly on management of the MHV–LHV common trunk [7, 9, 10, 22] or on total hepatic outflow exclusion techniques developed for open liver surgery [5, 8]. Nakano et al. [10] and Marchese et al. [9] demonstrated the technical feasibility of controlling the MHV–LHV trunk through the Ligamentum Arantius approach. However, unlike the left hepatic venous system, the RHV lacks stable and reproducible extrahepatic anatomical landmarks and demonstrates substantial variability in its orientation relative to the IVC, rendering extrahepatic RHV dissection considerably more technically demanding. The “Liver Double-Tightened Maneuver” proposed by Piardi et al. [8] achieves vascular exclusion of the transection plane using lateral slings without direct IVC occlusion. Nevertheless, this method was primarily designed for open surgery, and the presence of multiple slings may create technical interference and instrument entanglement within the restricted laparoscopic operative field. The SOC technique described in the present study addresses these limitations by extending the concept of laparoscopic outflow control beyond the left hepatic venous system and enabling effective laparoscopic control of both the RHV and the entire hepatic outflow tract.

The width of the surgical margin remains an important determinant of oncological prognosis in HCC. Prospective studies have demonstrated that wider resection margins, particularly those ≥ 2 cm, are associated with superior long-term survival compared with narrow-margin resections [15, 16]. However, achieving an adequately wide margin for tumors located adjacent to major hepatic outflow structures is often technically prohibitive. In conventional surgical practice, concerns regarding catastrophic hemorrhage or postoperative venous outflow compromise may necessitate narrow-margin or even zero-margin resections. In contrast, a principal advantage of the SOC technique lies in its ability to safely incorporate invaded vascular structures into the resection field under controlled vascular exclusion. By enabling precise sharp excision and controlled reconstruction of the involved IVC wall within a bloodless operative environment, the SOC strategy facilitated the achievement of a 100% R0 resection rate in the present cohort, with a median surgical margin of 1.5 cm. Although this margin does not uniformly reach the ideal 2-cm threshold proposed for all HCC cases, it represents a clinically meaningful improvement over the narrow or zero-margin resections frequently encountered in this anatomically complex region. Nevertheless, whether these favorable technical and pathological outcomes ultimately translate into improved long-term oncological survival remains uncertain and requires validation in future large-scale, controlled studies.

With respect to postoperative morbidity, the observed incidences of pleural effusion (50.0%), transient liver insufficiency (31.8%), and thromboembolic events (13.6%) appear closely related to both the anatomical characteristics of the right posterosuperior segments and the technical maneuvers inherent to the SOC procedure. Because tumors located in segments 7 and 8 are deeply situated and anatomically contiguous with the diaphragm, complete laparoscopic mobilization of the right liver necessitates extensive dissection of the right coronary and triangular ligaments. The resulting regional tissue trauma and thermal irritation likely contributed to the relatively high incidence of reactive pleural effusion. Importantly, only 2 patients (9.9%) developed symptomatic pleural effusion requiring ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage (Clavien–Dindo grade IIIa), whereas the remaining cases were mild and self-limiting (grade I–II). In addition, the SOC technique requires temporary hepatic inflow and selective outflow occlusion to maintain a bloodless operative field. The associated ischemia–reperfusion injury, reflected by a median inflow occlusion duration of 54.0 min and a median outflow occlusion duration of 25.0 min, likely contributed to the transient postoperative elevations in transaminases and bilirubin levels and the incidence of temporary liver insufficiency observed on postoperative day 3. Nevertheless, because vascular occlusion times were maintained within acceptable physiological limits and adequate future liver remnant volume was preserved, all patients recovered uneventfully with standard hepatoprotective management, without progression to clinically significant post-hepatectomy liver failure.

Three patients developed postoperative thromboembolic complications. One patient in the Total Outflow Occlusion group developed asymptomatic intermuscular venous thrombosis of the lower extremity (grade I), which required no specific therapeutic intervention. Two patients in the RHV Occlusion group developed low-risk pulmonary embolisms (grade II), both of which resolved completely following standard anticoagulation therapy. These thromboembolic events may reflect the combined effects of prolonged operative duration, reverse Trendelenburg positioning required for optimal exposure, and the temporary venous stasis induced by targeted hepatic outflow control. These findings further emphasize the importance of routine perioperative thromboprophylaxis and early postoperative mobilization when applying the SOC technique.

Despite the encouraging short-term perioperative outcomes observed in this series, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center case series without a control group; therefore, the observed perioperative advantages cannot be definitively attributed to the SOC technique alone. Second, follow-up duration remains relatively limited. Although all 22 patients remain alive and only 3 recurrences have been identified to date, the available follow-up data remain insufficient for robust long-term oncological survival analysis. Accordingly, the present findings primarily support the technical feasibility and short-term safety of the SOC approach rather than definitive oncological superiority. Finally, several anatomical and pathological factors may limit broader applicability of the technique. In patients with marked anatomical variation, severe spatial restriction, or substantial IVC compression, safe establishment of the retrohepatic tunnel may be technically challenging. Furthermore, moderate-to-severe cirrhosis may result in dense and inelastic hepatic parenchyma, thereby increasing susceptibility to parenchymal tearing and hemorrhage during tunnel creation. Collectively, these findings suggest that although the SOC technique represents a promising strategy for complex HCC located in the right posterosuperior segments, future prospective multicenter investigations with larger patient cohorts and extended follow-up are necessary to validate its reproducibility and long-term oncological value.

Conclusions

This study provides a systematic description of the SOC technique for laparoscopic hepatectomy involving the right posterosuperior segments. The present findings demonstrate that the SOC strategy is technically feasible and safe, providing a flexible and precision-oriented approach to vascular management for tumors involving major hepatic outflow structures. By facilitating reliable vascular control and precise R0 resection, the SOC framework offers a valuable operative strategy for management of anatomically complex hepatocellular carcinoma in the right posterosuperior area. Although the initial perioperative outcomes are encouraging, future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate the reproducibility of the SOC technique and to clarify its long-term oncological implications across broader patient populations.
