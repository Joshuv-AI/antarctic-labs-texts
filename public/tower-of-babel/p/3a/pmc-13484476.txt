Scaling antibody language models improves structure aware representation for antibody engineering
Authors: Shengyuan Bai, Zijing Liu, Bin Feng, Jiying Zhang, Yu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibody language models have emerged as powerful tools in antibody engineering by capturing patterns in sequences to innovate discovery and optimization. While recent advances in machine learning have significantly enhanced this field, current approaches often struggle to capture the full structural complexity inherent in antibody sequences. To address this, we present AbLingua, a family of language models pre-trained on antibody sequences. The largest model in this family contains 1.7 billion parameters and is trained on 1.4 billion sequences, making it the largest encoder-based language model specific to antibodies. AbLingua employs an advanced tokenization method that expands the vocabulary to a level comparable with human language, which enables the model to capture complex structural motifs influencing antibody behavior. Building upon this method, AbLingua introduces an improved pre-training approach that processes amino acid units to better represent structural interdependencies. We demonstrate that AbLingua achieves superior performance across multiple applications, including paratope prediction, neutralizing capacity assessment, and therapeutic antibody design. Furthermore, it excels in the unsupervised classification of B-cell developmental stages and virus-specific antibodies. Our findings demonstrate that the synergy of advanced tokenization, robust scaling laws, and curated datasets constitutes a superior foundation for antibody engineering, significantly driving development efficiency.

Introduction

Antibodies serve as fundamental effector molecules of the adaptive immune system, mediating antigen recognition and orchestrating diverse immune responses across numerous biological systems1,2. The molecular interactions between antibodies and antigens exhibit precise structural complementarity at binding interfaces, establishing key principles for modern antibody engineering and therapeutic development3,4. Rapid advances in antibody engineering have subsequently enabled systematic modification of antibodies to enhance their affinity, stability, and functional properties, substantially expanding their therapeutic applications across multiple disease states5–7.

Computational systems capable of capturing antibody structure and function across diverse repertoires are essential for advancing antibody engineering and immunological research8. Such frameworks must identify fundamental patterns that generalize across multiple antibody properties and functions to be truly effective. However, current machine learning approaches primarily focus on specialized models for discrete tasks, including binding affinity prediction and stability assessment, rather than integrated solutions9–11. Progress in this field, therefore, requires models that understand fundamental amino acid interaction principles applicable across diverse antibodies, overcoming the limited transferability of current specialized systems12–14.

To address these challenges, a robust language model specifically designed for antibodies represents a transformative approach to computational immunology. Such a model learns from vast datasets of antibody sequences to capture underlying evolutionary conservation patterns and the complex interdependencies between sequence, structure, and function. In recent years, the landscape of antibody modeling has expanded rapidly across diverse architectural paradigms. Encoder-based models such as AntiBERTy15, AbLang216, AntiBERTa217, ESM218, and IgBERT19 have been developed to provide high-resolution representations of immune repertoires for classification and property prediction. To facilitate sequence-to-sequence tasks, encoder–decoder frameworks like IgT519 have introduced versatile capabilities for antibody sequence infilling and optimization. Concurrently, generative research has been propelled by autoregressive decoder models, including p-IgGen20, IgLM21, and Progen222, which enable the de novo design of therapeutic candidates. By mapping antibodies into a continuous latent space, these computational frameworks enable comprehensive analysis of antibody repertoires and guide rational design decisions.

Despite the proliferation of these diverse architectures, a fundamental bottleneck remains common to all existing paradigms. We contend that the primary limitation resides in the granularity of information encoding rather than in model capacity or specific structural design. Most current frameworks treat amino acids as independent characters. Specifically, the character-level representation fails to capture the biophysical reality of antibody folding, which is governed by local cooperative interactions among adjacent residues23,24. A single amino acid token inherently lacks sufficient distinctiveness to resolve the structural ambiguity of these cooperative local structural motifs. This semantic gap forces models to implicitly infer complex backbone geometries, such as dihedral angles (ϕ, ψ), from a sequence of structurally ambiguous symbols. Such misalignment limits representational capacity for distinguishing between the rigid framework regions and the hypervariable complementarity-determining regions25. Addressing this challenge requires a fundamental shift in how sequence information is granulated. Specifically, computational methodologies must evolve to capture the collective biophysical signals of adjacent residues rather than treating them as isolated entities. Establishing such a structurally aware encoding remains a critical prerequisite for advanced antibody modeling.

In this study, we introduce AbLingua, a family of antibody language models scaling from 300M to 1.7B parameters. These models are trained on an extensive dataset of 1.4B antibody sequences across multiple immunological repertoires. This expanded capacity enables AbLingua to identify complex patterns that were previously undetectable in smaller models. To overcome the inherent limitations of character-level encoding, we developed TripleAA, a word-level tokenization scheme that clusters co-occurring amino acids into discrete tokens. This approach expands the vocabulary to a scale comparable with natural language models and effectively captures patterns correlated with structural motifs. Building upon this enriched vocabulary, AbLingua implements the context residue transformation (CRT) strategy to further refine the pre-training process. Unlike standard masked language modeling, CRT operates specifically on these multi-residue units to represent the structural interdependencies critical for antibody function and stability. This integration ensures that the model recognizes biologically relevant patterns that individual amino acid tokens cannot effectively capture.

Our research demonstrates that AbLingua possesses broad application value in the prediction and design of antibodies at the amino acid, fragment, and sequence scales. In paratope prediction, AbLingua outperforms state-of-the-art language models while surpassing computationally intensive MSA-driven approaches. Regarding sequence scale analysis, the model accurately predicts the neutralizing capacity of antibodies even within imbalanced datasets, effectively capturing functional changes resulting from subtle mutations. The practical utility of AbLingua is particularly evident in CDRH3 fragment design, where the model enables the accurate prediction of binders using only high-throughput rough screening data. This capability substantially reduces the traditional reliance on high-precision wet-lab experiments during therapeutic development. Furthermore, unsupervised analysis reveals that AbLingua can distinguish different developmental stages of B cell sequences and classify virus-specific antibodies without prior labeling. In conclusion, AbLingua establishes a solid foundation for antibody engineering by bridging advanced tokenization with effective scaling laws.

Results

AbLingua demonstrates favorable scaling laws on antibody sequence modeling

To establish a quantitative understanding of how model design choices impact performance, we characterize the scaling law in antibody sequence modeling and demonstrate its direct impact on language model performance. We conduct systematic analyses across three fundamental dimensions to investigate the scaling law of antibody language models: tokenization approach, model parameter count, and pre-training data volume. This multi-dimensional exploration allows us to examine whether antibody language models follow predictable performance patterns similar to natural language models in other domains.

For this analysis, we develop AbLingua, a family of BERT-based antibody language models (Fig. 1A). Our model incorporates an advanced tokenizer (TripleAA) that enables AbLingua to capture meaningful tripeptide patterns correlated with secondary structure elements (Fig. 1B). These tripeptide motifs frequently represent conserved structural building blocks in antibodies, including β-turns and loop regions that critically determine CDR conformations and antigen binding. The TripleAA expands the vocabulary from the traditional 20 amino acid tokens to approximately 20,000 tokens, thereby establishing a more balanced token frequency distribution that resembles patterns typically observed in natural language corpora (Fig. 1C). We perform systematic scaling experiments with AbLingua models containing 300M, 600M, and 1.7B parameters pre-trained on progressively larger antibody sequence datasets (Fig. 1D). The 300M and 600M models are pre-trained on 300M antibody sequences. The largest model, AbLingua-1.7B, is pre-trained on 1.4 billion antibody sequences from the OAS database26, which exceeds previous antibody-specific encoder-based language models in both model parameter scale and pre-training data volume (Fig. 1E, F).

Our experiments demonstrate that antibody language models utilizing TripleAA tokenization adhere to scaling law principles. As model parameter count and pre-training data volume increase, perplexity decreases in a quantifiable manner, following a power-law relationship with corresponding scaling coefficients (Fig. 1G). These scaling patterns align with observations previously reported in natural language models27,28, confirming the fundamental relationship among token vocabulary augmentation, model parameter scaling, pre-training data expansion, and antibody language model performance. The consistent scaling behavior across different model parameter counts supports the suitability of the AbLingua architecture design for antibody sequence representation while establishing a data-driven foundation for future development of larger-scale models with predictable performance characteristics.

AbLingua predicts paratopes encompassing both CDR and FR sequences

We extend our evaluation beyond perplexity assessment to examine AbLingua's performance on specialized immunological applications. Specifically, we focus on the critical challenge of antibody paratope prediction. This task is central to rational antibody engineering and therapeutic development. Antibody paratopes comprise specialized regions that interact with antigens, thereby determining both binding specificity and affinity. Current methods struggle to accurately identify paratope residues across both complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) and framework regions (FRs), primarily because conventional approaches emphasize CDRs while neglecting framework regions that also contribute to binding interactions. This CDR-centric focus has resulted in an incomplete characterization of the complex antibody-antigen interaction landscape.

To address existing limitations and demonstrate the enhanced capabilities of AbLingua, we evaluate AbLingua under both transfer learning and fine-tuning settings for unified paratope prediction that simultaneously considers both CDR and FR sequences (Fig. 2A). The paratope distribution analysis on the test set reveals a concentration of binding residues within CDRs, with only a small fraction occurring in FR regions (Fig. 2B). This marked imbalance highlights the considerable challenge in accurately predicting the sparse yet functionally important paratopes within FR regions. The prediction confusion matrix demonstrates that AbLingua-1.7B achieves a high precision of 76% for CDR paratopes and 62% for FR paratopes (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, our experimental results show that AbLingua consistently surpasses leading protein and antibody language models across the evaluated metrics (Figs. 2D, S1). The comparison with proABC-229 is particularly revealing: in the transfer-learning setting, AbLingua achieves comparable performance despite proABC-2 being a specialized model utilizing MSA-derived information. However, after undergoing full fine-tuning, AbLingua demonstrates a clear advantage and significantly outperforms proABC-2 across all metrics. Specifically, our model achieves a superior recall rate of ~72% and an F1 score of 75% in the fine-tuning setting. This advantage is remarkable considering that our approach relies on sequence-based pre-training while proABC-2 utilizes task-specific training data curated for paratope prediction.

Additionally, we extend our investigation through a case study of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) antibody binding (PDB: 6apb) to examine what the attention mechanism captures without explicit supervision (Fig. 2E). The attention visualization reveals that our model successfully captures all three CDRs in the heavy chain, as evidenced by the attention distributed across CDRH1, CDRH2, and CDRH3 (Fig. 2F). Although a focus on the CDRH3 region is a baseline expectation for antibody models, the internal distribution of these weights within the loop is particularly noteworthy. Instead of assigning uniform weights across the entire CDRH3 sequence, the attention heatmaps show a sharp concentration on residues at positions 108 through 110. These specific residues precisely overlap with the actual binding paratope defined in the structural data. This fine-grained alignment suggests that AbLingua can capture localized, functionally relevant features. Consequently, the model demonstrates a capacity to recover residue-level binding information despite the absence of explicit paratope annotation during training.

AbLingua enables accurate prediction of antibody neutralization across different SARS-CoV variants

Beyond residue-level prediction, we further investigate fundamental immunological processes that govern viral neutralization using AbLingua. Antibody binding and neutralization represent distinct molecular events in host–pathogen interactions. In the context of direct viral neutralization, precise epitope targeting that functionally inhibits viral entry mechanisms is required, whereas binding alone is insufficient to impede infection. In SARS-CoV infections, effective neutralization occurs when antibodies bind to critical spike protein epitopes that disrupt receptor recognition, while non-neutralizing antibodies bind without affecting viral infectivity.

This functional distinction poses a critical challenge in antibody engineering because most binding antibodies identified through high-throughput screening fail to exhibit potent neutralizing activity. Accurate prediction of neutralizing capacity directly from sequences would therefore accelerate therapeutic discovery by enabling the rapid identification of candidates with optimal protective properties against emerging viral variants. We employ language models for transfer learning and utilize ResNet architecture as the downstream model across six distinct SARS-CoV antibody datasets from CoV-AbDab, encompassing 11,008 antibody sequences targeting both N-terminal domain (NTD) and receptor-binding domain (RBD) epitopes across multiple SARS-CoV variants (Fig. 3B). Despite the inherent scarcity of antibodies that both bind and neutralize viral particles, we ensure a minimum 20% representation of neutralizing antibodies across all datasets, providing a reasonable proportion of positive examples for performance assessment (Fig. 3C). The experimental design incorporates both light and heavy chain sequences to capture the determinants of antibody-mediated neutralization.

Interestingly, our results demonstrate that the prediction performance across different SARS-CoV variants shows no correlation with the proportion of neutralizing antibodies in each dataset. Instead, our results suggest that the key determinant of prediction accuracy is the degree of mutation, with Omicron BA.1–BA.2 exhibiting relatively lower prediction accuracy. This may be attributed to the higher mutation rates in these variants, resulting in structural characteristics that deviate significantly from those in the training data. Notably, even for the most challenging variants, AbLingua-1.7B consistently maintains an AUROC performance of ~65%, indicating robustness to viral mutations (Fig. 3D).

Comparative evaluation demonstrates that AbLingua-1.7B exhibits superior performance in antibody neutralization prediction across diverse SARS-CoV variants. We observe that models pre-trained specifically on antibody sequences consistently achieve higher predictive accuracy than general-purpose protein language models such as ESM2. The performance gap is most pronounced in MCC evaluations, where ESM2 shows restricted efficacy while AbLingua maintains a substantial lead (Fig. S2). Furthermore, AbLingua displays high classification stability in both F1 and AUROC across various epitopes, whereas other models frequently experience performance degradation when analyzing antibodies targeting NTD or highly mutated variants (Figs. S3, S4). These findings indicate that specialized pre-training and optimized tokenization are essential for capturing the complex relationships between antibody sequence and function within antibody–antigen interactions.

AbLingua accelerates the therapeutic antibody discovery

Following our analysis of immunological processes, we next evaluate AbLingua in therapeutic antibody discovery, where identifying antibody sequences with suitable specificity and affinity remains a significant challenge. Current approaches require screening libraries of more than 109 antibody variants while optimizing multiple parameters simultaneously (expression, pharmacokinetics, solubility, immunogenicity), with success rates below 0.01%30. These screening limitations are particularly evident when targeting the heavy chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDRH3), which contributes significantly to antibody–antigen interactions31. The hyper-variable CDRH3 presents a considerable challenge due to its sequence diversity spanning a theoretical space of ~1013 possible variants for a 10-residue segment. This makes traditional screening approaches resource-intensive and time-consuming32. Given these constraints in conventional methods, we develop an alternative machine learning framework to accelerate the identification of functional candidates by leveraging the patterns found in high-throughput experimental data. We employ language models combined with downstream neural networks to predict binding using sequences derived from high-throughput screening30. The predictive power demonstrated here is most applicable to data-rich scenarios where extensive, target-specific libraries are available. While such screening volumes are not yet universal for all early-stage discovery targets, this framework establishes a robust precedent for accelerating lead optimization once initial screening data is acquired. Although high-throughput classification inherently involves experimental uncertainty, we validate our predictors on an independent set of sequences from gold-standard assays to ensure reliability in practical applications (Fig. 4A and B).

We systematically evaluate predictors that combine language models with neural architectures for therapeutic antibody discovery. The AbLingua-1.7B + ResNet model demonstrates superior performance on the validation set, achieving a recall of 58.0% and outperforming all other evaluated predictors (Figs. 4C, S5). For instance, it surpasses ESM2 + FC (57.0%), IgBert + ResNet (56.3%), and IgT5 + ResNet (55.2%), as well as other benchmarks such as AntiBERTa2 + FC (54.5%), AbLang2 + ResNet (54.0%), and AntiBERTy + FC (52.5%). These findings suggest that larger antibody language models paired with optimized downstream architectures effectively capture the complex relationships between antibody sequences and binding properties. Our ablation studies demonstrate that AbLingua-1.7B performance scales with the volume of training data. Validation recall improves from 36% to 58% as the training data expands (Fig. 4D). Notably, the AbLingua-1.7B+ResNet approach outperforms alternative methods on real-world test sets, where it achieves the highest metrics with a recall of 85% (Fig. 4E). Performance on the test set improves substantially with increased training data availability, which highlights the model’s robust generalization capabilities (Fig. 4F). In low-sample real-world testing scenarios, our model meets the requirements for antibody development by accurately identifying CDRH3 sequences with genuine binding capabilities. Our analysis of CDRH3 mutation position numbers reveals stable performance across different mutation counts. AbLingua-1.7B exhibits strong prediction capabilities at 4 mutation positions and maintains consistent performance as mutation counts increase to 8 positions within the CDRH3 region (Fig. 4G). This consistency across varying mutation counts indicates that our model has effectively learned the fundamental principles governing antibody binding rather than memorizing specific sequence patterns. Such results highlight its potential value for real-world therapeutic antibody discovery applications.

AbLingua provides meaningful representation across sequences and tokens

To gain deeper insights beyond supervised metrics, we conduct unsupervised visualization of the latent space using uniform manifold approximation projection (UMAP)33. This approach reveals how the language models naturally encode immunological information without explicit training. Understanding these organizational principles is critical for antibody research, as functional properties are determined by subtle sequence patterns that traditional methods often miss. The unsupervised latent space provides a window into how different models capture the biological relationships governing antibody development, specificity, and function, while preserving both local and global structure in the high-dimensional embeddings.

First, we embed B-cell sequences from five developmental stages and project them using UMAP (Figs. 4H, S6). AbLingua-1.7B effectively separates sequences by developmental stage, while ESM2 and AntiBERTy models achieve only partial separation. This distinct clustering ability suggests AbLingua captures subtle immunological signatures defining B-cell maturation pathways that other models fail to distinguish.

Second, we embed antibody sequences specific to HIV, SARS, and Ebola and project them onto a two-dimensional UMAP space (Figs. 4I, S7). AbLingua-1.7B demonstrates superior clustering for these virus-specific antibodies, compared to ESM2 model’s moderate clustering and AntiBERTy’s less well-defined clusters. This visualization reveals AbLingua's ability to capture functional properties related to antigen specificity, an essential feature for computational antibody design.

Following our sequence-level analysis, we investigate the token-level representational capabilities of AbLingua, which is critical for understanding how the model captures fine-grained features within antibody sequences. We project token embeddings from the final layer of language models into a two-dimensional UMAP space. The TripleAA employed in AbLingua enabled us to obtain contextualized representations for tokens such as ’GSG’, where ’S’ is the central amino acid. This allows us to directly compare how different models represent the same amino acid ’S’ when it appears in various contexts, revealing how surrounding residues influence the embedding of the central amino acid. For this analysis, we focus on the four most frequently occurring amino acids in antibody sequences (G, S, T, and A), allowing us to compare how models represent identical amino acids across varying contextual environments.

The projections reveal that AbLingua generates more discrete and diverse representations for identical amino acid characters in different contexts (Figs. 4J, S8), demonstrating effective capture of contextual information that reflects the unique functional properties of amino acids within specific environments. In contrast, ESM2-650M clusters most ’S’ and ’G’ tokens into a few centers, suggesting context-insensitive representations that may limit its ability to capture fine-grained biological features. Similarly, AntiBERTy generates nearly identical representations for the same amino acid across contexts, likely due to parameter constraints and limited training data.

Discussion

This work demonstrates that an antibody language model with our innovative TripleAA tokenization enables robust performance across diverse immunological tasks. By learning statistical properties of antibody sequences through this refined methodology, AbLingua achieves state-of-the-art capabilities in paratope prediction, neutralization assessment, and therapeutic discovery applications. To the best of our knowledge, AbLingua represents the pioneering antibody language model in demonstrating predictable scaling laws akin to natural language models, in contrast to previous studies that reported limited performance gains with increased model scale.

Moreover, by capturing structural information during pre-training through our tokenization methodology, we create embeddings that effectively support diverse downstream tasks without task-specific architectural modifications. Our approach builds on the established observation that antibody structures depend on combinations of residues rather than individual amino acids23, reformulating antibody sequences as functional units. Unlike traditional NLP tokenization, which merges multiple amino acids into a single token and thereby prevents proper alignment between individual amino acids and their corresponding labels in downstream tasks34, our approach expands vocabulary from 20 amino acids to ~20,000 tokens representing contextual residue patterns, enabling more effective learning of the patterns governing antibody–antigen interactions.

Our results demonstrate the broad applicability of our approach across multiple immunological contexts. The consistent performance across diverse applications indicates that AbLingua captures principles that generalize across immunological tasks. Furthermore, the unsupervised organization of B-cell developmental stages and disease-specific antibodies suggests that AbLingua has learned meaningful biological representations without explicit guidance, potentially providing insights into antibody evolution patterns that may inform future therapeutic development.

Despite these advances, several limitations in our approach warrant consideration. A notable limitation is our reliance on the BERT architecture rather than exploring generative models based on GPT architecture21,35,36. While BERT effectively models contextual relationships within sequences and provides meaningful embeddings for downstream tasks, generative models might provide additional capabilities for antibody design by producing novel sequences with desired properties. Additionally, although our tokenization approach implicitly captures some structural information, it does not explicitly incorporate three-dimensional conformations that influence antibody function37,38. This limitation suggests a direction for future research: integrating explicit structural data into larger-scale protein-language models to better capture the complex relationship between sequence, conformation, and function.

Future work should explore several promising directions to extend our approach. Multi-property optimization frameworks could simultaneously consider binding affinity, developability, and immunogenicity for more comprehensive antibody engineering39. Integration of antibody–antigen complex modeling could enhance predictive power for novel targets lacking experimental data12. Cross-species applications may reveal evolutionary constraints in antibody design that could inform the development of therapeutics for both human and zoonotic diseases40. Domain adaptation techniques could extend the model’s utility to related protein families such as T-cell receptors, expanding the methodological advances beyond antibodies to broader immunological applications41.

In conclusion, AbLingua advances antibody language modeling through improved tokenization methods and effective scaling. These developments establish a foundation for antibody engineering by demonstrating that tokenization-aware pre-training and predictable scaling can jointly improve performance across diverse immunological tasks. The integration of these findings with advances in generative modeling and structural biology presents opportunities for further improvements in antibody engineering methodologies that may accelerate therapeutic discovery.

Methods

Training and test data for pre-training

The data utilized for the pre-training of the AbLingua was derived from the Observed Antibody Space (OAS) database (accessed in September 2022)26. The OAS, established in 2018, aggregates millions of paired antibody sequences and several billion unpaired sequences. To maximize the utilization of the extensive antibody sequence data, we exclusively employed unpaired sequences for pre-training. Their greater quantity and diversity reduce data selection bias and enhance model robustness and generalization capabilities.

For constructing the pre-training dataset, we extracted 2.4 billion unpaired sequences from the OAS database. We then processed these raw antibody sequences through a three-stage pre-processing pipeline. First, we employed MMseqs2 Cluster42 with a sequence identity threshold of 0.9 to prevent data leakage between the pre-training, training, and test sets. Next, we removed sequences containing unknown amino acids (denoted by *) to ensure only standard amino acid letters were included in the model vocabulary. These pre-processing steps yielded 1.4 billion unpaired antibody sequences (1.2 billion heavy chains and 200 million light chains). Finally, we randomly allocated 0.5% of these sequences to the test set and assigned the remaining sequences to the training set, yielding the dataset termed OAS-Unpaired-1.4B. During the pre-training process, heavy chain and light chain sequences were randomly shuffled and combined into a unified training dataset, rather than being trained separately. This random mixing strategy allows the model to learn generalizable sequence representations from both chain types simultaneously. To accommodate computational constraints for smaller model variants, we sampled a subset named OAS-Unpaired-300M from OAS-Unpaired-1.4B, which was used specifically for training AbLingua-300M and AbLingua-600M. All CDRs within these datasets were annotated using ANARCI43 with the Chothia44 numbering system. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Supervised downstream task datasets

Antibody paratope prediction

To evaluate the performance of various language models in antibody paratope prediction, we collected structural data of antibody–antigen complexes from the SAbDab database45 and created an optimized dataset through a rigorous pre-processing pipeline46.

First, we filtered structures by removing those with crystallographic resolutions poorer than 3.0 Å and defined paratope contacts based on a 4.5 Å distance threshold between the antibody and the antigen. To prioritize genuine biological interfaces over crystallographic artifacts, we derived these contacts exclusively from the curated biological assemblies in SAbDab. We retained only complexes where both heavy and light chains interacted with the target, while excluding structures where the antigen contacted non-CDR regions exclusively. This selection strategy ensures that any identified framework region paratopes represent functional binding interactions rather than lattice packing effects. Next, we clustered the antibody sequences using CD-HIT47 with a sequence identity threshold of 0.99, followed by hierarchical clustering on the V genes of both light and heavy chains as assigned by IgBLAST48. We excluded clusters with fewer than three antibody sequences and chains with outlier numbers of contact sites. Finally, we partitioned the resulting light and heavy chain sequences into training, validation, and test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio to enable comprehensive model performance evaluation.

Antibody neutralization prediction

To comprehensively investigate the performance of language models in predicting antibody neutralization efficacy against viruses, we meticulously curated data from the CoV-AbDab database49. Established in 2020, CoV-AbDab has amassed an extensive collection of β-coronavirus-related antibody and nanobody data. The dataset used in this study was updated until June 2023.

To construct a robust and accurate antibody neutralization prediction dataset, we implemented a rigorous, multi-step processing pipeline for all sequences in CoV-AbDab. First, we categorized the data based on the binding specificity of antibodies to different viruses, assigning antibody sequences to corresponding datasets according to their ability to bind to a designated virus. Next, we quantified the proportion of sequences within each dataset exhibiting neutralizing capacity. While most studies report antibody sequences possessing both binding and neutralizing capabilities, sequences with binding ability but lacking neutralizing capacity are comparatively scarce. To ensure dataset diversity, we included antibody datasets with varying proportions of sequences lacking neutralizing capacity, ranging from 20% to 50%. Furthermore, we considered the potential influence of antibody interactions with distinct viral binding sites, such as the NTD or RBD, on neutralizing activity50. Consequently, we segregated sequences exhibiting binding capacity to the NTD or RBD into separate datasets, enabling precise model training and evaluation at a finer granularity. Ultimately, we generated six datasets: two for predicting antibody neutralization of the viral NTD region and four for the RBD region. This multi-step processing approach facilitated the construction of a comprehensive, diverse, and targeted antibody-neutralization prediction dataset, providing a solid foundation for subsequent model development.

Therapeutic antibody discovery

To evaluate the performance of language models in real-world therapeutic antibody discovery, we utilized the designed CDRH3 sequences and experimental validation CDRH3 sequences from ref. 30. For training and validation of the language model-based binding predictor, we employed designed antibody CDRH3 sequences derived from a complex, multi-step process involving biological experiments and computational tools. Since these CDRH3 sequences were derived from methods that may not represent the gold standard in the field, the labels of the designed data are inevitably error-prone, resulting in label noise in both the training and validation datasets. The process began with generating a non-binding trastuzumab variant with a mutated CDRH3 sequence in a hybridoma cell line. Homology-directed mutagenesis (HDM)51 mediated by CRISPR-Cas952 was then used to create libraries targeting the CDRH3. These libraries were screened using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)53, and binding and non-binding populations were isolated and subjected to deep sequencing. A combinatorial mutagenesis library was rationally designed based on the deep mutational scanning (DMS)54 data. Antigen-binding cells were isolated through two rounds of FACS enrichment, and binding or non-binding populations were deep-sequenced, identifying 11,300 unique binders and 27,539 unique non-binders.

To assess the reliability and robustness of the language model-based binding predictor on real therapeutic antibody sequences, we utilized data from the study by Mason et al. The researchers selected CDRH3 sequences from designed data and validated their binding capacity using the bio-layer interferometer (BLI)55. These experimentally validated sequences were used as the test set to evaluate the predictor’s performance on real-world data.

Unsupervised downstream task datasets

Virus-specific antibody sequences

To evaluate the performance of antibody and protein language models in a realistic disease antibody classification task, we curated a dataset from the OAS database26. The dataset consists of antibody sequences for three viruses: human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Ebola virus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), ensuring that these sequences were excluded from the pre-training data. We collected 1000 unique antibody sequences for each virus and preprocessed them using CD-HIT47 with a sequence identity threshold of 0.99 to remove highly similar sequences.

B-cell developmental stage sequences

To investigate the effectiveness of language model sequence representations in unsupervised visualization tasks, we utilized a comprehensive dataset of antibody heavy chain sequences spanning various B cell developmental stages. The dataset was generated by isolating CD19+ cells from the blood of a healthy female subject. CD19 is a B-cell-specific marker present in all developmental stages except plasma cells56. These stages include immature, transitional, mature, memory, and plasma cells. To ensure a balanced representation of each developmental stage and facilitate computational analysis, we randomly sampled 10,000 non-redundant antibody heavy chain sequences from each of the five B cell developmental stages. Non-redundant sequences differ at the amino-acid level, thus avoiding duplicates in the dataset. This sampling process yielded 50,000 sequences, with approximately 10,000 sequences per developmental stage, ensuring an equal distribution across the five stages. Equalizing the number of sequences per stage mitigated potential biases arising from uneven class sizes.

AbLingua architecture

AbLingua is built upon the BERT architecture57, with architectural modifications and training strategies tailored to antibody sequence data. To better capture positional relationships during pre-training, AbLingua incorporates relative positional embeddings58 instead of the absolute positional embeddings used in BERT. This approach introduces positional information into the attention mechanism, enabling the model to learn richer representations of the spatial relationships between amino acids in the antibody sequences. In the tokenization process, we employed the TripleAA to enhance AbLingua and capture antibody sequences' structural properties. We introduced the context residue transformation (CRT) strategy to adapt the MLM task to the TripleAA. In contrast to the original MLM task, where all tokens have a 15% probability of being masked, AbLingua assigns a higher masking probability of 30% to tokens in the CDR regions. This increased masking probability in the functionally important CDR regions enables the model to focus on learning meaningful representations of these critical segments. During the pre-training, we set the maximum sequence length to 256 tokens, which was empirically determined to enable efficient learning while maintaining computational efficiency. This choice was supported by the observation that most antibody sequences in the OAS dataset were shorter than 150 amino acids, ensuring that the selected maximum sequence length adequately captures the essential information in most antibody sequences. Within each batch, all sequences were padded to match the length of the longest sequence, ensuring consistent input dimensions for the model.

AbLingua offers three model sizes for various computational requirements and task complexities: 300M, 600M, and 1.7B. The 300M model is well-suited for small-scale antibody downstream tasks and serves as a benchmark for evaluating the performance of models at different scales. The 600M model, a commonly used parameter range for antibody and protein downstream tasks, is also frequently employed in structure prediction tasks based on language models. The 1.7B model provides high-quality representations and is particularly valuable in scenarios where extensive fine-tuning is not feasible, making transfer learning a more suitable approach. By providing models with different parameter sizes, AbLingua accommodates a wide range of antibody research tasks, from small-scale antibody analysis to large-scale structure prediction and function exploration. This flexibility positions AbLingua as a versatile antibody language model that can be applied in various domains, including antibody engineering, drug development, and disease diagnosis.

TripleAA tokenization

We present TripleAA, a specialized tokenization algorithm designed for antibody language models to capture complex structural dependencies within amino-acid sequences. This approach is informed by the observation that the three-dimensional conformation of antibodies, particularly the critical dihedral angles (ϕ, ψ) governing protein folding, depends significantly on interactions between adjacent residues rather than individual amino acids in isolation. Traditional amino-acid-level tokenization is inherently limited by a small vocabulary of approximately 20 tokens, which fails to adequately represent the structural complexity of antibody sequences. The TripleAA algorithm addresses this limitation by treating tripeptide units as cohesive structural elements.

While general natural language processing techniques have been widely applied to biological sequences, their direct implementation often introduces significant limitations. For instance, the adoption of byte pair encoding (BPE)59 for protein sequences often introduces variable-length tokens that represent multiple amino acids simultaneously. Such schemes lack a consistent one-to-one or fixed-length mapping between tokens and residues, which complicates the interpretation of specific latent representations at the residue level. This phenomenon, where tokens encompass heterogeneous and variable sequence spans, is referred to as the label island problem60. In contrast, the TripleAA scheme ensures that sequence information remains traceable by maintaining a uniform structural resolution. This approach avoids the inconsistent granularity associated with label islands while providing the increased contextual window necessary for precise antibody modeling.

Context residue transformation for masked language modeling

The masked language modeling (MLM) task forms the foundation of antibody language model pre-training. To optimize this approach for antibody sequences, we developed context residue transformation (CRT), a specialized adaptation designed specifically to work with TripleAA. CRT enhances AbLingua to learn meaningful contextual representations through two complementary strategies: (1) joint mask strategy and (2) joint replacement strategy. The joint mask strategy prevents information leakage by masking all tokens containing a selected central residue, while the joint replacement strategy maintains biological coherence by consistently replacing the central residue across all affected tokens while preserving natural amino acid distributions.

This approach aligns naturally with antibody biology, where specific residues within their local structural context significantly influence protein folding and functional properties. By encouraging the model to learn representations that account for the influence of surrounding residues, CRT enables more effective modeling of the complex dependencies that govern antibody functionality. The combination of TripleAA with CRT creates a powerful framework for capturing the subtle patterns in sequence–structure–function relationships that are essential for accurate prediction of antibody properties and behavior.

Joint mask strategy

When a residue xi is targeted for masking in CRT, we must ensure that all information about this central residue is completely removed from the visible context. Within the TripleAA framework, each internal residue (excluding terminal positions) is incorporated into exactly three consecutive tripeptide tokens. For a selected residue xi, these affected tokens are Ti−1 = (xi−2xi−1xi), Ti = (xi−1xixi+1), and Ti+1 = (xixi+1xi+2).

To prevent any information leakage, our joint mask strategy replaces all three tokens containing the target residue xi with the [MASK] identifier simultaneously. Joint replacement strategy

Rather than replacing entire tokens with random selections, our approach focuses on replacing only the central residue of the selected unit while maintaining structural consistency across all affected tokens. If a residue xi is selected for replacement, it is substituted with a randomly sampled residue xr based on the natural frequency distribution of amino acids in the training set.

To ensure biological coherence, all tokens containing xi must be updated in a coordinated manner. AbLingua pre-training details

AbLingua models were pre-trained using the AdamW optimizer61 with learning rates adjusted based on model parameters. The training process was implemented using PyTorch-Lightning62, leveraging the BERT implementation from Transformers63 for the model architecture. The training was accelerated using Deepspeed64 to optimize computational efficiency. A warmup period of 10% of the total training steps was applied for all model sizes to stabilize the learning process and prevent early overfitting. The AbLingua-300M and AbLingua-600M models were trained on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, while the larger AbLingua-1.7B model was trained on 24 NVIDIA A100 GPUs to accommodate its increased computational requirements. All models were trained on the full dataset without early stopping, ensuring that the models had sufficient exposure to the entire antibody sequence space. Specific pre-training hyperparameters, settings, time consumption, and other relevant details for each model are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistics and reproducibility

To ensure the technical reproducibility and statistical significance of our findings, we implemented a rigorous evaluation protocol. All experiments were conducted over five independent runs (n = 5), each utilizing a distinct random seed to account for stochastic variations in model initialization and data partitioning. We report the mean and standard deviation (SD) for all performance metrics across these five runs to provide a reliable measure of model stability and performance variance. No data meeting the inclusion criteria were excluded from the analyses. Randomization was implemented through random seed variation across independent runs. The model performance was comprehensively assessed through a suite of standardized metrics, including Accuracy, Recall, F1 score, Precision, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). These metrics were computed using TorchMetrics66 with the macro averaging strategy to ensure equal weighting across all classes. To determine the statistical significance of the improvements achieved by our proposed model, we performed the Wilcoxon signed-rank test against each baseline method. These statistical tests and all subsequent data analyses were implemented using SciPy67. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
