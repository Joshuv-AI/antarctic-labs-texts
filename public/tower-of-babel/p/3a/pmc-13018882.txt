Improvement in low back pain following endoscopic decompression for spinal stenosis
Authors: Maria Auron, Yihyun Roh, Maria C Pedraza Ciro, Win Boonsirikamchai, Yi-Hao Liang, Jung Hoon Kim, Jin-Sung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) commonly presents with neurogenic claudication and low back pain (LBP). Decompression surgery effectively relieves leg symptoms, but its impact on LBP remains inconsistent. While full-endoscopic decompression has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative, evidence regarding its specific effect on LBP is limited.Research questionTo evaluate the improvement in LBP following full-endoscopic decompression for LSS with or without low-grade degenerative spondylolisthesis (SPL), and to identify the clinical and radiological factors influencing these changes.Materials and methodsConsecutive patients undergoing full-endoscopic decompression for LSS between 2017 and 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Introduction

The prevalence of lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is high in the aging population (Kalichman et al., 2009), often resulting in significantly impaired health-related quality of life and disability (Otani et al., 2013). Currently, symptomatic stenosis is the leading indication for lumbar spine surgery in older adults, with strong evidence supporting its effectiveness in relieving leg symptoms and improving function and disability (Weinstein et al., 2008; Amundsen et al., 2000). It has been observed that some patients experience considerable relief from low back pain (LBP) following lumbar decompression (Jones et al., 2013; Srinivas et al., 2019; Knight et al., 2023; Kitagawa et al., 2021); nonetheless, the improvement in LBP is often considered unreliable (Divi et al., 2021; Oba et al., 2017; Herkowitz and Kurz, 1991). This may reflect the complex and poorly understood nature of axial pain in LSS, with possible contributors including disc and facet degeneration, neural compression, and sagittal misalignment (Katz et al., 2022; Park et al., 2023; Knezevic et al., 2021).

Conventional open decompression for LSS is effective but may lead to morbidity from extensive soft-tissue dissection and risk of iatrogenic instability (Guha et al., 2015; Ha et al., 2020). Especially in cases of concomitant degenerative spondylolisthesis with significant axial back pain, fusion is often recommended to address potential concerns about instability and achieve more consistent pain relief (Herkowitz and Kurz, 1991; Ghogawala et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2018). Minimally invasive techniques in spine surgery were developed to minimize soft tissue trauma, thereby maintaining the dynamic stability of the spine (Kim, 2009; Oertel et al., 2006; Spetzger et al., 1997). Endoscopic spine surgery has gained global interest over the past two decades, with continuously evolving techniques (Wu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023a, 2023b). Compared to open or microscopic methods, endoscopic lumbar decompression is associated with less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and quicker recovery, making it a viable treatment for spinal stenosis (Komp et al., 2015; Kotheeranurak et al., 2023; Perez-Roman et al., 2022; Yoshikane et al., 2023; Ruetten et al., 2009).

Despite its growing use, the effects of full-endoscopic lumbar decompression on low back pain have not been well characterized. This study aimed to evaluate improvement in LBP following full-endoscopic decompression for LSS and to identify clinical and radiological factors associated with achieving a clinically meaningful change.

Materials and methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from a consecutive series of patients who underwent full-endoscopic decompression for lumbar spinal stenosis at our institution between January 2017 and February 2024 (illustrative cases shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Indications for decompression only were patients with central spinal stenosis with back and lower extremity pain without radiographic instability requiring further surgical intervention (sagittal translation >4 mm on dynamic lateral radiographs and/or sagittal range of motion >15°). Patients with a minimum of 1-year clinical follow-up were included. Exclusion criteria were: baseline Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) back pain score of <2.0, concomitant discectomy, symptomatic stenosis involving three levels or more, lumbar scoliosis ≥15°, degenerative spondylolisthesis of Meyerding grade ≥3, and prior lumbar fusion. A flowchart of patient selection is presented in Fig. 3. The study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (IRBN: KC25RISI0172), and the requirement for signing an Informed Consent Form was waived. For patients featured as illustrative cases, written consent was obtained for publication.

Surgical procedure

Full-endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression (ULBD) through the interlaminar window was performed as previously described (Komp et al., 2011). In brief, the patient was positioned in the prone position under general anesthesia. A single paraspinous stab wound was made over the affected vertebral level, followed by the insertion of a dilator and working sleeve under fluoroscopic guidance. The endoscope was inserted through the working sleeve, and flexible radiofrequency was used for hemostasis and soft tissue dissection to expose the laminae. Bony pathology and ligamentum flavum were removed using endoscopic drills, forceps, and a Kerrison punch under full-endoscopic visualization. Contralateral decompression was performed over-the-top, by ventral subspinous and sublaminar drilling. A surgical drain was placed on the decompression site at the surgeon's discretion and removed after 1 to 3 days. The skin incision was closed with a non-absorbable suture. Patients were mobilized without a corset the same or next day after the surgery.

Study measures

Our primary outcome was reaching the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) in back pain in the VAS 10-point scale. As described by the Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) consensus, the MCID was considered as a VAS reduction of two points (Dworkin et al., 2008). When analyzing MCID in relation to different levels of preoperative back pain, it is important to consider how baseline pain severity influences the threshold for meaningful improvement. Therefore, we conducted a separate analysis for patients with mild (VAS 2-4) versus moderate-to-severe (VAS ≥5) back pain (Turner et al., 2004; Serlin et al., 1995). The secondary outcome measures were the VAS for leg pain, the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) (Fairbank and Pynsent, 2000) and modified MacNab (mMacNab) criteria. Subsequently, a comparative analysis was performed based on the preoperative presence of low-grade degenerative spondylolisthesis. Patients were divided into two groups: the SPL group, defined by an initial slip percentage between 10% and 50% (including Meyerding grades 1 and 2), and the non-SPL group (<10% slip).

Demographic data (age, sex, BMI, smoking status, ASA class, follow-up duration) were collected from electronic records. Clinical and radiological assessments were done at baseline and for all follow-up time periods, including 1, 3, 6 and 12 months, and yearly thereafter, as part of routine clinical care. Diagnosis of lumbar spinal stenosis, assessment of spinal canal cross-sectional area (CSA) and Modic change type were made by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Modic et al., 1988; Andreisek et al., 2014). Spondylolisthesis slip percentage and lateral listhesis were evaluated by standing lumbar X-ray (Freedman et al., 2009). Sagittal parameters, including sagittal vertebral axis (SVA), lumbar lordosis (LL), segmental lordosis (SLL), thoracolumbar kyphosis (TLK) (Schwab et al., 2002), pelvic incidence (PI) and pelvic tilt (PT), were measured by full-length standing X-ray. Facet joint opening (FJO) was determined by preoperative CT (Hasegawa et al., 2011). Operation time, estimated blood loss, number, and levels of surgery, length of hospitalization, complications, and reoperations at index or adjacent level were recorded from patient charts.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and Graphpad Prism 10. The data are presented as descriptive statistics, means with SD, and as counts with percentage. For pairwise comparisons, normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Consequently, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used when appropriate. Predictive factor analysis was conducted using univariable and multivariable logistic regression to assess factors associated with achieving MCID in VAS back pain. Univariable analysis was first performed for all clinically relevant baseline and radiographic variables. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model, with preoperative and postoperative variables analyzed in separate models. Model fit was assessed using Cox & Snell R2, Nagelkerke R2, Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and classification percentage. Patients lost to follow-up were excluded from outcome analysis but included in baseline data to assess selection bias. Missing data was handled automatically without imputation. Statistical significance was accepted at P < 0.05.

Results

Sixty-four patients with a mean follow-up 24.9 (range 12 – 84) months were included in the final analysis. 53.1% were females, the mean age was 63.8 (range 35 – 85) years, and the mean BMI was 25.9 kg/m2. The majority of patients (87.5%) had no history of smoking. Low-grade degenerative spondylolisthesis was present in 23 patients (SPL group: 35.9%), with a mean slippage of 16.3% (range 10.5 - 25.7%). Only one patient met the criteria for Meyerding Grade II spondylolisthesis (25.7% slip), while the remainder were classified as Grade I. One- and 2-level surgeries were done in 61 (95.3%) and 3 (4.7%) patients, respectively. The most common level of surgery was L4-5 (70.1% of cases). The mean surgical time was 169.6 ± 49.0 min. Surgical complications occurred in 3 patients (4.7%), all of whom required reoperation: delayed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks due to incidental dural tears in 2 patients (3.1%) and postoperative hematoma in 1 patient (1.6%). In addition to these early reoperations, two patients from the non-SPL group (3.1%) underwent delayed fusion, one at 17 months for spondylolisthesis slip progression and one at 3 years for persistent low back pain. The total reoperation rate was 7.8% (n = 5). No postoperative infections or wound healing complications occurred in the cohort (Table 1). Baseline PROMs were available in all 64 patients. Follow-up PROMs were available for 58 patients (90.6%) at 12 months and 64 patients (100.0%) beyond 12 months. Radiological follow-up was available for 54 patients (84.4%) at 12 months and 63 patients (98.4%) beyond 12 months.

Clinical outcomes

An MCID in low back pain at 12 months was achieved in 84.5% of all patients and at last follow-up in 79.7% of patients. Mean preoperative VAS back score was 5.78, decreasing to 1.53 at 12 months and 1.93 at last follow-up (p < 0.001). Analyses were stratified according to preoperative back pain severity. A statistically significant reduction in VAS back pain was found both in patients with moderate to severe preoperative LBP (p < 0.001) and patients with mild preoperative LBP (p = 0.012). However, patients with moderate to severe preoperative LBP were more likely to achieve MCID than those with mild LBP at the 12-month follow-up (93.2% vs. 57.1%, p = 0.004), whereas by the last follow-up, the MCID achievement rates were 88% and 50%, respectively (p = 0.002). For the secondary outcomes, VAS leg pain and ODI showed statistically significant improvements at both 12 month and final follow-up. Additionally, the mMacNab criteria also reflected good to excellent outcomes (Table 2).

Low-grade spondylolisthesis

In subgroup analysis by presence of low-grade degenerative SPL, SPL patients were older (67.8 vs. 61.6 years), more often female (69.6% vs. 43.9%), and had a slightly longer hospital stay than non-SPL patients, with no other significant demographic or surgical differences between groups (supplementary table A). Both subgroups demonstrated a significant reduction in VAS back at the last follow-up (p < 0.001). MCID in VAS low back pain was achieved in a higher proportion of patients in the non-SPL group compared to the SPL group (at 12-month FU: 86.5% vs. 81.0%, p = 0.710; last FU: 80.5% vs. 78.3%, p = 0.832), though the difference was not statistically significant (Table 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Radiological assessment

Radiographic analysis demonstrated a significant postoperative increase in canal CSA (p < 0.001), along with improved sagittal lumbopelvic parameters including LL (42.2° vs. 46.8°, p < 0.001), SLL (16.3° vs. 17.7°, p = 0.017), PT (19.2° vs. 17.7°, p = 0.014) and PI-LL (7.8° vs. 3.5°, p < 0.001) at last follow-up. However, no significant postoperative changes were observed in SVA, TLK, scoliosis, lateral listhesis or SPL slip percentage. At all time points, the non-SPL group showed significantly better PI-LL, global alignment, and PT than the SPL group. Overall, there was no significant progression of scoliosis or SPL slip percentage in either subgroup. LL and PI-LL improved significantly in both subgroups. SLL showed significant improvement in the non-SPL group only (p = 0.002 and p = 0.048 at 12 months and last FU, respectively). On the other hand, significant improvement of PT and SS was demonstrated in the SPL-group at 12-months, only (p = 0.003). To sum up, we observed a postoperative lumbar sagittal alignment improvement after surgery in the total cohort. On analysis by SPL status, both groups demonstrated improvement in lumbopelvic alignment after surgery, with the non-SPL group showing overall better alignment at the last follow-up (Table 4).

Factors associated with MCID achievement

In the primary analyses for the 64 patients, the predictors closely associated with achieving MCID at last FU were preoperative VAS back pain severity (p = 0.010), LL (p = 0.030) and SS (p = 0.037). Additionally, LL and SS at last FU also turned out to be associated with MCID achievement (p = 0.015 and 0.041, respectively). Patients with baseline Modic 1 had over 5 times lower odds of achieving MCID (OR = 0.199, p = 0.062), while those with SVA <40 mm at last FU were over 4 times more likely to achieve MCID (OR = 4.062, p = 0.073). When accounting for confounders, higher preoperative VAS back score remained the only statistically significant factor correlated with achieving MCID (OR = 2.309, 95% CI 1.31 - 4.08, p = 0.004) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study following full-endoscopic lumbar decompression for LSS, we observed significant improvements in back pain, leg pain, and ODI, with MCID for VAS back pain achieved in 84.5% of patients at a minimum of 1-year follow-up and maintained in 79.7% of patients at last follow up. Significant relief of back pain was observed regardless of low-grade SPL. Greater baseline back pain severity was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving MCID (OR = 2.309). In this cohort, we also observed a postoperative improvement in lumbar sagittal alignment, with no significant worsening of spondylolisthesis or global alignment over time. The overall surgical complication rate was 4.7%, and the total reoperation rate was 7.8%, including a 3.1% rate of delayed fusion.

While lumbar decompression for spinal stenosis effectively alleviates neurogenic claudication and radicular pain, previous studies have highlighted its inconsistent effect on low back pain (Divi et al., 2021; Oba et al., 2017; Herkowitz and Kurz, 1991). Axial low back pain in LSS is complex and likely multifactorial, involving nociceptive, neuropathic, and pain sensitization mechanisms (Katz et al., 2022; Knezevic et al., 2021). Up to 80% of patients with low back pain and accompanying radicular symptoms may have a neurogenic component related to neural compression (Attal et al., 2011). Nociceptive pain results from stimulation of structures such as the facet joints, intervertebral discs, and vertebral endplates. Growing evidence supports vertebrogenic pain, mediated by the sinuvertebral nerve and its branch, the basivertebral nerve, as a key contributor to axial low back pain (Mohd et al., 2022; Quinones et al., 2021). Although the exact pain source is often difficult to localize, diagnostic injections may aid in identifying a neurogenic component and help predict the likely benefit of a decompression-only procedure (Voelker et al., 2024). The rationale for additional fusion is to stabilize the spine with added indirect decompression of neural structures, thereby addressing multiple potential sources of back pain. While central decompression targets mainly the dura and nerve roots, the rationale for additional fusion is to stabilize the spine with added indirect decompression of neural structures, thereby addressing multiple potential sources of back pain. This has led many surgeons to opt for spinal fusion procedures in cases where low back pain is predominant or significant, particularly in the presence of low-grade spondylolisthesis. In fact, the number of spinal fusion surgeries for degenerative lumbar pathologies continues to rise despite increasing evidence questioning its necessity in certain cases (Sastry et al., 2023; Kgomotso et al., 2024).

Our findings align with other studies demonstrating that decompression alone can provide substantial back pain relief (Srinivas et al., 2019; Knight et al., 2023; Ikuta et al., 2016; Kakiuchi et al., 2021; Taiji et al., 2021). Notably, some studies did not account for surgical technique (Srinivas et al., 2019; Knight et al., 2023), making it difficult to distinguish the effects of minimally invasive versus open procedures on LBP outcomes. Endoscopic spine surgery provides several advantages compared to conventional techniques, including less damage to soft tissues, reduced blood loss, a lower rate of complications, shorter hospital stays, and a faster return to work (Komp et al., 2015; Kotheeranurak et al., 2023; Perez-Roman et al., 2022; Ruetten et al., 2009; Chin et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024). A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported a clear trend of reduced early postoperative back pain with endoscopic versus microscopic decompression (Chin et al., 2024). Potential factors associated with residual back pain after surgical decompression include loss of muscle mass, inadequate decompression, iatrogenic instability, spinopelvic sagittal imbalance, degenerative disease progression, adjacent segment disease, and epidural fibrosis (Park et al., 2023; Sebaaly et al., 2018). Endoscopic techniques offer enhanced visualization and soft tissue preservation, which may mitigate postoperative muscle changes and lower the risk of iatrogenic instability (Chen et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023). To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to specifically evaluate the outcomes regarding axial back pain improvement following full-endoscopic decompression.

Previous studies have found associations between residual LBP and radiographic factors such as preoperative lumbar lordosis and limited range of motion (Xia et al., 2008), thoracolumbar kyphosis (Kitagawa et al., 2021) and a higher postoperative SVA (Hikata et al., 2015). We were unable to identify imaging findings significantly associated with achieving MCID in multivariable analysis. However, patients with postoperative SVA<40 mm showed a positive trend toward reaching MCID, suggesting a role of global sagittal balance in improving low back pain outcomes. Modic 1 change, reflecting inflammatory endplate change early in the degenerative process, has in large cohort studies been associated with low back pain (Toyone et al., 1994; Mera et al., 2021; Brinjikji et al., 2015), and in some studies found to have a correlation with residual back pain after lumbar decompression (Kakiuchi et al., 2021; Taiji et al., 2021). Similarly, we observed a negative impact of preoperative Modic 1 changes on the likelihood of achieving MCID, suggesting that purely neural decompression may be less effective for axial pain originating from vertebrogenic pain pathways associated with inflammatory endplate changes. Further research is needed to clarify the role of radiological parameters in surgical decision-making, including the significance of Modic endplate changes and the associated basivertebral nerve as a potential therapeutic target.

Current evidence indicates that decompression-only procedures may be adequate for managing low-grade degenerative spondylolisthesis (Ghogawala et al., 2016; Corluka et al., 2025). Less invasive techniques preserving posterior elements may offer superior outcomes, though the topic remains controversial (Ikuta et al., 2016; Montano et al., 2018; Muthu et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024). In our cohort, SPL did not significantly impact back pain relief, and most patients reported reduced pain after surgery. Similarly, in a retrospective study by Ikuta et al. (2016), relief of low back pain was achieved in 73% of selected patients who underwent minimally invasive decompression for LSS with grade I SPL. In addition, they noted an improved lumbopelvic alignment in those with pain relief, suggesting a potential link between the relief of LBP and restoration of the lumbar lordosis, as supported by other previous studies (Dohzono et al., 2016; Fujii et al., 2015; Jeon et al., 2015; Hatakka et al., 2021). In our study, postoperative improvement of the lumbar sagittal alignment was significant for patients with and without SPL. Notably, no patient from the SPL-group required an additional fusion procedure during follow-up. Our findings suggest that the full-endoscopic technique may be a favorable decompression-only procedure of choice whenever possible for patients with stable low-grade spondylolisthesis. However, the relationship between changes in radiographic alignment and the relief of low back pain after decompression-only remains unclear, and further research is needed to resolve this topic.

The strengths of this study include a well-defined cohort with a relatively long follow-up, a detailed analysis of MCID in back pain based on preoperative severity, and the use of both clinical and radiographic parameters to assess outcomes. As previously reported, clinically significant changes in pain are non-uniform along the VAS scale, with numerically equal gains that may differ in their meaning depending on baseline health status (Stucki et al., 1996; Salaffi et al., 2004). We observed a higher treatment effect in patients with higher preoperative pain levels, similar to results reported by the CSORN study (Srinivas et al., 2019). Patients with mild low back pain reported significantly improved pain scores, yet a lower proportion achieved the MCID, which may reflect a ceiling effect - though it does not exclude the possibility that even small changes could be clinically meaningful in this population. Nonetheless, based on our results, even the presence of severe preoperative low back pain does not appear to support the routine addition of fusion to full-endoscopic decompression for spinal stenosis with or without low-grade degenerative SPL. However, it is important to note that all patients had radiologically confirmed spinal stenosis and underwent surgery primarily for lower-limb symptoms, not for isolated back pain, and that we do not advocate decompression as a treatment for nonspecific low back pain.

There are several limitations to our study. The retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size restrict the generalizability of our findings and power of statistical analysis. All surgeries and postoperative follow-ups were conducted by a single surgeon in a non-blinded manner, although patients completed the questionnaire independently before clinic follow-up. Another limitation is the absence of a control group to assess the effect of endoscopic methods and traditional open or MIS techniques. As for the common confounding factors considered, we cannot exclude that there may still be important factors that have not been accounted for. Specifically, we did not analyze the duration of preoperative back pain symptoms or the time for MCID achievement. Duration of LBP has been proposed to have a role in persistent back pain after decompression for LSS (Kakiuchi et al., 2021).Furthermore, the present study did not analyze the association between postoperative soft tissues or muscle changes and residual back pain, especially with regard to preserving spinal stability in long-term follow-up. Despite these limitations, we believe that our results support the relevance of our findings in the context of real-world surgical decision-making.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a significant improvement in back pain following full-endoscopic lumbar decompression for lumbar spinal stenosis with or without low-grade degenerative SPL. Higher preoperative back pain was identified as a significant predictor of achieving MCID, which indicates that severe preoperative low back pain does not seem to contraindicate full-endoscopic decompression alone for LSS. The presence of low-grade SPL did not negatively affect MCID achievement. Radiographic analysis showed improved postoperative sagittal lumbopelvic alignment after surgery, although no specific radiographic parameters were identified as predictors of clinically meaningful back pain improvement. Our findings support the efficacy and long-term stability of endoscopic decompression for lumbar spinal stenosis, including degenerative low-grade spondylolisthesis. While the results are encouraging, further discussion is needed to clarify the role of full-endoscopic surgery in the management of various degenerative lumbar conditions.
