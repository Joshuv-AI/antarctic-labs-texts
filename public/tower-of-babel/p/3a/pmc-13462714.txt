[68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET enables non-invasive monitoring of hepatic fibrosis progression during TGF-β inhibition
Authors: Yeeun Kim, Myeongguk Jeong, Go-Eun Choi, Hyeokjin Kwon, Hyun Jin Yoon, Young-Jin Jeong, Ji Yong Park, Do-Young Kang, Sungmin Jun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatic fibrosis, a precursor to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, affects millions worldwide but lacks effective therapies and depends on invasive liver biopsies for diagnosis, which hinder early intervention and monitoring. Non-invasive imaging with [68Ga]Ga-fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI) positron emission tomography (PET) targets fibroblast activation protein (FAP) in fibrotic tissues, enabling assessment of disease progression without biopsy risks. This preclinical study evaluated pharmacological TGF-β inhibition using A83-01 and assessed [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET as a quantitative imaging biomarker. Hepatic fibrosis was induced in rats by thioacetamide (TAA) and evaluated at 4, 6, and 8 weeks. TAA administration caused progressive liver injury, increased collagen deposition, and elevated fibrotic marker expression. Although A83-01 effectively suppressed serum TGF-β1 levels and markedly reduced SMAD2/3 phosphorylation, fibrosis progression was not attenuated. Instead, α-SMA and FAP expression remained elevated, with higher levels observed at 8 weeks in the TAA + A83-01 group, indicating persistent fibroblast activation despite canonical pathway inhibition. Importantly, [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET demonstrated a time-dependent increase in hepatic tracer uptake and provided clear differentiation between control and fibrotic livers, closely reflecting histological and molecular findings. Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis, a pathological consequence of chronic liver injury, represents a critical stage in the progression toward cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, posing a significant global health burden1–3. Cirrhosis ranks as the 12th leading cause of death in the United States and contributes to over one million deaths annually worldwide, driven by factors such as viral hepatitis, alcohol abuse, and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis4,5. Importantly, hepatic fibrosis is potentially reversible in its early stages; however, advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis induces largely irreversible pathological changes that are strongly associated with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development. Notably, 80–90% of HCC cases arise in the context of underlying advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis. Thus, early detection of hepatic fibrosis and timely therapeutic intervention are critical to halt disease progression and mitigate HCC risk6,7. However, conventional blood-based inflammatory markers, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, along with computed tomography (CT), have limited sensitivity and specificity for accurately assessing hepatic inflammation and fibrosis progression8. In addition, liver biopsy, although considered the reference standard, is invasive and associated with procedural risks and sampling variability, providing only limited spatial information9. Collectively, these limitations highlight the urgent need for novel non-invasive diagnostic strategies with improved accuracy for fibrosis detection, staging, and monitoring.

Central to hepatic fibrogenesis is the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), which transition from a quiescent state to myofibroblast-like cells, leading to excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition10,11. Activated HSCs exhibit increased expression of α-SMA and collagens types I and III, resulting in scar tissue formation that disrupts liver architecture12. This process involves complex interactions among inflammation, oxidative stress, and growth factor signaling pathways, perpetuating a fibrotic microenvironment13–15. Among these pathways, TGF-β plays a pivotal role as the most potent profibrogenic cytokine in hepatic fibrosis, promoting HSC activation and ECM production15. TGF-β signaling is primarily mediated through receptor binding, which induces phosphorylation of SMAD2/3 proteins, facilitating transcriptional regulation of fibrogenic genes16. Given its central involvement, targeting the TGF-β pathway has shown promise in inhibiting fibrosis in multiple studies, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target17. Despite advances in understanding its mechanisms, no approved antifibrotic therapies exist, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies and diagnostic tools18,19.

Fibroblast Activation Protein (FAP) is recognized as a novel biomarker for fibrosis, showing a stronger correlation with fibrosis grades compared to established markers such as α-SMA, hyaluronic acid, and collagen20,21. Primarily derived from activated HSCs, FAP quantitatively reflects fibrosis severity and demonstrates excellent diagnostic performance in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis22. Based on this, the fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI) tracer is a novel approach for non-invasive imaging that specifically targets FAP expression in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) or fibrotic tissues23. Recently, a study in a swine model of hepatic fibrosis demonstrated a correlation between [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 uptake and collagen proportional area (CPA) (r = 0.89, p < 0.001), and reported that FAPI-PET is an effective method for non-invasively diagnosing hepatic fibrosis in oncological and non-oncological patient cohorts24,25.

However, the clinical applicability of FAPI-PET described in prior studies calls for more rigorous testing in specific patient populations, verified against histopathological evidence, along with thorough preclinical research that addresses a broad spectrum of liver disorders25. Therefore, this study provides preclinical evidence for potential clinical translation by non-invasively assessing hepatic fibrosis using [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET and evaluating the effect of TGF-β inhibition on hepatic fibrosis progression.

Methods

Animals

Female Sprague–Dawley rats (4 weeks old, body weight 120–130 g) were obtained from KOSA BIO (Seongnam, Republic of Korea) and housed under standard conditions: 12-h light/dark cycle, temperature 21 ± 2 °C, humidity 50 ± 10%, with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. The rats were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5 per group). All the animal experiments were approved by the Dong-A University Animal Experiment Ethics Committee (Approval Number: DIACUC-25-11) and were conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 and relevant institutional and national regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Experimental design

Hepatic fibrosis was induced by administering thioacetamide (TAA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in sterile saline solution at 150 mg/kg intraperitoneally twice weekly for 8 weeks. In the TAA + A83-01 group, A83-01 (a selective TGF-β receptor inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was co-administered intraperitoneally at 2 mg/kg twice weekly starting from week 4 of TAA administration.

Biochemical analysis

All rats were euthanized by overdose of isoflurane in an induction chamber, followed by blood and tissue collection for biochemical and histological analyses. Serum was obtained by centrifuging the blood samples at 3000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until analysis. The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and total bilirubin (TBIL) were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Pointcare M4; MNCHIP Technologies, Tianjin, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Gross morphology and histological analysis

The excised livers were photographed for gross morphological assessments. For histological examination, liver samples were preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin, processed into paraffin blocks, and sliced to 4 μm thickness. The resulting sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) to assess overall tissue morphology and to stage hepatic fibrosis according to the Ishak scoring system (stage 0–6), and with Masson’s trichrome (MT) to identify and quantitatively assess collagen deposition26. Collagen-positive areas were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; https://imagej.net/ij/). For each sample, three random fields were analyzed, and the percentage of the blue-stained (collagen) area was calculated relative to the total area.

Radiosynthesis

Radiosynthesis of [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 was performed by eluting [68Ga]GaCl3 with 3 mL of 0.1 M hydrochloric acid. The pH of the eluted [68Ga]GaCl3 solution was adjusted to 5 using 1 M sodium acetate buffer. Subsequently, 40 μg of DOTA-FAPI-46 was added to the buffered [68Ga]GaCl3 solution. The reaction mixture was incubated at 90 °C for 20 min to facilitate radiolabeling. The labeling efficiency was determined by spotting the resulting [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 onto radio instant thin-layer chromatography silica gel (iTLC-SG) strips using 0.1 M citric acid as the mobile phase. The activity of [68Ga]GaCl3 used for radiolabeling was approximately 450 MBq. The molar activity of [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 at the end of synthesis was estimated to be approximately 30 GBq/μmol, corresponding to a specific activity of 0.75 GBq/μg. The preparations were used for experiments only if they exhibited a radiochemical purity exceeding 95%, which served as the threshold for injection suitability.

Micro-PET/CT imaging

Micro-positron emission tomography/computed tomography (micro-PET/CT) imaging was performed using an Inveon micro-PET/CT scanner (Siemens, Knoxville, TN, USA) to assess hepatic metabolic changes. The rats were fasted for 12 h prior to imaging and anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane in oxygen. Approximately 300 μCi of [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 was injected. After a 40 min uptake period, PET scans were acquired for 20 min, followed by CT scans for anatomical co-registration. Regions of interest (ROIs) for the liver and thigh muscles were delineated using PMOD software (version 3.603, PMOD Technologies Ltd., Zurich, Switzerland; https://www.pmod.com/web/). Radiotracer uptake was expressed as the percentage of injected dose per cubic centimeter (%ID/cc) for both mean and maximum liver values, and liver-to-muscle ratios were calculated as liver mean or max/muscle mean.

Western blotting

The liver tissues were homogenized in PRO-PREP Protein Extraction Solution (Intron Biotechnology, Seongnam, Korea) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (GenDEPOT, Baker, TX, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the SMART BCA Protein Assay Kit (Intron Biotechnology) and equal amounts of protein (15 μg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with blocking buffer (BWB-0500; Biomax, Guri, Republic of Korea) for 10 min at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: SMAD2/3 (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), phospho-SMAD2/3 (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology), α-SMA (1:1,000, Abclonal, Wuhan, China), FAP (1:1,000, Invitrogen, CA, USA), and GAPDH (1:10,000, Cell Signaling Technology) as the loading control. Following washing, the membranes were treated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (1:1,000 or 1:10,000, Cell Signaling Technology). Visualization was achieved using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Cytiva), and images were captured using a ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Densitometric analysis was conducted using ImageJ software and band intensities were normalized to those of GAPDH.

Immunohistochemistry

Liver sections embedded in paraffin (thickness: 4 μm) were dewaxed, rehydrated, and antigen-retrieved by incubation in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide and non-specific binding was blocked using 2.5% normal goat serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: α-SMA (1:200, Abclonal) and FAP (1:200, Invitrogen). After washing, the sections were sequentially incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:200 with blocking serum) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with VECTASTAIN Elite ABC Reagent (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 30 min. Visualization was achieved by applying a diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Vector Laboratories) until the desired stain intensity developed. The sections were then rinsed, counterstained with hematoxylin, and examined under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple groups, using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com/). Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Biochemical and morphological validation of hepatic fibrosis progression

Figure 1 provides an overview of the experimental design and imaging workflow used in this study. Serum ALT, AST, and total bilirubin levels were significantly elevated in the TAA groups and TAA + A83-01 groups compared with those of the controls, peaking at 8 weeks (Fig. 2). Following TAA administration, the liver exhibited progressive morphological changes, including increased size and surface nodularity, indicating fibrosis. The liver-to-body weight ratios significantly increased in all TAA-exposed groups compared with those of controls at each time point, with the greatest increase observed at 8 weeks. A83-01 co-administration did not prevent this increase, and the highest liver/body weight ratio was observed in the TAA + A83-01 group at 8 weeks. These results suggested that both TAA-induced fibrosis and A83-01 treatment were associated with sustained liver enlargement and an elevated liver/body weight ratio (Fig. 3).

Histological examination demonstrated a time dependent progression of hepatic fibrosis following TAA administration, which was not alleviated by A83-01 treatment. H&E stained liver sections from TAA treated rats exhibited progressive architectural distortion, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrotic septa formation at 4, 6, and 8 weeks compared with control animals. Notably, livers from rats receiving A83-01 in combination with TAA displayed histopathological features comparable to those of the TAA only groups at earlier time points, and showed more pronounced architectural disruption and inflammatory changes at 8 weeks (Fig. 4A). Quantitative assessment using the Ishak fibrosis scoring system confirmed a significant and progressive increase in fibrosis severity following TAA exposure (Fig. 4B). Similarly, morphometric analysis of Masson’s trichrome stained sections demonstrated a significant increase in collagen positive area in TAA treated groups. At 8 weeks, the collagen positive area was slightly increased in the TAA + A83 01 group compared with the TAA only group, consistent with the histological and scoring data (Fig. 4C).

[68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 pet imaging of progressive fibrosis and pharmacologic effects

To evaluate the feasibility of non-invasive imaging for the detection and monitoring of hepatic fibrosis, micro-PET/CT imaging was performed using [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 at different stages of TAA induced liver injury (Fig. 5A). PET images demonstrated a progressive increase in hepatic tracer uptake in TAA-treated rats over time, whereas minimal uptake was observed in control animals (Fig. 5B). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant increase in both liver mean uptake and liver maximum uptake values in TAA treated groups, which increased in a time dependent manner from 4 (liver mean uptake 0.22 ± 0.02; liver max uptake 0.36 ± 0.01) to 8 weeks (liver mean uptake 0.41 ± 0.04; liver max uptake 0.55 ± 0.07). In animals receiving A83-01 in combination with TAA, hepatic tracer uptake remained comparable to or higher than that observed in TAA only groups (liver mean uptake 0.43 ± 0.05; liver max uptake 0.61 ± 0.08 at 8 weeks), with a further increase at 8 weeks, consistent with advanced fibrotic changes (Fig. 5C–F). To account for systemic tracer distribution, liver to muscle uptake ratios were additionally evaluated. Both liver mean/muscle mean uptake and liver maximum/muscle mean uptake ratios increased significantly with fibrosis progression and showed similar temporal patterns to absolute liver uptake values. To further evaluate whether [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 uptake reflects fibrosis severity, correlation analyses were performed between PET-derived uptake parameters and histological indices. Liver mean uptake, liver maximum uptake, liver mean/muscle mean uptake ratio, and liver maximum/muscle mean uptake ratio all showed strong positive correlations with Ishak fibrosis score (r = 0.9269–0.9645) and collagen-positive area quantified by Masson’s trichrome staining (r = 0.8906–0.9628). These findings quantitatively support that [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET uptake closely reflects the degree of hepatic fibrosis (Fig. 6). Collectively, these results demonstrate that [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET enables sensitive, non-invasive detection and longitudinal monitoring of hepatic fibrosis progression, underscoring its potential as a diagnostic imaging modality for fibrosis assessment.

Effects of A83-01 on TGF-β1 signaling and fibrosis associated markers

Serum TGF-β1 concentrations rose progressively from 4 to 8 weeks in TAA groups (399 ± 58 at 4 weeks; 724 ± 119 at 6 weeks; 1430 ± 311 at 8 weeks). Co-administration of A83-01 partially inhibited this rise at 6 weeks (216 ± 23) and achieved near-complete suppression at 8 weeks (122 ± 8), suggesting effective blockade of TGF-β1 signaling at the ligand level (Fig. 7A). In the TAA only groups, p-SMAD2/3 expression increased in a time- and dose-dependent manner, with the greatest increase observed at 8 weeks. In contrast, the TAA + A83-01 groups showed a slight reduction in p-SMAD2/3 at 6 weeks, and at 8 weeks, p-SMAD2/3 was markedly reduced to levels similar to the 4-week TAA group, further supporting that A83-01 effectively inhibited downstream TGF-β1 signaling. At 6 weeks, both the TAA and TAA + A83-01 groups showed similar upregulation of α-SMA and FAP, whereas at 8 weeks the TAA + A83-01 groups exhibited even higher α-SMA and FAP levels than the TAA groups. This finding indicates that hepatic fibrosis progressed despite TGF-β1 inhibition by A83-01, suggesting involvement of additional TGF-β independent signaling pathways that require further research.

Discussion

Hepatic fibrosis remains a major challenge in chronic liver disease because its progression is highly dynamic and difficult to evaluate accurately over time27,28. Although substantial efforts have been made to develop targeted antifibrotic therapies, clinical outcomes have remained inconsistent, partly because of the lack of reliable methods for longitudinal in vivo assessment of fibrotic activity29. Current clinical tools are limited in their ability to capture ongoing fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix remodeling across different disease stages30. Therefore, integrating pathway-targeted interventions with sensitive non-invasive imaging biomarkers may provide a more effective strategy for evaluating fibrosis progression and treatment response31,32.

In this preclinical study, we evaluated the effect of pharmacological TGF-β inhibition on hepatic fibrosis progression using A83-01 and investigated the utility of [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET as a quantitative imaging biomarker25,33. Our results demonstrate progressive fibrosis development in the TAA model from 4 to 8 weeks, supported by biochemical liver injury markers, histological collagen accumulation, and increasing expression of fibrotic activation markers. Importantly, [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET sensitively detected these temporal changes, indicating its potential value for non-invasive staging and longitudinal monitoring of hepatic fibrosis.

TGF-β signaling is widely recognized as a central driver of hepatic stellate cell activation and extracellular matrix deposition34,35. Consistent with its established profibrogenic role, A83-01 administration effectively suppressed serum TGF-β1 levels and markedly reduced downstream SMAD2/3 phosphorylation, confirming successful inhibition of canonical TGF-β signaling in vivo. Nevertheless, this biochemical blockade did not translate into attenuation of fibrosis progression. Instead, α-SMA and FAP expression remained elevated, and at 8 weeks the A83-01 treated group exhibited even greater fibrotic marker expansion compared with TAA alone. These findings suggest that hepatic fibrogenesis may proceed through additional pathways beyond canonical TGF-β signaling, particularly in advanced stages of injury36.

The observed dissociation between suppressed SMAD signaling and persistent fibrotic activation highlights the complexity of fibrosis biology37,38. Activated hepatic stellate cells are regulated not only by TGF-β but also by inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and non-canonical signaling cascades such as MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways39. Previous studies have shown that MAPK signaling, including ERK and JNK activation, promotes hepatic stellate cell proliferation and autophagy, whereas PI3K/AKT signaling regulates cell survival, migration, and extracellular matrix production during fibrogenesis. Persistent activation of these pathways may therefore bypass canonical SMAD-dependent inhibition and sustain profibrotic responses despite TGF-β receptor blockade40. It is therefore plausible that compensatory mechanisms sustain myofibroblast activation despite receptor level inhibition. Moreover, the exacerbation of fibrotic markers at later time points suggests that stage dependent responses or maladaptive repair processes may influence therapeutic outcomes, underscoring the need for combination strategies targeting multiple profibrotic drivers41. In addition, potential off-target or context-dependent effects of TGF-β receptor inhibition should also be considered. TGF-β signaling plays a complex and context-specific role in liver homeostasis, including regulation of immune responses and tissue repair42. Its inhibition may therefore lead to unintended alterations in the hepatic microenvironment, such as changes in inflammatory signaling or intercellular communication, which could indirectly sustain or even exacerbate fibrogenesis38. These findings highlight the need for a more comprehensive understanding of pathway interactions and suggest that targeting a single signaling axis may be insufficient to effectively control fibrosis progression.

A major translational implication of this work is the performance of [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET imaging43,44. FAP has emerged as a promising fibrosis-associated biomarker primarily derived from activated stellate cells, with prior studies reporting strong correlations between FAPI uptake and histological fibrosis burden45,46. In our study, hepatic tracer uptake increased progressively with fibrosis severity and provided clear contrast between control and fibrotic livers. Importantly, PET-derived quantitative parameters remained high or even increased further in the TAA + A83-01 group at 8 weeks, mirroring the histological and molecular evidence of sustained fibroblast activation. These results support the ability of FAPI PET not only to diagnose fibrosis but also to noninvasively monitor fibrotic activity during therapeutic intervention.

The limitations of this study include the inability of the animal model to fully replicate human fibrosis, the small sample size (n = 5), and unassessed long-term safety of FAPI PET. The exclusive use of the FAPI without FDG comparison also limits direct tracer evaluation. In particular, although this study did not elucidate the specific pathways through which A83-01 failed to alleviate fibrosis, subsequent research should investigate non-canonical pathways in greater detail, alongside other factors beyond TGF-β that contribute to fibrosis and inflammation, potentially uncovering novel therapeutic targets for hepatic fibrosis47.

Conclusion

In conclusion, A83-01 effectively inhibited canonical TGF-β/SMAD signaling but failed to prevent hepatic fibrosis progression in the TAA model. [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET provided sensitive, quantitative, and non-invasive monitoring of fibrosis severity and treatment response, supporting its translational potential as an imaging biomarker for hepatic fibrosis assessment.
