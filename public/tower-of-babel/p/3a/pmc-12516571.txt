Non-osteoporotic multilevel contiguous vertebral compression: a rare clinical case report
Authors: Wongthawat Liawrungrueang, Watcharaporn Cholamjiak, Peem Sarasombath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple-level contiguous vertebral compression fractures (VCFs) are typically associated with osteoporosis or pathological conditions. The occurrence of such fractures in individuals with normal bone mineral density following high-energy trauma is exceedingly rare.Case DescriptionWe report the case of a 64-year-old male construction worker who sustained a fall from a height of approximately 4 meters, landing on his feet. He presented with severe back and right heel pain but exhibited no neurological deficits. Additionally, a displaced intra-articular fracture of the right calcaneus was identified. Bone mineral density assessment demonstrated normal bone health (T-score: 0.3). A comprehensive evaluation, including bone density assessment and thorough imaging, is essential to differentiate these cases from fractures caused by underlying pathologies. Introduction

Vertebral compression fractures (VCFs) are prevalent injuries, typically observed in elderly patients with osteoporosis, and are most commonly associated with low-energy trauma. These fractures typically result from minimal trauma or even routine activities in osteoporotic individuals, leading to significant morbidity, including pain, reduced mobility, and spinal deformities (1). VCFs resulting from high-energy trauma, such as falls from significant heights or motor vehicle accidents, are less common and often occur in younger individuals with normal bone mineral density. These traumatic VCFs can involve single or multiple vertebral levels and may present with varying degrees of severity. While single-level fractures are more frequently documented, multiple-level contiguous VCFs in the absence of osteoporosis are exceedingly rare and pose unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges (2,3). The distinction between osteoporotic and non-osteoporotic VCFs is crucial, as it influences both management strategies and prognostic outcomes. Non-osteoporotic VCFs often result from significant axial loading forces and may be associated with concurrent injuries, necessitating a comprehensive evaluation to guide appropriate treatment. Despite their clinical significance, there is a paucity of literature addressing the optimal management of multiple-level contiguous VCFs in patients with normal bone density.

In this report, we present a rare case of a 64-year-old male who sustained multiple contiguous lumbar compression fractures (L1–L3) without underlying osteoporosis following a high-energy traumatic event. This case underscores the importance of considering such injury patterns in non-osteoporotic patients and highlights the need for individualized management approaches to optimize patient outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-61/rc) (4).

Case presentation

A 64-year-old male construction worker with no underlying medical conditions presented to the emergency department after falling from a height of approximately 4 meters while working on scaffolding. He landed on both feet and experienced immediate severe low back pain and right heel pain. Due to the severity of pain, the patient was unable to ambulate at the scene and required assistance for transport to the emergency department. There was no loss of consciousness, no signs of head trauma, and no bladder or bowel dysfunction. He denied any numbness or weakness in the lower limbs. On physical examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable and neurologically intact. Localized tenderness was noted over the thoracolumbar spine and right hindfoot. Sensation, motor function, and deep tendon reflexes were normal in all extremities. No signs of open injury or deformity were observed. Radiographic evaluation was initiated. Computed tomography (CT) of the thoracolumbar spine revealed acute compression fractures at L1, L2, and L3, with the most significant anterior height loss and wedging at L1. There was no evidence of retropulsion or posterior element involvement. Coronal images confirmed that alignment was preserved in the coronal plane (Figure 1A-1D). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated marrow edema within the fractured vertebrae and no compromise of the spinal canal, spinal cord, or posterior ligamentous complex (Figure 2A,2B). A right foot lateral and axial radiograph revealed a displaced intra-articular calcaneal fracture, classified as Sanders type III, with depression and widening of the posterior facet (Figure 3A,3B). Given the patient’s high functional demand and preserved bone quality, surgical intervention was planned for both injuries. Bone mineral density assessment (DEXA) showed normal bone health with a lumbar spine bone mineral density (BMD) of 1.152 g/cm2 [T-score +0.3 standard deviation (SD)], femoral neck BMD of 1.008 g/cm2 (T-score −0.1 SD), and total hip BMD of 1.103 g/cm2 (T-score 0.0 SD). To rule out secondary metabolic causes, serum calcium, phosphate, intact parathyroid hormone (PTH), and 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OHD) levels were assessed and found to be within normal ranges.

For the Calcaneus, the patient underwent open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the right calcaneus first. Under spinal anesthesia, an extended lateral L-shaped approach was made. Full-thickness flaps were raised, and the lateral wall was mobilized. The depressed posterior facet fragment was elevated and reduced anatomically under fluoroscopic guidance. Bone voids were filled with cancellous autograft harvested from the calcaneal tuberosity. A pre-contoured calcaneal locking plate was applied laterally, secured with multiple cortical and locking screws, ensuring buttress effect and restoration of Bohler’s angle (Figure 3C,3D). Postoperatively, the patient was placed in a well-padded posterior splint, non-weight-bearing on the right side, and pain was controlled with multimodal analgesia. For the spine, the patient underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fusion from T12 to L4. Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a prone position on a radiolucent spinal frame. A standard midline posterior incision was made from T12 to L4. Subperiosteal dissection exposed the posterior elements. Pedicle screws were placed bilaterally at T12, L1, L2, L3, and L4 under fluoroscopic guidance. The L1 vertebral body, which had the greatest collapse, was carefully distracted and supported using the instrumentation. Titanium rods were contoured to restore lumbar lordosis and secured with set screws. Posterolateral decortication was performed at all levels using a high-speed burr. Autologous local bone graft harvested during decompression and from the spinous processes was packed along the transverse processes and facets to enhance fusion (Figure 4A,4B). No intraoperative complications occurred. Blood loss was approximately 300 mL, and operative time was 165 minutes. The patient was mobilized on postoperative day 2 in a thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO) brace, which was maintained for 12 weeks during ambulation. Physical therapy was initiated concurrently to support gradual return to upright posture and core strengthening. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient had minimal lumbar discomfort, was ambulatory without assistive devices, and had returned to light work duties. Radiographs confirmed maintenance of sagittal alignment, hardware integrity, and early fusion signs (Figure 4C). At final follow-up, the patient reported satisfaction with a visual analog scale (VAS) back pain score of 1/10 and an American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) hindfoot score of 90/100. These outcomes are better than typical values reported in similar injuries, where mean VAS scores are 2–4/10 and the AOFAS scores range between 70–85 following standard surgical management (5,6) (Figure 4D).

Discussion

VCFs are a frequent consequence of osteoporosis, particularly in elderly populations, and are commonly associated with low-energy trauma. However, traumatic multiple-level contiguous VCFs in patients with normal BMD are exceedingly rare and represent a unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, with only a few case reports and small retrospective series describing such patterns (7,8). This case highlights a 64-year-old male with three-level compression fractures (L1–L3) and an associated intra-articular calcaneal fracture, all in the context of normal bone health (T-score +0.3). The combination of these injuries illustrates a classical axial load mechanism, often seen in falls from height, where forces are transmitted from the feet to the spine.

Although vertebral fractures involving multiple levels are not uncommon in high-energy trauma, they are typically non-contiguous—that is, they affect separate spinal segments with normal levels in between. This pattern is often referred to as ‘multifocal’ and may raise suspicion for pathological causes such as osteoporosis, metastatic lesions, or myeloma. In contrast, our case involved contiguous compression fractures spanning L1–L3, with no intervening normal vertebrae. This specific pattern, especially in a patient with normal BMD, is rare. A retrospective review by Denis (1983) described that burst fractures commonly involve a single level and are typically accompanied by retropulsion and canal compromise (9). Multiple-level injuries, when present, are often noncontiguous and may indicate underlying skeletal fragility or malignancy. The presence of a calcaneal fracture, a well-known marker of axial load trauma, further supports the mechanism of injury. Falls from height resulting in concurrent spinal compression and calcaneal fractures have been well-documented (2,10). Studies by Court-Brown et al. emphasized the need for a thorough evaluation of the spine when a calcaneus fracture is present, and vice versa (11). In this case, the early identification of both injuries and timely intervention contributed to a successful outcome. Management of traumatic multilevel VCFs without neurological deficits is controversial. While some authors advocate for conservative treatment in stable fractures, others argue that surgical stabilization provides earlier mobilization, prevents progressive kyphosis, and improves functional recovery (3). In this case, the most significant collapse at L1 with additional L2 and L3 involvement prompted surgical intervention. Posterior spinal instrumentation from T12 to L4 allowed for effective stabilization, sagittal alignment correction, and prevention of late deformity. L1 and L3 showed moderate height loss at presentation, which improved following surgical alignment but did not fully return to normal. The CT was obtained approximately 24 hours prior to the X-ray, during which some dynamic vertebral compression may have progressed. While this case report does not introduce a novel diagnostic technique, it reinforces the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for multilevel spinal injuries in patients who experience high-energy trauma, even when neurological symptoms are absent and bone density is normal.

The calcaneal fracture was managed with ORIF using a locking plate through an extended lateral approach. This technique is well established for Sanders type II and III fractures, and allows anatomical restoration of the posterior facet and subtalar joint (12). At 1-year follow-up, our patient had returned to full activity with minimal symptoms and no neurological impairment. Radiographs confirmed solid spinal fusion and full union of calcaneus, supporting the decision for early surgical management. Spinal hardware removal is not currently planned, as the patient is asymptomatic with radiographic evidence of solid fusion and no implant-related complications. Height restoration was achieved via intraoperative distraction and alignment correction, maintained through successful fusion. Indications for posterior fusion in multilevel VCFs include instability, significant collapse or kyphosis, failed conservative management, or high functional demand—applicable in this physically active patient. Our case emphasizes the importance of recognizing traumatic multilevel contiguous compression fractures in patients with normal bone density, particularly following high-energy axial load injuries. It also underlines the clinical value of a thorough skeletal evaluation in such trauma cases and demonstrates that early, well-planned surgical intervention can result in excellent outcomes even in complex injury patterns.

To our knowledge, this is one of the few documented cases involving non-osteoporotic multilevel contiguous VCFs with a concomitant calcaneal injury, treated successfully with a dual-surgical approach. Future case series or registry-based studies may help clarify the true incidence and best practices in managing such rare injury patterns in non-osteoporotic patients.

Conclusions

Traumatic multiple-level contiguous VCFs in patients with normal BMD are rare and often underrecognized. This case demonstrates that high-energy axial loading, such as a fall from height, can result in significant spinal injury even in the absence of osteoporosis or other pathological conditions. The co-occurrence of a calcaneal fracture further emphasizes the vertical force transmission typical of this mechanism. Early identification through comprehensive imaging, exclusion of metabolic or neoplastic causes, and timely surgical intervention—including posterior spinal instrumentation and calcaneal ORIF—can lead to excellent clinical and radiological outcomes. This report highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for multilevel spinal injury in patients presenting after vertical impact trauma, even when neurological status is intact and bone quality is normal.
