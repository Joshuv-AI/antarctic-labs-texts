Comparison of candidemia caused by Candida auris and non-auris Candida spp.: a retrospective cohort study on clinical characteristics, risk factors, and antifungal resistance profiles
Authors: Ramazan Korkusuz, Selda Kömeç, Esin Çevik, Alev Öztaş, Özlem Altuntaş Aydın, Onur Özalp, Gülçin Hilal Alay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCandida auris has emerged as a significant global health threat due to its resistance to multiple antifungal agents and its prominent role in healthcare-associated infections. This study aims to compare the clinical characteristics, risk factors, antifungal resistance profiles, and mortality rates of candidemia caused by C. auris and non-auris Candida spp. (NACS).Materials and methodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital from 2021 to 2024. Patients aged 18 years and older with positive blood cultures for Candida spp. were included. Demographic data, comorbidities, risk factors, mortality rates, and treatment protocols were analyzed.ResultsA total of 1,088 candidemia cases were included in the study (C. auris: 126 cases, NACS: 962 cases). The length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the C. auris group compared to the NACS group (50.98 ± 38.79 vs. 33.05 ± 25.57 days, p < 0.001). Independent risk factors for C. auris candidemia included longer hospitalization before candidemia (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02, p < 0.001), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.07–3.31, p = 0.026), prior antifungal use within 30 days (OR 1.92, 95% CI 1.22–3.03, p = 0.005), and ICU admission (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.10–3.92, p = 0.023). Antifungal resistance rates among C. auris isolates were 96.49% for fluconazole, 72.81% for amphotericin B, and 25.89% for caspofungin. The 30-day mortality rate was lower in the C. auris group (48.4%) compared to the NACS group (60.2%) (p = 0.012).ConclusionAlthough crude 30-day mortality was lower in the C. auris group, adjusted analysis showed comparable 30-day mortality between the C. auris and NACS groups. These findings highlight the increasing clinical burden of C. auris and support accurate species identification, local antifungal susceptibility surveillance, and rigorous infection-control measures.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Candidemia is a major healthcare-associated infection that occurs predominantly in immunocompromised patients and in those receiving care in intensive care units (ICUs) [1, 2]. The primary risk factors associated with the development of candidemia include advanced age, hemodialysis, hematologic malignancies, use of central venous catheters, total parenteral nutrition, immunosuppressive therapies, broad-spectrum antibiotic use, urinary catheterization, and prolonged hospitalization [1–4]. While Candida albicans remains the most prevalent cause of candidemia, the increasing incidence of non-albicans Candida spp. warrants attention [5, 6]. Since its first identification in Japan in 2009, Candida auris has caused outbreaks worldwide, raising concern because of multidrug resistance and its ability to form biofilms on medical devices and environmental surfaces in healthcare settings [7–9]. C. auris has at least five major phylogenetic clades associated with geographic origin, and a sixth clade has been reported more recently: Clade 1 (South Asia), Clade 2 (East Asia), Clade 3 (South Africa), Clade 4 (South America), Clade 5 (Iran) and Clade 6 (Singapore). Among these clades, Clade 1, 3, and 4 exhibit high levels of antifungal resistance [10]. In 2021, our hospital and another centre in the region reported the first cases of C. auris in Türkiye [11, 12]. These isolates belonged to Clade I (South Asia), a clade associated with multidrug resistance and outbreak potential, with important implications for infection control in Türkiye [13]. Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) classified C. auris as a priority fungal pathogen in the guide released in October 2022 [14].

Candidemia is linked to high morbidity and mortality rates, with a 30-day mortality rate ranging from 32% to 72% [1, 15–17]. This variability probably reflects differences in infecting species, antifungal therapy, baseline comorbidity, and severity of illness [18]. Current guidelines recommend echinocandins as the first-line treatment for candidemia, with fluconazole as an alternative for patients who have not recently been exposed to azoles and are unlikely to harbor azole-resistant Candida spp [19, 20].

Although C. auris has emerged in recent years as a multidrug-resistant pathogen and a major infection-control concern globally and in Türkiye, data on its clinical outcomes—particularly mortality—remain limited and inconsistent [14, 21]. Recent studies, including those conducted in globally, have reported conflicting results, with some demonstrating higher mortality rates associated with C. auris candidemia, while others have shown outcomes comparable to those observed in candidemia caused by non-auris Candida spp. (NACS) [22–25]. These discrepancies may result from variations in study design, patient populations, and sample sizes. Therefore, large-scale, well-designed studies are needed to better define the clinical impact of C. auris candidemia.

In this study, we aimed to compare the clinical characteristics, risk factors, antifungal resistance patterns, and mortality outcomes of candidemia caused by C. auris and NACS in a large tertiary-care center.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study was conducted at our hospital, a tertiary care center with 2,800 beds, including 288 adult ICU beds. Our hospital is a tertiary care facility located in a city with a population of approximately 16 million. In addition to providing general medicine and surgical services, our institution offers specialized intensive care, including a burn unit and programs for solid organ and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, with a particular focus on the care of immunosuppressed patients.

Records from our microbiology laboratory were retrospectively reviewed to identify cases of candidemia. We included patients aged 18 years or older with at least one blood culture positive for Candida spp. between Jan 1, 2021, and Dec 31, 2024. Candidemia was defined as the presence of Candida spp. in at least one blood culture from a patient with clinical symptoms suggestive of systemic infection. These symptoms may include fever (≥ 38 °C) or hypothermia (< 36 °C), leukocytosis (more than 10,000/mm³) or leukopenia (fewer than 4,000/mm³), elevated inflammatory markers, hemodynamic instability that necessitates vasopressor support, and/or clinical signs consistent with sepsis or septic shock.

Cases considered to represent contamination or colonization based on clinical assessment by an Infectious Diseases specialist were excluded. Exclusion criteria included the absence of systemic signs of infection, normal inflammatory markers, no requirement for antifungal therapy, rapid clinical improvement without antifungal treatment, and/or an alternative diagnosis that explained the clinical presentation. For patients with multiple episodes of candidemia, only the first episode was included. Demographic data, comorbidities, and risk factors related to the development of candidemia were recorded from the hospital’s electronic medical records system. The risk factors identified included central venous catheterization, total parenteral nutrition, an ICU stay, and mechanical ventilation—each of which must have been present for at least 48 h during the previous 7 days. Other factors included abdominal surgery within the last 30 days, chemotherapy within the past 3 months, and exposure to antibiotics, antifungals, or corticosteroids within the last 30 days. As part of routine infection control practices, colonization screening for C. auris was conducted upon admission to the ICU.

Early mortality was defined as death within 7 days after candidemia onset. Thirty-day mortality was defined as death within 30 days after candidemia onset. Septic shock was defined according to Sepsis-3 as a vasopressor requirement to maintain a mean arterial pressure of at least 65 mm Hg despite adequate fluid resuscitation, together with a serum lactate concentration greater than 2 mmol/L [26].

Patients were excluded from the study if their medical records were incomplete, if they refused treatment after a diagnosis of candidemia, or if they were discharged or transferred to another facility. Additionally, individuals with two or more Candida spp. Isolated from blood cultures or those with concurrent bacteremia involving Candida spp., were excluded from the analysis.

Antifungal treatment is defined as the use of any systemic antifungal medication during the course of candidemia. Empirical antifungal therapy refers to treatment started before the identification of the fungal species.

Microbiological information

The Bactec FX automated system (Becton Dickinson, USA) was used for blood cultures. Once a positive signal was detected, the samples were inoculated into media for further growth and identification. The identification process utilized Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization-Time of Flight (MALDI-TOF) with the Microflex LT/SH Smart MS system (Bruker Daltonics, Germany). Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed to determine the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) using the microtube dilution method (Yeast One, Thermo Scientific, USA). The results were then evaluated based on the clinical breakpoints established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [27]. Since clinical breakpoints for C. auris are not available according to CLSI guidelines, the breakpoints recommended by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) were used. Interpretation of caspofungin MIC values for C. auris should be approached with caution, as interlaboratory variability in caspofungin susceptibility testing has been documented for Candida spp. In addition, paradoxical growth (the Eagle effect) has also been described with caspofungin in C. auris. The CDC tentative breakpoints used were: 32 µg/mL for fluconazole, 2 µg/mL for amphotericin B, 2 µg/mL for caspofungin, and 4 µg/mL for both micafungin and anidulafungin [28].

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Türkiye, Basaksehir Cam and Sakura City Hospital (Approval Number: KAEK/25.09.2024/2024-02). The Institutional Review Board waived the need for informed consent from patients involved, as the data were analyzed retrospectively and anonymously.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and Python 3.12. In Python, pandas was used for data management, scikit-learn for LASSO penalized regression, and matplotlib/seaborn for visualization. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The duration of hospitalization prior to candidemia onset was calculated only for patients diagnosed ≥ 48 h after admission, excluding transferred patients to avoid bias from incomplete prior hospitalization data.

Group comparisons were conducted using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, depending on expected cell counts. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test due to non-normal distributions, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plot inspection.

Candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression models were selected using a hybrid approach combining clinical relevance and data-driven selection via LASSO regression [29]. LASSO was implemented with 10-fold cross-validation, and the optimal λ was chosen based on the minimum cross-validated binomial deviance. Continuous variables were standardized prior to penalization. Variables with non-zero coefficients were considered for inclusion, along with clinically important variables identified a priori.

Two multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. In the first model, the outcome was Candida auris versus NACS. In the second model, the outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. Covariates in the mortality model included age, Charlson Comorbidity Index, ICU admission, septic shock, renal failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and solid cancer. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and discrimination was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Model stability was ensured by maintaining an adequate number of events per variable.

Missing data were limited and handled by case-wise exclusion in relevant analyses. No multiple imputation was performed. Results of bivariate analyses were interpreted descriptively without adjustment for multiple comparisons, whereas multivariable models were considered the primary inferential analyses.

Results

Between 2021 and 2024, a total of 1,371 patients aged 18 or older were evaluated for their first positive blood culture for Candida spp. Out of these patients, 283 were excluded from the study, as detailed in Fig. 1. The most common reason for exclusion from the study was concomitant bacteremia, accounting for 198 cases. Other reasons for exclusion included referral to another hospital (46 cases), instances in which the clinician did not interpret a positive blood culture as true candidemia (31 cases), treatment refusal (3 cases), and detection of more than one Candida spp. in the same patient (5 cases). Consequently, 1,088 cases of candidemia were assessed. A comparative analysis of variables among the 1,088 candidemia cases is presented in Table 3. Of the 1,088 episodes of candidemia, 97 were diagnosed within the first 48 h after hospital admission. All of these patients had been transferred from other healthcare facilities and were classified as healthcare-associated bloodstream infection cases, rather than true community-onset infections. Among these 97 early-onset cases, 5 were caused by C. auris and 92 by NACS. The remaining candidemia episodes were diagnosed ≥ 48 h after admission. The duration of hospitalization prior to developing candidemia was significantly longer in the C. auris group, with an average of 50.98 days (± 38.79) compared with 33.05 days (± 25.57) in the NACS group (p < 0.001). In terms of comorbidities, solid cancer was observed at a significantly lower rate in the C. auris group (12.7%) than in the NACS group (25.1%; p = 0.002). Similarly, renal failure occurred less frequently in the C. auris group (11.9%) than in the NACS group (21.4%; p = 0.013). Conversely, the frequency of COPD was higher in the C. auris group (16.7%) than in the NACS group (10.2%, p = 0.028). Additionally, the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics was noted more frequently in the C. auris group (87.3%) than in the NACS group (79.3%, p = 0.034). A history of antifungal treatment within the past month was also significantly more prevalent in the C. auris group (30.2%) than in the other group (16.8%; p < 0.001).

Recent abdominal surgery was less common in the C. auris group than in the NACS group (p = 0.015).

In the evaluation of antifungal treatment management, empirical antifungal treatment was administered more frequently to patients with C. auris, at a rate of 45.2% compared to 34.7% in other groups (p = 0.021). The use of empirical echinocandins was also significantly higher among C. auris patients, with 50.9% compared to 28.2% (p < 0.001). The 30-day mortality rate was lower in the C. auris group, with rates of 48.4% versus 60.2% (p = 0.012).

Discussion

Our findings show that C. albicans comprised 43.3% of the Candida spp. isolated, followed by C. parapsilosis and C. glabrata, which aligns with previous studies conducted in our country [30, 31]. When analyzing the distribution of fungal agents by year, we observed that C. auris became the second most frequently isolated fungal species, after C. albicans, in 2024. This increase might reflect the larger number of referrals from regional healthcare centers in 2024. Additionally, the increase in C. auris colonization among patients transferred from external centers is consistent with this interpretation.

The predominance of Candida spp., other than C. auris, in candidemia among immunosuppressed patients, such as the elderly, those with renal failure, individuals with solid cancers, and those who have undergone abdominal surgery, can be attributed to the endogenous origin of these yeasts. In other published studies, significantly longer hospital stays were noted in the C. auris group, which aligns with our findings [24, 25, 32]. This is likely due to C. auris being strongly associated with healthcare-related infections. Deficiencies in infection-control practices contribute to the spread of this pathogen, particularly as hospitalization duration increases. In our study, the use of fluconazole before C. auris candidemia was common. Similar studies have suggested that this might be due to fluconazole suppressing the growth of fluconazole-sensitive Candida spp. prior to the onset of candidemia [22, 24, 25].

In our cohort, 25.9% of C. auris isolates were categorized as resistant to caspofungin based on CDC tentative breakpoints; this rate is higher than that reported in many studies, yet lower than the 40% resistance reported in India [33–35]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the well-documented variability and reproducibility concerns in caspofungin susceptibility testing for Candida spp. Therefore, the observed resistance rates may not fully reflect true clinical resistance. Although echinocandins remain the first-line treatment for C. auris infections, further studies using standardized and reliable susceptibility testing methods are needed to better define the clinical significance of echinocandin resistance.

In various studies comparing 30-day mortality rates between C. auris and NACS groups, the mortality rate was lower in the C. auris group than in the NACS group [22, 24]. However, these differences were not statistically significant. In contrast, a study conducted in Colombia found that the mortality rate for the C. auris group was significantly lower than that for the NACS group [23]. In our study, the 30-day crude mortality rate was significantly lower in the C. auris group than in the NACS group (p = 0.012), but this finding should be interpreted with caution. Once adjusted for age, Charlson Comorbidity Index, ICU admission, septic shock, renal failure, COPD, and solid cancer, the difference in 30-day mortality between C. auris candidemia and NACS-induced candidemia was not statistically significant. This finding suggests that the difference in mortality between the groups is more strongly determined by patient-related factors.

Given that it may take 2–4 days to identify the specific Candida species after blood cultures are drawn, this delay can result in prolonged diagnosis and treatment of candidemia [19]. Clinicians should consider candidemia as a potential diagnosis to prevent delays in treatment initiation. Furthermore, it is crucial for healthcare providers to have thorough knowledge of fungal pathogens and antifungal resistance as part of institutional surveillance programs. Our study showed that 42.3% of patients received treatment based on blood culture results, while 35.9% received empirical antifungal treatment. Unfortunately, 21.8% of patients died before receiving antifungal therapy. This highlights the need for heightened awareness of fungal infections among clinicians and the use of PCR-based diagnostic tests to enable earlier detection of candidemia [36, 37].

In our cohort, fluconazole was the first-line treatment in 68.5% of cases. This pattern might reflect reluctance to initiate empirical antifungal therapy before culture results are available, historical local fluconazole susceptibility patterns, and the higher cost of echinocandins, particularly in resource-constrained or middle-income healthcare settings. However, we observed a significantly higher preference for empirical echinocandin treatment in C. auris candidemia cases compared to those caused by NACS. This shift in treatment preference can be attributed to two factors: first, patients had a history of fluconazole use; second, surveillance for C. auris conducted on all patients upon ICU admission influenced clinicians’ decisions to prefer echinocandins for empirical therapy.

Our hospital treats a significant number of patients in both inpatient and ICU, including immunosuppressed individuals. Although our infection control team adheres to international guidelines, shortcomings in training healthcare personnel have led to challenges in managing infections. This situation poses a risk of the rapid spread of pathogens such as C. auris within the hospital environment. Consequently, our study examines risk factors, mortality rates, and differences in candidemia caused by C. auris compared with the NACS group. However, the study has several limitations. The retrospective design limits causal inference; because it was conducted in a single tertiary-care center with a high proportion of ICU patients, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Additionally, antifungal susceptibility testing for C. auris, particularly for echinocandins, may be affected by methodological variability. Finally, molecular characterization of isolates was not performed.

Conclusion

This single-center retrospective study provides a comprehensive assessment of the epidemiology, antifungal resistance, and mortality associated with candidemia after the emergence of C. auris in Türkiye. Although crude 30-day mortality was lower in the C. auris group, adjusted analysis showed that 30-day mortality was comparable between the C. auris and NACS groups. Mortality in candidemia appears to be more strongly influenced by host-related factors and severity of illness rather than the infecting species alone. This study highlights the increasing clinical burden posed by C. auris and the associated threat of antifungal resistance.
