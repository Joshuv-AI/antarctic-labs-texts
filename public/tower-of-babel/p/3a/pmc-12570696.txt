A framework for identifying and prioritizing SNPs in genes of the hypothalamic- pituitary-gonadal axis in Guzerat cattle using amplicon-based NGS
Authors: Olivia Marcuzzi, Francisco Calcaterra, Leónidas H Olivera, Analía Arizmendi, Marco RJM Henry, Danielle Cunha Cardoso, Ana M Loaiza Echeverri, Juan P Liron, María E Fernández, Denise A Andrade de Oliveira, Guillermo Giovambattista
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe combined use of NGS technologies with bioinformatics tools has significantly advanced research by enabling comprehensive analyses of entire genomes, specific genomic regions of interest, and transcriptomes. Targeted NGS methods, which focus on smaller genome fractions, are widely used to study genetic diseases, epigenetic modifications, microbiomes, and environmental DNA, among other applications. This study aimed to develop a roadmap for detecting and selecting polymorphisms in candidate genes by integrating amplicon NGS-Target techniques with bioinformatics analyses. Sixty-eight genes associated with the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis were selected to develop the amplicon NGS assay, comprising 730 regions that cover a total of 136,274 bp. This method was used to sequence 75 Guzerat cattle, a dual-purpose breed from Brazil, renowned for their high rusticity and adaptability. This Zebu cattle exhibit certain limitations, such as delayed puberty onset, which can reduce reproductive efficiency. Using the GATK protocol a total of 2,600 SNPs and 1,615 indels were detected. A series of consecutive filtering steps (maf, the detection of non-synonymous substitution, phylogenetic amino acid conservation, and biochemical properties) were used, resulting in a subset of 30 candidate SNPs. Then, these polymorphisms were analysed using bioinformatic tools (SIFT, PANTHER, PolyPhen2, and MutPred), identifying 5 SNPs with high effect on the protein. Their structure and stability were estimated using AlphaFold and DDMut. Finally, 3 candidate polymorphisms (IGF1R, LHCGR, TAC3R) with potentially significant effects on the protein remained to be validated through dynamic simulations or in vitro and in vivo experimental assays.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12863-025-01365-8.Keywords: Bovine, Genotyping by sequencing, Reproductive traits, Polymorphism, Protein modeling

Background

The Brazilian Guzerat zebu cattle originated from the Kankrej breed in Gujarat, India [1]. These animals evolved in semiarid regions, which conferred the ability to withstand harsh environmental conditions, including drought and fodder scarcity [2]. The Guzerat breed was first introduced to Brazil in the late 19th century and rapidly spread across the country due to its adaptability to tropical and dry conditions [3]. This dual-purpose breed is renowned for its high rusticity and adaptability, and has become one of the most important breeds in Brazil. It is well adapted to the country’s diverse and often challenging environmental conditions, ranging from semi-arid regions to tropical areas [4]. With approximately 500,000 animals, it is the second most-raised breed, playing a significant role in national livestock production systems [5]. Although zebu cattle are highly resilient, they also exhibit certain drawbacks, including temperament issues and fertility challenges, such as delayed onset of puberty, which can impair reproductive efficiency [6]. While the delay in sexual precocity and maturity is largely influenced by nutritional and management factors, genetic components also play a significant role in this process. In Guzerat cattle, moderate heritabilities have been reported for both growth and reproductive traits, indicating that genetic improvement of sexual precocity is not only possible but also feasible [7]. Therefore, selection strategies could be implemented to reduce the age at which animals reach puberty [8].

The onset of puberty is characterized by a sustained increase in the pulsatile release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus, which is critical for activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis and initiating the reproductive hormone cascade. While this event is central to the onset of puberty, numerous neuroendocrine subsystems also contribute to this process, including transsynaptic and glial networks that modulate hormone release through various signaling pathways. These systems can either promote or inhibit puberty via a neuronal component that involves the release of excitatory amino acids (e.g., glutamate), kisspeptin, growth factors, and small molecules facilitating cell-cell signaling. Conversely, inhibitory systems, such as GABAergic and opioid neurons, also play a significant role in regulating puberty [9]. Recent research has focused on reducing the age at which bulls reach puberty through genomic selection. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying puberty attainment and the interactions between GnRH receptors, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and the HPG axis is crucial for improving reproductive efficiency [10].

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies were developed in the early 2000 s, making significant advancements primarily in the fields of medicine, biology, and genomics. NGS platforms can sequence millions of DNA fragments in parallel, enabling the analysis of entire genomes, specific regions of interest, or transcriptomes through the use of bioinformatics tools [11]. The choice between whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and targeted sequencing depends on the study’s objectives, as each approach has distinct advantages and limitations. Targeted NGS methods sequence a smaller fraction of the genome (< 5%), which leads to reduced costs per sample, lower computational, and storage requirements [12]. Although these methods offer lower coverage, they provide greater sequencing depth [13]. Obtaining more reads per genomic position enhances accuracy, facilitating the detection of false positives and rare variants. Targeted NGS methods are used for exome sequencing, the study of genetic diseases and epigenetic modifications, microbiome analysis, and environmental DNA sampling, among other applications [14, 15]. Currently, targeted NGS sequencing is achievable through various enrichment methods, such as restriction site-associated DNA sequencing (RADSeq), genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS), which rely on the enrichment of multiple amplicons, and hybrid capture techniques, which sequence target regions using a set of probes [16, 17].

The aim of this work was to develop a proof of concept for the implementation of a detection and selection design for polymorphisms in candidate genes by combining targeted NGS techniques with bioinformatics analysis. After applying a series of filters, the expected outcome is a set of polymorphisms in candidate genes with potentially significant phenotypic effects, which will then be validated through experimental assays. As an example, genes involved in the HPG axis in Guzerat cattle from Brazil were used.

Methods

Animals and DNA extraction

A total of 75 hair samples were collected from Guzerat bulls raised in the state of Minas Gerais, Brazil. Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cattle analyzed in this study were evaluated for puberty onset based on two criteria: scrotal circumference and sperm quality, as detailed in Fernández et al. [18].

Design, library preparation and sequencing

A list of genes involved in the HPG axis was selected based on extensive published studies and expert reviews in humans, experimental animals, and domestic species, and their genomic positions were identified in the bovine reference genome UMD 3.1 (BosTau6), which was the available reference genome at the moment of the experimental design, library preparation, and sequencing. However, for the bioinformatic analysis, these genome positions were converted to the ARS-UCD 2.0 genome assembly (Table 1). The target regions were used to design a custom AmpliSeq panel by adding the target genes and genomic regions of interest in the Illumina Design Studio software (https://designstudio.illumina.com). The TruSeq Custom Amplicon v1.5 kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used to construct libraries from the amplicons of the target regions. The designed panel captured most exons of 68 candidate genes related to reproductive traits in cattle, including the 3’ and 5’ UTR sequences and 10 bp intron-exon boundaries. The libraries were sequenced using a pair-end method of 300 bp with Illumina Miseq equipment (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, EE. UU.) in the Escola de Veterinária da UFMG (Belo Horizonte, Minas Gerais) genetic laboratory. Library quality control analysis was performed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Bioinformatic analysis

The quality of the DNA sequences obtained in ‘fastq’ format was analyzed using FastQC, a quality control tool for high-throughput sequencing data [19]. The results were consolidated using MultiQC software to generate a single report for all samples. The fastq files were processed using the GATK workflow [20] (Germline short variant discovery (SNPs + Indels)) v4.4.0 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360035535932-Germline-short-variant-discovery-SNPs-Indels-). Processed fastq files were aligned to the bovine reference genome ARS-UCD 2.0 utilizing BWA MEM software [21]. SAM files were converted to BAM files through SAMtools v1.7 software [22]. The next step was to identify the variants (SNPs and indels) per sample with the HaplotypeCaller software [23] in order to produce a file in GVCF format utilizing Genotype GVCFs [24] which was separated in two, one with SNPs data and the other containing indels. These two files were filtered, applying the recommended parameters of the GATK workflow (Hard filter SNPs and indels). Lastly SNPeff 5.1v [25] software (https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff) was used to annotate the different SNPs and indels and evaluate their effect in the target regions. This program classifies by effect into the following categories: high effect (the variant is assumed to have a high impact), moderate (it is a non disruptive variant which could affect the proteins effectiveness), low (low probability of changing protein behavior) and modifier predictions are difficult or there is no evidence of its impact). Lastly, polymorphisms were visualized using IGV software [26].

Coverage and depth analysis

Coverage (number of analyzed positions) and depth (measured as the number of reads per position) were evaluated. These metrics were estimated for each animal and gene, and the average and maximum depth values were calculated using SAMtools v1.7 software. In addition, the coverage and depth were visualized using the IGV software.

Population analyses

Gene frequencies and observed and expected heterozygosity of the detected SNPs were estimated from the VCF file using the PLINK v1.9 software [27]. SNPs were filtered using the command --maf 0.05 to retain those with a minor allele frequency ≥ 5%. Subsequently, the SNPs were evaluated against the ARS-UCD 2.0 reference genome to identify mutations resulting in non-synonymous substitutions. Genes containing such changes were selected and its protein fasta sequences were downloaded from the NCBI. The sequences were analyzed using BlastP (protein-protein BLAST) to search for homologous sequences in other mammalian species and the alignments were performed using the MEGA 11 software [28]. Finally, the degree of conservation of each polymorphic site was analyzed across species. Amino acid positions that were highly conserved in all or most species but differed in the Guzerat breed were considered to potentially have effect. Furthermore, changes in polarity were also evaluated, as these are expected to have greater impact. Non-synonymous substitutions that presented changes in their polarity were analyzed with four bioinformatic tools to predict the functional effect on the protein structure, stability, and function. These tools assess mutations using diverse approaches, each with unique strengths and limitations based on their underlying algorithms and criteria, and included: (i) SIFT (Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant; [29]), a sequence homology-based tool that sorts intolerant from tolerant amino acid substitutions and predicts whether an amino acid substitution in a protein will have a phenotypic effect. Positions with normalized probabilities < 0.05 are predicted to be deleterious and ≥ 0.05 are predicted to be tolerated; (ii) PANTHER-PSEP (Position-Specific Evolutionary Preservation-cSNP Scoring; [30]), analyzes the effect of mutations from an evolutionary perspective estimating the likelihood of a particular non-synonymous (amino-acid changing) coding SNP to cause a functional impact on the protein. Mutations are classified as probably harmful (preservation time of the position > 450 million years), possibly harmful (preservation time range 450 − 200 million years) and probably benign (preservation time < 200 million years); (iii) PolyPhen-2 (Polymorphism Phenotyping v2; [31]), is an automatic tool for prediction of possible impact of an amino acid substitution on the structure and function of a protein. Variants are classified as probably harmful (≥ 0.85), potentially harmful (0.15–0.85) and benign (0–0.15). This qualitative classification is based on pairs of false positive rates (FPR) thresholds (5%/10% FPR for HumDiv model), where the lowest FPR (up to 5%) is the most confident prediction (probably harmful), the following threshold is a less confident prediction and is classified as potentially harmful. The highest values of FPR are considered benign, and unknown if there is a lack of data which does not allow a prediction to be made; (iv) MutPred 2 [32], is a software package that integrates genetic and molecular data to reason probabilistically about the pathogenicity of amino acid substitutions. The values are considered pathogenic (score > 0.8) or non pathogenic (< 0.8).

Structural modelling of proteins

The proteins that exhibited a high or significant effect in at least three of the bioinformatic tools were searched in the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org [33]),. This database contains structural information of biomolecules obtained through different experimental techniques. For those proteins that were not reported or incomplete in PDB, the AlphaFold 2 software [34], a machine learning based predictor, was used to model the three-dimensional structure based on the protein sequence. This tool provides 5 different models as a result with different quality metrics based on Per-residue Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT). pLDDT is a per-residue confidence metric that estimates the agreement between the predicted structure and an experimental reference, based on the local distance difference test Cα (lDDT-Cα), and has been shown to be well-calibrated [35]. Moreover, lDDT has been demonstrated as a robust method for the automated assessment and evaluation of protein structure models in comparison to a reference structure [36]. High pLDDT indicate trustworthy models (90–100 range) meaning high precision and near the real structure, intermediate values (70–90 range) are close to the real structure and can have minor deviations, low values (50–69) are less reliable and can present significant deviations and < 50 are low trustworthiness as they can present major deviations. The output of the modeled proteins was visualized using ChimeraX software [37]. Finally, DDMut, a deep learning method, was used to predict changes in Gibbs Free Energy (ΔΔG) upon multiple or single point amino acid substitutions on protein stability. The results present the amino acid interactions, the number of hydrogen bonds, and the Predicted Stability Change (ΔΔGStability wt->mt), with values classified as stabilising (ΔΔG ≥ 0 kcal/mol) or destabilising (ΔΔG < 0 kcal/mol) mutations [38].

Results

Performance of target NGS assay

The workflow implemented in this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, 68 candidate genes associated with the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis were selected. Target regions were identified based on the UMD 3.1 reference genome, the version available at the time of the design. The final TruSeq Custom Amplicon panel comprised 730 regions, covering a total of 136,274 bp. DNA samples were used to amplify the amplicons, which were subsequently sequenced using NGS. The performance of the assay was evaluated in 75 bovine samples using the FastQC software. A total of 48 million reads were obtained, with an average of 600,000 reads per sample, ranging from 200,000 to 1.4 million reads. The percentage of overrepresented sequences was low (< 10%). All sequences had a length of 301 bp, with phred quality scores averaging above 25 up to the 150th nucleotide position.

Workflow implemented to select candidate SNPs using the NGS-target amplicon method

The maximum sequencing depth per animal ranged from 264 to 5,158, with an average depth ranging between 6 and 222. Individual coverage spanned from 3,326 to 57,869 bp. Regarding gene performance, the maximum depth ranged from 40 to 5,158, with an average depth between 5 and 181. Gene coverage ranged from 229 to 161,517 bp. Only three individuals and two genes had an average depth below 20X, rendering them unsuitable for accurate variant detection. The substantial variation in performance between individuals and genes could be attributed to the quality of the DNA samples and the amplification efficiency of the designed primers, respectively. These results demonstrate the robust performance of the developed NGS target assay.

From the BAM files and following the GATK protocol, a total of 2,600 SNPs and 1,615 indels were detected. Subsequent analysis using SNPeff software identified 103 multiallelic SNPs. The average polymorphism density was 1 SNP every 52 bp and 1 indel every 84 bp. The impact analysis revealed that 0.23% of the variants had high effects, 23.04% moderate, 35.16% low, and 39.50% modifier effects. These impact effects were distributed across various genomic regions: 59.52% were located within exons, 11.28% in introns, 14.08% in the 3’ UTR, while the remaining percentage was distributed across upstream and downstream regions, intergenic regions, 5’ UTR, and splicing sites. The functional analysis of SNPs, which assesses their effect on the amino acid sequence, found 58.95% non-synonymous and 37.40% synonymous substitutions, and 3.64% nonsense changes, resulting in a non-synonymous/synonymous ratio of 1.58. For indels, the impact analysis showed 44.88% with high impact, 12.62% moderate, 0.08% low, and 41.69% with modifier effects. Regarding their genomic distribution, 57.51% were located within exons, 15.9% in the 3’ UTR, 11.79% in introns, with the remainder distributed across the previously mentioned regions (Table 2).

For the subsequent analyses, indels were excluded due to their tendency to cause frameshifts in coding regions and their incompatibility with the PLINK v1.9 software. Additionally, multiallelic SNPs were removed, resulting in a total of 2,497 biallelic SNPs. The resulting VCF file was converted into PED and MAP formats to calculate allele frequencies and genetic diversity measured through heterozygosity, which ranged from 0.20 to 0.60 among individuals. SNPs were then filtered using the command --maf 0.05, resulting in a total of 475 SNPs.

For the next stage of this proof-of-concept study, SNPs causing non-synonymous changes that potentially affect the protein function were selected. Based on this criterion, 102 SNPs distributed in 40 genes remained (SI). To assess the degree of phylogenetic conservation of these amino acid sites, the gene sequences were aligned with those from the following species: Bos taurus, Bos javanicus, Bos grunniens, Bubalus bubalis, Camelus dromedarius, Canis lupus familiaris, Capra hircus, Delphinus delphis, Macroplus, Equus caballus, Felis Catus, Homo Sapiens, Ovis aries, Rattus norvegicus and Ursus arctos. Of the 102 non-synonymous changes, 66 positions were highly conserved across the mentioned species, which could be evidence of strong positive selection. Therefore, the presence of mutations in these positions were considered to have a significant effect in the protein structure and/or function. The other 36 positions were variable presenting multiple amino acids, or the alternative amino acid residue found in Guzerat was also present within the Bovidae family. For the 66 conserved positions, the polarity of the reference and alternative amino acids was analyzed to determine whether their chemical characteristics had changed, resulting in a total of 30 residues with opposite polarity.

Prediction of the functional impact of a non-synonymous amino acid substitution

Multiple tools were employed to predict the impact of the 30 non-synonymous substitutions. SIFT analysis predicted that 18 mutations would affect protein function, while 11 mutations were predicted to have no impact. PANTHER-PSEP analysis classified 8 substitutions as probably damaging, 9 possibly damaging, 8 probably benign, and 5 unscored mutations. Poly-Phen analysis identified 15 substitutions as probably damaging, 11 as possibly damaging, 2 as benign, and 2 as unrecognized mutations. MutPred2 analysis found only 2 mutations that affected protein function, with the remaining mutations classified as non-pathogenic (Table 3). When these analyses were combined, only one mutation (Cys372Gly, TAC3R) was significant in the four programs. Four substitutions (Thr74Met, CGA; Ser570Phe, HELB; Arg1095Gly, IGF1R; Thr469Pro, LHCGR) were significant in three programs, 8 mutations in two programs, 11 in one program, and 7 were not significant in any of the tools.

Structural modelling of proteins

Proteins that exhibited a high or significant effect in at least three of the bioinformatic tools (CGA, HELB, IGF1R, LHCGR, TAC3R) were searched in the PDB. However, none of these were available or were incomplete in the amino acid position of interest, therefore the structure predictor AlphaFold2 was used to obtain five structural models. The model that ranked best in AlphaFold2 for pLDDT was chosen for each protein, ranging from 65.1 to 86.4 (Table 4). The superposition of the modeled proteins using the reference and alternative amino acid were visualized using ChimeraX (Fig. 2). The predicted 3D structures were evaluated using DDMut. This program uses a computational method that predicts stability changes on a protein after a mutation. For IGF1R, LHCGR, and TAC3R SNPs, the analysis revealed destabilizing values in the predicted stability change (ΔΔG < 0 kcal/mol), while two substitutions exhibited a stabilizing effect (CGA and HELB; ΔΔG > 0 kcal/mol). With the exception of HELB, all substitutions led to changes in the number of hydrogen bonds, either increasing or decreasing their quantity. TAC3 showed the greatest difference, with 5 hydrogen bonds in the reference protein, compared to only 2 in the alternative. Additionally, the substitutions resulted in variations in amino acid interactions, altering the properties of the involved residues. For instance, LHCGR changed the interaction from Arg (basic and positive) to Glu (acidic and negatively charged), which represents a change in both charge and polarity. The reduced stability could be attributed to the differences in the number of hydrogen bonds and the altered amino acid interactions between the evaluated position and neighboring residues (Fig. 3; Table 4).

Discussion

The development and availability of second and third-generation sequencing technologies have enabled the detection of several million polymorphisms (SNPs and indels) in many species, including cattle [39, 41]. This information has been used in different type of studies, such as genome-wide association studies (GWAS), selection footprints, runs of homozygosity (ROH), and the identification of causal mutations for genetic conditions, among others [42, 43]. Several of these techniques base their analysis on linkage disequilibrium, which can hinder the identification of the causal mutation in the associated region. This led to the development of different strategies which involve application of successive filters to reduce, as much as possible, the number of candidate mutations that can be validated at an experimental level. The first step in the application of these strategies consists in deciding the relationship between coverage, depth and number of samples taking into account the performance of the used sequencer. Thus, for example, scientific researchers could choose to sequence an entire genome, an exome or a group of Target sequences which had been previously selected based on their particular needs and interests.

In this work, the NGS Target technique was applied, focusing on sequencing selected amplicons corresponding to 68 genes associated with reproductive traits in cattle, mainly involving the HPG axis. Sequencing these regions, followed by quality filtering, identified over 4,000 polymorphisms, corresponding to 1 SNP every 52 bp and 1 indel every 84 bp. The observed polymorphism rate in this study was similar to that reported by the 1000 Bull Genomes Project up to 2018 (1 variant every 33 bp). The discrepancy could be attributed to this study’s focus on sequencing exons rather than the entire genome analyzed by the consortium. Similar polymorphism density was reported in other published works [44, 46].

The next step in the proposed strategy involved discarding variants with frequencies below 5% to prioritize polymorphisms with a greater impact on the analyzed population. This resulted in fewer than 500 SNPs, though this number remains too high for experimental validation. As an alternative prior to experimental assays, in silico evaluations of the polymorphisms’ effects on protein structure, splicing, and gene expression, among others, can be performed. In this case, the selection was focused on SNPs located in exons that produced non-synonymous changes, potentially affecting the protein function and structure. These polymorphisms were evaluated through phylogenetic conservation analysis of the amino acid residues. This method assumes that amino acids playing significant roles in protein structure and function exhibit little variability among taxa [47]. Overall, this workflow identified 244 polymorphisms, of which 102 were non-synonymous substitutions. Based on phylogenetic conservation analysis, 66 of these SNPs were determined to potentially have a high impact on protein function. The remaining 36 were classified as benign because these positions exhibited high variability across the evaluated species, therefore it is assumed they have little effect on the protein structure and function.

Within the 102 non-synonymous changes, the LEPR gene presented 5 substitutions and was taken as an example of phylogenetic conservation analysis, since it is an extensively studied gene. The leptin receptor complex comprises a single subunit belonging to the class I cytokine receptor family. Its extracellular portion contains two cytokine receptor homolog (CRH) domains separated by an immunoglobulin-like domain, followed by two fibronectin type III (FN3) domains near the membrane [48]. FN3 domains display both functional and structural modularity (GeneCards; www.genecards.org/). Comparative analysis across species revealed variable results. Notably, amino acid 604, located within the FN3 domain (UniProt; www.uniprot.org), was conserved across all analyzed species but showed a non-synonymous change in the Guzerat cattle population. This change resulted in an aromatic (phenylalanine) replacing a conserved polar non-charged amino acid (cysteine), potentially indicating a significant impact on protein modulation. Similarly, amino acid 743 exhibited a non-synonymous change exclusively in Guzerat cattle, with leucine (non polar) replacing serine (polar, non-charged). Conversely, the change in the amino acid 207 (aspartic acid > asparagine) was also found in other Bovidae species (B. javanicus, B. mutus, B. bubalis, O. aries, C. hircus), suggesting that asparagine represents the ancestral variant for this taxonomic group, while the reference genome (B. taurus) displays aspartic acid. Also, the presence of amino acid diversity at this position across species suggests that this change is not critical for protein function. Similarly, the change in amino acid 800 found in Guzerat cattle (histidine) appears to be ancestral, as it is present in all analyzed species except for the bovine ARS 2.0 reference genome, which has a tyrosine. Lastly, the change in amino acid 197 (histidine > arginine) may represent a recent change since it was identified only in Guzerat and B. bubalis. Based on these results, amino acid 604 stands out as the best candidate for conducting more complex bioinformatics analyses to predict changes in protein function and ultimately perform in vivo and in vitro experimental assays.

Further filtering of substitution significance was performed using four different programs: SIFT, PANTHER-PSEP, PolyPhen-2, and MutPred 2. These programs yielded varying results due to the differences in their underlying algorithms. Only 5 mutations (16.6%) were identified as significant by at least three programs. Remarkably, the 604 aa (LEPR) discussed above presented significant effects in only two bioinformatic tools. These results highlight the importance of using multiple tools and models to increase accuracy and reliability, thereby reducing the number of SNPs requiring experimental validation [49, 50]. Similar findings were reported by previous works, for example, Girmay et al. [51]. initially identified 543 SNPs in the ovine LEP gene, and through combined analyses of bioinformatic tools (SIFT, PredictSNP2, and SNAP2) predicted 8 potentially harmful substitutions. Additional computational assessments of protein stability and function narrowed these to five SNPs that required in vivo and vitro validation. Another similar approach was conducted by Ali et al. [52], where three nucleotide substitutions in transferrin gene, related to mastitis resistance, were identified as probably harmful and with a diminishing effect on protein stability using bioinformatic tools (SIFT, PROVEAN, and PolyPhen-2). Matosinho et al. [53]. studied the effect of genetic variants on milk proteins in Guzerat and Gyr cattle through WGS, and with the use of bioinformatic tools (SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and PANTHER), 13 of the 71 variants (7 indels and 64 SNPs) for 7 genes were considered candidate functional variants.

The methodology employed in this study enabled the identification of five mutations in genes that play key roles in reproduction. These significant substitutions include CGA aa74, HELB aa570, IGF1R aa1095, LHCGR aa469 and TAC3R aa372. Among these, TAC3R plays a crucial role in the regulation of feed intake and reproduction [54]. Similarly, CGA is essential for maintaining basic functions of the pituitary gland [55], and HELB is associated with yearling weight and adaptation to tropical climates [56]. IGF1R has key functions in ovulation and pregnancy [], while LHCGR is involved in ovulation, luteinization, and has been linked to superovulation traits [58]. These mutations were significant in at least three bioinformatic tools and were further analyzed using AlphaFold2 and DDMut to predict the 3D structure and to assess their potential effects on protein stability, respectively. Also, in order to continue the example mentioned above, although aa604 LEPR was found significant in only two bioinformatic tools, this protein was modeled and then analysed using DDMut. The result predicted that this amino acid change was destabilizing, showing the importance of combining different approaches. Furthermore, this program allowed us to identify 3 variants with destabilising effect and the differences in hydrogen bonds and neighbor amino acid residues (IGF1R, LHCGR, TAC3R). The reduced stability could be attributed to the difference in the number of hydrogen bonds, as they provide rigidity to the protein structure and specificity to molecular interaction and recognition. Moreover, they are crucial for protein folding and protein structure [59]. The unique polymorphism that was found potentially harmful using the different bioinformatic tools (aa372, TAC3R) also exhibited the highest change in hydrogen bonds number (reference = 5; alternative = 2) and a Predicted Stability Change value of −0.29 kcal/mol.

Conclusion

In conclusion, genotyping by sequencing using amplicons Target NGS assays allow the detection of hundreds to thousands polymorphisms in genomic regions of interest. This approach combined with different bioinformatics tools can be useful to determine the potential effect on the protein function and structure in order to prioritize certain polymorphisms for experimental validation. In the present work we proposed a framework to genotype and select SNPs, an approach that could be highly beneficial for studying and identifying polymorphisms associated with important traits, such as productive traits (growth rate, intramuscular fat), reproductive traits (fertility, age at first calving), or identification of causal variants of genetic conditions. In particular, in this work it was applied in genes involved in the HPG axis as the onset of puberty is a common reproductive issue in zebuine breeds. This workflow allowed us to reduce from > 4,000 polymorphisms to 3 candidate mutations. In order to have a more precise prediction, further studies such as molecular dynamic simulations or in vitro and in vivo models are needed.
