Rebound warfarin toxicity presenting with spontaneous hemopericardium in cardiac tamponade: a case report

Background

Cardiac tamponade is a medical or traumatic emergency, caused by the accumulation of fluids around the pericardial sac, leading to reduced cardiac output and shock. The degree of tamponade depends on the pericardial compliance and rate of pericardial fluid accumulation. In the case of rapid accumulation of pericardial fluid, the compensatory pericardial stretch is not achieved, leading to cardiac tamponade [1]. The spontaneous hemopericardium due to warfarin toxicity is uncommon but can be fatal if the intervention is delayed, especially in the setting of cardiac tamponade [2]. Managing warfarin toxicity is challenging as the risks and benefits between bleeding and thrombotic events need to be considered. In the event of reversal of coagulopathy, the half-life of the offending agent and the reversal agent must be considered. We present a case of rebound warfarin toxicity, presenting with spontaneous hemopericardium and cardiac tamponade, despite correcting coagulopathy with FFP and discontinuing the warfarin during a previous presentation. We attribute the rebound warfarin toxicity to the shorter half-life of the FFP compared to warfarin.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old male with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension, and atrial fibrillation presented to our ED with shortness of breath and left-sided chest pain for a two-day duration. There was no associated fever, cough, or sputum production. He was on warfarin for atrial fibrillation, started five years ago, when he presented with a transient ischemic attack. Over the past six months he had multiple ED visits due to warfarin toxicity, presenting as calf and popliteal fossa hematoma; however, it did not necessitate ward admission. On examination, he appeared to be in distress with a pain score of 8/10. His vital parameters were significant for tachycardia of 124/min, borderline blood pressure of 90/60mmHg, and normal SPO2 of 94% on room air. On auscultation, muffled heart sounds were noted. The patient’s most recent ED visit occurred three days prior, during which he received four units of fresh frozen plasma for a supratherapeutic INR of 8.66. Although there were no clinical signs of bleeding, the INR was corrected to 2.27, and warfarin was discontinued upon discharge. After discharge from ED, the patient was started on dabigatran as an outpatient and took evening dose, which was approximately 12 h prior to current presentation with cardiac tamponade. The electrocardiogram demonstrated a low-voltage QRS complex and atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (Fig. 2). A chest radiograph revealed an enlarged cardiac silhouette (Fig. 3). Point-of-care cardiac ultrasound identified a large pericardial effusion with a maximum diameter of 5 cm (Fig. 4). The emergent 2-D echocardiography confirmed a massive pericardial effusion with diastolic collapse of right ventricle consistent with cardiac tamponade.

Repeat laboratory testing following pericardiocentesis revealed a marked rise in INR from 2.27 at recent discharge to 6.34 despite the discontinuation of warfarin. The patient confirmed that he had not mistakenly taken warfarin after his discharge from emergency department. Given the widened mediastinum on chest radiography and the presence of clotted blood in the hemopericardium- suggestive of recent active bleeding- aortic dissection was suspected. However, CT-aortography showed no evidence of dissection. Hemopericardial fluid was tested for Mycobacterium tuberculosis using GeneXpert, which returned negative.

Ultrasound-guided emergent pericardiocentesis via the apical approach drained 525 ml of hemopericardial fluid. Following the pericardiocentesis, the patient’s hemodynamic status stabilized and his chest pain resolved. The patient also received 10 ml/kg of FFP and 10 mg of vitamin K daily, correcting the INR from 6.34 to 1.74. The possibility of dabigatran toxicity in the context of recent warfarin toxicity, was considered; however neither idarucizumab, nor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) was available in our setting and thus they were not administered. The patient was admitted to the medical ward for observation and was discharged after one week, once no recurrent hemopericardium was confirmed. At the three-month cardiology outpatient department follow-up, the patient had no evidence of recurrent pericardial effusion and was doing well on dabigatran for stroke prevention.

Discussion

Managing warfarin toxicity presents a significant clinical challenge as physicians must balance the risks of bleeding against the potential for thrombotic events. When correcting coagulopathy, it is important to consider both the half-life of the anticoagulant and the reversal agent used. Warfarin has an elimination half-life of approximately 36 to 42 h, while dabigatran’s half-life ranges from 7 to 17 h [3]. In contrast, vitamin K, a reversal agent, has a much shorter half-life of about 1.7 h, and the fresh frozen plasma (FFP) has an effective duration of action of approximately 2 to 6 h, necessitating repeat transfusions every 6 h in cases of clinically significant bleeding [4].

In this case, the patient initially presented with an INR of 8.66 but showed no signs of bleeding. He was discharged after correcting with FFP, with a discharge INR value of 2.27. Two days later, the patient was reviewed in the outpatient department, and dabigatran was initiated without bridging anticoagulation, given his multiple ED visit for warfarin toxicity. However, the prolonged half-life of warfarin was not accounted for and the patient returned the following day with a rebound INR of 6.38 and cardiac tamponade due to spontaneous hemopericardium. Notably, the hemopericardium may have been exacerbated by a single dose of dabigatran taken approximately 12 h prior to current ED visit, as the peak plasma concentration is typically reached within two hours of administration [5].

Non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs), including dabigatran, rivaroxaban, and apixaban, are commonly used for prevention of ischemic stroke and systemic embolism in non-valvular atrial fibrillation [6]. Dabigatran is a direct thrombin (factor IIa) inhibitor, whereas rivaroxaban and apixaban are direct factor Xa inhibitors. Initiation of dabigatran typically requires bridging parenteral anticoagulation with low molecular weight heparin. In contrast rivaroxaban and apixaban can be initiated directly without the need for heparin bridging [6].

Clinical trials demonstrated that in patients with atrial fibrillation, dabigatran given at a dose of 110 mg was associated with rates of stroke and systemic embolism that were similar to those associated with warfarin, as well as lower rates of major hemorrhage. Dabigatran administered at a dose of 150 mg, as compared with warfarin, was associated with lower rates of stroke and systemic embolism but similar rates of major hemorrhage. (ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT00262600). In patients with atrial fibrillation, rivaroxaban was non-inferior to warfarin for the prevention of stroke or systemic embolism. There was no significant between-group difference in the risk of major bleeding, although intracranial and fatal bleeding occurred less frequently in the rivaroxaban group. (ROCKET AF ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT00403767.)

However, in patients with atrial fibrillation, apixaban was superior to warfarin in preventing stroke or systemic embolism, caused less bleeding, and resulted in lower mortality (ARISTOTLE ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT00412984.) [7].

Due to the higher cost of NOACs and their antidotes, warfarin remains the most widely used oral anticoagulant in many low and middle-income countries, including Bhutan. Our patient was prescribed warfarin for non-valvular atrial fibrillation, as NOACs such as dabigatran were not readily available. A study in national referral hospital of Bhutan, on warfarin toxicity identified warfarin as the most commonly used oral anticoagulant, with gastrointestinal bleeding and muscular hematomas as the most frequent bleeding complications [8]. Consistent with these findings, over the past six months, our patient had frequent emergency department visit with a calf muscle hematoma prior to the current presentation with hemopericardium.

In the management of warfarin toxicity, it is essential to consider both the half-life of the reversal agent and that of warfarin itself. Although spontaneous hemopericardium is a rare complication of warfarin toxicity, cardiac tamponade demands prompt expert intervention to perform pericardiocentesis.

Conclusion

When managing any form of toxicity in the ED, the half-lives of both the offending agent and its antidote must be considered. For example, patients with opioid overdose who receive naloxone are monitored for a specific duration, due to shorter half-life of naloxone compared to that of most opioids. A similar principle applies to warfarin toxicity. In cases of warfarin toxicity treated with FFP and vitamin K, clinicians must remain vigilant for rebound warfarin toxicity, given warfarin’s prolonged half-life. Although hemopericardium is a rare manifestation of warfarin toxicity, timely pericardiocentesis is critical for patient survival.
