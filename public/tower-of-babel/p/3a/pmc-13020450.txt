Staphylococcus lugdunensis‐Infected Left Atrial Myxoma Complicated by Mitral Valve Endocarditis and Septic Emboli: A Case Report
Authors: Karuna Rayamajhi, Fnu Parul, Zan Siddiqi, Majid Yavari, Michael J. Kehdi, Subhashis Mitra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiac myxomas are benign tumors; however, infected myxomas represent a rare clinical presentation. Infected myxomas can cause concomitant infective valve endocarditis, which may lead to complications such as persistent bacteremia, valvular insufficiency/stenosis, and embolic phenomena if not treated promptly. Early diagnosis and treatment, often including antibiotics and urgent surgery, are crucial to prevent severe complications.

Introduction

Cardiac myxomas are the most common benign primary cardiac tumors, predominantly involving the left atrium, though cases in the right atrium and ventricles have been reported [1]. Infected myxomas are rare, with fewer than 90 documented cases [2]. While infected and non‐infected myxomas share similar symptoms, the former presents with more severe manifestations, including persistent fever and a heightened risk of embolic complications [3]. We report a case of left atrial myxoma infected with Staphylococcus lugdunensis , complicated by acute severe mitral regurgitation, mitral valve endocarditis, and septic emboli to the spleen and brain.

Case Presentation/Examination

A 38‐year‐old male with no significant medical history presented with a 3‐week history of flu‐like symptoms, including dry cough, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, myalgia, loss of appetite, night sweats, chills, and diarrhea. Initial evaluation revealed a temperature of 102°F, tachycardia (130 bpm), and otherwise normal blood pressure. Physical examination didn't reveal any abnormal heart sounds.

Methods

Investigations

ECG showed atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. CT imaging was suggestive of acute pulmonary edema, and significant laboratory findings included leukocytosis (17.2 × 103/μL), elevated CRP (10.8 mg/dL), ESR (69 mm/h), BNP (522 pg/mL), and hemoglobin (8.2 mg/dL). Initial management was done with rate control, anticoagulation, and empirical antibiotic coverage with third‐generation cephalosporin and vancomycin.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a 4.5 × 3.6 cm left atrial mass attached to the interatrial septum. TEE confirmed a 5.6 × 3 cm myxoma prolapsing through the mitral valve, causing dynamic mitral regurgitation and severe mitral stenosis (transmitral gradient: 12 mmHg). A 2 × 1 cm vegetation was also noted at the tip of the myxoma (Figure 1, Video 1). Of note, the patient didn't have any history of intravenous drug abuse, recent dental procedures/infections, or implanted devices/catheters; however, initial presentation was suggestive of ongoing sinusitis, which could have served as a potential source of infection. Blood cultures grew S. lugdunensis . Pre‐surgical evaluation revealed cardioembolic phenomena, including brain and splenic infarcts, though the patient had no focal neurological deficits (Figure 2).

Treatment

The patient underwent urgent complex cardiac surgery at day seven of presentation, including myxoma excision, mitral valve replacement, atrial septal defect repair with a bovine pericardial patch, biatrial MAZE procedure with bilateral pulmonary vein isolation, and left atrial appendage ligation. Gross pathology of the excised mass showed a 6.3 × 4.5 × 2.5 cm tumor with an anterior mitral leaflet vegetation measuring 2.5 × 1.5 × 0.5 cm (Figure 3). Histopathology confirmed acute inflammation, granulation tissue, and numerous bacterial colonies, confirming infected cardiac myxoma and infective endocarditis (Figure 4). Surgical cultures also grew S. lugdunensis .

Outcome and Follow Up

Based on the above, the patient's final diagnosis was infected left atrial myxoma with mitral valve infective endocarditis complicated by septic emboli. The patient recovered uneventfully and completed a six‐week course of IV nafcillin for the same. He was discharged on anticoagulation, aspirin, a statin, and oral diuretics.

Discussion

Left atrial myxoma is the most common cardiac tumor. Although benign, complications such as mitral regurgitation and systemic embolism have been previously associated with atrial myxomas. Most cases of atrial myxomas are uninfected, and to date, fewer than 90 cases of infected atrial myxomas have been reported in the literature. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, only two cases of left atrial myxomas infected with S. lugdunensis have been documented [4, 5]. S. lugdunensis, a virulent coagulase‐negative staphylococcus, is a species of coagulase‐negative staphylococci (CNS), known to be a normal skin commensal that preferentially colonizes the perineal region and is also substantially present in the axilla and nares. S. lugdunensis is more aggressive compared to other coagulase‐negative staphylococci (CoNS) due to its unique virulence factors and mechanisms. It possesses an iron‐regulated surface determinant (Isd) system and small cytolytic synergistic hemolysins (SLUSH) that enhance its cytotoxicity. Additionally, S. lugdunensis can bind directly to von Willebrand factor (VWF) under shear stress conditions, which is crucial for its adhesion to endothelial cells and cardiac valves, leading to severe infections like endocarditis. Furthermore, the Agr quorum‐sensing system in S. lugdunensis regulates the expression of various virulence factors, enhancing its resistance to host innate immune defenses [6, 7, 8]. It can cause severe infections, including infective endocarditis, which often necessitates surgical intervention and can lead to high mortality rates. Children, the elderly, cancer patients, and individuals with implanted medical devices or undergoing invasive procedures are at higher risk for these infections. Untreated S. lugdunensis infections can result in aggressive endocarditis, bacteremia, skin and soft tissue infections, catheter‐related bacteremia, deep‐seated infections, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, and postoperative endophthalmitis [9, 10]. S. lugdunensis is often misidentified as S. aureus due to the presence of a membrane‐bound form of coagulase in some isolates, although it lacks the secreted coagulase typical of S. aureus . Diagnostic accuracy has improved with the use of matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionization‐time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS) and real‐time PCR assays [11]. The organism is generally susceptible to a wide range of antibiotics, although resistance to penicillin and oxacillin has been reported [12]. Therefore, prompt recognition and treatment in the infected cases is of utmost importance.

The concurrent presence of infective endocarditis with mitral valve involvement, secondary to direct seeding from the infected myxoma, is another rare finding observed in the reported case. The diagnosis of infected myxoma is based on clinical and pathological criteria, which include evidence of myxoma on pathological evaluation, demonstration of organisms and/or signs of inflammation within the myxoma tissue, and/or positive blood cultures [13]. Based on these criteria, infected atrial myxomas are classified into three categories: definite, probable, and possible. Our patient met all the criteria and was therefore diagnosed with a definite infected cardiac myxoma.

Although there is an overlap in clinical features between infected and uninfected myxomas, the former is typically associated with a higher risk of complications, including multiple embolic events—elevating the risk by two to three times due to increased tissue friability compared to uninfected myxomas—as well as moderate‐to‐high‐grade fever, which in some cases may be persistent [3]. In the reported case, the patient was found to have systemic embolization, as evidenced by acute parietal and splenic infarcts on imaging. Therefore, we recommend that any patient presenting with fever in the setting of a myxoma should undergo blood culture testing to guide appropriate management.

Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography serve as valuable tools for assessing myxomas, detecting concurrent endocarditis, and identifying associated complications. Given the limited evidence regarding the optimal timing of surgery in patients with atrial myxomas, various factors must be considered to perform a proper risk–benefit analysis. According to current guidelines for the surgical management of infective endocarditis complicated by concurrent non‐hemorrhagic stroke, surgery should ideally be delayed by at least one to 2 weeks to minimize the risk of stroke exacerbation, which is highest within the first 7 days following an acute stroke. Additionally, these patients should undergo neurological imaging, such as CT or MRI, to assess readiness for cardiac surgery [14]. However, in addition to the absence of guidelines for surgical treatment in cases of infected cardiac myxoma, the increased risk of complications—such as severe mitral regurgitation and heart failure, as seen in our case—necessitates an individualized assessment of surgical timing. When indicated, surgery should not be delayed, and a multidisciplinary team approach should be considered.

Currently, no specific guidelines exist regarding antibiotic therapy for infected myxomas, and most cases are treated according to infective endocarditis guidelines. In our case, the patient received a six‐week course of intravenous nafcillin and demonstrated a complete recovery without complications, with full resolution of bacteremia.

Our case highlights the importance of prompt diagnosis and management of infected atrial myxomas. Due to the lack of comprehensive guidelines, management should be individualized on a case‐by‐case basis, and a multidisciplinary approach should be employed when necessary to ensure appropriate treatment. Further research is needed to develop standardized guidelines for the management of infected myxomas.
