Changes in brain morphology over the first year in adult mixed-mechanism mild traumatic brain injury: the role of injury severity factors☆
Authors: Jonas Stenberg, Gerard Janez Brett Clarke, Hallvard Røe Evensmoen, Anne Vik, Toril Skandsen, Asta Kristine Håberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•mTBI is associated with ventricular enlargement.•The ventricular enlargement is particularly pronounced in patients with complicated mTBI.•Complicated mTBI is also characterized by cortical area decreases and subcortical volume loss.•Most of the brain atrophy occurs during the first 3 months after injury and persists at 12 months.•Gray matter/white matter intensity ratio affects measures of cortical thickness.Keywords: Concussion, Mild head injury, Post-traumatic confusional state, Longitudinal

Introduction

Moderate-severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) is characterized by brain morphometric changes such as ventricular enlargement, white matter (WM) loss, and gray matter (GM) loss in cortex as well as in hippocampus and deep nuclei (Ross, 2011, Brezova et al., 2014, Cole et al., 2018). To what extent this is the case also for mild TBI (mTBI), however, remains unclear, as findings from studies investigating this have been mixed (Richter et al., 2021, Hellstrøm et al., 2017, Zagorchev et al., 2016, Muller et al., 2021, Dall'Acqua et al., 2017, Govindarajan et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2015, Dall’Acqua et al., 2016, Bajaj et al., 2018, Narayana et al., 2015, Ling et al., 2013). Still, most longitudinal mTBI studies over the last decade have reported morphometric changes, such as ventricular enlargement (Richter et al., 2021) and brain volume loss in subcortical structures (Hellstrøm et al., 2017, Zagorchev et al., 2016), WM (Richter et al., 2021), and cortex (Muller et al., 2021). Regarding the cortex, some mTBI studies have uncovered cortical thickening (Dall'Acqua et al., 2017), or both thickening and thinning, depending on the cortical region (Govindarajan et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2015). Cortical area is another measure of interest, albeit less investigated than cortical thickness. Cross-sectional mTBI studies have found both smaller (Dall’Acqua et al., 2016, Bajaj et al., 2018) and larger (Bajaj et al., 2018) cortical area compared to healthy controls. However, a few studies have not found any brain volume or cortical changes at all following mTBI (Narayana et al., 2015, Ling et al., 2013).

Importantly, the majority of prospective brain morphological studies in people with mTBI have assessed brain structures at maximum two timepoints, and with sample sizes too small to detect a 10% difference between groups (Liem et al., 2015). Scan intervals have also varied between studies, which hinders cross-study comparability. In order to more accurately describe the nature of morphometric changes in different regions, three or more time points are preferred, yet studies with more than two brain MRI assessments are rare in the mTBI field. One such study on sport-related concussion investigated 36 concussed individuals with surface-based morphometry at three time points during the first month after the injury, finding no significant change in GM volume or cortical thickness (Españ et al., 2017). Likewise, Zhou et al. investigated patients with mTBI (n = 56) three times (within 10 days, and at 1 and 6 months after injury) and found a reduction in thalamic and hippocampal volume during the first month post-injury (Zhuo et al., 2021). However, the controls in this study were scanned only once, which is problematic since the random variation in brain measures over time in healthy controls was thus unaccounted for.

Furthermore, there is a notable paucity of studies investigating the effect of injury severity factors, such as the presence of loss of consciousness (LOC), the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, or the duration of post-traumatic amnesia (PTA), on morphological changes in mTBI. Whether the presence of intracranial traumatic findings, often referred to as complicated mTBI, influences brain morphology, was investigated by Hellstrøm et al. (Hellstrøm et al., 2017). Using surface-based morphometry, they did not find a greater reduction in total GM volume or cortical volume in 29 patients with complicated mTBI compared to 33 patients with uncomplicated mTBI, from 1 to 12 months post-injury. Other studies have restricted their recruitment to patients with uncomplicated mTBI. For example, Narayana et al. (Narayana et al., 2015) found no brain volume changes in 62 patients with uncomplicated mTBI from admission to 3 months using tensor-based morphometry, while Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2015) – using surface-based morphometry – found decreases in cortical thickness in 21 patients with uncomplicated mTBI, from 7 days to 3 months, and Dall’Acqua et al. (Dall'Acqua et al., 2017) found an increase in cortical thickness between 7 days and 12 months in 49 patients with uncomplicated mTBI. Muller et al. (Muller et al., 2021), using voxel-based morphometry, found a decrease in both gray and WM volume in 48 patients with uncomplicated mTBI from 6 weeks to 12 months. Taken together, the effect of injury severity variables on brain morphological changes after mTBI remains unclear.

The discrepant findings related to cortical morphology changes over time following mTBI (Muller et al., 2021, Dall'Acqua et al., 2017, Govindarajan et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2015) could be due to differences in tissue contrast in the transition zone between the lower part of the cortex (GM) and the outer part of hemispheric WM. With T1-weighted MRI scans used in studies of brain morphometry, T1 relaxation times are longer in WM due to high lipid content and greater cellular density, than in GM. Microscopic changes in the WM, related to for instance traumatic axonal injury (TAI), could lead to less myelinated and less dense cellular packing in WM. This could reduce WM signal intensity, thus affecting the appearance of where GM ends and WM begins, along with cortical thickness and volume measures. Previous studies have revealed associations between axonal properties and tissue intensities in the junction between GM and WM (Koenig, 1991, Bansal et al., 2013). Iron depositions as part of TAI could also influence signal intensity in this junction (Ogg and Steen, 1998). The GM/WM intensity ratio quantifies the signal contrast between GM and WM and authors such as Mills et al. (Mills et al., 2020) have suggested it should be taken into account in mTBI studies to clarify if GM-WM signal intensity changes following the traumatic injury influence the cortical measurements derived from T1-weighted scans.

Here, we addressed the limitations of previous studies by investigating brain morphological changes in a large sample of patients with mTBI assessed with MRI at admission, 3 months, and 12 months after injury. A matched community control group was assessed at the same scanning intervals as the patients. We computed cortical area and cortical thickness separately and investigated how the inclusion of the GM/WM intensity ratio in the models affected changes in cortical thickness and area. Finally, we performed sub-group analyses in which we stratified the mTBI group on several injury severity-related factors.

Materials and methods

Participants and recruitment

The Trondheim mTBI study is a large-scale prospective cohort study with follow up for 12 months in patients with mTBI between 16–60 years of age and age-, sex- and education-matched community controls. The study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics (2013/754), and participants gave informed consent. Patients with mTBI (n = 378) were included from April 1st 2014 to December 15th 2015. They were recruited from two emergency departments (EDs): St. Olavs hospital (Trondheim University Hospital), a regional level 1 trauma center in Trondheim, Norway, and Trondheim Municipal Emergency clinic, a general practitioner-run, 24-hour/7-day out-patient clinic.

Inclusion criteria for patients with mTBI were having sustained TBI (Menon et al., 2010) that was further classified as mild according to World Health Organization criteria (Carroll et al., 2004), i.e. GCS score of 13–15, < 30 min LOC, and < 24 h PTA. Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-fluency in the Norwegian language, (2) pre-existing neurological, psychiatric, somatic, or substance use disorder; determined to be severe enough to interfere with follow-up and outcome assessment, (3) a prior history of a complicated mild (i.e. having trauma-related intracranial findings on CT or MRI), moderate or severe TBI, (4) other major trauma that could interfere with follow-up or outcome assessment, or (5) presentation > 48 h after the initial trauma. See Skandsen et al. (Skandsen et al., 2018) and Einarsen et al. (Einarsen et al., 2019) for more details regarding patient enrolment and clinical ratings.

Clinical and demographic information was obtained from patient interviews and medical records. Education was recorded as total years of education. LOC was rated as present only if observed. GCS score was assessed in the ED or inferred from records. Duration of PTA was recorded as time after injury for which the patient had no continuous memory (< 1 h, or 1–24 h).

MRI scanning protocol

A subgroup, included in an extended follow-up study, underwent a standardized brain MRI scan at admission (defined as within 72 h of injury: median = 58, interquartile range = 40–64 (Einarsen et al., 2019)), at 3 months and at 12 months. All MRI scans were acquired using the same protocol on the same 3.0 Tesla Siemens Skyra MRI scanner with a 32-channel head coil. The protocol included 3D volumes with T1-weighted (Magnetization Prepared Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echo), T2-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, and susceptibility-weighted scans. Scan parameters are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

The MRI scans at admission were read by neuroradiologists to identify visible traumatic intracranial lesions, according to standard criteria (Einarsen et al., 2019). Further details regarding the MRI findings and their development over time are presented in Einarsen et al. (Einarsen et al., 2019). One patient with a positive CT scan was unable to undergo MRI at inclusion, hence the reading of the CT scan was used instead of MRI to describe TBI-related intracranial findings.

MRI findings were dichotomized into intracranial traumatic lesions “yes” or “no” in this study – the “yes” category includes all possible findings (contusions, hematomas and traumatic axonal injuries, TAI). A complicated mTBI is defined as a mTBI with intracranial traumatic lesions and a uncomplicated mTBI as a mTBI without intracranial traumatic lesions.

MRI image processing

Cortical thickness, cortical area, GM/WM intensity ratio, and subcortical volumes were estimated using the automated segmentation and cortical parcellation pipeline in FreeSurfer version 6.0.0 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) (Dale et al., 1999, Fischl et al., 1999, Fischl et al., 1999, Fischl et al., 2002, Fischl et al., 2004, Fischl et al., 2004, Fischl and Dale, 2000, Winkler et al., 2012) including registration to a common spherical atlas based on cortical folding patterns (Fischl et al., 1999). The GM/WM intensity ratio estimates the difference in the signal in the junction between cortical GM and the underlying WM and was calculated using the following formula:

GM/WM intensity ratio=(100(WM-GM))/(0.5(WM + GM)).

With WM being the WM signal intensity 1 mm below the WM surface, and GM the GM signal intensity 30% into the cortex from the gray/white border (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/pub/dist/freesurfer/dev_binaries/centos6_x86_64/pctsurfcon) (Putcha et al., 2023, Salat et al., 2009, Salat et al., 2011). The vertex-wise cortical surface (Dale et al., 1999, Fischl et al., 1999) and cortical thickness (Fischl and Dale, 2000) results from the surface based stream, as well as the GM/WM intensity ratio results were combined with an automatic parcellation procedure (Fischl et al., 2004), to get the mean cortical thickness and area for the frontal-, parietal-, temporal-, and occipital lobe, and the cingulate- and insular cortex (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/FreeSurferWiki/LCN-CortLobes) (Klein and Tourville, 2012). Subcortical volumes of the hippocampus, basal ganglia, thalamus, cerebral WM, and ventricles were estimated using the volume-based stream (Fischl et al., 2002, Fischl et al., 2004). Intracranial volume (ICV) was estimated in SPM8 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) with the automatic reverse brain mask method (Hansen et al., 2015).

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize demographic and clinical categorical variables. The mean, standard deviation, and range of age are presented.

Linear mixed model (LMM) analyses were conducted to assess changes in cortical thickness, cortical area, and subcortical volumes over time. Measures of cortical area and volume were first corrected for ICV using the residuals method described in Hansen et al. (Hansen et al., 2015). Time, group, and a time-by-group interaction were entered as fixed effects. Interactions were retained in all models regardless of statistical significance. Age, sex and education were also included as fixed covariates. Additionally, we included GM/WM intensity ratio as a covariate of interest in the LMMs assessing cortical thickness and area over time. The results from models without GM/WM intensity ratio as a covariate are presented in the supplementary materials.

As primary analyses, we assessed changes in morphometric brain measures in all patients with mTBI compared to community controls. We then performed subgroup analyses on patients with mTBI, comparing morphometric changes on indices of (1) complicated mTBI (complicated mTBI vs uncomplicated mTBI); (2) LOC (observed LOC vs unobserved LOC); (3) GCS (13–14 vs 15); and (4) PTA (1–24 h vs < 1 h). P-values of main effects were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Benjamini & Hochberg’s correction for false discovery rate (Holm, 1979), with significance level set to p < 0.05. For brain measures showing a significant main effect of group or a significant group-by-time interaction, group differences at each timepoint were assessed using post-hoc contrasts adjusted by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD). Within-group changes across time were assessed only if there was a significant time-by-group interaction.

Prior to model specification, all brain morphometric values were assessed for timepoint differences between admission and 3 months, and 3 months and 12 months, that would constitute outliers. Any timepoint differences in individual metrics that were greater or less than 3 SD were deemed outliers and removed.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2022). Linear mixed models were computed using the nlme package (Pinheiro et al., 2025), and post-hoc contrasts were calculated using the emmeans package (Lenth and Piaskowski, 2025).

Results

Sample characteristics

The total sample included 194 patients with mTBI and 83 community controls that were scanned with brain MRI at one or more timepoints. By 12 months, 150 patients with mTBI and 67 community controls remained in the study, giving a retention rate of 77.3% and 80.7% for each group, respectively (Fig. 1).

Enrolment and follow-up of (A) Patients with mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) and (B) Community controls.

Patients with mTBI were predominantly male (63.4%), with a mean age of 32.5 and an average education of 13.9 years. The GCS score was 13–14 in 18.6%, LOC was observed in 47.9%, and 31.4% had PTA between 1 h and 24 h. Traumatic intracranial findings on MRI at admission were identified in 12.4% and 34.5% experienced extracranial injuries (Table 1).

A significant increase in ventricular volume in patients with mTBI between admission and 3 months was found (Table 4, Fig. 2). No significant changes in ventricular volume over time were evident in the community control group.

Time differences

A significant decrease in cortical area in the frontal and temporal lobes from admission to 3 months in patients with complicated mTBI was found (Table 4, Fig. 3). There were no significant changes from 3 months to 12 months in either cortical area, and cortical area at 12 months remained significantly lower compared to admission in the frontal lobe (p < 0.001), but not the temporal lobe (p = 0.092). Further, volume reductions from admission to 3 months were evident in patients with complicated mTBI in the hippocampus, and the thalamus, while the cerebral WM decrease did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.105, Table 4, Fig. 4). Ventricular volume significantly increased from admission to 3 months in both patients with complicated and uncomplicated mTBI, with a larger increase found in patients with complicated mTBI (estimated increase = 973 mm3, Table 4) than uncomplicated (estimated increase = 312 mm3, Table 4). From 3 to 12 months, there were no significant changes in any of the volumes or groups assessed. The significant decrease in cortical frontal lobe area, thalamic and hippocampal volume in patients with complicated mTBI, along with the significant increase in ventricular volume in both patients with complicated and uncomplicated mTBI, remained significant at 12 months compared to admission. The decrease in cerebral WM volume in complicated mTBI patients was significant only at 12 months compared to admission. For patients with uncomplicated mTBI, there were no significant changes over time, except for the ventricular volume increase.

Group differences

Our results uncovered thicker insular cortices in patients with complicated mTBI, compared to uncomplicated mTBI, which reached statistical significance at admission and 3 months, but not 12 months (p = 0.117) (Table 5, Fig. 3).

The effect of GM/WM intensity ratio

The effect of GM/WM intensity ratio on morphometric structures was significant in the majority of models (Table 2, Table 3), confirming the general relationship between GM/WM ratio and cortical thickness and area across groups (in mTBI, and mTBI subgroups as well as the controls).

All models were conducted also without GM/WM ratio as a covariate and the results are reported in Supplementary Tables 2–5. In the comparisons between patients with mTBI and community controls, there were no differences in significant main effects (i.e. interaction or group effects) when GM/WM intensity ratio was not included as a covariate (Supplementary Table 2).

In the comparisons between complicated and uncomplicated mTBI, there were no differences in significant main effects when GM/WM intensity ratio was not included as a covariate in most models (Supplementary Tables 3–5), but a significant group by time interaction for occipital lobe cortical area (similar to the results on frontal and temporal lobe area described above) was evident and the significant group effect in insular cortex was uncovered only when GM/WM intensity ratio was accounted for.

In the comparisons between patients with observed and unobserved LOC, without GM/WM intensity ratio included as a covariate, the group difference in temporal lobe area remained (Supplementary Tables 3–5). In addition, group by time interaction effects for frontal and parietal lobe cortical thickness were evident.

No differences in significant main effects were determined when GM/WM intensity ratio was not included as a covariate in analyses for the GCS or PTA comparisons (Supplementary Table 3).

Summary of the main results

Patients with mTBI had larger ventricular volumes compared to controls at all timepoints, along with significant volume increase over 12 months. The largest increase occurred during the first 3 months and was more pronounced in patients with complicated mTBI. Additionally, patients with complicated mTBI, but not uncomplicated mTBI, showed a decrease in cortical area, cerebral WM, thalamic and hippocampal volumes over time. Patients with observed LOC had a significant decrease in hippocampal volume between admission and 3 months compared to patients without observed LOC. Cortical thickness remained stable in patients with observed LOC over the 12 months when GM/WM intensity ratio was adjusted for.

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective study of patients with mixed-mechanism mTBI, we found a significant ventricular volume increase over the course of one year, with the primary growth evident within the first 3 months after injury. Furthermore, injury severity factors influenced brain atrophy. In particular, patients with complicated mTBI experienced greater and prolonged ventricular dilation across the entire first year after injury, as well as marked cortical area reductions and decreased hippocampal, thalamic, and WM volumes. Changes in GM/WM intensity ratio affected cortical thickness measures the most.

mTBI and community controls

Our results regarding ventricular enlargement in patients with mTBI are in line with Richter et al. who found a similar result during the first 3 weeks after injury in 81 patients with mixed-mechanism mTBI (Richter et al., 2021). However, unlike Richter et al., we found that ventricular volume differed significantly between patients with mTBI and controls already at admission (i.e., within 72 h after the injury). The larger sample size in our study could have facilitated the unveiling of this difference, but the underlying cause of baseline differences remains inherently uncertain, and we cannot rule out the possibility that these differences reflect pre-injury characteristics. Because baseline differences in ventricular volume could potentially be related to prior mTBIs or alcohol consumption, we conducted a post hoc model controlling for these factors. The group difference in ventricular volume between patients with mTBI and community controls nevertheless remained significant (p = 0.018, Supplementary Table 11). A recent cross-sectional study in the late chronic phase after mTBI (median 37 months post injury) found that around 50% of the patients with mTBI had abnormally large ventricular volume when compared to normative data (Rahmani et al., 2024). Thus, brain structural changes following mTBI appear to converge on affecting the ventricles, which is reasonable given the heterogeneous nature of mTBI. Greater ventricular volumes are considered an indirect measure of parenchymal changes (i.e., larger ventricular volume equals volume loss in other brain structures) (Bigler, 2023) and our findings provide evidence that ventricular enlargement occurs in mTBI, in addition to the well documented ventricular dilation in moderate-to-severe TBI (Ross, 2011, Brezova et al., 2014, Cole et al., 2018). Thus, our results add explanatory value to previous findings of accelerated brain aging following mTBI (Santhanam et al., 2019) and increased risk of dementia after TBI in general (Livingston et al., 2024).

Complicated and uncomplicated mTBI

Compared to the uncomplicated mTBI group, the complicated mTBI group experienced both a greater magnitude and duration of ventricular dilation. Patients with complicated mTBI varied in their intracranial findings (Table 1). These different brain injuries following mTBI appeared to exacerbate the increase in ventricular volume.

Patients with complicated mTBI also showed reductions in frontal and temporal cortical area from admission to 3 months, which was not evidenced in patients with uncomplicated mTBI. Reduced cortical area in patients with complicated mTBI, but no significant changes in cortical thickness, is interesting and in line with cortical thickness and area being independent features of the cortex, genetically uncorrelated and differentially sensitive to pathology (Panizzon et al., 2009, Grasby et al., 2020). In 67 patients with moderate to severe TBI from 5 months to 7 years after the injury, it has been demonstrated that decreased frontal cortical area was accompanied with increased cortical thickness (Mazaharally et al., 2022). A cross-sectional study on 22 patients with mTBI 3–6 months after the injury found the same pattern of smaller cortical area and thicker cortex compared to healthy controls (Bajaj et al., 2018). Although we did not find any changes in frontal or temporal cortical thickness, we did find significantly thicker insular cortex in the complicated group at admission and 3 months, which may be related to the observed cortical area reductions. Other studies find no change in cortical area following mTBI. For example, Dall’Acqua et al. found no changes from 7 days to 12 months in cortical area in 49 patients with mTBI when compared to controls (Dall'Acqua et al., 2017). However, only patients with uncomplicated mTBI were included in their study, which is in line with findings in the present study regarding the lack of cortical area change over time in the uncomplicated mTBI group.

A hippocampal volume reduction in patients with complicated mTBI, but not uncomplicated mTBI, was evident from admission to 3 months. The hippocampus is a vulnerable structure that undergoes changes in a range of disorders, with hippocampal atrophy often documented following moderate to severe TBI (Anand and Dhikav, 2012, Bigler, 2023, Brezova et al., 2014). A longitudinal mTBI study (n = 56) with a high percentage of CT-positive patients (36%) (i.e. complicated mTBI) found a reduction in hippocampal volume over the first months following injury. The controls were however scanned only once, which make the finding somewhat less reliable (Zhuo et al., 2021) Another cross-sectional study on retired Canadian Football League players with a history of multiple concussions found smaller hippocampal volume in players compared to healthy controls (Misquitta et al., 2018). Our finding thus adds to the evidence for accelerated hippocampal atrophy being a consequence of mTBI, especially complicated mTBI.

Patients with complicated mTBI also showed a greater loss of thalamic volume and cerebral WM, while no such change was found in those with uncomplicated mTBI. These findings are, however, not straightforward to interpret, because the complicated group had larger volumes at admission and over time the complicated and uncomplicated group became more similar in their cerebral WM and thalamic volumes (Fig. 4). Thus, the observed change in thalamic and cerebral WM volume could represent (a) a pathological process (the non-significant group difference at admission would then probably represent a preinjury difference); (b) recovery (the non-significant difference at admission would then likely be injury-related); or (c) regression to the mean, without any real biological significance. It is essentially impossible to know which of the alternatives are correct, but the ventricular dilation in the complicated mTBI group supports the presence of pathological processes. Our finding of WM volume loss is partially in line with a previous mTBI study, with a high portion of patients with intracranial lesions on MRI (42%), reporting WM loss from 72 h to 3 weeks after the injury (Richter et al., 2021). Though we were unable to determine a statistically significant volume loss between admission and 3 months in the complicated mTBI group, a relatively large volume reduction was nonetheless evident, and the reduction persisted over time, reaching statistical significance when comparing 12 months to admission. Considering that TAI is the most common intracranial finding in our cohort and that loss of WM integrity has been reported in the same cohort (Stenberg et al., 2023), WM volume reduction is an expected finding. Regarding the observed thalamic volume reduction, WM changes in the thalamic radiation were among the findings previously reported in the cohort (Stenberg et al., 2023), supporting the interpretation of the thalamic volume reduction as being part of a pathological process. Altogether, our results suggest that morphological changes are more pronounced in patients with complicated compared to uncomplicated mTBI in both cortical, hippocampal, and subcortical structures.

Observed LOC and unobserved LOC

Patients with observed LOC exhibited a decrease in hippocampal volume from admission to 3 months, which persisted also at 12 months compared to admission. While no significant group differences were evident at any timepoint, the observed LOC group consistently had larger hippocampal volume, with the difference approaching significance at admission, and smaller differences at 3 and 12 months. The estimated decrease in hippocampal volume from admission to 12 months in patients with observed LOC (75 mm3, Table 4) was actually smaller than the observed decrease in community controls (97 mm3, Supplementary Table 6. Raw means and standard deviations of morphometric structures are shown in Supplementary Tables 6–10). Additionally, the observed interaction effect was partly driven by an unexpected increase in hippocampal volume in patients with unobserved LOC that approached significance. Thus, the reduction in hippocampal volume in patients with LOC is not necessarily related to a pathological process. Further, the volume change does not seem to be driven by an overrepresentation of patients with complicated mTBI in the observed LOC group since a post-hoc chi-square comparison of patients with complicated mTBI by LOC indicated no significant association (χ2 = 0.19, p = 0.514).

Patients with LOC had larger temporal area than patients without observed LOC (but no group by time interaction). Given the significant difference already at the admission timepoint, it is possible that this is a preinjury difference rather than related to a pathological process. Patients with observed LOC had larger values on almost all of the morphometric measures (Supplementary Table 8), and we tentatively speculate that this difference may render them more susceptible to experiencing LOC.

GCS scores

We found no differences in morphometric brain measures between patients with GCS 13–14 and GCS 15. To the best of our knowledge, no previous brain morphology mTBI study has stratified patients on GCS scores and our findings suggest that GCS scores are of little importance for brain structural changes after mTBI. The GCS score is usually assessed at admission. However, the time between injury and admission varies considerably between patients and GCS scores typically increase with time, which calls into question its usefulness as a measure of injury severity in mTBI.

PTA duration

The finding of increased ventricular volume in patients with short (<1h) compared to long PTA was unexpected. PTA is an established injury severity indicator in moderate and severe TBI (Vile et al., 2022), but its role in mTBI is more uncertain. This is likely related to difficulties with reliable estimation of PTA duration in mTBI. In moderate-to-severe TBI, PTA can be roughly estimated in days or even weeks and still have clinical implications (Vile et al., 2022), while in mTBI, PTA must usually be estimated in hours, as if the PTA lasts longer than 24 h, the TBI is by definition no longer a mTBI. This fine-grained estimation is not always possible, nor reliable, especially not in patients influenced by alcohol. Thus, PTA duration may in practice be a less useful marker of injury severity in mTBI compared to in moderate-severe TBI.

The timeline of changes in brain morphology

The morphological changes detected in our study were mainly found between admission and 3 months, with changes remaining at 12 months compared to admission, indicating prolonged and lasting changes. Many previous mTBI studies have performed initial assessments relatively early after the injury and performed a follow-up assessment around one-year post-injury (Hellstrøm et al., 2017, Zagorchev et al., 2016, Muller et al., 2021, Dall'Acqua et al., 2017), making it impossible to conclude when during the first year the changes occur. Our findings suggest that brain structural changes mainly take place within the first few months after the injury.

Future studies should aim for a more granulated scanning period in the acute and subacute phase of mTBI, to pinpoint when exactly over the first 3 months changes occur. Scanning timepoints beyond 12 months would also be pertinent to determine whether patients with, especially complicated mTBI, show evidence of long-term accelerated brain aging or return to following the trajectories of people without mTBI.

The effect of GM/WM intensity ratio

GM/WM intensity ratio was significantly associated with cortical thickness and area in all areas assessed expect in the cingulate and occipital lobe area. Although GM/WM intensity ratio has been suggested to influence results in previous brain morphology studies on mTBI (Mills et al., 2020), to the authors’ knowledge, no prior study has included this ratio as a covariate and/or presented results with and without this covariate, which is somewhat surprising considering that GM/WM has been shown useful in other contexts (Salat et al., 2011, Vidal-Piñeiro et al., 2016, Xie et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, for most models in the present study, omitting GM/WM intensity ratio did not significantly alter the results (Supplementary Tables 2–5). However, in LOC comparisons, we found no significant changes in cortical thickness when GM/WM intensity ratio was adjusted for, but a decrease in frontal and parietal lobe cortical thickness from admission to 3 months in patients without LOC when this covariate was not adjusted for. The literature on cortical thickness is highly heterogenous, with some studies reporting both cortical thickening and thinning (Muller et al., 2021, Dall'Acqua et al., 2017, Govindarajan et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2015). Importantly, none of the previous studies adjusted for GM/WM intensity ratio. Because cortical thickness was related to the GM/WM intensity ratio, our results suggest that the cortex is not changing in thickness per se (in vivo), rather the apparent boundary between grey and WM changes, possibly due to loss of myelination (Koenig, 1991).

Strengths and limitations

Unlike previous studies, the present study included a large sample of patients with mTBI who underwent MRI at three distinct timepoints over the first year following the injury. The control group was also scanned on three occasions, with the same time intervals, enabling us to conduct appropriate statistical comparisons (i.e. linear mixed models with group by time interaction terms). Recruiting from both a level 1 emergency department and a general practitioner-run clinic led to a more representative sample of patients with mTBI than samples recruiting exclusively from level 1 emergency departments or only athletes. Furthermore, we used the longitudinal pipeline in FreeSurfer, which has shown good reliability (Hedges et al., 2022). Surface-based morphometry is also reliable and superior to voxel-based methods in detecting group differences that are subtle and spatially complex (Davatzikos, 2004, Cardinale et al., 2014). There are, however, several limitations to our study. Firstly, only 12% of patients exhibited intracranial findings on MRI, therefore determined effects are based on small subsets of patients in these sub-group analyses. However, we consider this a consequence of recruiting a more representative sample from both the emergency department and ambulatory clinics. Secondly, we cannot exclude morphological changes outside the areas chosen for analyses in the present study. However, the areas analyzed correspond to areas most likely affected after mTBI from a biomechanical perspective (Bigler, 2023). Thirdly, estimating presence of LOC and duration of PTA is challenging. We decided to classify LOC as present if it was observed (since LOC and PTA can be difficult to distinguish in retrospect for the patient themselves). Consequently, a significant number of patients in the unobserved LOC group may have experienced LOC. Finally, participants were not assessed in a structured manner regarding new-onset disorders or injuries during the follow-up period. This was partly because it would have been challenging to distinguish mTBI-related symptoms (e.g., post-concussion symptoms) from new conditions (e.g., depression). However, if a new brain injury (such as a complicated mTBI or stroke) or a suspected neurological disease was detected on follow-up MRI, the participant would have been excluded. We consider the risk of additional uncomplicated mTBI during the follow-up period to be low, as none of the participants engaged in collision sports.

Conclusions

In this large prospective study, with three MRI assessments from admission to 12 months in both patients with mTBI and community controls, we found increased ventricular volume during the first year following the injury. The ventricular enlargement was particularly pronounced during the first 3 months after the mTBI and persisted at 12 months. Ventricular enlargement was even more pronounced in patients with complicated mTBI, who also showed significant reductions in cortical area, hippocampal and subcortical volumes. Changes in GM/WM intensity ratio affected cortical thickness measures and should, together with injury severity factors, be accounted for in future mTBI studies.
