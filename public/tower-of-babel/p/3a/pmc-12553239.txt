Meta-analysis of the efficacy of proximal fibular osteotomy versus unicompartmental knee arthroplasty in the treatment of medial compartment knee osteoarthritis in Chinese patients
Authors: Zhen-yan Dai, Bo Xie, Xi-yong Li, Cun-xian Lv, Yong-le Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study comprehensively and methodically assessed the effectiveness of proximal fibular osteotomy (PFO) compared to unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) as treatment for medial compartment knee osteoarthritis (KOA) to offer direction and evidence to support clinical surgical decision-making.MethodsLiterature screening was strictly conducted according to the inclusion criteria, and reasonable outcome indicators were selected from the included studies. The PubMed, Web of Science, Wanfang Data, CQVIP, and CNKI databases were searched using a predefined search strategy. Quality assessment was stratified by study design: the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for non-RCTs. After extracting relevant data from the included studies, a meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 software.ResultsThirteen studies involving 698 patients were included, with 355 and 343 patients in the PFO and UKA groups, respectively. The meta-analysis revealed that the PFO group had a shorter surgical duration, less intraoperative blood loss, shorter hospital stay, and lower hospitalization costs than the UKA group. Nevertheless, no statistically significant differences in the postoperative visual analog scale scores (VAS), Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) scores, knee range of motion (ROM), Knee Society Score (KSS), femorotibial angle, and incidence of postoperative complications were observed between the PFO and UKA groups.ConclusionsPFO and UKA provide comparable short-term functional outcomes for medial compartment KOA, with PFO offering advantages in surgical efficiency and cost. However, given the high heterogeneity and limited long-term data, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and further high-quality studies are needed to confirm the durability and broader applicability of PFO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03262-4.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Knee-preserving treatment, Proximal fibular osteotomy, Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, Meta-analysis

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative and chronic joint disease that leads to structural alterations in joint tissues, primarily damaging the articular cartilage and causing clinical symptoms such as pain, swelling, and stiffness around the joints [1, 2]. The knee joint, the largest synovial joint in humans, generates synovial fluid to lubricate and nourish the articular cartilage despite its frequent use and high stress, making it a common site for OA development [3]. Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) can affect all knee joint compartments; however, involvement of the medial compartment is more prevalent [4]. Compared with total knee arthroplasty (TKA), unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) offers advantages such as less surgical trauma and bone resection, preservation of the cruciate ligaments, faster postoperative recovery, and better postoperative functional scores [5, 6]. In addition to knee replacement, peripheral osteotomies based on knee preservation are therapeutic options for medial compartment KOA [7] These procedures correct lower-limb alignment through osteotomies without compromising the normal structure of the knee joint, thereby achieving good knee function [7, 8]. Common knee osteotomies include high tibial osteotomy (HTO) and proximal fibular osteotomy (PFO) [9]. While both HTO and UKA are effective surgical methods for treating medial compartment KOA, patients undergoing either procedure have been reported to exhibit comparable joint function at one year postoperatively [10, 11]. PFO disrupts the fibula and weakens its support for the lateral tibial plateau, laterally shifting the weight-bearing line of the knee joint and ultimately reducing the pressure on the medial compartment of the knee joint [12]. Moreover, the minimal trauma associated with PFO allows patients to undergo early postoperative rehabilitation and weight-bearing exercises, leading to better postoperative outcomes [7, 13]. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate and compare the effectiveness and safety of PFO and UKA in treating patients with medial compartment KOA, offering evidence-based medical insights to guide the selection of surgical interventions for medial compartment KOA.

Methods

Search strategy

This study strictly followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14]. The search strategy of this study integrates core terms related to proximal fibular osteotomy, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, and knee osteoarthritis, and conducts literature retrieval in databases such as PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI through logical relationship combinations (S1). The search period was from the establishment of the electronic databases until January 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Study participants: Patients diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis who underwent either proximal fibular osteotomy or unicompartmental knee arthroplasty; (2) Study design: Prospective cohort studies, retrospective cohort studies, and case-control studies; (3) Data requirement: Studies providing complete data on outcome indicators; (4) Language requirement: Studies published in Chinese or English.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies categorized as systematic reviews, summaries, case reports, or letters; (2) publications that were duplicated or redundantly reported; and (3) studies focusing on alternative surgical interventions for medial KOA diagnosis; (4) Studies must enroll Chinese patients and be conducted in China.

The outcome measures were as follows: (1) operative time, (2) intraoperative blood loss, (3) hospital stay, (4) hospitalization costs, (5) postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) scores [15], (6) Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) scores [16], (7) postoperative range of motion (ROM), (8) Knee Society Score (KSS) [17], (9) femorotibial angle, and (10) complications.

Data extraction and quality evaluation

Two researchers (BX and ZYD) independently extracted key details following the established search strategy, including the first author of each article, publication year, country where the study was conducted, sex of the study subjects, number of cases and controls in the study groups, and the researchers’ conclusions. For quality and bias assessment, differentiated tools were applied based on study design: The randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool, focusing on five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. The remaining non-RCTs were assessed using both the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [18] and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. The NOS evaluates nine items across three domains: selection [19], comparability, and outcome/exposure assessment. Meanwhile, the ROBINS-I tool was applied for bias risk assessment, covering seven domains: confounding bias, selection bias, bias in classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in measurement of outcomes, and bias in selection of the reported result. Two independent reviewers (BX and ZYD) performed all assessments, with disagreements resolved via consensus with a third reviewer (XYL).

Statistical methods

A meta-analysis was conducted on the collected data using RevMan 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration) software. For dichotomous variables, the odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated as effect measures, whereas for continuous variables, the mean difference and its CI were calculated as effect measures. To evaluate the heterogeneity among the studies, a chi-squared test was performed. Specifically, when the I2 value was 50% or higher and the P-value was less than 0.1, significant heterogeneity was deemed present, prompting the use of a random-effects model for analysis. Conversely, if the I2 value was below 50% and the P-value was 0.1 or greater, heterogeneity was considered low, leading to the adoption of a fixed-effects model for analysis [20]. In case of high heterogeneity, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the stability of the results. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Publication bias assessment

For visual assessment, funnel plots were generated using RevMan 5.4 software, and publication bias was judged by observing the symmetry of effect size scatter distribution: symmetric distribution of scatter points around the mean effect size line indicates a low risk of publication bias, while asymmetric distribution suggests the possible existence of publication bias.

Results

Study selection

A total of 556 relevant articles were identified based on the search strategy. The reduction of 556 initially retrieved articles to 33 potentially relevant ones was conducted via a two-stage independent screening process: the first stage was “initial screening of titles and abstracts”, where researchers BX and ZYD independently reviewed all articles. Based on the inclusion criteria (including study type as clinical comparative studies, core intervention measures as PFO vs. UKA), 523 ineligible articles were excluded (comprising 6 case reports, 178 review articles, and 339 non-case-control studies without comparison of core interventions). In case of discrepancies during the initial screening, the two researchers first conducted joint review and consultation; if a consensus was still not reached, researcher XYL intervened to make a final decision. Eventually, 33 articles were determined to enter the second stage of “full-text re-screening”. After a thorough review of the full texts of these 33 articles, an additional 20 articles were excluded (six due to incomplete data, five due to irrelevant surgical procedures, four contained overlapping patient cohorts, three lacked a control group and two failed to confirm medial compartment KOA diagnosis via radiography). Ultimately, 13 clinical comparative studies were included [21–33]. The literature screening process and the final results are presented in Fig. 1.

Meta-analysis results

Operative time

This study included 13 comparisons [21–33] of operative duration between the PFO and UKA procedures involving 698 patients. Among them, 355 and 343 patients were included in the PFO and UKA groups, respectively. The outcome of the heterogeneity test revealed an I2 value of 99% and a P-value of < 0.001, suggesting the presence of substantial heterogeneity. Consequently, a random-effects model was used for the subsequent analyses. The results demonstrated that the PFO group had a significant advantage over the UKA group in terms of operative duration, with a statistically significant difference (95% CI: −65.74, −43.29; P < 0.001). The detailed findings are presented in Fig. 2. Subgroup analysis was performed on the 13 included studies based on whether they were RCTs. The results showed that in both subgroups, the operative time in the PFO group was shorter than that in the UKA group. Specifically, the I2 value was 66% in the RCT subgroup and 98% in the non-RCT subgroup, suggesting that the study design may be a source of heterogeneity. The detailed results are shown in Fig. 3.

Intraoperative blood loss

This study included ten comparisons [22, 24–26, 28–33] of intraoperative blood loss between PFO and UKA surgeries in 565 patients. Among them, 283 and 282 patients were included in the PFO and UKA groups, respectively. The heterogeneity test results indicated significant heterogeneity (I2 = 100%, P < 0.001); thus, a random-effects model was used for the analysis. The results demonstrated a statistically significant difference in intraoperative blood loss between PFO and UKA (95% CI: −103.43, −35.79; P < 0.001). The results revealed that PFO was associated with a lower volume of intraoperative blood loss than UKA. The detailed findings are presented in Fig. 4. Similarly, subgroup analysis was conducted on the ten included studies based on whether they were RCTs. The I² value was 100% in the RCT subgroup and 96% in the non-RCT subgroup, suggesting that study design is not a source of heterogeneity. Potential sources of heterogeneity may include other factors such as differences in patient age. Detailed results are shown in Fig. 5.

Hospital stay

This study encompassed 12 separate evaluations [21–23, 25–33] comparing the length of hospital stay between PFO and UKA procedures in 638 patients. Specifically, 325 patients underwent PFO and 313 underwent UKA. The heterogeneity test results indicated significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98%, P < 0.001), prompting the use of a random-effects model for analysis. The results demonstrated a statistically significant difference in the length of hospital stay between the two groups (95% CI: −7.37, −3.89; P < 0.001). In particular, the PFO group had a shorter length of hospitalization. The detailed results are presented in. Figure 6. Similarly, subgroup analysis was performed on the 12 included studies grouped by whether they were RCTs. The results showed that the I² value was 22% in the RCT subgroup and 98% in the non-RCT subgroup, suggesting that the study design may be a source of heterogeneity. Hospitalization costs

This study comprised ten distinct analyses [22–25, 27, 28, 30–33] examining the hospitalization costs associated with the PFO and UKA procedures, encompassing a total of 495 patients. Of them, 254 and 241 were assigned to the PFO and UKA groups, respectively. The heterogeneity test results indicated significant heterogeneity (I2 = 100%, P < 0.001), prompting the use of a random-effects model for analysis. The results demonstrated a statistically significant difference in hospitalization costs between PFO and UKA (95% CI: −3.52, −2.30; P < 0.001). Specifically, hospitalization costs for PFO were significantly lower than those for UKA. The detailed results are presented in Fig. 8. This research encompassed ten separate evaluations [21–24, 27–32] comparing postoperative VAS scores between PFO and UKA procedures, with a total of 520 patients included. Five research articles [21, 26–28, 32] focused on postoperative complications in the comparative analyses of the PFO and UKA procedures. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were also performed using RevMan 5.4 to verify the stability of meta-analysis results. For outcomes with high heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50%), we sequentially excluded each study and recalculated the pooled effect size. For example, in the analysis of postoperative complications, excluding the study by Liu et al. [21] reduced heterogeneity from I² = 55% to I² = 0% (P = 0.86), confirming the robustness of the non-significant difference between groups. Similar analyses for other outcomes (e.g., operative time, intraoperative blood loss) showed no substantial changes in pooled effect directions, indicating stable results. Subgroup analyses were performed for operative time, intraoperative blood loss, hospital stay, hospitalization costs, postoperative VAS scores, and HSS scores to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis stratified by follow-up duration revealed distinct temporal associations between VAS scores (a measure of pain relief) and HSS scores (a measure of knee function): Specifically, the advantage of PFO in VAS scores exhibited significant temporal specificity—only in short-term follow-up (< 12 months) were PFO’s VAS scores significantly lower than those of UKA, indicating better pain relief; in long-term follow-up (≥ 12 months), however, there was no statistically significant difference in VAS scores between PFO and UKA, and the pain relief effects of the two procedures tended to be consistent; regarding HSS scores, no significant difference was observed between the two groups across different follow-up time periods. The high heterogeneity (I² = 98%–100%) observed in key outcomes (operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and hospitalization costs) is also associated with surgical techniques (variations in PFO incision size: 2–4 cm; differences in UKA positioning methods: manual vs. computer-assisted), surgeon experience (inferred from the research institutions, with differences in specialized experience: ≥5 years vs. <5 years), KOA severity, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols (variations in early weight-bearing timing: postoperative day 1 vs. day 3).

Publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots generated via RevMan 5.4 software [34], which visually assess the symmetry of effect size distribution across included studies. Funnel plots for key outcome indicators (operative time, intraoperative blood loss, hospital stay, hospitalization costs, postoperative VAS scores, etc.) showed symmetrical scatter points on both sides of the mean effect line (Fig. 18), suggesting no significant publication bias. However, for outcomes with fewer included studies (e.g., femorotibial angle, complications), the reliability of funnel plot results may be limited due to insufficient statistical power, which should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

OA is a common musculoskeletal disease and is currently one of the major causes of disability [2]. Studies have found that the KOA population accounts for approximately 16% of the world’s population, with women comprising the majority [35]. Patients with KOA can undergo surgery to reduce pain, minimize disability, and improve quality of life. The specific surgical approach varies depending on the patient’s functional status, disease severity, and underlying conditions [1, 7]. TKA is a highly effective option for the treatment of advanced KOA as it effectively reduces pain and improves knee function [36, 37]. However, TKA not only disrupts the other normal compartments of the knee joint but also requires resection of the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments in most TKA prostheses, resulting in a decrease in proprioception in patients [38, 39]. UKA is an alternative to TKA that preserves the bone and ligaments and is suitable for patients with medial compartment KOA [40]. Compared with TKA, UKA involves less intraoperative trauma and results in stronger postoperative knee function [5]. It also preserves more bone mass for revision surgery, and the intact anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments and partial meniscus allow patients to retain a better subjective sensations [41]. In recent years, the “knee-preserving concept” has gradually gained attention, and in addition to UKA, the application of osteotomies in the treatment of medial compartment KOA has also become increasingly common These osteotomies primarily included HTO and PFO [7]. PFO is a simple and less invasive procedure based on the “uneven settlement theory of the knee joint [7].” By disrupting the fibula and weakening its support for the lateral tibial plateau, tension is transferred to the lateral femoral condyle during weight bearing, reducing the lateral gap of the knee joint, and correcting the force line [42, 43]. Some studies have also found that PFO achieves favorable postoperative outcomes over a follow-up period of up to 27.5 months after medial KOA treatment, demonstrating that PFO is a reliable long-term outcome indicator for the treatment of medial KOA [44]. In another study, four cases were reported where patients underwent conversion to TKA due to residual pain and unsatisfactory function after PFO, indicating that TKA remains a viable option following the failure of PFO surgery [45]. Although PFO is a new concept in the field of knee-preserving osteotomies, studies have shown that it has comparable short-term efficacy to HTO in the treatment of medial compartment KOA [9]. Notably, PFO also offers advantages, such as shorter operative time, shorter hospital stay, less intraoperative blood loss, and a lower incidence of postoperative complications [9]. This study is the first to use a meta-analysis approach to compare the clinical efficacy and safety of PFO and UKA in the treatment of medial compartment KOA, with the aim of providing a reference for the selection of clinical treatment strategies. In this study, some outcome indicators (such as postoperative HSS scores and operation time) showed high heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50%), which suggests that the differences in effects observed may not be universally applicable. Although this study confirms that PFO has statistically significant advantages in terms of surgical trauma (including operation time and intraoperative blood loss) and treatment cost—though the magnitude of these advantages may vary due to the high heterogeneity observed in key outcomes (e.g., operative time, intraoperative blood loss, I²=98%~100%)—and its short-term functional effects are comparable to those of UKA, it is not widely used in clinical practice. This may be related to two factors: first, the long-term efficacy of PFO is still unclear; second, for severe KOA, simple osteotomy is difficult to effectively correct the lower extremity biomechanical alignment. In terms of long-term survival rate, the aggregated data from the currently referenced documents shows that the failure rate (revision rate) of UKA in follow-ups of more than five years is 3.2%, which is significantly lower than that of PFO (8.7%) [46]. However, it should be noted that PFO has an obvious feasibility advantage when converted to TKA after failure: due to the smaller surgical trauma of PFO, which preserves the soft tissues around the joint and bone mass, the surgical difficulty and complication risk of performing TKA after PFO failure are significantly lower than those of UKA revision [30].

This meta-analysis included 13 studies and found a statistically significant difference in operative time between the PFO and UKA groups, with PFO having a significantly shorter operative time than UKA. This is mainly related to the surgical approach of PFO, as it requires only a small incision to disrupt the fibula support for the lateral plateau and does not require internal fixation [47]. Although UKA has certain advantages in terms of operative compared with TKA, it still requires appropriate osteotomy and prosthesis placement, thus having a disadvantage in terms of operative time compared with PFO [48]. In terms of intraoperative blood loss, this analysis included ten studies and found that in addition to operative time, PFO had a significant advantage over UKA in terms of intraoperative blood loss. The PFO procedure is simple and straightforward [9]. Although it also involves osteotomy, it does not involve the handling of the complex internal structures of the knee joint [49]. In contrast, UKA requires more delicate manipulation to ensure the accuracy and fit of the replaced part, including the processing of cartilage, ligaments, and other structures, which increases the complexity and operating time of the surgery and may thus increase the risk of intraoperative bleeding [13]. Similarly, PFO has advantages in terms of hospital stay and costs [50]. For example, HTO requires patients to spend approximately two weeks before they can fully bear weight and walk [33]. In both UKA and TKA, while patients may attempt weight-bearing as early as the second postoperative day, achieving full weight-bearing and complete rehabilitation typically requires a period of three to six weeks [51]. However, patients undergoing PFO surgery achieved full weight-bearing walking with minimal assistance on the second postoperative day [43]. This is consistent with the results of our current analysis, which attributed the faster recovery of PFO to its minimal damage to the surrounding soft tissues and weight-bearing tibia. The results showed that the PFO technology could effectively release the proximal fibula from the constraints of the tibiofibular syndesmosis and distal fibula, thereby significantly improving the ROM of the proximal tibiofibular joint and providing a wider ROM for the fibular head. Notably, increased mobility of the fibular head plays a significant role in correcting varus deformities, relieving medial compartment pressure, and significantly improving clinical pain symptoms [52]. In this analysis, we found no statistically significant differences between the PFO and UKA groups in terms of the postoperative VAS scores, HSS scores, KSS, and knee ROM. In the subgroup analysis, PFO has an advantage in VAS scores in the short term, but this advantage in VAS scores disappears in the long term. This suggests that the stability of PFO’s pain relief requires verification through follow-up of ≥ 24 months, and also reflects the durability of UKA in long-term pain control. Studies have found that patients with more severe narrowing of the medial joint space show more significant clinical improvement after PFO treatment, which manifests as improved limb alignment and hip-knee-ankle angles [53]. Compared to UKA, PFO has comparable effects on the femorotibial angle; therefore, there is little difference in postoperative function. Another study showed that for each additional year after PFO surgery, the odds of functional satisfaction increased by 7.2% [53]. On the one hand, postoperative complications of UKA include progression of arthritis, aseptic loosening, bearing dislocation, and periprosthetic fractures [54]. On the other hand, the most commonly reported complications of PFO are related to injuries to the peroneal nerve and its branches because it is close to the fibula and the dissection process surrounds the fibula [44]. However, this analysis found no statistically significant differences in postoperative complications between PFO and UKA.

This study exclusively included Chinese patients, which may be associated with specific epidemiological characteristics of KOA in the Chinese population and standardized surgical practices in China. Due to potential differences in anatomical features, clinical management, and patient demographics across regions, the generalizability of our findings to non-Chinese populations should be interpreted with caution. The high heterogeneity (I² = 98%–100%) in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and hospitalization costs in this study is jointly caused by study design (randomized controlled trials [RCTs] vs. non-RCTs) and clinical factors. On one hand, the standardized procedures in RCTs (e.g., unified requirement of surgeon experience ≥ 5 years) lead to lower subgroup heterogeneity (I² = 66%) compared with non-RCTs (I² = 98%). On the other hand, variations in surgical techniques (PFO incision size, UKA prosthesis positioning methods), KOA severity (proportion of Kellgren-Lawrence [K-L] grades II–IV), and postoperative rehabilitation protocols (early weight-bearing timing) further amplify outcome variability. For instance, in patients with K-L grade IV KOA, UKA requires additional management of intra-articular osteophytes, resulting in an average increase of 20–30 ml in intraoperative blood loss and a 15–20-minute prolongation in operative time compared with patients with K-L grade II KOA; this explains why the blood loss difference between UKA and PFO is more significant in some studies. Clinically, surgical methods should be selected based on individual patient conditions: for K-L grades II–III KOA and in institutions with limited surgeon experience, PFO is more likely to achieve stable surgical efficiency and cost advantages due to its simpler technique (small incision, no need for prosthesis positioning). For K-L grade IV patients, the surgical trauma of UKA and its long-term efficacy need to be further weighed. Future studies with multi-center international collaboration are warranted to validate the universality of these results.

Limitations

Several potential limitations exist in this meta-analysis: (1) the majority of the included studies were retrospective case-control studies, with only four RCTs and one prospective study, potentially influencing the research outcomes; (2) certain effect indicators were based on a limited number of studies, leading to a somewhat weaker evidence base; and (3) considerable heterogeneity was observed in the analysis results for individual effect indicators. In future research, it is advisable to incorporate a broader range of effect indicators, larger sample sizes, studies from diverse countries, and an increased number of RCTs to strengthen the evidence supporting these findings.

Conclusion

PFO and UKA can provide comparable short-term functional outcomes in the treatment of medial compartment KOA, with PFO demonstrating advantages in surgical efficiency and cost. However, given the high heterogeneity among the included studies and the limited long-term follow-up data, the above conclusions should be interpreted with caution; more high-quality studies are still needed in the future to verify the durability of PFO’s efficacy and its broader applicability.
