Analysis of FNDC1 as a diagnostic marker and potential therapeutic target for ovarian serous cancer
Authors: Hongying Jiao, Jing Tian, Qiao Liu, Meng Jiang, Xia Niu, Wei Zhang, Li Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibronectin type III domain containing 1 (FNDC1) is a member of fibronectin type III domain protein family, which is known to play an important role in the metastasis of some cancers. However, it has not been reported about the relationship between FNDC1 and ovarian serous cancer.MethodsFirstly, we investigated the expression of FNDC1 in various cancer types, and analyzed its diagnostic value and potential role in ovarian serous cancer using TCGA database. Subsequently, a series of bioinformatics methods were used to explore the potential oncogenic effects of FNDC1, including the relationship between FNDC1 expression and immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint molecules, protein–protein interaction network, gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis, gene set enrichment analysis, and toxicity analysis. Finally, the results were further verified by cell function experiments.ResultsFNDC1 was highly expressed in ovarian serous cancer, which was further verified in cell line, indicating that it might be as a diagnostic marker of ovarian serous cancer. In addition, the expression of FNDC1 and TNFSF4, an immune checkpoint molecule, showed a strong positive correlation, and both them were involved in the same signaling pathway, indicating that they might jointly affect T-cell responses in ovarian serous cancer. Moreover, this effect might be inhibited by austocystin D.ConclusionFNDC1 was a potential diagnostic marker, prognostic indicator, and target for the treatment of ovarian serous cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14924-0.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is one of the deadliest cancer in women. By 2050, about 12 million people will be diagnosed with ovarian cancer, and a total of 8 million women will die of the disease [1]. The most common treatment of ovarian cancer is based on a combination of cytoreductive surgery and chemotherapy. Platinum chemotherapy combined with paclitaxel is the gold-standard first-line chemotherapy scheme for ovarian cancer. However, approximately 70% of ovarian cancer patients relapse within the first 18 months after the first treatment [2]. At present, the main treatments for recurrent patients include chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) [3]. However, in order to improve the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy, ICI and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies must overcome the immunosuppressive factors within tumor microenvironment [3, 4]. In addition, the preventive resection of fallopian tubes can reduce the incidence of ovarian cancer to some extent, but it is still challenging to choose an appropriate time to remove them [5]. Epithelial ovarian cancer accounts for more than 90% of ovarian cancer patients [6], while ovarian serous carcinoma accounts for 70% of epithelial ovarian cancer [7]. Therefore, in this study, we mainly focused on ovarian serous cancer to develop more effective prognostic monitoring indicators, which is critical for improving treatment outcomes and reducing treatment side effects and mortality.

FNDC1 contains a conserved fibronectin type III (FN III) domain [8]. Upon mechanical stretching, the unfolded intermediate of this domain can induce fibril formation [9]. The FNDC1 alias AGS8, involved in VEGF-mediated signal processing during angiogenesis [10], has been reported to be associated with immune infiltration and therapeutic sensitivity in the hypoxic environment [11, 12]. In addition, it is associated with immunity, inflammation, coagulation hemostasis, oxidative antioxidation, and lipid metabolic transport [13].

It is reported that FNDC1 is highly expressed in various tumors, which is associated with poor prognosis and plays an important role in the development of human cancers. For example, the high expression of FNDC1 in gastric cancer promotes the invasion and peritoneal metastasis of cancer cells, which is related to poor prognosis [14]. Similarly, FNDC1 protein is also highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma, and closely associated with its occurrence, progression, and prognosis [15]. In prostate cancer, the high expression of FNDC1 promotes cancer proliferation and migration, and inhibits apoptosis [16, 17]. In breast cancer tissues, the silencing of FNDC1 inhibits the occurrence of cancer cells by regulating PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [18]. In a word, the high expression of FNDC1 is associated with the invasion, metastasis, and poor prognosis of many cancers.

Currently, FNDC1 has been documented as a prognostic biomarker in cancer. Moreover, some studies have demonstrated that it may become a biomarker to predict the sensitivity to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and therapeutic target in colorectal cancer in the future [19]. Similarly, FNDC1 may also be used as an indicator to assess survival and prognosis in patients with lung adenocarcinoma [15]. In addition, it is reported that targeting small RNA is a strategy to intervene the role of FNDC1 in cancer [16]. Directly targeting the fibronectin III domain of FNDC1 can alter the formation of fibrin necessary for cancer metastasis [9]. Real-time immunotherapy can also be carried out by modeling the fibronectin III domain as an immunoglobulin constant region [8]. Therefore, FNDC1 shows the prospect as a target for cancer therapy. However, the molecular mechanisms of FNDC1 affecting ovarian serous cancer have not been reported.

In this study, we first downloaded FNDC1 data for tumor and normal samples of various cancers from TCGA database, and elucidated its differential expression and diagnostic value. Then, the value of FNDC1 in ovarian serous cancer was analyzed emphatically, and further verified by our clinical samples. The results demonstrated that FNDC1 could be used as an indicator for prognosis monitoring and therapeutic target, which provided a theoretical basis for individualized immunotherapy of ovarian serous cancer.

Materials and methods

Analysis of FNDC1 expression

Firstly, the expression data of FNDC1 was downloaded in 33 cancer types using the R package TCGA biolinks [20] (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Then, group comparison and radar plots were constructed to compare the expression levels of FNDC1 in the above tumor and normal samples. Finally, the comparison results were evaluated using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2) database [21] (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn).

Univariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

In the TCGA database, a total of 31 cancer types with normal samples as control were used to evaluate the diagnostic value of FNDC1 by logistic regression analysis. Simultaneously, the logistic regression model of pan-cancer was constructed, and the grouped expression of FNDC1 was displayed in each cancer type by a forest plot. To further explore the expression difference of FNDC1 between OV and normal tissues, the R package pROC was used to construct the ROC and calculate the area under the curve (AUC). In addition, the paired tumors and adjacent normal tissues of 30 patients with ovarian serous cancer were analyzed by RT-PCR to detect the expression of FNDC1.

CIBERSORT immune infiltration analysis

In Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA (CIBERSORT) [22], linear support vector regression was used to deconvolute the transcriptome expression matrix and estimate the composition and abundance of immune cells in a mixture of cells. In this study, CIBERSORT algorithm was combined with LM22 characteristic gene matrix. After filtering out the data with immune cell enrichment scores greater than zero, we obtained the immune infiltration matrix of 33 TCGA cancer types. Subsequently, the CIBERSORT correlation heat map of pan-cancer was constructed to visualize the association between immune cells and FNDC1 in them. A correlation scatter plot was also constructed to show the relationship between FNDC1 and the abundance of infiltrating immune cells significantly in OV.

Immune checkpoint gene (ICG) analysis

ICG refers to a ligand-receptor pair that inhibits or stimulates immune response, and plays an important role in maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing autoimmunity. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has shown significant clinical benefits against several solid malignancies. Thus, 47 ICGs were firstly obtained from the literature [23] via the PubMed database (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Then, the correlation of ICG and FNDC1 with 33 TCGA cancer types and their expression in pan-cancer were presented by a heat map and scatter plot, respectively.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

PPI refers to proteins that interact with each other and participate in biological processes, such as signal transmission, gene expression regulation, energy and substance metabolism, cell cycle regulation, etc. The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database [24] (https://string-db.org/) is a database of interactions between known and predicted proteins. Moreover, in this PPI network visualization, the weight of each edge directly represented the interaction confidence score calculated by STRING. Although low-confidence edges had been filtered out by applying a threshold (minimum interaction score of 0.400 and a maximum of 50) in the analysis process. However, edge thickness in the default visualization was not scaled according to interaction confidence scores. Interacting genes were selected in the PPI network for subsequent analysis.

The GeneMANIA database [25] (https://genemania.org/) can be used to generate hypotheses about gene function, analyze gene function, and determine gene priority. Simultaneously, it can not only find functionally similar genes using a large set of genomics and proteomics data, but also predict gene function. The PPI interaction network was constructed by predicting the functionally similar genes of FNDC1 via the online GeneMANIA tool. The GeneMANIA visualization scheme represents interaction types through color coding, whereas edge width remains uniform and unweighted.

Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (GO KEGG) analysis

GO [26] analysis is a common method for large-scale studies of functional enrichment, including biological process (BP), cell component (CC), and molecular function (MF). KEGG [27] is a widely used database containing information on genomes, biological pathways, diseases, and drugs. We used R package clusterProfiler [28] to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of FNDC1 gene and interacting genes. The entry screening criteria were as follows: adjusted p < 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. The Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method was used to correct multiple comparisons.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA [29] was used to evaluate the distribution trend of genes in a predefined gene set in a gene table, which was ranked by the correlation with phenotype, thereby determining their contribution to the phenotype. The samples in ovarian serous cancer dataset (TCGA-OV) were divided into FNDC1 high expression group and low expression group according to the median expression level of FNDC1. R package limma was used for differential analysis. The genes of OV samples were classified according to the logFC value, and GSEA of all genes was performed by R package clusterProfiler. GSEA parameters were as follows: the random seed was 2020; the minimum number of genes contained in each gene set was 10; and the maximum number of genes contained in each gene set was 500. We used Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) access to c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt [All Canonical Pathways] (3050) gene set to perform GSEA. The screening criteria for GSEA were as follows: adjusted p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25. The BH method was used for correcting multiple comparisons.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Alterations of cancer genome strongly influence the clinical response to therapy, and in many cases, they are effective biomarkers for drug therapy response. The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database [30] (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) is the largest public database of molecular markers of cancer drug sensitivity and drug reaction. The Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) database [31] (http://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp/) contains genetic, lineage, and other characteristics of cancer cell lines related to the sensitivity of small molecules. These databases help to accelerate the identification of cancer treatments suitable for individual patients. Based on the expression of FNDC1 and the drug data in GDSC and CTRP databases, the drug sensitivity of FNDC1 was analyzed.

Expression of FNDC1 in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue (FFPE)

Firstly, RNA was extracted from FFPE samples with ovarian serous cancer and normal control (DP439, TIANGEN, China), and the expression of FNDC1 was detected by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) (FP217, KR116, TIANGEN, China) with specific primers (Table S3). The relative expression level was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Cell culture

IOSE80 (BNCC358126; BNCC, Henan, China), A2780 (BNCC351906; BNCC, Henan, China), SKOV3 (CL-0215; Pricella, Wuhan, China), and 293 T (BNCC353535; Henan, China) cells cultured in complete medium containing basal medium, 10% FBS (Cell Box), and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin) were placed in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Among them, DMEM basal medium was used for 293 T cells, RPMI-1640 basal medium was used for IOSE80 and A2780 cells, and McCoy’s 5 A basal medium was used for SKOV3 cells.

Construction of stable cell lines

The four target sequences (Table S3) designed for silencing different sites of FNDC1 were ligated into the pLKO.1 vector using AgeI and EcoRI non-restriction enzymes, then transformed into DH5α competent cells (B528413 Sangon Biotech) and plated for culture. A single clone was selected for amplification and culture, then the plasmid was extracted (DP118, TIANGEN, China) and sequenced for verification.

Lentivirus packaging was performed using 293 T cells when the cells were in a healthy growth state with a density of approximately 70%. 4 h before packaging, the culture medium was replaced with basic DMEM and 10% FBS; 1 h before packaging, the medium was replaced with basic DMEM. Transfection complexes were prepared using two sterile 1.5-mL EP tubes labeled A and B. Tube A contained 960 µL of basal DMEM and 40 µL of Lip2000. Tube B contained 960 µL of basal DMEM, 4 µg of the shuttle plasmid pLKO.1, 3 µg of pSPAX2, and 1 µg of pMD2.G. After mixing solutions A and B respectively, the contents of tube B were added to tube A, then gently mixed and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The transfection complex was added to 293 T cells, and after 8–12 h, it was replaced with 7–8 mL normal medium to continue the culture. The supernatant was collected at 48 and 72 h after changing the medium, centrifuged at 1500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The viral supernatant collected twice were combined and filtered through a 0.45-μm filter to remove debris. The filtered supernatant could be stored at − 80 °C for long-term use or at 4 °C for up to one week.

The packaged virus was used to infect SKOV3 cells. SKOV3 cells plated in a 6-well plate were cultured to approximately 20%–30%, and then cultured overnight in 5 A basic culture medium containing 10% FBS. The virus stock solution and culture medium were added to a 6-well plate at a ratio of 1:1. After adding polybrene to the final concentration of 10 µg/mL, the plate was placed in an incubator for culture. After repeated infection the next day, 0.9 µg/mL puromycin was used for screening until all cells in the untransfected control group were killed. The infected cells were amplified and cultured. Among them, some were used to extract RNA (MF036, Mei5 Biotechnology, China) and verify the silencing effect, while others were frozen for subsequent experiments. Of them, two FNDC1-silenced cell lines (targeting different regions) with confirmed knockdown efficiency were selected for subsequent functional assays.

CCK8 detection of cell proliferation

The cells in logarithmic growth phase were digested, collected, and counted. A total of 2000 cells were resuspended in 100 µL 5 A whole culture per well and seeded in 96-well plates, with a total of 5 plates and 7 replicate wells per sample. The time point after cell adhesion was set as the initial detection. Subsequently, 10 µL CCK8 reagent (MK001A) was added to each well every 24 h and incubated in an incubator for 70 min. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the cell survival rate was calculated. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Cell scratch assay

After the cells in the logarithmic growth phase being digested, collected, and counted, 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 2 mL of 5 A whole culture and inoculated into 6-well plates. After the cells adhesion, a 20-μl pipette tip was used to draw a vertical line perpendicular to the plate surface. After washing with PBS, the scratch location was photographed and marked. Subsequently, 1 mL of 5A medium containing 2% FBS was added to continue the culture. After 20 h, the scratch location was washed with PBS and recorded again. The cell scratch area of each group was analyzed using Image software, and the percentage of scratch healing within 20 h was calculated. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Cell invasion

The cells in logarithmic growth phase were starved for 24 h, then digested, centrifuged and resuspended in 5 A basal medium for counting. 200 μL cell suspension containing 2 × 104 cells was gently added to the upper chamber along the tube wall. Subsequently, 500 μl of 5 A medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber of 24-well plate and placed in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. The chambers were washed slowly with PBS, fixed with methanol for 30 min, stained with 1% crystal violet for 20 min, and washed with clean water in turn. Finally, the inserts were inverted, allowed it dry naturally, and photographed. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and analyses were conducted using R software (Version 4.2.2). Gene expression values for all samples were normalized to TPM (Transcripts Per Million) format. For comparison of continuous variables between two groups, normally distributed variables were analyzed by independent Student’s t-test. Mann–Whitney U-test (Wilcoxon rank sum test) was used to analyze differences between variables that were not normally distributed. Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparison of three or more groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used to calculate the correlation coefficients between different molecules. All statistical p-values were two-sided if not specified otherwise, and a p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The notation used to indicate significance is summarized as follows: ns, p > 0.05, not statistically significant; *, p < 0.05, statistically significant; **, p < 0.01, highly statistically significant; and ***, p < 0.001, extremely statistically significant.

FNDC1 univariate logistic regression and ROC curve analysis

In 33 TCGA cancer types database, 31 had the corresponding normal samples. In addition, 265 out of 565 thymoma (THYM) did not express FNDC1. Thus, they were excluded when the diagnostic value of FNDC1 was analyzed in 30 TCGA cancer types by constructing logistic regression models of FNDC1. The results demonstrated that FNDC1 showed high diagnostic value in 18 TCGA cancer types, including OV, BRCA, and PAAD (p < 0.001), with odds ratios (OR) consistently greater than 1 (Fig. 3A). This suggested that high expression of FNDC1 was associated with increased tumorigenic risk. BETA = ln (OR), for instance, ‘OV = 1.35 (1.02–1.71)’ indicated that the odds of FNDC1 being highly expressed were approximately 3 times higher in OV tumors compared with normal tissues, and the result was statistically significant. CIBERSORT immune infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to calculate the infiltration abundance of 22 kinds of immune cells in 33 TCGA cancer types, and the heat map of their correlation with FNDC1 expression was constructed (Fig. 4A, see Table S1 for details). Then, the scatter plots of infiltration abundance of immune cells with the greatest positive/negative correlation with FNDC1 expression were plotted in OV (Figs. 4B and 4 C). The results showed that the expression of FNDC1 in OV was positively and negatively correlated with T cells CD4 memory resting (cor = 0.222, p < 0.001; Fig. 4B) and B cell memory (cor = −0.261, p < 0.001; Fig. 4C), respectively. These results suggested that FNDC1 might exert a dual regulatory role in the tumor immune microenvironment of OV. On the one hand, it may promote T cell-mediated immune responses to enhance anti-tumor cellular immunity; on the other hand, it may suppress B cell-mediated humoral immunity, thereby influencing tumor immune evasion or microenvironment stability. The correlation analysis between ICG and FNDC1

A total of 47 ICGs were identified in the published literature, and their intersections with genes contained in the TCGA cancer expression profile resulted in the identification of 45 ICGs. Then, the correlation between 45 ICGs and FNDC1 expression was examined and visualized in 33 TCGA cancer types (Fig. 5A). The results showed that FNDC1 expression was most positively correlated with TNFSF4 (alias OX40L) in OV (cor = 0.6, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Notably, TNFSF4 is a critical T-cell costimulatory molecule involved in T-cell activation and immune regulation, whose high expression is closely associated with shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. The strong correlation between FNDC1 and TNFSF4 suggested that FNDC1 may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment by influencing T-cell activation and immune regulatory mechanisms, thereby affecting tumor immune responses and immunotherapy outcomes. Discussion

Most patients already have extensive intra-abdominal metastasis at the time of ovarian cancer diagnosis, and acquired drug resistance will occur if it is common during late chemotherapy. Epithelial ovarian cancer is highly heterogeneous, and the efficacy of immunotherapy remains limited [34, 35]. Although some authors have described that the low expression of fibronectin increases the sensitivity of paclitaxel in ovarian cancer [36], the role of FNDC1, a member of the fibronectin type III domain protein family, has not been reported.

In this study, we found that the expression of FNDC1 showed statistically significant differences in 20 TCGA cancer types, of which 18 had high diagnostic value, including OV, BRCA, and PAAD, along with high diagnostic accuracy for OV (AUC = 0.827). Specifically, FNDC1 expression was significantly higher in OV tumor tissues than that in adjacent non-cancerous tissue, and confirmed by our clinical samples. Moreover, the high expression of FNDC1 might be related to MAPK signaling pathways. These results suggested that FNDC1 played an important role in the development of ovarian serous cancer, and had the potential as a diagnostic biomarker.

Further analysis revealed that FNDC1 expression demonstrated a strong positive correlation with immune checkpoint molecule TNFSF4 (also known as OX40L) in ovarian serous cancer. TNFSF4, a member of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) family, primarily mediates the adhesion between activated T cells and endothelial cells, thereby enhancing T cell-tumor cell interactions and promoting tumor cell clearance. Previous studies have demonstrated that high expression of TNFSF4 is significantly associated with reduced patient survival rates in ovarian serous cancer [37, 38]. Notably, the fibronectin III domain in FNDC1 exhibits structural similarity to immunoglobulin constant regions, providing a molecular basis for potential FNDC1-TNFSF4 interactions [8]. At the signaling pathway level, both existing studies and our pathway enrichment results indicate that FNDC1 and TNFSF4 (OX40L) can activate key signaling pathways such as MAPK [39, 40], thereby cooperatively regulating T cell activation, proliferation, and functional states, ultimately influencing the dynamic balance of the tumor immune microenvironment and tumor progression. Based on the biological functions of immune checkpoint molecules, we hypothesize that FNDC1 may enhance T cell-mediated immune responses by regulating TNFSF4 expression, or modulate T cell tumor recognition and cytotoxic capacity through pathways such as MAPK signaling. Furthermore, the synergistic interaction between FNDC1 and TNFSF4 may contribute to immune evasion by therapy-resistant cancer cells, suggesting their potential as novel targets for clinical immunotherapy.

The interaction between extracellular matrix proteins in ovarian serous cancer affects the migration and invasion of tumor cells, in which fibrin, a main component of extracellular matrix, plays a key role [41]. Through GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, we found that FNDC1 and its interacting partners were significantly enriched in biological processes related to collagen fibril organization, extracellular matrix structural constitution, and the protein digestion and absorption. This indicated that FNDC1 and its interacting genes might participate in regulating the maintenance of the tumor microenvironment and malignant progression.

In addition, it is reported that the activation of PI3K/Akt signaling pathway increases the survival and chemotherapy resistance of ovarian serous cancer [42, 43]. Simultaneously, the fibronectin-dependent signal transduction process involves MAPK signaling pathway [44]. Interestingly, in this study, we found that the genes associated with low-expression group of FNDC1 were significantly enriched in focal adhesion PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, while the genes associated with high-expression group were enriched in FcɛRI-mediated MAPK activation pathway. In addition, some studies demonstrate that the formation of FNDC1 + Gbg + VEGFR2 complex activate MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, and promote the progression of gastric cancer [39]. In a word, these results suggested that there were different molecular biological characteristics and signaling mechanisms between high and low expression group of FNDC1. Moreover, it also established a connection between MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, and provided clues for personalized treatment of ovarian serous cancer. Meanwhile, our findings enriched the theoretical basis for chemotherapy resistance in advanced ovarian serous cancer patients, thereby improving the efficacy of personalized chemotherapy.

The key of clinical individualized treatment is to screen drugs suitable for patients. In this study, we predicted the sensitivity of FNDC1 to common anticancer drugs by determining IC50 values through GDSC and CTRP databases. Austcystin D, a water-insoluble natural product isolated from Aspergillus, is an effective cytotoxic agent screened from 26 cell lines. Its remarkable cancer cell-autonomous activity indicates its potential to target multidrug-resistant tumors [45]. For example, it is related to drug resistance of liver cancer and colorectal cancer cells [46, 47]; simultaneously, it contributes to drug resistance by inhibiting T-cell proliferation [48]. In this study, drug sensitivity analysis indicated that FNDC1 was sensitive to austocystin D, suggesting that austocystin D might mediate the expression of TNFSF4 by inhibiting T cells, thereby affecting the expression and related phenotypes of FNDC1. This also supported the existence of an interaction between FNDC1 and TNFSF4. In conclusion, the findings provided a theoretical basis for individualized treatment of cancer patients with specific FNDC1 expression levels in the future. Moreover, austocystin D is expected to be a candidate drug for patients with high FNDC1 expression.

Limitation of this study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First of all, the functional experiments were conducted solely in the SKOV3 ovarian cancer cell line, which may not fully capture the biological heterogeneity across different ovarian cancer subtypes. The generalizability of our findings requires further validation in additional cell models and in vivo systems. Secondly, while bioinformatics analyses revealed the correlations between FNDC1 and immune cell infiltration/checkpoint molecules, functional immunological assays (e.g. immune cell co-culture experiments) were not performed. The precise role of FNDC1 in tumor immune regulation remains to be experimentally elucidated. In addition, current evidence regarding FNDC1-mediated drug sensitivity relied primarily on public database analysis. Direct experimental validation using FNDC1-knockdown or overexpression models in drug response assays should be performed to confirm its potential as a therapeutic target. Finally,the multi-cohort analysis may be influenced by batch effects, and the sample size remains limited. Larger-scale clinical validations are essential to strengthen our conclusions. In summary, the above limitations suggest that the conclusions of this study need more experimental and clinical evidence to support them.

Conclusions

In summary, the results showed that FNDC1 was differentially expressed in 20 kinds of cancers, and had high diagnostic value in 18 kinds of cancers. Moreover, it was found for the first time that FNDC1 had high diagnostic accuracy for ovarian serous cancer. Importantly, we also found a strong correlation between FNDC1 and immune checkpoint TNFSF4. In addition, FNDC1 was significantly positive correlation with austocystin D. Thus, FNDC1 can be used not only for the diagnosis of ovarian serous cancer, but also as a potential target of immunotherapy, which will play an important guiding role in the individualized treatment of ovarian serous cancer. In the future studies, we will focus on the functional verification of FNDC1 at the cellular level.
