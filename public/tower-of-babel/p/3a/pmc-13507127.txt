Evaluating the tourism ecological security in rural regions using the DPSIR framework
Authors: Milad Heydari, Fatemeh Razzaghi Borkhani, Ali Akbar Barati, Taher Azizi-Khalkheili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, the rapid growth of tourism and the increasing number of tourists in Iran have emerged as a serious threat to wildlife conservation and environmental protection. Although rural tourism development has been widely promoted in recent decades as a key strategy for economic growth and improving the quality of life in rural areas, its implications for ecological security are becoming increasingly evident. Ecological security in tourism is defined as a state of harmony in which the natural environment, social activities, and economic structures coexist in balance. This study adopts a critical and forward-looking perspective to evaluate the sustainability of rural tourism and address its associated environmental instability. Using the driving, pressures, state, impacts, and responses (DPSIR) framework and partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM), we analyze data from 88 villages in Mazandaran County, Iran, to assess the impact of tourism growth on ecological security. The results indicate a significant and direct deterioration of the ecological State driven by environmental Pressures, explaining 50% of the variance. In contrast, current governance Responses are insufficient, mitigating only 11% of driving forces and 24% of pressures. Overall, the findings highlight a fundamental mismatch between economic development drivers and ecological integrity. Addressing this structural imbalance requires a systemic shift toward integrated and proactive governance frameworks. Ensuring ecological sustainability depends on coordinated action among local communities, government institutions, and non-governmental organizations, with an emphasis on transformative approaches such as sustainable rural tourism and agritourism to rebalance development and ecological security.

Introduction

Rural areas, endowed with abundant natural, cultural, and historical assets, have long attracted tourists seeking authentic experiences in less urbanized settings. These regions offer a wide range of potentials that form the basis for diverse tourism activities, particularly rural tourism1,2. Recognized as a viable pathway toward sustainable development, Rural tourism contributes to job creation, income generation, and rural revitalization, paralleling the documented benefits of urban tourism development3. It also plays a critical role in curbing rural outmigration, reinforcing socio-economic networks, improving residents’ quality of life, and safeguarding both cultural heritage and natural landscapes4. In the current context, where the sustainable development of the agricultural sector in Iran has faced serious challenges due to climate change and economic conditions5, it has gained even greater importance. However, the rapid and often unregulated expansion of rural tourism, especially in ecologically sensitive and scenic areas, has given rise to a range of environmental and social concerns. These include the overexploitation of natural resources, contamination of soil and water bodies, disruption of ecosystem services, and the degradation of habitats vital to biodiversity6–8, this can itself intensify the prevailing widespread rural poverty in Iran’s rural areas9 and other existing crises, such as extensive land-use change10 and the critical challenge of declining access to water resources11. Such challenges not only compromise the ecological integrity of these destinations but also hinder progress toward achieving long-term sustainability goals in the tourism sector12–14.

In northern Iran, and particularly within Mazandaran Province, the combination of rich natural landscapes and a temperate climate15 has positioned the region as a prominent tourism hub3. Yet, the unrestrained growth of tourism (exacerbated by human-driven pressures such as overgrazing, land-use transformation, and the unregulated expansion of transport infrastructure) has significantly eroded the area’s ecological security16. This underscores the urgent need to evaluate the ecological consequences of tourism development and to formulate evidence-based management strategies that can ensure a sustainable balance between tourism growth and environmental preservation.

The concept of ecological security emerged in the early 1990 s as a foundational principle within sustainable development discourse17. It denotes a state in which the resilience and integrity of ecosystems are safeguarded, enabling human societies to pursue development without compromising the natural systems that sustain them. Ecological security encompasses environmental, economic, and social dimensions, emphasizing the interconnectedness of natural and human systems18–20. Within tourism contexts, ecological security reflects a dynamic equilibrium in which environmental conservation, social well-being, and economic activities coexist in a manner that preserves ecological functions over time21,22. To assess ecological security, several analytical models have been developed, among which the DPSIR framework, consisting of Drivers, Pressures, State, Impacts, and Responses, has gained wide recognition. This model has been applied extensively in fields such as environmental management, water resource protection, sustainable planning23,24, and rural human settlements25. Despite its broad utility, the DPSIR framework remains underutilized in the domain of tourism, and particularly in rural tourism contexts. Empirical applications in this area are limited, with a notable absence of studies from Iran focusing on ecological security within rural tourism through the lens of the DPSIR model. Addressing this gap, the present study seeks to evaluate the ecological security of rural tourism in Mazandaran Province, northern Iran, by systematically applying the DPSIR framework. The aim is to provide a comprehensive understanding of the underlying drivers and pressures associated with rural tourism development and to inform strategic responses that support ecological sustainability in rural destinations.

The existing body of research on tourism ecological security (TES), within the framework of the DPSIR model, can be broadly classified into two thematic categories: (i) studies emphasizing the drivers and pressures affecting ecological security, and (ii) studies focusing on systemic responses and policy interventions aimed at promoting sustainability. The first category centers on identifying the underlying drivers (such as population growth, industrialization, and tourism development) and the consequent environmental pressures, including increased resource consumption, pollution, and ecosystem degradation. For instance, Ruan et al.24 examined the Yangtze River Basin in China and highlighted rapid tourism growth and visitor density as primary drivers contributing to environmental deterioration and reduced ecological resilience. Similarly, Kristiadi et al.26, in their sustainability assessment of Jakarta, identified climate change and industrial expansion as key drivers, resulting in greater water demand, wastewater production, and air quality deterioration. In Iran, Sobhani et al.16 identified population growth, escalating land use demands, and the proliferation of ecotourism in the Central Alborz Protected Area as significant pressures undermining ecological security. Collectively, these studies underscore the necessity of recognizing and managing the root causes of environmental stress to safeguard ecological integrity. Huang et al.27 further emphasized that TES is inherently linked to environmental change, patterns of resource utilization, and the intensity of tourism activities, factors that vary across spatial and temporal scales. Liang28, identified variables such as urbanization, water use, green space availability, tourism revenue, and the presence of academic institutions as influential in shaping TES outcomes.

The second body of research shifts focus to the design and implementation of strategic responses intended to mitigate ecological threats and foster sustainable development. For example, Liu and Yin22, studying TES in China, underscored the role of service quality, technological innovation, environmental governance, and institutional responsiveness in promoting ecological sustainability in tourism. Similarly, Wang et al.29, investigating the Tishan Reservoir watershed, proposed interventions such as stricter environmental legislation, the promotion of ecological farming practices, and the development of green tourism models. Liang28, recommended strategies including the advancement of forest-based tourism, ecological rehabilitation of post-mining areas, investment in skilled tourism personnel, and streamlined administrative procedures for tourists. In another study, Tien et al.30 highlighted the importance of maintaining a delicate balance between tourism development and ecological preservation in Vietnam’s community-based ecotourism, advocating for localized, organizational responses to maintain this equilibrium. These findings consistently point to the necessity of coordinated, multi-dimensional policy frameworks for sustaining ecological security in tourism settings.

Together, these studies reveal that TES is shaped by a dynamic and interconnected set of factors, ranging from anthropogenic drivers and environmental pressures to strategic responses. Within the DPSIR framework, these elements interact to define the trajectory of ecological change and development in tourism-intensive areas. A comprehensive and integrated analysis of these interdependencies is therefore essential to inform effective planning and governance.

In light of these insights, the present study seeks to evaluate the ecological security of rural tourism in northern Iran, specifically within Mazandaran Province, using the DPSIR framework. The research is guided by the following specific objectives:

(i)To identify the primary drivers and pressures influencing TES in rural tourism regions,(ii)To assess the current ecological conditions,(iii)To analyze the environmental and socio-economic impacts of rural tourism development, and.(iv)To evaluate the effectiveness of existing management responses and propose strategies for strengthening ecological security and sustainability in rural tourism.

A key theoretical contribution of this study is the systematic adaption of the DPSIR framework to more accurately represent the recursive dynamics of tourism–ecology systems. Specifically, the modified framework introduces a direct pathway from Driving Forces to the ecological State and repositions Responses as proactive, multi-directional governance instruments capable of influencing all other DPSIR components simultaneously. This modification departs from the original EEA model, in which Responses are conceptualized solely as reactive outcomes of Impacts, thereby enabling a more realistic representation of feedback loops in tourism governance systems.

Theoretical framework

Assessing TES has emerged as a vital approach to evaluating the sustainability of tourist destinations Ruan24. Within this context, the DPSIR model, developed by the European Environment Agency (EEA) in 1997, offers a comprehensive and adaptable framework for analyzing ecological security31. This model builds upon earlier analytical structures, particularly the Pressure–State–Response (PSR) and Driving Force–State–Response (DSR) models, by incorporating the “Impact” component to reflect broader system feedbacks32.

The PSR model, initially introduced by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), served as a foundational tool for environmental assessments. The EEA later extended this model by adding the “Driving” and “Impact” elements, leading to the development of the DPSIR framework29. In this model, indicators are classified into distinct categories, each contributing to a systemic understanding of ecological dynamics. The five components are defined as follows: (1) Driving (D): Refers to underlying factors, natural or anthropogenic, that initiate pressures on ecosystems. These drivers can influence environmental conditions both directly and indirectly24,33. (2) Pressure (P): Represents the stresses exerted on the environment due to resource consumption, pollution, or other human activities. When these pressures exceed ecological thresholds, they can disrupt the balance of ecosystems and lead to significant changes34,35. (3) State (S): Describes the current condition of ecological, social, and economic systems as influenced by prevailing pressures. These states can reflect both degradation and resilience, affecting human well-being and ecosystem services24,33. (4) Impact (I): Encompasses the consequences of shifts in the system’s state, which may manifest across environmental, economic, and social dimensions. These impacts often influence quality of life, ecosystem functionality, and resource availability24,33,36. (5) Response (R): Involves the strategies and interventions implemented by governments, institutions, and communities to mitigate negative impacts and foster sustainability. These responses may include preventive, adaptive, or restorative measures36.

An innovative aspect of the current study is the explicit integration of the three pillars of sustainable development (economic, social, and environmental) into the DPSIR model. This expanded framework allows for a more holistic and accurate evaluation of ecological security in rural tourism settings. Previous research has also adopted similar integrated approaches within the DPSIR structure16,22,29,37–39. The conceptual framework of this study (Fig. 1) presents the interrelationships among the DPSIR components. In the original structure, driving forces influence pressures, which in turn alter the system’s state. These changes generate impacts, prompting specific responses. In the revised version of the model, however, driving forces are hypothesized to directly influence both pressure and state. Moreover, responses are no longer viewed solely as a reaction to impacts; instead, they are conceptualized as dynamic factors that can affect all stages of the system, driving forces, pressures, state, and impacts. This refined model draws on insights from earlier studies23,24,29,40. The framework used in this study thus serves as a comprehensive tool for evaluating TES across the full spectrum of sustainability. It enables a clearer understanding of the interdependencies among the system’s components and informs the development of targeted strategies to support sustainable rural tourism development.

In summary, the DPSIR model, especially in its revised form, offers a robust analytical lens through which the drivers, pressures, system states, impacts, and responses in rural tourism can be systematically examined. By evaluating each of these components within the economic, social, and environmental dimensions, the model facilitates a nuanced and integrated assessment of tourism-related ecological security.

In this study, we employed the DPSIR model to address the following two key questions: (1) Has the development of rural tourism served as an appropriate response to the current state of ecological security in the study area? (2) Has this response been able to influence and improve the drivers, pressures, states, and impacts associated with the current condition of ecological security? Research hypotheses

Building upon the conceptual framework illustrated in Fig. 1 and informed by the theoretical interrelations among the variables, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1:

The identified Drivers have a significant influence on the emergence of the identified Pressures.

H2:

The identified Pressures have a significant impact on altering the State of ecological security.

H3:

The altered State of ecological security has a significant effect on the intensity of the resulting Impacts.

H4:

The development of rural tourism constitutes a significant and appropriate Response to the impacts of the current state of ecological security.

H5:

This tourism-driven Response has a significant mitigating effect on the primary Drivers of ecological security degradation.

H6:

The implemented Response leads to a significant reduction in the environmental Pressures.

H7:

As a Response, rural tourism development has a significant influence on the State of ecological security.

These hypotheses are constructed to reflect the dynamic and interdependent nature of the DPSIR framework, enabling a deeper investigation into the mechanisms through which tourism development affects ecological security in rural contexts (Fig. 2).

Methodology

Study area

Mahmoudabad County, situated in Mazandaran Province in northern Iran, is one of the region’s prominent rural tourism destinations, owing to its strategic coastal location along the southern shores of the Caspian Sea. The county encompasses two administrative divisions, Central and Sorkhrud, and includes a total of 88 rural villages. According to the 2016 national census, the rural population of the area stood at 59,864 individuals across 20,163 households. The county is home to approximately 200 rural tourism units, collectively employing nearly 2,000 individuals and attracting more than 1.17 million tourists annually.

Statistical population and sampling method

The target population of this study comprised all village administrators (Dehyar) operating within the boundaries of Mahmoudabad County. Given the limited size of this population and the importance of obtaining data from each administrative unit, a full enumeration strategy was employed. As the total number of villages in the county is 88, the final sample included all 88 Dehyars. The details of this census-based sampling approach are presented in Table 1.

The selection of Dehyars as informants was based on their exclusive access to village-level administrative, land-use, and tourism data, as well as their daily interactions with both resident populations and tourism operators, making them as well-informed observers of ecological change. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, Dehyars represent a single governance actor and their assessments may not capture the full diversity of stakeholder perspectives, including those of tourists, local entrepreneurs, or agricultural households. Second, as government-appointed officials, respondents may be influenced by social desirability bias when evaluating the adequacy of policy responses. Future research should employ multi-stakeholder sampling strategies that incorporate tourists, local residents, and land managers to provide a more comprehensive and triangulated assessment of tourism ecological security (Table 2).

Data collection instrument

Data were collected through a structured questionnaire specifically designed for this study, grounded in the DPSIR framework. The instrument comprised 90 items grouped across five principal sections, corresponding to three major thematic domains: economic, social, and environmental. Responses were recorded using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Very Low”) to 5 (“Very High”). Additionally, a separate section of the questionnaire gathered demographic information to provide a descriptive profile of the respondents.

Data analysis method

The collected data were analyzed using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), applying the Partial Least Squares (PLS) technique. This method was chosen due to its appropriateness for analyzing complex, multivariate models involving latent variables, particularly when the sample size is relatively small and the data may not follow a normal distribution41,42.

The adequacy of the sample size for PLS-SEM was verified using multiple criteria. According to the ten-times rule43, the required minimum was n = 90, calculated as ten times the largest number of indicators assigned to a single construct. In the present model, the construct I-Env included the largest number of indicators (nine), yielding a recommended minimum sample size of 90. Although the achieved sample size (n = 88) was marginally below this guideline, the difference was negligible and is unlikely to compromise the robustness of the PLS-SEM analysis. A formal a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.144, assuming a moderate effect size (f² = 0.15), α = 0.05, and three predictors (according to the DPSIR model construct), indicated a minimum required sample size of n = 77 (power = 0.80). The census sample of n = 88 exceeded this threshold (power ≈ 0.84) and represented the entire target population, thereby ensuring both statistical adequacy and full population coverage.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Version 26 and SmartPLS Version 3. The application of SEM was particularly suitable for this study, as it enabled the simultaneous assessment of multiple interrelationships among the variables within the conceptual model. Due to the presence of feedback loops in the DPSIR structure, the model fit process was conducted in two phases. The initial phase involved testing a base model to examine the influence of drivers on subsequent components including pressures, state, impacts, and response strategies. In the second phase, the structural model was expanded to include hypothesized reciprocal paths, particularly the effects of response strategies on earlier variables, including drivers, pressures, and impacts, ultimately influencing the state of ecological security.

The findings indicate that the villages of Mahmoudabad County host, on average, about 227 rural households, with an average rural population of approximately 685 residents. Convergent validity was examined through the average variance extracted (AVE) and variance inflation factor (VIF). For both models, AVE values exceeded 0.50 for all latent constructs (Tables 5 and 6), indicating adequate convergence. To further enhance AVE values and strengthen model fit, several observed variables were excluded. Collinearity was assessed using VIF values, all values were below 5, demonstrating that multicollinearity was not a concern in the reflective measurement models.

The final step in evaluating the reflective measurement models is the assessment of discriminant validity, which examines whether the defined latent constructs are sufficiently distinct from one another and free from substantial conceptual or statistical overlap. For this purpose, the Fornell–Larcker criterion45 was applied. As shown in Tables 7 and 8, all constructs met the required thresholds, confirming that the model achieved satisfactory discriminant validity. Overall, the measurement models demonstrated a sound factor structure, consistent with the study’s theoretical framework, with both validity and reliability confirmed.

Evaluation of the structural model

The structural model for the endogenous latent variables was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), the predictive relevance index (Q²), collinearity (Variance Inflation Factor, VIF), the effect size (f2), and path coefficients (ꞵ). The R² statistic, which measures the model’s predictive accuracy, ranges from 0 to 1, with values above 0.19 generally considered acceptable (Chin, 1998). In the first model, the R² values for “Pressures” and “Responses,” and in the second model for “Driving Forces,” were not statistically significant, possibly reflecting the influence of omitted variables not captured in the current framework. The significance of estimated parameters was assessed using t-statistics at a two-tailed threshold of 1.96 (α = 0.05). As reported in Table 9, the majority of structural paths achieved statistical significance; however, three paths did not reach this threshold: Driving → Pressure in Model 1 (t = 1.137, p = 0.256), Impact → Response in Model 1 (t = 1.019, p = 0.309), and Response → State in Model 2 (t = 1.077, p = 0.282). These non-significant paths are retained in the integrated model and discussed in relation to the theoretical framework (see Sect. 5).

Results of the structural equation model

The integrated model presented in Fig. 5 results from combining two distinct models designed to address the study’s two main research questions. Based on this model and the results in Table 9, the findings indicate, first, that the identified drivers did not have a significant impact on explaining the variance of the pressures. In the absence of rural tourism development (the first model, with solid lines), this effect was not statistically significant (β = 0.134, t = 1.137, p = 0.256). However, in the second model (with dashed lines), the presence of rural tourism development rendered the effect of these same drivers on the pressures statistically significant (β = 0.297, t = 2.513, p = 0.000). This model explains only 22.5% of the variance in pressures, implying that a substantial proportion of the drivers contributing to the emergence of pressures remain unidentified in this study.

Second, the identified pressures explain approximately 49% of the variance in the current state of tourism ecological security in the study area, and their effect was statistically significant at a 1% error level (β = 0.592 in the first model and β = 0.541 in the second). A comparison of the two models reveals that with the development of rural tourism, the magnitude of the pressures’ impact on the state decreased from 0.592 to 0.544. Furthermore, the explained variance of the state decreased from 0.491 to 0.481.

The findings also indicate that the current state of ecological security determines approximately 41% of the variance in the identified impacts, a proportion that remained nearly consistent both without (0.417) and with (0.410) rural tourism development.

Finally, this study demonstrated that rural tourism development alone did not constitute an adequate response to the effects stemming from the current state of ecological security in the study area. In other words, this policy or intervention was not commensurate with the nature and scale of the existing impacts. However, the proposed model revealed that rural tourism development could significantly mitigate the pressures on ecological security (β = −0.501), explaining approximately 24% (R² = 0.242) of their variance. This study also revealed that the identified drivers have a direct effect on the state of ecological security. Based on the path between drivers and state, the path coefficient was 0.544, which was statistically significant (p = 0.001) (Table 9).

The results highlight the interconnected nature of the DPSIR components and underscore the need to consider both direct and indirect effects when evaluating sustainability in tourism ecosystems.

Discussion

This study evaluated tourism ecological security (TES) in Mahmoudabad County using DPSIR framework that integrates economic, social, and environmental dimensions. The near-equal distribution of villages across high- and low-TES categories in all three sustainability dimensions suggests that ecological stress in Mahmoudabad is systemic rather than sector-specific. Instead of affecting isolated components of the rural system, it simultaneously influences livelihood structures, social cohesion, and natural capital. This systemic character underscores the inadequacy of sector-by-sector governance interventions and calls for integrated, multi-dimensional management frameworks.

Regarding Hypothesis 1, the conditional nature of the Driver → Pressure relationship is significant only when governance responses are incorporated into the model, carries important theoretical implications. Ecological pressures do not arise automatically from driving forces; rather, their emergence depends on the capacity of governance structures to interrupt this transmission chain. When response mechanisms are weak or misaligned, drivers translate more readily into harmful pressures. This pattern is consistent with findings from the Yangtze River Basin24 and multi-province Chinese contexts28, where driver-to-pressure conversion intensified under fragmented governance. Among environmental drivers, the region’s natural endowment (favorable coastal climate and scenic landscapes) emerged as the primary structural attractor for tourist flows15, while agricultural income insufficiency and limited government support dominated economic drivers, reflecting the broader rural-poverty context of northern Iran9. The policy priority arising from this finding is clear: proactive governance must be in place before driver intensity escalates. Pre-emptive land-use zoning, agricultural water security policy, and investment in rural service infrastructure, which ranked lowest among social driver indicators, are more effective at the point of driver activation than at the point of pressure management.

Hypothesis 2 received the strongest empirical support in the study, confirming that pressures are the dominant determinant of TES state. The magnitude of this relationship is comparable to findings reported for the Yangtze River Basin24 and the multi-provincial analysis of Liu and Yin22. However, the slightly stronger effect observed in the present study reflects the compound nature of ecological stressors in a small coastal county, where mass tourism, agricultural land conversion, and hydrological pressure operate simultaneously rather than sequentially16. Agricultural land conversion to accommodation and recreational facilities emerged as the most prominent pressure indicator, a pattern that aligns closely with documented land-use transformation dynamics in northern Iran10, and one that simultaneously contracts the agricultural base underpinning rural livelihood resilience. Forest degradation and freshwater depletion driven by unregulated tourism ranked second, posing dual threats to ecological resilience and the long-term viability of tourism itself. Mandatory carrying capacity assessments for coastal and forest-adjacent zones, enforcement of agricultural land preservation ordinances, and community-based resource stewardship programs28 are the most directly scalable policy instruments for breaking the dominant pressure-to-state degradation pathway.

Hypothesis 3 confirmed that shifts in ecological state generate significant downstream socio-economic impacts, and notably, this relationship remained structurally stable across both models. The stability of the State → Impact pathway (unlike the more context-dependent Driver → Pressure and Response → State relationships) suggests that once ecological conditions deteriorate beyond a threshold, the cascading socio-economic consequences proceed largely independently of governance intervention in the short term. He et al.39, and Jahanishakib and Mohammadpour46reported analogous findings in comparable rural tourism contexts, and Wang et al.29, documented measurable contractions in eco-agricultural productivity and tourism revenue under ecological degradation in the Tishan Reservoir watershed. Where ecological state is maintained or improved, agritourism expansion, marketing of organic agricultural products, and cooperative development for women and youth generate mutually reinforcing economic and social benefits. These positive pathways are, however, contingent on preserving sufficient ecological quality, their erosion under sustained pressure would undermine both the tourism product and its agricultural foundation. Preventing ecological degradation is likely to be more cost-effective than managing its downstream socio-economic consequences; therefore, tourism carrying-capacity limits, equitable benefit-sharing arrangements, and formalized agritourism cooperatives30 should be prioritized.

Hypothesis 4, that rural tourism development constitutes a commensurate response to ecological impacts, was not supported. The absence of a statistically significant Impact → Response relationship indicates that the strategies prioritized by local administrators were not systematically informed by the ecological consequences of tourism. This structural governance failure reflects an economic optimization logic rather than a sustainability logic: policy design is driven by visitor numbers and revenue targets rather than calibrated to the severity of observed ecological damage. The contrast with contexts where governance responses do track ecological impacts, such as Liu and Yin22 Chinese multi-province sample, which embedded formalized environmental monitoring requirements in local administrative structures, reveals that the critical missing element is institutional feedback architecture rather than willingness to respond. Bridging this governance gap requires village-level ecological monitoring, ecological compliance indicators for tourism licensing, and participatory monitoring involving local communities and tourism operators23.

Perhaps the most theoretically significant finding concerns Hypotheses 5 through 7. The model reveals a governance paradox: although current response strategies significantly reduce driving forces and environmental pressures, they do not produce measurable improvements in ecological state. This pattern may reflect both the temporal lag of ecological recovery and the predominance of regulatory measures over active ecological restoration. The subordination of environmental responses to social and economic priorities (environmental response indicators ranked lowest in priority among all DPSIR response dimensions) reinforces this reading. A governance system dominated by social and economic logics cannot achieve ecological security even when it succeeds in moderating pressures. This finding aligns with Liang et al.28, who reported an analogous pattern in a post-mining tourism transition context in China, and with Jahanishakib and Mohammadpour46, who found that institutional pressure-reduction measures in the Central Alborz area did not generate ecological recovery in the absence of active restoration programs. Accordingly, governance should shift from reactive regulation toward anticipatory ecological restoration through environmental levies, green agritourism development, and stronger cross-sectoral institutional coordination.

Comparing these findings with international DPSIR-based tourism research reveals both convergences and instructive divergences. The dominance of the Pressure → State pathway is a consistent finding across the Yangtze River Basin24, Jakarta26, and multi-province China22,47, confirming that direct anthropogenic land and resource stressors are the universal primary mechanism of TES deterioration. The divergence, however, concerns the effectiveness of governance responses. In institutional environments with formalized environmental monitoring and performance accountability systems, response strategies generate measurable ecological improvements22. In contexts characterized by weaker institutional capacity, the present study and Tien et al., and30 Vietnamese community-based ecotourism analysis, responses reduce pressures but fail to improve state, pointing to governance quality rather than resource endowment as the binding constraint on ecological security. Future research applying the modified DPSIR framework in comparable rural coastal and semi-arid developing-country contexts would help establish whether this governance paradox is a systematic feature of limited-capacity institutional environments or a context-specific characteristic of Mazandaran’s tourism governance system.

The findings of this study further clarify the contribution of the proposed DPSIR framework relative to the conventional model. In the original DPSIR framework, Driving Forces are generally assumed to influence ecological conditions indirectly through the generation of environmental Pressures. In contrast, the results of the present study demonstrate that Driving Forces may also exert a statistically significant direct influence on the ecological State, indicating that broad socioeconomic and environmental drivers can affect ecosystem conditions independently of intermediate pressures. Moreover, while the conventional framework primarily conceptualizes Responses as reactive measures following environmental impacts, the modified framework adopted in this study views Responses as adaptive governance mechanisms capable of influencing multiple components of the system simultaneously. These findings provide empirical support for a more dynamic representation of tourism ecological security and extend the explanatory capability of the traditional DPSIR framework for complex rural tourism systems.

Conclusion

Sustainable tourism development has become a global priority due to its dual role in promoting economic growth and enhancing the cultural and environmental profile of destinations. However, the rapid expansion of tourism worldwide has intensified environmental pressures in many regions, threatening ecological security and the long-term sustainability of the sector. Mazandaran Province, characterized by rich ecotourism resources, a northern coastline, and diverse natural attractions, attracts a large number of visitors throughout the year. While tourism growth in recent years has contributed positively to the local economy, it has also generated significant environmental and socio-cultural challenges. If not properly managed, these pressures may lead to the degradation of natural resources and reduce the resilience of rural communities.

This study evaluated the ecological security of tourism in Mahmoudabad County using an adapted DPSIR framework integrating economic, social, and environmental dimensions. The proposed model introduces two main contributions. First, it establishes a direct causal link from Driving Forces to the ecological State, confirming a significant pathway not explicitly represented in the original DPSIR framework (β = 0.544, p = 0.001). Second, it reconceptualizes Responses as dynamic, multi-directional governance instruments that simultaneously affect Driving Forces, Pressures, and State, rather than serving only as reactive mechanisms.

This modification enhances the explanatory power of the DPSIR framework and provides more nuanced insights into the interactions between tourism development, environmental pressures, and governance responses. The findings confirm that the modified model is effective in capturing complex system interdependencies. Future studies should adopt multi-stakeholder research designs by integrating the perspectives of village administrators with those of local residents, tourism operators, visitors, environmental organizations, and agricultural land managers. Such an approach would allow triangulation of stakeholder perceptions, improve the robustness of tourism ecological security assessments, and provide a more comprehensive understanding of governance effectiveness across different institutional and community contexts.

Based on the study findings, the following priority actions are recommended: Strengthen integrated cross-sectoral governance by improving coordination among tourism, agriculture, forestry, fisheries, and environmental agencies.
