Rivaroxaban: A Rare Cause of Spontaneous Bilateral Adrenal Haematomas
Authors: Preet Mukesh Shah, Robert D Murray
Published: 2023

An 86-year-old male was admitted with a two-day history of dysuria, poor appetite, and abdominal pain and fever. He reported the onset of right costovertebral angle pain a month previously after sustaining a mechanical fall with trauma to his head and right flank. He had a past medical history of chronic kidney disease, ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and atrial fibrillation. Due to atrial fibrillation, he was anticoagulated with rivaroxaban for stroke prevention, and had been taking a stable dose of 15 mg once daily since March 2019.

On evaluation he was haemodynamically stable, apyrexial, with tenderness over the right costovertebral angle. His white blood cell count and C-reactive protein were raised in keeping with infection. His platelet count was normal and fibrinogen >4.5 (normal: 1.6-5.9) g/L. His renal function was stable, with creatinine at baseline level (121 µmol/L). His clotting screen showed INR 2.9 and aPTT 39.7 (normal: 23.5-37.5) seconds relating to the rivaroxaban and sepsis. His sodium and potassium levels were normal. Empirical antibiotics were commenced to treat putative urosepsis. A computerised tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen was performed for suspected pyelonephritis and to rule out trauma to the right kidney. Imaging showed the possibility of bilateral adrenal nodules. A subsequent dedicated adrenal triple phase CT showed bilateral high attenuation adrenals on non-contrast images with only marginal enhancement post-contrast (left adrenal pre-contrast 54 HU and post-contrast 59 HU; right adrenal precontrast 60 HU and post-contrast 57 HU) (Fig. 1) in keeping with bilateral adrenal haematomas. Rivaroxaban was withheld and a random cortisol level was recorded at 271 nmol/L. A confirmatory short synacthen test showed baseline cortisol 176 nmol/L and 30-minute cortisol 203 nmol/L, supporting a diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency. He commenced an oral hydrocortisone regime of 10 mg in the morning, 5 mg in the afternoon and 5 mg early evening.

Adrenal haemorrhage (AH) is an uncommon but life-threatening condition.1 This can be attributed to anticoagulation therapy, abdominal trauma, focal adrenal lesions, pregnancy, sepsis and congenital or acquired bleeding problems.2 The exact mechanism by which AH occurs is unknown, however, it has been hypothesized that the adrenal glands are prone to haemorrhage due to an abundant blood supply from three arteries draining to a single vein, associated with co-localization to the adrenal medulla and thus catecholamine secretion that may precipitate episodes of vasoconstriction.3

Unilateral AH, due to the contralateral normal adrenal gland essentially maintains a normal biochemical and endocrine profile. Bilateral AH leads to adrenal insufficiency (AI) in 16-50% of cases which can precipitate an acute life-threatening adrenal crisis.24 Early diagnosis is therefore crucial.4 Only seven cases of AH with AI have been reported in the literature directly relating to direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) therapy, with the majority associated with Rivaroxaban.2 In our patient, the detection of AH was incidental as the imaging was performed to characterise a putative pyelonephritis. To the best of our knowledge, our case is the first case of DOAC-induced adrenal haematomas with AI reported in the United Kingdom.
