Integrating TAM–ISSM–SOR framework to explore how virtual tourism experience influences tourists’ travel intention
Authors: Li Chen, Xianling Wu, Yidong Liu, Zhiyuan Hu, Xing Chen, Zheyue Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid development of immersive technologies has transformed the tourism experience, enabling tourists to explore destinations virtually before physical visits. This study investigates how virtual tourism experiences influence tourists’ intentions to visit real-world destinations, using the online museum of Porcelain Temple as a case study. Integrating the Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR) framework with the Information System Success Model (ISSM) and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the research examines the effects of system quality (SYQ), information quality (IQ), and service quality (SEQ) on perceived usefulness (PU), perceived ease of use (PEU), and perceived enjoyment (PE), which in turn affect tourists’ travel intentions (TI). Data collected from 602 online museum users were analyzed using structural equation modeling. Results indicate that IQ and SEQ significantly influence PU, PEU, and PE, while SYQ significantly impacts PEU and PE but not PU. PU, PEU, and PE all positively affect TI, highlighting the importance of both cognitive and affective responses in translating virtual experiences into real-world travel behavior. These findings provide theoretical insights into virtual-to-physical tourism conversion and practical guidance for optimizing online museum platforms to stimulate on-site visitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24793-z.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of immersive technologies such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and 360-degree video has profoundly reshaped the tourism industry, enabling visitors to engage with destinations through interactive digital experiences1,2. Virtual tourism has emerged as a novel form of digital experience that transcends temporal and spatial limitations, offering convenient, flexible, and multi-sensory interactions with cultural heritage, landscapes, and exhibitions3,4. Online museums and virtual heritage sites, in particular, provide opportunities for cultural preservation and education while stimulating tourists’ curiosity and emotional engagement5,6. This trend has accelerated in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, when mobility restrictions heightened reliance on technology-mediated tourism experiences and revealed their potential for stress relief and emotional well-being7. As digital platforms increasingly serve as a prelude—or even an alternative—to physical visitation, understanding how virtual experiences influence tourists’ real-world travel decisions has become both timely and essential.

Despite the growing attention to virtual tourism, research remains fragmented in several respects. First, extant studies have primarily emphasized technological design, immersion, and authenticity3,6, but have paid relatively limited attention to the psychological mechanisms through which tourists’ virtual experiences are translated into actual travel intentions. Second, most investigations rely on single theoretical lenses such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) or the Information System Success Model (ISSM), which provide valuable insights into system use but fall short of capturing the interplay between technological quality, cognitive evaluations, and affective responses. Recent attempts to integrate TAM and ISSM have enriched contexts such as digital libraries8, location-based mobile tourism applications9, and healthcare systems10. Yet these studies stop short of addressing cultural heritage virtual tourism, where visitors’ decisions are shaped not only by utilitarian and ease-of-use considerations but also by cultural appreciation, emotional resonance, and enjoyment11. Finally, few studies have systematically examined the nexus between virtual and physical tourism, leaving incomplete our understanding of how technology-mediated heritage experiences translate into real-world travel behavior.

To address these gaps, this study develops an integrative framework by combining the Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR) paradigm with TAM and ISSM. Within this model, system quality (SYQ), information quality (IQ), and service quality (SEQ) function as the external stimuli (S) that shape tourists’ internal evaluations (O). These evaluations are operationalized through perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) from TAM, along with perceived enjoyment (PE), reflecting both cognitive and affective dimensions of user response. Ultimately, these internal states influence the response (R): tourists’ behavioral intention to physically visit the destination. By embedding TAM constructs within the organism stage and positioning ISSM dimensions as stimuli, this model provides a comprehensive explanation of how virtual tourism experiences foster actual travel intentions, thereby bridging the digital–physical divide in tourism behavior.

This study contributes to the literature in several important ways. Theoretically, it extends prior ISSM–TAM integrations into the underexplored domain of cultural heritage virtual tourism, offering new insights into how technological, informational, and service-related qualities influence both cognitive and affective evaluations. It further advances SOR applications in tourism by capturing the mediating role of perceived enjoyment alongside perceived usefulness and ease of use, thereby providing a more holistic account of tourists’ psychological mechanisms. Empirically, this study examines the case of Nanjing Porcelain Temple Virtual Museum, offering one of the first investigations into how virtual heritage experiences stimulate real-world travel intentions. Practically, the findings provide actionable implications for tourism managers and platform developers, highlighting strategies to enhance online museum experiences that encourage offline visitation and contribute to the sustainable development of cultural heritage tourism.

Literature review and hypotheses development

Virtual tourism

Virtual tourism has gradually evolved into a distinct form of technology-mediated tourism experience, enabled by immersive technologies such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and 360-degree digital platforms. Unlike traditional online information searching, virtual tourism emphasizes immersion, interactivity, and sensory engagement, allowing users to simulate aspects of physical travel without geographical or temporal constraints1,12. This conceptual transformation has repositioned virtual tourism from a supplementary tool of destination marketing to an independent experience pathway that blends cultural learning, emotional engagement, and hedonic enjoyment3,13.

The development of virtual tourism has been shaped by both technological progress and changing tourist demands. Early applications were often limited to low-immersion promotional tools, but improvements in system quality, information richness, and service delivery have expanded its role to include virtual museums, digital exhibitions, and heritage site reconstructions2,14. The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated its adoption, as travel restrictions highlighted its potential to provide alternative access to cultural and natural destinations7,15. Recent studies suggest that virtual tourism not only satisfies curiosity and hedonic needs but also stimulates actual travel intentions by enhancing perceived usefulness, ease of use, and joyfulness16,17. In this regard, virtual tourism functions as a bridge between digital engagement and physical visitation, offering both immediate experiential value and long-term destination promotion5,6.

SOR: from environmental stimuli to cognitive–affective states and responses

The Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR) paradigm explains how external cues (S) shape internal cognitive–affective states (O), which in turn drive behavioral responses (R)18. Contemporary tourism and digital behavior studies consistently validate this sequence while clarifying what “counts” as effective stimuli and organismic states. In technology-mediated contexts, platform or environmental features—such as content salience, sensory cues, and interaction design—serve as stimuli that elicit cognitive appraisals (e.g., usefulness, ease) and affect (e.g., enjoyment, arousal), culminating in approach or avoidance behaviors (e.g., purchase, visit intention)19. Extensions of SOR in tourism identify sensory and informational stimuli as especially potent: for instance, natural soundscapes heighten attention and emotional engagement that translate into satisfaction and pro-environmental behaviors (Jiang, 2020)18. Within broader digital information environments, SOR has also been used to explain selective approach/avoidance under intense information flows, showing that stimulus valence and intensity shape internal states (e.g., anxiety, perceived control) and subsequent behavioral choices20.

Implication for this study. Positioning system quality (SYQ), information quality (IQ), and service quality (SEQ) as S captures the principal environmental levers of a virtual museum; embedding perceived usefulness (PU), perceived ease of use (PEU), and perceived enjoyment (PE) as O reflects the dual cognitive–affective engine that links virtual experiences to the R of on-site travel intention. The SOR lens therefore furnishes the causal scaffolding for your ISSM–TAM integration.

ISSM: quality dimensions as upstream drivers in digital experiences

The Information Systems Success Model (ISSM) conceptualizes IS outcomes through interrelated quality constructs—system quality, information quality, service quality—that shape user evaluations and behaviors21. Subsequent extensions in e-commerce and digital library contexts reinforce that these qualities differentially predict downstream perceptions and continuance: information quality often dominates perceived value and decision confidence, service quality supports trust and satisfaction, and system quality undergirds usability and reliability22,23. Earlier work on success measurement provides the meta framework and metrics logic that legitimize these constructs as actionable antecedents24.

Implication for this study. Treating SYQ/IQ/SEQ as SOR-S variables imports a mature success-measurement tradition into a virtual-heritage setting, where interpretive richness (IQ) and supportive interaction (SEQ) are theoretically expected to have stronger effects on usefulness and enjoyment than raw technical performance—while SYQ should primarily enable ease of use.

TAM (and hedonic extensions): cognitive appraisals and adoption-related behaviors

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) provides a theoretical framework for explaining users’ acceptance of new technologies by emphasizing the roles of perceived ease of use (PEU) and perceived usefulness (PU). Specifically, it posits that perceived ease of use influences perceived usefulness, and together these factors shape individuals’ attitudes and behavioral intentions toward a system25. In immersive tourism, a growing body of synthesis shows that cognitive appraisals (PU, PEU) remain central, but hedonic/affective responses carry independent weight for experience quality and downstream intentions (meta-analytic evidence in Fan et al., 2022)26. In digital museums, user-centered evaluation highlights how narrative design and interaction patterns elevate engagement and learning, aligning with TAM’s utility–usability core while adding experiential criteria27.

Implication for this study. Locating PU, PEU, and PE at SOR-O integrates TAM’s cognitive pathway (PEU → PU) with a hedonic channel (PE), which is theoretically salient for virtual heritage where enjoyment/presence co-determine intention along with instrumental value.

Rationale for integration in a virtual-heritage setting

Bringing ISSM quality dimensions into SOR as stimuli and embedding TAM constructs as the organism resolves a common limitation of single-theory approaches: ISSM specifies what to improve (system, information, service), TAM explains how users evaluate (usefulness, ease) and why they adopt (intention), and SOR provides the behavioral conduit from environment to action. This stacked logic is particularly apt for a virtual museum, where interpretive content and guidance (IQ/SEQ) are theorized to drive usefulness and enjoyment, and technical smoothness (SYQ) primarily ensures ease, together bridging virtual engagement and physical visitation.

Hypothesis development

System quality (SYQ)

System quality has been shown to play a decisive role in shaping user perceptions of technological systems. Glassey & Glassey (2005) highlighted in the e-government context that systems requiring fewer operational steps enhance perceived usefulness (PU), suggesting that effective system design translates directly into users’ evaluations of functional value28. Similarly, Al-Adwan et al. (2021) demonstrated in e-learning that stable and efficient systems strengthen learners’ perceptions of usefulness, further supporting the positive effect of system quality on PU29.

Beyond usefulness, system quality also reduces operational barriers and improves ease of interaction. AL-Hawamleh (2024), integrating ISSM and TAM, confirmed that system quality exerts a direct positive effect on perceived ease of use (PEU)30. Evidence from healthcare supports this finding: Lee (2022) found that the quality of electronic medical records significantly enhanced nurses’ perceptions of both usefulness and ease of use, highlighting the universal relevance of system quality in influencing PEU31.

In addition to functional aspects, system quality contributes to enjoyment. Putri & Pujani (2019) showed that high-quality e-commerce systems improve consumer satisfaction by making interactions more pleasant, pointing to a direct link with perceived enjoyment (PE)32. In a tourism context, Chung et al. (2018) found that high-quality augmented reality systems enhanced immersion, which in turn increased tourists’ enjoyment during cultural heritage visits33. These findings underline that superior system quality not only ensures technical efficiency but also creates a more engaging and enjoyable user experience.

Taken together, these studies indicate that system quality strengthens tourists’ perceptions of usefulness, simplifies their interaction with the platform, and enhances the enjoyment of online tours.

Information quality (IQ)

Information quality is a central determinant of user evaluations in digital environments. Setia et al. (2013) found that high-quality, accurate, and relevant information enhances customer service performance, underscoring its role in shaping perceived usefulness (PU)34. Similarly, Gorla et al. (2010) showed that superior information quality strengthens organizational outcomes, reflecting its direct contribution to users’ perception of a system’s utility35.

Information quality also influences ease of interaction. Park et al. (2007), in the context of online travel agencies, demonstrated that clear and reliable information reduces users’ cognitive effort, thereby improving perceived ease of use (PEU)36. Consistent with this, Alyoussef (2023) reported that in higher education e-learning, information quality enhances both PU and PEU by aligning system outputs with user tasks37. AL-Hawamleh (2024) further confirmed that information quality exerts significant positive effects on ease of use in e-learning systems, emphasizing its broader relevance across digital contexts30.

Beyond usefulness and ease, information quality contributes to enjoyment. Orden-Mejía & Huertas (2022) found that information-rich destination chatbots foster tourists’ satisfaction and enjoyment (PE), as reliable content enhances trust and enriches user experiences38. Supporting evidence from healthcare also aligns with this view: Lee (2022) demonstrated that accurate and high-quality medical record information improves nurses’ perceptions of both usefulness and ease of use, reflecting how quality information can create both functional and affective value31.

Taken together, these findings suggest that high-quality information fosters perceptions of usefulness, simplifies user interaction, and enhances enjoyment in digital tourism platforms.

Service quality (SEQ)

Service quality has been widely recognized as a core determinant of user perceptions in digital environments. Delone & McLean (2003) identified service quality as a central driver of system success, showing that responsive and reliable support enhances users’ evaluations of a system’s usefulness (PU)39. Extending this, Elliot et al. (2013) found that in virtual travel communities, higher service quality directly strengthened users’ perceived value, confirming its role in shaping PU40. Similarly, Li & Shang (2020) reported that in e-government platforms, improved service quality positively affected perceived usefulness41, while Alkhawaja et al. (2022) demonstrated that this relationship also facilitates technology acceptance in educational contexts42.

Service quality is also crucial for reducing barriers to use and fostering ease of interaction. Petter & McLean (2009), through a meta-analysis, confirmed that service quality has a strong positive influence on users’ perceived ease of use (PEU)43. Supporting this view, Gao et al. (2015) showed that in mobile commerce, superior service quality increased users’ trust and simplified their interactions, thereby enhancing PEU44. Evidence from the education sector aligns with this: Du et al. (2022) found that teachers’ perceptions of VR service quality significantly improved both PU and PEU45, while Lee (2022) similarly observed that better service quality in electronic medical record systems led to higher perceptions of ease of use31.

Beyond functional benefits, service quality contributes to hedonic experiences. Papadomichelaki & Mentzas (2009) demonstrated that service quality enhances user satisfaction and enjoyment in e-government services46, while Bonis et al. (2009) found that high-quality personalized services in virtual museums increased user enjoyment (PE)47. In a similar vein, Elliot et al. (2013) confirmed that quality interactions in virtual tourism platforms strengthen positive emotional experiences, highlighting the affective dimension of service quality40.

Taken together, these studies underscore that service quality not only enhances perceived usefulness and ease of use but also fosters enjoyment in digital tourism environments.

Perceived ease of use (PEU), perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived enjoyment (PE)

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) identifies perceived ease of use (PEU) as a fundamental determinant of perceived usefulness (PU), positing that technologies perceived as easier to use are also judged as more useful48. This logic has been consistently supported and extended in later frameworks such as UTAUT, which emphasizes the predictive role of effort expectancy for performance expectancy49. In the context of online travel services, empirical evidence confirms this relationship: Luo et al. (2007) showed that the ease of navigating the Lonely Planet website significantly enhanced users’ perceptions of its utility for information search and trip planning50. Similarly, Chen & Li (2020) found that the operational simplicity of online booking systems lowered users’ cognitive burden, thereby reinforcing their evaluations of usefulness51, while Lin et al. (2020) reported the same mechanism in the use of mobile applications for travel information search52. Together, these findings indicate that PEU is a consistent antecedent of PU across tourism-related digital contexts.

Beyond its impact on usefulness, PEU also influences users’ hedonic experience. Research on intrinsic motivation and hedonic systems suggests that effortless interaction reduces cognitive load, allowing users to experience enjoyment and flow states53,54. Empirical studies support this mechanism: Hsu et al. (2012) demonstrated that ease of use fosters perceived playfulness and flow on websites, thereby enhancing enjoyment55. McLean et al. (2020) further observed in the m-commerce setting that higher ease of use evokes more positive affective responses, including enjoyment, which strengthen continued engagement56. Similarly, Nascimento et al. (2018) found that ease of use enhanced the fun associated with wearable technology, ultimately increasing users’ continuance intention. These findings consistently suggest that PEU is not only instrumental in shaping usefulness but also critical for generating enjoyment57.

In the context of the Porcelain Temple Online Museum, effortless interaction with the platform is expected to help visitors recognize its informational value while simultaneously fostering a more engaging and enjoyable experience. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Travel intention (TI)

Within the TAM framework, both PU and PEU are core predictors of users’ behavioral intention to adopt a technology25. Extensive empirical research has confirmed this mechanism across diverse contexts. For example, Chen & Li (2020) demonstrated that both PU and PEU significantly determined college students’ intentions to book tourism products online51. Similarly, Luo et al. (2007) found that users’ acceptance of the Lonely Planet website was shaped by the perceived practical benefits and operational simplicity of the platform50. In mobile tourism contexts, Lin et al. (2020) likewise reported that both usefulness and ease of use enhanced travelers’ willingness to use smartphone applications for information search52. These results consistently highlight the central role of PU and PEU in shaping intention to use tourism-related digital technologies.

In addition to utilitarian beliefs, hedonic perceptions also play a crucial role in predicting intention. Van der Heijden (2004) showed that in hedonic information systems, PE often exerts a stronger influence on intention than usefulness54. Supporting this, Hsu et al. (2012) confirmed that website playfulness and flow significantly boosted purchase intention through their impact on enjoyment55. McLean et al. (2020) further highlighted that enjoyment derived from mobile commerce applications strongly predicted continued engagement56, while Nascimento et al. (2018) observed the same effect in the adoption of wearable devices57. In online travel communities, Casaló et al., (2010) also found that enjoyment was a decisive driver of participation and future behavioral intentions58. Collectively, these studies establish enjoyment as a robust motivational factor that complements PU and PEU in determining technology use intention.

Research methods

Research sample

Our research setting is Porcelain Temple online museum. The Porcelain Temple is one of the most ancient Buddhist temples in China. It is widely considered as the first Buddhist temple established in southern China. Since its launch, the Porcelain Temple Online Museum has quickly attracted the attention of visitors, and the online platform has a particularly high number of views. The selection of Porcelain Temple as the research context for this study is both practical and theoretically justified. As a prominent historical and cultural site in China, Porcelain Temple possesses rich architectural heritage and profound cultural significance, attracting both domestic and international tourists. The recent development of its online museum platform provides a timely and relevant context to examine virtual tourism experiences, aligning closely with the objectives of this study to explore the connection between digital and physical travel behaviors.

From a research perspective, the study leverages the integrated SOR-ISSM-TAM framework, in which SYQ, IQ, and SEQ constitute the external stimuli; PU, PEU, and PE capture the internal psychological responses; and tourists’ intention to physically visit the site (TI) serves as the behavioral outcome. Porcelain Temple’s online museum is an ideal case for this framework because it incorporates multiple technological and service features, including interactive 3D exhibits, high-resolution image galleries, guided virtual tours, and responsive user support. These features allow for a comprehensive evaluation of ISSM dimensions and TAM variables within the SOR model.

Moreover, the platform’s accessibility and engagement capabilities make it suitable for studying the mediating effects of both cognitive (PU, PEU) and affective (PE) responses on travel intentions. For instance, visitors can explore detailed virtual reconstructions of the temple, experience immersive storytelling, and interact with educational content—activities that directly relate to system, information, and service quality, as well as perceived usefulness, ease of use, and enjoyment. These characteristics ensure that the research can empirically capture meaningful variations in user perceptions and behavioral intentions, thereby providing robust and generalizable insights into the virtual–physical tourism nexus.

In sum, Porcelain Temple’s online museum represents a highly relevant and feasible research setting. Its combination of cultural significance, technological sophistication, and multi-dimensional user engagement aligns seamlessly with the study’s conceptual framework, offering a rich context to investigate how virtual tourism experiences influence subsequent real-world visitation behaviors.

Questionnaire

The content of our survey includes two sections. The first one focuses on the basic information, including 5 questions (see appendix A). The second one focuses on tourists’ virtual tourism experiences and feelings when visiting The Porcelain Temple Online Museum and it has three aspects. The first one involves the basic situation of The Porcelain Temple online museums’ information system as perceived by the tourists, specifically evaluating it from the dimensions of SYQ, IQ and SEQ32,35,37,40. The second aspect is about tourists’ acceptance of technology that building The Porcelain Temple Online Museum, focusing on the PU, PEU and PE of the platform50,53,54,57. The third aspect surveys tourists’ intentions to go on corporeal tourism (TI) after completing the online tour55–57. This study adopts a random sampling method for distributing questionnaires, and will use online platforms to find individuals who have had the experience of visiting the Porcelain Temple to fill out the questionnaire.

A structured questionnaire was designed using a seven-point Likert scale, and the survey was administered to participants in Chinese to ensure clarity and comprehension. For the purpose of academic reporting, all questionnaire items were subsequently translated into precise and accurate English, with the finalized version presented in the appendix. The data collected were subjected to statistical analysis using SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 24.0, which facilitated both descriptive and structural modeling procedures, ultimately yielding the final empirical results.

Conclusions, discussion, and implications

Conclusions and discussion

This study developed an integrated framework combining the Information System Success Model (ISSM), the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), and the Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR) paradigm to explore how virtual museum experiences affect visitors’ intentions to physically visit destinations. Using the case of the Nanjing Porcelain Temple Virtual Museum, the results provided broad support for the proposed hypotheses, with only H1a (SYQ → PU) not confirmed.

System quality

The finding that (SYQ) significantly influenced perceived ease of use (PEU) (H1b) and perceived enjoyment (PE) (H1c), but not perceived usefulness (PU) (H1a), is theoretically revealing. Prior ISSM–TAM integration studies often identified SYQ as a predictor of PU (e.g., Chen et al., 2016; Chen & Tsai, 2019)8,9, emphasizing its role in enhancing functional outcomes. However, in the virtual heritage context, technological smoothness appears to matter more for enabling usability and hedonic immersion rather than perceptions of instrumental value. This suggests that system performance is a necessary enabler but not a direct determinant of usefulness, diverging from findings in digital library and healthcare systems8,10. Instead, as TAM predicts, the path from SYQ → PEU → PU may serve as the more relevant mechanism in immersive cultural experiences.

Information and service quality

In contrast, information quality (IQ) showed strong positive effects on PU, PEU, and PE (H2a–H2c), confirming that accuracy, richness, and interpretive depth of content are central to virtual heritage experiences. Similarly, service quality (SEQ) exerted significant effects on PU, PEU, and PE (H3a–H3c), underlining the importance of responsiveness, guidance, and support in digital cultural platforms. These findings are consistent with earlier studies highlighting the decisive role of information and service quality in shaping user satisfaction and perceived value in mobile tourism and educational platforms9,61. Together, IQ and SEQ emerge as the primary drivers of usefulness perceptions, distinguishing cultural heritage contexts from more function-oriented digital systems where SYQ typically dominates.

Cognitive–affective pathways

The relationships among TAM constructs were also validated. As expected, PEU significantly enhanced PU (H4), echoing the original TAM logic that ease of use fosters perceptions of usefulness25. Moreover, PEU positively influenced enjoyment (H8), extending TAM by showing that ease of use not only facilitates cognitive assessments but also enhances hedonic engagement—an effect particularly relevant in immersive tourism, where enjoyment and playfulness are key to sustained involvement10.

Crucially, PU, PEU, and PE all significantly predicted travel intention (TI) (H5–H7), confirming the dual cognitive–affective pathway linking virtual to physical tourism. PU’s effect aligns with prior studies demonstrating that technology-mediated usefulness encourages adoption and downstream behaviors8,62. PEU’s influence on TI indicates that simplicity and intuitive design reduce barriers to transitioning from virtual to real visits, consistent with findings in e-learning adoption63. Importantly, the strong role of PE in predicting TI resonates with research on presence, immersion, and affective engagement in virtual tourism5,6, reinforcing the argument that enjoyment and emotional resonance are as critical as utilitarian benefits in motivating real-world visitation.

Integrative insights

Taken together, these results advance both theory and practice. Theoretically, they demonstrate the value of embedding ISSM dimensions within the SOR framework and positioning TAM constructs at the organism stage, thereby providing a holistic account of both cognitive and affective mediators. The unexpected rejection of H1a highlights a boundary condition: unlike in information service contexts where system quality directly informs usefulness, in immersive tourism, content and service dimensions overshadow pure system performance in shaping value perceptions. This represents a key theoretical refinement to ISSM–TAM integration in tourism.

Practically, the findings emphasize that tourism organizations should prioritize high-quality interpretive information and responsive service design over technical sophistication alone. While stable and user-friendly systems are indispensable for facilitating ease and enjoyment, it is the quality of content and visitor support that ultimately drives perceptions of usefulness and, in turn, stimulates intentions for physical visitation.

Implications

Theoretical implications

This study makes several important theoretical contributions to the literature on virtual tourism, technology acceptance, and information systems.

First, the integration of ISSM and TAM within the SOR framework offers a novel theoretical lens for examining the mechanism through which virtual tourism experiences influence real-world travel intentions. While prior studies have tended to employ TAM or ISSM independently8–10, this research bridges the two perspectives and situates them within a broader behavioral framework. By conceptualizing system quality, information quality, and service quality as external stimuli, and embedding cognitive (PU, PEU) and affective (PE) evaluations as the organism, the study provides a holistic explanation of how virtual tourism platforms affect tourists’ behavioral responses. This integrative approach advances the current literature beyond fragmented theoretical models, thereby enriching the conceptual foundations for technology-mediated tourism research.

Second, the findings highlight the dual role of cognitive and affective processes in shaping tourists’ behavioral intentions. The significant effects of perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and perceived enjoyment suggest that both utilitarian and hedonic dimensions are indispensable in explaining virtual tourism adoption. While TAM traditionally emphasizes utilitarian appraisals such as usefulness and ease of use25, this study demonstrates that hedonic elements such as enjoyment exert equally strong influence, aligning with recent calls to consider affective factors in digital experience research16,17. By doing so, the study broadens the theoretical scope of TAM, underscoring the importance of integrating emotional responses into models of tourism technology adoption.

Third, the non-significant relationship between system quality and perceived usefulness provides a meaningful theoretical insight. Contrary to earlier research in digital library services8 or mobile tourism applications9, where system quality was found to strongly influence perceived usefulness, this study reveals that in the context of virtual museums, high system quality does not necessarily translate into higher perceived usefulness. This suggests that for experience-oriented platforms such as virtual tourism, the functional reliability of the system is taken as a baseline expectation rather than a determinant of perceived value. Instead, information quality and service quality emerge as more salient drivers of perceived usefulness, reflecting the experience-centric nature of tourism contexts where the richness, authenticity, and guidance of the content matter more than technical attributes alone. This nuanced finding contributes to theoretical refinements of ISSM by emphasizing contextual contingencies in the relationships among system quality, usefulness, and behavioral outcomes.

Finally, this study contributes to the emerging literature on the virtual–physical tourism nexus. While existing research has extensively examined virtual reality as a tool for destination promotion1,2,4, few studies have systematically explored how virtual experiences translate into actual travel intentions. By confirming that both cognitive and affective evaluations mediate the influence of platform quality on tourists’ intentions, the study sheds light on the psychological pathways linking virtual experiences to physical visitation. This contribution enhances the theoretical understanding of virtual tourism not merely as a substitute for physical travel but as a complementary and potentially catalytic experience in the broader tourism decision-making process.

In sum, this research advances theory by (1) integrating ISSM and TAM within the SOR framework, (2) balancing cognitive and affective dimensions of technology adoption, (3) revealing a context-specific exception to the assumed role of system quality, and (4) extending the virtual tourism literature to the physical intention domain. Together, these contributions provide a solid theoretical foundation for future investigations into technology-driven transformations of tourism.

Practical implications

Beyond its theoretical contributions, this study offers several practical implications for tourism managers, virtual platform developers, and policymakers seeking to harness digital technologies for destination promotion and sustainable tourism development.

First, the results highlight the critical role of information quality and service quality in shaping tourists’ perceived usefulness of virtual tourism platforms. Unlike traditional IS contexts where system quality often dominates usefulness perceptions, this study shows that in virtual museums, tourists prioritize the richness, authenticity, and clarity of the information provided, as well as the responsiveness and supportiveness of the service. For managers, this implies that investment should go beyond technical infrastructure to focus on curating engaging and credible content, designing user-centered narratives, and offering personalized guidance or interactive assistance. Enhancing these aspects will significantly improve tourists’ perceived value and strengthen their willingness to translate virtual experiences into physical visits.

Second, the findings emphasize that hedonic dimensions—particularly perceived enjoyment—play a decisive role in driving behavioral intentions. While cognitive factors such as usefulness and ease of use remain important, the enjoyment derived from the virtual experience emerges as a powerful determinant of travel intention. For platform developers, this underscores the necessity of creating immersive, emotionally engaging environments that stimulate curiosity and enjoyment. Incorporating elements such as gamification, interactive storytelling, or multi-sensory design can enhance affective engagement and ensure that virtual experiences are not only functional but also memorable.

Third, the non-significant effect of system quality on perceived usefulness provides a cautionary insight for practitioners. Tourists appear to treat system stability and technical performance as a baseline requirement rather than a value-adding feature. Therefore, while ensuring smooth performance remains essential, allocating disproportionate resources to technical perfection may not directly enhance perceived usefulness. Instead, resources should be strategically allocated toward content innovation, experiential design, and service improvement, which are more influential in shaping tourists’ evaluations and behavioral outcomes.

Fourth, the study provides practical guidance for linking virtual experiences with actual tourism development. Since both cognitive (PU, PEU) and affective (PE) factors significantly mediate the impact of platform quality on travel intention, tourism managers should position virtual tourism not merely as a substitute for physical travel but as a complementary channel that fosters destination awareness, emotional attachment, and pre-travel engagement. For example, virtual museums can function as “experience previews” that spark tourists’ interest and reduce uncertainty, ultimately encouraging them to visit the destination in person. This strategy is particularly relevant in post-pandemic tourism recovery and for destinations seeking to attract younger, digitally native audiences.

Finally, the findings have implications for the sustainable development of tourism. Virtual experiences, when well-designed, can help distribute visitor flows, reduce over tourism pressures on sensitive heritage sites, and promote inclusive access for those unable to travel. By strengthening the bridge between virtual and physical visitation, tourism managers can leverage digital platforms to balance economic growth with cultural preservation and environmental sustainability.

In summary, practitioners should prioritize high-quality content and service, design for both utility and enjoyment, and strategically use virtual platforms as gateways to physical visitation. By doing so, they can maximize the potential of virtual tourism as both a marketing tool and a sustainable development strategy in an increasingly digitalized tourism landscape.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration and provide avenues for future research.

First, the study’s context is limited to a single virtual tourism platform, the online museum of Porcelain Temple. While this setting offers rich insights into how immersive virtual experiences influence tourists’ behavioral intentions, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other types of virtual tourism, such as destination-wide VR tours, heritage sites, or adventure tourism experiences. Future research could replicate the proposed model across diverse virtual tourism platforms and cultural contexts to enhance external validity and explore potential variations in tourists’ cognitive and affective responses.

Second, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Although the SOR framework provides a theoretical basis for the directionality of effects, longitudinal studies or experimental designs could more rigorously examine how virtual tourism experiences influence tourists’ perceptions and travel intentions over time, including post-visit behavior and repeat engagement. At the meanwhile, the sample composition was heavily skewed toward younger participants, with more than 60% of respondents being students aged 18–25. Such an overrepresentation of a single age group inevitably restricts the generalizability of the findings, as the perceptions and behavioral patterns of younger users may differ significantly from those of older or more diverse populations. Future research should therefore seek to incorporate a broader demographic profile, particularly by including participants across different age groups and socio-economic backgrounds. Doing so would enhance the robustness, credibility, and practical applicability of the results, offering a more comprehensive understanding of how virtual tourism experiences influence real-world travel intentions.

Third, the study focuses primarily on general cognitive and affective constructs (PU, PEU, PE) as mediators between platform quality and travel intention. Other psychological factors, such as perceived authenticity, presence, trust, or social influence, may also play important roles in shaping tourists’ decision-making. Future research could incorporate these additional mediators or moderators to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms linking virtual and physical tourism experiences.

Fourth, the measurement of perceived enjoyment (PE) captures only one dimension of hedonic experience. Virtual tourism can elicit a range of emotional responses, including curiosity, awe, nostalgia, or relaxation, which may differently influence behavioral intentions. Subsequent studies could adopt multi-dimensional affective scales or employ psychophysiological measures to capture richer emotional data.

Finally, the non-significant relationship between system quality and perceived usefulness (H1a) suggests potential boundary conditions that remain unexplored. Future research could investigate factors such as prior experience with technology, digital literacy, or cultural differences in technology perception, which may moderate the effect of system quality on perceived usefulness. Understanding these contingencies would provide more nuanced guidance for platform design and resource allocation.

In conclusion, while this study advances theoretical and practical understanding of virtual tourism and its influence on physical visitation, addressing these limitations offers promising directions for future research. By expanding contexts, integrating longitudinal and multi-dimensional approaches, and exploring moderating factors, future studies can further clarify the complex interplay between virtual experiences, cognitive and affective evaluations, and real-world tourism behavior.
