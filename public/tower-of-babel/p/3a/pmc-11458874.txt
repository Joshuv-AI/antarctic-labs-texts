Cytokinin-related genes regulate cucumber fruit pedicel length
Authors: Shi Yane, Ding Zhuo, Sun Chengzhen, Ba Dun, Cui Haonan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pedicel length is a crucial agronomic trait of cucumbers. Fruit deformation can occur When the pedicel is too long or too short. Moreover, an appropriate pedicel length is advantageous for mechanized harvesting. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying cucumber pedicel length. In the current study, we obtained a short pedicel mutant through EMS mutagenesis and discovered that the reduced cell number was the primary cause of the shortened pedicel. Upon analyzing the hormone content, we found that the level of trans zeatin in the long-pedicel material was significantly higher than that in the short-pedicel material. Further transcriptome sequencing analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes were enriched in cytokinin synthesis-related pathways. Based on these results, the present study concluded that cucumber pedicel length is regulated by genes related to the cytokinin synthesis pathway and that differences in length result from differences in zeatin content and cell number.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75186-7.

Introduction

Cucumber (Cucumis sativusL.) is extensively cultivated in nearly all temperate countries and has a significant economic value1. Cucumbers are high in water content and low in calories and can be eaten as fresh fruits or cooked as vegetable. It is not only used as food, but also in therapeutic medicine and beauty treatments2–4. The pedicel developed from the peduncle. In the early stages of floral organ formation, the flower primordium of the inflorescence meristem produces a pedicel at the proximal end5. After pollination by female flowers, fruit growth and development gradually expanded. The flower pedicel is then transformed into a pedicel that connects the plant and the fruit, delivering nutrients for the growth and development of the fruit6. During the growth and development of cucumbers, excessively long or short pedicels can result in uneven nutrient distributions, leading to fruit bending and deformities7.

Currently, research on plant pedicels primarily focuses on the pedicel orientation and length. A recessive single gene controls the orientation of cucumber pedicels, and CsUpregulates their orientation of cucumber pedicels8. The LEAFY genes BP, KNAT2, KNAT6, AS1, AS2, ATH1, and Leafygenes are involved in regulating pedicel orientation9–13. Arabidopsis crm1/big mutants showed a reduction in pedicel and internode length. The CRM1/BIGgene encodes a transport-related protein that regulates pedicel length14. Studies have shown that ERECTA (ER) can also regulate the pedicel length of Arabidopsis thaliana, which encodes a leucine-rich repetitive receptor-like kinase15.

For cucumber pedicel length, researchers used an F2:3population to construct a genetic linkage map for QTL analysis and found three QTL loci related to cucumber pedicel length in linkage group 216. A genetic linkage map was constructed using a recombinant inbred line population for QTL analysis, and six QTL loci related to cucumber pedicel length were found in cucumber linkage groups 1, 2, 5, and 617. In melon, three loci regulating fruit stalk length have been reported, located on chromosomes 1, 3, and 8. The cytokinin dehydrogenase gene and gibberellin 3-β-dioxygenase synthase gene are candidate genes18,19. The pedicel length in luffa fruit is determined by a pair of major genes with additive-dominant effects and an additive-dominant-epistatic polygene (D-O)20. The main gene (B-1) genetic model of zucchini pedicel length involves additive, dominant, and epistatic genes, resulting in low genetic efficiency and significant environmental impact21. Amanullah et al. first conducted QTL mapping of watermelon stems and identified six QTL loci22.

The length of cucumber pedicels is an important agronomic trait that affects the appearance of cucumber fruits and is closely related to mechanized harvesting. However, research on the molecular regulatory mechanism of cucumber pedicel length is still limited. In this study, we utilized a short pedicel mutant material to investigate the molecular regulatory mechanism of cucumber pedicel length through microscopic observation, hormone determination, and transcriptome sequencing analysis. This study aimed to provide a theoretical basis for breeding high-quality cucumbers.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The South China-type cucumber variety ‘CPL’ had a pedicel length of 2 cm or more on flowering day. EMS mutagenesis of ‘CPL’ led to the discovery of a short pedicel plant. After three generations of self-crossing, a genetically stable short pedicel material was obtained named ‘CPS’, ‘CPS’ has a pedicel length of less than 1 cm on flowering day. The short pedicel is a recessive trait. Fifteen plants, each of which was ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’, were planted and routinely managed for this study. Pedicels were collected before differences in length, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C for hormone assays, transcriptome sequencing, and qRT-PCR.

Microscopy analysis and hormone determination

FAA (a mixture of 70% ethanol, acetic acid, and formaldehyde in an 18:1:1 ratio) was used to fix pedicel samples. The pedicel size and number of cells were analyzed using paraffin sections23. The cell sizes of the pedicel in ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’ were counted using ImageJ1 (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). The number of cells was counted using the Cell Counter plugin (http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij/plugins/cell-counter.html) in ImageJ. The size of the fields of counted cells was used to determine the mean longitudinal sectional area per cell and, in combination with the whole pedicel size, to calculate the total cell number.

The relevant hormones were determined using the UHPLC-MS-MS method at Biomarker Technologies (Beijing) Co., Ltd., and three biological replicates were performed for each sample.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Plant RNA was extracted using an RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used to measure RNA concentration and purity. This analysis was performed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) equipped with an RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit. Assessment of RNA integrity RNA libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded RNA Kit, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at Biomarker Technologies (Beijing) Co., Ltd.

A Perl script was used to process the raw data in the fastq format. After removing adapters, poly-N, and low-quality reads from the raw data, clean data were obtained. Clean and high-quality data were used for all the downstream analyses. Adaptor sequences and low-quality sequence reads were removed from the dataset. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome sequence. Only reads with a perfect match or one mismatch were analyzed and annotated using the reference genome. Hisat2 v2.2.1 (https://cloud.biohpc.swmed.edu/index.php/s/fE9QCsX3NH4QwBi/download) was used to map the reference genome “Chinese Long v3”24.

Analysis of Differential expressed genes (DEGs)

Gene expression levels were quantified using the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped. The DEGs between the two conditions/groups were analyzed using DESeq2 v1.0 (https://genepattern.github.io/DESeq2/v1/index.html). DESeq2 uses a negative binomial model to identify differential expression in the digital gene expression data. Benjamini and Hochberg’s p-values were adjusted to control for false discovery rate. A differentially expressed gene had an adjusted p-value of less than 0.01, as determined by DESeq2.

DEGs enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis of differential expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using the GOseq R package, which is based on the Wallenius non-central hypergeometric distribution, to assess the statistical enrichment of DEGs in KEGG pathways25.

qRT-PCR

After extracting total RNA, the genomic DNA was removed. A Transcript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Japan) was used to synthesize the first strand of cDNA. For qRT-PCR analysis, gene-specific primers were used with the SYBR Ex Taq II Kit (Takara, Japan) on a StepOnePlusTM real-time PCR machine (ABI). CsActin (CsaV3_2G018090) was used as the internal control, three biological replicates were performed for each gene. The gene IDs for qRT-PCR are provided in Supplementary Table 3. The relative expression level of each gene was calculated using the comparative Ct method.

Statistics analysis

A two-tailed Student’s t-test (P < 0.01) and Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05) were used for the statistical analysis of the data using IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0.

Results

Cytokinin is responsible for the difference in pedicel length

The phenotypes of the ‘CPL’ and short pedicel mutant ‘CPS’ are depicted in Fig. 1A. On the day of flowering, the pedicel length of ‘CPL’ was 1.8 ± 0.3 cm, while that of ‘CPS’ was 0.3 ± 0.1 cm. Thus, the pedicel length of ‘CPL’ was longer than ‘CPS.’

We evaluated paraffin-embedded pedicel tissue samples obtained from ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’ on the day of flowering. In transverse cut, cell lengths of pedicel in ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’ were 150 and 143 μm, and cell numbers were 0.18 × 104 and 0.15 × 104, respectively (Fig. 1B and D). In longitudinal cut, cell lengths of pedicel in ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’ were 100 and 120µm, and cell numbers were 1.49 × 104 and 0.22 × 104, respectively (Fig. 1C and E).

In this study, we determined the levels of phytohormones in the pedicel. It was found that the levels of indole-3-methyl acetate, trans-zeatin nucleoside, gibberellin A4, gibberellin A3, amino cyclopropane carboxylic acid, and dehydroamino acid were significantly different between ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’. The concentration of trans-zeatin nucleoside in the pedicel of ‘CPL’ was substantially higher than that in ‘CPS’ (Fig. 1F).

To further investigate the factors influencing the length of pedicel, in this study, the cytokinin analogue 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA) was utilized to treat ‘CPS’, while the cell division inhibitor Lovastatin was applied to ‘CPL’, distilled water served as the control treatment. The concentration for all treatments was set at 10 µmol/L, with applications initiated at the onset of female flower development. Results demonstrated that treatment with 6-BA resulted in a pedicel length of 1.0 ± 0.2 cm for ‘CPS’, which was significantly greater than that observed in control plants (0.3 ± 0.1 cm). Conversely, following Lovastatin treatment, ‘CPL’ exhibited a pedicel length of 0.8 ± 0.3 cm, markedly lower than that of control plants (1.8 ± 0.3 cm) (Fig. 1G). In summary, we hypothesized that the disparity in pedicel length between ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’ is attributed to variances in cell number induced by cytokinin.

Analysis of RNA-seq data

To investigate the molecular mechanisms regulating the length of cucumber pedicels, we conducted transcriptome sequencing of the ‘CPL’ and ‘CPS’ pedicels. The original reads were obtained by sequencing and reads containing connectors and redundant and low-quality reads were removed. A total of 38.73 Gb of clean data were obtained for six samples, each containing 6.05 Gb of clean data. The percentage of bases with adequate data quality higher than 30 ranged from 93.3–95.68%, and the rate of filtered sequence bases GC ranged from 43.71–44.25% (Table S1).

The results revealed that the efficiency of comparing reads to the reference genome of each sample ranged from 94.61 to 97.46%. In addition, over 80% of the clean reads in each sample were uniquely mapped, and the proportion of clean reads with multiple mappings did not exceed 4.98% (Table S2). These results indicated that a high proportion of reads were successfully mapped to the genome, and the location information of a significant number of reads on the reference genome could be obtained. The proportion of reads in the intron, exon, and intergenic regions of the genome were calculated. The results showed that the proportion of reads aligned to the exon region in all mapped reads ranged from 87.15 to 89.22%. The proportion of reads targeted to intronic areas of all mapped reads ranged from 5.82 to 5.85%, and the proportion of reads targeted to intergenic regions in all mapped reads ranged from 5.51 to 7.09% (Fig. S1).

To compare gene expression between long and short pedicels, we identified differential genes using the DESeq2 software. In comparing long and short pedicels, 242 DEGs were identified using a screening criterion of Fold Change ≥ 2 and FDR < 0.01. Among these, 69 genes were upregulated, and 173 were downregulated (Fig. S2).

Functional classification of Differential expressed genes (DEGs)

To understand the function of DEGs, GO enrichment analysis was performed for biochemical processes, and the results showed that DEGs were mainly enriched in phloem development and cytokinin biosynthesis (Fig. 2A). The DEGs were primarily enriched in the plasma membrane and whole membrane components (Fig. 2B). In terms of molecular function, the DEGs were mainly concentrated in cysteine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity, transmembrane transporter activity, and calcium ion binding (Fig. 2C). The upregulated DEGs primarily focused on hydroxy lipoprotein biosynthesis, positive regulation of transcription from DNA templates, nuclei, and DNA binding (Fig. 2D-F). Downregulated DEGs were mainly concentrated in phloem development and cytokinin biosynthesis, plasma membrane and membrane components, cysteine-endopeptidase inhibitor activity, and transmembrane transporter activity (Fig. 2G-I).

The cytokinin metabolic pathway is key to the regulation of pedicel length

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were primarily enriched in 20 metabolic pathways. Among these, DEGs were mainly concentrated in zeatin biosynthesis, cyanoamino acid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, vitamin B6 metabolism, and the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis endocytosis pathway (Fig. 3A). Among these pathways, zeatin biosynthesis had the highest q value. Hormone measurements showed differences in cytokinin content between short and long pedicels. In this pathway, we focused on four DEGs: CsaV3_1G040500, CsaV3_6G018570, CsaV3_4G028950, and CsaV3_3G013770 (Fig. 3B). Upregulation of cytokinin dehydrogenase gene expression results in a reduction in trans-zeatin content. This caused the emergence of a short-pedicel phenotype, which aligns with the microscopic observations and hormone assays (Fig. 3C).

The results showed that the relative expression levels were consistent with the expression trends observed in transcriptome data.

Discussion

The pedicel serves as a bridge between the fruit and plant, providing a channel for transporting nutrients to the fruit during its growth and development. If the pedicel is too long or too short, the fruit will bend and produce a deformed fruit. Pedicel length in cucumbers is an essential parameter in molecular genetics and cucumber breeding.

NtBPL, the tobacco homolog of BP, has been shown to play a role in pedicel elongation27,28. In vegetable crops, such as Cucurbita pepo and Luffa cylindrica, studies have indicated that two pairs of major-effect genes control the genetic pattern of pedicel length21,29. Furthermore, researchers have successfully mapped CsFnl7.1, which is responsible for regulating fruit neck length in cucumber30. In melon, the cytokinin dehydrogenase gene and the gibberellin 3-β-dioxygenase synthase gene have been identified as candidate genes regulating pedicel length18,19. Based on the research, it is suggested that hormones such as auxin, gibberellin, and cytokinin may exert influence on the length of plant pedicel through the regulation of cell growth or division. This study has revealed a significant role of cytokinin in modulating the length of cucumber pedicel, however, further inquiry is warranted to elucidate the potential involvement and underlying mechanisms of action of other hormones such as auxin and gibberellin in regulating cucumber pedicel.

Cytokinin is a plant hormone that plays a crucial role in various stages of plant development, such as seed germination and senescence31–34. They can be classified as isoprenoids or aromatic cytokinins depending on their N6-side chains. Trans-zeatin (tZ) is essential for higher plants because of its widespread occurrence and potent activity. Additionally, other isoprenoid cytokinins such as N6-(D2-isopentenyl) adenine (iP), dihydrozeatin (DHZ), and cis-zeatin (cZ) are commonly found in most plant tissues35.

Zeatin, a naturally occurring cytokinin found in plants, downregulates enzymes involved in the zeatin synthesis pathway, including cytokinin dehydrogenase, adenylate dimethylallyl transferase, and cis-zeatin O-glucosyltransferase. Furthermore, irreversible degradation of cytokinins, such as isopentenyl adenosine, zeatin, and their nucleosides, occurs through oxidative side chain cleavage facilitated by the action of cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase (CKX)36.

The regulation of pedicel length is critically mediated by cytokinin oxidase, which controls the cell number in the pedicel, similar to the regulatory mechanism underlying the dwarf trait in cucumbers37. Initially discovered in tobacco38, As a result, the first CKX gene (ZmCKX1) was isolated from maize39. In various plant species, CKX proteins of varying sizes are encoded by small gene families. For example, Arabidopsis has seven CKX genes, rice has eleven CKX genes, maize has thirteen CKX genes, and wheat has thirteen CKX genes40,41. Rice yield can be improved by reducing the expression of cis-zeatin O-glucosyltransferases, such as tillering number, spike number, total grain number per spike, and grain weight42.

According to a previous study37, cucumber dwarfism has been linked to CKX. Similarly, research on melon suggests that CKX controls shorter internodes. The ‘Tibetan dwarf’ wheat is known to accumulate high levels of cytokinin, which is regulated by cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase (CKX)43–45. Cytokinin molecules are irreversibly degraded by CKX46. CKX enzymatic activity was reduced in developing spikelets of barley plants that had undergone genetic modification. Additionally, the average height of these GMO plants is lower than that of their wild-type counterparts47.

This study found that reducing cell number is the morphological basis for shortening cucumber pedicel length. Trans-zeatin is a crucial hormone that regulates the cell number. Transcriptome sequencing revealed that the cytokinin synthesis pathway is a key pathway that regulates pedicel length. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for further analysis of the molecular regulatory mechanisms of cucumber pedicel length.
