An integrated deep learning and large language model for automated knee osteoarthritis grading and reporting from the Vietnam Osteoporosis Study
Authors: Dinh T Nguyen, Duy K Hoang, Linh N Luu, An T Truong, Hien Q Kha, Huy T Tran, Phu D Ho, Thach S Tran, Sai Ho Ling, David J Hunter, Lan T Ho-Pham, Tuan V Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent joint condition affecting multiple structures of the knee. Clinicians need reliable tools for early detection and accurate grading to support timely management, but radiographic grading remains subjective and time-consuming. We aimed to develop a deep learning and large language model (LLM) hybrid system for KOA severity classification and automated clinical reporting.MethodsWe analysed bilateral knee radiographs from 2435 adults (≥40 years) in the Vietnam Osteoporosis Study (VOS), using the Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) scale. Data were split into training (N = 1944) and independent testing (N = 491) sets. A hybrid deep learning pipeline combined knee-region object detection with KL grading, screening and feature localization. Outputs were integrated into a Gemini LLM module to generate structured clinical reports.ResultsThe hybrid model achieved 79.1% accuracy and AUC 88.8% for five-class KL grading, and 89.5% accuracy (sensitivity: 87.5%, specificity: 90.2%) for KOA screening. The localization module consistently highlighted KOA-relevant features, including osteophytes, joint space narrowing, and subchondral sclerosis. The Gemini module converted model outputs into coherent, structured reports and provided context-aware answers to clinician queries grounded in the imaging results.ConclusionsWe developed a deployable deep learning–Gemini system for KOA assessment. Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is now widely recognized as an inflammatory joint disease rather than simply a degenerative condition, affecting cartilage, bone, ligaments, synovium, and periarticular muscles [1]. Current treatments remain suboptimal, making timely and accurate assessment of KOA severity essential for effective management and intervention [2,3]. Globally, osteoarthritis affected approximately 595 million people in 2020 (7.6% of the population) [4], with knee OA cases projected to rise by nearly 75% by 2050 [5]. In Australia, over 2.1 million people live with osteoarthritis [6], while Vietnam reports a notably high radiographic knee OA prevalence of 34.2% among adults aged ≥40 years [7] underscoring an urgent public health challenge.

Clinically, KOA diagnosis relies on symptoms such as chronic pain, stiffness, and functional limitation, with plain radiographs used for confirmation or severity grading via the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) system when needed [8,9]. While considered the gold standard, this approach struggles in high-volume clinical settings due to radiologist burnout from heavy workloads, long hours, and administrative demands, increasing risks of diagnostic errors [10]. These challenges are particularly acute in resource-limited settings like Vietnam, where radiologist shortages exacerbate the issue [11].

Artificial intelligence (AI) offers a promising way to reduce workload, improve consistency, and support decision-making in musculoskeletal imaging [[12], [13], [14]]. Existing deep learning models (eg, VGG, DenseNet, ResNet networks) have achieved strong performance in KL grade prediction on datasets from North America and Europe [[15], [16], [17]]. However, most focus solely on overall severity grading without localizing specific pathological features, such as osteophytes, joint space narrowing, or subchondral sclerosis that underpin routine radiographic assessment [18]. Feature-level identification enhances model transparency, builds clinician trust by aligning outputs with standard signs, enables phenotype differentiation, and supports longitudinal monitoring and treatment planning [19].

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT and Gemini, have facilitated the generation of clinician-oriented reports, structured summaries, and responses to queries [20]. Although LLMs have shown potential in imaging assistance, decision support, and patient communication in KOA [20,21] their integration with radiograph-based deep learning for comprehensive disease assessment remains limited.

This study introduces a novel integrated system combining deep learning with Gemini, developed and evaluated using Vietnamese knee radiograph data. It addresses key gaps by [1] training a hybrid model to classify KL severity while localizing pathological features [2]; coupling this with an LLM to generate descriptive radiological findings, structured reports, and answers to clinician questions; and [3] evaluating performance and clinician feedback on the system. This approach advances beyond conventional AI by emphasizing interpretable, feature-level insights and LLM-enhanced clinical utility.

Methods

Dataset: Vietnam osteoporosis study

We used data from the Vietnam Osteoporosis Study (VOS), the first large-scale, family-based cohort in Vietnam investigating osteoporosis and related conditions, including osteoarthritis [22]. Between 2015 and 2018, the study enrolled 4517 participants from 817 families in Ho Chi Minh City and nearby rural areas. After excluding individuals under 40 years old and those with missing or poor-quality bilateral knee radiographs, 2435 participants (1501 women and 934 men) were eligible for KOA analysis (Fig. S1) [Supplementary Material 1].

Each participant underwent standardized bilateral knee X-ray imaging using high-resolution digital systems. Radiographs were graded for OA severity by expert radiologists using the KL scale (0-4), with detailed annotations provided in Table S1 [Supplementary Material 1]. For model development, the dataset was temporally split at the participant level to simulate real-world prospective evaluation: images acquired before February 2017 formed the training and validation set (1944 participants), while later acquisitions comprised the independent test set (491 participants). Knee-level images were then derived from each participant's bilateral radiographs, ensuring that both knees from the same participant remained in the same dataset and avoiding data leakage between training and testing sets. Model training and KL-grade evaluation were conducted at the knee level, whereas participant-level KOA status was defined as KL grade ≥2 in at least one knee. Prior to final evaluation, validation-set images underwent manual radiologist review per clinical standards to confirm high quality (Fig. S2) [Supplementary Material 2].

All images underwent standardized pre-processing, including enhancement (contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization), normalization, and data augmentation (random rotation, flipping, brightness/contrast adjustments) to minimize acquisition variability and improve robustness.

Ethical approval

The use of the VOS dataset for research collaborations by academic research received ethical approval from the research and ethics committee of the People's Hospital 115 on 6 August 2015 (approval number: 297/BV-NCKH). The VOS data were fully de-identified and released only according to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants gave written informed consent.

Deep learning workflow

We developed the proposed hybrid framework for robust KOA recognition and grading from radiographs. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the pipeline comprises four main stages. First, radiographs were standardized using CLAHE to reduce variability from acquisition differences and improve visibility of structural features. Second, an object detection model (YOLOv11) localizes the left and right knee joints and automatically crops knee-centered regions, thereby removing irrelevant background and reducing spurious context that can confound downstream classification. Third, KOA-relevant representations are extracted using optimized convolutional neural network (CNN) backbones evaluated in this study, which learn discriminative patterns associated with structural OA changes. Fourth, a hybrid architecture combined probabilistic classification (predicting calibrated KL grade probabilities rather than hard labels) with a dedicated sub-model for localizing key pathological features, including joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis.

A flowchart illustrates our proposed method.

The imaging pipeline was coupled with Google's Gemini 1.5 API to generate clinician-oriented outputs. The LLM received structured inputs from the deep learning model (KL grade probabilities, localized feature maps, and key findings) and produced three types of outputs [1] a descriptive radiology report summarising findings and highlighting salient features [2]; a structured, PDF ready report formatted with standardised headings and [3] responses to free text clinician queries about the case, drawing on both imaging results and general medical knowledge. To minimize hallucination and ensure factual accuracy, we employed targeted prompt engineering that constrained the LLM to the provided patient-specific data and model outputs. Please refer to Supplementary Material 3 for detailed descriptions of the model architecture and training procedure. Gemini was used as a constrained reporting layer rather than an independent diagnostic model. It generated reports from structured imaging-model outputs, including predicted KL grade, KL probabilities, and detected radiographic features, and was not allowed to override the imaging prediction. This design was intended to reduce hallucination by preventing the LLM from introducing unsupported image findings beyond the structured outputs.

Performance evaluation

To rigorously benchmark the proposed hybrid deep learning framework, we evaluated a diverse set of established and state-of-the-art CNN architectures commonly applied in KOA research, including those benchmarked on large cohorts such as the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) [23]. The evaluated backbones were VGG [24], DenseNet [17], FastViT [25], ResNet101 [17], ResNet50 [17], ConvNeXtv2 [26], and EfficientNetv2 [17]. These architectures were selected based on prior work and their frequent use in both natural-image and medical-imaging studies as strong, well-established feature extractors. We used them as competitive baselines to benchmark performance and to contextualise the gains achieved by the proposed model. Each model was trained and tested under the same protocol, and performance was assessed and ranked based on its ability to classify KOA severity from radiographic images. Diagnostic performance of the classification models was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC and Brier score [Supplementary Material 3]. Five class accuracy and macro averaged area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were computed for KL grading, while binary metrics were calculated for OA screening (KL ≥ 2 vs KL < 2) [27,28].

The system was deployed as a prototype image-upload application for clinician interaction. Preliminary qualitative feedback was obtained from three clinicians from Pham Ngoc Thach University of Medicine with clinical experience (over 20 years) in musculoskeletal medicine, who were independent from the original image-labelling rheumatologists. The generated reports were assessed based on clinical experience and medical knowledge, focusing on report structure, coherence, logical consistency, and discrepancies between the original imaging-model outputs and the generated text. No formal numerical rating scale was used. Qualitative summaries of clinician impressions are reported in the Results section.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The training set included 1944 participants, and the independent test set comprised 491 individuals. Both sets were demographically and clinically comparable. Women predominated (61.9% in training; 60.7% in testing), with mean age slightly higher in the training set (55.9 ± 9.5 years) compared to the testing set (54.5 ± 9.8 years). Height, weight, and BMI were nearly identical across sets. Smoking prevalence was higher in training (16%) than testing (9%), likely due to sampling variation (Table 1).

Radiographic distribution followed the intended ∼80:20 temporal split (79% training; 21% testing). Prevalence of radiographic KOA (KL ≥ 2) was comparable (23% training; 26% testing). KL grade distributions were similar: KL 0 predominated (62.6% training; 61.2% testing), with progressively fewer cases in higher grades.

Model performance

In the independent test set (N = 962), the proposed hybrid model achieved 79% multi-class accuracy for KL grading (0–4), correctly classifying 761/962 cases. Performance was strongest for KL 0 (95%; 561/589) and KL 2 (80%; 143/179) (Fig. 2). Most errors occurred between adjacent grades, particularly moderate (KL 3: 64%; 30/47) and severe (KL 4: 55%; 12/22). The Doubtful (KL 1) class remained challenging (12% sensitivity; 15/125), often misclassified as KL 0 or 2, consistent with its borderline nature.

Concordance between clinical & AI prediction in classification and screening tasks.

For binary OA screening (KL ≥ 2 vs KL < 2), accuracy was 90% (862/962) with 87% sensitivity (216/248) and 91% specificity (646/714); false positives were ∼10% (68/714) and false negatives 13% (32/248).

Compared with evaluated backbones (VGG, DenseNet, ResNet-50/101, EfficientNetV2, ConvNeXtV2, and FastViT), the proposed model achieved the best overall performance for five-class KL grading, with the highest accuracy (79%), F1-score (76%) and AUC (89%), alongside the best calibration (Brier score 8.4%) (Table S2) [Supplementary Material 7]. Class-level analysis showed a marked improvement for mild KOA (KL 2), with sensitivity 81%, exceeding all baselines (26–62%). Performance for moderate and severe grades remained comparable to other models (64% and 55%, respectively), while doubtful KL remained challenging across architectures (proposed model 12% sensitivity; 0.0–27% in baselines). The low sensitivity observed for KL grade I (12%) reflects the inherent ambiguity of this category, which represents “doubtful osteoarthritis” and often overlaps with both normal (KL 0) and early disease (KL 2). Radiographic features in this category are subtle and inconsistently defined, making classification challenging even for experienced clinicians. Consistent with prior studies, most misclassifications occurred between adjacent grades, particularly at the boundary between normal and early osteoarthritis. This limitation highlights the challenges of applying discrete grading systems to a continuum of disease severity.

For binary OA screening, the proposed model achieved ∼90% accuracy with the highest sensitivity (88%) and F1-score (81%), indicating improved case-finding, albeit with lower specificity (90%) and precision (76%) than some backbones, consistent with a screening-oriented operating point that prioritises fewer missed OA cases.

Fig. 3 illustrates the model's interpretability across representative cases. Grad-CAM heatmaps consistently focused on clinically relevant regions (tibiofemoral joint space and margins). The feature-localization module identifies key OA signs that increase with severity, including osteophyte in mild cases, combined osteophytes and joint space narrowing in moderate cases, and added subchondral sclerosis in severe cases. These visualizations align model decisions with standard radiographic criteria, enhancing transparency and clinical trust.

Report generation by Gemini

The Gemini 1.5 module effectively converted model outputs (KL probabilities, localized features) into natural-language reports. Descriptive reports were coherent, aligned with predictions and detected lesions, and required only minor edits for clarity in a small proportion of cases. Structured PDF-ready reports adhered to conventional radiology formats including demographics, technique, findings, impression, recommendations.

For clinician queries (eg, “What is the KL grade?” “Which features indicate OA?”), responses accurately incorporated model results and provided evidence-based management suggestions drawn from general knowledge (Fig. S5) [Supplementary Material 6]. Preliminary qualitative clinician feedback suggested that the generated reports were generally coherent and consistent with the structured model outputs and visual findings. However, this assessment was exploratory and should not be interpreted as formal clinical validation. Clinician feedback was positive [Supplementary Material 6], the system was rated as a valuable screening-level decision support tool, with strong alignment between text and visual findings, high clinical relevance, and no significant hallucinations. Responses appeared context-aware and useful in preliminary qualitative review.

Discussion

This study introduces a clinically oriented framework integrating radiograph-based deep learning with a LLM for KOA assessment in a Vietnamese cohort. Key innovations include a hybrid imaging pipeline that delivers calibrated KL grade probabilities alongside localized detection of pathological features (osteophytes, joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis), coupled with Google's Gemini 1.5 for generating descriptive reports, structured summaries, and query responses. This integration enhances transparency by aligning model outputs with standard radiographic signs, addressing the “black-box” limitations of end-to-end classifiers and fostering clinician trust. The feature-localization module, visualized via Grad-CAM, enables verification against clinical criteria, supporting phenotype differentiation, longitudinal monitoring, and personalized treatment planning.

The hybrid design rationale emphasizes practical utility: YOLOv11-based knee localization minimizes background noise; probabilistic classification provides uncertainty-aware estimates over rigid labels; and dedicated feature detection grounds predictions in interpretable evidence. Notably, the model showed strong performance in identifyding mild KOA (KL 2), a clinically important stage for early intervention in which subtle radiographic changes are frequently missed. Its overall classification accuracy, discriminatory capacity, and agreement with reference assessments suggest robust performance, In binary screening, the model emphasized sensitivity to reduce missed cases, supporting its potential use for triage in high-volume or resource-limited healthcare settings such as Vietnam.

Comparisons with international AI studies, predominantly on North American/European studies such as OAI and MOST, demonstrate that our findings are broadly consistent with previous work while showing promising performance. Prior studies using ensemble CNN models, DenseNet, EfficientNet, ResNet, RegNet and SuffleNet variants, have reported variable effectiveness in multi-class KOA grading [23] [3,24,29,30]. Our model performed comparably with stronger international benchmarks, showing robust classification ability, discrimination, and agreement with reference grading. Persistent difficulty in classifying borderline grades, particularly early or doubtful KOA, mirrors finding from OAI and MOST studies and likely reflects inherent ambiguity of these radiographic categories [23].

The main new feature of this study is the LLM layer (using Gemini 1.5), which connects the deep learning model directly to real clinical work. Most previous LLM studies in KOA work with text only or use standalone models without strong link to X-ray features [[31], [32], [33]], generate reports from descriptions or MRI data [21,34], focus on general tasks with multimodal or vision-language models, not tight integration with CNN grading [35]. In contrast, this study's LLM layer represents a key advancement by acting as a post-processing interpreter specifically chained to the hybrid CNN outputs (KL grade probabilities with detected features like osteophytes, joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis); producing multiple clinically actionable outputs in one integrated system including clear descriptive radiology report, structured PDF-ready report with standard sections and context-aware responses to free-text clinician queries; incorporating safety-by-design mechanisms with careful prompt rules to stay factual, which mitigates hallucinations, a common concern in standalone LLM applications; enhancing workflow efficiency in high-burden or resource-limited settings by automating narrative drafting, standardizing communication, and enabling rapid query resolution for frontline clinicians, where specialist musculoskeletal radiology may be scarce.

This positions the LLM not as a standalone diagnostic tool but as an enhancer of the CNN pipeline's interpretability and usability. By translating quantitative, feature-localized predictions into natural-language, clinician-oriented formats, it bridges the gap between “black-box” imaging AI and practical clinical adoption, addressing transparency barriers that persist even in high-performing CNN models for KOA grading.

Clinician feedback in this study reinforced this value: reports aligned well with visual annotations, showed high coherence, and supported screening decision workflows without major hallucinations. While emerging multimodal frameworks (eg, Med-Gemini for radiology report generation or custom agents for KOA) are advancing similar directions, few achieve this tight, radiograph-to-report-to-query integration with explicit feature grounding and safety constraints in a non-Western cohort [36]. The quality and clinical utility of LLM-generated reports were evaluated through qualitative feedback from clinicians with experience in musculoskeletal imaging. The evaluation focused on several domains, including coherence of the narrative, alignment between textual descriptions and imaging findings, clinical relevance, and the presence of any unsupported or fabricated content. Although no formal scoring system was used, reviewers consistently reported that the generated reports were accurate, clinically meaningful, and required only minor edits in a small proportion of cases.

The study has several strengths, including high-definition radiographs acquired using standardized protocols, which enhance the reliability of feature extraction; rigorous benchmarking of state-of-the-art architectures against an OAI-inspired baseline; and the novel integration of a LLM to enable automated structured reporting, interactive question-and-answer functionality, and natural-language interpretation of imaging features and localizations. All of these components are seamlessly incorporated into an intuitive image-upload workflow, substantially improving clinical usability and interpretability compared with image-only AI models.

Nevertheless, the study has notable limitations. First, the absence of validation on independent external cohorts leaves the generalizability of the findings uncertain. Second, the modest sample size combined with an imbalance in KL grades, particularly the underrepresentation of KL 1 and KL 4 cases, likely constrains performance on less common presentations, although this imbalance is a well-recognized challenge in many OAI-derived datasets. Finally, because the approach relies exclusively on X-ray images, it does not account for the well-documented discordance between radiographic severity and clinical symptoms or functional impairment, which may limit the model's utility for comprehensive patient assessment or robust prognostic evaluation.

Although the LLM-generated reports were well received in qualitative clinician evaluations, formal quantitative validation against expert-written radiology reports was not performed. Future studies should incorporate structured evaluation frameworks, including inter-rater agreement analysis and blinded comparisons with radiologists and orthopaedic specialists, to rigorously assess report accuracy, reliability, and clinical impact. A key limitation of this study is the absence of external validation using independent cohorts. Although we employed a temporally separated test set to simulate prospective evaluation, the generalizability of the model to other populations and imaging settings remains to be established. Future work will focus on validating the proposed framework using publicly available datasets such as the OAI and the MOST, as well as multi-centre clinical cohorts that integrate imaging data with clinical information, such as pain scores, functional assessments, and relevant biomarkers, to better reflect real-world clinical decision-making and enhance translational potential. Clinician feedback indicated that the generated reports were coherent, clinically relevant, and well aligned with imaging findings, with no significant hallucinations observed. However, the evaluation was qualitative without standardized scoring criteria. Future studies should incorporate larger-scale, structured evaluations using validated assessment tools to better quantify clinical utility.

Conclusions

In summary, this study shows that a hybrid deep learning pipeline combined with an LLM-based reporting and Q&A layer markedly enhances both accuracy and usability in KOA assessment. The framework provides reliable Kellgren-Lawrence grading, interpretable feature localization, and structured clinician-friendly reports via a simple image-upload interface. With multi-center validation and prospective evaluation, it offers strong potential for scalable KOA screening and decision support, especially in high-volume and resource-limited settings.
