Histologic improvement predicts endoscopic remission in patients with ulcerative colitis
Authors: Ji Eun Kim, Minjee Kim, Min-Ji Kim, Eun Ran Kim, Sung Noh Hong, Dong Kyung Chang, Sang Yun Ha, Young-Ho Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Limited research has been performed to determine if histologic improvement serves as a prognosticator for endoscopic remission, a key therapeutic target for ulcerative colitis (UC). The primary aim of the study was to evaluate if histological activity could predict endoscopic remission in UC patients with Mayo endoscopic subscores (MES) of 0 or 1. In addition, we compared the clinical outcomes between histologic improvement group and active group. This research encompassed 492 individuals with UC with MES of 0 or 1, who underwent histological assessment as per the established protocol of Samsung Medical Center between January 2018 and December 2020. Participants were categorized into two cohorts based on the degree of histological activity: those showing histologic improvement and those with ongoing histologic activity. The endoscopic activity was assessed during follow-up, and the primary outcome was endoscopic remission according to histologic activity. Out of the total participants, endoscopic activity was scrutinized in 435 patients during the colonoscopic follow-up and in 146 during the subsequent one. The histologic improvement group at the index colonoscopy was more likely achieve endoscopic remission than the histologic active group. Clinical relapse was more likely in the histologic active group than in the histologic improvement group.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory condition of idiopathic origin predominantly affecting the colon, necessitating continuous management1,2. The current goal of UC treatment is to modify the natural course of the disease and prevent long-term bowel damage and subsequent disability using appropriate therapy and monitoring at appropriate intervals3–5.

The Selecting Therapeutic Targets in Inflammatory Bowel Disease (STRIDE) II committee introduced a treat-to-target (T2T) approach and proposed that the therapeutic target in UC should include clinical and patient-reported outcome (PRO) remission, alongside endoscopic remission, with Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES) of 0. In addition, histologic remission is also considered a treatment target6,7. Evidence from prior research indicates that achieving an MES of 0 correlates with a prolonged duration of clinical remission compared to those not reaching endoscopic remission6,8–12. However, endoscopic mucosal healing does not always indicate the quiescent microscopic disease13–15. Persistent microscopic inflammation, both acute and chronic, in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) has been linked to higher rates of relapse, hospitalization, colectomy and risk of colorectal neoplasia 16–27. Various studies have elucidated that histologic remission serves as a distinct treatment objective from endoscopic remission in UC, offering a superior prediction of clinical remission12,19,28,29.

The present treatment goal for UC encompasses achieving endoscopic remission with an MES of 0 and clinical remission. However, differentiating between MES 0 and MES 1 presents challenges30. For patients exhibiting minimal clinical symptoms of ulcerative colitis, adjusting therapy for individuals with MES 2 is relatively straightforward, while alterations in treatment for those at MES 1 necessitate careful consideration in actual clinical settings. In addition, histologic disease assessment has its main role in detecting the persistence of inflammation when endoscopy is negative or mildly active. In other words, when endoscopy already shows activity, histologic confirmation adds less prognostic information31. Our preceding research highlighted that histologic activity could be a significant prognostic indicator for clinical recurrence in patients with MES of 0 or 128. These findings suggest that achieving histologic improvement may be an essential therapeutic target for reducing the risk of clinical relapse in patients.

Nevertheless, the predictive value of histologic improvement for other target, such as endoscopic remission, has been less explored32. The aim of this study was to evaluate whether if histologic activity can predict future endoscopic remission in UC patients at follow-up surveillance colonoscopy. The primary aim of the study is to compare the endoscopic outcomes between the histologic improvement group and active group. In addition, we compared the clinical outcomes between the histologic improvement group and active group.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This study was a retrospective cohort study conducted at Samsung Medical Center, a tertiary institution in Seoul, South Korea, from January 2018 to December 2022. In this center, we routinely perform tissue sampling from the most inflamed areas. In accordance with our protocol, biopsies are always taken from the rectum, even with inactive endoscopic activity since 2018. Surveillance colonoscopies are recommended every 2–3 years to monitor mucosal healing at our center. A total of 492 patients with MES of 0 or 1 from 2018 to 2019 were screened (Fig. 1)28. The exclusion criteria were no or inadequate histologic examination, age < 18 years, prior colorectal cancer, prior colectomy, and insufficient follow-up for the study. Finally, a total of 435 UC patients with an MES of 0 or 1 were enrolled. The patients were divided into two groups according to the histologic activity at index colonoscopy. There were 303 patients in the histologic improvement and 132 patients in the histologic active group. At the first follow up of colonoscopy, all of the patients in each group were included respectively. At the second follow up of colonoscopy, there were 96 patients in the histologic improvement group and 50 patients in the histologic active group. Electronic medical records were reviewed to obtain variables for each patient for inclusion, exclusion, and follow-up. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2022-07-105-001). An exemption from informed consent was granted by the IRB of Samsung Medical Center because all data were analyzed anonymously. Variables and definition

The MES, a component of the Mayo score, classifies mucosal inflammation based on a 4-point scale from 0 to 3 according to endoscopic findings (0: normal; 1: erythema, decreased vascular pattern, mild friability; 2: marked erythema, absent vascular pattern, friability, erosions, and 3: ulceration, spontaneous bleeding). Endoscopy was performed by various endoscopic specialists in this center, but MES’s were re-evaluated separately by three different specialists28. These specialists were blinded to a clinical data during the study period. The pathologic findings were reviewed by an experienced pathologic specialist and recorded by the Geboes score. The Geboes score classifies structural (architectural) changes as grade 0, chronic inflammatory infiltrate as grade 1, lamina propria neutrophils and eosinophils as grade 2, neutrophils in the epithelium as grade 3, crypt destruction as 4, and erosion as ulceration as 533,34. All the endoscopic and pathologic reviewers were blinded for clinical information.

The endoscopic remission was defined as an MES of 0, and the histologic improvement was defined as a Geboes score of < 3.1. In addition, histologic remission was further defined as a Geboes score of < 2.133. Clinical relapse was defined by a change or escalation of medication, hospitalization or total colectomy due to the aggravation of UC28.

Clinical remission (CR) 2 at the time of index colonoscopy was defined by PRO, composed of a rectal bleeding sub-score of 0 (no rectal bleeding) and a stool frequency sub-score of 0 (normal stool frequency) or 1 (1 or 2 more daily stools than normal)6.

Statistical analysis

Values are expressed as the median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and the number (%) for categorical variables. The Mann–Whitney test, Chi-squared test, and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare the variables between the two histologic active groups.

Changes in endoscopic activity scores between the histologic improvement and active groups were analyzed by the generalized linear model using the generalized estimating equation (GEE) method after adjusting for the measurement time, duration from baseline and covariates determined by P < 0.2 in the univariable analysis. Clinical relapse-free survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the two groups were compared by the log-rank test and Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. The association between histologic activity and endoscopic activity at each time (first and second endoscopy) was evaluated by the Chi-squared test. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics approval statement

The study protocol conforms to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki as reflected in a priori approval by the institution’s human research committee. Written, informed consent was obtained from each patient included in the study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2022-07-105-001).

Achievement of endoscopic remission

All 435 patients had first colonoscopic follow-ups, and 146 patients had second follow-ups. The median time interval was 27.3 ± 10.4 months for the first colonoscopic follow-up and 41.4 ± 11.6 months for the second colonoscopic follow-up. During the study period, 164 patients achieved endoscopic remission, while 271 patients developed an MES score of > 0.

In the analysis of all 435 patients, the histological improvement group had high proportion of patients with endoscopic remission compared to the active group at follow up. (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S1). Forty-eight percent of the histologic improvement group and 34% of the histologic active group had endoscopic remission at first follow up (P < 0.01). Forty-three percent of the histologic improvement group and 24% of the histologic active group had endoscopic remission at the second follow up (P = 0.03).Figure 2Achievement of Endoscopic remission according to histologic activity MES mayo endoscopic subscore, HI+ Histological improvement, HI− Histological active.

Clinical relapse was also more likely in the histologic active group than in the histologic improvement group (HR = 1.37, P = 0.03; Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1). There was lower clinical relapse with patients with MES0 compared to MES1, but there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.18; Supplementary Fig. 2). Similarly, there was lower clinical relapse in histologic improvement group compared to histologic active group in MES0 and MES1 respectively, but there was no statistically significant difference (P = 0.17, P = 0.21; Supplementary Fig. 3).

Factors associated with endoscopic remission

The GEE was performed for factors associated with endoscopic remission (Table 2). In univariate analysis, both histologic improvement group and histologic remission group were more likely to achieve endoscopic remission (OR 3.6 and OR 2.5) compared to the counterpart (Geboes score ≥ 3.1, Geboes score ≥ 2.1, respectively). When excluding patients who have changed the treatment during the study period, the histologic improvement group was more likely to achieve endoscopic remission than the histologic active group (P = 0.03, Supplementary Fig. 4). There was no statistically significant difference in age, WBC, Hb, platelet and PRO-2 remission in multivariate analysis. Association between histologic activity and change in endoscopic score

The change in MES over time according to histologic activity at the index colonoscopy was analyzed. In the histologic improvement group (n = 303), 146 (48.1%) patients were in endoscopic remission, and 157 (51.8%) patients were endoscopic active at the first endoscopic follow-up. However, at the second follow-up, 55 (57.3%) patients were in an endoscopic active state. In the histologic active group (n = 132), 87 (65.9%) patients were endoscopic active at the first endoscopic follow-up and 38 (76.0%) at the second follow-up (Supplementary Table S2). When the endoscopy interval was adjusted as a covariate, the difference in the proportion of patients in endoscopic remission in the histologic improvement groups and the histologic active group was statistically significant (P ≤ 0.01).

Discussion

The goals of treatment for UC have changed over time from symptomatic remission to mucosal healing. There is a growing interest in histologic remission and improvement as an additional treatment goal due to the increasing evidence that achieving histologic remission is associated with lower rates of symptomatic disease recurrence, the need for surgery, hospitalization, and a reduced risk of developing colorectal cancer35. Several studies showed a significant correlation between histologic remission and clinical relapse in UC7,11,19,29,36,37. This relationship has been extensively documented in the scientific literature, highlighting the importance of histologic remission as a predictor of disease outcomes.

UC is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the colon and cumulative histologic inflammation has also been identified as a predictive factor for colorectal neoplasia38. Previous study shows that histologic normalization (histologic remission with no chronic change) is associated with a decreased risk in patients with UC of developing subsequent dysplasia, compared with patients without histologic normalization39. Although further studies are needed to validate the association of histologic remission and dysplasia, treating patients with UC has shifted toward tight control, with mucosal healing as the preferred goal.

Our previous study demonstrated that histologic assessments could serve as a reliable predictor of clinical relapse in patients with MES of 0 or 1, a population of significant clinical concern. As histologic disease assessment has important role in detecting the persistence of inflammation when endoscopy is negative or mildly active, pathologic evaluation may be meaningful in cases with no (MES 0) or mild inflammation (MES 1) in endoscopy31. These findings highlight the value of incorporating histologic evaluations into the clinical management of patients with UC. In this study, data was collected to examine more recent information regarding clinical relapse after the previous study and found that patients with histologic improvement had favorable outcomes, consistent with our previous study. These findings suggest that achieving histologic improvement may be an essential therapeutic goal for reducing the risk of clinical relapse in patients with UC.

We found a trend toward lower clinical relapse with MES 0 compared to MES 1, but this did not reach statistical significance. In our previous study, both endoscopic remission and histologic improvement had lower clinical relapse rates, but when multivariate analysis was performed, endoscopic remission did not reach statistical significance28. This is consistent with our previous study that histologic improvement, rather than endoscopic improvement, is important in predicting clinical recurrence. This suggests that histologic improvement is more important than endoscopic remission. Therefore, we believe it is the point, not the surface, that is more important, and it is necessary to evaluate the surface more accurately in order to accurately predict the point.

While extensive research has explored the correlation between histologic remission and clinical relapse, few studies have investigated whether endoscopic remission, another important treatment target, can be predicted by histologic remission. Our study addressed this knowledge gap by demonstrating that achieving histologic improvement significantly predicted endoscopic remission in patients with UC, providing valuable insight into the utility of histologic assessments as a tool for predicting clinical outcomes. In addition, this study is novel in the way that it is a real-world study rather than randomized-controlled trial32. The real-world study overcome the limitation of the randomized-controlled study such as selecting patients by inclusion criteria, leading to populations that are not representative of the population as a whole. In addition, this study is a long-term study the patients were followed more than 3 years.

Our study findings showed that histologic improvement was a valuable indicator for predicting both clinical and endoscopic outcomes in UC. However, several challenges must be addressed to effectively translate these findings into clinical practice. First, histologic remission is challenging to obtain. Only one-third of patients with UC with endoscopic healing in the ACT trial (Active Ulcerative Colitis Trial) had histologic remission40. Second, the required therapies and evaluation necessary to achieve this objective might be a high burden. Considering the difficulty of achieving histologic remission, there can be high burden of adverse events and costs of the therapies. In addition, for the perspective of histologic evaluation, there can be challenges including interobserver variability and the labor-intensive nature of the process. However, the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) technology offers the potential to address these issues and streamline the histologic evaluation process in UC31. The development of AI-driven tools for prediction has spurred increased efforts in the realm of histologic remission. Our study could serve as a further robust underpinning within this emerging trend.

One strength of this study is the systematic sampling of rectum since 2018 based on a standardized protocol. In the patients with endoscopic remission or less active disease, the histologic degree of inflammation in UC is homogeneous and variability is low in the rectum41,42. It suggests that a single biopsy fragment obtained in the rectum even with UC patient with pancolitis is likely to have the same degree of histologic inflammation in the rectum and in other colonic segments41. In practice, biopsy by segment can be burdensome for patients, histopathologists, and endoscopists, and cost-expensive for patients. By following the protocol in our hospital, the burden and cost can be reduced.

This study had limitations. First, retrospective design conducted in a single tertiary center may have led to incomplete data collection and bias. There is incomplete data for the 57 patients out of 492 patients (11.6%) for the followed-up. However, we found that 435 out of 492 patients (88.4%) identified on the index date underwent follow-up colonoscopies, and this relatively high proportion of patients undergoing follow-up colonoscopies could offset the limitations of our retrospective study. Second, even though we analyzed a relatively large number of patients as a whole, the sample size was relatively small when dividing them into MES 0 and 1. In addition, there was few clinical relapses when analyzing the subgroup. These factors may have contributed to the failure to reach statistical significance when analyzing the clinical relapse in each subgroup. However, it is not easy to distinguish between MES 0 and 1, and the importance of histologic improvement in actual clinical practice is thought to be higher in patients with MES 0 and 1. Third, inter-observer differences in recording the MES may exist. However, the involvement of three expert gastroenterologists in the review process helped to reduce inter-observer variability and enhance the robustness of the findings. Fourth, intraobserver variability may exist in reviewing the pathology specimen. As histologic remission is not a formal target in STRIDE II, there is no standard guideline for the pathology review. As there are several indicators for pathology review such as Nancy Index, Robarts Histopathologic Index, and Geboes score, there may be different scoring system in other hospitals. However, the pathologist in our hospital reviewed the pathology data several times for this study and integrated the data to the Geboes score. Fifth, there is a significant variation of UC monitoring. In the past, regular endoscopy was recommended for cancer surveillance. Now that patients are being treated with the T2T approach, it is thought that regular endoscopy is necessary to confirm endoscopic remission, the goal of treatment, but there is no consensus on what interval is best. Endoscopy is a burdensome procedure so if histologic remission is predictive of future endoscopic remission, it may help determine the interval between endoscopic follow-up. Sixth, there is a lack of data on biomarkers such as fecal calprotectin. When we developed the biopsy protocol and designed the study to evaluate the significance of endoscopic improvement, fecal calprotectin had not been established as a therapeutic target. As a result, we did not routinely monitor fecal calprotectin, although it is now part of our standard follow-up. It would be beneficial if simple markers could predict both endoscopic and clinical remission. Further studies in this area are warranted.

This study emphasized the potential of histologic improvement as a treatment goal, whereas most previous studies investigated the association between clinical relapse and histologic activity. This study provided not only information on the clinical effects of histologic activity but also on the endoscopic changes associated with it. This information could enable us to appropriately apply the T2T to clinical practice.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that patients with histologic improvement are more likely to achieve endoscopic remission than those with histologic activity. Therefore, evaluating histologic activity can help to predict endoscopic relapse as well as clinical relapse.
