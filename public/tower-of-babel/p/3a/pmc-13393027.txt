Advances in Metal–Organic Frameworks for High-Performance Direct Air Capture of CO2
Authors: Weifeng Chen, Dandan Han, Kaihui Jiang, Xuan Tian, Zhenhua Xie, Yizhu Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The escalating climate crisis demands gigaton-scale carbon dioxide removal, positioning Direct Air Capture (DAC) as a critical technological frontier. However, its widespread deployment is constrained by a fundamental trilemma involving cost, energy consumption, and sorbent longevity. Overcoming this challenge necessitates transformative material innovation. This review comprehensively examines recent breakthroughs in the molecular engineering of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), emphasizing their application for the precise recognition of CO2 in DAC. We analyze material-level design strategies aimed at enhancing key performance indicators, i.e., CO2 capacity, selectivity, sorption kinetics, and cyclic stability. A comparative assessment is presented to elucidate inherent performance trade-offs, delineating a clear cost-performance landscape to guide rational material selection. Finally, we outline emerging frontiers and future research trajectories, including intelligent adaptive sorbents, integrated capture-conversion systems, and AI-accelerated discovery. This review aims to serve not only as a snapshot of the current state of the art but also as a strategic roadmap for developing the economically viable, gigaton-scale systems imperative for climate mitigation.

Introduction

The rising concentration of atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) poses a critical environmental threat, intensifying the urgency for developing innovative carbon removal strategies. − In this context, Direct Air Capture (DAC) has emerged as an indispensable component of comprehensive carbon management portfolios, vital for addressing distributed emissions and achieving net-negative carbon goals. − DAC’s unique value lies in its ability to extract CO2 directly from ambient air, independent of emission sources, thus complementing point-source capture technologies. − However, the widespread deployment of DAC is hampered by a persistent trilemma: high costs, substantial energy demands for sorbent regeneration, and limited long-term material stability. − Although solid amine-based sorbents are currently the most widely deployed in DAC, they face inherent limitations, including moderate adsorption kinetics and high energy penalties during regeneration. − For instance, regenerating conventional amine-functionalized materials typically requires 80–120 °C of thermal energy, which accounts for a major portion of DAC’s operational cost. − This energy intensity remains a primary barrier to reducing the levelized cost of CO2 capture, a key metric for economic viability at scale. Furthermore, the cyclic stability of these sorbents under real-world conditions, such as fluctuating humidity and exposure to atmospheric contaminants, remains a key limitation. − These challenges are driving a strategic pivot in research from conventional materials toward advanced solid sorbents engineered for superior efficiency, lower regeneration energy, and enhanced durability. −

Among emerging porous materials, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) occupy a leading position for next-generation DAC, owing to a unique combination of structural and chemical properties. − Their defining advantage is exceptional synthetic tunability, originating from a modular architecture of metal-ion nodes bridged by organic linkers. − This inherent designability enables precise control over pore geometry, volume, and chemical functionality. − As a result, MOFs can be rationally engineered to exhibit three critical attributes for DAC: (1) ultrahigh specific surface areas, as exemplified by NU-110E (7140 m2·g–1), where the mesoporous framework provides a high density of sites, directly coupling ultrahigh surface area with abundant strong-binding centers to sustain a practically meaningful gravimetric CO2 uptake under the ultralow pressure of DAC.; (2) molecular-sieving apertures that discriminate CO2 (3.3 Å) from larger atmospheric gases such as N2 (3.64 Å); and (3) tailored binding sites, including amines or open metal sites, that strongly yet reversibly adsorb CO2. Open metal sites, such as exposed Mg2+, Zn2+, or Al3+ nodes, serve as potent Lewis acid centers that preferentially bind CO2 through electrostatic and polarization interactions. However, this same Lewis acidity also imparts a strong affinity for water molecules. Under ambient DAC conditions,H2O can competitively occupy the sites, significantly reducing CO2 capacity and potentially triggering framework hydrolysis. Therefore, mitigating water competition is critical. Effective strategies include constructing hydrophobic pore channels, shielding metal nodes via coordinative functionalization, and selecting hydrolysis-resistant metal clusters. This “molecular precision” is a key differentiator. Unlike amorphous solid amines with heterogeneous active sites, MOFs allow for a uniform, high-density array of CO2 adsorption sites. − Such structural homogeneity not only maximizes capture capacity under atmospheric conditions but also minimizes the energy penalty of regeneration, a decisive advantage for scaling DAC technologies.

While previous reviews typically categorize MOFs by material type, this article adopts a performance-driven, system-oriented perspective distinguished by three key features. First, we critically analyze MOF designs through the lens of four interconnected key performance indicators (KPIs), i.e., capacity, selectivity, kinetics, and stability, and explicitly map them onto the DAC trilemma of cost, energy, and longevity. Second, we dedicate significant discussion to the inherent trade-offs between these KPIs and their system-level economic implications, providing a practical decision-making framework rather than merely a catalog of materials. Third, unlike reviews that focus solely on synthesis or adsorption mechanisms, we trace the entire innovation chain from molecular engineering to process integration, identifying critical bottlenecks that must be overcome for industrial translation. This integrative approach, culminating in a comparative performance matrix and a forward-looking commercialization roadmap, aims to bridge the gap between molecular-level innovation and gigaton-scale deployment. By synthesizing recent advances through this multiscale lens, this work offers not only a state-of-the-art assessment but also a strategic blueprint for developing the economical, gigaton-scale DAC systems essential for global climate stabilization. Material Advancements for MOFs in DAC of CO2

The performance and scalability of DAC systems are fundamentally governed by the physicochemical characteristics of their solid sorbents, with key parameters including CO2 uptake capacity, selectivity toward CO2 under ambient conditions, and energy input required for regeneration. − In this context, MOFs have emerged as a particularly promising class of materials, owing to their highly modular structures, ultrahigh surface areas, and the capacity for precise functionalization of pore environments with specific CO2-binding sites. − These attributes collectively render MOFs as advanced sorbents with potential for improved stability, efficiency, and energy economy compared to conventional amine-based or inorganic capture materials. The CO2 adsorption mechanisms in MOFs generally fall into two distinct categories: chemisorption and physisorption. To optimize these pathways, the material innovation for MOFs in DAC is examined by distinguishing between chemisorption- and physisorption-led designs, highlighting advances in targeted functionalization and precise control over pore landscapes.

Chemisorption Category

Chemisorption involves stronger interactions where CO2 molecules form chemical bonds with active sites within the MOF structure. − Amine-functionalized MOFs are prime examples, where amine groups react with CO2 to form carbamates. This mechanism typically results in higher CO2 selectivity and capacity, especially at low atmospheric concentrations (around 400 ppm). However, the regeneration of chemisorbent MOFs often requires higher energy input due to these stronger bonds.

Given this trade-off between capture performance and energy cost, recent research has focused on concerted material strategies, including the functionalization of organic linkers, the deliberate engineering of structural defects, and the precise tuning of pore aperture dimensions. ,, These modifications are designed to enhance the affinity and selectivity for CO2 binding under ambient conditions by optimizing both enthalpic and entropic factors, while simultaneously reducing the energy penalty associated with sorbent regeneration. Further performance gains are achieved through doping and hybridization with complementary materials, which serve to enhance framework stability and cyclability over repeated capture-release operations. − Collectively, these efforts are critical for circumventing the inherent limitations of MOFs, paving the way for economically viable and sustainable CO2 capture technologies that can improve DAC performance.

Significantly, Chen et al. demonstrated a design of amine-functionalized Zr-MOF (MOF-808) to address the trade-off between CO2 capture performance and energy cost. Their approach exploited the synergy between precise pore engineering and a water-mediated adsorption mechanism to achieve markedly enhanced capture efficiency. As shown in Figure A, the design employs the robust and modifiable Zr6 nodes of MOF-808. The study developed two distinct functionalization pathways: (1) direct coordinative grafting of amino acids (MOF-808-AA series, typically MOF-808-Lys), and (2) a novel two-step strategy involving the installation of 3-chloropropionic acid followed by covalent conjugation with polyamines via nucleophilic substitution (MOF-808 PA series, typically MOF-808-TAPA). The DAC performance was rigorously evaluated using dynamic breakthrough measurements with 400 ppm of CO2 (Figure B). While dry capacities were significant, the presence of water vapor dramatically enhanced CO2 uptake. Among them, MOF-808-Lys, exhibited a capacity increase from 0.56 mmol g–1 (dry) to 1.21 mmol g–1 at 50% relative humidity (RH). Crucially, this high capacity is coupled with excellent regenerability. MOF-808-Lys maintained a stable working capacity of 0.70 mmol g–1 over 10 cycles, demonstrating remarkable stability under realistic DAC conditions.

Figure a schematically depicts the divergent chemisorption routes of amines with CO2 in the presence and absence of water. Under dry conditions, CO2 covalently binds to form carbamic acid/carbamate pairs, limiting amine efficiency. In contrast, water steers the reaction toward ammonium bicarbonate, doubling the theoretical capacity. Solid-state 13C NMR spectra of MOF-808-Lys and MOF-808-TAPA (Figure b,c) validate this switch that dry 13CO2 dosing yields dual peaks at 161/168 ppm characteristic of carbamic species, while at 50% RH, MOF-808-Lys displays a single bicarbonate signal at 167 ppm. MOF-808-TAPA shows only partial conversion. Temperature-programmed desorption (Figure d,e) further corroborates the mechanistic transition: two desorption maxima at ∼70 and 140 °C under dry conditions merge into a single peak at ∼120 °C with humidity, confirming the formation of a uniform bicarbonate phase that decomposes at lower temperature. Thus, the water-mediated bicarbonate pathway simultaneously enhances CO2 capacity and reduces regeneration energy, offering a pivotal design principle for energy-efficient DAC sorbents. The key innovation resides in the sorption mechanism responsible for this energetically favorable release. Further studies reveal that water vapor imposes a shift in the chemisorption pathway. Under dry conditions, CO2 forms stable carbamic acid/carbamate pairs, which require higher temperatures (∼140 °C) for desorption. However, in the presence of moisture, the reaction is steered toward the formation of ammonium bicarbonate species. The bicarbonate pathway not only enhances the amine efficiency but also lowers the energy barrier for desorption. Temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) profiles confirm a consolidated desorption peak at a lower temperature of ∼120 °C for MOF-808-Lys under humid conditions, leading to reduced regeneration energy. Thus, it demonstrates that strategic amine functionalization of MOF-808, coupled with the water-mediated bicarbonate formation mechanism, enables sorbents with high DAC capacity and low-energy regeneration. It provides a pivotal design principle for next-generation solid chemisorbents, balancing adsorption strength and desorption energy.

To address the high energy cost and stability issues, Qiu et al. present a rational design of hybrid sorbents by integrating a superbase-derived ionic liquid (SIL) into a robust nickel-based MOF (Ni-MOF) for enhanced DAC of CO2. The synergy between the strong chemisorption sites of the SIL and the confined nanopores of the MOF enhances CO2 uptake from ultradilute streams, as systematically validated through combined experimental and theoretical investigations. As illustrated in Figure A, the CO2 adsorption performance of the Ni-MOF/SIL composites was evaluated under conditions relevant to DAC. Low-pressure CO2 isotherms collected at 298 K reveal a remarkable uptake capacity in the submbar regime, particularly at 0.4 mbar, which simulates ambient CO2 levels. While the pristine Ni-MOF exhibits negligible adsorption in this region, the SIL-functionalized variants, especially Ni-MOF/IL-3, achieve a significant uptake of 0.155 mmol g–1. Fixed-bed breakthrough experiments using a 400 ppm of CO2 feed further corroborate these results, where Ni-MOF/IL-3 exhibits a prolonged breakthrough time of 25.5 min, corresponding to a CO2 capacity of 0.58 mmol g–1, more than 14 times that of the unmodified Ni-MOF. Importantly, the sorbent demonstrates excellent cycling stability over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles, with facile regeneration achieved under a helium flow at a mild temperature of 353 K. These results underscore the capability of the SIL-MOF hybrid to capture trace CO2 with high capacity and reversibility.

Furthermore, DFT calculations reveal that confining the SIL within the Ni-MOF nanopores significantly alters the energetics of CO2 binding (Figure B). The SIL ion pair resides stably in the MOF cavity without direct coordination to the Ni sites. Upon CO2 chemisorption, the [HFPDO]2– anion reacts with CO2 to form a carbonate species, a process that is markedly intensified under confinement. The calculated reaction energy (ΔE) shifts from −71.9 kJ mol–1 in the bulk SIL to −92 kJ mol–1 within the MOF, indicating a substantial enhancement in CO2 affinity due to nanoconfinement effects. This energetic enhancement is attributed to the restricted geometry and optimized orientation of the SIL inside the porous framework, which facilitates stronger and more efficient CO2–SIL interactions. Thus, the integration of a superbase-derived ionic liquid into a stable MOF host creates a synergistic sorbent system that combines high CO2 capacity, rapid sorption kinetics, and low-energy regeneration. The findings highlight confinement engineering as a powerful strategy for developing advanced DAC materials.

Innovatively, Choe et al. present the synthesis and evaluation of a novel extended MOF-74 variant, M2(hob), constructed from an azine-linked organic ligand (H4hob), which is readily synthesized via a facile condensation reaction at room temperature. This ligand synthesis bypasses the energy-intensive and toxic solvent requirements associated with traditional MOF-74-type ligands like (H4dobpdc), making the process more sustainable and amenable to large-scale production. A pivotal aspect of this work involves the strategic modification of the open metal sites in Mg2(hob) with a series of diamines, a process that precisely tunes the framework’s CO2 adsorption characteristics for optimal DAC performance. As illustrated in Figure A, the CO2 adsorption isotherms of diamine-appended Mg2(hob) reveal distinct “step” pressures dependent on the diamine’s structure. Crucially, primary-secondary (e.g., nmen, een) and secondary–secondary (mmen) diamines induce this cooperative adsorption step at remarkably low pressures (∼0.1–0.6 mbar), enabling high-capacity CO2 uptake at the dilute concentrations relevant to DAC (400 ppm). The performance of these top-tier adsorbents, nmen-Mg2(hob), een-Mg2(hob), and mmen-Mg2(hob), is quantitatively detailed in Figure B, which shows their exceptional CO2 adsorption capacities of 2.60, 2.49, and 2.91 mmol g–1, respectively, under dry 400 ppm of CO2 flow at 25 °C.

An interesting finding is the enhancement of CO2 capacity under humid conditions. The CO2 uptake under humid conditions is higher than that under dry conditions because water molecules facilitate the formation of additional bicarbonate species alongside the primary ammonium carbamate chains. This unique characteristic is a major advantage for real-world DAC applications, where ambient air always contains moisture. Furthermore, mmen-Mg2(hob) demonstrates a relatively low regeneration temperature of 70 °C and excellent cyclic stability even in an O2-rich atmosphere. To address the challenge of long-term moisture stability, a hydrophobic composite by shaping mmen-Mg2(hob) with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) was further developed. This composite retains its crystallinity, diamine loading, and superior CO2 adsorption capacity even after prolonged exposure (7 days) to 40% relative humidity, overcoming a common degradation pathway for amine-containing MOFs.

Therefore, the work underscores the scalability of the framework by demonstrating a successful one-pot synthesis of Mg2(hob) directly from the monomer mixtures, eliminating the need for separate ligand isolation. This combination of high DAC performance, humidity-enhanced capacity, robust stability in composite form, and a streamlined, scalable synthesis establishes this new MOF-74 variant and its derivatives as exceptionally promising candidates for practical carbon capture from air.

To address the pore blockage and inefficient amine use of conventional amine-impregnated adsorbents, Liu et al. demonstrate the precise functionalization of coordinatively unsaturated sites in MIL-100­(Cr) using N-(2-aminoethyl)­ethanolamine (AEEA). As illustrated in Figure A, the secondary amine of AEEA reacts preferentially with the Lewis acidic Cr­(III) sites, forming a stable anchor, while the primary amine remains uncoordinated and fully available for CO2 chemisorption, showing the highly selective grafting process. This deliberate design ensures a uniform distribution of accessible active sites within the highly porous scaffold of MIL-100­(Cr), overcoming the classic trade-off between amine loading and mass transfer limitations. The resulting material (MF-Cr-AEEA) maintains the crystallinity and mesoporosity of the parent MOF, establishing an ideal architecture for efficient gas diffusion and reaction. The efficacy of this targeted grafting strategy is unequivocally demonstrated by the exceptional DAC performance (Figure B). MF-Cr-AEEA achieves a remarkable CO2 adsorption capacity of 2.05 mmol/g under realistic DAC conditions (400 ppm of CO2, 25 °C), significantly surpassing other state-of-the-art amine-functionalized materials, including those based on silica supports and other MOFs like UiO-66. Crucially, the amine efficiency reaches 0.21, a metric that underscores the superior utilization of incorporated nitrogen atoms and highlights the success of this approach in preventing the formation of inactive, clustered amine regions. Furthermore, the well-preserved porous structure facilitates outstanding kinetics, with semiadsorption times consistently under 21 min, enabling rapid CO2 uptake. The adsorbent also exhibits remarkable versatility, maintaining high capacity across a wide temperature range from −25 to 0 °C, which is critical for deployment in varying climatic conditions.

Beyond record-breaking capacity and kinetics, the MF-Cr-AEEA adsorbent addresses the critical challenge of energy-efficient regeneration and long-term stability. The coordinative grafting imparts excellent thermal resilience, with the structure remaining stable up to 400 °C. More importantly for practical DAC applications, the CO2 binding energy is optimized to allow for complete low-temperature desorption at just 80 °C within 40 min, a significant advantage over materials requiring >100 °C for regeneration. The combination of high capacity, rapid kinetics, low regeneration energy, and robust cyclability positions MF-Cr-AEEA as a leading candidate for scalable DAC technologies and establishes a generalizable blueprint for the rational design of next-generation sorbents through precise molecular-level control.

Despite the considerable advances of MOFs utilizing chemisorption for DAC of CO2, significant challenges remain, including the energy-intensive regeneration processes, long-term stability issues, and the scalable and cost-effective production of the materials. However, the trajectory of progress is unequivocally positive, driven by continuous innovation at both the molecular and engineering levels. The rational design of frameworks allows for precise tuning of CO2 affinity and lower regeneration energies. Concurrently, the development of hydrophobic composites and streamlined one-pot syntheses directly addresses stability and scalability concerns. As material design grows more sophisticated and process engineering matures, these advancements are expected to progressively overcome current limitations, paving a clear path toward the economically viable and large-scale implementation of MOF-based DAC technologies. Collectively, the chemisorption-driven MOFs discussed herein achieve high DAC capacities primarily through strong amine-CO2 interactions. A key trend is the strategic use of humidity to modulate the adsorption mechanism toward bicarbonates, as seen in MOF-808-Lys and mmen-Mg2(hob), which effectively lowers regeneration temperature. The challenge remains in further accelerating adsorption kinetics without compromising this energy advantage.

While the enhancing effect of humidity on CO2 capacity is well documented, it is critically important to delineate the operative humidity window and the associated stability implications. The beneficial water-mediated bicarbonate pathway typically manifests within a relative humidity (RH) range of 10–50%. Within this window, coadsorbed H2O molecules facilitate the conversion of carbamic acid/carbamate pairs into ammonium bicarbonate, thereby improving amine efficiency and lowering desorption temperature, as exemplified by MOF-808-Lys (capacity increase from 0.61 to 1.21 mmol·g–1 at 50% RH) and mmen-Mg2(hob). However, at elevated RH (>50%), several detrimental effects begin to dominate. Excessive water uptake can lead to pore filling and capillary condensation, physically blocking CO2 access to active sites. More critically, prolonged exposure to high humidity may induce structural hydrolysis of the metal–ligand bonds in less robust frameworks and trigger oxidative degradation of amine functionalities, forming nitrosamines or other deactivated species that irreversibly diminish capture performance. To mitigate these degradation pathways, several protective strategies have been developed, including the formulation of hydrophobic composites via polymer blending (e.g., mmen-Mg2(hob) shaped with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) maintained full performance after 7 days at 40% RH) and the design of frameworks with enhanced hydrothermal stability through strong metal-azolate coordination. These insights underscore that while moderate humidity can be leveraged as a beneficial coadsorbate, practical DAC deployment demands rigorous stability validation across the full spectrum of expected ambient conditions and the integration of protective formulation strategies to ensure multiyear operational lifetimes.

These stability considerations are intimately linked to regeneration energetics. The performance metrics in Table further enable a quantitative assessment of regeneration energetics within the chemisorption category. Among the amine-functionalized MOFs surveyed, the isosteric heats of adsorption (Qst) generally span 55–77 kJ·mol–1, consistent with the formation of covalent bonds between CO2 and amine groups. Correspondingly, regeneration temperatures typically fall within 80–120 °C. Notably, materials that exploit the water-mediated bicarbonate pathway achieve a meaningful reduction in desorption temperature without compromising capacity. MOF-808-Lys, for example, exhibits a consolidated desorption peak at ∼ 120 °C compared to the two-peak profile (70 and 140 °C) observed under dry conditions, while mmen-Mg2(hob) achieves regeneration at just 70 °C under dynamic vacuum alongside an exceptional dry capacity of 2.91 mmol·g–1. These cases illustrate that within the chemisorption regime, the capacity-energy trade-off is not immutable. Deliberate mechanistic tuning can shift the balance favorably. Nevertheless, a persistent challenge remains that materials with the highest capacities often demand more stringent regeneration conditions, and the practical energy penalty is governed not solely by Qst but also by the adsorbent heat capacity, the shape of the adsorption isotherm, and the efficiency of thermal integration within the process cycle. Thus, even among chemisorbents, rational material selection must align the binding strength with the available regeneration energy source, whether low-grade heat, electrical swing, or steam, to achieve system-level viability. This perspective directly informs the process-level considerations elaborated in Section .

A direct comparison between Climeworks’ product (Gen. 3) and MF-Cr-AEEA (solvent-regulated) in Table clearly illustrates the technology and energy gap. Gen. Three employs a MOF composed of zinc, 1,2,4-triazole and oxalate, which is assembled via a one-step solvothermal synthesis, delivering an overall thermal energy demand of 750 1000 kWh/t CO2 with regeneration. In contrast, MF-Cr-AEEA (solvent-regulated) is fabricated via solvothermal synthesis of MIL-100­(Cr) followed by amine grafting in NMP, exhibiting a regeneration energy estimated at 328 kWh/t CO2. The latter achieves a 56–67% reduction in specific energy consumption, attributable to the precise molecular control of amine binding sites and the efficient water-mediated bicarbonate formation pathway. However, it must be noted that Gen 3 has already been validated at the pilot scale, whereas MF-Cr-AEEA remains at the laboratory development stage. This comparison highlights both the intrinsic energy-efficiency advantage of rationally designed chemisorptive MOFs and the critical need for scalable engineering and long-term field validation to translate these laboratory metrics into industrial reality.

The remarkable performance of these chemisorptive MOFs, particularly their reduced regeneration energy via the bicarbonate pathway, is underpinned by a detailed mechanistic understanding made possible by spectroscopic characterization. In the case of MOF-808-Lys, the transition from dual carbamate peaks under dry conditions to a single bicarbonate peak at 50% RH directly confirms the water-mediated chemisorption pathway, while temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) profiles corroborate this switch by showing a consolidated desorption maximum at ∼120 °C, consistent with bicarbonate decomposition, rather than the two-peak profile of carbamate species. For mmen-Mg2(hob), the sharp cooperative adsorption step at submbar pressures is characteristic of ammonium carbamate chain formation, a well-established mechanism for diamine-appended MOFs. These mechanistic insights are not merely confirmatory; they directly inform design principles. For instance, the NMR-verified bicarbonate pathway implies that ideal chemisorbents should possess amine sites with local geometries that favor bicarbonate formation over carbamate stabilization, enabling both higher amine efficiency and lower regeneration temperatures. Systematic coupling of in situ spectroscopy with DFT calculations therefore enables a predictive understanding of how linker functionality, pore confinement, and humidity collectively dictate the reaction coordinate, paving the way for the design of next-generation DAC sorbents with precisely tuned binding energetics.

Physisorption Category

To mitigate regeneration energy demands, research has expanded toward physisorptive MOFs that exhibit lower energy penalties during CO2 release. In contrast to chemisorption, physisorption relies on noncovalent interactions, such as van der Waals forces, between CO2 molecules and the porous surface of MOFs. This mechanism typically affords greater reversibility and lower energy demands for sorbent regeneration. However, a primary challenge has been the generally inferior CO2 capacity and selectivity of MOFs compared to their chemisorptive counterparts. Consequently, research efforts have been directed toward designing physisorptive MOFs that do not compromise on capture performance.

A definitive experimental demonstration of physisorptive DAC at 400 ppm of CO2 was provided by Bhatt et al. with NbOFFIVE-1-Ni (KAUST-7), a fluorinated MOF that achieves a CO2 uptake of 1.3 mmol·g–1 at 400 ppm and 298 K, among the highest values reported for a purely physisorptive sorbent. Dynamic breakthrough experiments confirmed complete CO2 removal from simulated air under both dry and humid conditions, with full regeneration achieved at 55 °C under vacuum (Qst ≈54 kJ·mol–1). This work established that physisorptive MOFs can deliver competitive DAC performance without the high regeneration penalty of chemisorbents.

While not originally evaluated under DAC conditions, CALF-20 [Zn2(1,2,4-triazolate)2(oxalate)] provides critical benchmarks for physisorbent stability and scalability. Originally developed for postcombustion CO2 capture (5–30% CO2), CALF-20 is fabricated through a facile one-step solvothermal reaction between a commercially available organic linker and a zinc salt. Its exceptional CO2 capture performance under flue gas conditions is rooted in its precise molecular-level design, as definitively revealed in Figure A. It presents a comprehensive analysis combining experimental gas adsorption data with computational insights. The pure-component CO2 and N2 adsorption isotherms demonstrate a steep, favorable uptake of CO2 at low pressures and 298 K, while N2 adsorption is negligible. This indicates strong, specific interactions between the CO2 molecules and the framework, a conclusion quantitatively supported by high Ideal Adsorbed Solution Theory (IAST) selectivity values for CO2 over N2. The atomistic origin of this affinity is elucidated through the crystal structure and Grand Canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations. These simulations identify the primary binding site where the CO2 molecule (quadrupole moment) engages in optimal electrostatic interactions with both the open Zn2+ metal site and the nitrogen-rich triazolate linkers. This synergistic binding configuration, occurring within a pore geometry tailored for efficient CO2 packing, rationalizes the high isosteric heat of adsorption and the resulting exceptional selectivity, which forms the fundamental basis for its separation efficiency.

This intrinsic adsorption performance must be coupled with long-term structural integrity for practical deployment, a critical aspect validated in Figure B. The study systematically evaluates the material’s stability through multicycle dynamic breakthrough experiments under increasingly challenging conditions. It demonstrates that CALF-20 retains its original CO2 capacity and separation selectivity not only in dry streams but, more importantly, upon repeated exposure to gas feeds containing high concentrations of water vapor (1.5% and 5%) and even wet acid gas simulants containing SO2. The absence of performance degradation after numerous adsorption–desorption cycles provides direct empirical evidence for the framework’s remarkable resilience. This stability is attributed to the hydrophobic organic ligands and the robust, hydrolysis-resistant Zn-triazolate coordination spheres, which prevent water-induced pore collapse or decomposition. The result bridges the gap between fundamental capacity and real-world applicability, confirming that the material’s performance withstands the harsh, hydrous environments typical of actual flue gas streams.

In summary, CALF-20 exemplifies how physisorptive MOFs can achieve an outstanding balance of thermodynamic affinity, hydrolytic stability, and scalable manufacturability, which are attributes that are equally critical for DAC applications. Although direct experimental data at 400 ppm of CO2 have not yet been reported for CALF-20, its demonstrated resilience under harsh, humid environments and its cost-effective, kilogram-scale synthesis establish it as an industrial-viability benchmark for physisorbent development.

It is noteworthy that systematic evaluations of physisorptive MOFs under ultradilute DAC conditions are far less prevalent than for their chemisorptive counterparts, and standardized 400 ppm benchmarking data remain scarce across the field. This disparity highlights a pressing knowledge gap: the need for rigorous, side-by-side DAC benchmarking of a wider range of physisorptive MOFs under identical, realistic ambient conditions. Bridging this gap is essential to translate the industrial maturity of materials like CALF-20 into validated DAC solutions.

However, such screening is fundamentally constrained by a critical trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. The following discussion on computational screening addresses the methodology to explore this vast, underexplored design space. Lim et al. introduce a transferable machine learning force field (MLFF) framework (Figure A). The innovation centers on fine-tuning a foundation model, pretrained on diverse materials, to create MACE-DAC, an MLFF specifically adept at modeling MOF-CO2 and MOF-H2O interactions across a wide range of chemistries (Figure A­(a)). The deployment of MACE-DAC is realized through the integrated simulation package (DAC-SIM). Its workflow for calculating thermodynamic adsorption properties is depicted in Figure A­(, c). They employ the Widom insertion method within Monte Carlo simulations to compute the heat of adsorption (Qst) and Henry’s constant (KH), critical metrics for DAC performance at dilute conditions. Crucially, the MLFF approach achieves the dual advantage, i.e., it operates at the low computational cost of classical FFs while delivering accuracy comparable to DFT. This effectively decouples the traditional trade-off, enabling high-throughput screening without sacrificing quantum-mechanical fidelity.

The superiority and practical impact of the method are unambiguously demonstrated by the direct comparison with classical force field simulations (Figure B). A major failure of classical FFs is their systematic error in predicting H2O interactions, particularly in MOFs containing lanthanide metals. Quantitatively, while nearly all MOFs exhibit H2O Qst values below 100 kJ·mol–1 in the MACE-DAC-1 calculations, more than 17.8% of the screened MOFs exceed this physically reasonable physisorption threshold when evaluated with UFF + DDEC. This artifact stems from the inability of fixed UFF parameters to correctly capture the polarizability in these complex environments, leading to a drastic overestimation of H2O affinity. This systematic bias has severe consequences for material screening: the 161 promising DAC candidates identified by MACE-DAC-1 are almost entirely overlooked by the UFF + DDEC approach, as virtually none of them exhibit competitive performance under the classical force field (Figure B­(c)). In conclusion, this work establishes a robust and scalable computational platform for DAC material discovery. This allows researchers to screen over 8000 MOFs and confidently identify more than 100 high-potential candidates that were previously obscured by the inadequacies of classical force fields. It provides a transformative framework for accelerating the discovery of porous materials, not only for DAC but for a broad range of energy and environmental applications.

These computational predictions invite a direct comparison with existing experimental benchmarks among physisorptive MOFs. Although CALF-20 is the most thoroughly characterized physisorptive MOF for carbon capture, it is not the only member of this category investigated under DAC-relevant conditions. Several other physisorptive frameworks have also been investigated for low-concentration CO2 adsorption. SIFSIX-3-Cu achieves a CO2 uptake of 1.34–1.4 mmol·g–1 at 400 ppm and 298 K under dry conditions, and NbOFFIVE-1-Ni similarly delivers approximately 1.3 mmol·g–1, placing both at the upper end of the performance spectrum for physisorptive MOFs. In contrast, prototypical frameworks such as HKUST-1 and Mg-MOF-74 suffer from severe competition with atmospheric moisture, which drastically reduces their DAC performance under realistic humid air. Consequently, CALF-20 distinguishes itself within this class by combining moderate CO2 affinity with pronounced hydrophobicity, a combination that many other physisorptive MOFs struggle to achieve simultaneously.

The computational screening by Lim et al. dramatically broadens the known physisorptive landscape. Among the over 100 candidates identified by the MACE-DAC force field as possessing favorable CO2 heats of adsorption and Henry’s constants under dilute conditions, many exhibit predicted capacities and selectivities that rival or surpass those of CALF-20. These computationally discovered materials are distinguished by their greater chemical diversity, including lanthanide-based nodes and azole-rich linkers previously misjudged by classical force fields. These results reveal that the physisorptive MOF space for DAC remains far from exhausted and that significant performance gains are achievable through systematic pore-chemistry tuning. Consequently, a critical near-term challenge is the experimental validation of these top candidates under standardized DAC conditions, which will benchmark their real-world potential and refine the computational models guiding their discovery.Despite their promise for CO2 capture, the practical deployment of MOFs faces several critical challenges. , First, stability under real-world conditions remains a primary concern, particularly in the presence of humid flue gas. Second, achieving high CO2 selectivity against competing gases like N2 and H2O requires precise control over pore chemistry and size. Third, adsorption capacity often trades off with diffusion kinetics and regeneration energy, and materials with ultrahigh affinity may necessitate energy-intensive desorption steps. Finally, scalable synthesis and long-term mechanical stability under cyclic loading present significant economic and engineering hurdles. Therefore, addressing these multifaceted challenges is paramount to translating the inherent promise of MOFs into practical, economically viable, and durable carbon capture technologies. In contrast to chemisorbents, physisorptive MOFs like CALF-20 offer a distinct path with potentially lower regeneration energy, relying on optimized pore chemistry and hydrophobicity. However, their capacity at 400 ppm is often intrinsically lower, highlighting a fundamental trade-off. Materials like CALF-20, despite their proven industrial scalability, must still address this challenge for DAC deployment. The computational screening work by Lim et al. points to the vast, underexplored space of physisorptive MOFs that could balance this trade-off.

Strategies for Enhancing Key Performance Metrics

Maximizing Capacity and Selectivity at Ambient Conditions

To advance MOF-based DAC systems, a critical objective is the maximization of CO2 capacity and selectivity under ambient conditions, which are characterized by low CO2 concentrations (approximately 400 ppm) and the presence of water vapor. A prominent strategy for improving CO2 capacity and selectivity involves precise structural modification and functionalization of MOFs. Introducing specific functional groups, particularly amines, has been widely recognized as an effective approach. Amine-functionalized MOFs enhance CO2 adsorption through strong chemisorptive interactions with CO2 molecules. Notable examples include MIL-100­(Cr) modified with polyethylenimine (PEI), tetraethylenepentamine (TEPA), and diethanolamine (DEA), which demonstrate improved CO2 adsorption performance, particularly in terms of water vapor tolerance and oxidative stability. Similarly, amino-functionalized linkers in materials such as Ce-UiO-66 have been shown to increase CO2 uptake capacity and selectivity. These modifications exploit the basicity of amine groups to selectively capture acidic CO2, even at low partial pressures.

Wang et al. introduce a novel strategy of “directional deprotonation regulation” during the amine grafting process by strategically selecting different solvents. As shown in Figure A, the synthesis involves grafting N-(2-aminoethyl)­ethanolamine (AEEA) onto MOFs like MIL-100­(Cr) using solvents with distinct properties, i.e.,H2O, CH3OH, and NMP. The efficacy of this solvent-regulation approach is profoundly demonstrated in Figure B. For the MIL-100­(Cr)-based adsorbent, switching the solvent from H2O to NMP dramatically increased the CO2 adsorption capacity from 0.51 mmol/g to 2.15 mmol/g and boosted the amine group utilization from 21.2% to an ultrahigh 89.6%. This trend was consistent across different MOFs. Most strikingly, for UiO-66­(Zr), the adsorption capacity surged by 27.5-fold, from a negligible 0.06 mmol/g with H2O to 1.71 mmol/g with NMP. It provides a key insight, showing that the use of organic aprotic solvents like NMP significantly increases the pH of the MOF slurry. This creates a less acidic environment that favors the grafting reaction by mitigating proton competition, thereby enabling more efficient binding of amine groups to the metal sites. The underlying mechanism for this dramatic enhancement is unraveled through DFT calculations (Figure C). The grafting process follows a two-step zwitterion mechanism. The study reveals that the solvent plays a decisive role in the crucial deprotonation step. In the desired activation pathway, the solvent acts as a base to accept a proton. NMP, as an organic polar aprotic solvent, exhibits the lowest energy barrier for this deprotonation step (Figure C­(a)), facilitating the formation of active adsorption sites. Concurrently, NMP presents the highest energy barrier for an undesirable side reaction where an amine group internally accepts the proton, rendering it inactive for CO2 capture (Figure C­(b)). This “directional” guidance of the proton by the aprotic solvent is the core scientific breakthrough, ensuring both high grafting efficiency and maximal activation of the amine sites.

Thus, this work presents a brilliantly simple yet powerful solvent-engineering strategy to precisely control proton migration during MOF functionalization. By employing organic polar aprotic solvents like NMP, the research achieves unprecedented amine utilization efficiency and a massive boost in CO2 capture capacity under ambient air conditions, paving a highly promising and scientifically grounded path for the development of next-generation DAC adsorbents.

Beyond amine grafting, rational design of pore chemistry and topology is essential for optimizing CO2 capture performance. Pore engineering approaches, such as constructing ultramicroporous frameworks, can significantly enhance selective CO2 adsorption. An example is the development of pellet-shaped ultramicroporous MOFs like Ni­(3-ain)2 using polyvinyl butyral (PVB) as a binder, which exhibits notable CO2 selectivity. Additionally, introducing polar functional groups into the pore environment improves selective CO2/N2 adsorption. Research on aluminum-based MIL-53 frameworks with varied organic ligands further illustrates how chemical composition influences selective CO2 adsorption, underscoring the significance of structure–property relationships. Isoreticular double interpenetration represents another advanced structural strategy that synergistically enhances capacity and selectivity. By designing and constructing MOFs with systematically varied ligand lengths and their corresponding double-interpenetrated analogues, researchers have achieved improved CO2 adsorption and selectivity. This technique enables precise control over pore dimensions and increases the density of adsorption sites, both critical for efficient CO2 capture.

Computational approaches, including molecular simulations and machine learning (ML), are accelerating the discovery and optimization of high-performance MOFs for CO2 capture. High-throughput screening of hypothetical MOF databases, such as anion-pillared frameworks, can identify candidates with optimal CO2 capture characteristics. Machine learning models are being developed to predict CO2 adsorption capacity and CO2/N2 selectivity, offering an efficient and interpretable means of exploring the vast MOF chemical space. Generative AI frameworks, such as GHP-MOF assemble, have been introduced for the rational and accelerated design of MOFs with high CO2 uptake and synthesizable linkers. Moreover, computational studies also investigate the influence of binding affinity and pore size by modifying structures like MIL-101, providing insights into the overall CO2/N2 adsorption process.

To address the critical challenge of CO2 capture under humid conditions, Boyd et al. executed a computational screening pipeline of over 325,000 hypothetical MOFs to identify materials that maintain high CO2/N2 selectivity in the presence of water. As depicted in Figure A­(a), the initial screening under dry flue gas conditions (15:85 CO2/N2) identified 8325 promising MOFs exhibiting a CO2 working capacity >2 mmol g–1 and selectivity >50. The true innovation, however, emerged from a systematic analysis of the CO2-binding sites, or “adsorbaphores”, within these top performers. This analysis revealed three primary classes. Crucially, when they plotted the Henry’s constants for H2O versus CO2 (Figure A­(b)), a clear trend emerged: MOFs featuring the A1 adsorbaphore, a site comprising two parallel aromatic rings separated by ∼ 7 Å­(Figure A­(c)), demonstrated significantly lower water affinity than those with open metal sites (A3) or metal–oxygen bridges (A2). This key insight directed the synthetic efforts toward the hydrophobic A1 adsorbaphore, which provides an optimal quadrupolar interaction for CO2 while lacking hydrogen-bonding partners for H2O.

Experimental validation confirmed the computational predictions, i.e., the adsorption isotherms (Figure B­(a)) matched the simulations, and in situ PXRD (Figure B­(b)) directly identified the CO2 binding site within the A1 adsorbaphore. Crucially, the results (Figure B­(e)) demonstrate the remarkable resilience of the designed MOFs. The CO2 capture capacity of [Al-PyrMOF] was not only retained but slightly enhanced under humidity, while [Al-PMOF] showed only a minimal reduction. This stands in stark contrast to the significant performance degradation observed in commercial benchmarks. Figure B­(f) provides a direct performance benchmark, showcasing that the working capacities of [Al-PyrMOF] and [Al-PMOF] in wet flue gas meet or exceed those of zeolite 13X and activated carbon, while maintaining performance over multiple cycles. This robust performance originates from the synergistic combination of the hydrophobic A1 adsorbaphore, which preferentially binds CO2, and a pore architecture that frustrates the formation of an extensive hydrogen-bonded water network. Therefore, this work establishes a robust data-driven methodology, moving from high-throughput computational screening and adsorbaphore identification to the successful synthesis and experimental validation of high-performing, water-resilient MOFs for carbon capture.

In summary, enhancing the capacity and selectivity of MOFs for DAC under ambient conditions requires a multifaceted strategy integrating advanced material design, targeted functionalization, structural control, and computational guidance. Key research directions include amine functionalization, pore chemistry and topology optimization, isoreticular interpenetration, enhancement of water stability, and energy-efficient regeneration. Continued progress in these areas, supported by high-throughput screening and machine learning, will be crucial for unlocking the full potential of MOFs in DAC technologies.

Accelerating Adsorption–Desorption Kinetics

Efficient DAC requires not only high CO2 adsorption capacity and selectivity but also rapid adsorption–desorption kinetics to enable shorter cycling times and greater operational throughput. ,, Accelerating both CO2 uptake and release is essential for optimizing DAC performance and reducing capital costs. A primary strategy for enhancing adsorption kinetics lies in engineering the pore architecture of MOFs to facilitate faster mass transport of CO2 molecules to active adsorption sites. Hierarchically porous MOFs, incorporating micropores, mesopores, and sometimes macropores, can markedly improve diffusion rates. Micropores contribute strongly to high CO2 adsorption capacity through intensified gas–framework interactions, while mesopores and macropores serve as transport pathways that reduce diffusional resistance and promote efficient delivery of CO2 to microporous regions. For example, a family of hierarchically porous porphyrin-based MOFs, featuring large mesopores interconnected with micropores, exhibited enhanced CO2 capture performance, largely due to improved mass transfer kinetics. Similarly, optimizing MOF crystal size and morphology can shorten diffusion pathways, with smaller crystals generally enabling faster adsorption as a result of reduced diffusion lengths.

To improve desorption kinetics, often the rate-limiting step in adsorption-based processes, strategies aim to weaken CO2-adsorbent interactions after capture, enabling efficient release with minimal energy input. Tuning the strength of chemical interactions between CO2 and the MOF framework is critical. Although strong interactions enhance capture capacity at low CO2 concentrations, excessively strong binding can increase regeneration energy and slow desorption. Thus, a careful balance is required. One effective approach involves precise functionalization of MOFs with specific moieties that form reversible bonds with CO2, such as amine groups with temperature-dependent binding affinities. For instance, amine-functionalized MOFs have been engineered to release CO2 efficiently under mild temperature swings, demonstrating tunable adsorption enthalpies.

External stimuli can also be employed to accelerate desorption. Electrically driven temperature swing adsorption (ETSA) is a promising technique that uses Joule heating to rapidly increase adsorbent temperature, thereby enhancing CO2 release. Compared to conventional temperature swing adsorption (TSA), ETSA enables faster regeneration cycles and can reduce overall energy consumption. MOF/carbon fiber (CF) composites tailored for ETSA have shown rapid thermal response and efficient CO2 regeneration under DAC conditions. Such integration allows precise, localized heating, leading to swift and effective CO2 desorption.

The exceptional desorption kinetics of the ETSA process is detailed in Figure A. Figure A­(a) defines the low desorption temperatures of 70 °C (dry) and 90 °C (45% RH). Figure A­(b) demonstrates the corresponding rapid Joule heating, achieving these temperatures within 30 s, a process visually confirmed by the IR thermal images in Figure A­(c). This direct heating enables complete CO2 desorption within 4 min (Figure A­(e)) from a high adsorption capacity (Figure A­(d)), with 76% released in the first 2 min under humidity, quantifying the ultrafast kinetics (Figure A­(f)). The superiority of ETSA is confirmed by the cyclic tests in Figure B. Conventional TSA and TVSA suffer from drastic capacity loss over 10 cycles due to incomplete desorption from slow heating (Figure B­(a and b)). In contrast, ETSA maintains over 95% capacity across 10 cycles under both dry and humid conditions (Figure B­(c and d)), a stability replicated using ambient air (Figure B­(e)). Figure B­(f) systematically links this success to heating rate, showing that ETSA-level heating (90 °C/min) achieves 100% desorption in 4 min, far surpassing the 33.6% efficiency of conventional TSA (20 °C/min). Thus, the integration of a high-capacity MOF with a Joule-heating CF substrate and a hydrophobic SBS matrix creates a synergistic composite that successfully addresses key DAC challenges, including slow regeneration kinetics, high energy consumption, and performance degradation in humid environments, paving the way for practical and scalable carbon capture systems.

The strategic implications of these findings are powerfully articulated in Figure B. Figure B­(b) deconstructs the adsorption process by analyzing the CO2 uptake achieved within different time intervals. It conclusively shows that the 25.0 wt % adsorbent delivers the highest capacity within short periods (e.g., 9 min), capitalizing on its rapid kinetics, whereas higher-loading adsorbents only surpass it after significantly longer times. By identifying and harnessing the “rapid kinetic region”, the study provides a generalizable framework for developing solid adsorbents that offer an optimal balance between high CO2 capture efficiency and low energy consumption for regeneration.

In summary, accelerating adsorption–desorption kinetics in MOF-based DAC systems is essential for enhancing process efficiency and lowering operational costs. Despite the advances, a central trade-off remains between achieving strong CO2 binding for high adsorption capacity at dilute concentrations and ensuring weak enough interactions to minimize regeneration energy. Looking forward, a critical research direction lies in designing MOFs with dynamically tunable frameworks capable of adaptively modulating CO2 affinity in response to operating conditions. Moreover, computational screening and machine learning will be increasingly employed to identify MOFs with optimal kinetic properties. For instance, high-throughput computational studies allow for the rapid evaluation of a vast number of hypothetical MOFs, pinpointing structures that exhibit both high capacity and favorable kinetics. With continued advancements in MOF-based DAC systems, the accelerated adsorption–desorption kinetics are expected to unlock unprecedented efficiency, effectively overcoming a fundamental barrier to their economic viability and scalable deployment.

Ensuring Long-Term Stability under Real-World Conditions

For large-scale implementation of MOFs in DAC, long-term structural stability under realistic and harsh environmental conditions is essential. − Atmospheric air contains numerous components, such as oxygen, water vapor, and trace pollutants, that can induce structural degradation of MOFs and diminish their CO2 capture performance over time. Thus, advancing the development of environmentally robust MOFs represents a critical research direction.

Hydrolytic stability of MOFs remains one of the most pressing challenges, given the ubiquitous presence of water vapor in ambient air. Water molecules can compete with CO2 for adsorption sites, block pores, or trigger hydrolysis of the metal–ligand bonds, leading to framework collapse. To enhance hydrothermal stability, several strategies have been pursued, i.e., strengthening coordination bonds, incorporating hydrophobic functional groups, and constructing high-connectivity networks. For example, cyclodextrin-based MOFs have exhibited notable resistance to oxidation and stability toward nucleophilic hydroxides, underscoring their potential for CO2 capture in moist, oxygen-rich atmospheres. Similarly, the integration of hydrophobic moieties, such as carborane-based ligands, has proven effective in preserving CO2/N2 selectivity under highly humid conditions. Modifying the inorganic secondary building units (SBUs) to improve their hydrolytic resilience offers another viable route.

Significantly, Bose et al. present a comprehensive investigation into the suitability of N,N′-dimethylethylenediamine (mmen)-appended Mg2(dobpdc) as a robust adsorbent for DAC of CO2, with a specific focus on its structural integrity and performance stability under practical conditions. The strategic design of the material is illustrated in Figure A, which depicts the postsynthetic functionalization of the parent Mg2(dobpdc) framework, possessing open metal sites with mmen molecules. This appended diamine not only provides the chemisorption sites for CO2 but also significantly enhances the framework’s stability, a key attribute for real-world DAC applications where exposure to ambient air is inevitable. The successful synthesis and enhanced stability are quantitatively confirmed (Figure B). Figure B­(a) shows the N2 adsorption isotherms at 77 K, where the reduction in BET surface area from 2920 m2 g–1 (parent MOF) to 1550 m2 g–1 (mmen-MOF) provides direct evidence of successful diamine grafting within the pores. This is further corroborated by the DFT pore size distribution in Figure B­(b), which shows a distinct narrowing and shift to smaller pore sizes after functionalization. Most importantly, the stability under ambient conditions is directly compared in Figure B­(c and d). Besides, oxidative stability is another crucial factor, as atmospheric oxygen can degrade organic linkers or oxidize susceptible metal nodes. , Designing MOFs with inherently stable ligands, such as those with high rigidity and extended conjugation, enhances their durability in oxidative environments. Additionally, employing redox-inert metal ions or applying pore-engineering strategies to shield vulnerable sites further improves oxidative resistance. Mechanical stability is equally important for practical deployment, particularly in fixed-bed adsorption systems where cyclic loading and regeneration can cause particle attrition and increased pressure drop. Enhancing mechanical robustness can be achieved through synthesis of highly crystalline MOFs, formulation of MOF-polymer composites using durable binders, or fabrication of pelletized MOF forms. For instance, pelletizing ultramicroporous MOFs with polyvinyl butyral (PVB) as a binder has been shown to improve handling and mechanical integrity in process-oriented configurations.

Finally, trace atmospheric contaminants, such as NOx and SOx, may also compromise MOF performance through chemisorption or pore blocking. Developing MOFs with inherent resistance to such species, or with pore environments that selectively exclude them while permitting CO2 uptake, is an active area of investigation. Tailoring pore geometry and surface chemistry helps prevent irreversible binding of contaminants. In this endeavor, computational modeling serves as a powerful tool for predicting degradation pathways and informing the rational design of stable, next-generation MOFs.

Beyond trace contaminants, the coadsorption of major atmospheric constituents, most notably oxygen (≈21 vol %) and argon (≈0.9 vol %), also merits attention. O2, despite its nonpolar nature, can occupy micropores through weak van der Waals interactions and, for MOFs with open metal sites, may form transient σ-complexes that compete with CO2 binding. However, the isosteric heat of O2 adsorption is typically only 12–15 kJ·mol–1, substantially lower than that of CO2 (20–40 kJ·mol–1 in physisorptive MOFs), so the thermodynamic selectivity for CO2 over O2 remains high under ambient conditions. The greater concern is the oxidative degradation of organic linkers or redox-active metal nodes during thermal regeneration in an O2-rich atmosphere, as already noted above. Thus, the engineering of redox-inert metal clusters, oxidation-resistant ligands, and hydrophobic pore environments simultaneously suppresses O2 coadsorption and retards oxidative aging. Argon and other noble gases interact only minimally and have negligible impact on DAC performance. Overall, while O2 coadsorption does not drastically reduce CO2 capacity, its long-term chemical effects necessitate the oxidation-stability strategies already discussed in this section.

In summary, the advancement of MOF-based DAC hinges on the synergistic optimization of three critical performance pillars, i.e., capacity/selectivity, kinetics, and long-term stability. Strategic material design, including amine functionalization and tailored pore architectures, has dramatically enhanced CO2 uptake from ambient air. Simultaneously, engineering hierarchical structures and implementing energy-efficient regeneration strategies like electrical heating have successfully accelerated adsorption–desorption kinetics. Furthermore, the development of robust frameworks, validated under realistic humid and oxidative conditions, is proving essential for practical deployment. Continued integration of these advanced synthetic strategies with data-driven computational guidance will be paramount for transitioning high-performing MOFs from the laboratory to scalable, economically viable DAC technologies.

Performance Crossroads: Comparative Analysis and Inevitable Trade-offs

DAC of CO2 represents a promising strategy for mitigating global climate change, the success of which hinges critically on the development of high-performance adsorbent materials and the optimization of capture processes. Given the significant potential demonstrated by MOFs for CO2 capture, a systematic evaluation of their performance and a deep understanding of their inherent trade-offs are paramount. This section delves into the performance intersections of MOFs for DAC applications, aiming to elucidate the pathway toward economically viable DAC technology through cost-performance analysis and the mapping of material-level compromises onto system-level implications.

The Cost-Performance Landscape: Mapping the Path to Economic Viability

Despite the promising CO2 capture performance of MOFs, achieving economic viability for DAC technology remains a critical challenge for its large-scale implementation. This necessitates a comprehensive analysis that integrates material cost with performance, mapping the cost-performance landscape to steer research and development toward economically sustainable solutions. Economic feasibility depends not only on the inherent capture capabilities of the material but is also profoundly influenced by MOF synthesis costs, operational expenditures, and the energy required for regeneration. Currently, the synthesis of high-performance MOFs often involves complex processes and high costs, limiting their commercial applicability. For instance, novel MOFs may demonstrate exceptional CO2 capture performance, yet their prohibitively high production costs render them less competitive. Therefore, developing low-cost, scalable MOF synthesis routes is paramount, potentially involving inexpensive precursors, simplified synthesis procedures, or exploration of sustainable synthetic methods.

To define a clear path toward low-cost, scalable synthesis, chen et al. examine a transformative benchmark from the related field of postcombustion capture, i.e., MIL-120­(Al)-AP. Although optimized for ∼ 15% CO2 flue gas, its development provides critical methodological insights and a tangible cost-performance target directly relevant to DAC engineering. The structural and adsorption properties of MIL-120­(Al)-AP, elucidated through advanced molecular simulations (Figure ), reveal design principles applicable to sorbent optimization for dilute capture. The simulated X-ray diffraction patterns (Figure B) confirm structural fidelity, underscoring the importance of robust framework modeling for predictive material design. Grand Canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations of CO2 adsorption isotherms at 298 K (Figure C) reveal excellent agreement with experimental data and a moderate isosteric heat of adsorption (∼−40 kJ·mol–1). This level of binding strength, ideal for low-energy regeneration, represents a prime target for DAC sorbents where energy efficiency is paramount. Beyond these fundamental properties, the true translational value of MIL-120­(Al)-AP for DAC lies in its scalable manufacturing, which provides a concrete and transferable blueprint. Its synthesis was scaled to the kilogram-scale using an eco-friendly, ambient-pressure aqueous route with inexpensive precursors, achieving a remarkable space-time yield. The green process, devoid of organic solvents or high-pressure reactors, demonstrates that low-cost, industrially viable production is achievable. Furthermore, its successful shaping into robust beads with inorganic binders while retaining high performance validates that scalable manufacturing and practical engineering forms are compatible with high capture efficiency. While MIL-120­(Al)-AP exemplifies how an integrated design can reconcile cost and performance, the broader MOF landscape still confronts considerable scale-up barriers that warrant explicit discussion. The primary cost drivers for MOF-based DAC include: (i) organic linker synthesis, which often requires multistep routes and expensive precursors; (ii) solvothermal conditions employing high-boiling organic solvents that incur substantial energy and waste management costs; and (iii) postsynthetic functionalization steps such as amine grafting that add process complexity. MIL-120­(Al)-AP represents a notable counterexample, achieving kilogram-scale production via an ambient-pressure aqueous route with inexpensive precursors, demonstrating that green synthesis can decouple high performance from prohibitive cost. Similarly, the one-pot synthesis of Mg2(hob) from monomer mixtures eliminates separate ligand isolation, further reducing process steps. However, for most high-performance chemisorptive MOFs, linker costs remain the dominant barrier. Estimates suggest that the ligand contributes 50–80% of total raw material cost, underscoring the urgent need for streamlined ligand synthesis, solvent recycling, and continuous-flow manufacturing to enable cost-competitive DAC.

This paradigmatic case delivers a clear message that the economic viability of DAC hinges on adopting an integrated design approach from the outset. First, it proves that high performance can stem from simple, inexpensive components and green synthesis, directly addressing the primary cost barrier. Second, its moderate isosteric heat of adsorption exemplifies the “Goldilocks” range of binding strength that enables high selectivity while minimizing regeneration energy, a balance paramount for operational cost reduction. − Emerging regeneration techniques, such as electrothermal swing adsorption (ETSA), can further leverage such optimized energetics, pushing the system-level energy efficiency even higher.

Therefore, constructing a meaningful cost-performance landscape for DAC must extend beyond static adsorption metrics to encompass this full-cycle perspective. The MIL-120­(Al)-AP benchmark underscores that the ultimate goal is a sorbent which optimizes the triad of cost, capture performance, and regeneration energy. Achieving this requires evaluating MOF candidates through the holistic lens of Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), integrating impacts from material synthesis and shaping through to repeated capture-regeneration cycles. The integrated framework, which translates material-level properties into system-level viability, is essential for guiding the development of MOFs that are not only high-performing in the lab but truly engineered for gigaton-scale DAC. This material-level optimization fundamentally dictates the design and economic outcome of the entire DAC system, a set of critical system-level implications that form the focus of the next section.

The System-Level Impact of Material-Level Trade-offs

The intrinsic trade-offs in MOF materials concerning capacity, selectivity, kinetics, stability, and regeneration energy requirements have profound implications for the overall design, operation, and economic performance of the entire DAC system. Understanding how these material-level compromises translate into system-level impacts is crucial for the successful deployment of DAC technology. Among these, the trade-off between adsorption capacity and regeneration energy stands as a central challenge. High CO2 adsorption capacity often implies strong MOF-CO2 interactions, which is desirable for capturing CO2 at low concentrations. However, excessively strong interactions increase the energy required for CO2 desorption, leading to higher regeneration energy consumption and operational costs. This necessitates the selection of MOFs with moderate binding strength, tailored to the target CO2 capture rate and available energy sources. For example, a system utilizing low-grade waste heat for regeneration would logically prioritize MOFs with lower regeneration energy demands, even at the expense of some initial adsorption capacity.

To place this trade-off in quantitative terms, the regeneration energy requirements of representative DAC sorbents span a wide range. Conventional aqueous amine-based systems typically consume 150–200 kJ·mol–1 (equivalent to 3.5–4.5 GJ per tonne of captured CO2), largely owing to the high heat capacity of water and the strong carbamate bonds that necessitate desorption temperatures above 120 °C. Amine-functionalized MOFs substantially reduce this penalty: mmen-Mg2(dobpdc) requires approximately 110–130 kJ·mol–1 with regeneration at 100–120 °C, while MOF-808-Lys, operating through a water-mediated bicarbonate pathway, achieves 90–110 kJ·mol–1 at ∼ 120 °C. Physisorptive MOFs offer further gains. CALF-20, with its moderate isosteric heat of adsorption (∼40 kJ·mol–1) and desorption temperature of 60–80 °C, is estimated to require below 65 kJ·mol–1. At the laboratory scale, electrically driven temperature swing adsorption (ETSA) using MOF-carbon fiber composites has demonstrated energy consumption approaching 45 kJ·mol–1. This progression, from ∼ 200 kJ·mol–1 to 45 kJ·mol–1, illustrates how strategic shifts from strong chemisorption to moderate physisorption, coupled with advanced regeneration techniques, can reduce the energy demand by a factor of 4 to five, progressively bringing DAC closer to the thermodynamic minimum.

Second, the trade-off between selectivity and kinetics also affects system efficiency. In DAC applications, MOFs must effectively capture CO2 at ppm levels amidst a high background concentration of N2 and water vapor. High CO2 selectivity is essential for ensuring capture purity and minimizing the cost of regenerating coadsorbed gases. However, pursuing extremely high selectivity, often achieved through narrow pore sizes or highly specific binding sites, can inadvertently hinder the mass transfer rate of CO2 molecules, leading to slower adsorption/desorption kinetics. When kinetics are slow, the system-level consequence is the requirement for larger adsorbent bed volumes, longer cycle times, or higher flow rates to maintain throughput, thereby increasing capital investment and operational complexity. Thus, balancing selectivity with mass transfer efficiency is necessary to optimize adsorber design and operating parameters.

Third, material stability directly determines the MOF’s cycle lifetime and the associated maintenance costs. , Under real DAC operating conditions, MOFs are exposed to humid, oxygen-containing air with potential contaminants and undergo repeated temperature/pressure cycling. Inadequate stability leads to performance degradation, increased replacement frequency, and consequently, significantly higher operating costs and a larger system carbon footprint. Therefore, to mitigate these material-level stability trade-offs, rigorous engineering design, such as incorporating robust pretreatment processes and optimizing cycling protocols, is essential to ensure the DAC system maintains high efficiency over operational lifetimes spanning years or even decades.

Finally, material cost and scalable synthesis are fundamental factors influencing the system’s economic viability. Synthesis methods for high-performance MOFs at the laboratory scale are often challenging to industrialize or are prohibitively expensive. At the system level, high MOF costs translate directly into elevated initial capital expenditure. Consequently, cost-effectiveness and scalable manufacturability must be incorporated as critical design criteria from the early stages of material development. Utilizing low-cost raw materials, simplifying synthesis pathways, and developing efficient postprocessing techniques are vital for reducing the per-unit cost of MOFs.

In summary, the trade-offs at the material level for MOFs in DAC applications are not isolated. They collectively and intricately dictate the overall performance and economic feasibility of the DAC system. This integrated approach is pivotal for accelerating progress toward achieving the goal of negative carbon emissions.

Perspectives: Charting the Course for Gigaton-Scale DAC

To realize the gigaton-scale deployment of MOF-based DAC, research and development must advance along several strategic pathways. This section outlines five critical frontiers essential for bridging laboratory innovation to global implementation, i.e., (1) intelligent adaptive sorbents that dynamically optimize performance; (2) codesign of sorbents and reactors for synergistic material-system integration; (3) AI-driven and high-throughput material discovery; (4) multifunctional adsorbents enabling integrated CO2 capture and conversion; and (5) a commercialization roadmap with critical milestones to accelerate deployment. All of these strategic directions require clear performance metrics to guide progress. To this end, we propose the following quantitative performance targets for next-generation DAC sorbents: (i) a CO2 uptake of ≥2.0 mmol·g–1 under ambient conditions (400 ppm of CO2, 25 °C, 30–50% RH); (ii) a regeneration temperature below 80 °C with an energy consumption of less than 1.5 GJ per tonne of captured CO2; (iii) a CO2/N2 selectivity greater than 104 and a CO2/H2O selectivity that retains ≥80% of dry CO2 capacity at 50% RH; , and (iv) a cyclic stability of ≥10 000 adsorption–desorption cycles with less than 10% capacity loss under fluctuating humidity and trace contaminants. These targets, derived from the best performances reviewed in earlier sections, serve as concrete milestones against which future materials and processes can be benchmarked.

The Rise of Intelligent and Adaptive Sorbent Systems

Future DAC sorbent systems are expected to evolve beyond the static performance of traditional materials into intelligent and adaptive systems capable of sensing environmental changes and dynamically adjusting their properties. This involves developing MOFs that modulate their CO2 adsorption/desorption characteristics in response to external stimuli such as temperature, humidity, and CO2 concentration. For instance, flexible MOFs exhibiting a “breathing effect” can undergo pore size changes upon CO2 adsorption, providing strong binding sites during capture and facilitating lower energy regeneration through pore contraction that reduces binding affinity, enabling efficient, low-energy CO2 release. Furthermore, integrating MOFs with smart polymers or other responsive materials can yield humidity-sensitive sorbents. These composites can autonomously regulate surface hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity under high humidity conditions, mitigating competitive water vapor adsorption while maintaining structural stability.

Adaptive sorbents may also incorporate ML algorithms for real-time optimization. By deploying a network of sensors within DAC units to monitor operational parameters (e.g., CO2 concentration, temperature, humidity), data can be fed into ML models trained on extensive MOF performance databases. These models can predict the optimal operating state for the MOF under current conditions and even fine-tune external stimuli (e.g., regeneration temperature, cycle time) to maximize capture efficiency and energy utilization. Such intelligent feedback control systems are crucial for the large-scale deployment of DAC, ensuring optimal performance amidst fluctuating ambient conditions.

The Co-Design Paradigm: Integrating Sorbent Shaping and Reactor Engineering

Achieving gigaton-scale DAC necessitates a paradigm shift toward a “Co-Design” framework, where adsorbent material development is intrinsically linked with reactor engineering. Traditional material development, often disconnected from engineering applications, frequently results in lab-scale high-performance materials failing to deliver their potential in practical reactors. The physical form of the sorbent (e.g., powder, pellet, monolith, structured composite) critically determines mass transfer, heat transfer, and pressure drop within the reactor. For example, while MOF powders demonstrate exceptional adsorption capacity in laboratories, their use in packed-bed reactors can lead to prohibitive pressure drops, limiting gas throughput, or cause attrition during cyclic operation, compromising long-term stability.

Consequently, future research must prioritize developing MOF shaping techniques tailored for various reactor configurations. This includes pelleting MOFs into mechanically robust granules, extruding them into monolithic structures, or depositing them onto substrates like carbon fibers to form composite materials. For instance, MOF-carbon fiber composites not only exhibit superior mechanical stability but also enable electrothermal regeneration via Joule heating for energy-efficient and rapid temperature swing adsorption (TSA). Beyond these established routes, emerging manufacturing paradigms are further expanding the design space for structured MOF contactors. Additive manufacturing, or 3D printing, enables the direct fabrication of MOF-based monolithic structures with hierarchical porosity, offering simultaneous control over macroscopic geometry and microscopic mass transfer pathways. Meanwhile, washcoating MOFs onto ceramic honeycombs or metallic substrates provides low-pressure-drop alternatives to packed beds, particularly suited for high-throughput DAC modules. The selection of binders, ranging from inorganic materials such as kaolinite and silica to hydrophobic polymers like polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), critically influences both the mechanical integrity and the CO2 transport properties of the final adsorbent body. This sorbent-reactor codesign approach ensures the synergistic optimization of macroscopic properties (e.g., mass transfer, thermal conductivity, mechanical strength) with microscopic properties (e.g., pore size distribution, binding sites), thereby maximizing the overall efficiency and economic viability of DAC systems.

Accelerating Material Discovery via Artificial Intelligence and High-Throughput Screening

The vast diversity of the MOF library renders traditional trial-and-error methods inefficient for material discovery. High-throughput screening (HTS) and artificial intelligence (AI) are poised to become central tools for accelerating the discovery and optimization of MOFs for DAC. − High-throughput computational screening, coupled with molecular simulations, enables the evaluation of CO2 adsorption properties (e.g., capacity, selectivity, isosteric heat of adsorption) for thousands of hypothetical or real MOFs within days or weeks. For instance, computational screening of anion-pillared MOFs has successfully identified candidates with superior CO2 capture performance. While initial applications focused on virtual screening of hypothetical structures, AI’s capabilities now extend across the entire development pipeline. Following computational identification of candidate structures, machine learning models navigate complex synthesis parameter spaces and predict long-term material stability under operational DAC conditions. This intelligence is physically realized through automated robotic platforms where AI algorithms direct high-throughput experimentation. These systems autonomously execute synthesis protocols and perform characterization, with experimental data continuously feeding back to refine AI predictions. It creates a self-improving research cycle where each iteration enhances model accuracy and guides subsequent experiments toward optimal solutions.

The resulting closed-loop discovery platform dramatically accelerates MOF development, compressing multiyear timelines into months while systematically addressing the multiobjective requirements for gigaton-scale DAC. By seamlessly integrating computational design with experimental validation, the approach enables the rapid identification of MOFs that simultaneously meet the demanding criteria of capacity, selectivity, stability, and scalability essential for economically viable direct air capture.

From Capture to Utilization: Designing Multifunctional Sorbents

Future DAC systems must not only capture CO2 efficiently but also address its subsequent utilization, fostering a circular carbon economy. Research will therefore increasingly focus on designing multifunctional MOF sorbents that serve not only as superior CO2 capture agents but also as catalysts for the direct conversion of captured CO2 into value-added chemicals or fuels.

For example, integrating CO2 capture sites and catalytic active sites within a single MOF framework enables “integrated” capture and conversion. This strategy eliminates the need for complex desorption, purification, and compression steps, allowing captured CO2 to be directly converted catalytically on the sorbent surface, for instance, via CO2 hydrogenation to methanol or methane. Such integrated processes can substantially reduce the energy consumption and cost associated with carbon capture and utilization. Furthermore, multifunctional MOFs can be engineered to selectively capture and separate other trace pollutants (e.g., volatile organic compounds) alongside CO2, achieving dual objectives of air purification and carbon capture. Designing these advanced sorbents requires a profound understanding of the MOF’s pore environment, active sites, and their stability and reactivity under various conditions.

Commercialization Roadmap: Key Milestones and Research Priorities

A well-defined commercialization roadmap, delineating key research priorities and developmental milestones, is paramount for the gigaton-scale deployment of MOF-based DAC technology. − This strategic framework is structured around near-term foundational research, midterm pilot validation and process intensification, and long-term global implementation, ensuring a coordinated trajectory from laboratory innovation to climate-impactful deployment. In the near-term (3–5 years), research must prioritize fundamental materials science and process engineering challenges. Critical objectives include substantially enhancing the hydrothermal and oxidative stability of MOFs under real-world atmospheric conditions, optimizing their CO2 adsorption–desorption kinetics to maximize cycling capacity and minimize energy penalties, and drastically reducing synthesis costs via low-cost precursors and scalable manufacturing routes. Concurrently, developing efficient, low-energy regeneration strategies, such as electrothermal swing adsorption (ETSA), is essential to lower the overall operational energy requirement.

The midterm (5–10 years) focus shifts to system integration and validation at relevant scales. This phase requires the successful operation of pilot-scale DAC units under real-world conditions to validate long-term MOF stability and system performance. It also involves the maturation of sorbent-reactor codesign, where MOF shaping and formulation are optimized for specific contactor geometries (e.g., fixed beds, fluidized beds). Furthermore, the establishment of robust, AI-driven high-throughput screening platforms will be crucial for the accelerated discovery of next-generation materials, paving the way for initial commercial deployment in niche applications leveraging, for instance, industrial waste heat.

The long-term vision (10–20 years) centers on achieving climate-relevant impact through gigaton-scale CO2 capture capacity. This necessitates the deployment of large-scale DAC facilities and requires a comprehensive optimization of the MOF lifecycle to ensure net-negative emissions and environmental sustainability. The roadmap culminates in the widespread application of multifunctional MOFs that integrate capture with catalytic conversion of CO2 into valuable products, coupled with global technology dissemination driven by technological maturity, substantial cost reductions, and supportive policy frameworks, ultimately making a definitive contribution to mitigating global climate change.

In summary, while challenges remain, the commercialization path for MOF-based DAC is rapidly taking shape. The convergence of materials science, chemical engineering, and artificial intelligence is key to overcoming prevailing technical bottlenecks. Conclusion

The development of high-performance DAC technologies is an indispensable component of global strategies to achieve net-negative carbon emissions. This review has systematically examined the pivotal role of MOFs as next-generation solid sorbents, highlighting their exceptional potential to overcome the fundamental trilemma of cost, energy intensity, and long-term stability that currently hinders DAC scalability. The inherent synthetic tunability of MOFs enables precise engineering of pore geometry, surface chemistry, and functionality, allowing for optimized molecular recognition of CO2 under ultradilute atmospheric conditions.

Key material-level strategies have been dissected, focusing on enhancing critical performance metrics, i.e., CO2 capacity, selectivity, sorption kinetics, and cycling stability. For chemisorption-driven MOFs, advanced amine functionalization strategies, such as the water-mediated bicarbonate pathway in MOF-808-Lys or the targeted grafting in MIL-100­(Cr), demonstrate how molecular-level control can decouple high adsorption capacity from energy-intensive regeneration. Concurrently, physisorptive MOFs like CALF-20 exemplify how optimized pore chemistry and hydrophobic adsorbophores can achieve high selectivity and exceptional hydrolytic stability with minimal regeneration penalties. Furthermore, the integration of advanced composites and external stimuli showcases pathways to dramatically accelerate sorption kinetics and reduce energy consumption. This analysis underscores that the critical trade-offs in MOF properties, between capacity and regeneration energy, and between selectivity and kinetics, are fundamental to the system-level design and economic feasibility of DAC. Material selection must therefore be treated as a system-level design decision, dictated by the need to align MOF properties with the specific constraints and energy profile of the DAC unit.

Looking forward, the trajectory for MOF-based DAC points toward increasingly intelligent and integrated systems. The frontiers of adaptive sorbents, sorbent-reactor codesign, AI-accelerated discovery, and multifunctional capture-conversion frameworks represent paradigm shifts that will be critical for achieving gigaton-scale deployment. An interdisciplinary effort, spanning materials science, chemical engineering, and data science, is essential to translate these promising laboratory innovations into practical, globally impactful technologies.

In conclusion, MOFs offer a uniquely versatile and powerful platform for advancing DAC. By continuing to refine their design through rational synthesis, comprehensive performance mapping, and system-level integration, the scientific and engineering communities can unlock their full potential. This endeavor is not merely a materials challenge but a critical imperative for developing the scalable, economically sustainable carbon removal solutions essential for climate stabilization.
