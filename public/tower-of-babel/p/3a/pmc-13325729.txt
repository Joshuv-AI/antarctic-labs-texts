Lifetime cost-effectiveness of semaglutide for secondary prevention in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease: A select-based simulation study
Authors: Sai Rahul Ponnana, Nik Surya, Feriel Presswalla, Gokul Parameswaran, Yuvraj Narang, Tong Zhang, Zhuo Chen, Jean-Eudes Dazard, Santosh Kumar Sirasapalli, Kanimozhi Sivanantham, Robert Okyere, Shamsa Elhussain, Ian J Neeland, Sanjay Rajagopalan, Salil V Deo
Published: 2026

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the US and presently accounts for nearly one in five deaths and a large economic burden on the healthcare system [1]

Recently, Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have become first-line agents for treating T2D in people, especially in those that have either established ASCVD or already have risk factors for ASCVD [2] However, GLP-1RA drugs, particularly Semaglutide, has also demonstrated cardiovascular benefit among obese ASCVD patients without T2D. The SELECT trial was the first study that reported a 20 % relative reduction in MACE risk with weekly 2.4 mg Semaglutide therapy [3] However, Semaglutide is expensive and a recent study analyzed data from the Medical Expenditure Panel survey (2022) reported that drug-eligible low-income individuals were less likely to receive Semaglutide therapy for obesity [4] Among young adults with obesity, retrospective analysis from a large healthcare system reported that Hispanic ethnicity and public insurance were associated with lower odds for Semaglutide uptake [5] While a recent study from UK reported that Semaglutide as an anti-obesity drug was cost-effective in the UK [6], a recent study using US data reported an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of $148,100 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) [7], well above the acceptable tradition ICER threshold for both the US ($100,000) and the UK (£ 25,000 - £ 35,000) [8] The US study used the CVD policy model to fit lifetime adverse events. Although the CVD policy model is developed using data from the National Health and Nutritional Survey, it may not always reflect real-world evidence. Hence, in our study, Semaglutide cost-effectiveness was investigated using real-world data from a large nationwide cohort of US Veterans with ASCVD and obesity.

Methods

A cohort of US Veterans with established stable ASCVD and obesity (body mass index > 30 kg/m2) without type 2 diabetes was identified using the ICD10th version codes. Annual event rates for myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, and all-cause mortality were obtained from a retrospective cohort of 519,566 US Veterans (mean age 65 years, Black 8 %, Female 4 %) (Fig. 1A). MI and stroke were identified using admission data while all-cause mortality from the National Death Index. Time-to-event models were fitted to obtain annual baseline transition rates and hazard ratios (HR) from the SELECT trial were applied to calculate corresponding rates for the Semaglutide arm. Stochastic uncertainty was modelled with 1000 Monte Carlo iterations (beta and lognormal distributions for transition rates and HR). A discrete-time homogenous multi-state Markov model (annual transition, 35-year time span) was used; 3 % discounting applied to costs and utilities, and the Simpsons half-cycle correction used to model transition probabilities. Further details such as Markov parameters with sources are as per Fig. 1B Primary analysis was fitted with lifetime 100 % adherence to Semaglutide therapy and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). Alternatively, modelling was done with 30 % drug discontinuation in year 1 and 5 % annually thereafter for the first decade. Additionally, Semaglutide cost was incrementally reduced to determine possible drug cost that may lead to an ICER at standard US willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of $ 100,000.

Results

Modelling lifetime 100 % Semaglutide therapy may potentially avoid 10 % MI and 7 % deaths over a 10-year study period compared to the baseline arm. In primary analysis, with lifetime Semaglutide therapy, an increase in QALY from 9.72 to 11.03 and a corresponding increase in healthcare costs from $130,853 to $302,821 per patient was observed. This resulted in a median ICER of $118,996 (25th - 75th percentiles: $115,633 - $122,433) with all simulations fitting into the north-east quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane. With drug discontinuation modelled as above, health benefits are very minimal (0.7QALY gained over the 35-year horizon) resulting in ICER of $ 124,523. Across a range of cost-effectiveness thresholds, at price of Semaglutide used in our study, it became more cost-effective than current care only above willingness-to-pay threshold of $ 130,000 per QALY is acceptable (Fig. 1C). In our model, Semaglutide costs need to be below $10,000/year to ensure ICER of less than $100,000.

Discussion

In a cost-effectiveness modeling study using nationwide data from US Veterans to inform the lifetime Markov model, Semaglutide use among obese non-diabetic ASCVD patients led to a modest increase in QALY; however, wider use of the drug is still prohibitively expensive at the annual cost $ 14,000 used to fit our model.

Many prior studies, primarily from Europe, have investigated the cost-effectiveness of using Semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly as an anti-obesity drug. A UK study, without disclosing the unit price of Semaglutide for the National Health Services, reported that the drug was cost-effective for routine use in that country [6] Another recently published study from Portugal reported the annual cost of Semaglutide as € 2736.00 and reported an ICER of € 13,459 per QALY putting it 100 % cost-effective at the national willingness-to-pay threshold of € 20,000 per QALY [9] As with most newer, expensive therapies such as Inclisiran or proprotein convertase subtilsin/kexin type 9 inhibitors, the pragmatic use of Semaglutide is only possible if the only a select subpopulation of all eligible individuals receive the medication, or drug costs are lowered so that wider use is financially possible for most patients. Unfortunately, a recent modelling study have reported that within 10 years, almost half of the US adult population is likely to be obese; even more concerning is that rates among Hispanic and low-income adults are predicted to be even higher [10] However, a combination of lifestyle changes and the wider use of Semaglutide therapy can possibly greatly reduce these odds. Hence, given these alarming statistics, it is essential for US public health that drug costs are reduced to more equitable levels. A recent modelling study reported that if Semaglutide costs in the US were similar to those in the UK, the ICER would be $ 38,000 per QALY gained; a fraction of the ICER that this study reported ($ 148,100) and what we have observed in our models [7]

Our study has certain limitations such as - (I) use of published literature for costs and utilities, (ii) assumptions that Semaglutide cost remains constant over the 35-year modelling period, and (iii) inability to model obesity trajectories and the concomitant use of other preventive therapies. However, these modeling choices are well accepted in cost-effectiveness studies and our observations mirror those reported by others using US data sources [7,11] In Conclusion, wider use of Semaglutide as an anti-obesity drug in the US may be practical when drug costs are lowered.
