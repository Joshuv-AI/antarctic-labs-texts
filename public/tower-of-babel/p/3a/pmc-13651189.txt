Ultrasound-derived fat fraction and automated point shear-wave elastography in diagnosing intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy and preeclampsia
Authors: Ke-xiong Niu, Jing-jing Ye, Wen-dong Zhang, Bin Ma, Tian-gang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic value of Ultrasound-derived fat fraction (UDFF) and Automatic point Shear Wave Elastography (Auto pSWE) for intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) and preeclampsia (PE).MethodsThis prospective study included 135 healthy pregnant women and patients with ICP and PE. Data collected included medical history, anthropometry, liver ultrasound, UDFF, Elastic Modulus (E), Shear Wave Velocity (SWV), and biochemical parameters. Each ICP or PE case was matched 1:1 with a healthy control by age (± 2 years) and gestational age (± 1 week). Differences in UDFF, Auto pSWE, and markers of liver and endothelial function were analyzed among ICP, PE, and healthy controls. The diagnostic value of UDFF and Auto pSWE was evaluated using univariate, correlation, and multivariate logistic regression analyses, and sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cutoffs were determined by ROC analysis.Results(1) Significant differences were observed between the ICP and PE groups and the control group in clinical indicators such as BMI, ALT, TBA, T-Bil, PT, and ALB (P < 0.05).(2) UDFF, E, and SWV parameters showed significant differences between the ICP, PE, and control groups (P < 0.01). E was identified as the preferred screening marker for ICP, with an optimal cutoff value of 7.471 kPa. UDFF and E were independently associated with PE; however, UDFF showed stronger, moderate-to-good performance for assessing PE severity, while E provided complementary stiffness information.ConclusionUDFF and Auto pSWE non-invasively quantify hepatic fat and stiffness in pregnancy and demonstrate clinically useful accuracy for diagnosing and staging ICP and PE, supporting their role as quantitative screening tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09655-4.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) and preeclampsia (PE) are serious pregnancy complications that threaten maternal-fetal health. ICP typically arises in the second or third trimester and is characterized by elevated serum bile acids and intense pruritus, often with hepatic dysfunction [1, 2]. Nonspecific early symptoms can lead to confusion with normal gestational pruritus, delaying diagnosis; without timely intervention, ICP can cause severe complications such as preterm birth or fetal distress [3].

PE is defined by new-onset hypertension (≥ 140/90 mmHg) and proteinuria (≥ 0.3 g/24 h) [4, 5]. Approximately 20–30% of PE patients develop liver involvement, and severe cases can progress to HELLP syndrome, further threatening maternal-fetal health [6, 7]. Early PE diagnosis relies on blood pressure and proteinuria measurements, but these provide limited assessment of liver function; indeed, recent studies indicate that PE-related hepatic pathology extends beyond enzyme elevations to include fat deposition, which exacerbates systemic inflammation and microcirculatory disturbances and accelerates disease progression [8].

Conventional ultrasound is highly sensitive for moderate-to-severe steatosis but has limited sensitivity for mild fatty liver and cannot quantify fat or fibrosis [9, 10]. This has prompted the development of quantitative ultrasound (QUS) methods for noninvasive liver fat assessment. Attenuation-based QUS techniques allow rapid fat evaluation but often require specialized equipment and use only a single parameter, limiting their utility [11, 12]. Additionally, automated point shear wave elastography (Auto pSWE) measures liver stiffness as a surrogate of fibrosis and is widely used for staging fibrosis and grading fatty liver [13, 14].

Ultrasound-derived fat fraction (UDFF) is a new QUS technique that quantifies liver fat from ultrasound backscatter and attenuation data. Its measurements correlate strongly with liver fat content and closely match MRI proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) [10, 15, 16]. The UDFF system also incorporates Auto pSWE for rapid stiffness assessment (with multiple stiffness measurements in one acquisition), enabling simultaneous evaluation of liver fat and stiffness and providing diagnostic information [17–19].

UDFF has shown high diagnostic performance in conditions like non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and cirrhosis [20–23], but its application in gestational liver diseases remains limited. ICP diagnosis relies on serum bile acids and liver function tests, and PE diagnosis on blood pressure and proteinuria; however, these methods do not provide a comprehensive assessment of metabolic or structural liver changes [1, 24]; moreover, ICP patients show increased liver fat content, while PE patients have elevated liver stiffness [25].Given the metabolic and inflammatory changes associated with ICP and PE, we hypothesized that combined UDFF and Auto pSWE assessment may provide complementary, noninvasive information on hepatic fat and stiffness in pregnancy-related liver disorders. However, the diagnostic value of these techniques in gestational pathologies involving the liver has not yet been established.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of UDFF and Auto pSWE in ICP and PE and to generate preliminary evidence on whether these techniques could support the assessment of pregnancy-related liver involvement.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This prospective study (Jan 2023 – Aug 2025) enrolled 270 pregnant women (135 healthy controls, 45 with ICP, and 90 with PE, including 45 mild and 45 severe PE). Each participant underwent detailed history, physical examination, routine obstetric ultrasound, ultrasound-derived fat fraction (UDFF) and point shear-wave elastography (Auto pSWE) measurements, and relevant blood tests. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (No. 2022-66), and all participants provided written informed consent. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

To minimize potential selection bias and improve comparability between groups, a 1:1 matching strategy was applied during control selection. Each participant with ICP or PE was matched with one healthy pregnant control according to maternal age (± 2 years) and gestational age at ultrasound examination (± 1 week). Matching was performed separately for the ICP and PE cohorts, yielding 45 matched ICP-control pairs and 90 matched PE-control pairs. The matched dataset was then used for group comparisons and diagnostic performance analyses.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were singleton pregnant women aged 18–40 years with a confirmed diagnosis of ICP or PE (mild or severe) based on standard clinical criteria, without severe comorbidities or chronic liver disease. Multiple pregnancies were excluded because of their distinct maternal hemodynamic and metabolic profiles and higher baseline risk of PE. The upper age limit of 40 years was applied to reduce heterogeneity and potential confounding related to advanced maternal age, including hypertensive disorders and metabolic comorbidities.

Chronic liver disease was excluded based not only on self-reported history but also on available pre-pregnancy or early prenatal records, baseline liver-related biochemical tests, and conventional liver ultrasound findings at enrollment.

Additional exclusion criteria were other hepatic diseases, severe pregnancy-related complications, long-term hormonal medication use, major genetic disorders, other serious systemic illness, incomplete data, or withdrawal of consent. Severe pregnancy-related complications were defined as major obstetric conditions other than ICP or PE that could independently affect liver function or preclude standardized imaging assessment, including acute fatty liver of pregnancy, HELLP syndrome, eclampsia, severe obstetric hemorrhage, severe infection, or conditions requiring emergency or intensive care.

Diagnostic criteria

PE: Diagnostic Definitions: PE was defined according to American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) criteria as new-onset hypertension (≥ 140/90 mmHg) with proteinuria after 20 weeks’ gestation. Severe PE was defined by the presence of any ACOG severe feature, including severe-range blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 110 mmHg), thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, new-onset headache or visual symptoms not explained by alternative diagnoses, or impaired liver function not attributable to other causes, as indicated by liver enzymes elevated to more than twice the upper reference limit or by severe persistent right upper quadrant or epigastric pain unresponsive to medication. Ultrasound equipment

Ultrasound examinations were performed on an ACUSON Sequoia (Siemens Healthineers) system using a 5C1 abdominal probe (3–5 MHz) and a DAX low-frequency convex probe (1–3.5 MHz).

UDFF and auto pSWE data acquisition

Image acquisition followed the EFSUMB guidelines for clinical use of ultrasound elastography [27]. All participants fasted for at least 8 h prior to imaging. To standardize conditions and reduce potential variation in the results, fasting was confirmed prior to measurement. First, a conventional ultrasound scan with the 5C1 probe assessed liver size, morphology, parenchymal echotexture, intrahepatic bile ducts, and vasculature; the right hepatic lobe oblique diameter and liver length in the midclavicular line were measured, and other abdominal organs were screened for abnormalities. Next, liver fat fraction and stiffness were measured using the DAX probe. Participants were positioned supine or in left lateral decubitus with the right arm fully raised to improve intercostal access. The probe was placed in a right intercostal space with the ultrasound beam perpendicular to the liver capsule. A homogenous area in segment V or VIII of the right liver lobe was selected, avoiding large vessels, rib shadows, and focal lesions.A fixed region of interest (ROI) ~ 3 cm × 3 cm was positioned just below the liver capsule.Participants were instructed to hold their breath during each UDFF and Auto pSWE measurement (if unable, quiet breathing was allowed). Each acquisition automatically sampled 15 sub-ROIs. Four valid measurements were obtained for each patient and averaged. UDFF results were expressed as a percentage (%) liver fat content, and Auto pSWE results were recorded as liver stiffness (Young’s modulus in kPa) with corresponding shear wave velocity (SWV in m/s). A result displayed as “XXX” indicated a measurement failure. Only measurements with an interquartile range to median ratio (IQR/Median) < 60% were considered valid.All ultrasounds were performed by MFM–certified sonographers with a minimum of 5 years of hepatic ultrasound experience. Although formal inter- and intra-operator reproducibility tests were not conducted in this pilot, consistency was supported by using standardized machine presets, a fixed ROI depth, and on-screen quality metrics. Data processing and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) if normally distributed, or as median (interquartile range) if not. Categorical data are expressed as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons (ICP vs. mild PE vs. severe PE vs. controls) were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. ROC curves were generated to evaluate the diagnostic performance of UDFF and Auto pSWE for identifying ICP and for differentiating degrees of PE; the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each, and optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity – 1). To control for potential confounders, multivariable logistic regression models were fitted with UDFF and Auto pSWE as independent variables, adjusted for age, BMI, ALT, TBA, T-Bil and other relevant covariates, to evaluate their independent association with ICP and PE. Multivariable logistic regression models included UDFF and Auto pSWE as imaging variables, with age, BMI, ALT, TBA, T-Bil, PT, INR, and ALB entered as covariates because these factors may affect hepatic fat content or stiffness. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

General clinical characteristics

A total of 270 participants were analyzed (45 ICP, 90 PE, 135 controls). Among the 90 women with PE, 45 were classified as having severe PE because they met at least one predefined criterion for PE with severe features at the time of diagnosis. Gestational age at ultrasound examination was comparable across the four groups (P = 0.844). The proportions of nulliparous women and women who conceived through assisted reproductive technology also did not differ significantly among groups (P = 0.095 and P = 0.369, respectively). The ICP group showed significant differences in several clinical indices compared with healthy controls. In particular, women with ICP had higher BMI and elevated liver function markers, including ALT, TBA, and T-Bil, as well as lower ALB levels (all P < 0.05). Age and INR did not differ significantly among the study groups (P = 0.302 and P = 0.899, respectively). Compared with healthy controls, both mild and severe PE groups had higher BMI, whereas BMI was highest in the severe PE group (overall P < 0.001). ALT, TBA, T-Bil, PT, and ALB also differed significantly across the four groups (all P < 0.05). These baseline demographic, obstetric, and laboratory characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

UDFF and stiffness differences between groups

Liver fat fraction and stiffness differed significantly among the four groups (controls, ICP, mild PE, severe PE). Mean UDFF values were highest in the severe PE group (7.65 ± 1.37%), significantly greater than those of controls and mild PE. Liver stiffness (elastic modulus, E) also differed by group (ANOVA F = 9.831); the severe PE group’s mean E was 8.99 ± 2.88 kPa, about 54.9% higher than the control group’s value. Similarly, SWV increased in stepwise fashion from controls to severe PE (approximately 1.45 m/s in controls vs. 1.88 m/s in severe PE; F = 399.176). These trends indicate that both hepatic fat content and stiffness rise with PE severity (Table 2).

ICP vs. Control: UDFF and pSWE Performance

Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that both UDFF and Auto pSWE were independent predictors distinguishing ICP patients from healthy pregnancies. For each 1% increase in liver fat (UDFF), the odds of ICP increased by ~ 1.54-fold (β = 0.434, SE = 0.211, Wald χ² = 4.252, P = 0.039). Liver stiffness was an even stronger predictor: each 1 kPa increase in Auto pSWE was associated with ~ 1.73-fold higher odds of ICP (β = 0.548, SE = 0.180, χ² = 9.229, P = 0.002). ROC curve analysis confirmed the diagnostic value of both methods for ICP, with Auto pSWE showing better discrimination. UDFF had an AUC of 0.701 (95% CI: 0.592–0.810). At an optimal UDFF cutoff ~ 5.86%, sensitivity was 56.7% and specificity 80.0% for detecting ICP. Auto pSWE yielded a higher AUC of 0.756 (95% CI: 0.656–0.856), and at a stiffness cutoff ~ 7.47 kPa it achieved 56.7% sensitivity with 93.3% specificity. The difference in AUC between Auto pSWE and UDFF was statistically significant (χ² = 4.12, P = 0.042), indicating superior performance of stiffness measurement for ICP. Mild PE vs. control: UDFF and pSWE performance

Both UDFF and Auto pSWE also independently differentiated mild PE from normal pregnancy. In logistic regression, higher liver fat was strongly associated with mild PE (β = 1.231, P = 0.001; OR ~ 3.43 per 1% UDFF). Severe PE vs. control: UDFF and pSWE performance

In severe PE, both UDFF and stiffness remained significant independent predictors versus controls. The logistic model showed UDFF had β = 1.357 (P = 0.001), corresponding to an OR of ~ 3.88 per 1% fat increase, and Auto pSWE had β = 0.461 (P = 0.033, OR ~ 1.59 per 1 kPa). (See Table S2.)

UDFF demonstrated outstanding diagnostic performance for severe PE, with an AUC of 0.930 (95% CI: 0.879–0.981). A UDFF threshold of ~ 6.73% yielded 76.7% sensitivity and 96.7% specificity. Auto pSWE was also informative (AUC 0.827, 95% CI: 0.735–0.919) but less accurate than UDFF. At a stiffness cutoff ~ 7.35 kPa, sensitivity was 63.3% and specificity 90.0%. The higher AUC for UDFF compared to Auto pSWE in severe PE was statistically significant (P < 0.05, DeLong test), highlighting UDFF’s superior ability to identify severe cases. Discussion

This is the first study to systematically compare UDFF and Auto pSWE in two pregnancy-related liver disorders (ICP and PE). In this cross-sectional cohort, UDFF and liver stiffness (Auto pSWE) were higher in ICP and PE than in healthy pregnancy, suggesting an association with pregnancy-related liver involvement Notably, UDFF demonstrated superior diagnostic performance for assessing PE severity. In severe PE, UDFF values were markedly higher than in mild PE or normal pregnancy (AUC ~ 0.90, significantly above the stiffness AUC ~ 0.80). A UDFF threshold of ~ 7% liver fat provided high sensitivity and specificity for severe PE. This suggests severe PE often involves increased hepatic fat that UDFF can detect. Stiffness was also elevated in PE but slightly less sensitive for grading severity. In mild PE, both UDFF and stiffness showed high specificity (> 90%), but UDFF had higher sensitivity, detecting more mild cases (fewer missed diagnoses) to aid early detection. Although neither metric replaces comprehensive clinical evaluation, our findings support UDFF as a preferred imaging marker for PE screening, with stiffness measurement as a complementary tool to enhance specificity.

In ICP, liver stiffness was notably elevated. Auto pSWE achieved an AUC ~ 0.75 for diagnosing ICP, with ~ 93% specificity and ~ 60% sensitivity at a ~ 7.47 kPa threshold. By contrast, UDFF levels in ICP were only slightly above controls, yielding an AUC ~ 0.70 that was not significantly different from the AUC for stiffness. This is likely because ICP pathophysiology involves cholestasis and impaired hepatocyte transport rather than overt steatosis [25]. Nevertheless, UDFF detected minor hepatic fat accumulation, suggesting ICP may involve lipid metabolism disturbances. Indeed, ICP is associated with a metabolic syndrome-like lipid profile (elevated triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL and reduced HDL) [28]. These lipid changes could result from cholestasis disrupting bile acid metabolism and hepatic lipid output, increasing the risk of liver fat deposition. This mechanism may explain the modest UDFF elevation in ICP. However, liver stiffness changes appear more sensitive for diagnosing ICP. Mild hepatic dysfunction and inflammation in ICP can cause edema and raise stiffness. Our findings support using stiffness measurement (Auto pSWE) as a non-invasive test for ICP: values > 7 kPa are common in ICP but rare in healthy late pregnancy. This aligns with a prospective study (Ammon et al.) showing healthy late-pregnancy stiffness ~ 6 kPa versus significantly higher values in ICP. Overall, stiffness is a clearer imaging marker for ICP, while UDFF provides additional metabolic insight. Combining these parameters may further improve ICP screening sensitivity.

We compared the diagnostic performance of UDFF and Auto pSWE using AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and related metrics. Overall, their AUCs were comparable (no significant differences), except in severe PE where UDFF’s AUC was significantly higher than pSWE’s (P < 0.05). This highlights UDFF’s advantage in that subgroup. Optimal thresholds were identified: for example, a UDFF cutoff ~ 6.5% for mild PE yielded ~ 70% sensitivity and 93% specificity, whereas an Auto pSWE cutoff ~ 7.3 kPa gave ~ 60% sensitivity and 93% specificity. These values can be adjusted based on clinical priorities: a lower cutoff raises sensitivity (fewer false negatives) and a higher cutoff raises specificity (fewer false positives). In practice, using both metrics together could balance sensitivity and specificity.

In PE, maternal metabolic disturbances and placental dysfunction likely underlie the observed changes. Preeclampsia is associated with insulin resistance and hyperlipidemia; elevated early-pregnancy cholesterol and triglyceride levels increase PE risk [29, 30]. These BMI and biochemical differences are clinically plausible in PE but may also confound quantitative ultrasound; therefore, adjusted models were used to assess UDFF and Auto pSWE beyond these covariates. Placental overproduction of anti-angiogenic factors (e.g., sFlt-1) in PE can suppress VEGF/PlGF signaling, leading to endothelial dysfunction and disrupting maternal lipid metabolism. These changes increase mobilization of free fatty acids and hepatic fat deposition, explaining the high UDFF in PE. Severe PE may also induce liver sinusoidal endothelial damage and local ischemia, with reperfusion injury triggering inflammation [6, 31]. These processes cause hepatocyte swelling, microthrombi, and elevated sinusoidal pressure, which acutely increase liver stiffness similar to changes in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis. Indeed, in PE complicated by HELLP, liver stiffness rises significantly but normalizes postpartum, supporting transient mechanical effects [25, 32–34].

By comparison, in ICP the imaging changes appear driven by cholestasis-induced injury. Pregnancies with ICP have markedly elevated bile acids, which are toxic to hepatocytes and induce oxidative stress and inflammation, resulting in mild cholestatic hepatitis and increased stiffness [35, 36]. Mechanistically, progesterone metabolites in ICP may antagonize hepatic farnesoid X receptor (FXR), impairing lipid transport [37]. FXR suppression reduces SHP activity and VLDL secretion, causing hepatic retention of cholesterol and triglycerides (with elevated LDL) [38]. High bile acid levels might concurrently activate PPARα, promoting fatty acid β-oxidation as a compensatory mechanism [39–41]. These hypotheses provide a biological context for our imaging findings but remain speculative due to limited direct evidence. We did not directly investigate these pathways, and further research is needed to confirm these mechanisms [42].

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, so multi-center research is needed to validate generalizability. Second, our measurements were mostly in late pregnancy; we did not assess predictive value in early pregnancy or asymptomatic stages. Thus, while these ultrasound metrics show promise, their utility for early risk stratification remains unproven. Future prospective studies should evaluate UDFF and pSWE earlier in high-risk pregnancies to determine optimal screening windows and predictive performance. Third, the PE/ICP cohorts differed from controls in demographics (e.g., BMI, comorbidities). We adjusted for confounders, but factors like obesity and insulin resistance (risk factors for PE/ICP) could still influence liver fat and stiffness independently. Our results should be confirmed in more homogeneous populations. Third, differences in BMI and liver-related biochemical markers may have influenced UDFF and Auto pSWE. Although adjusted models included these variables, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Additionally, we did not collect placental biomarkers (e.g., sFlt-1/PlGF) [43], so we could not correlate imaging findings with those markers. Combining imaging with biomarker data in future studies may yield more robust prediction models. Finally, formal intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility analyses were not performed. Despite standardized acquisition settings, fixed ROI criteria, repeated measurements, and predefined quality-control thresholds applied by experienced sonographers, operator-related variability cannot be excluded. Future multicenter studies should assess reproducibility across operators and ultrasound systems to confirm the robustness and generalizability of UDFF and Auto pSWE measurements in pregnant populations.

In summary, this study lays a foundation for using UDFF and Auto pSWE in pregnancy-related liver diseases. Larger multi-center studies with refined design and multiparametric analysis are needed to validate and extend our findings. Our results highlight the potential of UDFF and pSWE for early detection of high-risk pregnancy complications. We anticipate that future research will establish standardized protocols and confirm the benefits of these non-invasive imaging tools in improving maternal and fetal outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, UDFF and Auto pSWE are promising non-invasive tools for detecting ICP and PE. UDFF shows superior sensitivity for assessing PE severity, whereas pSWE offers higher specificity for ICP. These findings support the potential role of UDFF and Auto pSWE as adjunctive imaging markers to complement clinical and biochemical assessment of pregnancy-related liver involvement. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate their clinical utility and determine whether their use can improve risk stratification and maternal-fetal outcomes.
