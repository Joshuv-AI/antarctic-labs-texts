Seed quality and nutritional composition of conventional and zinc biofortified wheat under hermetic and conventional storage techniques
Authors: Muhammad Muazzam, Muhammad Amir Bakhtavar, Umar Farooq, Shahid Iqbal, Shabbir Ahmad, Mujahid Iqbal, Syed Qaisar Saeed, Tannaza Sadaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiofortified varieties of wheat have emerged as potential zinc carriers. There is a dire need to study their storage shelf life in terms of nutritional quality and physiological seed quality. Biofortified and non-biofortified wheat varieties were stored in hermetic bags, woven polypropylene bags (PP bags) and metal silos at four different locations. After 12 months, germination of Nawab-21 drastically reduced in metal silos and PP bags compared to hermetic bags at Khanewal-II. Minor variations of crude protein and minerals (zinc and iron) contents were recorded in hermetic bags. However, significant reduction in nutritional quality was observed in the seeds stored in PP bags and metal silos across all locations. Maximum total soluble sugars were observed in seed stored in hermetic bags. Minimum reducing sugars, malondialdehyde (MDA) contents and aflatoxins contamination was observed in hermetic bags. Around 30% weight losses and 66% grains damaged from stored grain pests were recorded in metal silos and PP bags. Whereas, hermetic bags had less than 1% weight losses. Overall, Nawab-21 wheat variety showed higher losses in seed and nutritional quality under conventional storage techniques. Hermetic storage is an effective technique to reduce storage, seed and nutritional quality losses in both biofortified and non-biofortified wheat.Keywords: Aflatoxins, Germination, Losses, Malondialdehyde, Minerals, SeedSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Progress towards achieving zero hunger globally is constrained by unequal economic access to nutritious diets and lack of significant improvements in food security1. Wheat (Triticum aestivum) is a global staple crop, feeding around 35% of world population2. Global annual production of wheat was around 798.4 million tons during 20243. About 17% of people of world are at the risk of zinc deficiency, which varies by geography and can impair immune function and increase susceptibility to diseases4. According to studies, zinc deficiency is linked to stunted growth, decreased sexual function, reduced immunity, and impaired cognitive abilities5. In Pakistan, 22.1% of total 207 million people particularly women and 18.6% of children (under 5 years) are Zn deficient6. In Pakistan, three varieties of zinc biofortified wheat (ZnBW) including Zincol-2016, Akbar-2019 and Nawab-2021 have been approved for cultivation with the objective to increase the zinc intake. So, it is imperative to study their storage life in terms of nutritional quality and physiological seed quality.

Poor storage techniques, lack of knowledge and inadequate storage infrastructure results in 50–60% losses in cereal grains during storage7. On-farm storage is the fundamental element of postharvest activities8. Researchers have examined farm storage losses using a range of techniques, but the outcomes have been somewhat inconsistent.

Reducing cereal grain post-harvest losses, which are mostly caused by poor storage conditions is crucial9. According to studies, excessive humidity in the storage environment causes the attack of insect pest, aging of seed and contamination of aflatoxins10. Farmers in underdeveloped and developing nations store their wheat in jute bags, woven polypropylene bags and metal silos. Majority of the farmers utilize this stored wheat as seed for the next season. The use of high-quality seed is essential for successful crop development. Seed storage life is affected by seed moisture contents, composition of seed, storage temperature and stored grain pests11. Cereal grains stored in porous packaging materials (such as cloth bags, polypropylene and jute bags) under high relative humidity absorb moisture from surrounding air. High seed moisture increased the risk of stored grain pests infestation, reduced the physiological seed quality and nutritional quality12.

Lesser grain borer (Rhyzopertha dominica), red flour beetle (Tribolium castaneum) and Trogoderma granarium are the significant pest of stored grains especially wheat and maize13,14. Mostly fumigants are used to control stored grain pests but these insecticides leave behind hazardous residues15. Hermetic storage technology is the chemical free alternative to control insect pest in stored grains16.

Stored grain pests populations and storage losses have been successfully reduced using hermetic bags17. PICS bags and GrainPro Super bags are the most common used hermetic bags. The idea of hermetic bag technology is to prevent moisture and oxygen entry inside the bags. This low moisture and oxygen condition is highly effective to maintain high seed germination and control stored grain pests. Hermetic storage bags keep the seeds dry and reduce postharvest losses by maintaining Dry Chain18.

Keeping in view the benefits of hermetic storage technology, hermetic bags were tested to minimize post-harvest storage losses and ascertain the nutritional and seed quality of zinc biofortified and non-biofortified wheat cultivars.

Discussion

The primary cause of seed aging during storage was high seed moisture content. Seeds are hygroscopic entities. They absorb and desorb moisture in accordance with the relative humidity of the storage environment to achieve equilibrium19. The moisture contents were higher in PP bags and metal silos especially at Khanewal-I and Khanewal-II. Both these structures are not hermetically sealed. So, seed moisture fluctuated and increased in these structures compared to hermetic bags (Fig. 2). Relative humidity was higher at Khanewal-I and Khanewal-II which is the main cause of high seed moisture in PP bags and metal silos at these locations (Fig. 16). No significant change in seed moisture was observed in hermetic bags (Both imported and local) at all locations due to low water vapor transmission rate, generally lower than 5 gm−2 day−120.

Data of temperature and relative humidity of all four locations (a) Jalalpur Pirwala (b) Multan (c) Khanewal-I and (d) Khanewal-II.

Germination of the stored seeds decreased with the passage of time in all storage techniques. High seed moisture might enhance the activities of hydrolytic enzymes and encouraged biochemical processes such as respiration that depleted food reserves21. Seed moisture is inversely linked to seed germination and other quality attributes (Fig. 15). Higher seed moisture contents resulted into higher activities of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals that resulted into production of MDA through lipid peroxidation22. High MDA contents of seed stored in PP bags and metal silos indicated higher oxidative damage (Fig. 11). Lipid peroxidation and oxidation of biomolecules have been considered as the major reason of seed deterioration and germination loss23.

The EC of seed leachate increased over time during storage, which is a sign of seed aging and loss of membrane integrity24. EC of seed leachates was higher in our study mainly because of free radicals that caused lipid peroxidation and membrane damage25. Malondialdehyde are the final product of lipid peroxidation. We have found high MDA contents from the seeds having high moisture contents. There was no difference of seed quality attributes among biofortified and non-biofortified wheat. Higher deterioration in Nawab-21 was the result of high seed moisture contents. Seed stored in conventional storage structures was subjected to more fluctuations in seed moisture due to high ambient relative humidity, whereas negligible change in seed moisture was observed in hermetic storage26.

Zn and Fe contents did not vary significantly in initial samplings. In PP bags and metal silos, Zn and Fe contents declined in 9 and 12-months sampling due to grain damage by stored grain pests especially Trogoderma granarium (Fig. 13). According to Jood et al.27 germ contain 80% of the total mineral deposits of seed and T. granarium mainly feed on germ portion.

Initial protein contents of studied wheat varieties were in range of 12–13% that declined up to 8% in wheat that was stored in metal silos and PP bags (Fig. 7). High seed moisture in conventional storage techniques promote biochemical degradation that accelerate loss of protein contents28. Moreover, stored grain pests heavily feeding on wheat grains reduced the protein contents of the seed (Fig. 13).

Reduced total soluble sugars were the outcome of seed deterioration in conventional packaging materials, as was the case with wheat seeds in earlier research22. The total soluble sugars declined in PP bags and metal silos (Fig. 8), while reducing sugars increased in PP bags and metal silos due to high seed moisture and grain damage (Fig. 9). Seed aging causes the production of ROS, which react with carbohydrates and change them into reducing sugars, interfering with the membranes’ ability to function normally. Reducing sugars play a significant influence in seed deterioration because they participate in the Maillard reaction by damaging protein amino groups and nucleic acid/protein complexes29.

Some molds, namely Aspergillus parasiticus and Aspergillus flavus, release toxic mycotoxins and carcinogens called aflatoxins. Cottonseed, millet, peanuts, rice, cassava, sesame seeds, chili, pepper, sweet corn, tree nuts, sunflower seeds, sorghum, spices, and wheat are among the foods that frequently contain aflatoxins due to improper storage30. Development of storge fungi and production of aflatoxins is favored by high relative humidity18. Both locations of Khanewal had (0.07 ppb) aflatoxins contamination due to high RH level and grains damaged by stored grain pests (Fig. 12). Although the detected aflatoxins are below the permissible limit but wheat being staple food is consumed daily by majority of population. A very small intake on regular basis posed concerns of food safety.

Higher grain damage by stored grain pests and weight losses in PP bags and metal silos were the result of high stored grain pest infestation (Figs. 13, 14). Excessive seed moisture causes the attack of insect pests, aging of seed and contamination of aflatoxins17. In metal silos and PP bags, seed was exposed to high ambient humidity and free oxygen levels. Study showed that insect infestation decreases with low O2 and high CO2 levels. In hermetic bags, CO2 levels increased and O2 levels decreased due to respiration of stored grain and insect pest invasion31. Hermetically sealed bags restrict moisture and oxygen entry into seeds. These conditions in hermetic storage bags are not suitable for insects to thrive.

Lipid peroxidation, a crucial step in the seed aging, produces malondialdehydes (MDA) as a byproduct32. Higher levels of seed moisture led to increased activities of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radical attack which that resulted in lipid peroxidation and production of MDA32. Higher MDA contents have been observed in our findings. The oxidation of biomolecules including DNA, lipids, and phospholipids is caused by ROS, which are formed when there is free availability of oxygen33. The high activity of alpha amylase means starch is hydrolyzed in the endosperm tissues34. The activity of alpha amylase was higher in PP bags and metal silos due to high seed moisture in these structures.

Hermetic storage bags performed better than PP bags and metal silos because they are airtight and resist moisture entry due to gas barrier present in between three sandwiched high density polyethylene layers. The fundamental difference creates a dry and low oxygen environment could not be achieved in PP bags and metal silos.

In conclusion, hermetic bags preserved seed and nutritional quality of both biofortified and non-biofortified wheat varieties by maintaining low seed moisture contents. Seed moisture increased in woven polypropylene bags and metal silos that resulted in seed germination and vigor losses. Higher aflatoxins contamination and storage losses due to stored grain pests were observed in PP bags and metal silos compared to hermetic bags.

Materials and methods

Experiment details

The study was conducted at storerooms of farmers in Multan and Khanewal districts of Punjab Pakistan at four different locations including Multan, Jalalpur Pirwala, Khanewal I and Khanewal II. Factors of this study include storage locations, storage techniques and wheat varieties (Storage locations × Storage techniques × Wheat varieties). Each experimental unit was replicated four times making 256 experimental units (Table 1). Four wheat varieties were stored from which Akbar-19 and Nawab-21 were zinc biofortified while Dilkash-20 and Arooj-22 were non zinc biofortified. The storage techniques included PP bags and metal silos (conventional structures), hermetic bags including imported (GrainPro, USA) and local hermetic bags (UAF, Pakistan).

The experiment was laid out according to Completely Randomized Design with factorial arrangement. Data of each sampling interval was analyzed separately. The Fisher’s Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) technique was used for statistical analysis of data using computer program Statistix 10. Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s HSD test with a 5% probability level35. The visual representation of data is carried out using correlation mapping and PCA on R software visualization36. These techniques are used to assess various interactions37.

Experimental material

The freshly harvested seed of wheat varieties was purchased from Punjab Seed Corporation. Total 20 kg seed per experimental unit was packed in different storage structures and prepared material was then transported to above mentioned locations. Air was removed manually from hermetic bags by pressing from top open end. After removing as much air possible, open end was twisted, folded back and tied with plastic cable tie to seal the hermetic bags. The seed was stored at ambient conditions of farmer’s store houses. Data of temperature and relative humidity was recorded through data logger (Benetch gm1365, China) and presented in Fig. 1. Seed was stored for a period of one year and sampling was done after every three-month interval. At each sampling, one replicate was opened and 500 g seed was sampled to determine nutritional and physiological seed quality attributes. The sampling process adhered to random sampling techniques as outlined by the International Seed Testing Association standards38.

Seed quality parameter

The moisture contents (%) were determined on wet basis (WB) by high-constant-temperature oven method. Three replicates of fine ground seed samples (10 g) were placed in drying oven (Memmert, Germany) for the period of 2 h at 132 °C38. Dry weight was recorded and seed moisture contents were calculated using following equation:

M1 = Combined weight (g) of container and its cover. M2 = Combined weight (g) of container, cover and seed contents before drying. M3 = Combined weight (g) of container, cover and seed contents after drying.

Germination test was conducted according to ISTA rule38 with slight modifications. Three replications were made for each sample and 100 seeds per replicate were counted and placed on top of the moist filter paper in petri dishes. Data of seed germination was recorded on daily basis. Nutritional quality analysis

The zinc and iron contents of wheat flour was measured using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer39. Wet digestion of flour sample (1–2 g W.B.) was carried out in hot plate for 2 h using a 3:1 mixture of HNO3:HCLO4 at simmering point (50 °C). Digested samples were diluted to 100 mL using distilled water. The Zn and Fe concentrations in the digested samples were determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Novaa 400p, analytic jena, Germany) using specific hollow cathode lamp. Samples were run on AAS after blank and standard solutions of various concentrations i.e. (0, 0.25 ppm, 0.5 ppm, 1 ppm, 2 ppm, 4 ppm, 8 ppm for Zn and 0, 20 ppm, 40 ppm, 60 ppm, 80 ppm for Fe).

Crude protein content was analyzed through Kjeldhal apparatus (Model: D-40599, Behr labor technik, Gmbh-Germany) in the cereal samples following AACC40 method No. 46-10. Sample (500 mg; W.B.) was placed in a Kjeldhal digestion flask and digested with 25 mL concentrated H2SO4 and 5 g digestion mixture (K2SO4:FeSO4:CuSO4 with 85:5:10) until the color was light greenish. The digested material was diluted to 250 ml with distilled water and 10 mL of it was distilled with 30 mL of 40% NaOH. The distillate (ammonia) was collected in the 10mL of 4% boric acid solution having 3 to 3 drops of methyl red indicator. The ammonium borate was formed and solution color has changed to yellow color. At the end, ammonium borate was titrated with 0.1 N sulphuric acid till light pink color and volume of acid was noted for nitrogen determination in sample.

Total soluble sugars

Biochemical attributes including total soluble sugars, reducing sugars, α-amylase activity, MDA contents and total aflatoxins were determined only at last sampling after 12 months of storage. Total soluble sugars were measured by anthrone reagent method by following protocol given by Sadasivam and Manickam41 with slight modifications. Ground wheat seed (0.5 g) was extracted in 1 mL methanol. Mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000g for 7 min. From supernatant, 0.5 mL was mixed into 2 mL of chilled anthrone reagent. Mixture was then incubated at 60 °C for 1 h. Absorbance was taken at 630 nm wavelength (Epoch microplate reader, biotek gen5, USA).

Reducing Sugars

The procedure followed by Sadasivam and Manickam41 by DNS (Dinitrosalicylic acid) method. Reducing sugars were measured in 0.5 g ground seeds by mixing in 5 mL of 200 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.9). Mixture was centrifuged at 10,000g for 7 min. From supernatant, 50 µL was mixed in 450 µL distilled water into which 750 µL freshly prepared DNS solution was added. Mixture was boiled at 100 °C for 5 min and color turned into dark brown. After that 1% sodium potassium tartrate was added into the mixture and absorbance was taken at the wavelength of 510 nm (Epoch microplate reader, biotek gen5, USA).

α-Amylase activity

The α-amylase activity was determined according to Bhattacharyya and Sen-Mandi42. The ground wheat seed (0.5 g) was mixed into 5 mL of 200 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.9). The supernatant (250 µL) was added into 250 µL and 1% starch solution for reaction. Reaction mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. After that 500 µL Dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) solution was added. Mixture was boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. After cooling, absorbance was taken at wavelength of 540 nm (Epoch microplate reader, biotek gen5, USA).

MDA contents

MDA contents were measured by following method of Zheng and Tian43 with slight modifications. 1 g ground wheat seed was mixed into 8 ml of 10% TCA solution. Centrifugation was carried out at 10,000 g for 10 min. From supernatant, 2 ml was added into same volume of 0.6% TBA prepared in 20% TCA. Reaction mixture was incubated at 100 °C for 20 min. After that solution was cooled to ambient room temperature and then 70 µL were loaded into Elisa plate. Total aflatoxins determination

Total aflatoxins contents were quantified by using Aflatoxins Total ELISA Kit (Zokeyo, China) having 0.05 ppb LOD44. In first step wheat seeds were ground and 5 g sample was used to determine total aflatoxins. Extraction buffer (Acetonitrile 70%) was prepared and added to each sample for extraction. Samples were filtered through filter paper and purified samples was kept for further process. Then 50 µL extracted samples were added to 96 well plate along with enzyme and antibodies and incubated for half an hour at room temperature. After incubation samples were washed by using wash buffer three to four times then substrate was added to each sample. Samples were hold for 15 min and stop solution was added. All solution and chemicals used for this experiment were used according to the protocol of ELISA Kit. Plate was read by using ELISA reader (Epoch microplate reader, biotek gen5, USA).
