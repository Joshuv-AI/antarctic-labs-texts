Synergistic analysis of climate and anthropogenic impacts on NPP dynamics in India’s Western Ghats using geodetector
Authors: Sanjay Kumar, Jyothika Karkala, Joyal Arya, Rajiv Kumar Chaturvedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe importance of integrating climate resilience into conservation efforts to safeguard the Western Ghats, one of the hottest hotspots in the world, is underscored by our findings. The research was designed to provide an integrated approach to help preserve the ecological productivity in a biodiversity sensitive area. The study investigates the dynamics of terrestrial productivity, examining its trends over time (2001–2023) and identifying the various factors that influence it, a topic that has been understudied in this biodiversity hotspot. In 2019, Net Primary Productivity (NPP) results experienced significant declines across all land cover types, with the decline starting in 2015 when forest NPP dropped by 0.24 and cropland by 0.1 kg C m year. The NPP steadily recovered the following year, especially in the croplands. The factor detector q value indicated the degree of influence of each factor as vegetation parameters (), climatic indicators (), anthropogenic factor (), and topographic condition (). Results from the innovative approach to assess spatial heterogeneity emphasize the influence of the combinations of Land Use Land Cover (LULC) with Fraction of Photosynthetically Active Radiation (FPAR), Leaf Area Index (LAI), Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Land Surface Temperature (LST), and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), followed by LST interacting with FPAR, and NDVI with LAI, had the greatest impact on NPP. The study employs multiple regression to evaluate the effects of variables, the Mann–Kendall method for trend analysis, and Geo-detector for spatial variation.Subject terms: Climate-change impacts, Environmental health, Climate-change ecology

Introduction

The role of forests in regulating climate, transfer of energy, water balance, and carbon cycle by exchanging huge quantities of carbon with the atmosphere is indispensable in the terrestrial ecosystem1,2. Roughly 31% of the land area on Earth is made up of forests3 with the ability to absorb higher amounts of carbon in comparison to all the other ecosystems combined while providing ecosystem services worth trillions of dollars. Forests not only provide a spectrum of benefits but also possess the power to withstand and mitigate climate change4. The sustenance of the forest ecosystem is defined by the primary productivity. The rate at which autotrophic organisms along with plants convert solar energy into organic compounds through the process of photosynthesis in an ecosystem is the Gross Primary Productivity(GPP). The NPP is the GPP minus the rate at which some of these compounds are used through cellular respiration5. Globally, the GPP between the atmosphere and land makes up for the largest carbon flux about 130 Gt C year as photosynthesis fixes the carbon determining the potential for carbon absorption2. NPP serves as a popularly utilized pivotal marker for the assessment of ecological security and as a measure of vegetation dynamics1.

Climate change is contributing to the unfortunate decline in the productivity of forests6. Climate change marked by changing temperature (TEMP), and precipitation (PPT) conditions accompanied by a rise in affects a spectrum of forest services7. The continued human activity has been contributing to the rise of long-lived greenhouse gases and the build-up of these gases is likely to increase the surface air temperature by 1.5–4.5 °C in this century. Global NPP of the terrestrial ecosystem is bound to be affected by a change in climate of such a magnitude8. A key contributor to the negative feedback on climate warming is the enhanced vegetation productivity driven by the increasing concentration of carbon dioxide() although there is a lack of clarity in the temporal dynamics of the process9. Lately, a climate-driven substantial change in forest ecosystems, characterized by an increase in the frequency and severity of disturbance regimes, has significantly impacted the productivity of ecosystem services. There is a high probability that the climate-induced disturbances could overshadow the ecological resilience of forests which possibly could perpetually shift the ecosystem balance10. A noteworthy number of interdependent climate factors fuel a series of short-term or permanent complications and ill effects on natural and human systems are the climate extremes such as drought, heavy precipitation, and heatwaves which can co-occur across the globe11–18. Apart from posing a threat to society, the concurrent extreme climate events are a danger to the forest ecosystem as hot and dry weather inhibits the capacity to suppress and can kick start wildfires causing extensive environmental impacts, forest mortality, and fire damages11. Another facet of forest stress comes from urban land expansion, as the impervious surface takes over the natural vegetation on the city-to-country scale, studies have indicated a reduction in the terrestrial NPP19.

The current scenario of forest productivity has driven the conceptualization and application of multiple techniques to assess it. Among the land cover types, forests prove to be the most productive which has an average GPP of 2.0 kg C m year while cropland and grassland are estimated to be 1.8 and 1.5 kg C m year respectively2. Global biosphere productivity represents the most significant absorption flux of atmospheric carbon dioxide () and is crucial for understanding both historical and future carbon cycles20. The temporal and spatial components of the NPP in the source region of the Yangtze River in China were carried out using data from the Carnegie-Ames-Stanford Approach model. The impact of climatic factors was studied and the result highlighted the key factors as temperature and precipitation on an inter-annual time scale and human activities in the downstream region1. Net ecosystem productivity (NEP) is fundamental to the forest carbon sink, and comparing NEP between natural and planted forests can aid in reaching carbon neutrality. As a result of soil fertilizer and higher stand age, the NEP in natural forests was lower than in planted ones. At the same time, as the mean annual air temperature decreased due to latitudinal variations, the GPP dropped in both the planted and natural forests21. The impact of meteorological and biophysical parameters on the NPP in the tropical deciduous forest of India showed that a decrease in NPP was attributed to rise in temperature combined with vapour pressure deficit22. The significant effect of precipitation on the NPP was revealed when studying the NPP in the forest plantation in India. Also, the modeled data tends to underestimate the value in comparison to the field-estimated data23. A study in India demonstrated that an 18.51% increase in LAI from 2001 to 2019 did not result in increased carbon uptake caused by warming constraints. It also revealed that in forests which are temporally consistent, a 6.19% decrease in NPP occurred despite a 6.75% increase in LAI24. The use of remote sensing, Residual Trend analysis, and correlation analysis showed that precipitation is the primary driver of NPP over 72% of the area in India. At the same time, temperature and solar radiation influenced NPP in the Himalayas and forested regions, respectively with a slight decline caused by human activities25. The geographic detector model (GDM) is utilized to understand the relative contribution of multiple climatic factors on vegetation activities while detecting the heterogeneity in spatial stratification26–28.

Globally, 420 million hectares of forest land have vanished since 1990 from the conversion to anthropogenic uses. During the period 2015–2020, 10 million hectares of forest were cut down every year which is less than in the 1900s, when 16 million hectares per year were lost3. To safeguard and elevate the forest carbon sinks, multiple efforts for policy development and economic incentives are introduced such as the advancement in the carbon offset projects for forests on the global scale, and the Bonn Challenge for the restoration of landscape4. India is determined to expand its tree and forest cover creating an add-on of nearly 3 billion tonnes of carbon sink by the year 203029. Western Ghats, well known for its distinctive ecological and biophysical processes, is one of the ancient mountains. It stands at eighth position on the list of the world’s hottest hotspots due to its biodiversity. Unfortunately, the high-altitude montane forest ecosystem that controls the weather pattern of the monsoon in India is under threat due to the stress on habitat and climate change. Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 15 “Life on land” of the United Nations (UN) encourages the promotion, restoration, and protection of the land. Achieving the SDG will be harmed if the downward trend in ecosystem and biodiversity persists3. The changing natural vegetation as a direct result of climate change modifies vegetation productivity. Reversing the fragmentation of forests is more of an impossible task with a focus on biodiversity loss along with encroachment activity and increased population pressure on the forest edges3. There is a lack of clarity when decoding the underlying mechanism and bringing remote sensing into play renders a resourceful and convenient channel for estimating NPP. Although being a sensitive zone, accompanied with growing anthropogenic activities, not enough research has been carried out in this region to understand the climate change dynamics. The study aims to understand the spatiotemporal aspect of the trend in NPP in the Western Ghats from 2001 to 2023. The GDM provides a multi dimensional look at the relationship between the various explanatory variables and NPP. It is useful in not only determining the magnitude but also the potential to independently influence the target variable. The influence of climatic parameters, vegetation indices, topography, and anthropogenic activities forms the focus of the research.

Results

The combined impact of variables can be categorized as vegetation parameters (EVI, NDVI, LAI, FPAR), climate indicators (Precipitation, Temperature, LST), anthropogenic factors (LULC), and topographic condition (slope) in this section. The q value is a measure of the magnitude of the driving force determined by the factor detector. The range of the q value is from 0 to 1. It signifies the 100 x q% of how the independent variable can explain the change in NPP. The statistical significance is denoted by the p value. The result of the MK test is given as z value based on which the significance of the trend is assessed.

Spatial association of NPP with explanatory variables

The distribution of NPP indicates that the value in the southern states of Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Karnataka prominently populated by wet evergreen forests are significantly higher in comparison to the NPP in Goa, Maharashtra, and Gujarat which have moist deciduous and semi-evergreen forests (Fig. S1). The NPP around the Eravikulam forest region is seen to be reasonably consistent throughout owing to the higher elevation and closeness to the equator, and evergreen vegetation resulting in year-round photosynthesis. The average NPP peaked in 2011 and 2022 at 0.95 kg C m year, with notable drops observed during 2003–2004, 2009–2010, and in 2012 and 2014. A significant decline occurred from 2017 to 2019 when NPP reached as low as 0.73 kg C m year (Fig. 1).

The evolution of the mean NPP in the Western Ghats from 2001 to 2023.

Decreased rainfall in 2002 (annual average of 143.15 mm) led to the fall of EVI, NDVI, LAI, and FPAR, in the following years of 2003 and 2004 where NPP also dropped to 0.83 (kg C m year). At the same time, a rise in LST was recorded. Similar conditions were observed periodically over the years. Consequently, as PPT increased in the following years, all the above-mentioned PPT dependent variables also increased and NPP levels were recovered.

A huge decrease in NPP was observed in 2019 when it reached an annual average of 0.73 kg C m year. This change can be attributed to continuous low rainfall without respite since 2015 which steered a reduction of vegetation parameters in 2016. In the same period, the yearly maximum of TEMP and LST peaked at 30.3 C and 41 C, respectively causing heat stress, increased respiration rate, and creating feasible conditions for NPP to drop. However the NPP rose to a normal level after 2019 as PPT picked up followed by improvements in the other variables. Since 2019, LST has seen a declining trend (Table 1).

Response of NPP to land use transitions

During the first five years of the century, the average NPP in the forest was 1.23 kg C m year which decreased to 1.11 kg C m year in the last 5 years of the study, marking a decline despite an increase in forest area by 9432.07 sqkm, or 1.93% (Table 2). The total mean NPP comes up to 1.17 kg C m year, with a maximum (in 2002) and minimum (in 2019) amounting to 1.33 and 0.96 kg C m year respectively. The savannas have a mean NPP of 0.96 kg C m year, which peaked at 1.08 kg C m year in 2002 and dropped to 0.8 kg C m year in 2018. The change in area is not prominent with only a decrease of about 2100 sqkm when the NPP dropped from 1.05 to 0.95 kg C m year. Despite the reduction in croplands, the NPP remained relatively stable at 0.57 kg C m year. A significant decrease in the area of the shrublands has occurred from 35.51 sqkm in 2001 to 2.62 sqkm in 2022, almost a reduction of 92.63% over 22 years. The mean NPP in shrublands, urban area, and barren land is 0.85, 0.57, and 0.26 kg C m year respectively. The grassland area decreased from 7800 sqkm to 4900 sqkm during the study period where the average NPP in grasslands is 0.65 kg C m year (Table S5).

The trend in 2001–2006 is mainly shared between decreasing and increasing patterns with about 75,000 sqkm and 33,000 sqkm respectively. In the 2007–2012 period, a similar pattern in the overall trend was seen where about 72,000 sqkm and 37,000 sqkm of the area showed downward and upward trends respectively. A drastic change was seen in 2013–2018 when the declining trend dominated the region covering about 98,000 sqkm and a rise in the significant decreasing pattern was also witnessed in nearly 3000 sqkm of the region. A shift in the trend was noticed during 2019–2023 when about 94,000 sqkm of the area fell under an increasing trend and 5500 sqkm indicating a significantly increasing trend while the decreasing pattern was only left with about 9000 sqkm.

The Western Ghats has over the decades stood proof of the changing land use and fluctuating meteorological conditions. From 2001 to 2023 (Fig. S3), the region saw an increase in forests by 10,000 sqkm, built-up area by 10 sqkm, and water bodies by 65 sqkm. The reduction in area is seen in barren land by 95 sqkm, wetlands by 85 sqkm, and shrublands by 32 sqkm, while croplands, grasslands, and savannas were down by less than 4 sqkm. The overall trend is categorized into different classes based on significance levels, indicating areas of significantly decreasing (13,000 sqkm), decreasing (52,000 sqkm), increasing (30,000 sqkm), and significantly increasing (11,000 sqkm) scenario (Table S5).

Discussion

Rainfall has proven to be a crucial factor in deciding the NPP levels30 although a delayed effect is seen in the study (Table S8). Precipitation also indirectly decides the magnitude of NDVI, EVI, LAI, and FPAR through edaphic factors. The consequent delayed impact on vegetation health due to reduced precipitation is because it takes time to influence soil moisture followed by vegetation indices. Optimal temperature is necessary for balanced NPP as either extremes in temperature will cause stress, reducing NPP. LST can directly influence the plant temperature along with the soil moisture. An increased plant respiration and evapotranspiration leading to duress in vegetation is seen in higher LST which is more apparent during periods of reduced PPT. NDVI and EVI which are fed by precipitation are measures of density and health of vegetation and rise with NPP. The drought study in India31,32, observed that there was a monsoon drought during the year 2002, which was also reflected in the NPP in the area in the subsequent year. This could have happened because the NPP is strongly related to the soil moisture and the soil moisture decreases significantly, affecting soil moisture recovery, and plant water availability.

A noticeable dip in NPP in 2019 (Fig. 4) indicates a general declining trend in the productivity of forest areas, despite an overall increase in forests. The LAI for forests shows a slight increase from 2001 to 2022 but the increase is not substantial enough to offset the decline in NPP, suggesting that other factors (like tree density or health) might be influencing productivity. Another reason could be that due to the expansion of forest areas into less fertile regions or younger forests that are less productive. During the 2016–2019 period, when NPP dropped by 0.24 kg C m year, a decline in vegetation health and productivity is observed through reduced NDVI and EVI (Fig. 9). The decline of NPP in Savannas had a change rate of to %, indicating a possibility of ecological shifts or substantial reduction in trees outside forests or sparse trees33 during that period (Fig. S2). Moderate impact of LST was observed in the croplands, influencing productivity to some extent while indices such as FPAR and LAI remained stable indicating consistent photosynthetic activity and vegetation cover. This period corresponds to a high negative change rate, suggesting severe ecological stress or conversion to other land uses. Since the NDVI and EVI show significant declines, confirming reduced vegetation health and productivity and also the higher temperatures during the critical period exacerbated stress. The significant reduction in barren land and a slight increase in water bodies suggest efforts in land reclamation or natural changes.

The graphical representation of the result features the impact of different parameters in creating altering scenarios of NPP in the region. The NPP during 2003–2005 showed a decrease for a prolonged duration owing to increased LST, along with a decline in the indicated variables. In the years 2011, and 2022 a similar condition of higher NPP was recorded. This was preceded by high rainfall and reduced LST and temperature. The NPP witnessed the worst conditions in 2019 with very high temperatures and LST scenarios coupled with moderate rainfall and NDVI.

The region witnessed a decreasing trend in nearly of the area while saw an increasing trend. The significantly increasing and decreasing pattern occupied about and respectively. The increasing trend is spread out mainly in the northern end of the boundary from Vansda all the way down to the Chandoli Forest which covers the states of Gujarat and Maharashtra. Towards the eastern half, the significance of the trend is more pronounced as the slope is favorable for agricultural practices in comparison to the northwestern part. The scattered distribution of a significantly increasing trend is also seen near Periyar region among others all of which are found along the periphery and characterised by low elevation and gentle slope. It is noticed that most of the consequential rises in NPP fall under the Tropical-Savanna and Arid-Steppe-Hot climatic zones. The savannas throughout the area, show a combination of increasing and decreasing trends. Forest prominently shows a decreasing and significantly decreasing trend. The highly downward trend of NPP is observed in the lower elevation of Tropical-monsoon and Tropical-Savanna zones in Karnataka and Kerala (Fig. 8).

The magnitude of influence of each factor is largely indirect as none of the factor shows more than impact on NPP. It is clear that the anthropogenic factor indicated by LULC is the main variable in increasing NPP. Although it is common knowledge that the increase in human activity results in decreased forest cover and hence should bring down the NPP, this is not true in this case. We observe a rise in NPP prominently witnessed along the edges of the boundary where there is an increase in cropland which is a vital contributor. A high value of annual average NPP is found in the built-up as croplands surround them. The interaction detector aids in comprehending how each pair of variables impacts NPP detailed in Table 6. The type of interaction helps to understand whether the combined effects of variables are synergistic, additive, or antagonistic. All interactions with PPT result in a nonlinear enhancement, indicating a strong synergistic effect where the combination of PPT with other variables (like TEMP, LULC, etc.) produces a higher impact than the sum of their individual impacts. Most interactions involving TEMP result in a bi-variable enhancement, showing that the combined impact is greater than the individual impacts but less than their sum. The interaction with other variables such as vegetation indices (EVI, NDVI) and land use (LULC) suggests that moderate temperature changes, when combined with optimal land use and vegetation conditions, can significantly boost NPP. However, the interaction with slope results in a uni-variable weaken, indicating a weaker combined effect. LULC interactions show a bi-variable enhancement for all combinations, suggesting the interaction amplifies the effects but not to the extent of their sum. This highlights the significant role of land use practices and vegetation cover in modulating ecological productivity. Proper management and conservation of land resources in the Western Ghats can enhance the positive effects of climatic variables on NPP. EVI, NDVI, LST, LAI, and FPAR interactions generally exhibit bi-variable enhancement except when combined with slope, where a nonlinear enhance is observed. When combined with slope, the interactions show nonlinear enhancement, indicating that in areas with varied topography, the presence of healthy vegetation can lead to significantly higher NPP.

Despite the linear relationship linking EVI and NDVI, there is a striking difference in their impact on NPP which can be explained by the ability of EVI to highlight the thick vegetation cover. The MLR model revealed that NDVI had the highest estimate followed by the intercept value. The coefficient for precipitation is highly significant and positive across most years which directly implies an increase in NPP. However, in some years, such as 2006 and 2018, the coefficient is negative, suggesting that other factors might be interacting with precipitation to reduce NPP in those years. LST coefficients are consistently negative and highly significant, indicating that higher land surface temperatures generally reduce NPP.

Conclusion

The key indicators of NPP were PPT, NDVI, and LAI affecting positively, while EVI, and LST had generally a negative impact. The temperature displayed a varying effect depending on the year. The influence of predictors like temperature and precipitation on NPP is not uniform across years. This variability can be attributed to different environmental conditions, such as soil moisture, nutrient availability, and interaction with other climate factors. The consistently negative EVI coefficients suggest a complex dynamic where densely vegetated areas might not always translate to higher productivity. The power of the anthropogenic factor proved to be the highest followed by vegetation parameters driven by climatic conditions and barely any by the topographic indicator. The results from the interaction detector indicate that the interplay of precipitation, temperature, LULC, and vegetation indices significantly amplify NPP, highlighting the critical role of these variables in driving ecological productivity. The reduction in cropland area reflects ongoing changes in land use patterns within the Western Ghats, necessitating sustainable land management practices to maintain and potentially enhance cropland productivity amidst environmental and anthropogenic pressures. Although the average NPP of the Western Ghats saw a large drop in 2019, the ecological resilience helped the NPP to bounce back to the normal level by 2020. The NPP is decreasing in the forests even though the forests are increasing which may be attributed to the introduction of newer and younger forests. Apart from the chosen parameters, NPP is also influenced by soil moisture, level, humidity, dew point temperature, soil type and quality, wind, ecosystem type, and more. Future studies including all these parameters will provide more insights into the dynamics of NPP. In conclusion, the Western Ghats’ productivity is shaped by a combination of climatic variables, land use changes, and vegetation health. Effective management and conservation strategies are essential to mitigate the negative impacts and enhance the positive effects of these factors on NPP. The findings underscore the need for a holistic approach to preserve the biodiversity hotspot and sustain its ecological productivity. From a policy perspective, the results indicate a need to build a strong local governance through community based conservation efforts, and effective landscape level zoning. To sustain the forest productivity, as strict and locally driven conservation practices should be implemented to monitor the changing land use pattern. Integrating sustainable practices with stronger policy enforcement will be key to preserving the resilience of this critical biodiversity hotspot. The research underscores the urgency of integrating climate resilience into conservation strategies for the Western Ghats, renowned for its biodiversity and ecological importance, in response to significant climate threats.

Methods

Study area

The Western Ghats boasts of being a landscape with rich ecological diversity sustaining all the forest classifications from grasslands to tropical wet evergreen forests, flowering plants having about 4000 different species, and ample fauna34. The forest types in the Western Ghats characterized as non-equatorial tropical evergreen forests have four major phenological attributes. The majority of the area is occupied by moist deciduous forests succeeded by semi-evergreen forests which have a greater level of vegetation diversity, evergreen forests with maximum level of endemism, and lastly, the dry deciduous forests prevalent on the leeward side of the mountain34. The Western Ghats sees a shift in climate with proximity to the equator as the latitude changes from 8 07’ 58” N to 21 15’ 58” N and the longitude from 72 53’ 31” E to 78 03’ 45” E (Fig. 10). The area expands from the coastline to a height of 2619 m above the mean sea level witnessing a range of topographic and climatic conditions. Stretching to a length of approximately 1500 Km, the study area stretches for 107,770 sq km from the southern tip of India, the Kanyakumari in Kerala to south of Gujarat including the Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, and Tamilnadu states35. The annual average precipitation is about 190 cm whereas the mean maximum and minimum precipitation is about 590 cm and 40 cm respectively. The minimum and maximum temperature in the region average 15 C and 30 C respectively while the annual average is around 26 C. This mountain range surfaced as a significant uplift along a rift shoulder during the Deccan Trap volcanic activity at the boundary between the Late Cretaceous and Paleocene epochs36. Owing to the tectonic action during the formation period, the northern and southern Western Ghats display contrasting geology with the Deccan traps consisting of basalt in the north and Charnockites, Granulites, Khondalites, etc in the south34.

The research site accessorized with the climate classification and elevation.

Data

Terrestrial productivity in the Western Ghats is quantified using NPP as the primary indicator. To understand the influence of various factors on NPP, the climate indicators (precipitation, air temperature, and land surface temperature), topography (slope), vegetation indices (NDVI, EVI, LAI, and FPAR), and anthropogenic factors, specifically Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) changes were examined (Table S2).

NPP

The NPP refers to the fuel, fiber, and food source for the sustenance of human life. The spatial variation in the terrestrial biological productivity is crucial for measuring the global change pointing to the CO levels. This variation spans from approximately 30 gCm for desert landscapes to as high as 1000 gCm for the lush evergreen tropical rain forests. The product’s design is aimed to precisely measure the growth or activity of the terrestrial vegetation on a regular interval. Three major components form the basis for estimating terrestrial NPP from satellites. They include the direct relation of NPP to the absorbed solar energy, the theoretical link between satellite-derived vegetation characteristics and the absorbed solar energy, and the dissection of the reason for fluctuation in the efficiency of final conversion37.

The MOD17A3H MODIS/Terra Net Primary Production Yearly L4 Global 500 m product was used in the study. The crop productivity shows a linear relation with the quantity of the absorbed solar energy and the absorbed Photosynthetically Active Radiation (APAR) according to the radiation use efficiency logic proposed by Monteith38 which forms the basis for the MOD17 algorithm. The actual productivity is estimated by translating the APAR using the conversion efficiency parameter, , which is determined by the climatic condition and vegetation type. MOD17 also calculates the respiration parameters. Precipitation

Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation, version 2 (MSWEP V2)40 gridded precipitation product provides 3 hourly global data since 1979 with spatial resolution. The data is formulated by merging the precipitation estimates from multiple sources such as the gauge data, satellite, and reanalysis products. To enhance the accuracy in the frequency of precipitation, bias correction methods, and correction for regional variations are introduced. The spatial pattern of magnitude, frequency, and mean precipitation demonstrated by MSWEP V2 is highly realistic. Precipitation datasets usually underestimate the magnitude in regions with mountains which is overcome by MSWEP V2.

Temperature

The MSWX is the Multi-Source Weather near-surface global meteorological product for temperature with 3-h temporal resolution and spatial resolution41. The data is bias-corrected and with the help of reference climatological data, it is also down-scaled. The temperature information is obtained through a process based on cumulative distribution function (CDF)-matching. This process entails resampling the model data to 0.1 using nearest neighbor interpolation, converting the model data to indices via a static matrix of evenly spaced data points, choosing the relevant correction factors from another static matrix of correction factors based on these indices, and finally, applying these correction factors to the model data through multiplication.

LULC

This data plays a crucial role in understanding the changing features of the region. The Annual International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme (IGBP) classification from the MODIS Land Cover data (MCD12Q1.061) which has a 500 m spatial resolution and 17 classes is adopted for the study. The dataset is created and prepared by applying a decision tree algorithm based on the combination of MODIS NDVI, surface reflectance, albedo band parameters, and land surface temperature42. For the purpose of the study, as the 2023 LULC was not available, 2022 LULC was used for 2023 as well. The IGBP classification has 17 classes which were grouped into 9 classes based on their characteristics for better interpretation and understanding (Table S2).

NDVI

The MODIS NDVI product (MOD13Q1.061) is available with a spatial resolution of 500 m at 16-day intervals. The usage of NDVI in the biogeochemical and circulation models is crucial at all scales from local to global43.

where variables and represent the bidirectional reflectance factors for the corresponding MODIS bands at the surface level.

EVI

EVI is a vegetation monitoring tool with higher sensitivity than NDVI. The MODIS EVI product (MOD13Q1.061) uses the sensor’s near-infrared (), red (), and blue () bands. It is beneficial in studying the vegetation characteristics such as phenology, stress, and productivity in areas of dense biomass as it reduces the influence of atmosphere and surpasses the saturation problem present in NDVI. The calculation of EVI involves the soil-adjustment factor, coefficients of variation adjustments, and canopy background adjustments44.

Land surface temperature (LST)

The MODIS LST product (MOD11A2.061) which is a 8 day 1 Km resolution data is derived using the generalized split-window algorithm. Based on the land cover types, and atmospheric column water vapour, lower boundary air surface temperature, the 31 and 32 bands are estimated for the emissivities. Manageable sub-ranges of temperatures are calculated for efficient retrieval45.

LAI and FPAR

The LAI and FPAR products from MODIS (MOD15A2H.061) are developed from surface reflectances using the vegetation canopy radiative transfer algorithm46. This was put into implementation before the launch of MODIS on the TERRA platform.

Digital elevation model (DEM)

A globally continuous DEM with 30 m spatial resolution depends on applying radar interferometry algorithms to the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) data, which is then processed into a locally spherical coordinate system47.

GDM

Spatial heterogeneity is a fundamental characteristic of geographic phenomena. The models are statistically designed to detect spatially stratified heterogeneity and uncover the underlying driving forces27. The analysis of the spatial variability is covered by the GDM using the four geographical detectors. It is used to assess the influence of climatic and meteorological parameters on the productivity of the forest through geographical strata. The areas of risk are identified by the risk detector, and the factor responsible for it is recognized by the factor detector. The relative prominence of the parameters is revealed by the ecological factor and the interaction detector decides if a parameter can independently contribute to the fluctuation in the change in NPP51.

Factor detector

It is used to determine how much each natural factor contributes to the spatial distribution of NPP. The goal is to assess how each natural factor individually impacts the spatial distribution of NPP. The methodology involves several steps: first, spatial superposition and analysis of the NPP layer and natural factor layers; second, division of the natural factors into different spatial categories or classes; and finally, calculation of the mean differences of these natural factors. The significance test is then used to determine the relative importance of each natural factor. The F-test determines if there is a significant difference in the variances.
