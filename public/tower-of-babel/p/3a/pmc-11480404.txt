Lithium enrichment in high-enthalpy geothermal system influenced by seawater, Indonesia
Authors: Daffa Arrofi, Israa S. Abu-Mahfouz, Sinatrya Diko Prayudi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Lithium holds significant importance in energy storage and can be extracted from geothermal water, making it an additional resource to traditional mining. Combining lithium extraction from geothermal water with electricity generation from geothermal energy offers a compelling strategy to address future energy challenges. Situated in Southeast Sumatra, Indonesia, Way Ratai has considerable geothermal potential and is known for its hot springs. This study primarily aims to characterize lithium enrichment within the Way Ratai geothermal system. Water samples from various sources (one spring, seven hot springs, one well, and seawater) were analyzed for cations, anions, trace elements, and isotopic composition. The results show that Way Ratai is an old geothermal system with sodium-chloride water, where lithium enrichment is attributed to interactions between water and rocks, with some influence from seawater. The discrepancy between calculated and observed magnesium (Mg) concentrations suggests a pre-heating mixing process between rainwater and seawater. The reservoir rock in Way Ratai is mostly andesitic, with temperatures ranging from 182o to 227oC. This study enhances our understanding of thermal water characteristics, reservoir temperatures, and water origins in high enthalpy geothermal systems, offering valuable insights into lithium enrichment in coastal geothermal regions.

Introduction

Lithium (Li) is one of the economically essential elements for energy storage, with almost 87% of global lithium consumption used in Li-ion rechargeable batteries1. It has a large consumption rate that is growing yearly. Lithium demand has significantly increased since 2016 and is forecasted to exceed 0.38 MTons by 20282. In 2022, global lithium production rose by 23% from the previous year to about 130,000 tonnes due to high demand from the lithium-ion battery market and rising lithium prices3. The primary exploitation of lithium currently comes from the hard rocks, dominated by granites and pegmatites, and has exceeded the demand for the next decade4,5. Despite the surplus, potential mining challenges such as environmental and social concerns could affect future production stability6. This could create a gap between production and demand, especially as demand continues to increase. Therefore, exploring additional resources such as lithium extracted from brine or geothermal water, has the potential to significantly contribute to meeting future global demands.

Brine water in sedimentary basins has been widely recognized to have a high potential for unconventional lithium extraction due to its high concentration7–11. In many sedimentary formations, waters are typically produced in large quantities, with lithium concentrations within these waters being widely reported. Particularly, these include in oil/gas fields, low-enthalpy geothermal systems, and carbon capture and storage (CCS) operations in saline reservoirs. Several studies have investigated the water-rock interaction enriching the lithium concentration in brine water12–16. However, exploiting lithium from brine water is unprofitable due to the high Mg/Li ratio, exceeding 10 for its ratio17,18. Hence, the cost-effective separation of lithium from magnesium is significantly challenging19. Moreover, brine water formation typically forms due to long geological processes20–22. Compared to brines, geothermal water has a relatively lower Mg/Li ratio even though the Li concentration is also typically lower23. This happened because high fluid salinity and temperature mainly controlled lithium concentration12. According to an experimental study by24, the Mg/Li ratio decreases as the temperature of the solution increases, commonly involving hydrothermal or volcanic processes. Therefore, the extraction of lithium from geothermal water could be more efficient. In addition, extracting lithium from geothermal water could reduce the environmental impacts and be economically viable for long-term investment together for electricity generation25,26.

The high concentrations of Na and Cl significantly increase the lithium content in hydrothermal processes12,24. Furthermore, the higher temperature during water-rock interactions correlates with the enriching lithium content in thermal water24, making seawater mixing in a high-enthalpy geothermal system a promising method of lithium extraction alongside electricity generation. Although lithium enrichment in water sources has been widely studied and documented in many settings, including the water-rock interaction, the temperature effect on lithium enrichment, and the correlation between the enrichment of lithium and other elements5,8,12,17,24, there is still limited study about lithium enrichment in geothermal systems mixing with seawater. Therefore, this study mainly aims to identify the lithium enrichment in a high enthalpy geothermal system influenced by seawater recharge, improving the characterization of lithium enrichment in high-enthalpy geothermal systems, particularly near the coastal area.

Geological setting

The study area, which is located in the Way Ratai Geothermal System, Southeastern Sumatra (Indonesia), is classified as a high-temperature geothermal system with liquid-dominated but unidentified sources27 (Fig. 1). Sumatra is recognized by the active subduction of the Indian Ocean Plate with the Eurasian Continental Plate from the tertiary to the quaternary period28. Consequently, an active volcanic arc along the western part of Sumatra was formed, with 11 active volcanoes27. This arc is bisected by the deep crustal Sumatra Fault Zone (also known as the Barisan Fault Zone), which is characterized by right-lateral strike-slip movement and is associated with a deeper E-W-aligned fracture zone29. The fracture zone allows the circulation of deep circulating meteoric water, transferring heat from hot crustal rocks and cooling intrusion to the surface in high-temperature geothermal systems27. Geothermal heat flow in Sumatra is estimated to be from 37 W/m2 to 369 W/m2, with the lowest values observed in the northern part and increasing towards the south30.

Generally, the morphology of the study area is a composite of a stratovolcano with two prominent volcanic cones, namely Mount Ratai and Mount Betung31. Both are characterized by the NW-SE and NE-SW faults and lineaments32,33. The study area consists of several rock sequences which are dominated by recent volcanic deposits (Qhv), such as andesite to basalt lava, breccia, and tuff32 (Fig. 2).

Several studies have been conducted within Way Ratai Geothermal Systems, but none have specifically focused on lithium enrichment in geothermal water31,34–39. The hot springs have 89oC as the maximum temperature and neutral pH NaCl water, with the estimated reservoir temperature between 174oC and 210oC27,35,39. Most hot springs are identified within the high lineament density and high radon-thoron anomaly area31,35. The reservoir depth was estimated to reach up to 3.5 km at the subsurface, with the reservoir thickness range around 0.5 to 1.5 km37,40 (Fig. 3).

Methodology

Sampling

This study mainly used hydrochemical analysis of hot springs to investigate lithium enrichment in the Way Ratai Geothermal System. Ten samples were collected from different locations, including seven samples from hot springs, one warm water sample from a well drilled at shallow depth, one cold spring water sample, and one seawater sample (Figs. 2 and 4). Hotsprings are spatially distributed near the coastal area, around 4 km from the shoreline and 9 Km from the Way Ratai summit (Fig. 2). The temperature, pH, and total dissolved solids (TDS) were measured in the field. Temperature and TDS were measured using a handheld-multiparameter HFTDS-M7 that had been calibrated using commercial standard solution at room temperature around 25oC, while pH was measured using a pH test pen with 0.01 accuracy precision that also had been calibrated using buffer solutions at pH 4 and pH 7. TDS and pH were re-measured in the laboratory before further analysis was conducted. Each sample was collected and stored in a 250 ml High-Density Polyethylene (HDPE) bottle; the bottle was rinsed three times with each water sample before the sample took place. Water sample filtration using a 0.45 μm string was done for all samples. Then, some samples were acidified with supra-pure HNO3 to determine cations and SiO2 analysis, and the others were kept unacidified for anion and isotopic analysis. The bottle was tightly sealed, particularly in the cap, to make sure no leaking was happening. The collected samples were kept in a 4oC refrigerator before being transported to the laboratory for further analysis.

Laboratory analysis

The water samples were comprehensively analyzed at the College of Petroleum Engineering and Geosciences, King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals. Major cations and anions were analyzed using the Dionex ICS-6000 Ion Chromatograph, following the EPA 9056 A standard method41. TDS and pH were re-measured using a multiparameter Hanna HI 9828 to validate the field measurements. The results showed no significant difference in TDS between field and laboratory measurements, while pH was slightly more alkaline in the laboratory, with a maximum deviation of approximately 0.5 units from the field measurements across all samples. Then, the samples were diluted according to their value with a total volume of 10 ml of water sample and distilled water to ensure that no solute was precipitated in the machine and caused an error during the analysis. Lithium enrichment identification

This study predominantly utilized the chemical composition of the geothermal water collected from hot springs to investigate the lithium enrichment within the system. Following the classification of water types, identifying seawater mixing is imperative because the area from which the samples were collected is adjacent to the coastal region (Fig. 2). The identification of seawater mixing assumes critical importance as it enables the estimation of mixing ratios. Otherwise, if seawater mixing is not identified, the potential lithium enrichment mechanism in geothermal water is due to water-rock interaction (Fig. 5). Subsequently, determining the timing of heating relative to the mixing process in the reservoir becomes pivotal to understanding the influence of seawater on the lithium enrichment process whether there is seawater mixing in the system. If heating occurs before mixing, the lithium enrichment could be attributed to the mixing process with seawater. In contrast, if the heating occurs after seawater mixing, water-rock interaction emerges as the primary mechanism affecting lithium enrichment. Therefore, estimating reservoir temperature becomes crucial. Cation geothermometers were applied to estimate the reservoir temperatures for the chemical composition of the geothermal water. The results were compared with the integrated multicomponent geothermometry (IMG), calculated using the IGeoT 2.1 - verion (v1.0) (https://marketplace.lbl.gov/geoenvironment/water-and-the-environment/igeot_1-0) software package45. This software uses comprehensive chemical analyses of water samples and the mineralogical compositions of geothermal reservoirs to compute the saturation indices of reservoir minerals over a temperature range of 100oC to 260oC. The United States Geological Surveys’ PHREEQC package was also employed to calculate the saturation indices for some minerals46. Since there is limited information about minerals within the reservoir, an initial assumption of mineral composition was assigned. However, it is well-known that achieving chemical equilibrium with a substantial number of minerals is improbable in natural systems. Therefore, an elimination algorithm within IGeoT was employed to reduce the number of minerals into eight minerals47. Subsequent numerical optimization was conducted using the statistical function of the Log (Q/K) or saturation indices (SI), including the mean of residuals (RMED), root mean square error (RMSE), standard deviation (SDEV), and MEAN. Furthermore, the estimated reservoir temperature serves as a critical parameter for assessing reservoir depth. Hence, the relation between lithium and ionic composition and depth can be determined well. Consequently, establishing the relationship between lithium content, ionic composition, and depth enhances a comprehensive understanding of lithium enrichment within the system. Results

Hydrochemistry

Water temperature ranges from 26oC to 72oC, with the lowest recorded at spring (WR-SP) and the highest at hot spring. Most of the hot spring samples have high temperatures, above 42.5oC, and are considered geothermal water. The water collected from the well (WR-W) is also classified as thermal water, with an in-situ temperature measurement of 39.4oC, exceeding the average surface temperature recorded in 2023, which was 29.55oC48. The pH values remain slightly alkaline and comparable across all samples, ranging from 7.19 to 7.87 at around 25oC. The chemical composition is primarily characterized by sodium (Na) and chloride (Cl) in hot spring and seawater samples, Na and bicarbonate (HCO3) in the well sample, calcium (Ca) and HCO3 for spring water (see Table 1; Fig. 6). Sulfate (SO4) exhibits as a minor cation in all samples, except seawater, where HCO3 is observed as the lowest cation. Potassium (K) acts as a minor anion in seawater, well, and spring samples, while magnesium (Mg) is the minor anion notably observed in hot spring samples. Lithium (Li) is only detected in hotspring samples, ranging from 1.24 to 8.11 mg/L with an average is 5.74 mg/L. The lithium concentration in seawater is very low due to high dilution or less lithium leaching discharged to the seawater, even though most of the lithium reserves are located within the seawater49. Boron (B) concentrations are below 1 mg/L in all samples except for the seawater sample, which shows a higher concentration (3.24 mg/L). All of the geothermal samples exhibit Li/B ratios rangin from 3.46 to 32.1 with an average of 19.18. In contrast, both spring and well samples show a comparatively low Li/B ratio of 1.56, while seawater present an ever lower ratio of 0.02. Bromine (Br) levels vary across samples, with the highest concentration observed in the seawater sample. Silicon dioxide (SiO2) concentrations were measured exclusively in geothermal water samples, with most values below the detection limit of 17.33 mg/L, except for samples WR-4 and WR-5. Discussion

Characteristics of water samples

Piper diagram depicts the interrelationship existing between cations (Na + K, Ca, and Mg) and anions (HCO3, Cl, and SO4) to classify water type50. The diagram exhibits two main water types: sodium-chloride (Na-Cl) in hot springs and seawater samples, and calcium-bicarbonate (Ca-HCO3) in well and spring water samples (Fig. 7). Despite both hotspring and well samples being defined as thermal water, they are classified into distinct water types.

Figure 7Piper diagram exhibits the type of water samples.

Piper diagram exhibits the type of water samples.

Na and Cl are the typical major ions in seawater51. However, in geothermal water, their abundance is influenced by water-rock interaction or seawater mixing22,52–54. As the hot springs are adjacent to coastal areas, seawater mixing is plausible. Nonetheless, high concentrations of Na in high-temperature geothermal systems typically exceed 400 mg/L with high salinity55–57. Most of the hot spring samples in this study, except WR-5, have Na concentration above 1000 mg/L, posing a challenge for determining if it caused by the mixing of seawater or due to typical high-temperature geothermal water, hence, evaluating salinity is crucial for geothermal water. In contrast, Cl concentrations remain relatively unaffected by water-rock interactions, even under high-temperature conditions58. Thus, the high chloride content in alkali-chloride or neutral-chloride water types is typical of the deep geothermal fluid found in most high-temperature systems59. Therefore, this suggests that geothermal Na-Cl water types originated from deep reservoirs, as evidenced by their slightly alkaline properties (Table 1).

Conversely, the Ca-HCO3 water type observed in well and spring samples suggests the result of steam and gas condensation of CO2 into poorly-oxygenated subsurface groundwaters59. This water type is typically found in the low terrain or periphery of geothermal systems, indicating the outflow zone in the system27,59,60. Despite Ca is considered the dominant cation in this water type, its concentration remains relatively low (Table 1). This is common in high-temperature fluids where the Ca concentration is below 50 mg/L59.

Seawater influence

Gibbs diagrams illustrate the dominant factors affecting water salinization69. The ratio of Na and Na + Ca relative to TDS indicates seawater mixing in the hot spring samples, and dominance of water-rock interaction in well and spring samples (Fig. 10.a). Similarly, the ratios of Cl and Cl + HCO3 relative to TDS allow to distinguish the seawater influence in hotspring samples from the rock dominantly influenced of well and spring samples (Fig. 10b). Thus, according to the Gibbs diagrams, the salinization of hotsprings samples is primarily influenced by seawater mixing, while spring and well samples are predominantly influenced by water-rock interaction.

Furthermore, the influence of seawater on the samples is discerned through the Na/Cl ratio and SO4/Cl ratios (Fig. 11). Lithium enrichment in way ratai geothermal system

Li/TDS ratio represents the concentration of Lithium within thermal water (Table 6). The value ranges from 0.08 to 0.2% with an average of 0.14%. Notably, the values are relatively lower than comparable systems of high-temperature geothermal systems in Southern Xizang, China, ranging from 0.25 to 1.14% 8. The observed difference is possibly influenced by the composition of the rock interacting with geothermal water. High Li content relative to other ions within the water suggests an intense interaction with granitic rock8,86. However, this study reports a lower Li/TDS ratio compared to finding from a similar system8. Since the granitic rocks are Li-bearing rocks87, they are expected to release more lithium into geothermal water at high temperatures, leading to a higher Li/TDS ratio. In contrast, rocks with lower lithium content, such as andesitic, basaltic, or sedimentary rocks, would likely exhibit a lower Li/TDS ratio when interacting with geothermal water. The low Br content in the geothermal waters of this study indicates that the geothermal system is more likely to interact with andesitic or basaltic rocks. This is because sedimentary rocks typically have Br concentration exceeding 100 mg/L, and are not prominently characterized in this system (Table 1)12,59. The interpretation of intense interaction with andesitic–basaltic rock is consistent with the geological setting, where the Ratai volcano is dominated by andesitic-to-basaltic young volcanic deposits (Qhv) (Fig. 2). Moreover, a moderate positive correlation between the depth and the Li/TDS ratio (R2 = 0.68) suggests an enrichment of lithium content as geothermal water ascends towards the surface (Fig. 17a).

This study reveals a strong negative correlation between depth and the Mg/TDS ratio (R2 = 0.75) (Fig. 17b), indicating a decrease in the Mg/TDS ratio as the water ascends towards the surface. Chloride-rich water typically has a low Mg concentration at high temperatures, supported by the negative correlation between Mg concentration and temperature16. However, this study reveals the opposite trend, as temperature decreases at shallower depths. Therefore, the observed decline in the Mg/TDS ratio with shallower depth suggests a significant role in water-rock interaction, particularly with a minor magnesium rock type.

The Mg/Li ratio of geothermal water samples exhibits a range from 2.54 to 11.51, with an average of 5.81 (Table 6). This range shows a significantly lower ratio compared to the majority of salt-lake brines, typically reaching up to 500 17. Furthermore, a strong inverse correlation between the depth and Mg/Li ratio (R2 = 0.94) is also observed. This implies a constant decreasing Mg/Li ratio as the geothermal water flows towards the surface (Fig. 17c).

The Li content and the Mg/Li ratio relative to Cl in this study were compared with various geothermal systems both influenced and unaffected by seawater (Fig. 18). Cl is a key element that can enhance Li enrichment in geothermal water23. In seawater-influenced geothermal systems, the addition of Cl facilitates increased Li concentrations (Fig. 18a). However, temperature and the composition of the host rock also significantly impact Li enrichment. Higher temperatures accelerate water-rock interaction, and rocks with higher Li content release more Li during these interactions24. Although seawater acts as a catalyst for Li enrichment, systems with substantial seawater influence often exhibit lower Li concentrations than expected due to reduced contributions from seawater (Fig. 18a). Thus, a moderate level of seawater mixing is optimal for effective Li enrichment, and the timing of this mixing relative to reservoir heating is critical. There is no significant difference in the Mg/Li ratio between seawater-influenced and non-influenced geothermal systems despite the higher Cl content in the seawater-influenced systems (Fig. 18b). This could be attributed to the type of the reservoir rock and the rate of seawater mixing, as increased magnesium corresponds with the higher seawater content. The decreasing Mg/Li ratio is seawater-influenced systems correlates with reduced seawater contributions. This pattern may also result from water-rock interactions at specific temperatures, leading to Mg-mineral precipitation or Li dissolution due to water-rock interaction.

The analysis of cation reactions in geothermal water and well samples using Eq. 4, focusing on Mg, Na, K, Ca, and Li, reveals notable variations that indicate processes of ion depletion and enrichment, as shown by their respective positive and negative values (Fig. 19). Mg shows positive values, while Na, K, Ca, and Li generally exhibit negative values across all geothermal water samples. Li does not integrate into feldspar structures and has a very low capacity to substitute for sodium and potassium in feldspar rather than magnesium during these reactions88. This pattern is indicative of an action exchange mechanism occurring during water-rock interaction, where Mg is precipitated into the rock, while the other cations, including Li, are released into the solution, enabling the lower Mg/Li ratio as the more intense water-rock interaction93. The observed predominance of cation enrichment in the solution may account for the deviation of most geothermal waters from the full equilibrium line (see Fig. 14).

Additionally, the Mg precipitation is also exhibited from the saturation indices of minerals. Saturation indices for some minerals exhibit the precipitation and dissolution reactions occurring between geothermal water and the surrounding reservoir rock, as indicated by their positive and negative values (Fig. 20). Aragonite, calcite, and dolomite exhibit oversaturation in all samples, suggesting the precipitation of Ca, Mg, and carbonate (CO3) minerals. This is consistent with the precipitation of Mg from the calculated reaction of Mg, even Ca is dissolved in the solution (Fig. 19). The dissolution of Ca in the solution is attributed to the higher rate of Ca released from other types of minerals rather than precipitated to form calcite and aragonite. Conversely, anhydrite, gypsum, and halite are undersaturated, which promotes the dissolution of Na-, Cl-, and SO4–bearing minerals. The very low of saturation indices of halite could be the reason why the geothermal water samples are dominated by Na-Cl composition. The saturation indices for quartz and chalcedony are near equilibrium, with quartz emerging as the more stable silica phase in all samples except for WR-4 and WR-5.

Initially, both meteoric and seawater do not have lithium content (see the Li concentration in Table 1). Therefore, seawater mixing has no significant influence on the lithium enrichment of geothermal water if this process is not followed by the interaction with sourrounding rock. Moreover78, have suggested that seawater, being the sole recharge source for the reservoir, has a minor role in the ion migration from volcanic rock to thermal water since it is already saturated with several ions such as Na+, Cl−, and SO42. However, the addition of NaCl to fresh water in high-temperature (> 200oC) environments significantly enhances lithium enrichment in thermal water when interacting with rock24. Consequently, seawater influences lithium enrichment when mixed with rainwater. Following heating, the geothermal water flows ascending to the surface and interacts with the surrounding rocks. The increased ion concentrations within geothermal water arise from mineral dissolutions in the rock24,78. The observed lithium enrichment within the geothermal water is attributed to this process, as shown by a strong positive correlation (R2 = 0.92) between the depth and lithium content (Fig. 21b). This suggests the lithium content within the water increases with the shallower depths, sourced by mineral dissolution from andesitic to basaltic rock type during their interaction.

Conclusions

This study investigated the lithium enrichment mechanism in the Way Ratai Geothermal System, using the hydrochemical analysis of hot springs. The key findings are concluded as follows:

The geothermal water in the Way Ratai Geothermal System is classified as Na-Cl type, suggesting a typical significant reservoir depth. However, this study finds that the Na-Cl water is also due to seawater mixing within the reservoir. The origin of geothermal water involves a mixing process between rainwater and seawater, with rainwater being the dominant component. The pre-heating mixing results in geothermal water with elevated salinity. As a result, the higher salinity enhances the water’s ability to dissolve lithium from surrounding rock formations during subsequent high-temperature interactions. In contrast, if mixing occurs after the geothermal water has been heated, the interaction with the rock is less effective, leading to a reduced capacity for lithium dissolution.The Na/K, Na-K-Ca, and Na-K-Mg geothermometers estimate that the reservoir temperature ranges from 182oC to 227oC, increasing with depth. These temperatures are consistent with the calculated temperature from integrated multicomponent geothermometry, implying that cation geothermometers remain applicable for estimating temperatures in deep-seawater-influenced reservoirs.Lithium enrichment is attributed to intense high-temperature water-rock interaction with andesitic rock formations within the Way Ratai Geothermal System. Additionally, water-rock interaction at high temperatures reduces the Mg/Li ratio, potentially enhancing the economic viability of lithium extraction from geothermal water.
