Blood pressure variability and the risk of post-stroke cognitive impairment following atrial fibrillation related acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Dong A Yea, Sangwon Choi, Minwoo Lee, Mi-Sun Oh, Kyung-Ho Yu, Byung Chul Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood-pressure variability (BPV) has been associated with adverse outcomes after stroke, but its relevance to post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) remains unclear, particularly in cardioembolic stroke. We investigated the association between acute-phase systolic and diastolic BPV and PSCI in patients with atrial fibrillation–related ischemic stroke.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of a prospective stroke registry including consecutive patients with cardioembolic stroke who underwent standardized neuropsychological assessment at 3 months post-stroke. PSCI was defined as any domain z-score < −2 after adjustment for age, sex, and education. BPV metrics were derived from all systolic and diastolic measurements obtained during the first 7 days of hospitalization. Multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for demographic factors, vascular risk factors, initial National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, and neuroimaging characteristics.ResultsAmong 143 patients (mean age 70 years; 60.1% male; median NIHSS 5), PSCI occurred in 67 (46.9%). Higher short-term variability in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) was independently associated with PSCI. Each 1-standard deviation (SD) increase in SBP SD conferred an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 1.09 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.01–1.17), and each 1-SD increase in diastolic BP SD an aOR of 1.14 (95% CI, 1.01–1.29). Mean SBP and DBP were also independently associated with PSCI.ConclusionsIn patients with cardioembolic stroke, short-term variability in both SBP and DBP during the acute phase was independently associated with PSCI. Acute BPV may represent a modifiable hemodynamic marker of early cognitive vulnerability, warranting further investigation in larger and mechanistic studies.Keywords: Blood pressure variability, Post stroke cognitive impairment, Atrial fibrillation, Acute ischemic stroke

BACKGROUND

Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is a frequent neurological consequence of ischemic stroke, with prevalence estimates ranging from 20% to 75% across different study populations and diagnostic criteria [1,2]. PSCI is associated with higher risks of recurrent cerebrovascular events and mortality [2,3] and contributes to long-term individual burden, reduced social functioning, and deterioration in health-related quality of life [4]. Despite its substantial clinical impact, determinants of PSCI—particularly those that may be modifiable during the acute phase of stroke—remain insufficiently characterized. Identifying early physiological markers that influence post-stroke cognitive trajectories is therefore essential for improving long-term neurological outcomes [5].

Blood pressure (BP) is a major modifiable vascular risk factor in ischemic stroke and PSCI [6,7]. Beyond absolute blood-pressure levels, blood-pressure variability (BPV) may reflect impaired cerebral autoregulation and unstable cerebral perfusion, pathophysiological processes linked to secondary brain injury after acute cerebrovascular events [8]. Although higher BPV has been associated with adverse functional outcomes following stroke [9,10], evidence connecting acute-phase BPV to cognitive outcomes is limited. Recent reviews have highlighted BP and BPV as underrecognized contributors to PSCI and emphasized the need for studies clarifying their modifying effects and defining appropriate BPV metrics [2]. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant in atrial fibrillation (AF)-related ischemic stroke, a biologically distinct subtype characterized by larger infarcts, poorer collateral circulation, and less favorable clinical outcomes than atherosclerotic stroke [11]. Because AF-related stroke is associated with pronounced hemodynamic instability and impaired cerebral autoregulation during the acute phase [12], it represents a particularly suitable clinical model in which short-term BPV is more likely to exert downstream effects on cerebral perfusion and large-scale cognitive networks. AF-related strokes comprise approximately 14–30% of ischemic strokes and show higher rates of disability, mortality, and early and late embolic recurrence [13], making the identification of acute hemodynamic determinants of cognition especially important in this population. Moreover, restricting the study population to AF-related stroke reduces etiologic heterogeneity and allows a more precise examination of the mechanistic relationship between acute-phase BPV and post-stroke cognitive impairment.

To address these gaps, this study investigated the association between acute-phase BPV and PSCI in patients with AF-related acute ischemic stroke. Multiple systolic and diastolic BPV metrics measured during the first 7 days after stroke were evaluated to determine whether short-term hemodynamic fluctuations are associated with global and domain-specific cognitive outcomes at 3–6 months.

METHODS

Study population

This study is a retrospective analysis of a prospective stroke registry cohort at Hallym University Sacred Heart Hospital. We analyzed patients enrolled between January 2011 and December 2021. At the time of admission, written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives for inclusion in the registry. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hallym University Sacred Heart Hospital approved the present study and waived additional consent because of its retrospective design and minimal risk to participants (IRB No. 2023-01-026).

The inclusion criteria for this study were: 1) consecutive patients diagnosed with AF-related acute ischemic stroke; 2) presence of a relevant ischemic lesion on diffusion-weighted imaging; 3) admission within 7 days of symptom onset; 4) availability of serial BP measurements during the first 7 days of hospitalization; and 5) completion of the Korean Vascular Cognitive Impairment Harmonization Standards–Neuropsychological Protocol neuropsychological test battery at 3 months after stroke. Participants were excluded if they had premorbid dementia, defined as an Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) > 3.6 or a documented diagnosis of dementia; a premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score > 2; or neurological or functional limitations precluding neuropsychological assessment, including severe aphasia, hearing difficulty, poor cooperation, or profound motor deficits. After applying these criteria, 143 patients comprised the final study population. All required clinical, imaging, and BP variables were available without missing data; therefore, all analyses were performed using the full study population.

Clinical variables

Baseline demographic variables included age, sex, years of education, and pre-stroke mRS. Index stroke characteristics included initial stroke severity, measured using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and type of reperfusion therapy (no thrombolysis, intravenous thrombolysis alone, endovascular thrombectomy alone, or combined therapy). Vascular risk factors recorded at admission were hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking history, and previous stroke. Brain imaging characteristics were evaluated on diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging. Acute ischemic lesions were classified by cortical or subcortical involvement, hemispheric laterality (left, right, or bilateral), and lesion multiplicity (single or multiple). White-matter hyperintensity burden was assessed using the modified Fazekas scale. Routine laboratory data—including white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, creatinine, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and fasting glucose—were collected at presentation to describe baseline systemic status.

BPV metrics

BP was measured using automated oscillometric devices as part of routine clinical monitoring. Manual BP measurements were not included, and the automated devices used did not incorporate AF-specific detection or correction algorithms. In general, BP was assessed 3 times daily, with additional measurements obtained at the discretion of treating clinicians when clinically indicated. All patients had at least 3 systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) values available, and all measurements recorded in the electronic medical record were extracted for analysis. Short-term BPV was quantified using multiple time-domain indices that have been applied in previous BPV studies and reflect distinct aspects of intraindividual fluctuation [14,15,16].

All BPV indices were calculated separately for SBP and DBP. In addition, mean SBP and mean DBP across the 7-day period were included as covariates in multivariable analyses.

Neuropsychological outcome

Neuropsychological assessment was conducted at 3 months after the index stroke using the Korean version of the Vascular Cognitive Impairment Harmonization Standards–Neuropsychological Protocol. The battery comprises a standardized 60-minute evaluation covering four cognitive domains: executive/activation, memory, language, and visuospatial functions. The executive/activation domain was assessed using the Controlled Oral Word Association Test (phonemic fluency), semantic fluency (animal naming), the Digit Symbol Coding Test, and the Korean version of the Trail Making Test for elderly adults. The memory domain was evaluated using the Seoul Verbal Learning Test, including immediate recall, delayed recall, and recognition tasks. The language domain was assessed using the short form A of the Korean version of the Boston Naming Test, and the visuospatial domain was evaluated using the copy condition of the Rey Complex Figure Test. Global cognition was additionally measured using the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination. Scores from all neuropsychological tests were converted into age-, sex-, and education-adjusted z-scores based on established normative data. Domain-specific z-scores were calculated as the mean of adjusted component test scores within each cognitive domain. Premorbid cognitive status was evaluated using the Korean version of the IQCODE. The primary outcome, PSCI, was defined as performance more than two standard deviations below the normative mean (z-score < −2) in at least one of the four cognitive domains, consistent with operational definitions used in prior PSCI research.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with SDs or as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. Group differences between patients with and without PSCI were assessed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The associations between individual BPV metrics and PSCI were examined using multivariable logistic regression models. Age and sex were included in all multivariable models a priori, given their established associations with both AF-related stroke and PSCI. Additional covariates were selected based on established clinical relevance and variables demonstrating a P-value < 0.10 in univariable analyses. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for each BPV index. To evaluate dose-response patterns, BPV metrics were additionally analyzed according to quartiles, and their associations with PSCI were estimated using logistic regression with the lowest quartile as the reference category. Domain-specific cognitive z-scores were compared across BPV quartiles using analysis of covariance, adjusting for age, sex, education, and initial NIHSS score.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0 or later; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 143 patients with AF-related acute ischemic stroke were included (Fig. 1), of whom 67 (46.9%) developed PSCI at 3 months. Baseline demographic characteristics were similar between patients with and without PSCI, including age (70.8 ± 9.9 vs. 69.2 ± 11.1 years, P = 0.384), sex (61.2% vs. 59.2% male, P = 0.944), and years of education. In contrast, initial stroke severity differed between groups, with higher NIHSS scores observed in the PSCI group (8.1 ± 6.9 vs. 5.7 ± 5.7, P = 0.024). Vascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking, and prior stroke, showed no significant differences, and the distribution of reperfusion therapies also did not differ between groups. On diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging, strategic lesion involvement and white-matter hyperintensity severity did not vary significantly by cognitive outcome (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that short-term BPV during the acute phase of AF-related ischemic stroke was independently associated with post-stroke cognitive impairment at 3 months. In particular, both systolic and diastolic standard deviation measures showed robust associations with PSCI after adjustment for demographic, clinical, and imaging covariates, indicating that variability in BP, rather than absolute values alone, contributes to early cognitive vulnerability in this population. In contrast, BPV exhibited only minimal influence on domain-specific cognitive performance, with no statistically significant differences across executive, memory, visuospatial, or language domains. Taken together, these findings suggest that acute hemodynamic instability may primarily affect global cognitive integrity rather than selectively impairing individual cognitive domains in patients with cardioembolic stroke. Although absolute DBP levels were also associated with post-stroke cognitive impairment and are clinically relevant, our study was primarily focused on BPV during the acute phase of AF-related stroke.

While interpreting these findings, it is important to consider the measurement characteristics of BP in AF. Single oscillometric readings can be less accurate, however, evidence from ambulatory BP monitoring studies—including the TEMPLAR project—and validation analyses of non-invasive BP devices in AF demonstrate that repeated measurements remain reliable, and variability indices derived from serial readings preserve clinically meaningful signal [17,18]. Therefore, although AF and heterogeneity in the number and timing of BP measurements may introduce nondifferential measurement noise, this would be expected to attenuate rather than exaggerate observed associations.

Several studies have linked BPV to functional and cognitive outcomes after stroke. The PICASSO substudy demonstrated that higher visit-to-visit BPV independently predicted faster long-term cognitive decline [19], and mid-term BPV has similarly been associated with poorer cognitive performance, progressive white-matter injury, and adverse neurological outcomes [14]. Acute-phase BPV is also clinically relevant, as increased short-term variability has been associated with early neurological deterioration [16,20] and greater risk of hemorrhagic transformation following reperfusion therapy [20]. However, these investigations were largely conducted in mixed stroke populations, and evidence in AF-related stroke is lacking.

The AF-related ischemic stroke subtype represents a biologically vulnerable group in whom the impact of BPV may be amplified. Cardioembolic strokes exhibit poorer collateral circulation, larger infarct volumes, and worse functional outcomes than atherosclerotic strokes [11], and dynamic cerebral autoregulation is frequently impaired in acute cardioembolic stroke. These features increase susceptibility to perfusion instability and secondary injury when short-term BP fluctuations occur. Moreover, AF is independently associated with greater long-term cognitive decline and dementia risk, suggesting an inherent vulnerability of cognitive networks [21,22]. By evaluating acute-phase systolic and diastolic BPV specifically in AF-related stroke, our study addresses a critical gap in the literature and highlights acute hemodynamic instability as a potential early marker of PSCI in this high-risk subgroup. Although AF-related stroke is more prevalent among older individuals and women, baseline age and sex distributions did not differ significantly between patients with and without PSCI in our cohort. The associations between BPV and PSCI were therefore unlikely to be explained solely by demographic differences.

BPV may exert its influence on cognition through several interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms. Dynamic cerebral autoregulation is markedly impaired after acute ischemic stroke, leading to an inability to buffer fluctuations in systemic BP [23]. Increased short-term variability therefore causes intermittent cerebral hypoperfusion and reperfusion, exacerbating secondary brain injury through oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier [8]. Experimental and clinical data indicate that heightened BPV provokes inflammatory responses, microvascular damage, and enlargement of ischemic lesions—effects that are magnified when autoregulation is already compromised [8]. As described above, in AF-related cardioembolic stroke, this vulnerability is further amplified. In this setting, recurrent micro-embolic burden can exacerbate diffuse microvascular injury [24,25], in which BPV is more likely to translate into network-level cerebral damage and PSCI.

The absence of statistically significant domain-specific associations warrants consideration. Given the relatively modest sample size (n = 143), the present study was primarily powered to detect associations with global PSCI rather than subtle inter-domain differences. Post-stroke cognitive impairment at 3 months often manifests as a global decline rather than isolated domain deficits [26]; moreover, domain-specific tests have greater measurement variability and ceiling effects than composite PSCI definitions. In AF-related stroke, cardioembolic infarcts frequently cause diffuse network disruption across fronto-subcortical circuits, making selective domain impairment less common [27]. Collectively, these factors likely contributed to the minimal domain-level signal, although the trend towards lower scores with increasing BPV suggests that larger cohorts may reveal domain-specific vulnerabilities.

From a clinical perspective, our findings underscore the potential importance of acute hemodynamic stability in cardioembolic stroke care. BPV is modifiable; close monitoring and management of short-term fluctuations during the first week after stroke may complement efforts to control absolute BP [8,20]. Strategies to minimize oscillations—through individualized titration of antihypertensives, careful sedation, and avoidance of rapid BP changes—could reduce secondary brain injury risk. Incorporating BPV metrics into early risk stratification might identify patients at higher risk of PSCI who could benefit from intensified cognitive follow-up and rehabilitation. Our results therefore support the concept that BPV is not just a prognostic marker but a potential therapeutic target during acute stroke management [8].

Several limitations merit acknowledgment. This was a single-center study with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit generalizability; however, the homogeneity of AF-related stroke enhances internal validity. BP measurements were obtained at intermittent clinical intervals rather than continuously, which may underestimate rapid fluctuations, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Furthermore, acute antihypertensive and anticoagulant therapies were dynamically tailored according to individual clinical conditions, and their complex, time-varying effects could not be accounted for in the analyses. Although we adjusted for multiple confounders, unmeasured factors—such as medication adherence, autonomic dysfunction, or microvascular pathology—could influence the observed associations. In addition, although previous stroke history was adjusted for in the multivariable analyses, residual confounding related to prior cerebrovascular injury cannot be completely excluded. Cognitive outcomes were assessed at 3 months, which primarily reflects early PSCI rather than permanent cognitive decline or long-term vascular dementia; longer follow-up would be informative to determine whether acute BPV predicts persistent or delayed cognitive decline [27]. And these findings pertain specifically to cardioembolic stroke and may not extend to other stroke subtypes. Finally, this was an observational study; therefore, the reported relationships represent associations and should not be interpreted as causal.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our study demonstrates that short-term variability in SBP and DBP during the acute phase of AF-related ischemic stroke is independently associated with PSCI. Acute BPV appears to be a clinically meaningful and potentially modifiable factor in early cognitive trajectories, while its influence on specific cognitive domains is less pronounced in this context. Future multicenter studies incorporating continuous BP monitoring, mechanistic imaging markers, and longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings and to evaluate whether targeted interventions to stabilize acute BPV can mitigate post-stroke cognitive decline in patients with cardioembolic stroke.
