Spontaneous natural conception and live birth in three patients with likely pathogenic variants in TUBB8, ZP2, and ZP3 associated with female subfertility
Authors: Juepu Zhou, Hui He, Ruolin Mao, Meng Wang, Rui Long, Xiangfei Wang, Hong Chen, Lei Jin, Lixia Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaseThree patients with genetically confirmed female subfertility due to heterozygous likely pathogenic variants in TUBB8 (c.613G > A), ZP2 (c.1421T > C), or ZP3 (c.518 C > G), respectively, had histories of recurrent adverse assisted reproductive technology (ART) outcomes, including zona pellucida (ZP) abnormalities or oocyte degeneration, reduced fertilization, poor embryo development or limited viable embryo availability, and implantation failure.OutcomeNotably, all three patients eventually achieved successful live births. Patient 1, after two failed ART cycles, delivered a male infant following a third ICSI attempt, and later conceived naturally, delivering a female infant. Patient 2, after five unsuccessful IVF/ICSI cycles and no implantation, conceived naturally years later and delivered a male infant. Patient 3, following two failed IVF/ICSI attempts, conceived via donor oocytes and later delivered a male infant through natural conception.ConclusionThese cases expand the reported reproductive outcome spectrum of women carrying likely pathogenic variants in oocyte- and ZP-related genes. The findings suggest that reproductive outcomes in selected heterozygous carriers may be more variable than previously appreciated and that residual reproductive potential may occasionally be retained. These observations may help refine reproductive genetic counseling for women with genetically associated subfertility, while further case accumulation and functional studies are required.Keywords: Subfertility; Natural conception; Live birth; TUBB8 protein, human; ZP2 protein, human; ZP3 protein, human

Introduction

Reproductive disorders and infertility affect approximately 10–15% of couples worldwide, representing a significant global health concern [1]. In recent years, assisted reproductive technology (ART) has been widely utilized to help infertile couples achieve offspring through in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) [2]. However, the efficacy of ART at present is still limited, with success rates ranging from 30% to 40% [3]. Recently, with the rapid advancement of next-generation sequencing technologies, numerous critical genes associated with oocyte maturation, fertilization, and early embryonic development have been identified, establishing causal relationships with female infertility [4].

TUBB8 is the first pathogenic gene identified to be associated with oocyte maturation arrest [5]. Subsequent studies have gradually identified other pathogenic genes associated with oocyte maturation arrest, including PATL2, TRIP13, and others [6, 7]. Variants in the ZP1, ZP2, ZP3, and ZP4 genes have been identified in cases of zona pellucida (ZP) developmental defects [8, 9]. WEE2, TLE6, CDC20 and BTG4 are considered to be associated with fertilization failure and zygotic cleavage failure [10–13]. Furthermore, several genes have been identified as being associated with early embryonic developmental arrest, including PADI6, NLRP2, NLRP5, NLRP7, REC114, among others [14–18]. This series of genes closely associated with female infertility has been established as molecular markers for evaluating the developmental potential of oocytes and early embryos, playing a significant role in genetic counseling and fertility guidance.

Although current sequencing technologies enable the identification of pathogenic genes, there remains a lack of ideal therapeutic strategies for genetic defects occurring at the earliest stages of life development. Despite numerous ART cycles, there are few reported cases of successful pregnancies among women harboring pathogenic variants linked to oocyte and early embryonic development. It has been reported that a few patients with ZP1, ZP2, and ZP4 variants have achieved successful pregnancies through multiple ART cycles [9, 19–21]. Additionally, two patients carrying NLRP2 variants successfully gave birth after several rounds of embryo transfer [22]. Furthermore, patients with biallelic variants in the NLRP7 gene have approximately a 1% chance of achieving natural conception [23]. In severe cases with recurrent failure of oocyte maturation, fertilization, or early embryonic development, oocyte donation is often considered an important reproductive option. However, reproductive prognosis may vary depending on the gene, variant, zygosity, ovarian reserve, and ART phenotype. Given the scarcity of successful pregnancy reports in this population, documenting and analyzing such cases is essential for improving genetic counseling and developing optimized treatment strategies.

The three probands underwent whole-exome sequencing (WES) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with Agilent SureSelect exome enrichment. Sequencing data were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome, and variants were filtered with a minor allele frequency threshold of < 1%. All candidate variants were validated by Sanger sequencing in probands and available family members. Detailed methodologies have been described previously [24]. Through this approach, a likely pathogenic variant in TUBB8, ZP2, or ZP3 was identified in each of the three patients with primary subfertility. In this study, we report the successful pregnancies and live births achieved by these three patients, providing novel insights into the clinical outcomes of genetically defined female subfertility. This case report was not designed to provide new functional validation, but rather to document clinically informative reproductive outcomes in genetically characterized women with adverse ART histories and to discuss their implications for reproductive counseling.

Case report

First case

Patient 1 was a 23-year-old woman with a 4-year history of primary subfertility at the time of consultation. She had normal ovarian reserves and normal levels of sex hormones with basal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) 5.71 mIU/mL, luteinizing hormone (LH) 3.08 mIU/mL, and anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) 2.41 ng/mL, and had a normal karyotype (46, XX). The seminal parameters of her husband showed 285.1 million per milliliter of sperm concentration, 50.17% progressive motility, and 5.0% normal sperm morphology per ejaculate.

Patient 1 had previously experienced one unsuccessful IVF cycle at another hospital, where 13 oocytes were retrieved but no viable embryos were obtained. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) analysis of the proband with validation by Sanger sequencing of the family identified a heterozygous TUBB8 variant (NM_177987.3: c.613G > A, p.Glu205Lys) based on the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome, which was inherited from her father (Fig. 1A, B). This variant was interpreted as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP guidelines, with supporting evidence summarized in Table 2. The Glu205 residue is located within the Tubulin/FtsZ family GTPase domain and is highly conserved across primate species (Fig. 1C, D). In the second IVF cycle (the first cycle at our institution), a total of 19 oocytes were retrieved, 8 of the 16 MII-stage oocytes were successfully fertilized. Subsequently, two day-3 fresh embryos were transferred but failed to implant. The remaining embryos did not develop into viable blastocysts during prolonged culture. In the third ICSI cycle, 8 oocytes were retrieved, 3 of the 7 MII oocytes were successfully fertilized. Subsequently, two day-3 fresh embryos were transferred, and one of them successfully implanted. She finally achieved a successful pregnancy and delivered a 4 kg male infant via cesarean section, with no congenital abnormalities reported (Fig. 1A, III-1). Two years later, Patient 1 conceived naturally and delivered a 3 kg female infant via cesarean section, with no birth defects reported (Fig. 1A, III-2). However, she experienced gestational diabetes and postpartum hemorrhage during this pregnancy (Table 1). Notably, genetic analysis of the proband’s family revealed that her sister (II-2) also carries the same heterozygous variant and presents with primary subfertility, further supporting co-segregation of this variant with the disease phenotype (Fig. 1A, B).

Second case

Patient 2 was a 31-year-old woman with a 5-year history of primary subfertility at the time of consultation. She had normal levels of sex hormones with basal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) 7.74 mIU/mL, luteinizing hormone (LH) 2.41 mIU/mL, and a normal karyotype (46, XX). She had abnormal ovarian reserves with anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) 0.44 ng/mL. The seminal parameters of her husband showed 30.9 million per milliliter of sperm concentration, 74.0% progressive motility, and 6.0% normal sperm morphology per ejaculate.

Patient 2 underwent a total of five IVF/ICSI cycles, resulting in six transferable embryos, none of which achieved successful implantation (Table 1). During these ART cycles, the retrieved oocytes consistently showed a thin ZP, as previously described [24]. WES analysis of the proband with validation by Sanger sequencing of the family revealed a heterozygous ZP2 variant (NM_003460.3: c.1421T > C, p.Leu474Pro) based on the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome. The proband’s variant was inherited from her father, following an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern (Fig. 2A, B). This variant was interpreted as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP guidelines, with supporting evidence summarized in Table 2. The Leu474 residue is located within the ZP-N/ZP-C linker region and is evolutionarily conserved across species (Fig. 2C, D). This variant was initially identified by our research group, and its pathogenicity was experimentally validated through structural modeling, secretion assays, and co-immunoprecipitation in our previous study [24]. In that study, structural modeling suggested that p.Leu474Pro resides within the α-helix of the ZP-N/ZP-C linker, potentially impairing the interactions between ZP2 and other ZP proteins. Functional assays further demonstrated that p.Leu474Pro exhibited significantly reduced secretion in CHO cells compared to wild-type ZP2. Moreover, the p.Leu474Pro variant showed significantly reduced interaction with ZP3 compared to wild-type ZP2, potentially disrupting ZP2-ZP3 heterodimer formation and subsequent ZP matrix assembly [24].

However, during a follow-up several years later, it was reported that Patient 2 conceived naturally and delivered a 4.15 kg male infant via cesarean section at 39 weeks of gestation, with no birth defects observed (Fig. 2A, III-1). The patient experienced no complications during pregnancy or delivery. In addition, the proband’s sister (II-2) also carried the same heterozygous ZP2 variant and had a history of primary subfertility, supporting familial segregation of the variant with the subfertility phenotype (Fig. 2A, B).

Third case

Patient 3 was a 30-year-old woman with a 2-year history of primary subfertility at the time of consultation. She had normal levels of sex hormones with basal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) 4.76 mIU/mL, luteinizing hormone (LH) 3.15 mIU/mL, and a normal karyotype (46, XX). She had normal ovarian reserves with anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) 2.98 ng/mL. The seminal parameters of her husband showed 110 million per milliliter of sperm concentration, 56.78% progressive motility, and 12.0% normal sperm morphology per ejaculate.

Patient 3 underwent two unsuccessful IVF/ICSI cycles (Table 1) [25]. During the first IVF cycle, six cumulus-oocyte complexes were retrieved, but all oocytes lacked surrounding ZP and were verified as degenerated. During the second ICSI cycle, only two oocytes without ZP were retrieved, one of which degenerated. The remaining ZP-free oocyte formed a 3-pronuclei zygote after ICSI and failed to undergo cleavage. WES analysis of the proband with validation by Sanger sequencing of the family identified a heterozygous ZP3 variant (NM_001110354.2: c.518 C > G, p.Ser173Cys) based on the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome (Fig. 3A, B). The proband’s variant was inherited from her father, following an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. This variant was interpreted as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP guidelines, with supporting evidence summarized in Table 2. The Ser173 residue is located within the ZP-C domain and is evolutionarily conserved across species (Fig. 3C, D). This variant was first identified by our group, and its pathogenicity was experimentally validated through bioinformatic prediction, disulfide bond analysis, and co-immunoprecipitation in our previous study [25]. In that study, the Ser173 residue of ZP3 was shown to be evolutionarily conserved and predicted to be damaging by multiple bioinformatic tools. DISULFIND analysis suggested that the Ser173Cys substitution may disrupt disulfide bond formation, potentially compromising ZP3’s interactions with other ZP proteins. Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed significantly reduced interaction between ZP2 and mutant ZP3 compared to wild-type controls [25].

Discussion

Here, we report successful delivery in three patients with likely pathogenic gene variants associated with female subfertility. The three patients carried variants in TUBB8 (c.613G > A, p.Glu205Lys), ZP2 (c.1421T > C, p.Leu474Pro), and ZP3 (c.518 C > G, p.Ser173Cys), respectively. Recent large-scale sequencing studies have expanded the genetic and phenotypic framework for oocyte and early embryo competence defects. Chen et al. outlined the genetic landscape of human oocyte/embryo defects, identifying TUBB8 as the most frequently implicated known gene and highlighting the contribution of ZP genes to sperm-oocyte interaction and ZP formation [26]. Zhang et al. further showed that oocyte and early embryo competence defects are phenotypically heterogeneous, with genetic profiles and diagnostic yields differing across ART phenotype categories [27]. Together, these studies emphasize that genetically associated oocyte and embryo defects are heterogeneous in both genetic cause and clinical presentation. In this context, the present cases add clinical outcome information by suggesting that selected heterozygous carriers of likely pathogenic oocyte- or ZP-related variants may occasionally achieve live birth, including through natural conception, despite previous adverse ART outcomes.

Since TUBB8 is the predominant β-tubulin isotype in human oocyte spindles, variants in this gene typically exert severe effects on oocyte meiosis and development [5]. Current studies have revealed a variety of TUBB8 variants and inheritance patterns, with differences in pathogenicity among these variants leading to diverse clinical phenotypes, such as oocyte maturation arrest, fertilization failure, cleavage failure, and early embryonic developmental arrest, among others [28–30]. Although over 200 cases carrying TUBB8 variants have been reported, there are few reports of successful pregnancies in patients carrying pathogenic variants in TUBB8 to date. Several successful pregnancies have been reported in genetic screening for TUBB8 variants, but the associated variants were ultimately indicated to be non-pathogenic [31, 32]. For patients with likely pathogenic TUBB8 variants and recurrent severe oocyte or embryo developmental defects, oocyte donation is often discussed as an important reproductive option [31]. However, the present case suggests that reproductive outcomes may be variable among selected heterozygous carriers, and counseling should integrate the specific variant evidence, ART phenotype, ovarian reserve, and reproductive history. The identical TUBB8 variant (c.613G > A, p.Glu205Lys) has been reported in two previous studies [33, 34]. Chen et al. reported a patient presenting with a high proportion of immature oocytes, limited viable embryo availability, and failed pregnancy after embryo transfer, while Yang et al. documented multi-pronuclear formation and no high-quality embryos available for transfer. The Glu205 residue is located within the Tubulin/FtsZ family GTPase domain, a region involved in nucleotide binding and protein stability. The variant is extremely rare in population databases. In the present patient, adverse ART outcomes were also observed, including no viable embryos in the first IVF cycle, failed implantation after embryo transfer in the second cycle, and reduced fertilization in the subsequent ICSI cycle. Notably, the same variant led to different phenotypes across patients, suggesting that genetic background may modulate the clinical manifestation of TUBB8 variants [33, 34]. Remarkably, the patient in this study not only achieved successful pregnancy through ART but also experienced a natural conception, with both pregnancies resulting in successful deliveries. To our knowledge, successful pregnancies in patients carrying this variant have not been previously reported. Together, these observations suggest phenotypic variability among patients carrying TUBB8 p.Glu205Lys, ranging from immature oocytes or abnormal fertilization to partial ART success and subsequent natural conception. Although these observations support the likely pathogenic classification of TUBB8 p.Glu205Lys, no direct functional assay has yet been reported for this variant. Therefore, additional functional studies are needed to further clarify its pathogenic effect and penetrance.

The ZP plays a critical role in oocyte maturation, early embryonic development, and the fertilization process [35, 36]. ZP glycoproteins contain multiple functional domains and are synthesized, processed, secreted, and assembled into long cross-linked filaments by growing oocytes [37, 38]. Among these, ZP2 is recognized as a secondary sperm-binding receptor, playing a significant role in sperm-egg binding and the prevention of polyspermy [39]. Variants in ZP2 have been found to cause various clinical phenotypes, including ZP deficiency, thin ZP, empty follicle syndrome, oocyte degeneration, and large perivitelline space [19, 20, 40]. Although ZP2 has been predominantly reported as an autosomal recessive gene for female infertility, emerging evidence suggests that heterozygous ZP2 variants can also cause subfertility through a haploinsufficiency mechanism [41]. Liu et al. reported dosage effects of heterozygous ZP2 and ZP3 variants causing human infertility [42]. In our previous study, both sisters in Family 2 carrying the same heterozygous p.Leu474Pro variant exhibited thin ZP and primary subfertility, demonstrating co-segregation of the variant with the disease phenotype. The pathogenicity of the ZP2 variant (c.1421T > C, p.Leu474Pro) has been experimentally validated in our previous study [24]. As described in the Case report, the patient’s retrieved oocytes consistently exhibited a thin ZP. Functional assays in our previous study demonstrated that the mutant protein exhibited significantly reduced secretion and impaired interaction with ZP3, indicating disrupted ZP2-ZP3 heterodimer formation and subsequent ZP matrix assembly [24]. The milder phenotype observed in heterozygous carriers—thin ZP rather than complete ZP absence—is consistent with a haploinsufficiency model, where reduced but not abolished ZP2 function leads to subfertility rather than complete infertility. The clinical outcomes of ZP2 variants depend on the extent of oocyte damage. Currently reported cases of successful pregnancies have all been associated with oocytes exhibiting thin ZP, indicating a milder degree of mutational impact [19, 20]. Therefore, for patients identified with ZP2 variants, ICSI is currently recommended as the preferred assisted reproductive approach [8]. In the early ART cycles of our patient, IVF was initially attempted before the genetic diagnosis was established; following genetic identification, ICSI was adopted as the preferred approach, and day-3 embryo transfer was performed due to the limited number of viable embryos, which precluded extended culture to the blastocyst stage. Successful pregnancies through ICSI have been reported in selected patients with ZP2 variants, particularly when oocytes with a thin ZP remain available. It is particularly noteworthy that the proband in this study, despite experiencing five failed ART cycles, subsequently achieved natural conception and a live birth. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case with natural conception among patients carrying an identified likely pathogenic ZP2 variant. This finding opens new perspectives for research on ZP2 variants and provides novel insights and potential clinical guidance for fertility management in affected patients.

Compared to ZP2, ZP3 is considered indispensable for the formation of the ZP and is also essential for ovulation and embryonic cleavage [43, 44]. Therefore, ZP3 variants often lead to more severe consequences [45]. Sun et al. previously reported a successful pregnancy in a patient with a ZP3 variant [8]. However, the pathogenicity of this variant site was not experimentally validated, and predictive software analysis suggested that the variant is likely benign. Therefore, prior to this report, there have been no documented cases of successful pregnancies in patients definitively carrying pathogenic variants in ZP3 [45, 46]. The pathogenicity of the ZP3 variant (c.518 C > G, p.Ser173Cys) has been experimentally validated in our previous study [25]. The Ser173 residue is located within the ZP-C domain, and multiple prediction tools consistently indicated a damaging effect. Functional analyses in our previous study demonstrated that the p.Ser173Cys variant specifically reduced the interaction between ZP3 and ZP2 while the interaction with ZP1 remained unchanged, suggesting impaired ZP filament network assembly [25]. This is consistent with the empty follicle syndrome and ZP-free oocyte degeneration phenotype observed in this patient. Although the patient experienced failure in the ART cycle due to oocyte degeneration, follow-up revealed that she achieved natural conception and subsequently delivered a live birth, marking the first reported case of such an outcome to the best of our knowledge. This notable finding expands our understanding of the potential for conception in patients with ZP3 variants and provides a novel reference for fertility guidance at the genetic level.

These observations should be interpreted as clinical outcome data from a case report, rather than as direct functional or mechanistic evidence. They should be considered in light of both ART-associated and spontaneous reproductive success, including the ICSI-associated live birth in Patient 1 and the subsequent natural-conception live births in all three patients. The potential contributing factors discussed below should therefore be regarded as hypotheses. First, incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, which are well-recognized phenomena in medical genetics [47], may contribute to heterogeneous reproductive outcomes among heterozygous carriers. Second, haploinsufficiency rather than complete loss of function may contribute to the phenotype in these patients. Animal models have demonstrated that haploinsufficiency of genes critical for oocyte development can lead to subfertility rather than complete infertility [48, 49]. This is particularly relevant for the ZP2 and ZP3 variants, where functional studies demonstrated reduced but not abolished protein interactions [24, 25]. Third, differences between the in vivo reproductive tract and conventional in vitro culture conditions may contribute to discrepancies between ART outcomes and natural conception, as the oviductal environment supports gamete function, fertilization, and early embryo development [50]. However, this explanation remains hypothetical, and no direct evidence currently demonstrates that the fallopian tube environment can rescue or compensate for the specific molecular defects caused by TUBB8, ZP2, or ZP3 variants. Finally, genetic background and modifier genes may influence phenotypic severity [47], although this requires further investigation. Collectively, these possibilities support the concept that selected heterozygous carriers may present with subfertility rather than absolute infertility; however, the mechanisms underlying residual reproductive potential remain to be clarified.

From a clinical perspective, these cases suggest that a heterozygous likely pathogenic variant in an oocyte- or ZP-related gene should not automatically be interpreted as indicating absolute infertility. Reproductive counseling should instead be individualized by integrating the specific gene and variant evidence, ovarian reserve, maternal age, duration of subfertility, previous ART responses, oocyte or embryo phenotype, family history, and the couple’s reproductive preferences. For women with repeated adverse ART outcomes, continued IVF/ICSI attempts, modification of embryo-transfer strategies, expectant management with attempts at natural conception, and oocyte donation may all be discussed in a balanced manner. Importantly, the present observations do not allow estimation of the probability or optimal duration of natural conception attempts, and they should not delay time-sensitive treatment in women with advanced age or diminished ovarian reserve. Nevertheless, in selected heterozygous carriers, especially when some oocytes or embryos remain developmentally competent, counseling may include the possibility of residual reproductive potential and variable reproductive outcomes rather than a uniformly poor prognosis.

Due to the limited sample size, we have currently identified only these three exceptional cases, and the findings cannot be used to define diagnostic cut-off values or estimate the probability of natural conception or live birth in carriers of these variants. In addition, none of the patients showed complete oocyte maturation arrest, complete fertilization failure, or complete embryo-development arrest across all ART cycles; thus, their ART phenotypes should be interpreted as recurrent adverse but partial and variable outcomes. This is particularly relevant for Patient 2, whose low number of retrieved oocytes constrains interpretation of fertilization and embryo-development outcomes. Another limitation is that prenatal genetic testing for the familial variants was not performed, and the variant status of the live-born offspring could not be determined because offspring samples were unavailable. In particular, no direct functional assay has yet been reported for TUBB8 p.Glu205Lys. Further studies, such as in vitro microtubule assembly assays, spindle morphology analysis, and oocyte- or embryo-relevant functional models, are needed to better define the pathogenicity, penetrance, and mechanisms underlying phenotypic variability among carriers of this variant. In subsequent studies, continued collection and follow-up of related cases will be essential to provide more comprehensive fertility guidance for affected patients.

In summary, we report three cases of successful live births in patients carrying likely pathogenic gene variants associated with female subfertility, including an ICSI-associated live birth in Patient 1 and subsequent natural-conception live births in all three patients. These observations expand the reported reproductive outcome spectrum associated with oocyte- and ZP-related variants and highlight the variability of reproductive outcomes among selected heterozygous carriers. The findings suggest that residual reproductive potential may occasionally be retained, but the underlying mechanisms remain uncertain. With continued case accumulation and functional studies, more precise fertility guidance strategies can be developed for patients carrying variants in oocyte- or ZP-related genes.
