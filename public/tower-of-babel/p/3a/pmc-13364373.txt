Accuracy assessment of a hybrid deep learning and image processing approach for lung CT segmentation in external radiotherapy
Authors: Hamza Sekkat, Abdellah Khallouqi, Yasmina Berrada, Mohammed Talbi, Ahmed Bannan, Oussama El Mouden, Abdellah Halimi, Youssef Madkouri, Omar El Rhazouani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate lung segmentation in computed tomography (CT) is a fundamental step for numerous clinical applications, including radiotherapy planning and thoracic disease diagnosis. Manual segmentation, however, remains time-consuming and operator-dependent. This study aims to develop a fully automated segmentation framework combining a well-structured pipeline of image processing techniques with a deep learning model based on a U-Net architecture and a pre-trained VGG16 encoder.Materials and methodsNinety anonymized thoracic CT scans, including lung and breast cancer patients, were collected, pre-processed and used to generate binary lung masks using a custom Python pipeline. The dataset was split patient-wise into training (70%), validation (15%) and testing (15%) subsets. The model was trained using binary crossentropy loss, the Adam optimizer and data augmentation strategies to improve generalization.ResultsHigh correlations were observed between automatically computed lung volumes and mean Hounsfield units (HU) compared to the treatment planning system (TPS) references (R2 = 0.84 for volume, R2 = 0.72 for HU, both with p < 1 × 10−33) and the deep learning model achieved a mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of ~95% and intersection over union (IoU) of ~95% on an independent test set. Mean loss and pixel-wise accuracy were 0.004 and ~95%, respectively.ConclusionsThe proposed VGG16-based U-Net model demonstrates high accuracy and robustness in segmenting lungs from thoracic CT scans, achieving ~95% DSC and ~99% pixel-wise accuracy. It effectively replaces manual segmentation with a fully automated, scalable solution. This framework streamlines lung segmentation in clinical radiotherapy workflows, reducing operator variability and time consumption.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains among the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide. In 2022, an estimated 2.48 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths were reported globally. In Morocco, 8,825 new cases and 7,970 deaths were recorded that year, affecting both men and women (IARC). Without changes in incidence or mortality, global lung cancer cases could reach 4.62 million and deaths 3.55 million by 2050 [1–3].

External beam radiotherapy is a cornerstone in thoracic cancer management, allowing precise dose delivery to tumors while sparing surrounding organs at risk (OARs). Accurate delineation of targets and critical structures is therefore essential, and computed tomography (CT) imaging remains the foundation for diagnosis, staging and treatment planning [4–11]. However, manual contouring is laborious, subjective and prone to inter- and intra-observer variability due to heterogeneous lung anatomy, image artifacts and the proximity of vital OARs. A study of 1,085 lung tumors showed a mean volume overlap variability of 20.8% between observers [12–14].

To overcome these limitations, automatic segmentation methods, particularly those based on artificial intelligence (AI), have gained traction. U-Net-like convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are widely used for medical image segmentation owing to their accuracy, efficiency and ability to handle large datasets. They substantially reduce contouring time and variability, though expert review remains necessary [15–17].

This study proposes an automated lung segmentation model using a visual geometry group 16-layer network (VGG16)-based CNN integrated with a developed and structured pipeline of preprocessing techniques such as windowing, thresholding and morphological operations. The goal is to develop a Python-based framework capable of robust and precise lung delineation from CT images, suitable for radiotherapy planning and adaptable to clinical workflows.

Materials and methods

Dataset preparation and development environment

The automated segmentation model was developed using a combination of clinical CT data, image processing techniques and deep learning tools. A total of 90 thoracic CT scans were retrospectively collected from our radiotherapy department in the international clinic of Settat: 27 lung cancer and 63 breast cancer patients (mean age ~60 years; 22.5% male, 77.5% female). All images were stored in DICOM format, preserving metadata such as Hounsfield units (HU), slice thickness and pixel spacing. To ensure confidentiality, personal identifiers were removed using an anonymization script.

Only thoracic slices were retained, yielding 80–120 slices per patient. Scans were acquired with a GE Discovery RT scanner (16-detector system, 80-cm gantry aperture), with a 512 × 512 in-plane resolution and 1.25–2.5 mm slice thickness. For initial contouring reference, the Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS), version 15.6 (Varian Medical Systems), was used, though its semi-automatic segmentation tools required manual adjustment. This study replaced these steps with a Python-based automated pipeline. Development was conducted in Python 3.13.1/3.10.16 within an Anaconda environment. Supplementary File — Table S1 summarizes the main libraries used.

Implementation workflow and mask generation

The objective was to automate lung contour delineation from CT scans using deep learning. The workflow includes preprocessing, mask generation, dataset structuring, model training and evaluation. Initially, ground-truth masks were generated via a custom Python script applying classical image processing operations. For each patient, average HU and total lung volume were also calculated. Each CT slice was paired with its mask for supervised training. The workflow steps are outlined in Supplementary File — Table S2. A dedicated Python script created binary masks by applying sequential operations to each slice as shown in Supplementary File — Table S3. Masks were saved in PNG format and paired with original slices. The script also computed average HU and lung volume for each patient, compared against Eclipse-derived values for validation.

Dataset preparation and model architecture

Generated image–mask pairs were organized into training (70%), validation (15%) and test (15%) subsets. Splitting was patient-wise to prevent data leakage across sets. All files followed consistent naming conventions (image_0000.png), ensuring seamless integration into the pipeline.

The segmentation model combines U-Net architecture with a VGG16 encoder pre-trained on ImageNet, chosen for robust feature extraction and medical image performance. Fully connected layers were removed, keeping convolutional layers for feature encoding. The decoder reconstructs spatial resolution via upsampling and skip connections, integrating semantic and spatial details. Input CT slices (DICOM) were resized to 224 × 224 px to match VGG16’s input. Results

In the initial phase, prior to training the segmentation model, image processing pipeline was implemented to prepare thoracic CT data extracted from DICOM files, aiming to automatically generate accurate lung masks for supervised learning. The original axial CT images as shown in Supplementary File — Figure S1A were first converted from raw pixel values to HU using the DICOM metadata parameters (RescaleIntercept and RescaleSlope), enabling reliable differentiation of tissue densities (Supplementary File — Fig. S1B). A Gaussian filter was then applied to reduce noise while preserving key anatomical contours (Supplementary File — Fig. S1C). To enhance soft tissue contrast around the lungs, a mediastinal windowing was performed (Supplementary File — Fig. S1D). Subsequently, Otsu’s automatic thresholding method was used to binarize the image (Supplementary File — Fig. S1E), effectively separating low-density pulmonary regions from denser surrounding tissues. Morphological operations, including erosion, dilation and hole filling, were applied to refine the binary mask by removing small artifacts and closing gaps within lung regions (Supplementary File — Fig. S1F). Contours of all segmented regions were extracted and overlaid in magenta to visually assess boundary accuracy (Supplementary File — Fig. S1G) and finally, lung-specific contours were isolated to define the precise lung masks serving as ground truth for the deep learning model (Supplementary File — Fig. S1H).

The analysis of absolute lung volume differences between automatic segmentation and TPS revealed a wide range of variation across cases as shown in Supplementary File — Figure S2. The full sample of patients was analyzed, with a mean absolute difference of 208.48 cm3 and a standard deviation of 154.38 cm3, representing inter-case variability. The minimum difference was 16.72 cm3, while the maximum reached 860.31 cm3. The histogram showed a right-skewed distribution, with a higher frequency of lower lung volume differences, suggesting that in most cases the automated segmentation closely approximates the TPS-defined lung volumes. However, the presence of several high-difference outliers, as confirmed by the boxplot, reflects occasional substantial deviations. These findings support the potential utility of automatic segmentation while also highlighting the importance of quality control to identify and manage outlier cases. A high correlation was also observed between the lung volumes estimated by the automatic segmentation algorithm and those obtained from the TPS reference (Fig. 1). The scatter plot indicates high agreement between the two methods. R2 was found to be 0.84, demonstrating that 84% of the variability in TPS-derived volumes can be explained by the volumes calculated automatically. The correlation was statistically significant, with a p-value of 7.51 × 10−34.

To evaluate the agreement between automatic segmentation and the TPS, the absolute differences in mean HU across all patients’ lung volumes was also analyzed (Supplementary File — Fig. S3). The distribution of HU differences revealed a mean of 39.39 HU with a standard deviation of 50.97 HU. The minimum observed difference was 2.77 HU, while the maximum reached 388.27 HU, suggesting the presence of occasional outliers. The histogram shows the majority of differences clustered below 60 HU. The accompanying boxplot highlights the presence of extreme values, reinforcing the variability seen in some cases. Despite these outliers, the central tendency reflects a generally acceptable deviation between automated and reference segmentations. To assess the consistency of lung density quantification, a correlation analysis was performed between the mean HU obtained using this study’s automated method and those derived from the TPS as presented in Figure 2. The results revealed an important positive correlation with a coefficient of determination R2 = 0.72 and a highly significant p-value (p = 7.51 × 10−34), indicating a robust linear relationship.

The model was trained on preprocessed CT images using a U-Net architecture with a pre-trained VGG16 encoder. The training and validation curves demonstrate effective learning behavior. Specifically, the loss curve shows a rapid decline during the initial epochs, with the validation loss stabilizing around 0.005 from the fourth epoch onward, indicating good model convergence and minimal overfitting. Notably, the training and validation loss curves remain closely aligned throughout, suggesting strong and promising generalization capabilities. Concurrently, the accuracy curve reaches above 99.6% within the first few epochs, with only marginal improvements thereafter. The fact that validation accuracy consistently exceeds training accuracy further supports the absence of overfitting and highlights the model’s ability to generalize well to unseen data (Supplementary File — Fig. S4). After saving and validating the trained model, a comprehensive testing phase was conducted on an independent dataset comprising 1,157 unseen CT slices to assess final performance. Quantitative evaluation yielded excellent segmentation outcomes, with a mean loss of 0.0035 and an accuracy of 99.69%, reflecting high main consistency between the predicted and ground truth lung masks. Furthermore, the DSC reached an average of 0.9560, indicating a high degree of spatial overlap, while the IoU averaged 0.9237, further confirming the model’s ability to delineate lung boundaries with precision (Tab. 2). To complement these numerical findings, representative examples of segmentation outputs are provided, offering visual confirmation of the model’s high fidelity in reproducing lung contours (Fig. 3; Supplementary File — Fig. S5).

Discussion

The automated preprocessing pipeline accurately reproduced expected lung morphology, as demonstrated by the strong correlations in both lung volume (R2 = 0.84) and mean HU (R2 = 0.72) when compared with TPS references. These findings indicate that the generated masks capture both anatomical boundaries and tissue density characteristics relevant to radiotherapy planning. Discrepancies seen in a minority of cases were primarily associated with tumor-induced density alterations or borderline anatomical regions where rule-based preprocessing tends to under- or over-segment. Similar challenges in pathological parenchymal regions have been described in earlier deep learning studies, including Anthimopoulos et al. (2016) [18], underscoring the difficulty of segmenting diseased lung tissues with heterogeneous density patterns [15–17].

From a deep learning perspective, the VGG16-based U-Net achieved excellent quantitative performance (DSC = 0.956; IoU = 0.923), placing our results in the upper range of those reported in the literature. As highlighted in Figure 4, our performance is highly comparable to several established architectures. This trend is consistent with findings from recent systematic reviews, such as Tan et al. [19], who compared U-Net, Attention-based models, GAN-based methods and multi-scale fusion networks for lung CT segmentation and concluded that most modern architectures achieve similarly high overlap metrics. For example, standard U-Net implementations typically achieve DSC values around 0.94–0.96 in large thoracic datasets, as demonstrated by Hofmanninger et al. [21]. Likewise, ResU-Net and Attention-based variants frequently report DSC values in the 0.92–0.95 range depending on dataset diversity and imaging protocol differences [20, 22, 23].

GAN-based segmentation systems have occasionally surpassed classical U-Net performance, achieving DSC values close to ~0.97. Such results were observed in studies like Cai et al. [24], who validated deep learning-based lung and lobe segmentation on large databases. This observation aligns with Tan et al. [25], who demonstrated that GAN-based lung segmentation architectures can outperform traditional U-Net variants by offering improved boundary precision and higher computational efficiency. However, these marginal performance gains often come with increased architectural complexity, greater computational cost and reduced predictability in clinical datasets. Consequently, the similar DSC values observed among modern architectures reflect the maturity of lung segmentation as a task and the presence of an upper performance ceiling due to organ size, high contrast and consistent HU distribution.

Despite these similarities, our work presents several differentiating strengths. First, unlike many studies that rely solely on dataset-level metrics, we validated our segmentations against TPS-derived lung volume and HU measurements, thereby improving the clinical relevance of our evaluation. This is particularly important in radiotherapy workflows, where segmented lung masks directly influence dose calculation and treatment planning. Second, our integration of a structured preprocessing pipeline with deep learning allowed the model to correct systematic rule-based errors, especially in apical and basal slices. This demonstrates that combining classical image-processing with deep learning can improve robustness in anatomically challenging regions.

Consistent with recent advancements in clinical workflow automation, Ryczkowski et al. [26] developed a cross-platform scripting framework linking RayStation AI segmentation with Eclipse TPS, demonstrating how automated pipelines can streamline contouring and enable scalable integration of deep learning tools into routine planning environments. Their results emphasize the growing importance of interoperable AI solutions in radiotherapy workflows, aligning with the motivations and potential clinical deployment pathways of the present work.

In addition, as highlighted by Hofmanninger et al. [21], the choice and diversity of the training dataset play a critical role in segmentation robustness, often outweighing architectural differences. Their publicly available lungmask implementation (https://github.com/JoHof/lungmask) provides an important open-science baseline for the community. While our present study focused on institution-specific radiotherapy CT data and TPS-based clinical validation, benchmarking our model against external datasets, such as the LCTCS 2017 challenge, represents a valuable direction for future work. We now explicitly acknowledge that evaluating performance on multi-institutional, heterogeneous datasets is necessary to further establish generalizability and enable unbiased comparison with alternative algorithms.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our dataset was derived from a single institution and included lung and breast cancer patients acquired with a specific scanner model and reconstruction protocol. These factors may limit generalizability to scans obtained with different imaging systems, kernels or patient populations. Additionally, our model was trained using 2D slices, which simplifies implementation but does not leverage full 3D contextual information. Future work may explore 3D U-Net, V-Net or hybrid 2.5D strategies, as well as external multicenter validation to improve generalizability and capture broader anatomical variability.

Overall, the proposed structured preprocessing and VGG16-U-Net pipeline offers a robust, accurate and clinically practical solution for thoracic CT lung segmentation. By balancing methodological simplicity, strong performance and workflow compatibility, this framework aligns with current trends in automated segmentation and supports future integration into TPS-assisted radiotherapy planning.

Conclusions

This study introduces a deep learning approach for automatic lung segmentation in thoracic CT images using a U-Net with a pre-trained VGG16 encoder. Achieving a Dice score of 95.6%, the model delivers competitive accuracy with reduced complexity. It effectively captures detailed lung structures and offers a robust, generalizable and clinically practical solution for thoracic imaging applications.
