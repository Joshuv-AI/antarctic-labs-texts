The global burden, trends, and inequalities of HIV/AIDS: a multicountry observational analysis
Authors: Huiqing Cheng, Zhuang Tong, RuiZheng Han, Guoer Yang, Ming Gao, Fangfang Cui, Ruixia Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInequality is a consistent issue throughout the process of HIV/AIDS prevention and control. There is currently a lack of quantitative research on cross-country inequalities in HIV/AIDS.MethodsThis study quantified the burden and inequalities of HIV/AIDS across 204 countries using GBD 2021 data. Age-standardized rates (ASRs) and uncertainty intervals (UIs) were used to quantify the burden of HIV/AIDS. Estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) of ASRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess trends. The Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Concentration Index (CI) were employed to quantify the degree of inequality in countries or regions with different Sociodemographic Index (SDI) levels.FindingsIn 2021, the ASRs of incidence(ASIR), prevalence(ASPR), mortality(ASMR) and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs)(ASDR) of HIV/AIDS per 100,000 people were 20.77 (95% UI: 18.77-23.02), 483.14 (95% UI: 459.02-511.43), 8.71 (95% UI: 8.10-9.56) and 496.39(95% UI: 456.10-554.21) globally. In 204 countries or regions, Lesotho with the highest prevalence in 2021 is 22,564.56 times the lowest in Albania. In terms of changes in ASIR, ASPR, ASMR and ASDR, there were 100 countries, 171 countries, 102 countries and 101 countries showed upward trends, respectively. According to the cross-country social inequalities analysis, the SII changed from -291.78 to -67.24 for ASIR, from -1117.87 to-2312.47 for ASPR, from -42.48 to -49.32 for ASMR and from -2354.77 to -2557.03 for ASDR. The concentration index values ranged from 0.3 to 0.6.ConclusionsCountries or regions with lower socio-demographic development disproportionately bear a heavier burden of HIV/AIDS, and these disparities are widening over time between the lowest and highest socioeconomic status groups. Additionally, the rising trend of HIV/AIDS burden in populations with high and middle SDI levels is alarming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11683-y.

Introduction

HIV/AIDS remains a major global public health issue, continuing to spread across all countries and posing a threat to the health and well-being of populations, particularly marginalized groups [1, 2]. Inequality has consistently impacted the effectiveness of HIV/AIDS prevention and control [3, 4]. Deng et al. conducted an analysis of the time-trend inequality related to Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) of HIV/AIDS. Sub-Saharan Africa had both the highest HIV burden and the greatest progress between 1990 and 2019 [5]. Additionally, new forms of inequity are continuously emerging. For instance, the prevalence of HIV drug resistance has notably increased in many low- and middle-income countries [6], which may lead to increase the costs for prevention and treatment.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has released the Global Health Sector Strategies on HIV, Viral Hepatitis, and Sexually Transmitted Infections for 2022–2030. The strategy emphasizes focusing efforts on regions that may have a higher burden of HIV/AIDS or where service gaps exist. Each country needs to identify essential HIV interventions based on disease burden data [7]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently a lack of information regarding the cross-country inequalities of HIV/AIDS. This study attempts to quantify cross-country inequalities of HIV/AIDS and may contribute to addressing the harmful effects of income inequality on pandemic outcomes.

Methods

Data source

The global burden of diseases, injuries, and risk factors study (GBD) 2021 is an international collaborative study. It utilizes a total of 328,938 data sources from around the world, including censuses, household surveys, civil registration and vital statistics, disease registries, disease notifications, healthcare utilization data, air pollution monitoring, satellite imaging, etc. GBD 2021 quantifies extensive data on 371 diseases and injuries in 204 countries and regions worldwide by using geospatial modeling tools and standardized algorithms and is freely available [8]. The overall objectives, data sources, modeling methods, and tools of the GBD 2021 have been detailed elsewhere [9, 10].

This study is a secondary analysis of the data on HIV/AIDS published in GBD 2021. Define HIV/AIDS according to the International Classification of Diseases Version 9 (ICD-9) and Version 10 (ICD-10) codes. In the GBD 2021 data download interface, parameters were set as follows: under “Cause,” we selected “HIV/AIDS”; under “Measure,” we chose “Incidence,” “Prevalence,” “Mortality,” and “Disability-Adjusted Life Years”; under “Age,” we selected “All Ages,” “Age-standardized,” and detailed breakdowns for each five-year age group. Under “Sex,” we selected “Both,” “Female,” and “Male,” and so on.

Disease burden and its trend of HIV/AIDS analysis

In GBD study, the underlying data used to model HIV incidence and mortality include vital registration data, household seroprevalence surveys, antenatal care clinic data, demographic estimates, intervention coverage data reported to UNAIDS—including antiretroviral therapy, prevention of mother-to-child transmission, HIV mortality before and after antiretroviral therapy—and disease progression rates from systematic reviews [11, 12]. The GBD Framework of HIV/AIDS uses two estimation models: the Estimation and Projection Package and Spectrum to produce internally consistent estimates. HIV incidence, prevalence, and mortality are estimated after grouping countries based on data availability and quality [12]. DALYs is the sum of Years of Life Lost (YLLs) and Years Lived with Disability (YLDs), measured in person-years. Uncertainty intervals (UIs) are provided for each point uncertainty using 1000 draws from the 25th and 75th percentiles of the posterior distribution [9].

Age-standardized rates (ASRs) were used to describe the burden of HIV/AIDS in different countries or regions. Estimated Annual Percentage Changes (EAPCs) of ASRs and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were employed to assess the trends. If the lower 95% CI of the EAPCs estimate is greater than 0, ASRs is considered to be increasing during the observation period. On the contrary, it is considered to be a downward trend. If the 95% CI includes 0, the trend change is not statistically significant.

Cross-country social inequalities

The Sociodemographic Index (SDI) is a composite indicator to represent the level of development closely related to health outcomes. It is the geometric average of various factors, including the total fertility rate under age 25 (TFU25), the mean years of education for individuals aged 15 and older (EDU15+), and the lag-distributed income per capita (LDI). The SDI ranges from 0 to 1, with 0 representing the theoretical lowest level of health-related development and 1 representing the highest level [8, 10]. We used the standard health equity analysis methods recommended by the WHO to analyze the multicountry inequality of HIV/AIDS. The Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Concentration Index (CI) were employed to quantify the degree of inequality in the distribution of HIV/AIDS burden in countries or regions with different SDI levels [15].

The SII is used to assess absolute inequality. It is calculated by regression of the rate of all ages’ population in each country to a scale defined by the midpoint of the cumulative range of the SDI ranking. SII has an intuitive interpretation and shares the same unit of measurement as health indicators, making it highly practical. CI is used to assess relative inequality. Its calculation involves assuming an imaginary line of equality (45° diagonal from bottom left to top right) and doubling the area between this line and the concentrated curve. The concentration curve is fitted using a Lorenz concentration curve, which utilizes the cumulative range of rates and the cumulative population range ranked by SDI. If the burden of HIV/AIDS is concentrated at higher SDI levels, the concentration curve is below the equality line. Conversely, it lies above the line of equality if the burden is concentrated at lower SDI levels. In the absence of inequality, the concentration curve coincides with the line of equality [16, 17]. SII is more sensitive to extreme values (i.e., the lowest and highest SDI levels), CI considers the cumulative distribution across all SDI levels, making it potentially more sensitive to differences in the middle groups and less influenced by the extremes.

Data cleaning, analysis, and visualization were conducted using Python and R software.

Results

The global burden of HIV/AIDS in 1990 and 2021 and the Temporal trends from 1990 to 2021

There were 40,036,935.84 (95% UI: 38,036,248.59-42,372,668.87) prevalent cases, an absolute increase of 32.10 million cases compared to 1990. The age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) per 100,000 people increased from 149.23 (95% UI: 139.61-159.64) in 1990 to 483.14 (95% UI: 459.02-511.43) in 2021, showing a growth of 334%, the EAPC of ASPR was 2.68 (95% CI: 2.05–3.31). (Table 1)

In 2021, the global number of deaths attributable to HIV/AIDS was 718,079.22 (95% UI: 669,270.74–785,447.05), and the DALYs was 40,266,791.75 (95% UI: 37,138,091.60–44,796,386.90). The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) per 100,000 people from 5.90 (95% UI: 4.49–7.83) in 1990 to 8.71 (95% UI: 8.10–9.56) in 2021, showing a growth of 2.81%, the EAPC of ASMR was − 0.49 (95% CI:−2.04, 1.07). The age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) per 100,000 people from 346.90 (95% UI: 273.11-451.84) to 496.39(95% UI: 456.10-554.21), showing a growth of 149.49%, the EAPC of ASPR was − 0.54(95% CI: −2.04, 0.98). (Table 1)

In terms of the burden by sex, in 2021, females had higher ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR than males, while males had a slightly higher ASIR than females. The ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR for females were 20.44 (95% UI: 18.13–23.28), 537.47 (95% UI: 514.55-564.32), 8.92 (95% UI: 8.12-10.00), and 528.11 (95% UI: 476.70-592.28), respectively. For males, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR were 21.16 (95% UI: 18.97–23.49), 430.84 (95% UI: 398.19-466.82), 8.54 (95% UI: 7.97–9.37), and 466.45 (95% UI: 434.78-517.04), respectively. Discussion

This study offers an important and timely contribution to the global understanding of HIV/AIDS by quantifying the burden(including incidence, prevalence, mortality rate, and DALYs rate) and inequalities across 204 countries using GBD 2021 data. We have made some important discoveries. Firstly, there are significant disparities in the burden of HIV/AIDS between different countries or regions. For example, the country or region with the highest prevalence in 2021 is 22,564.56 times the lowest, which is greater than the elevation difference between Mount Everest and the Mariana Trench (19,771.86). Secondly, regardless of incidence, prevalence, mortality, or DALYs, the values of ASRs for both SII and CI quantitatively indicate that the burden of HIV/AIDS is more concentrated among populations with lower SDI levels, with a significant degree of concentration. Thirdly, over the last three decades, the global burden of HIV/AIDS, apart from prevalence rates, has generally shown a declining trend. However, in approximately 50% of the 204 countries or regions, this burden has been continuously increasing, with a particularly notable rise among populations in high-middle and middle SDI categories.

There is currently no cure for HIV/AIDS. The management of HIV infection through the implementation of effective HIV prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and care interventions, including measures targeting opportunistic infections, is the primary approach to reducing the risk of infection, as well as improving the quality of life and survival time of those infected [18, 19]. Therefore, understanding the epidemiological trends of HIV/AIDS and identifying high-risk regions and populations are crucial for promoting equitable access to health resources. This study on trends has demonstrated that, over the past thirty years, the overall global incidence, death and DALYs burden of HIV/AIDS has declined. This reflects the positive outcomes of global collective efforts in managing and controlling the pandemic. However, it is important to note that in regions with high-middle and middle SDI, mainly concentrated in Eastern European, Southeast Asia and American countries, this burden has instead increased markedly, especially the prevalence rate. Survival prolongation due to antiretroviral therapy (ART) versus gaps in early diagnosis [20]. Moreover, This may be related to the decline in HIV/AIDS health funding, the spread of HIV among men who have sex with men (MSM), and the transmission of drug-resistant HIV strains in these regions [21, 22]. In the above-mentioned countries or regions, the prevention and treatment of HIV/AIDS should attach importance to health education for high-risk groups, reduce high-risk sexual behaviors, and at the same time attach importance to drug resistance monitoring and treatment optimization.

Consistent with the findings of previous studies [5, 11, 23]the global burden of HIV/AIDS is concentrated in countries or regions with lower SDI levels. But the magnitude of this disparity has not been previously reported. Moreover, despite the global decline in incidence, the absolute inequalities in prevalence, death, and DALYs, as measured by the SII, have increased in 2021 compared to 1990. Income inequality is associated with weaker response capacity for pandemics. This leads to significant negative impacts on health financing, access to medical resources, and public health surveillance [24]. For example, in low-income countries such as those in sub-Saharan Africa, individuals living with HIV and high-risk populations do not receive timely healthcare services, such as HIV testing, enrollment in prevention of mother-to-child transmission programs, and sustained antiretroviral therapy, owing to the lack of primary health-care infrastructure [25, 26]. This contrasts sharply with the comprehensive and high-quality HIV management available in economically developed countries or regions [27, 28]. Additionally, in terms of funding sources, HIV-positive individuals in high-income countries are typically covered by social health insurance, medical assistance, private health insurance, or a combination of these insurances. In contrast, nearly half of the total health expenditures for HIV/AIDS in low-income and lower-middle-income countries are reliant on donations each year [29, 30]. This suggests that in low-income areas, primary health care facilities should be actively expanded to ensure the accessibility of HIV testing, ART, and mother-to-child transmission prevention (PMTCT) services.

Although HIV/AIDS is no longer among the top ten causes of death globally, its lifelong nature remains unchanged [31]. All children under the age of five who are HIV-positive are considered to advanced HIV [32]. This implies that regardless of specific clinical manifestations or CD4 cell counts, children under the age of 5 need closer monitoring and treatment once they are diagnosed with HIV infection [33]. Moreover, treatment for vertically transmitted HIV/AIDS requires particularly potent and well-tolerated regimens due to challenges such as poor adherence and limited options for medication [34]. Therefore, special attention has also been given to the situation of HIV/AIDS among the global population under the age of 5. The results show that, despite a noticeable decline, the global burden of HIV/AIDS among children under 5 remains concentrated in economically disadvantaged countries or regions. In South Africa, vertical transmission and AIDS-related mortality among children persist, especially in rural areas [35], and that ineffective implementation of health policies and intervention strategies is a key factor in the poor management of children living with HIV [36].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its cross-country analysis, utilizing data from 204 countries or regions globally available in GBD 2021, employing the SDI as a standardized economic development indicator to quantitatively assess cross-country inequalities in HIV/AIDS through the calculation of SII and CI. There are also some limitations in the study. Firstly, the lack of capacity to conduct population-based epidemiological research in underdeveloped countries or regions, which is the primary source of GBD data, may lead to an underestimation of the HIV/AIDS burden in these areas. Additionally, due to varying quality of raw data across different countries, GBD employs extensive statistical modeling for smoothing, which underscores the need for careful interpretation of the results. Secondly, this study focuses solely on the relationship between income inequality and the prevalence and trends of HIV/AIDS, without exploring other micro or macro factors, such as race, sexual orientation, and cultural behaviors, which are often intertwined with income inequality, exacerbating cross-country inequalities of HIV/AIDS. Moreover, this study is descriptive and cannot establish causal relationships, but it undeniably aids in understanding the current global disparities and trends in HIV/AIDS burden.

Conclusions

Emphasizing the changes to key populations at highest risk is one of the significant shifts in the core areas of modern epidemiology for HIV/AIDS prevention and control [28]. Among the 204 countries globally, there is substantial variation in the distribution and trends of HIV/AIDS burden. It has been identified that countries with low levels of socio-demographic development disproportionately bear a heavier burden, and that these disparities are widening over time between the lowest and highest socioeconomic status groups. Additionally, the rising trend of HIV/AIDS burden in populations with high and middle SDI levels should be alarming. These findings may provide new evidence for understanding the cross-country inequities in HIV/AIDS.
