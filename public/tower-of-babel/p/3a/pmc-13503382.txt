Estimating the burden of imported malaria in the Asia Pacific region: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kinley Penjor, Binay Thapa, Tsheten Tsheten, Soumya Mazumdar, Nasser Bagheri, Manas Kotepui, Kinley Wangdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImported malaria is a major barrier to achieve and sustain malaria elimination for countries in Asia Pacific. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantified the pooled prevalence of imported malaria in the region.MethodsThe systematic review and meta-analysis protocol was registered in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251008817). Three databases including PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science were used for literature search from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2024 for imported malaria in 22 Asia Pacific malaria eliminating countries. Pooled prevalence with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated using an inverse variance random-effects meta-analysis. Heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis and publication bias were also assessed.ResultsBased on 42 studies conducted in nine Asia Pacific countries with a total of 435 454 malaria cases, the pooled prevalence of imported malaria was 89.0% (95% CI: 75.9–95.5, I2–100%, n = 166 412 imported cases). The pooled prevalence of imported malaria in males, Plasmodium falciparum, and origin from Africa was 94% (95% CI: 92.0–96.0, I2–97.5%, n = 70 619), 57.0% (95% CI: 47.2–65.5, I2–99.5%, n = 60 999) and 64.3% (95% CI: 43.1–81.0, I2–99.5%, n = 67 511), respectively. The imported malaria varied by occupation with 53.0% (95% CI: 34.0–71.0, I2–99.7%, n = 7989) in labourer to 40.0% (95% CI: 15.0–72.0, I2–99.7%, n = 9611) in farmers. The subgroup meta-analysis revealed pooled prevalence of 84.6% (95% CI: 68.3–93.3, I2 –100% n = 154 792) in pre-elimination phase, and it ranged between 95.0% in Sri Lanka (95% CI: 13.0–100, n = 761) and China (95% CI:85.8–98.0, n = 156 031) to 3.2% (95% CI: 2.87–3.47, n = 407) in South Korea.ConclusionImported malaria constitutes a significant proportion of malaria cases in Asia Pacific malaria eliminating countries. The findings highlight the critical need for strengthened cross border coordination for surveillance, and tailored interventions to achieve and sustain malaria elimination efforts in the region.Keywords: Imported, malaria, cross border, elimination, Asia, Pacific, meta-analysis, prevalence

Introduction

Malaria has a long history of global eradication efforts,1 however it still remains a major public health problem, causing 263 million cases and 597 000 deaths in 2023,2 and this figure is three times more than the target set in the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Technical Strategy 2016–30.2 The renewed global efforts in the first 15 years of 21st century brought significant gains in reduction of global malaria burden. The expanded anti-malaria intervention from 2001–15 had resulted in an estimated 30% global malaria incidence reduction and a 47% decline in malaria mortality rates.3 The global malaria incidence declined from 79 per 1000 population in 2000 to 59 per 1000 population at risk by 2015.2 As a result of this renewed global efforts, 47 countries and one territory were certified as malaria free by WHO as of 2025.2,4 Notwithstanding, the progress has not continued along the same trajectory as trend of global malaria incidence increased to an estimated 60.4 per 1000 population at risk in 2023. At the regional level, WHO South-East Asia Regional and Western Pacific region accounted for the highest malaria burden outside of Africa with 5.7 million cases in 2023.2

The Asia Pacific region through Asia Pacific Malaria Eliminating Network (APMEN) have declared to eliminate malaria in the region by 2030.5,6 Since 2010, the region has made significant strides towards elimination with cases halving and deaths reducing by almost 90%.7 However, imported malaria (malaria infection acquired in another country) remains the key challenge to malaria eliminating efforts in Asia Pacific region.8–13 For instance, Bhutan reported nearly 60% of the total malaria infection as cross border imported cases while the imported infection tripled in Thailand from 2426 cases in 2021 to 9129 in 2023, with cases imported from Myanmar.14,15 Imported malaria in the Asia Pacific region are driven primarily due to increased travel and migration across international borders which often share long contiguous land borders.12,16,17 This has not only stalled the elimination progress,8,18,19 but poses a continued threats of re-introduction to malaria eliminated countries.20–22 Imported malaria is not only public health challenge but also poses clinical problem due to diagnostic delays resulting in severe and complicated disease outcomes.23,24 These delays are often driven by low risk perception among the population, overall low compliance to personal protective measures and a lack of clinical suspicion by healthcare providers.25,26 Therefore, it is essential to conduct a comprehensive review of the burden and epidemiology of imported malaria to guide strategies aimed at preventing re-establishment and sustaining progress towards malaria elimination.11,12,27,28 Past studies did not provide a sufficiently broad synthesis on imported malaria burden. For example, previous studies focused narrowly on specific demographic groups like pregnant women29 or were limited to outcomes like severe imported malaria.30 In addition, many studies were conducted in non-endemic settings that did not necessarily reflect contextual realities of cross border importation through population mobility across porous land border, typical in many Asia Pacific countries.31–33 Consequently, the current evidence base is fragmented, leaving significant gaps in knowledge of the magnitude and characteristics of imported malaria within malaria elimination settings. Therefore, this study aimed to generate pooled estimate of imported malaria in Asia Pacific region and factors driving it. The evidence will also be useful to inform policy and program to review, re-focus and re-prioritise strategic interventions to address travel related cross border importation and contribute to the efforts of regional malaria elimination goal by 2030.

Methods

Study protocol and registration

The systematic review protocol was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the identification number CRD420251008817. This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework.34

PICo framework and systematic search strategy

The research question was developed and refined through an iterative consultative process conducted amongst the research team. A PICo (population or problem, Intervention or interest and context) framework was deemed suitable for a non-interventional research questions and guided formulation of our research question. The target participant or population was imported malaria cases reported from the APMEN countries. The main outcome of interest was proportion of imported malaria cases. For this review, ‘imported malaria’ follows the WHO definition referring to cases where the infection was acquired outside the country of diagnosis.35 As a prevalence-oriented study, exposure or intervention was not relevant. The 22-malaria eliminating APMEN countries formed context for this study.36,37 The search terms were generated and refined using the PICo framework and then supplemented with a Medical Subject Headings query. The key search terms, employing Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ included as follows; (malaria OR plasmodium OR falciparum OR vivax OR ovale OR malariae) AND (import* OR ‘cross-border’) AND (prevalence OR incidence) AND (determinants OR ‘risk factor’ OR predictors) AND (Asia OR ‘Pacific region’ OR ‘Asia Pacific’). It was combined using ‘AND’ with individual names of 22 APMEN countries. A detailed search terms and Boolean operators are in Supplementary Table 1.

Comprehensive searches were undertaken for published peer-reviewed articles in three electronic multidisciplinary databases namely PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. The search was conducted from 23 April to 22 May 2025. The retrieved results were exported into Research Information Systems format and imported into online Covidence tool38 designed for systematic review and management. Duplicate articles were identified and removed in Covidence. The short-listed articles were managed in EndNote version 21.0 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA).39 The search was expanded further by backward and forward citation tracking. Backward search was performed by systematically reviewing the entries in the reference lists of the key included articles, while forward searches were conducted using Google Scholar to track any relevant later publication that have cited the included paper.

Eligibility criteria

The following inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied in this study. The inclusion criteria were (i) studies reporting data on the proportion of imported malaria in humans; (ii) studies that included any species of malaria—Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium malariae, Plasmodium ovale and Plasmodium knowlesi; (iii) studies from 22 APMEN countries; (iv) studies published in English language; and (v) study published between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2024, considering APMEN network was established only in 2009.37 Exclusion criteria were (i) non-human malaria; (ii) outside 22 APMEN countries; (iii) articles such as systematic review, case reports or series, conference abstracts and expert opinions; (iv) non-English publications; (v) studies before 1 January 2010 and after 31 December 2024.

Study selection and data extraction

Initially, two researchers (K.P. and B.T.) independently screened all the identified records in Covidence by titles and abstracts and excluded studies that did not address the scope and outcome of systematic review. Full texts of the remaining eligible studies were then retrieved and assessed by the same pair of researchers against predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies in the study short-listing process were resolved through discussion between K.P. and B.T. If no consensus was reached by the first two reviewers, a third independent researcher (K.W.) was involved to reach the final decision.

Data from eligible studies were extracted and entered into a pre-designed Excel template (Microsoft Excel 2021, Microsoft Corporation, USA). The study variables included were author, year of publication, study period, study design, country, phase [pre vs post elimination, which was defined as studies conducted in the year immediately before (pre-) or after (post-) the country interrupted indigenous malaria transmission leading to WHO malaria-free certification], total positive cases, total imported cases, imported malaria cases by species (P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, P. knowlesi and mixed infections), number in males, number of cases by main occupation (farmers, labourers, security force personnels, others), origin of imported cases by two regions—Asia and Africa, and laboratory diagnostic methods. The data extraction process was quality assured by cross checking the initial 10% of the data extraction by each other researcher for consistency and uniformity.

Data analysis and synthesis

The descriptive analysis was performed by calculating frequencies and percentages of categorical variables and counts of imported malaria. The proportion of imported malaria was derived by dividing the total imported malaria cases by the total number of positive cases reported in the studies. Additionally, we estimated pooled proportion of imported malaria by number of P. falciparum, others or mixed infections (includes all other species), male, business occupation, farmer occupation, labourer occupation, imported origin from Africa and Asia, after converting the count of these variables into proportion.

The Cochran’s Q statistic and I2 tests were used to assess the degree of heterogeneity in the outcomes across the included studies. The inconsistency index I2 statistic (measure of variability in the effect estimate due to heterogeneity other than just by chance) values >50% indicated a significant heterogeneity amongst the studies41 When significant high heterogeneity was found, a random effects model generated results was selected42 to better account for variability of outcome of interest across the included studies43 at 95% confidence interval (CI). Meta analysis results are visualised using forest or bar plots. A forest plot was created to present meta-analysis results where each study-specific estimate of proportion of imported malaria is visualised by a squared shape and horizontal lines to indicate the 95% CI. The vertical dashed line represents the overall proportion of imported malaria. Pooled prevalence is shown along with 95% prediction interval.

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis to investigate the variation of imported malaria proportion across studies was conducted with categorical variables—elimination phase, country, administrative level, diagnostic methods used for malaria diagnosis, study duration (period of study: < 5 years, 5–10 years, > 10 years), year of publication and assessed quality of included studies. A chi-squared test for subgroup differences was used to evaluate any statistically significant variation of effect size across studies. All statistical analyses and visualisation was carried out using ‘tidyverse’, ‘meta’ and ‘metaprop’ packages in R program (RStudio 2024.09.1 Build 394 © 2009–24 Posit Software, PBC).44

Publication bias and quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included articles was assessed using Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for research studies reporting prevalence studies.42 The same two authors (K.P. and B.T.) conducted the assessment using the nine key domains of JBI critical appraisal framework. The studies were assessed as ‘High’, for total yes score of >8, ‘Moderate’, for total yes score between 6–8 and rated ‘Low’, for total yes score <6 (Supplementary Table 2). Potential publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and supplemented with Egger’s test for statistical evaluation of the observed asymmetry with cut-off P-value < 0.05. To assess the robustness of the combined effect size estimate, a sensitivity analysis was performed to rule out undue effects of influential studies by systematically excluding one studies at a time.

Results

Literature review and search results

Our initial databases searches yielded a total of 19 085 articles—Scopus (17 823), PubMed (1045) and Web of Science (217). After removing 709 duplicate articles, 18 376 underwent title and abstract screening. Further 18 270 articles were excluded following title and abstract screening, as they did not meet the study objective and inclusion criteria. A total of 106 articles were reviewed for full text with 68 were removed after full text review leaving 38 articles.15,45–81 Four additional articles82–85 were retrieved after backward and forward search, making a total of 42 articles for the final analysis (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

Out of 22 APMEN malaria eliminating countries, data from nine countries were included in this analysis. The maximum publications on imported malaria was from China (71%, 30/42) followed by three from Sri Lanka,46,53,80 two each from Malaysia49,81 and Nepal,62,66 and one study each from Bangladesh,64 Bhutan,15 Indonesia,52 South Korea71 and Thailand,47 respectively. Majority 91% (38/42) of studies were conducted in the pre-elimination phase. The studies on imported malaria were conducted at different administrative levels. Nearly half of studies presented data from provincial (47.6%, 20/42) whereas one-third reported data at the national level (28.6%, 12/42). There was notable increase in publications on imported malaria since 2010; 26.2% (11/42), 40.5% (17/42) and 33% (14/42) of studies were published between 2010–15, 2016–20 and 2021–24, respectively. Over 45% (17/42) of the studies analysed data for a duration ranging between 5 and 10 years.

A total of 435 454 malaria cases with 166 412 imported cases were included in the final analysis. China accounted for nearly 94% (156 031/166 412) of imported cases and 93% (154 762/166 412) of them were from the pre-elimination phase. Although the number of studies increased during the review period, a decline in overall imported malaria burden from 46.7% (77 671) to 42.7% (71 077), and 10.6% (17664) was noted. Malaria was diagnosed with microscopy alone in 1.9% (3236/166 005), microscopy and RDT in 3.8% (6403/166 005), microscopy and PCR in 0.8% (1343/166 005), and all three methods in 93.2% (155 023/166 005) of cases (Table 2). The proportion of imported malaria in nine countries ranged from 3.2% (407/12 916) in South Korea to 70% (499/713) in Bangladesh. Five of the nine countries in this study reported over 80% of imported cases in males. The four countries (China, 42.5%; Malaysia, 24.4%; Sri Lanka, 40.5%; and Thailand, 28.4%) saw imported malaria infection from Africa while importations in Bhutan and Thailand mainly originated from Asia. Imported malaria in security personnel were reported from the studies from Sri Lanka and Indonesia while Bangladesh, Bhutan, Malaysia and Thailand reported imported malaria mainly in labourers (Table 3).

Pooled proportion of imported malaria

The meta-analysis using random effect model from 42 studies estimated pooled proportion of imported malaria in the Asia Pacific region of 89.0% (95% CI: 75.9–95.5, I2–100%, n = 166 412 imported cases/435 454 total cases). The proportion of imported malaria across studies ranged between 3.2% and 100%. The predicted interval for the proportion of imported malaria in future studies ranged from 1.5% to 99.9% (Figure 2). The pooled proportion of imported malaria in: male was 94.0% (95% CI: 92.0–96.0, I2–97.5%, n = 70 619); P. falciparum was 57.0% (95% CI: 47.2–65.5, I2–99.5%, n = 60 999); and originating from Africa was 64.3% (95% CI: 43.1–81.0, I2–99.5%, n = 67 511). The pooled prevalence for different occupation was 53.0% (95% CI: 34.0–71.0, I2–99.7%, n = 7989) in labourer and 40.0% (95% CI: 15.0–72.0, I2–99.7%, n = 9611) in farmers (Figure 3).

Subgroup analysis

A very high degree of heterogeneity was observed across these studies (Tau = 9.38, I2 = 100.0%, P < 0.001). The subgroup analysis showed statistically significant differences in the prevalence of imported malaria across subgroups defined by elimination phase, countries and types of malaria diagnostic methods. In the pre-elimination phase, the pooled prevalence of imported malaria was 84.7% (95% CI: 68.1–93.5) as compared to 100% in post-elimination phase (P < 0.001). The pooled prevalence varied between 95% for Sri Lanka and China to 3.2% (95% CI: 2.87–3.47) in South Korea (P < 0.001), based on the lone studies of acute imported infection.71 The pooled prevalence of imported malaria also varied by different diagnosis methods used across studies [(All three methods—92.7% and 95% CI: 80.7–97.4; microscopy and RDT—70.0% and 95% CI:2 6.22–93.8; microscopy—43.5% and 95% CI: 12.2–81.0; Microscopy and PCR—98.8% & 95% CI: 92.6–99.8), P < 0.001]. Although the difference was not statistically significant, the pooled proportion of imported malaria increased from 83.6% (95% CI: 44.3–97.0, P < 0.589) during 2010–15 to 94.4% (95% CI: 74.5–99.0) during 2021–24 (Table 4 and Figure 4). However, the heterogeneity indicator remained high (I2 > 99%, P < 0.001) in subgroup meta-analysis, indicating the wide variation across studies.

Quality across included studies and publication bias assessment

Using the JBI critical appraisal tool,42 26% (11/42) of included studies were rated as high quality and 74% (31/42) as moderate. No studies were deemed of low quality or excluded on that basis (Supplementary Table 2). A significant asymmetry of funnel plot was observed (Supplementary Figure 1) using egger test (95% CI: 6.5–35.63, Intercept (β₀): 21.0, t-statistic: 2.85, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis performed by leave-one-out-meta-analysis by systematically omitting each studies showed a robust pooled estimate for proportion maintained within 0.85–0.87 (Supplementary Figure 2), demonstrating no undue influence of individual single studies or outlier study effect on the overall result. The overall random effect model estimate was 86.0% (78.0–91.0) and heterogeneity remained high between 99% and 100%, suggestive of collective effect across studies on the calculated effect size. Therefore, we inferred the robust estimate of the pooled imported malaria prevalence without influential single study but with high heterogeneity.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis of 42 studies published in nine APMEN countries provides a pooled imported malaria prevalence approaching 90%. In pre-elimination phase, the pooled prevalence was high at almost 85%, underscoring the persistent challenge of imported cases. The imported malaria was reported predominantly by males (94%) and nearly a third of imported malaria (57%) were P. falciparum. The prevalence of imported malaria in labourers and farmers were 53% and 40%, respectively. Almost two-thirds of imported infection originated from Africa region.

The high prevalence of imported malaria in the Asia Pacific region presents a significant obstacle to the region’s pursuit of malaria elimination goal by 2030.6 This rising trend of imported malaria cases is cited as one of the major challenges to malaria elimination efforts.15,53,73 The experiences from China and Sri Lanka demonstrate that imported malaria becomes more prominent as countries advance in elimination, and emerge as a major threat to sustaining the gains made in eliminating the disease.86,87 The problem of imported malaria will persists as a significant and growing challenge with more countries in the Asia Pacific reaching the pre-elimination stage, until global malaria eradication is realised.88,89

Many of the countries in pre-elimination stage are facing the burden of imported infection which has impeded progress, and lead to protracted ‘last mile’ in elimination journey in countries like Bhutan and Nepal, mainly attributed to cross border importation along international border with India.13,19 As global and regional malaria elimination drive gains momentum to reach its 2030 deadline, it is imperative that the problem of imported malaria is prioritised and addressed using evidence-based surveillance and response interventions, capacity building and cross border collaboration.11 The imported malaria is primarily driven by cross-border migration and travel for work, trade or conflict.89 The porous borders between the countries of Asia Pacific enables unregulated migration, making cross border surveillance for infectious disease like malaria ineffective. These factors not only risk reintroduced transmission in countries that has interrupted local malaria transmission but given the continued presence of competent mosquito vectors86,87,89 could trigger local outbreaks including significant clinical complications.

Males reported higher proportion of imported malaria in this study. Similar findings of high imported malaria in men have been reported in many countries in the region including Thailand47 and China,90 as well as from non-endemic European countries.91,92 Studies included in this systematic review from Bangladesh,64 Bhutan,15 Indonesia52 and Thailand47 recorded more than 80% of imported cases in males. The plausible reasons for this finding include men who may engage in high risk behaviour such as poor compliance with anti-malarial prophylaxis,91 occupational hazards like mining and construction works,48 and high mobility for economic reasons, all of which exposes them to the risk of malaria infection.93 Elevated risk amongst migrant male labourers were also identified in studies on cross border malaria transmission.47,62,79 For example, a study from Nepal found male migrant labourers with frequent travel to India were at a higher risk of malaria.62 Therefore, malaria elimination strategies must design deliberate measures to target men, specifically addressing their mobility patterns and associated risk behaviours.

The high prevalence of imported P. falciparum malaria observed in this study reflects the evolving epidemiology in elimination settings. Generally, P. vivax is the dominant species in the pre-elimination due to its relatively less rapid decline as compared to P. falciparum.48,94 However, increased P. falciparum cases amongst imported cases is reported in both previously endemic15,48 and non-endemic areas95 due to importation from endemic countries from Africa. This should be of concern as P. falciparum malaria is more likely to cause severe malaria with potential fatality,46,90 may carry drug resistant mutations96,97 and can potentially seed re-establishment of malaria transmission, particularly in countries with weak surveillance system.

In our study, labourers were the most affected occupational group. This is consistent with evidence from many malaria eliminating countries in the region including China,26,79,82 Thailand47 and Bhutan.15,17 Some of the reasons cited for elevated risk in this occupational group included high mobility and increased exposures to risk of malaria infection and limited access to malaria services due to marginalization.98 Farmers constituted another important occupational group amongst imported malaria patients. This could be due to increased exposure to mosquito bites while in the field which predispose them to malaria infection.15,99 These findings underscore the need for further risk characterisation and targeting of interventions tailored to specific high-risk groups, as these populations often also remain hardest to reach in elimination settings.100

As countries achieve malaria elimination, majority of imported malaria originated from Africa. It is contributed largely by returning Chinese and Sri Lankan nationals.79 They are either involved in construction business for Chinese nationals or security personnel deployed in United Nations peacekeeping missions and businessman like gem traders travelling to Tanzania and Madagascar in Sri Lankans.46,53 Similarly, other non-endemic countries also reported high importation of malaria infection amongst migrants originating from the Sub-Saharan African region.33 Therefore, concerted efforts should be undertaken to test and treat all travellers returning from high malaria endemic regions like Africa; firstly, to prevent re-introduction in malaria eliminated countries like China and Sri Lanka, and secondly, to support progress towards elimination in pre-elimination countries. As returning travellers from endemic areas can present in any parts of the malaria eliminating country and not just in border areas, nationwide post-elimination preparedness is recommended to prevent imported malaria and potential clinical complications through mandatory pre-travel counselling, chemoprophylaxis services and deployment of responsive and real time risk-based surveillance through use of digital tools.23,25

Limitation

This study has several limitations. The predominant number of studies were retrospective observational studies that were not designed to measure burden of imported malaria and frequently reported only imported malaria cases without total case data, or focused on a single Plasmodium species, leading to their exclusion. Variations in surveillance case definitions and diagnostic methods across studies may have influenced detection and classification, potentially biasing prevalence estimates. Clinically diagnosed cases were included in some studies that could have inflated estimates, and differences in travel history definitions (for instance, 18 days in Sri Lanka vs. 1 month in China) may have affected classification consistency. Additionally, reliance on routine surveillance data introduces risks of under-reporting and recall bias, particularly when travel history is self-reported. The high heterogeneity observed can be partly due to nature of proportional data and differences in study contexts, rather than inconsistent data.101 We did not apply the trim-and-fill method to adjust for the publication bias because of substantial between study heterogeneity. Furthermore, the analysis was limited to studies published between 2010 and 2024, potentially underrepresenting more recent trends, especially in countries newly entering elimination phases. Lastly, because the funnel plot showed significant asymmetry, our pooled estimates may be inflated due to publication bias but more importantly because of substantial between study heterogeneity found.

Conclusion

The study estimated 89% pooled prevalence that is nearly 9 in 10 malaria cases in Asia Pacific countries pursuing elimination are imported, underscoring the burden of imported malaria in the regional elimination efforts. Occupations such as labourers and farmers and being male were associated with imported malaria. Most of the imported malaria cases originated from African countries. Therefore, the effective prevention and control of cross border imported malaria requires enhanced cross-border collaboration and the development of robust surveillance and response systems. The elimination program should provide access to comprehensive and risk-based pre-travel counselling, chemoprophylaxis and timely diagnostic and treatment services that are tailored to address diverse high-risk occupation groups and traveller to/from regions with high malaria transmission like Africa.
