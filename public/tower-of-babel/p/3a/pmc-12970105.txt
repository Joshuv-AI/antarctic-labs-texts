Program LIFT: protocol for a pilot feasibility factorial randomised controlled trial of digital single-session interventions to address self-harm and suicidal ideation in Australian community-based adolescents
Authors: Amaani H Hatoum, Quincy Wong, Zinny Hunt, Bridianne O’Dea, Natasha Josifovski, Daniel Gan, Michelle Torok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSelf-injurious thoughts and behaviours (SITBs) represent a critical public and mental health challenge. Adolescents are disproportionately at risk for engaging in SITBs. Intervening early and addressing pathogenic and proximal risk/vulnerability factors is of critical importance for this vulnerable population. Digital Single-Session Interventions (D-SSIs) offer a promising solution to unmet need for accessible, scalable and engaging care. This pilot study aims to evaluate the acceptability and feasibility of the interventions and clinical trial protocol for Programme LIFT (Little Interventions for Teens), a newly developed suite of D-SSIs targeting emotional regulation, self-criticism, rumination and hopelessness as key risk processes involved in the maintenance of SITBs.Methods and analysisWe aim to recruit 216 Australian adolescents aged 15–19 years who have experienced SITBs in the past 2 months into an online, 2×2 factorial randomised controlled trial to pilot the acceptability and feasibility of the intervention protocol (D-SSI+28 days of skill practice) and trial procedures, and establish study retention. Random allocation procedures will be used to test factors that may affect adherence to the intervention protocol (primary feasibility outcome), by testing the effect of: (1) intervention assignment method (free choice vs clinician prescribed), and (2) a postintervention brief, supportive phone check-in at 7 days postcompletion of the D-SSI (phone call vs no call). Feasibility and acceptability will be assessed using online surveys at postintervention (T1) and 30 days follow-up (T3), along daily brief assessments of skill practice between T1 and T2 (T2). The trial and its protocol were developed and reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items for Interventional Trials checklist.Ethics and disseminationThis study is approved by the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC#9032). Results will be published in an open access peer-reviewed journal, and key study findings will be shared with participants and stakeholders within 3 months after completion of the study.Trial registration numberACTRN12625000963426.Keywords: Risk Factors, Psychosocial Intervention, Adolescent, Suicide & self-harm, Digital Technology

Introduction

Self-injurious thoughts and behaviours (SITBs) are recognised as a critical public health issue due to their life-threatening nature and disability they confer, and because of their widespread prevalence among young people.1 It is estimated that non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), suicidal ideation and suicide attempts have 12-month prevalence rates of 19.5%, 14.2% and 4.5%, respectively, in children and adolescents.2 Each of these suicide-related behaviours increases the risk of suicide.3 With an average age of onset between age 12 and 15 years, SITBs affect adolescents more than any other age group,4 making early intervention critical.

While psychotherapy is considered to be the gold standard treatment for SITBs, it is estimated that 46% of young people who receive specialist mental health services attend only a single (first) session.5 This high rate of dropout is also observed in digital mental health interventions, with estimates that only 66% of young people complete all modules.6 The single session therapy (SST) service delivery model emerged in response to these attrition patterns, as a planned effort to maximise the impact of the first, often only, session of treatment.7 Through a ‘coaching’ style interaction, SST helps the individual to understand their problem as manageable due to the strengths, skills and resources they have available to them. The session typically ends with a realistic, actionable plan to scaffold and achieve the desired change. However, while face-to-face SST has been shown to be effective for a range of clinical issues,8 the human workforce requirements mean that demand for care cannot be met, despite the brevity of the model.

Digital care models have come into prominence in the past decade as a way to increase the availability and accessibility of treatment.9 However, although many of these tools are brief, engagement with them is typically poor, raising questions about their effectiveness—particularly longer term. In this context, a relatively new subfamily of brief digital interventions has emerged, known as Digital Single Session Interventions (D-SSIs). Currently, these are standalone, self-directed psychological programmes focused on teaching a single therapeutic skill that is intended to increase an individual’s ability to manage and resolve their presenting problem, along with an action plan for how change will be achieved.10 While a recent review and meta-analysis showed that D-SSIs for the treatment of depressive symptoms and SITBs in young people have high acceptability and feasibility (mean completion rate=86%), and have demonstrated up to medium term reductions in depression symptoms (Hedge’s g=−0.18),10 and success as reducing internalising symptoms more broadly.11 At present, however, there is limited evidence that D-SSIs reduce severity of, or are linked to remission from, SITBs.10

Efforts to advance our understanding of how to build D-SSIs that are as effective as face-to-face service delivery models are warranted, given that the digital format offers a ‘maximal reach, minimum cost’ framework for improving mental healthcare delivery.12 Solving this challenge means that adolescents can access potentially life-saving low-cost, effective early intervention, reducing burden on healthcare systems. To date, only two D-SSIs specifically targeting SITBs in adolescents have been tested and published.13 14 One of these, Project SAVE (‘Stopping Adolescent Violence Everywhere’), aims to reduce negative self-beliefs via psychoeducation about the cognitive model and teaching distress tolerance skills.13 A randomised controlled trial (RCT) of Project SAVE (Stopping Adolescent Violence Everywhere) showed that both likelihood of, and desire for, future NSSI, as well as self-hatred, decreased postintervention.13 However, the intervention did not reduce actual self-harm or suicidal ideation at 3-month follow-up. Efforts to test varied and alternative direct, dominant mechanisms of change to reduce SITBs are needed to advance both scientific understanding of causal factors and therapeutic practice.

Risk targets for D-SSIs

Theoretical models and empirical studies relevant to NSSI and suicide largely concur on the intrapersonal vulnerability factors of importance for young people. Poor emotional regulation self-efficacy has been identified as a critical proximal intrapersonal vulnerability factor for SITBs, and specifically NSSI.15,17 For example, in the cognitive–emotional model of NSSI, individuals with high emotional reactivity may experience heightened emotional response when in an emotionally volatile situation.15 In this context, NSSI may be activated to downregulate the emotional response or facilitate experiential avoidance of unwanted feelings.15 Accordingly, acquiring and strengthening emotion regulation skills during adolescence has the potential to reduce reliance on NSSI as a coping strategy, as well as severity of suicide ideation.18

Self-criticism has also been linked to the onset and maintenance of SITBs,19 20 and with an increased risk of suicide.21 Self-criticism includes broader constructs of self-critical thoughts, such as negative self-schemas15 and high aversive cognitions as an intrapersonal proximal vulnerability factor.17 Self-criticism may lead to the development of SITBs as either a form of self-punishment,17 22 or as means to avoid or escape aversive self-awareness following attribution of failures to the self (escape theory).23 Currently, however, it is not well understood if self-criticism operates as a direct risk for SITBs, or indirectly via other risk processes such as poor emotion regulation.

Both the emotional cascade model24 and the cognitive–emotional model of NSSI15 hypothesise that emotionally volatile situations may result in excessive rumination, which in turn contributes to greater experience of negative emotions (the emotional cascade), and increasing vulnerability to self-harm as a form of experiential avoidance. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies involving self-injuring adolescents have found that feeling overwhelmed or anxious predicted NSSI most strongly when rumination was elevated, and that participants were at greater risk for engaging in NSSI behaviours when experiencing greater rumination earlier the same day.25 Meta-analytical data supports both the longitudinal relationship between rumination and prospective NSSI26 and between rumination and suicidal ideation.27

Several prominent ideation-to-action theories, such as the three-step theory of suicide,28 have identified hopelessness as one of the strongest proximal risks for suicidal ideation and central to the progression to suicidal behaviour.28 29 Ecological momentary research has shown that hopelessness contributes to daily fluctuations in suicidal ideation.30 Hopelessness has been proposed to have three main components: (1) dismal expectations about the future, (2) blocked goal-directed processing and (3) helplessness.31 32 Although the evidence is strongest for linking hopelessness to suicidality, meta-analytical evidence also indicates it may be an important risk factor for NSSI.33

Based on these theories and their supporting empirical evidence, we developed four new direct-acting D-SSIs purpose-designed to reduce SITBs by targeting proximal risk processes linked to their maintenance and escalation. Collectively, these D-SSIs are known as Programme LIFT (Little Interventions For Teens), conceived to address unmet need for access to brief, targeted, frontline treatment for adolescents at risk of suicide. Each intervention has been developed using best practice SST principles of providing young people with the skills and information needed to manage and mitigate key risks for SITBs through increased self-efficacy to manage the underlying problem/s.

Objectives

The primary objective of this pilot trial is to establish the feasibility and acceptability of the Programme LIFT protocol and trial methods, to inform the development of a high-quality RCT that will minimise biases and potential confounding. Feasibility is evaluated in terms of (A) adherence to the intervention protocol, (B) recruitment, (C) retention and (D) acceptability is evaluated in terms of participants’ subjective experiences of the intervention protocol and (E) the credibility of the intervention. Method

Patient and public involvement

There was no public or consumer involvement in the study design; however, the interventions were co-designed with a lived experience youth advisory group, who were involved in the selection and refinement of intervention targets and therapeutic skills, prototype design and user testing.

Trial design

This pilot study uses a 2×2 factorial superiority RCT design, involving two levels of random allocation. The first level of randomisation involves testing the role of ‘choice’ on adherence to the D-SSI. Prior research indicates that choice-based interventions result in significantly higher retention and adherence compared with no choice (eg, prescribed models).34 Participants will be randomised 1:1 to: (1) being prescribed a D-SSI that aligns with the highest standardised score of the prescreening measures, aligned to the risk factors targeted by each D-SSI (‘clinician-prescribed’ group) or (2) self-selecting a D-SSI from a choice of four programmes, regardless of vulnerability/risk profile (‘free choice’ group). While there will be equal numbers per group, the two conditions will likely have an unequal distribution of D-SSIs within each group. Immediately following this, the second level of random allocation will occur. In this second level, participants within each group will be randomised (1:1) to receive: (1) a brief, structured and supportive check-in phone call from the project clinician at 7 days following the provision of the D-SSI, to assess adherence to skills practice and provide encouragement for ongoing practice or (2) no phone call. Though some recent digital health trials have reported benefits of supportive contact on engagement and adherence to multimodule web-based and app-based interventions,35 the evidence is unclear or non-existent regarding the impact of brief contact on postintervention adherence (ie, skills use/practice). As such, this trial will examine the impact of the follow-up phone call on adherence to the postintervention skills use protocol and to the completion of brief, daily assessments (trial protocol adherence).

Trial setting and participants

The trial will be conducted online, in Australia. We aim to enrol a minimum of 216 community-based adolescents in the study. Our recruitment methods will mimic those we intend to use in a full RCT. We will advertise the trial online, via Black Dog Institute’s social media channels (Instagram, Facebook), via public registers and by using posters in public forums (eg, university campus noticeboards). There will be different recruitment advertisements targeted at the young person, parents of young people and peers, to examine the most effective recruitment pathway. Advertisements will include a URL to a Qualtrics trial landing page, which will be a portal to the consent process.

Adolescents will be eligible to participate if they are aged 15–19 years, reside in Australia at the time of the trial, are English fluent, own and have access to a computer/laptop or smartphone (minimum version iOS-9.0 or Android-5.0), and have experienced self-injurious thoughts or behaviours in the past 2 months, as measured by a bespoke single item (excluding suicide attempts). Adolescents can be engaged in psychotherapy and/or pharmacotherapy during the trial period. Exclusion criteria include having a diagnosis of psychosis or bipolar disorder as we will not know if individuals are on a stable treatment regime, potentially compromising their safety and adherence to the trial. For safety reasons also, adolescents will be ineligible if they report current active suicidal ideation with a specific plan and intent, or a suicide attempt in the past 30 days (as measured by the modified Patient Safety Screener-3). If they self-report a learning difficulty, visual impairment or other difficulty that would make it challenging to answer questions on a computer, laptop, tablet or smartphone, or are currently enrolled and participating in another research trial, they will also be excluded.

Interventions

Digital Single Session Interventions

Four therapeutic D-SSI have been developed for this trial, and each is structured in the same way. Each has an introduction to the session, which explains the structure and purpose of the session, including setting session expectations, to get users into a single-session mindset. Following this, psychoeducational validation statements are introduced and a brief, clinician-led psychoeducation video about maladaptive cycles is provided (eg, ‘what might be keeping you feeling stuck’). This includes defining the struggle in specific behavioural terms that are relatable for a young person. One therapeutic skill is then introduced, which varies according to each D-SSI (see below). The skill is taught via a video animation to demonstrate how to use the skill with fidelity. The skill video is accompanied by examples, written exercises and reflective questions to support understanding and practice of the therapeutic technique. This part of the session also includes encouraging storytelling from other young people to positively reinforce skill engagement postsession. In the final stage of the session, a downloadable action plan is developed, which outlines the steps the user will take to create change, including how they will practise the therapeutic skill for 28 days postintervention, as well as supplemental resources and emergency contacts (see online supplemental file 1 for detailed description of content).

Each D-SSI is approximately 30 min in duration. Participants will be provided with a link to their chosen or prescribed D-SSI, with instructions to complete it in a single sitting. After completing the D-SSI, participants will be instructed to practise the skill introduced in the session at least once per day for the next 28 days. The specific risk targets and therapeutic skills taught in each D-SSI are described below:

STEER: Strategies To Effectively Engage Emotional Regulation

Strategies to effectively engage emotional regulation (STEER) mode targets deficits in emotion regulation. Most theories of NSSI and suicide highlight the central role of emotional dysregulation in the development and maintenance of self-injury. Research outlines that those with higher emotional reactivity and lower perceived ability to cope during times of distress engage in experiential avoidance of unwanted emotion by using NSSI to modulate heightened emotional responses.15 STEER mode teaches cognitive reappraisal (from cognitive–behavioural therapy (CBT)) as the therapeutic skill to increase access to strategies, reduce affective reactivity and experiential avoidance.

RAISE Mode: Reframe Automatic Inner Self-talk Empathetically

Reframe automatic inner self-talk empathetically (RAISE) mode targets self-criticism. Self-criticism may increase risk of SITBs due to desire for self-punishment,17 22 or as means to avoid or escape aversive self-awareness following attribution of failures to the self.23 Using CBT principles, RAISE mode teaches thought challenging (eg, including learning about unhelpful thinking styles) with the aim of reducing cognitive biases, unhelpful thinking styles and increasing self-compassion.

WAVE Mode: Watch And View Experiences

Watch and view experiences (WAVE) mode targets rumination, which is often used, intentionally or unintentionally, as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy.15 24 36 WAVE mode is based on acceptance and commitment therapy, teaching decentering skills as the therapeutic component by introducing meta-awareness, promoting disidentification and teaching cognitive defusion.

HOPE Mode: Highlight values, Outline goals, Plan activities, Empower action

Highlight values, outline goals, plan activities, empower action (HOPE) mode targets hopelessness, as a critical risk process in the onset and maintenance of suicidal ideation.28 It aims to reduce the blocked goal-directed processing and helplessness components of hopelessness.32 In line with CBT principles, HOPE mode teaches values-based, goal-driven behavioural activation as the active therapeutic strategy to increase state hope (pathways to goals and perceived agency), to reduce hopelessness.

Interventions to improve adherence to the D-SSI and postintervention protocol

Participants randomly allocated to the ‘free choice’ group will be directed to a page within Qualtrics where they will be able to see a brief sentence description of all four D-SSIs, and make a single-choice selection as to which one they want to try. After selection, participants will receive a link to access their D-SSI of choice. Participants in the ‘clinician prescribed’ condition will have their D-SSI allocated after a researcher reviews their baseline scores on the risk profile questionnaires (see table 1), to determine what D-SSI may be the best fit based on the highest standardised score on each of the four questionnaires.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes are the feasibility and acceptability. Feasibility will be assessed in terms of: (A) adherence to the intervention (DSSI programme and intervention protocol), (B) recruitment and (C) survey retention. Acceptability will be assessed as the: (D) acceptability of the intervention protocol and (E) credibility of the intervention. These outcome measures will inform whether or not to proceed with a fully powered trial immediately following this study, whether amendments to the trial protocol and/or interventions are needed, or whether the study should not proceed to the next stage at all. The time points at which each outcome and moderator are assessed are included in table 1, in section ‘participant timeline’ and the stop–go decision rules are described in the Statistical analysis section.

Adherence (compliance) to the intervention protocol (feasibility)

The primary measure of feasibility will be completion rates for the D-SSI component. The mean completion time for all D-SSIs combined (and each separately) will also be reported, as well as any technical errors that prevented participants from accessing the programme. Rates of D-SSI initiation will also be reported by condition.

The Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) will be used as a secondary measure of perceived feasibility of the D-SSI.37 The FIM is rated on a 5-point scale (range 1–5), where higher scores indicate higher feasibility (Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (5), eg, ‘HOPE Mode seems easy to use’). It has previously demonstrated good internal consistency (α=0.85 to 0.91).37

The feasibility of the postintervention protocol (ie, the 28-day skill practice protocol) will be assessed as the rate of adherence to the skill practice. Skill practice will be assessed using a daily, single binary item (yes/no) asking participants if they used the skill that day. The adherence rate will be calculated as the percentage of days endorsed as yes. For each day that participants report using the skill, they will be asked about perceived helpfulness of it (5-point Likert item). If they did not use the skill, they will instead be asked about reasons for non-adherence (multiple choice, with option for open text response). There is also one daily item assessing the ease of understanding how to use the skill (7-point Likert item), with follow-up logic prompting response to an open-ended item asking what challenges have been encountered with skill practice. Feasibility of the trial protocol will be reported as a rate of response to the daily skills use survey.

Additionally, feasibility questions are included at the T2 survey to mitigate the risk of participants’ non-response to the daily experience measures. This includes items asking about the: (1) number of days the skill was used (open response); (2) difficulty/ease of daily skill practice (5-point Likert item, and open-ended item regarding factors contributing to difficulty/ease), (3) obstacles to skill use (yes/no, followed up with multiple choice and open response items), (4) confidence in skill use (7-point Likert item, with follow-up open-ended response for those indicating lack of confidence and if confidence was achieved, a Likert item assessing time taken to feel confident); (5) what additional supports would participants want or need to increase confidence (multiple choice, with open text option) and (6) perceived benefits of skill practice (open ended response).

Recruitment (feasibility)

To examine if our recruitment approach was effective, we will report on whether the minimum sample size target was met (primary measure), what proportion of persons who underwent screening were ineligible (and why) and the duration to recruit the minimum sample (reported in weeks). The proportion of individuals who consented to participate and the proportion who proceeded to baseline will also be reported. We will report which recruitment pathways most effectively enable us to reach and recruit our target population, using a question included at baseline that assesses how the participant found out about the study (via self, via parent/carer, or, via a friend/other).

Survey retention (feasibility)

The proportion of attrition (dropout) overall (primary measure) and at each survey time point (T1, T2) will be reported. New participants will not replace dropouts. We will also report those who begin but do not finish surveys at each time point and will report on those whose data will not be included due to concerns with data validity.

Acceptability of the intervention

The Programme Feedback Scale (PFS; Schleider et al) will be the primary measure used to assess acceptability. Seven items are rated on a 5-point scale (range 1–5), where higher scores indicate higher acceptability (Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (5), eg, ‘I enjoyed the program’). This scale has demonstrated good internal consistency in previous studies (α=0.87–0.93).38 39 The D-SSI model will be considered acceptable with a mean score ≥3 on the PFS at T1 (primary acceptability measure), while this same cut-off at T2 will be used to determine the acceptability of the intervention protocol (daily skills practice).

The Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM) and Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM) will be used as secondary measures of acceptability.37 Both the AIM and the IAM have four items rated on a 5-point scale (range 1–5), where higher scores indicate higher acceptability and appropriateness, respectively (Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (5), eg, ‘HOPE Mode is appealing to me’). These measures have both previously demonstrated good internal consistency (α=0.85–0.91).37

Four open-ended questions adapted from Crosby and Witte40 will be used to capture information regarding what aspects of the intervention were helpful, not helpful, difficult to understand and what participants would change (if anything). Two bespoke items (with embedded logic) will also be used to assess the perceived age appropriateness of content (yes/partially/no; with an open ended follow-up question asking for clarification for those who do not respond ‘yes’), and issues with reading or understanding the language used (yes/partially/no; with an open ended follow-up question asking for clarification for those who do not respond ‘yes’).

Credibility (acceptability)

The credibility subscale of the Credibility/Expectations Questionnaire (CEQ)41 will be used to examine the perceived credibility of the D-SSI. The three-item credibility subscale uses a 9-point Likert scale (range=1–9, where 1=not at all logical/successful/confident, 9=very logical/useful/confident) to evaluate items such as, ‘How successful do you think the intervention will be in helping your symptoms/functioning?’. Higher scores indicate higher perceived credibility. The CEQ has previously demonstrated good internal consistency (α=0.86).41 Interventions will be considered credible with a mean score ≥5 on the CEQ credibility subscale.

Sample Size

Consistent with methodological guidance on pilot and feasibility studies,54 this trial was not powered to detect efficacy. Instead, the sample size was selected to allow us to assess estimates of key feasibility parameters and is in line with published recommendations, as well as minimum rules-of-thumb for pilot trials.54 Estimates were also guided by comparable and relevant prior pilot feasibility studies.40 55 As such, a sample size of n=40 per group (arm) was considered sufficient to meet the main aims of this pilot trial. Assuming an attrition rate of 25% by the end of our final follow-up 4 weeks postintervention, 54 participants are needed (rounded up) per arm. Therefore, a minimum of 216 participants will be recruited. Recruitment for the trial will cease when the preregistered sample has been recruited, or when the funding period for the trial has ceased.

Randomisation and Masking

As per (figure 1), at the first level of randomisation, randomisation will occur following the completion of the baseline survey. Participants will be allocated (1:1) to either a free choice (intervention) or prescribed (control) group, automatically, using the internal randomisation function in Qualtrics. A biostatistician who is independent of the project will set up the randomisation sequence algorithm (1:1 randomisation ratio per level). This algorithm will be implemented using system embedded randomisation features and backend script within Qualtrics, such that the allocation sequence is concealed from investigators (aside from the study clinician).

Study flowchart.

Participants are not informed about how, and in what proximity to the baseline survey completion the D-SSI will be available to them, thus maintaining participant blinding to the allocated condition. Also, a blind-to-hypothesis approach will be used, in which participants will be aware that they will receive access to one D-SSI, but the role of ‘choice’ in the decision will not be communicated to them at any stage.

In the second level of randomisation, participants will be subsequently allocated (1:1) within each group (free choice and prescribed) to receive, or not receive, a follow-up supportive phone call at 7 days after completing the D-SSI. If participants assigned to the phone call group do not complete the D-SSI, then they will still receive the call, as per the randomisation. However, if participants respond to the call (in the event of non-dropout), the content will include brief discussion regarding non-adherence to the D-SSI (eg, barriers to engagement with intervention).

Unique numerical identifiers will be assigned to participants, as well as to the allocation conditions at each level of randomisation, so that the identities and exposure conditions are unknown to the trial statistician and the principal investigator (allocation concealment). Altogether, allocation will result in four arms.

Data Collection

Study recruitment began in October 2025, and we hope to recruit our planned sample by September 2026. All outcome surveys will be completed online, in Qualtrics. Participants will receive links to the Qualtrics surveys (T0, T1, T3) via the contact details they registered with. Data about the participants (demographic, clinical) and their expectations of the intervention will be collected at baseline (T0), prior to random allocation (see table 1). After completion of the intervention, participants will be asked to complete (1) a postintervention survey to assess feasibility and acceptability of the D-SSI (T1) and (2) a once–daily brief survey for 28 days to assess skill use and perceived helpfulness (T2). The daily surveys will be administered using the REDCap MyCap application, where participants will receive a notification at 8pm each evening to complete the survey. Participants will complete a final follow-up outcome survey at 30 days postintervention (T3). Each survey link will remain active for 5 days, with a maximum of two reminders sent at days 3 and 5 if they have not completed the survey. During the T2, daily assessment period, a maximum of two automated email reminders will be sent per week to remind participants of the skill to be practised alongside an information sheet to support them in doing so. Participants who are allocated to the ‘phone call’ condition will receive one telephone call 7 days after completing the D-SSI.

Data Management

Electronic data (online surveys, daily skills monitoring) will be stored digitally on a University of New South Wales (UNSW) protected server using password-protected access systems only known to the research investigators and research officers named on the UNSW approved ethics. In accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines, electronic data and informed consent forms will be retained for at least 15 years after completion of the pilot study. The electronic data collected has been classified as low risk, sensitive data.

Trial Safety Monitoring

During the study period, the principal investigator and trial manager with the support of the project psychologist will monitor for adverse events and adverse reactions and respond accordingly. Participants are given mental health support contacts during the consent process, and these are reprovided at each assessment point. Adverse events will be documented and reported to the trial sponsor, the relevant Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) and study investigators within 24 hours of the project team becoming aware of the event, with a plan for the measures that will be taken to minimise future risk. The study psychologist will contact these participants by phone and conduct a risk assessment to establish the immediate safety of the participant, and notification of local emergency services and caregivers/guardians, if applicable (for participants under 16’s). Check-ins will be conducted 7 days postreport or postincident.

Given the expected relatively short duration of the pilot study and that this trial does not collect clinical outcomes, no formal data safety monitoring committee will be established to periodically review the accumulating data or to determine whether the pilot should be modified or discontinued.56

Amendments

Any amendments to the protocol, including changes to study objectives, outcomes, study design, subject population, sample size or other study procedures that may impact the conduct of the study, need to be approved by the UNSW Human Ethics Research Committee and recorded and filed by the Sponsor.

Statistical Analysis

Acceptability: PFS scores (primary measure of D-SSI acceptability) at T1 will be calculated both as a mean and by examining individual item scores. Mean and item scores will be considered acceptable using cut-off scores ≥3 (indicating positive endorsement and perceptions of the D-SSI; as per Schleider et al).57 Mean scores of the AIM and IAM will also be reported (scores ≥3 indicate acceptability). Open-ended acceptability questions will be analysed qualitatively using thematic analysis, and descriptive statistics of closed-ended questions reported. PFS scores at T2 ≥3 will also indicate acceptability of the postintervention skill use protocol. The mean of the credibility subscale of the CEQ will be reported descriptively, with scores ≥5 indicating perceived credibility.

Feasibility: For the D-SSI, a completion rate of ≥85% will indicate feasibility of the interventions (as per the indicated mean from a systematic review of D-SSIs for SITBs and depression in young people).11 Completion rates of the D-SSI and FIM scores (secondary feasibility measure) will be reported overall and also by the level one intervention assignment condition (free choice vs clinician recommendation).

Overall, a 60% adherence rate to the postintervention skill use protocol would mean the recommended skills practice protocol is feasible, as indicated by previous adherence rates to a 1 month postintervention protocol.40 In terms of the feasibility of adherence to daily surveys, EMA studies in at-risk adults with suicidal ideation have demonstrated survey completion rates between 78% and 82% for similar time frames (21–28 days).58 Meta-analytical data also indicates that age does not have a significant effect on EMA compliance.59 This is in alignment with previous meta-analytical data obtained from child and adolescent samples, which indicated an average EMA compliance rate of 78%, whereby compliance did not differ by duration of EMA study period.60 Therefore, adherence to the postintervention survey protocol in the present study means reporting on skill use on at least 75% of the days (at least 21/28 days). Adherence to the intervention protocol in terms of skill use and skill use reporting will be described overall and for both brief check-in conditions (check in vs no check in) for each intervention assignment method condition separately.

The following criteria will be used to assess whether the study will proceed to a full-scale RCT:

≥85% of the sample fully complete the D-SSI programme, regardless of allocation.

≥85% of the sample fully complete the D-SSI programme, regardless of allocation.

≥75% of the sample is successfully recruited in the specified period.

≥75% of the sample is successfully recruited in the specified period.

≥80% of the sample complete T1 surveys and ≥70% complete T2 surveys.

≥80% of the sample complete T1 surveys and ≥70% complete T2 surveys.

Mean acceptability score of ≥3 on the PFS (total sample).

Mean acceptability score of ≥3 on the PFS (total sample).

Mean credibility score of ≥5 on the CEQ (total sample).

Mean credibility score of ≥5 on the CEQ (total sample).

The pilot study will proceed to a full trial if ≥3 of these criteria are met. In the event that criteria are partially met at this threshold, modifications to the design and/or intervention will be undertaken before proceeding. Failure to meet this threshold means that we will not proceed to a full trial.

The association between baseline individual and environmental factors and adherence to the treatment protocol in terms of D-SSI completion will be examined by correlating each of the factors with completion of D-SSI (yes vs no) across all study conditions. Associations of baseline factors with adherence to postintervention skill use will be examined by correlating each of the factors with the number of days of skill use across all study conditions. Missing baseline data will be handled via multiple imputation. Finally, the percentage of participants reporting recruitment via each pathway (via self vs via parent/carer vs via friend/other) will be reported. Stringent data quality criteria will be applied through several methods. For data quality assurance, at postintervention, one question will ask participants to select a predetermined answer. We will also identify and exclude responses that demonstrate a straight-lining pattern, whereby an individual provides identical responses to all survey items regardless of their content and direction.

Consent

All individuals who are interested in participating in this trial will be required to provide voluntary, informed opt-in consent before proceeding to eligibility screening. Once they enter the Qualtrics trial portal, they will be requested to read the ethics-approved participant information statement prior to being asked to provide digital consent. The consent process varies according to the age of the interested individual. Those aged 18 and 19 years will be able to consent themselves into the trial. Participants aged 16 and 17 years will follow a mature minor consent process whereby they will be able to self-consent into the trial after successfully completing a Gillick competence test. This test requires participants to correctly answer several questions designed to assess their competency (understanding) to consent to treatment.61 This process has previously been used in several adolescent mental health research studies conducted by the team62 and internationally.63 For those aged 16–19 years, they will need to click on an ‘I consent’ button, agreeing to all the main study tasks. Following consent, they will be directed onwards to complete the screening procedure online, and if eligible, to register their contact details for the study. Given the high rates and risks of false participation in online health research,64 registration also includes a ‘welcome’ phone call or SMS voicenote exchange for participants aged 16–19 years, conducted by a member of the research team, with the partial purpose of determining if the online participant is a genuine participant of the required demographic. The welcome call/SMS voicenote exchange also serves as an opportunity to briefly introduce intervention as ‘preparation’ for the session has been shown to increase the likelihood of treatment success.65

Participants under 16 years will provide consent but additionally require parental/guardian consent to participate before being able to register for the study. During the consent process, they are required to provide parental/guardian contact details in order to set up a videoconference for the combined consent and ‘welcome call’ needed for registration.

Ancillary and post-trial care. All participants who are ineligible due to having an active suicidal plan with intent at the time of screening are provided with a list of emergency contacts at the end of the screening procedure. For participants aged 16–19 years, those who self-complete the consent and screening process will be offered the opportunity to be contacted by the study psychologist to check their safety and refer the individual onto appropriate services for immediate support, if needed. For participants aged 15, consent and screening is conducted via phone call and, if determined to be ineligible, will be offered the opportunity to speak with the project psychologist during the call.

Participants will be provided with compensation to encourage non-attrition at our primary time points of interest. This includes an e-gift card following completion of the T1 and T2 surveys ($A10 at each timepoint) and additional compensation of $A2 per brief daily assessment completed (to a maximum total value of $A56). As such, the maximum reimbursement amount available is $A76, and this will be provided to participants at the end of the study as a single gift card.

Discussion

This protocol outlines the aims and methods of a pilot feasibility study of Programme LIFT, which aims to reduce SITBs in adolescents through targeted single session intervention. In this pilot trial, we seek to recruit a national, community-based, sample of Australian adolescents currently experiencing SITBs and examine the acceptability and feasibility of the intervention protocol, recruitment and retention with the intention of using this information to inform the decision to proceed to a full RCT. Our innovative pilot design uses randomisation to rigorously test specific factors (choice, follow-up brief contact) that are hypothesised to influence adherence to the intervention protocol. By examining the influence these factors have on feasibility, we will develop valuable data-driven insights that will help to refine the study design to optimise engagement and thus clinical benefits in a fully powered trial. This pilot trial will also help understand the extent to which adolescents are willing and able to engage with this type of intervention, establishing a preliminary understanding of whether D-SSIs have potential to increase access to evidence-based support for adolescents experiencing SITBs.

The present study has several other notable methodological strengths. Our D-SSIs target risk factors that have been theoretically and empirically linked to the development and maintenance of SITBs, providing a robust foundation for potential effectiveness. Each D-SSI uses evidence-based psychological principles from established treatment frameworks, whereby therapeutic components (active ingredients) are matched to the intervention target. It is anticipated that these D-SSIs may act as useful frontline treatments for young people experiencing SITBs, enabling them to self-manage risk or provide a gateway into other care pathways. Importantly, the D-SSIs were co-designed with a youth advisory group, involving five young people aged 15–18 years who have lived experience of SITBs, to better meet the needs and preferences of the intended end users. This study is designed according to best science principles for pilot trials, ensuring the findings can inform the development of a high-quality RCT that will minimise biases and confounding from poor methodology. Innovatively, this pilot trial uses randomisation processes to understand where the study design can be modified to strengthen the methodological rigour at the next stage of testing.

However, there are some limitations of the trial and its design. As a pilot feasibility trial, this study is constrained by a relatively small sample size and does not assess efficacy. While the latter may be viewed as a limitation, our pilot design is aligned with the true purpose of what a pilot trial is meant to do—that is, verify the feasibility of the intervention protocol and recruitment methods.66 67 These data will enable a robust clinical trial design to establish efficacy in subsequent testing stages. While the four D-SSIs follow the same format, there are differences in the content and graphic design between them. Using a two-arm randomisation process (choice vs no choice), rather than a four-arm design where individuals are allocated equally to each D-SSI means that it is not possible to know if observed adherence differences are attributable to autonomy or to differential acceptability of specific intervention content. Additionally, the trial does not offer a low threshold for participation, since individuals cannot register anonymously, that is, age verification and eligibility screening are required. These higher entry requirements may be seen by adolescents as incompatible with the self-guided/unsupervised design of the intervention, potentially acting as a threat to participant enrolment. Finally, assessment of daily skill use for 28 days postintervention has the risk of obtaining lower than desired response rates, which is common in studies with intensive or prolonged assessment schedules. However, to mitigate this attrition risk and accurately assess the true retention to the intervention protocol, participants will also be given a chance to retrospectively report on skill use at 1 month follow-up. Any issues with, and limitations of, the methods and interventions tested in this pilot study will be directly useful in informing adaptations to the design of a larger scale trial. Examining the acceptability and viability of Programme LIFT is an important first step in improving access to scalable digital interventions that address risk for SITBs in young people. There is strong potential for these novel D-SSIs to enhance prevention and early intervention efforts.
