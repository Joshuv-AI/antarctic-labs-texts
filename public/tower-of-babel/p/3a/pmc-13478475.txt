The shape of attention reflects flexible filtering of natural speech modulations
Authors: Moïra-Phoebé Huet, Mounya Elhilali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerception requires more than passive sensing; it involves prioritizing features relevant to cognitive goals, guided by selective attention. Natural stimuli, such as speech, involve spectrally entangled features, posing demands on attentional mechanisms. A central question is whether attention amplifies all features of the selected input or instead reshapes how stimulus information is represented according to the listener’s goal. Here, we recorded EEG while participants performed comprehension or identification tasks on identical continuous speech in noise. To characterize speech cortical representations, we introduce the Modulation Response Function (MRF), a neural analogue of the acoustic modulation spectrum that characterizes cortical sensitivity to spectrotemporal modulations. MRFs revealed distinct relative attentional strategies: comprehension enhanced formant modulations supporting intelligibility, whereas identification emphasized pitch cues linked to voice presence. These differences remained stable across time and listening difficulty, consistent with flexible, goal-dependent attentional filtering. These findings refine future models of how cognitive goals shape speech representations.Subject terms: Cognitive neuroscience, Attention, Perception

Introduction

Perception in natural environments is not passive; it requires prioritizing what matters amid competing input. Selective attention is widely viewed as the mechanism that filters sensory evidence, enhancing relevant signals and suppressing distraction1. From spotting a face in a crowd to following a single voice in noise, this selection serves different ends such as detection, identification, and comprehension. This raises a central question: to what extent does its operation depend on the listener’s goal?

Foundational models in cognitive science treat attention as a filter that selects inputs aligned with current goals and discards others early in processing2,3. While these models underscore the importance of relevance, they leave open whether attention uniformly boosts all features of selected inputs or selectively weights particular stimulus dimensions according to task demands. Attention has been shown to enhance selected objects or locations broadly, boosting multiple attributes in parallel even when only one is behaviorally relevant4–8. Other studies emphasize more flexible, goal-dependent selectivity, showing that performance can be optimized by enhancing the most informative dimensions rather than the cued feature itself9,10, and identical stimuli can elicit distinct cortical patterns depending on the task11–13. Yet most of this work concerns feature spaces defined by separable dimensions, such as orientation versus motion or pitch versus spatial location. By contrast, natural stimuli often involve features that are interwoven rather than perfectly separable. For instance, components of acoustic signals such as pitch and formant structure partly overlap in their spectral representation14,15, making them less orthogonal than features such as spatial localisation and pitch. This problem is crucial because entangled feature spaces reflect natural and complex situations, such as the perception of language or music. This poses a key challenge for understanding attention: how granularly can it operate in these conditions? Does attention operate coarsely, amplifying a stimulus as a whole, or can it finely reweight partially overlapping features according to the current goal? More concertely, does attention operate on stimulus targets, amplifying their sensory attributes uniformly, or does it operate on goals, selecetively enhancing the attributes that are most relevant to the current task? These two possibilities are often counfounded.

Speech is a natural testbed because the same signal supports distinct cognitive goals such as detecting that someone is speaking in noise, identifying a talker, or understanding the content. In noisy scenes, selective attention enhances the target stream16–18, but what counts as the target depends on the goal, especially in detection, identification and comprehension. For detection, attention lowers effective thresholds by increasing sensitivity to specific frequency bands or spatial locations19–21, with neural signatures consistent with sensory gain in animals and humans22–24. For comprehension, listeners must segregate and track a target stream amid competitors (the cocktail-party effect)25 using temporal and spatial cues26,27. Neurally, the attended stream is more strongly represented and can be recovered with neural tracking models (spectrotemporal receptive fields, STRF; and temporal response functions, TRF)28–31, including enhanced tracking of temporal structure and refined spectral selectivity for relevant features28,29. These findings highlight the central role of spectrotemporal structure in the neural representation of speech, irrespective of the listener’s goal.

Spectrotemporal modulations provide a key framework for speech acoustics. The modulation power spectrum (MPS) summarizes how energy is distributed over spectral and temporal modulation rates, highlighting the acoustic structures essential for speech32,33. Speech intelligibility is commensurate with the preservation of spectrotemporal modulations within specific frequency and temporal ranges34,35: formant structure concentrates at low spectral and intermediate temporal modulations, whereas gender cues, largely carried by pitch, lie at higher spectral modulations, with temporal modulation tending to be lower on average but partially overlapping with formant regions34,36. Figure 1 outlines these regions (see Supplementary Methods for corpus analyses). Because these cues do not contribute equally to different goals, pitch for identifying the presence and source of a voice and formants for phonemic recognition and intelligibility, this raises the possibility that attention selectively enhances distinct parts of the modulation space during identification versus comprehension37–39. Beyond selective attention alone, different listening goals may also recruit distinct levels of speech processing. In particular, comprehension engages hierarchical linguistic operations, including phonemic, lexical, and sentence integration, that can modulate the balance between acoustic and higher-level linguistic representation40,41. As a result, changes in spectrotemporal sensitivity could partly reflect acoustic feature weighting as well as higher-level linguistic influences.

To test these acoustic predictions at the neural level, we must at a minimum separate task effects from stimulus changes. This is a necessary condition that we satisfy in our design by holding the auditory input constant across tasks. Because neural responses depend on both cognitive demands and sensory features42, attributing task effects is difficult when the two covary. Only a few studies directly contrast cognitive goals, and when they do, they often change the input with the task9–11, use brief, highly controlled stimuli rather than identical continuous speech43–48, or stay within a single linguistic tier13,49, leaving it unclear how attention operates across qualitatively different goals. As such, these approaches fail to generalize to everyday listening scenarios, where the auditory system faces heightened demands. Here, we isolate the role of the goal by keeping the auditory stimulus strictly constant and varying only the task instruction for two cognitive goals: identifying the sound category (e.g. speech vs. other environmental sounds; identification) and understanding the speech meaning (comprehension). Previous fMRI work has reconstructed cortical representations of spectrotemporal modulations from neural responses different sounds35,36,46,50,51, but lacks the temporal resolution to isolate such rapid cues. We therefore use EEG neural tracking (temporal response functions, TRF) to obtain time readouts of continuous speech and introduce a new analysis framework, the Modulation Response Function (MRF), a cortical analogue of the acoustic modulation power spectrum that summarizes spectrotemporal sensitivity (see Methods).

With this approach, we evaluate two mechanistic possibilities while also examining goal effects on continuous speech and isolating acoustic cues that are relevant to the task (F0; formants). A global-gain, shape-invariant account predicts that task differences reduce to uniform scaling of spectrotemporal sensitivity, yielding similar task MRF profiles that could differ only in magnitude (Gain hypothesis). A goal-dependent, shape-flexible reweighting account predicts localized changes within the modulation space, for example, enhancing formant features during comprehension and pitch features during identification (Discriminative hypothesis). Figure 1 illustrates these predictions. Here we show, with identical continuous speech and EEG TRFs, that task demands reshape cortical spectrotemporal sensitivity: identification preferentially boosts pitch modulations, whereas comprehension selectively enhances formant modulations. By introducing a Modulation Response Function (MRF), a cortical analogue of the MPS, we localize these effects within modulation space, revealing goal-dependent reweighting even across entangled acoustic dimensions and extending modulation accounts from fMRI/MEG to time-resolved EEG.

Results

Participants performed comprehension and identification tasks while EEG was recorded. The same audio was used in both tasks: 15 s scenes mixing four sources, one speech track and three non-speech sources drawn from music and environmental categories, with asynchronous onsets and offsets (i.e., partial temporal overlap; not all sound sources were active at every moment). Background noise was present throughout each trial, and listening difficulty was manipulated at two levels (easy/difficult) using modulation-shaped noise designed to preserve key speech modulations (see Methods). In comprehension, participants selected a keyword from the sentence; in identification, they indicated which sound categories were present (Fig. 2).

Behavioral performance

Figure 3 summarizes performance in terms of accuracy, contrasting Easy vs. Difficult conditions for each task. For display, accuracy values were averaged within participant and condition and expressed as percentages (comprehension: correct = 100%, phonological/semantic = 50%, analogous = 0%; identification: proportion correct out of 7 × 100; see Methods). Statistical inference was performed at the trial level using binomial GLMMs with Task, Difficulty, and their interaction as fixed effects, and random intercepts for Subject and Trial. Significance of model terms was assessed using Wald χ² tests (see Methods; Table 1). The combined model revealed significant main effects of Task and Difficulty, but no significant Task x Difficulty interaction. The absence of interaction indicates that the effect of noise difficulty did not differ reliably between tasks. For clarity, results are therefore described separately by task. In comprehension (Fig. 3a), there was no significant main effect of Difficulty on overall accuracy. However, analyses by keyword type revealed two effects: correct keyword selections were more frequent in the easy condition, whereas semantically related keywords were chosen more often in the difficult condition. This shift toward semantic alternatives under challenging conditions suggests engagement of higher-level reasoning rather than simple word repetition. Participants did not merely rely on auditory matching, but actively processed meaning, indicating a deeper level of cognitive engagement. In identification (Fig. 3c), overall accuracy was higher in the easy condition, driven primarily by animal and human categories; other categories showed no reliable differences. For some categories (e.g., narration), performance was near ceiling, potentially limiting observable effects of noise.

Neural tracking across tasks

We first quantified neural tracking using envelope reconstruction before turning to more detailed spectrotemporal analyses. In this approach, linear models were trained to reconstruct the speech envelope from the EEG; the correlation between original and reconstructed envelopes provides a measure of how strongly cortical responses follow the speech signal. Neural tracking was estimated separately for each task, collapsing across listening difficulty, as this analysis aimed to quantify overall task differences rather than Task × Difficulty interactions (see difficulty analyses below). We fit trial-level LMMs (Task as a fixed effect; random intercepts for Subject and Trial) on reconstruction accuracy using Wald χ² tests (see Methods). Correlations between reconstructed and original stimulus envelopes were nearly twice as high during comprehension (μ R comprehension = 0.095) as during identification (μR identification = 0.047), indicating stronger brain-stimulus synchronization in the comprehension task (Fig. 4; β=−0.05,SE=0.003,χ21=171.49,p<0.001). For chance, we used a baseline derived from correlations with mismatched trials. Envelope reconstruction exceeded this chance baseline in both tasks (comprehension:β=0.09,SE=0.002,χ21=1860.9,p<0.001;identification:β=0.04,SE=0.002,χ21=527.43,p<0.001). TRF profiles showed similar temporal fluctuations across tasks, with prominent activity spanning early, mid-latency, and later time windows, and an additional early fluctuation in comprehension. Scalp topographies corresponding to these temporal windows are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

MRF across tasks

To test whether selective attention shapes speech spectrotemporal representations differently across tasks, we reconstructed the full speech spectrogram. Unlike envelope tracking, spectrogram reconstruction captures fine-grained acoustic features, such as formants and harmonics (see Supplementary Note 2), allowing us to examine whether attention enhances these features uniformly (Gain hypothesis) or in a manner that depends on the listener’s goal (Discriminative hypothesis; Fig. 2). Separate decoders were trained for each task to reconstruct the speech spectrograms. While overall spectrogram reconstruction accuracy was lower than envelope tracking, it was significantly above chance baseline in both tasks (comprehension: μR=0.041,β=0.04,SE=0.001,χ21=1910.4,p<0.001; identification: μR=0.019,β=0.02,SE=0.001,χ21=487.3,p<0.001). Critically, these spectrogram reconstructions served as the basis for deriving the cortical Modulation Response Function (MRF), allowing a direct comparison of spectrotemporal tuning across tasks. Both tasks produced MRFs that preserved the overall structure of the speech modulation profile. In both tasks, strong sensitivity was observed in the formant modulation region, while pitch modulations were also represented (Fig. 5a, b). Because these panels display task contours, they primarily illustrate the overall spectrotemporal structure within each task rather than enabling a direct comparison of feature prominence across tasks. To isolate task differences, we therefore compared the mean-centered MRFs, which reveal the relative weighting of modulation regions. A non-parametric, two tailed permutation test (Fig. 5c), with max statistic correction (p < 0.0001) revealed significant task differences: stronger formant sensitivity during comprehension and relatively greater pitch sensitivity during identification. To test this interpretation with approaches independent of modulation analysis, we conducted complementary analyses based on both backward reconstructions and forward encoding models. From the reconstructed spectrograms (backward approach), we extracted acoustic features directly, including: a harmonicity score indexing voice identification, and formant trajectories indexing speech comprehension. In parallel, we implemented forward TRF models using feature pooled spectrogram representations emphasizing pitch and formant structure. Both approaches yielded convergent results: voice cues (harmonicity and pitch) were enhanced during identification, whereas formant representations were comparable across tasks (see Supplementary Note 2). These results align more with the Discriminative hypothesis, suggesting that the attentional filter adapts to the specific demands of the cognitive task. In contrast, a Gain hypothesis would have predicted a uniform enhancement of speech features favoring one task over the other rather than goal-dependent modulations.

To probe temporal dynamics, we then performed a time analysis by dividing each narrative into three equal-duration segments (first, middle, final third) and recomputing MRFs within each segment. Across time, the overall spectrotemporal patterns remained qualitatively stable in both tasks: comprehension consistently exhibited stronger sensitivity to formant modulations, whereas identification showed relatively stronger sensitivity to pitch modulations in each third of the narrative (Fig. 5d, e). To quantify temporal dynamics, we tested for a linear effect across the three segments using a non-parametric two-tailed permutation test with max statistic correction (p < 0.0001). This analysis indicated that the primary temporal change was a gradual decrease in the overall magnitude of sensitivity over time, while the relative spectrotemporal structure associated with each task remained largely preserved. We further examined whether the difference between tasks changed across the course of the narratives by applying the same linear analysis to the task difference. This analysis did not reveal any significant temporal effect (Fig. 5f), indicating that the contrast between tasks remained stable over time.

Response‑dependent MRFs in comprehension

We next examined MRFs as a function of behavioral responses in the comprehension task. Overall profiles were similar across response types, with one notable deviation: trials with phonological keyword selections appeared to show a relatively more prominent pitch modulation peak than the other response types (Fig. 6). Direct contrasts between correct and phonological responses showed significant differences in both pitch and formant regions (TFCE-corrected p < 0.05), with stronger modulation for correct responses. Comparisons between correct semantic and correct analogous responses did not yield significant differences inside the canonical speech regions.

Noise difficulty analyses

To maximize sensitivity, four decoders were trained separately for each Task×Difficulty condition (control analyses with a shared decoder yielded comparable results). We fit trial-level LMMs (Task, Difficulty, and their interaction as fixed effects; random intercepts for Subject and Trial) on reconstruction accuracy and tested the main effects and interaction using Wald χ² tests. No significant effect of noise difficulty was observed, with average correlations remaining similar across conditions within each task (Table 2; Fig. 7), indicating that neural tracking was robust regardless of noise level; all reconstructions were above chance baseline. In the easy condition, TRFs exhibited temporal profiles with fluctuations spanning early to later latencies that were similar across tasks. Under difficult listening, these profiles diverged: during comprehension, mid-latency fluctuations were reduced while earlier fluctuations were relatively enhanced, whereas during identification only early-latency fluctuations remained prominent.

Discussion

Selective attention is often conceptualized as a mechanism that enhances the neural representation of relevant stimuli. Yet a critical question remains: does attention enhance all aspects of a stimulus uniformly, or does it adaptively reshape sensory neural representation to meet cognitive demands? Applied to speech, this question is particularly relevant because pitch and formant structure are not separable features but overlapping dimensions within the acoustic spectrum. By contrasting comprehension and identification tasks on identical acoustic input, our study provides evidence that task goals are associated with spectrotemporal neural resepresentations that differ in a manner consistent with flexible, goal-depedent filtering.

Behaviorally, listeners remained highly accurate across tasks, indicating sustained engagement even under adverse conditions. In comprehension, increased selection of semantic distractors under noise suggests reliance on top-down expectations when acoustic input was degraded, consistent with prior evidence that listeners recruit contextual knowledge to compensate for degraded speech52,53. Identification was generally robust, with difficulty effects limited to specific categories (e.g., human and animal sounds). Near-ceiling performance on narration further confirmed that participants remained engaged and able to process speech even in more challenging acoustic environments.

Neurally, attention effects were inconsistent with a uniform gain account and instead reflected selective changes in spectrotemporal features according to task goals. Envelope reconstruction accuracy was stronger during comprehension, consistent with prior evidence that neural envelope tracking scales with speech understanding54. Modulation Response Functions (MRFs) further revealed goal-dependent feature reweighting: comprehension selectively amplified formant modulations, consistent with their contribution to speech intelligibility34, while identification showed a relatively greater weighting of pitch modulations, diagnostic for speaker identity and voice presence. Accordingly, pitch and harmonicity cues supporting voice presence were represented in both tasks. Comprehension necessarily entails establishing voice presence, so pitch cues are expected to contribute to both tasks, although they carry proportionally greater weight during identification. Thus, the difference between tasks reflects not the presence or absence of pitch neural representation, but a shift in the relative weighting of spectrotemporal cues, with comprehension placing greater emphasis on formant structure. These findings directly relate to classical models of selective attention that contrast uniform gain mechanisms4–8 with discriminative, goal-dependent accounts9–13. Whereas gain models posit uniform amplification of all features of an attended object, discriminative models predict selective enhancement of task dimensions. The localized, goal-dependent modulation patterns observed here are inconsistent with a uniform gain mechanism and instead support goal-dependent reweighting of spectrotemporal features.

This interpretation concerns the structure of spectrotemporal modulation patterns rather than their absolute magnitude, and therefore does not rule out differences in overall response strength across tasks. While overall MRF magnitude was higher during comprehension, we interpret this as reflecting heightened cognitive engagement rather than general gain, consistent with evidence linking neural tracking to listening effort and intelligibility54–57. Because overall magnitude can reflect such global engagement effects, our statistical analyses focused on the spatial pattern of modulation sensitivity rather than on absolute response amplitude. To this end, MRFs were mean-centered prior to statistical testing so that task comparisons reflect localized changes in spectrotemporal tuning rather than global response gain. In line with this interpretation, and consistent with prior work linking task demands and listening effort to neural response strength54,57, overall neural tracking predicted MRF magnitude in our data. Noise difficulty redistributed modulation profiles without significantly altering neural tracking within tasks, suggesting that its neural effects were subtler than the behavioral changes observed and consistent with prior evidence that auditory filters retune under adverse listening conditions58.

Response‑dependent MRF analyses further connected neural representation with task performance. Correct keyword responses were associated with stronger modulations in both pitch and formant regions, reflecting more accurate spectrotemporal representation. Trials with phonological distractors showed enhanced pitch sensitivity, suggesting reliance on broad acoustic similarity (e.g., pitch contour) rather than detailed phoneme-level information. In contrast, semantic distractors, chosen more often under difficult noise, did not produce distinct acoustic neural representation, suggesting that semantic processing operates at a representational level beyond the spectrotemporal sensitivity captured here. This dissociation aligns with prior findings that semantic dissimilarity is tracked by later, distributed neural responses59. Complementary TRF analyses confirmed that comprehension engaged additional processing stages. Early components56 were observed in both tasks, with the deflection peaking near 200 ms disappearing under challenging listening noise, reflecting a disrupted mid-level processing. While TRFs are a useful tool for probing such temporal dynamics, their interpretation requires caution given methodological constraints and inter-individual variability60,61. Furthermore, it is important to note that TRFs derived from backward models, even when transformed into activation patterns62, primarily reflect the contribution of EEG patterns to stimulus reconstruction. Within this context, we observed a later negative deflection peaking near 300 ms during comprehension, comparable in timing to N300 effects reported in ERP studies, often linked to phonological expectancy and categorical priming63. Collectively, these results indicate that comprehension and identification engage processing at different temporal stages, consistent with fMRI evidence that task goals modulate cortical speech responses46,48.

Identification and comprehension impose qualitatively different goals, raising the possibility that they rely on distinct attentional strategies that evolve over time. Time analyses showed that this was not the case: goal-dependent MRF profiles remained stable throughout each trial, with identification consistently relatively emphasizing pitch and comprehension emphasizing formants. Although overall modulation sensitivity decreased gradually over the course of the narratives, the relative spectrotemporal structure associated with each task remained preserved. Consistent with this observation, the difference between tasks did not exhibit a significant temporal trend. These findings extend previous demonstrations that attention selectively modulates spectrotemporal features of speech46. However, earlier paradigms relied on short, highly controlled stimuli, Here we used continuous speech with time-resolved EEG, demonstrating that attentional filters remain stably deployed throughout extended listening and track goal-dependent differences as they accumulate over time.

This study introduces several methodological advances. First, the cortical MRF provides a direct analogue to the acoustic MPS, enabling principled comparison between spectrotemporal acoustics and neural representation. Importantly, complementary analyses using both backward feature extraction and forward encoding models yielded convergent goal-dependent effects, indicating that the observed spectrotemporal modulation patterns are robust across analytic approach and modeling direction. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that EEG reconstructions of acoustic spectrogram, despite modest absolute accuracy, can reveal interpretable, goal-dependent encoding patterns. Second, we developed a modulation-shaped noise to manipulate difficulty in a principled way. Unlike classical energetic masking, which broadly disrupts the signal through spectral or temporal overlap64, or informational masking, which introduces linguistic or perceptual competition65, our design strategically avoided regions of modulation space critical for speech. By shaping the noise in this space, we systematically varied task difficulty while preserving intelligibility, providing a more precise means of testing how noise and cognitive demands interact in shaping neural representations. Finally, using identical acoustic stimuli across tasks ensured that neural differences reflect cognitive goals rather than stimulus variation.

Nonetheless, several limitations warrant caution. Both tasks used closed-set response formats, but only identification provided participants with prior knowledge of the possible categories. This asymmetry may have influenced attentional strategies: in identification, listeners could pre-allocate attention to known targets, whereas comprehension required broader, more flexible engagement with the entire narrative. Prior knowledge is known to facilitate neural speech encoding and enhance tracking of relevant streams through expectation mechanisms66,67, and may therefore have contributed to the stronger tracking observed in comprehension. Reconstructed spectrograms also showed a bias toward slower temporal modulations (~ 1–2 Hz). This shift may partly reflect methodological factors, such as the regularization applied during TRF estimation that smooths responses and attenuates rapid fluctuations61, but it may also arise from intrinsic cortical integration, which preferentially tracks information at slower lexical and phrasal rates rather than at faster syllabic scales ( ~ 4–7 Hz)68. Such slower timescales have also been associated with higher-level speech processing during comprehension, involving integration over longer temporal windows69. In our study, this bias could further reflect task demands: extracting meaning or identifying speech under noisy conditions may naturally emphasize processing at lexical timescales.

In conclusion, our findings show that selective attention modulates cortical continuous speech representation in a goal-dependent manner, selectively enhancing the spectrotemporal features most relevant to the task: formants during comprehension and pitch during identification. Rather than applying a uniform gain, attention appears to operate as a flexible filter, reflected in goal-dependent reweighting of spectrotemporal sensitivity, consistent with a discriminative model of attentional control. A key advance of this study lies in the introduction of the Modulation Response Function (MRF), which, despite the spatial limitations of EEG, proved sensitive enough to reveal how attention reshapes spectrotemporal representations of continuous speech. Beyond speech, these results suggest that the brain sets internal objective functions dynamically within sensory domains, prioritizing features according to current goals. This perspective offers a conceptual bridge between neurophysiological data and theories of cognitive control, and can light the path for future efforts to model perception as an adaptive, goal driven process.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-two participants (19 women, 13 men; 18–30 years, μ = 20.4, σ = 2.2) took part in the study. All participants were undergraduate or graduate students and reported no hearing nor cognitive impairments. Participants provided written informed consent and received research credit for participation. The study was approved by the Homewood Institutional Review Board (HIRB). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Stimuli

Auditory stimuli were designed for use in both comprehension and identification tasks. Each stimulus consisted of four asynchronous sound samples drawn from seven predefined categories grounded in established classifications of sounds70,71: narration, slow-tempo classical music, fast-tempo electronic music, animal sounds, human non-speech sounds, nature sounds, and transport sounds. For narration, we extracted short excerpts from three audiobooks read by the same male voice (Scott Brick), characterized by deep pitch and controlled prosody. 88 passages were selected for coherence when isolated from context. Musical stimuli included 36 classical pieces at 70–85 BPM and 36 electronic tracks at 115–140 BPM, sourced from SoundCloud and the Free Music Archive, with tempo analysis performed using madmom and librosa72,73. Environmental sounds were drawn from online repositories and included 12 animals sounds (e.g., donkey, pig, hens, dog, birds), 12 non-speech human vocalizations (e.g., laughter, moans, screams, breathing, crying), 12 nature sounds (e.g., wind, sea, rain, thunder, waterfalls), and 12 transport sounds (e.g., car, train, bus, jet, helicopter). All samples were normalized, trimmed to 7–11 s, and resampled at 8 kHz, 16-bit (uncompressed, mono). Notably, all sound categories occupied distinct regions in the MPS (see Fig. S8; Supplementary Methods).

Each stimulus was 15 s long and contained four distinct categories arranged in predefined sequences with partial temporal overlap, ensuring variability in order and onset (Fig. 2). Music appeared in 40.9% of stimuli and environmental sounds in 54.5%, with 31.8% of trials containing no music and 13.6% containing no environmental sounds. When narration was included, its placement was controlled to avoid overlap with more than two simultaneous sounds and to balance its temporal position across stimuli. Since each narration contained a target keyword, its onset guided sequence selection to ensure it was neither masked nor positioned at the extremes of the trial. To smooth transitions, each sound was ramped with a 750ms fade-in/out.

Task difficulty was manipulated by embedding each stimulus in modulation-shaped noise generated with the NSL Toolbox74. The design relied on an anti-speech principle: a spectrotemporal mask was defined in modulation space (rate × scale) that excluded regions typically occupied by speech modulations. This ensured that noise energy was distributed in complementary regions of the modulation profile, thereby avoiding direct overlap with speech cues and preserving intelligibility while still providing interference. Task difficulty was varied by adjusting the size of this mask. In the easy condition, the mask was relatively small and targeted modulation regions outside those critical for speech, keeping noise energy separated from speech cues and preserving intelligibility. In the difficult condition, the mask was enlarged, extending into modulation regions that overlap with speech structure, thereby increasing interference. White noise was projected into modulation space, shaped by the mask, and then reconstructed into a time-domain signal (see Fig. S9; Supplementary Methods). The two noise types therefore differed in their degree of overlap with speech structure: the easy noise remained largely separated from speech regions, whereas the difficult noise exhibited marked overlap (Fig. 2). Parameters for mask size and reconstruction were validated in preliminary studies (see Fig. S10; Supplementary Methods). All final stimuli were mixed with the selected noise at a signal-to-noise ratio of +2 dB.

Experimental design

Each trial consisted of a 15 s stimulus scene presented in either the easy or difficult noise condition (see Stimuli). Participants completed two tasks, comprehension and identification, using the same set of stimuli, allowing a direct comparison of cognitive goals. Task order was counterbalanced across participants, yielding a 2 (task: comprehension vs. identification) × 2 (noise difficulty: easy vs. difficult) factorial design with balanced trial numbers.

In the identification task, participants indicated which of seven predefined categories were present in each scene. After each trial, all category names were displayed simultaneously, and participants could select any number of them. Participants were not informed that exactly four sources were always present, and behavioral responses confirmed that they sometimes reported more or fewer categories. To discourage reliance on narration alone, a subset of trials excluded speech, reducing scenes with narration to 81.5% while maintaining balanced distributions of music and environmental categories. For behavioral analyses, accuracy was computed for each category based on correct presence/absence judgments. For neural analyses, we focused on the subset of trials containing speech in order to isolate neural responses associated with voice processing.

In the comprehension task, participants focused on the narration and identified a target keyword at the end of each trial. Four alternatives were displayed: the correct keyword, a phonological distractor, a semantic distractor, and an analogical distractor combining both dimensions. For example, in the narration “In the dappled shadows of the tree a motionless dark green animal about the size of a baboon stood on a branch” (Fig. 2), the keyword green was paired with grown (phonological, similar in sound), black (semantic, another color term), and become (analogical, semantically related to grown and phonologically related to black). This approach follows previous use of keyword comprehension measures after short narrative excerpts52, here extended with a systematic distractor design to probe phonological and semantic influences. Full construction rules for distractor sets are provided in Supplementary Methods.

The experiment comprised eight comprehension blocks and ten identification blocks, each preceded by a practice block. In total, participants completed 88 comprehension trials and 108 identification trials. Breaks were self-paced between blocks. Testing was conducted in a sound-attenuated booth, with stimuli delivered through Etymotic ER-2 earphones. At the beginning of the session, participants were instructed to adjust the volume to a comfortable level. Stimuli were presented using OpenSesame75. Participants were instructed to minimize eye movements to reduce EEG artifacts. The entire session lasted 70–115 min per participant.

EEG acquisition and preprocessing

EEG data were recorded using a BioSemi ActiveTwo system with 64 channels at a sampling rate of 2048 Hz. Preprocessing was performed in MNE-Python76. Data were downsampled to 128 Hz and band-pass filtered between 2 and 30 Hz. Noisy channels were detected and interpolated using the PyPREP pipeline77.

Envelope and spectrogram processing

The speech envelope was extracted using a gammatone filterbank spanning 125–8000 Hz. The envelope was then computed by taking the absolute value of the filtered signal, raising it to the power of 0.6, and averaging across filter outputs. Spectrograms were generated using a cochlear gammatone filterbank with logarithmically spaced bands (125–8000 Hz, 32 filters per octave). Each band underwent half-wave rectification and smoothing, followed by logarithmic compression to enhance dynamic range. Envelopes and spectrograms were resampled to 128 Hz to match the EEG.

MPS and MRF

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the MPS was derived exclusively from the acoustic spectrograms. To compute it, we employed a two-dimensional Gabor filter bank to extract spectrotemporal modulation profiles15,78. The filter outputs captured the distribution of modulations across time and frequency. By collapsing across these axes, the resulting four-dimensional representation was reduced to a two-dimensional rate–scale profile. Positive and negative temporal rates were also averaged, yielding the overall MPS. The MRF was defined as the cortical analogue of the MPS. The same Gabor decomposition was applied not to the original spectrogram, but to spectrograms reconstructed from EEG responses. Whereas the MPS quantifies how acoustic energy is distributed in rate–scale space, the MRF quantifies how strongly the brain represents these modulations. This parallel construction allows direct comparison of spectrotemporal structure across acoustic and neural domains. To derive the MRF, we first reconstructed spectrograms from EEG signals using backward temporal response functions (TRFs). Conceptually, we speculate that reconstructing the spectrogram prior to modulation analysis may yield a representation closer to the acoustic structure available to the auditory system, given that spectrotemporal modulation reflects higher-order organization of the signal. This reconstruction step is described below.

Backward modeling and stimulus reconstruction

To link neural activity with stimulus features, we used regularized linear regression to reconstruct both the speech envelope and the spectrogram from EEG recordings. TRFs were estimated on a trial-by-trial basis for each participant and task. Reconstruction accuracy was quantified as the Pearson correlation between the reconstructed and original signal. These trial-level reconstructions formed the basis for subsequent analyses, including the derivation of cortical modulation response functions (MRFs; see above). Finally, because backward model coefficients do not directly reflect neural encoding weights, decoding filters were transformed into patterns using established procedures62. These activation patterns yield interpretable TRFs and are referred to as TRFs throughout the manuscript.

As a control for chance, each reconstructed signal was also correlated with the stimulus envelopes of all other (unrelated) trials. The average of these mismatched correlations served as a baseline. Chance accuracy was defined as the difference between the matched correlation (reconstruction vs. its true stimulus feature) and this baseline, ensuring that reported tracking reflects genuine speech–brain alignment rather than spurious correlations.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Behavioral responses were scored according to task demands. In the comprehension task, each trial was scored as 1 for the correct keyword, 0.5 for a phonological or semantic distractor, and 0 for the analogous distractor. These values {0, 0.5, 1} were converted to percentages {0, 50, 100} and averaged per participant and condition for plotting (Fig. 3). In the identification task, participants judged the presence/absence of seven sound categories. Although only four categories were present, all seven judgments were scored. Trial responses were encoded as successes out of 7 (number of correct presence/absence judgments vs. incorrect), including correct rejections. For visualization, the proportion correct (out of 7) was expressed as a percentage and averaged per participant and condition.

Both behavioral and neural measures were analyzed with (generalized) linear mixed-effects models ((G)LMMs) implemented in the lme4 package79. Behavioral trial scores were modeled with binomial GLMMs: comprehension responses were encoded as “successes out of 2” (0 → 0/2, 0.5 → 1/2, 1 → 2/2) and identification responses as “successes out of 7” (number correct vs incorrect). GLMMs were fit by maximum likelihood using the Laplace approximation. LMMs were fit by restricted maximum likelihood using. Significance was assessed with Wald χ² tests. Post hoc comparisons were conducted with estimated marginal means and corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR)80.

Differences between MRF across tasks were tested with non-parametric permutation tests using a max-statistic correction across rate/scale pairs, based on 20,000 permutations81. For difficulty noise MRF analyses, the counterbalanced trial design across participants was modeled using LMMs, with multiple comparisons corrected using Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) with 200 permutations82,83. For response-dependent MRF analyses, unequal numbers of trials across response categories precluded voxelwise trial modeling. We therefore averaged MRFs within subject and response condition, and compared the resulting subject maps using paired cluster permutation testing with sign-flip permutations across subjects (1000 permutations ; cluster threshold: t = 1.9)84. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the maximum cluster mass statistic, and analyses were restricted to the 1–4 Hz temporal rate and 0.25–4 cycles/octave spectral modulation range for computational efficiency.

To compare MRFs across tasks, we accounted for baseline differences in global magnitude. Neural tracking strength was first confirmed to covary with overall MRF amplitude (see Supplementary Note 4). To ensure that baseline shifts in global magnitude did not bias spectrotemporal comparisons between tasks, data were mean-centered within subjects and trials before statistical testing. This normalization ensured that observed differences reflected goal-dependent spectrotemporal patterns rather than baseline shifts in overall neural tracking strength.

Finally, all statistical analyses were conducted on data from 32 participants. For mixed-effects models, replicates were defined at the trial level, with trials treated as repeated observations nested within participants. Each trial contributed once per participant and task (88 comprehension trials and 108 identification trials per participant). Statistical inference for MRF analyses was performed at the participant level, including both max-statistic permutation tests and response-dependent MRF analyses, with unequal numbers of trials across conditions accounted for within participants.
