Prevalence, Age of Onset, Age at Diagnosis, and Family History of Hidradenitis Suppurativa in Pediatric Populations: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Grace Xiong, Mohamed Ali, Erfan Ghani Kakhki, Annie Xiong, Raed Alhusayen, David Croitoru, Cathryn Sibbald, Vincent Piguet, Ilya Mukovozov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionHidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition characterized by recurrent nodules and abscesses in intertriginous areas, ultimately resulting in scarring and formation of sinus tracts. HS significantly impacts quality of life and can also affect pediatric populations. We aimed to determine the prevalence, family history, age of onset, and age at diagnosis of HS in pediatric populations.MethodsA random‐effects meta‐analysis of observational studies was conducted following MOOSE guidelines. Five databases were searched through May 2024.ResultsThirty seven observational studies were included, representing 6941 patients from 13 countries. The weighted mean age was 14.7 years (range: 4–18). Ethnoracial distribution included White (36.5%), Black (38.3%), Hispanic (13.9%), Asian (0.8%), Indigenous (0.2%), Middle Eastern (0.7%), multiracial (1.2%), and unspecified populations (8.4%). Among studies reporting severity, 42.5% had Hurley Stage I, 46.3% Stage II, and 11.2% Stage III. The prevalence of HS in pediatric populations was 0.03% (95% CI [0.0001, 0.0005]), with 38% of patients having a family history of HS (95% CI [0.30, 0.45]). Pediatric‐onset HS predominantly affects females (78%, 95% CI [0.76, 0.80]). The average age of onset of HS in pediatric populations was 11.3 years (95% CI [10.15, 12.39]), but diagnosis typically occurred at 14.0 years (95% CI [12.65, 15.28]), indicating a delay in diagnosis. Female predominance of HS was associated with earlier age of onset and age at diagnosis, while no significant correlations between family history, age of onset, or age at diagnosis were identified.ConclusionThis study highlights the characteristics of pediatric‐onset HS and emphasizes the need for earlier detection to expedite diagnosis and initiation of treatment.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition that is characterized by deep‐seated nodules and abscesses, typically in intertriginous areas [1]. Although HS is typically discussed as a condition which affects adults, it can also impact pediatric populations. The etiology of HS involves both genetic and environmental factors, with a family history as a recognized risk factor [2]. The age of onset for pediatric HS ranges from prepuberty to adolescence, typically coinciding with hormonal changes that may exacerbate the condition [3]. Pediatric‐onset HS is believed to be rare, with existing prevalence estimates coming from studies in the United States (US) and Europe [2, 4]. However, the global prevalence of pediatric HS has yet to be established, and there is a lack of consensus surrounding the average age of onset, age at diagnosis, and family history of the condition.

Awareness of the characteristics of pediatric‐onset HS is crucial to clinical practice, as early diagnosis and treatment can prevent disease progression and improve prognosis. Knowledge of the genetic influences of HS can also empower patients to watch for early signs of the disease in their families. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, age of onset, age at diagnosis, and proportion of patients with a family history of HS in pediatric populations.

Methods

This review follows MOOSE guidelines and was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024550021).

Study Selection

Five databases were searched through May 2024 using keywords related to “hidradenitis suppurativa” and “pediatrics” (Table S1). Two independent reviewers screened articles, with conflicts resolved by a third. References of included studies were hand‐searched for missing articles.

Eligibility Criteria

Observational studies assessing pediatric patients (< 18 years of age) with clinically diagnosed HS were included. Survey‐based studies employing other methods of HS diagnoses were excluded. No language restrictions were applied. Studies were required to report on at least one outcome of interest (prevalence, age of onset, age at diagnosis, or family history) for inclusion.

Given the heterogeneity of studies reporting prevalence, only articles with similar methodologies were eligible. This included population‐level studies of administrative or chart data using ICD‐9 diagnostic codes.

Data Collection

Data were extracted in duplicate using a standardized form, capturing study/patient characteristics and outcomes of interest. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Quality appraisal was performed in duplicate using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklists [5].

Data Analysis

Random‐effects meta‐analysis was used to calculate pooled estimates for prevalence, age of onset, age at diagnosis, sex distribution, and proportion of patients with a family history of HS. I 2 > 50% indicated significant heterogeneity, prompting subgroup analyses. Plots were created using RStudio (v4.4.2, metafor). Potential correlation between outcomes was assessed using Pearson coefficients.

Results

Patient and Study Characteristics

After screening 1649 articles, 37 involving 6941 patients (79.7% female, mean age 14.7 years) were included (Figure 1). Studies originated from North America and Europe. Reported ethnicities included White (36.5%), Black (38.3%), Hispanic (13.9%), Asian (0.8%), Indigenous (0.2%), Middle Eastern (0.7%), and multiracial (1.2%). Disease severity spanned Hurley Stages I (42.5%), II (46.3%), and III (11.2%). Full characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Prevalence of HS in Pediatric Populations

Three US‐based studies were included in the prevalence meta‐analysis. Pooled prevalence was 0.03% [95% CI: 0.0001, 0.0005] (Figure S1).

Characteristics of Pediatric‐Onset HS

Thirty‐six studies reporting sex distribution revealed a female predominance of 78.0% [95% CI: 0.76, 0.80] (Figure S3). Among 27 studies, 38% [95% CI: 0.30, 0.45] of pediatric patients had a family history of HS (Figure S5).

The pooled mean age of onset from 13 studies was 11.3 years [95% CI: 10.15, 12.39] (Figure S7), while the mean age at diagnosis from 9 studies was 14 years [95% CI: 12.65, 15.28] (Figure S9), indicating an average diagnostic delay of approximately 3 years.

Higher female predominance of pediatric HS was associated with younger onset and age at diagnosis (p < 0.001) (Figure 2). Age of onset strongly correlated with age at diagnosis (r = 0.95). No other significant correlations were observed.

Scatter plot of the correlation between female predominance and age at onset or diagnosis of HS, showing weak association.

Subgroup analysis by study design and location (Figure 3) was conducted for age of onset, age at diagnosis, sex distribution, and family history (Table S3). No significant subgroup effects were observed (p = 0.122–0.857). Prevalence subgroup analysis was not performed due to limited studies and uniform design/location.

Subgroup analysis of proportion of pediatric HS patients with family history of HS based on study location.

Funnel plots showed minimal publication bias for sex distribution and family history, but moderate bias for age of onset, age at diagnosis, and prevalence (Figures S2, S4, S6, S8, and S10).

Quality Appraisal

Study quality varied, with 67.6% (25/38) rated as moderate or high quality (Table S2). All three prevalence studies were rated as high quality.

Discussion

This study characterizes the clinical and epidemiological features of pediatric HS. Our findings suggest that HS is less prevalent in pediatric populations than in adults (0.03% vs. 0.4%) [6]. Both groups demonstrate female predominance, with our result of 78% aligning with adult data (70%) [7]. While adult‐onset HS typically occurs in the early twenties [1], our findings show an average onset in pediatric HS of 11.3 years. Our analysis further suggests that female pediatric HS patients may experience earlier onset and diagnosis than males.

The female predominance in pediatric HS may reflect hormonal influences or sociocultural behaviors more common in girls such as shaving and tighter clothing. However, early‐onset HS in pediatric patients, even before menarche, indicates that factors outside hormones, including immune response, microbiome alterations, genetics, and comorbidities (e.g., Down syndrome) may contribute to pathogenesis [8, 9].

Interestingly, we found no significant association between family history and age of onset in pediatric HS, contrasting with adult findings [9]. This discrepancy may be due to methodological differences, as our analysis encompassed only pediatric patients (0–18 years). Within this narrower age range, genetic factors may have a less pronounced effect on age of onset, whereas their influence may become more evident when comparing across a broader age spectrum.

We also observed a shorter diagnostic delay in pediatric HS, averaging 3 years from symptom onset to diagnosis, compared to the seven‐year delay reported in adults [9]. This may reflect increased caregiver vigilance or fewer systemic barriers to accessing healthcare in children. Pediatric HS may also present more severely, especially in familial cases [9], prompting earlier evaluation. Furthermore, intertriginous acneiform lesions may raise more concern in children than adults. While mistrust of healthcare is common among HS patients of all ages [10], parental involvement may facilitate more proactive care‐seeking in pediatric cases.

Nonetheless, diagnostic delays persist and may stem from the misidentification of HS lesions as more common conditions such as folliculitis, abscesses, or acne [11], as well as limited awareness of HS among primary care providers [12] and the shortage of pediatric dermatologists [13].

Several limitations in the literature underscore the need for further research on pediatric HS and should be considered when interpreting our results. Our prevalence meta‐analysis included only three articles, selected for methodological consistency to reduce heterogeneity through predefined inclusion criteria. One excluded study by Kirsten et al. [14], which included individuals up to 19 years of age, reported a prevalence of 0.1% (60/547,112). Notably, this falls within the confidence interval of our pooled estimate, and including their data shifted our pooled prevalence estimate only slightly to 0.02%.

All included prevalence studies were also conducted in the United States, limiting insight into global differences in associated risk factors, including obesity, smoking, diet (e.g., high glycemic diets), genetic susceptibility, epigenetics, and microbiome composition. Further studies from diverse populations across Europe, Asia, Africa, and other regions are needed. Notably, incorporating the findings of Kirsten et al. may improve generalizability, as the study was conducted in Germany [14]. The three prevalence studies spanned limited timeframes and only encompassed data from 1999 to 2016, 2007, and 2014 to 2017, highlighting a gap in recent data on the epidemiology of HS in pediatric populations. This is relevant as the prevalence of HS has increased over the past decade [15], possibly due to greater disease awareness driven by advocacy and social media, and rising rates of associated metabolic disorders [16, 17].

While no significant subgroup effects were found, other factors such as variations in patient characteristics or healthcare access may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity; these were not sufficiently reported to allow for robust analysis. Variable accuracy in the identification of HS cases may have also contributed to the heterogeneity, with recent research suggesting that ICD codes alone may be insufficient given the propensity to misdiagnose HS, resulting in underestimated prevalence [18]. As such, future epidemiologic studies are encouraged to use diagnostic algorithms that better reflect the clinical criteria of HS [18].

Funnel plot asymmetry in the age of onset, diagnosis, and prevalence estimates suggests that underreporting of negative findings may have been present. Future meta‐analyses may benefit from including unpublished data to enhance the reliability of pooled estimates.

As HS is associated with significant diagnostic delay [19], with up to 50% of cases presenting during childhood or adolescence [20], our analysis may not have captured cases of pediatric‐onset HS that were later diagnosed during adulthood. Since our inclusion criteria identified cohorts with HS diagnosis occurring before 18 years of age, prevalence, age at diagnosis, and diagnostic delay are likely underestimated. This highlights a gap in the literature, where the timing and progression of pediatric‐onset HS remains poorly characterized. Furthermore, as up to 95% of pediatric HS cases occur in adolescence [3, 14], exploring adolescent‐onset HS (10–18 years) may be more clinically relevant than pediatric‐onset HS (0–18 years). Such stratification was not possible in our analysis, as studies typically aggregated patients under 18 without distinguishing between child and adolescent subgroups. Nonetheless, recognizing that HS can occur in prepubescent children is crucial to identification and reporting of such, rarer, cases [2, 21].

Although pediatric HS is relatively rare, it has devastating impacts on quality of life (QoL) [22, 23], causing greater QoL impairment than both atopic dermatitis and psoriasis. It is also associated with twice the incidence of depression and anxiety compared to the general pediatric population [23]. While the full psychosocial impacts of HS are not understood, patients frequently endorse widespread effects on self‐esteem, peer and familial relationships, and sexual development [22, 23]. Pediatric HS often co‐occurs with various inflammatory, metabolic, respiratory, musculoskeletal, genetic, and endocrine disorders, such as diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, arthritis, chronic kidney disease, chronic bronchitis, hypothyroidism, and polycystic ovary syndrome [8, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. HS may also be part of genodermatoses including pachyonychia congenita, Darier disease, Dent disease, and keratitis‐ichthyosis‐deafness syndrome, as well as syndromic HS in pediatric patients [29]. Clinicians should consider the wide range of potential comorbidities in patients with HS for comprehensive care.

Pediatric HS may be misdiagnosed as abscesses, epidermoid cysts, carbuncles, acne conglobata, or pilonidal cysts [1, 2]. Identifying hallmark features such as recurrent nodules and tunnels can aid in accurate diagnosis. Notably, around half of pediatric HS patients already have scarring at first presentation to dermatology [2]. Early intervention is crucial to prevent disease progression, chronic pain, scarring, and comorbidity development [7, 30].

In addition to greater clinician awareness of pediatric HS, addressing the social determinants of health is essential for timely diagnosis and care [31]. Access to pediatric dermatologists is lacking, particularly in rural areas, with 90%–98% of specialists based in urban settings [12, 32]. Additionally, ethnoracial disparities in pediatric HS remain underexplored, with studies suggesting higher prevalence and severity in racialized groups [21, 31]. In our meta‐analysis, only 43.2% of included studies reported ethnoracial data, highlighting the need for improved reporting and dedicated research to explore potential differences in HS among diverse populations. As HS is highly stigmatized [7], children and adolescents, particularly those navigating emotional and physical changes in puberty, may be reluctant to seek treatment [10]. Decreased care‐seeking behavior may be further pronounced in marginalized populations with cultural, historical, or personal mistrust of the healthcare system, necessitating sensitivity, confidentiality, and humility when approaching patients and their families [31].

In conclusion, this study highlights the prevalence and characteristics of HS in pediatric populations. We highlight the importance of early diagnosis and intervention to prevent disease progression and reduce psychosocial and physical sequelae. Enhancing awareness among pediatricians and primary care providers of HS's varied presentations is crucial, possibly through the incorporation of HS into residency and continuing medical education curricula. Future research should focus on characterizing HS within specific subgroups, including age‐stratified cohorts to better distinguish childhood from adolescent HS, racial/ethnic disparities, and the impact of comorbidities to support accurate diagnosis and treatment.
