Ni2‐Imido Bispincer Platforms: Cooperative Reactivity Enabled Through Substrate Addition Across a Ni2N Unit
Authors: David de los Santos, Kritika Gour, Manar M. Shoshani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTransition metal imido complexes are renowned for their capacity to undertake challenging 1,2 additions owing to the high basicity of the imido moiety. Access to transition metal imidos is often limited to the addition of organic azides to metal precursors, hindering the design of molecular constructs incorporating imido units. Bimetallic bispincer complexes, 1 and 2, are presented, which are anchored by a central imido moiety supported by pyridyl imine donors, forming highly planarized Ni2N‐aryl cores. Complex 2, which also features a Ni─Ni σ‐bond, is able to engage in cooperative substrate cleavage of main group hydrides via a formal 1,2 addition to generate paramagnetic Ni2–amido–hydrido complexes 3‐BPin and 3‐SiPh2H. The 1,2 addition products feature a geometric distortion from the planar core noted in 2, resulting in the destabilization of the Ni─Ni bond to generate a formally S = 1 state. The complexes have been characterized through x‐ray diffraction, spectroscopic studies, and DFT calculations. Catalytic hydrofunctionalization with 2 and 3‐BPin support of the 1,2 addition is an on‐cycle process in catalysis.

Introduction

The design of bimetallic transition‐metal complexes displaying ligand non‐innocence has emerged as a powerful strategy in the activation and functionalization of small molecules [1, 2]. These efforts have, in part, been motivated by the desire to emulate transformations carried out in metalloenzymes, which leverage multiple metal centers and are able to regulate proton and electron flux to facilitate cooperative substrate activation [3]. Further, in homogeneous catalysis, bimetallic intermediates are often posited as active species for in situ‐generated catalytic mixtures in coupling reactions. Toward access of the described reactivity in well‐defined systems, ligand scaffolds that enforce cooperative reactivity between metal centers as well as metals and ligands have been a prominent strategy in challenging bond cleavage and bond forming events [4].

Figure 1a features a representative set of examples wherein scaffolds have been implemented to generate dinickel complexes where the proximity of the transition metal centers can be leveraged in reactivity. For example, Meyer et al. demonstrated catalytic H/D exchange and N2 coordination using a dinickel complex coordinated to a substituted β‐diketiminato unit and bridged through a pyrazolate moiety [5, 6]. Derivatives of redox‐active pyridyl imine ligands have often been utilized in a similar vein. In addition to providing an avenue for electron storage [7, 8, 9, 10], their relative synthetic ease has been leveraged to invoke the design of unique ligands that construct novel frameworks imparting unique reactivity [11, 12]. Redox‐active binucleating ligands have been reported through functionalized N‐heterocyclic imine scaffolds, including examples from the groups of Uyeda and Tomson (Figure 1a) [9, 13, 14, 15]. Cumulatively, these frameworks have demonstrated the wide array of electron storage capabilities that have permitted stoichiometric and catalytic bond transformations such as N2 reduction and C─C coupling reactions [16].

In addition to redox, noninnocence in ligands has also been expressed through Lewis‐basic moieties built into ligand scaffolds that enable substrate coordination [17]. For example, Noyori‐type catalysts leverage ligands with neutral and anionic N‐atoms bound to Ru which enable a wide array of catalytic asymmetric hydrogenations [18, 19]. Pincer‐type ligands have also been popularized in this frontier via incorporation of a central basic unit, such as an N‐atom, to enable substrate coordination to aid in stoichiometric and catalytic reactivity [20]. Central amido groups have been utilized in pincer‐ligands [21, 22, 23], including examples by the groups of Ozerov and Mindiola who have demonstrated the amido moiety engages in substrate coordination and bond cleavage reactions via 1,2 addition of a variety of H‒X substrates (Figure 1b‐I, II) [24, 25]. Accessing bond‐cleavage reactions through 1,2 additions is particularly useful in cases where redox changes at the transition metal site are cumbersome. Highly basic imido ligands have also been leveraged in this vein to provide entry to highly challenging transformations such as C─H bond activations of methane and benzene reported by Bergman and Wolcanzski [26, 27, 28]. Studies with similarly π‐loaded imido fragments, as well as alkylidene and oxo moieties, bound to early transition metal centers provide a depth of scope for stoichiometric 1,2 additions [29, 30, 31]. Late‐transition metal nitrenoids have been extensively studied and have shown a wide breadth of C─H amination capabilities (Figure 1b‐III) [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Notably, Gao et al. have demonstrated unique hydrogen‐atom transfer reactivity using highly basic iron–imido complexes and provided an experimental measure of the imido basicity (pKa ∼ 36) [41].

A feature of the dianionic imido ligands, which is seldom leveraged in ligand scaffolds, is the application of the N‐atom as a bridging ligand for bimetallic complexes. In contrast to the bridged phenolates [42, 43], alkoxides [44], and amidos [45, 46], µ‐imido groups enforce strong multiple bonding interactions with both metals, typically resulting in short M─N bond distances [47, 48]. Thus, an imido bridge would provide value in accessing bimetallic complexes as both the imido and resultant amido, upon 1,2 addition, would be primed to serve as an anchor for bimetallic constructs. Unfortunately, synthetic access to imido ligands has generally been limited to the addition of aryl‐azides, relying on concomitant N2 loss, accordingly restricting the design of ligands that incorporate an imido fragment. Furthermore, bridged phenyl imido complexes have demonstrated 1,2 addition reactivity; though unfavorably, these systems also showcase the propensity to dissociate the ensuing amine/amide substrates upon functionalization [49]. To the best of our knowledge, a dinuclear Ti imido system is the only reported complex that utilizes a central amine to generate an imido‐bearing multidentate ligand framework (Figure 1b‐IV) [50].

Our group desired to explore methods to incorporate an imido moiety within a multidentate ligand scaffold and uncover its potential as multifunctional actors within bimetallic scaffolds. Specifically, we hypothesized that the usage of the basic imido moiety can be leveraged in 1,2 addition and subsequent reactivity can be accessed via the geometrically distorted and redox‐conserved bimetallic species (Figure 1c). Herein, we report bimetallic systems anchored by a central basic imido moiety on a multidentate ligand framework, which supports metal–metal bonding and directly showcases the utility of the central dianionic and primarily σ‐imido in cooperative substrate activation, highlighting new pathways to invoke metal–metal and metal–ligand cooperativity in catalysis.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Bimetallic Imido Bispincer Complexes

Inspiration in the design of the ligand was rooted in the desire to position two metal centers proximal to an imido moiety, primed for substrate interaction. To avoid dissociation of a resulting monoanionic amido species in deliberate reactivity, the bispincer motif, LH2 , was targeted. Our proposed scaffold builds on the well‐established precedent for pyridyl–imine frameworks to support bimetallic structures and introduces a central aniline unit poised to be doubly deprotonated and resultingly, generate a bridged imido moiety. The imido fragment not only is suited to encourage proximal metal–metal distances, but upon generation, the planarized N‐atom also serves as a Lewis basic site geared to aid in substrate activation. The binucleating proligand LH2 , was synthesized in three steps as per Figure S2.1–3, beginning with a Negishi coupling of 2,6‐dibromo tertbutyl aniline with an in situ‐generated zincated‐acetal‐functionalized pyridine. The subsequent steps include a deprotection of the acetal group followed by an imine condensation to generate the pentadentate ligand LH2 . The 1H NMR spectrum of LH2 indicates pseudo C2V symmetry and features a characteristic NH2 peak at 7.40 ppm (Figure S3.5). The solid‐state structure confirms formation of LH2 and provides informative baseline bond metrics for the uncoordinated system [51].

Our initial strategy for bimetallic imido formation was carried out through salt metathesis pathways with LH2 . Given the high basicity of imido fragments, both a precoordination and addition of base were envisioned as necessary steps to facilitate double deprotonation of the amine in order to combat competitive deprotonation of the methyl group bound to the imine carbon [52]. Addition of NiCl2(DME) and potassium‐tert‐butoxide to LH2 produced a deep blue colored solution, and 1H NMR spectroscopy reveals the formation of a single ligated transition metal species which exhibits C2V symmetry and lacks an NH resonance, supporting a structural assignment of two bound Ni centers to an imido fragment. Gratifyingly, single‐crystal x‐ray diffraction revealed the formation of cationic bispincer Ni2 imido complex 1. Complex 1 bears two Ni centers bridged by the planarized central imido and a chloride ligand, producing a diamond core structure (Scheme 1). The Ni centers are also bound to the pyridyl imine donors within the multidentate ligand scaffold. The dinickel complex bears half an equivalent of an outersphere NiCl4 2− dianion supporting an overall 32‐electron count with a Ni(II)/Ni(II) designation. Complex 1 introduces a hitherto unknown structural motif in which late transition metals are bound to an imido embedded within a multidentate ligand scaffold.

Synthesis of bispincer dinickel‐imido complexes 1 and 2.

Although exchange with non‐coordinating anions has been utilized in the displacement of select anions, attempts to displace the NiCl4 dianion using salts such as NaBPh4 and NaPF6 proved ineffective in our hands; thus, alternative synthetic pathways to generate a bimetallic dinickel imido complex were pursued. Addition of two equivalents of Ni(COD)2 to LH2 in benzene at room temperature resulted in an immediate color change from yellow to dark blue. The 1H NMR spectra displayed a series of resonances indicating the formation of several different metalated species. Notably, resonances were also observed ranging ‒20 to ‒22 ppm, suggesting the potential presence of Ni‐hydrides, presumably from N─H bond cleavage events. Isolation of a single species proved challenging; however, heating the sample at 70°C over the course of 16 h, resulted in full consumption of Ni(COD)2 and quantitative conversion to a single ligated transition metal species, which is deep emerald‐green in color. The product proved to be dinickel imido complex 2 (Scheme 1). Similar to complex 1, 1H and 13C{1H} NMR spectra demonstrate diamagnetic character and observation of a single set of resonances corresponding to the aromatic and aliphatic resonances, as well as the lack of NH resonances, suggesting the presence of a pseudo‐C 2 axis. Intriguingly, hydride resonances were no longer observed in solutions of 2, which, combined with the observation of cyclooctene formation, indicates that insertion and reductive elimination steps transpire to generate bimetallic imido complex 2.

Examination of LH2 , complex 1, and complex 2 via FT‐IR spectroscopy provided support of imido formation through the lack of NH stretches in complexes 1 and 2 while a strong stretch at ∼3400 cm−1 is observed in LH2 (Figure S4.7). These results are corroborated by the lack of NH‐based resonance in the 1H NMR spectra of complexes 1 and 2, as noted above, and thus supports complete deprotonation of the central amine. Finally, single crystal‐XRD analysis confirms that an imido moiety is generated in both complexes 1 and 2 given that the sum of the angles around the central N‐atom of 1 and 2 amount to 359.6(1) °and 360.0(1)°, respectively, indicating rigorous planarity (Figure 2). Considering that complex 2 can be considered a two‐electron reduced variant of complex 1, it is unsurprising that structural differences between the two species are observed through analysis of the solid‐state structures. For example, complex 1 bears a lengthened distance between the two Ni centers 2.873(1) Å, inconsistent with any metal‐metal bonding interaction, while complex 2 features a Ni‒Ni distance of 2.558(1) Å, which is in agreement with reported examples of crystallographically characterized Ni(I)‒Ni(I) species [53]. Resultingly, complex 2 also features a contraction of the Ni‐imido distances (Figure 2); however, the average of both Ni–imido distances (1.821(1) Å for 1 and 1.735(1) Å for 2) are in accordance with known examples of bridged metal–imido distances [54, 55, 56]. Cyclic voltammetry studies of complex 2 were undertaken in a three‐electrode cell, which consisted of a glassy carbon working electrode, a Pt wire counter electrode, and a Ag wire reference electrode. The voltammogram demonstrated two oxidation events with E1/2 of 1.03 V, 0.06 V and two reduction events with E 1/2 of −0.61 V, and −0.97 V (Figure S4.1–3). In most cases, the peak‐to‐peak separation exceeds 60 mV precluding true reversibility of the electrochemical events [57]. Notably, the two‐electron oxidation of complex 2 is chemically supported through the isolation of 1. Access to single‐component species able to demonstrate multielectron transfer capabilities have drawn considerable attention, including bimetallic Ni complexes previously reported [51].

POV‐Ray depictions of the solid‐state structure of complexes 1 and 2. H‐atoms and anion excluded for clarity. Ni2NAr cores with corresponding bond distances of one of two asymmetric units (in Å) are also shown.

Derivatives of pyridyl imine frameworks are renowned for their electron‐storage capabilities; thus, further evaluation of the electronic structure of 1 and 2 was sought. Examination of the solid‐state structures in complexes 1 and 2 outlines that the imine C═N bond elongates from an average of 1.296(2) Å in 1 to 1.314(3) Å to 2. Further, analysis of the chemical shift of the methyl resonance for LH2 , 1, and 2 showcases an increasingly upfield chemical shift, respectively (Figure S3.27). These findings support either ligand‐based reduction or enhanced backdonation to the imine π* in complex 2. To further assess the potential for ligand‐based reduction, density functional theory studies were pursued. DFT calculations were carried out at BP86‐D4/def2‐TZVP level of theory, which provided strong bond distance correlation with the crystallographically‐determined structure (See Table S6.1). A natural‐bonding orbital (NBO) analysis supports key features of 1 and 2 observed experimentally (Figure 3). The HOMO of complex 2 features Ni─Ni sigma‐bonding character, and the LUMO and LUMO(+1) correspond to ligand‐based orbitals, one that is symmetric with respect to the molecular C2 axis, and one that is antisymmetric. The HOMO(‐1) of complex 2, which is near degenerate with the HOMO, involves significant contribution from the out‐of‐plane p‐orbital of the central imido along with further ligand‐based character. NBO analysis of complex 1 outlines similar features when compared to complex 2. For example, the HOMO of 1 convincingly resembles the HOMO(‐1) of complex 2, while the LUMO and LUMO(+1) of complexes 1 and 2 share key features of the primarily ligand‐based orbitals. Crucially, the lack of occupation of the ligand‐based LUMO and LUMO(+1) orbitals for both complexes 1 and 2 provide support that the ligand is in a non‐reduced state in both complexes 1 and 2 [58]. Taken together, the diamagnetic nature of 2, the planarized central imido moiety, and the NBO analysis provide support that complex 2 is best viewed as a Ni(I)‒Ni(I) species with a metal–metal bond, the ligand non‐reduced, and the central nitrenoid in the dianionic imido state.

Cooperative Reactivity Leveraging Basic Imido Moiety in 2

We reasoned that the basicity of the imido fragment in 2 could be uniquely leveraged to facilitate a 1,2 addition of substrates over the metal imido unit to generate a bimetallic bridged hydrido amido species, which would retain the Ni(I)‒Ni(I) designation and undergo further reactivity. Supporting this interpretation, the NBO analysis demonstrates the dinickel imido core of 2 is well represented within the high‐energy valence orbitals, including the Ni─Ni sigma bond and the out‐of‐plane p‐orbital of the imido moiety. Gratifyingly, the addition of the moderately hydridic HBPin to 2 results in the formation of the corresponding dark brown 1,2 addition product 3‐BPin with the two Ni centers bridging through the ensuing amido unit and the cleaved hydride in a distorted diamond core (Figure 4a). Complex 3‐BPin was isolated in 59% yield, and single‐crystal diffraction of 3‐BPin reveals an elongation of the Ni─N distances within the central unit by approximately 0.2 Å, consistent with the weakened interaction through the now monoanionic bridge (Figure 4b). Further, a geometric distortion of the previously planarized system is observed due to the resultant tetrahedral amido moiety where the N‐atom lies 0.653(2) Å above the calculated ipso‐C─Ni─Ni mean plane in 3‐BPin (Figure 4c).

(a) Synthetic scheme for the generation of 3‐BPin. (b) POV‐Ray depictions of 3‐BPin. Most H‐atoms excluded for clarity. (c) POV‐Ray depiction of cores of complex 2 and 3‐BPin with a calculated mean plane of the two Ni centers and aryl ipso C‐atom, shown in blue.

Despite a slight contraction of the Ni─Ni distance to 2.4863(3) Å, likely due to the presence of the bridged hydride, the deviation from the rigorously trigonal planar imido unit results in a contortion of the Ni─Ni sigma bond previously observed in 2. Consequently, the 1,2 addition product exhibits paramagnetic behavior consistent with an S = 1 ground state from weakly coupling Ni(I) centers. The 1H NMR spectrum of 3‐BPin displays resonances ranging between δ −6.30 and +38.81. In the interest of extending the 1,2 addition to less hydridic substrates, diphenylsilane was exposed to complex 2, which results in the generation of a new set of paramagnetic resonances, most of which are slight perturbations of 3‐BPin, suggesting the formation of 3‐SiPh2H (Figure S3.35). More specifically, resonances are observed between δ −5.71 and +138.91 for 3‐SiPh2H, where the single shifted resonance at δ 138.91 likely corresponds to the Si─H, given the proximity to the unpaired electron density, vide infra, and the relative integration. Despite the generation of 3‐SiPh2H in situ, bulk isolation proved to be prohibitive as decomposition was observed upon workup. DFT calculations of the singlet and triplet states of 3‐BPin and 3‐SiPh2H support a triplet ground state with a singlet state approximately 2.7 and 1.9 kcal/mol higher in energy, respectively. Additionally, the solid‐state structure of 3‐BPin displays strong agreement with the DFT‐optimized triplet structure, which also exhibits consistency with the DFT‐optimized structure of 3‐SiPh2H. Evans' method of 3‐BPin elucidated an effective magnetic moment (µeff) of 3.18 Bohr magnetons, consistent with an S = 1 system. Related work by Adhikari et al. demonstrated relevant precedent through a dinickel system with diradical character undergoing monomer/dimer equilibria [25]. Comparison of the Wiberg Bond Index between the two Ni centers in 2 (0.37) to 3‐BPin (0.13) and 3‐SiPh2H (0.13) provides further support for the scission of the Ni─Ni bond in 2. Spin density calculations for both S = 1 species demonstrate that the d‐orbitals of the Ni centers are the largest contributors of unpaired spin (34% and 28%), which show no evidence of overlap (Figure 5). The remaining spin density is mostly localized to the N‐donors of the pyridyl imine moieties. Notably, metrical parameters of the bound ligand in 3‐BPin and 3‐SiPh2H do not support substantial access of the canonical reduced states of the pyridyl imine framework.

The ability to access 1,2 addition products within our dinickel imido system intrigued us to investigate catalytic studies in order to discover whether hydrofunctionalization proceeds via 1,2 addition from 2. Indeed 1,2 addition pathways have proved valuable in catalytic hydrosilylation by Ru‐thiolate systems [59, 60, 61]. A recent example also demonstrates access to Ni‐based catalytic hydroboration of N‐heterocycles through a Ni‐phosphinophenolate system, which generates a catalytically active Ni‐hydrido species bearing an unbound phenoxyborane substituent upon addition of hydridoboranes [62]. Relatedly, Ni‐imido moieties, generated via addition of azides, have been shown to carry out C─H amination via 1,2 addition‐associated mechanisms [39, 63]. We found evidence that complex 2 is able to facilitate catalytic hydrofunctionalization for unsaturated carbon–oxygen and carbon–nitrogen bonds under mild conditions with either HBpin or Ph2SiH2 (Table S5.1). In catalytic trials with 2, complexes 3‐BPin and 3‐SiPh2H are observed in solution in the hydroboration and hydrosilylation trials, respectively. Finally, examination of time‐dependent hydrofunctionalization studies of C6H5CN and CH3CN displays similar consumption over time of benzonitrile (Figure S5.7) and even elevated consumption of acetonitrile when using 3‐BPin as the catalyst (Figure S5.10), supporting that the 1,2 addition to generate 3‐BPin is an on‐cycle process. Catalysis observed here provides complementary pathways by exploiting a 1,2 insertion over a supported M2N unit as an elementary step in catalysis.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed bis‐pincer complexes bearing a central basic imido fragment capable of invoking cooperative substrate binding and bond cleavage events. The dinickel imido complexes (1 and 2) feature highly planarized Ni2N cores with each Ni center in a distorted square planar geometry. Reduced complex 2, features a combination of a strained Ni(I)‒Ni(I) σ‐bond and a highly basic central imido moiety within the Ni2N core, which results in strong representation of the core in the occupied high‐energy valence bonding orbitals. Resultingly, complex 2 facilitates 1,2 addition of borane and silane reagents, resulting in the S = 1 products, 3‐BPin and 3‐SiPh2H, which bear the ensuing functionalized central amido and a hydride as bridging ligands. The paramagnetic nature stems from the contortion of the square plane in 2, resulting in net diminished orbital overlap of the Ni(I) centers. Finally, experimental evidence demonstrated that catalytic pathways proceed via 1,2 addition. This work demonstrates the first example of late transition metal complexes bound to an imido fragment embedded within a multidentate ligand scaffold and reveals the distinctive metal‐ligand cooperative reactivity enabled by this framework. Continued efforts in leveraging basic imido moieties within constructed ligand frameworks to unlock new avenues of reactivity are underway.
