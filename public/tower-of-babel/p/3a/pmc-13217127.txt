Impact of artificial intelligence on the availability, accessibility, acceptability and quality of ophthalmic disease screening services: a scoping review
Authors: Senlin Lin, Juzhao Zhang, Catherine Jan, Liping Li, Yajun Peng, Dan Qian, Yao Yin, Mengjia Zhang, Jianxiu Feng, Mingguang He, Haidong Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis scoping review examines the existing literature on the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in screening for eye diseases, with a focus on evaluating whether AI-assisted diagnostic technologies enhance the availability, accessibility, acceptability and quality of screening services. 42 original studies were selected for in-depth analysis, including those employing health economic evaluations. Methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, with the majority of studies demonstrating high quality—24 scored 5/5, 15 scored 4/5 and the rest scored 3/5. Among the included studies, 34 compared manual screening with either AI-assisted or fully AI-driven approaches. Availability was the most frequently studied aspect (28 studies), followed by acceptability (12 studies), whereas accessibility and service quality were less commonly addressed. Overall, AI shows significant potential to improve the cost-effectiveness of eye care services and enhance patient access, particularly in remote or underserved regions. It was also well-accepted by patients, with high satisfaction and improved referral compliance. The findings suggest that AI holds promise for advancing eye disease screening, although large-scale, long-term trials are needed to effectively integrate AI into the reconstruction of screening processes and the reshaping of eye health service systems.Keywords: public health, telemedicine

Introduction

Blindness is one of the most feared medical conditions—second only to cancer and AIDS—and vision is considered the most important of all the senses.1 Ophthalmic diseases are a wide range of pathologies affecting the eye and associated tissues, including blinding eye diseases that can cause permanent vision loss. Recent trends in disease incidence have shown a shift from infectious to non-infectious aetiologies, with cataracts, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, macular degeneration and pathological myopia being the most common causes.2 3 These conditions are largely associated with metabolic and age-related factors. Early detection of these eye diseases is crucial for improving patient outcomes,4,6 as they are associated with avoidable visual impairment. However, the quality of care for these eye diseases requires improvement, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where staffing, financial and time constraints can hinder the implementation and sustainability of large-scale population-based screening programmes.7 8 Addressing these challenges is imperative to prevent avoidable visual impairment and improve the overall quality of ophthalmic care in these populations.

The advent of digital technologies has presented new opportunities for the healthcare industry, offering potential solutions to some of its most pressing challenges.9 10 In eye care, where imaging examinations play a crucial role in diagnostic support, artificial intelligence (AI) and telemedicine hold significant promise.11,13 Compared with conventional face-to-face or non-screening methods, telemedicine-based screening for eye diseases has demonstrated high efficacy and is becoming the primary mode of community-based eye disease screening.14,16 Additionally, the growing interest in AI technology has propelled its increased use in the screening and diagnosis of eye diseases.17,19 AI technology can rapidly and accurately analyse vast amounts of data from eye examinations, identifying abnormal conditions that may indicate eye diseases with high sensitivity and specificity.20,22 Despite the need for trained personnel to oversee the process and the fact that performance is not always perfect, and this can lead to missed diagnosis,23 AI-based screening is advantageous in situations requiring rapid image interpretation or where there is a shortage of primary care providers.24,26 Recent research suggests that AI-based telemedicine screening is cost-effective and accessible, enabling the wider and timelier detection of eye diseases.27,30 Nevertheless, the current state of AI technology for eye disease screening globally, and its impact on screening services, remains uncertain. As such, it is imperative to comprehend the potential of AI as a tool for improving eye disease screening services, and exploring the need and feasibility of related process restructuring and health system reconstruction.

Therefore, this scoping review aims to identify existing literature on the use of AI in eye diseases screening and to clarify whether the global implementation of AI-assisted diagnostic technologies in eye disease screening improves the availability, accessibility, acceptability and quality of screening services.

Method

Protocol and registration

We conducted a scoping review based on the framework of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines to systematically summarise the status and impact of the use of AI in ocular screening.31

Search strategy and selection criteria

We included original studies reporting the use of AI technologies for eye disease screening, regardless of study design or the type of AI technology used. Studies that applied health economics methods to analyse the benefits of using AI technologies for eye screening were also included. We excluded studies that did not meet the basic criteria and studies that did not report the impact of AI-based screening on the availability, accessibility, acceptability or quality of services. We also excluded studies that were not part of common blinding eye diseases to make the article more focused and informative. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in table 1.

We conducted a comprehensive systematic search of multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and MEDLINE, to identify relevant literature up to March 2025. In addition, we performed a grey literature search that included medRxiv and bioRxiv.

Selection process

The selection of studies was conducted in a three-stage process: in the first stage, all five reviewers reviewed the same 100 articles to ensure consistency in article selection and refined the selection criteria and data extraction procedures. In the second stage, three reviewers divided the workload and independently screened the titles and abstracts against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. If it was unclear from the titles and abstracts whether a study met the inclusion criteria or not, or if the reviewers were not sufficiently confident in their screening decisions, the study was retained. In the third stage, two reviewers reviewed the literature obtained from the previous step and performed a full-text reading and data extraction. Differences in literature screening and data extraction were resolved through consensus and, if necessary, discussion with all authors.

Data charting

Data extraction was conducted using a predesigned template, which was carefully constructed by all authors through a comprehensive discussion. This template comprised several key components, including author, year of publication, study purpose, type of eye disease, application and main results. To ensure data quality, two independent reviewers performed initial data extraction of the three selected articles and then discussed the results to establish a consensus on the data extraction template. Following this, two reviewers then independently collated the content of each eligible article, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or further adjudication by a third reviewer. All collated data were meticulously recorded using Microsoft Excel to facilitate data management and analysis.

We used descriptive statistics and framework synthesis to present a comprehensive summary of our findings. Framework synthesis is a well-established analytical method in healthcare policy research that offers a structured analysis involving five primary stages: problem familiarity, framework selection, indexing, charting and interpretation.32 This adaptable approach accommodates the diversity of study types, including quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods, and is particularly suited to cases where the theory in the field is nascent and underdeveloped, as is the case in this scoping review. The studies are classified by country and type of eye disease, with an overview provided of the forms, objectives and target populations of AI technologies within each category. We analysed the factors that contribute to improvement in cases where improvement has been observed and summarised the underlying drivers. In cases where there is no improvement, we investigated the associated challenges.

Critical appraisal

Two investigators independently evaluated the methodological quality of the studies using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT),33 with discrepancies being resolved by the senior investigator. The MMAT assesses studies based on five questions related to the sampling strategy, outcome measurement, confounders and statistical analysis. Studies were scored on a 5-point scale according to the MMAT criteria.

Results

Study contexts and characteristics

After conducting searches of electronic databases, grey literature and reference lists, we identified 23 449 titles and abstracts. Following the removal of duplicates, a preliminary review using inclusion and exclusion criteria excluded 21 943 articles as they did not meet at least one of the four criteria. To confirm that the included studies focused on the practical application of AI in eye disease screening rather than model development, we conducted a further in-depth review and ultimately identified 42 eligible studies for comprehensive analysis in this scoping review. The final selection of 42 papers is in English and covers a diverse range of study designs (online supplemental table 1). Multiple publications from the same author were included, as long as they discussed the phenomenon of interest among different populations. Most studies demonstrated good methodological quality, with 24 studies scoring 5/5, 15 studies scoring 4/5 and the remaining studies scoring 3/5. Nearly half of the studies were carried out in low- and middle-income countries (n=19), including China (n=14), Thailand (n=2), Rwanda (n=2) and Brazil (n=1). The other half of the studies (n=22) were conducted in high-income countries, including the USA (n=12), Australia (n=4), Singapore (n=1), New Zealand (n=1), the UK (n=1), Japan (n=1), Germany (n=1) and Canada (n=1). One study did not specify the country of research.

Most of the included studies (n=33) compared the performance of manual eye disease screening with that of AI-assisted or AI-replaced screening, with only three studies investigating the attitudes of both patients and physicians towards using AI for screening diabetic retinopathy. Availability was the most investigated aspect among the studies (n=28), with discussions on whether AI can improve screening accuracy or cost-effectiveness. Acceptability was the focus of a subset of studies (n=12), exploring the acceptance of AI software by both patients and physicians, as well as patient compliance with referral recommendations. Access to screening services was less frequently studied, with only two articles mentioning the potential of AI to help reach more individuals in need of eye screening. Similarly, most studies (n=34) did not address service quality-related issues. Online supplemental table 2 presents the specific information analysed in the included studies.

Population, eye disease and practical application form

Although many studies in ophthalmology focus on the development of new AI models with little practical application, 31 studies have reported the use of AI in actual clinical scenarios, including health economic studies using real screening projects or clinical data to set relevant parameters.2729 30 34,60 The number of studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries (n=14) and high-income countries (n=17) is roughly equal. All studies were published in the past 7 years (2019–2025).

In high-income countries, AI-based eye disease screening applications are predominantly focused on diabetic retinopathy. In contrast, while diabetic retinopathy remains a central focus in low- and middle-income countries, their AI-driven screening systems also encompass additional ocular conditions, including age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma, pathological myopia and cataracts. The majority of the target population are adults without pre-existing diagnosed eye conditions, with only two studies focusing on screening for premature infants with retinopathy of prematurity61 and congenital cataracts,45 respectively. Furthermore, although most existing studies concentrate on single ocular diseases, an increasing number of recent investigations have begun to address multiple eye diseases simultaneously.28 46 48 56 60

Among the studies with practical applications, the most common use of AI software is for immediate diagnosis or assisted diagnosis in opportunistic screening scenarios in primary care or outpatient clinics, with colour fundus images being the core examination data.

Availability

The use of AI-assisted diagnostic technology in the screening of eye diseases has shown promising results in terms of accuracy, screening efficiency and cost-effectiveness. Studies conducted in high-income countries have reported improvements in availability, particularly in cost-effectiveness3037 50 61,64 and efficiency.60 Compared with not screening, AI technology can achieve fully or semi-automated screening and provide real-time results and referral recommendations, effectively reducing labour costs and patient waiting times. Notably, while AI software demonstrates significantly greater time-efficiency in interpreting fundus images, its accuracy often remains comparable to or lower than that of ophthalmologists. It is important to note that improvements in cost-effectiveness do not necessarily translate to efficiency gains; they may instead arise from reductions in cost.29 36 As such, the availability of AI-assisted diagnosis is still controversial.

In low- and middle-income countries, 11 articles have reported similar positive results regarding the use of AI-assisted diagnostic technology in eye disease screening.28 39 46 48 53 55 59 65 However, there have been some exceptions. For instance, a study conducted in Brazil found that applying AI to assist clinical doctors in diagnosing diabetic retinopathy was not highly cost-effective.66 Another study found that the accuracy of CC-Cruiser, an AI system, in diagnosing and treating cataracts is significantly lower than that of senior ophthalmic experts.45 Several prior studies have reported inconclusive findings, indicating that improvements in cost-effectiveness may be conditional, and AI-assisted diagnostic technology can enhance the accessibility of eye disease screening only when a specific proportion of patients adhere to referral recommendations for follow-up treatment.27 67 On the other hand, Lin et al demonstrated that deploying AI for screening multiple ocular diseases simultaneously—rather than single-condition screening—yielded robust cost-effectiveness, particularly when AI integration facilitated a systemic redesign of the entire screening service workflow.55

Accessibility

The scope of population coverage by screening services represents a critical factor influencing the benefits of screening in disease detection. Despite limited research in this domain, two studies from low- and middle-income countries and four from high-income countries have documented the impact of AI-assisted diagnostic technologies on the accessibility of eye disease screening. The implementation of AI-based diagnostic and follow-up systems in rural regions lacking pre-existing eye screening services has been shown to significantly reduce the incidence of patients progressing to glaucoma, yielding population-level health benefits.40 Additionally, augmenting manual workflows with AI technologies can increase the annual patient caseload per ophthalmologist by severalfold compared with traditional models, thereby improving the ophthalmologist-to-population service ratio.41 52 54 58 Notably, these investigations also observed that AI integration reduces disparities in eye health screening uptake across ethnic groups, thereby enhancing service equity.51

Acceptability

The acceptability of AI-assisted diagnostic technology in eye disease screening encompasses factors such as physician acceptance, screening candidates’ satisfaction and patient compliance. Seven studies conducted in high-income countries such as Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Germany and the USA reported high patient satisfaction with AI-assisted eye disease screening, with better compliance with referral advice provided by AI and significantly increased long-term follow-up rates.35 37 38 42 43 47 56 In a randomised controlled trial conducted in Rwanda,34 compared with waiting 3–5 days after initial screening for manual grading and remote referral advice (control group), the use of AI technology for real-time feedback (intervention group) significantly improved population compliance with referral advice. However, it is important to note that while AI can reduce the workload of doctors and improve efficiency, it does not directly benefit every screened patient. Therefore, patients may have lower acceptability of AI compared with doctors and prefer to receive direct advice from doctors in eye disease screening services.27

Quality of services

The evaluation of service quality of AI-assisted diagnostic technology for eye disease screening involves measuring both patient satisfaction and screening effectiveness. Eight articles have reported the positive impacts of AI on enhancing screening service quality. Automated screening models received high satisfaction ratings from the majority of patients, likely attributed to the provision of rapid feedback and minimised waiting times.45 Challenges in retinopathy screening within endocrinology outpatient settings have been documented, where up to 40% of patients could not be recontacted after initial screening and 11% failed to review their eye examination results. Notably, AI-integrated screening processes enabled all patients to immediately access and review their examination reports,37 while also significantly improving referral rates.47 49 52 58 In contrast, manual result interpretation can lead to feedback delays that reduce the effectiveness of screening. By minimising these delays, AI technology enhances overall screening efficacy.

Discussion

AI is increasingly used for ophthalmic disease screening. Many studies have reported using AI in eye care scenarios, with diabetic retinopathy being the focus in high-income countries and multiple eye diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, macular degeneration, glaucoma, myopia and cataracts in low- and middle-income countries.45 61

AI-assisted diagnostic technology has demonstrated significant potential in improving the cost-effectiveness of healthcare services. By providing faster and more accurate diagnoses, AI can reduce the need for repeat tests and consultations, ultimately leading to cost savings. In high-income countries, the application of this technology has yielded improvements in cost-effectiveness and availability, particularly in reducing labour costs and patient waiting times, as evidenced in numerous studies.30 37 61 Similarly, low- and middle-income countries have also shown positive results in the adoption of this technology. For instance, population-based glaucoma screening has been found to be cost-effective in China and India,68 69 which together account for approximately 40% of the global population suffering from glaucoma.70 However, several studies have reported uncertain results, suggesting that the improvement in cost-effectiveness may be contingent on several factors, and specifically, the technology can only enhance the availability of eye disease screening if a certain proportion of patients can adhere to referral recommendations for further treatment.28 39 46 65

AI-assisted diagnostic technology holds significant promise for enhancing patient accessibility to healthcare services, particularly for those in remote or underserved areas.40 AI algorithms enable precise and rapid ophthalmic disease screening, minimising the need for patients to travel to specialised clinics or endure extended waiting periods to obtain a diagnosis. Furthermore, the utilisation of AI-based diagnostic technology can decrease the workload of healthcare professionals, enabling them to concentrate on more complex cases and improving healthcare efficiency overall. This can ultimately lead to better health outcomes for patients and more effective use of healthcare resources. Nevertheless, current research on coverage rates is limited, necessitating further investigation in this domain.

AI-assisted diagnostic technology for eye disease screening is generally well-accepted, with high patient satisfaction and improved compliance with referral advice.34 AI can significantly reduce delays in providing feedback, enabling healthcare providers to deliver prompt and effective interventions, thereby enhancing patient outcomes. However, patients may prefer direct advice from a healthcare provider.27 As such, it is crucial to strike a balance between using AI technology and incorporating human involvement in the diagnostic process.

The limitations and future directions of existing research on AI-assisted eye disease screening include the need for more empirical studies, particularly large-scale and long-term trials, exploration of standardised research methods and evaluation criteria and a deeper focus on the reconstruction of screening service processes and the reshaping of the eye health service system.

Our study has several limitations. First, we only searched for English-language literature, which may have excluded relevant articles published in other languages, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Second, we did not investigate legal and regulatory differences related to AI applications in healthcare services in different regions, which could be an area of exploration for future research.

Conclusion

Our review revealed the significant impact of AI technology on eye disease screening services. While numerous studies have demonstrated the positive effects of AI application in this field, the extent of digital transformation in eye health services remains restricted, and there is a dearth of evidence supporting the process reconstruction and health system restructuring involving AI. As such, there is a pressing need for further systematic research to comprehensively understand the real-world impact of AI on eye disease screening services and to explore strategies to optimise this technology’s potential.
