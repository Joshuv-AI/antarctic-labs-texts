A targeted epigenetic clock for simultaneous assessment of biological aging and cancer-associated methylation drift
Authors: Feng Liu, Haohao Li, Tianzhe Chen, Xiaoliang Chen, Bo Hong, Hongzhi Wang, Yeping Tu, Jinfu Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a key tool for assessing aging, DNA methylation clocks are mostly constructed based on European and American populations and rely on the high-cost Infinium MethylationEPIC microarray. These factors limit their widespread application in the Chinese population.MethodsThis study included two independent cohorts: the Fuyang cohort (n = 610, age range 2–89 years) for model development, and the Hefei cohort (n = 188, age range 15–86 years) for external validation. Using a multiplex PCR-based targeted capture sequencing technology, we examined 74 CpG sites located in seven age-associated genes, including ELOVL2, FHL2, C1orf132, KLF14, TRIM59, CCDC102B and PDE4C, to construct a DNA methylation-based age prediction model suitable for the Chinese population.ResultsThe performance of our model was consistent with that reported in the literature for this field. In the Fuyang cohort, the training set achieved R2=0.95, MAE = 3.39 years; the test set achieved R2 = 0.93, MAE = 4.04 years. External validation yielded R2 = 0.89, MAE = 4.11 years. Compared with age-prediction models based on biochemical markers, our methylation clock demonstrated superior performance. Further analysis revealed non-linear aging rates and sex-specific differences. In addition, blood samples from cancer patients displayed DNA methylation patterns distinct from those associated with physiological aging, suggesting that they are associated with epigenetic alterations that lead to an overestimation of DNA methylation age.ConclusionThis study developed a cost-effective, targeted methylation clock for the full age spectrum in the Chinese population. Its strong response to both physiological aging and tumor status underscores its potential for large-scale aging assessment and for translational applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02106-z.

Introduction

Aging is a complex biological process characterized by a gradual decline in physiological functions and a markedly increased susceptibility to age-related diseases such as cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancer [1–3]. Chronological age essentially measures the passage of time rather than biological status. In reality, human aging is shaped by the combined influence of genetic factors, epigenetic regulation, and environmental exposures [4–6].

Among the various hallmarks [7, 8], DNA methylation (DNAm)—one of the most studied epigenetic modifications—exhibits highly consistent and predictable patterns during aging. DNAm reflects the integrated effects of genetic background, environmental exposures, and lifestyle factors [9, 10]. The development of DNA methylation clocks has undergone substantial evolution: first-generation models such as the Horvath pan-tissue clock [5] and the Hannum blood-based clock [11] primarily predicted chronological age; second-generation clocks such as PhenoAge [12] further incorporated physiological biomarkers; and third-generation models such as GrimAge [13] enabled prediction of mortality risk and susceptibility to age-related diseases, thus providing powerful tools for aging research and clinical evaluation.

Currently, there is a notable scarcity of aging clocks specifically developed for Chinese populations. Jiaming Li et al. developed an integrative aging clock for Chinese women using multi-omics data, but the sample size was small (n = 113) and limited to females, and the complex computational requirements restricted clinical translation [14]. Zikai Zheng et al. developed the iCAS-DNAmAge clock for the Chinese population, with validation in the Quzhou cohort (R = 0.97, MAE = 3.45) and CAS cohort (R = 0.77, MAE = 4.37) [15]. However, this clock relied on the Infinium MethylationEPIC array, which is costly and difficult to scale for large-scale population screening.

Pyrosequencing is widely regarded as the gold standard for measuring DNA methylation levels, but it has low throughput and cannot accurately quantify methylation levels below 5% [16]. Freire-Aradas et al. compared the accuracy of four technologies—EpiTYPER®, pyrosequencing, high-throughput sequencing, and SNaPshot™—and found that high-throughput sequencing achieved the best performance in the test set, with the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) and the highest correct prediction rate [17]. Moreover, high-throughput sequencing not only enables the simultaneous measurement of a large number of methylation sites but also offers exceptionally high detection sensitivity [18].

Therefore, in this study, we developed a DNA methylation clock tailored to the Chinese population using a multiplex PCR-based targeted capture sequencing platform. By optimizing and selecting CpG sites specific to this population and establishing a highly cost-effective detection system, we aim to maintain prediction accuracy while significantly improving efficiency and affordability. This strategy could provide strong technical support for community-level aging screening and early intervention.

Methods

Sample collection

Two cohorts were included in this study: the Fuyang cohort (Fuyang City, China) for screening age-associated CpG sites and constructing the prediction model, and the Hefei cohort (Hefei City, China) for external validation. All participants provided informed consent. The combined age range across participants was 2–89 years. Individuals included were generally healthy, with no major diseases (e.g., cancer, immune disorders), no recent acute infections, and not pregnant. For all participants, 2 mL of venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes, and samples not processed immediately were stored at 4 °C.

Primer information, DNA extraction and library construction

Seven age-associated hotspot genes, including ELOVL2, FHL2, KLF14, TRIM59, CCDC102B, PDE4C and C1orf132 [19–24], were initially selected for this study (Supplementary Table 1). During primer design, universal sequences were added to the 5’ ends of both forward and reverse primers for all seven genes (Supplementary methods 1). Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 µL of whole blood using the Blood Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, China). A total of 100 ng of DNA was bisulfite-converted using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, USA). A multiplex PCR strategy was used to simultaneously capture and sequence the target regions of seven genes in a single reaction system. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA) using a 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing strategy. Detailed information is provided in Supplementary methods 2 and 3.

Bioinformatic analysis

A standardized pipeline was used to analyze DNA methylation data. Raw FASTQ files were processed with TrimGalore (v0.6.7) to remove adapters and filter low-quality reads. Reads were aligned to the reference genome using Bismark (v0.24.2). The DEswan tool [25] was used to analyze dynamic methylation changes. Detailed information is provided in Supplementary methods 4 and 5.

Biochemical data collection

In addition to DNA methylation detection, a total of 54 biochemical indicators were collected from a subset of study participants. These included 23 complete blood count parameters, 12 liver function markers, 3 kidney function markers, 4 lipid profile indices, 2 blood glucose parameters, 3 myocardial enzymes, 3 rheumatology-related markers, 3 gastrointestinal function markers, and 1 homocysteine measurement.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R 4.4.2. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables (p > 0.05) are summarized as mean ± SD and compared by t test; non-normal data were reported as median (IQR) and compared by Mann–Whitney U test.

The dataset was randomly split into training and validation sets (7:3). Key variables were selected and prediction models were constructed using regularized regression (Lasso α = 1, Ridge α = 0, Elastic Net α = 0.5) with 10-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning (λ).

Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s test. All tests were two-sided; statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Study design and participants

To investigate the correlation between methylation levels of the seven genes and age, an initial cohort of 60 healthy individuals was enrolled (male: female = 33:27), including groups aged 20–25 years (n = 20), 40–45 years (n = 20), and 60–65 years (n = 20). Subsequently, 610 samples from the Fuyang cohort (aged 2 ~ 89 years) were included for screening age-associated CpG sites. The dataset was randomly divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio, and DNA methylation clock models were constructed using three algorithms: Elastic Net, LASSO regression, and Ridge regression. Finally, an independent Hefei cohort (n = 188, aged 15 ~ 86 years) was used as an external validation set for evaluation (Fig. 1).

Construction of DNA methylation clock models

A total of 610 healthy individuals from the Fuyang cohort were included to construct the methylation-based aging model. The overall mean age was 45.45 ± 20.71 years (range: 2–89 years). Among them, 369 were males (mean age 43.46 ± 19.67) and 241 were females (mean age 48.49 ± 21.90).

A total of 74 CpG sites with absolute correlation coefficients |r| ≥ 0.2 were selected. Lasso regression, Ridge regression, and Elastic Net were used for feature selection and model construction: (1) Lasso and Elastic Net both selected 48 identical CpG sites, though with different coefficients; (2) Ridge retained all 74 CpG sites. Detailed information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Model performance in the training set showed similar predictive accuracy (Fig. 3B): Lasso (MAE = 3.38), Elastic Net (MAE = 3.39), Ridge (MAE = 3.49); with an R2 of 0.95 for each. In the testing set (Fig. 3C), all models maintained high predictive consistency with an R2 of 0.93. Among them, Elastic Net achieved the lowest MAE (4.04), followed by Lasso (MAE = 4.05) and Ridge (MAE = 4.08).

Model validation

For external validation, the model was tested on 188 healthy individuals from the Hefei cohort (overall mean age: 45.79 ± 16.22 years, range 15–86; 101 males, 87 females). All three models maintained excellent predictive performance (Fig. 3E), with Elastic Net (MAE = 4.11, R2 = 0.89) and Lasso (MAE = 4.12, R2 = 0.89) slightly outperforming Ridge (MAE = 4.17, R2 = 0.88). These results confirm the models’ strong accuracy and generalizability. (See Supplementary Table 3 for details).

Using Generalized Additive Models (GAM), we found that ELOVL2, KLF14, TRIM59, PDE4C showed relatively stable age-related methylation trajectories in both sexes, C1orf132 exhibited a consistent age-related decline in both males and females. CCDC102B and FHL2 displayed marked sexual dimorphism, FHL2 methylation declined with age in males but remained stable in females. For CCDC102B, its methylation level in males declined steeply before age 60 and then plateaued; in females, it dropped around age 40, rose to a small peak at ~ 60, and declined again thereafter (Fig. 3G and H).

Analysis in tumor cohorts

We investigated the impact of tumors on DNA methylation-based age prediction. Baseline information of tumor patients is summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Analysis of mean methylation levels showed that in leukemia patients, CpG site methylation levels were markedly altered, with six genes exhibiting levels significantly higher or lower than those observed in healthy individuals or solid tumor patients. In contrast, the methylation levels of all genes in solid tumor patients remained within the fluctuation range of healthy individuals (Fig. 5A).

The Elastic Net model produced an MAE of 34.38 years for leukemia patients, in stark contrast to the much lower errors observed across solid tumors (overall MAE: 3.88 years), specifically: lung cancer (4.82), gastric cancer (4.32), colorectal cancer (3.92), breast cancer (3.72), and liver cancer (2.47) (Fig. 5B).

Analysis of prediction bias revealed that predicted ages exceeded chronological ages in all leukemia patients, with a median bias of 37.60 years (IQR: 19.34, 41.81). In solid tumor patients, the overall median bias was notably lower at 3.19 years (IQR: −0.08, 4.93). When stratified by tumor type, the median biases were as follows: gastric cancer, 5.06 (2.47, 5.95) years; colorectal cancer, 4.61 (−0.08, 4.90); lung cancer, 4.04 (2.60, 5.93); liver cancer, 2.10 (−0.34, 3.19); and breast cancer, 1.51 (−1.29, 3.06).

For age-matched healthy individuals, the median prediction bias was − 0.99 (−3.61, 3.16) years. Comparison between groups indicated that prediction bias differed significantly across populations (P < 0.001), demonstrating that tumor patients—particularly those with leukemia—tend to have markedly overestimated epigenetic ages compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 5C–E).

Discussion

Current DNA methylation clocks still face significant limitations in clinical application and population-wide promotion. First, mainstream models were primarily developed based on European and American populations, and their applicability to the Chinese population may be constrained by ethnicity-specific methylation profiles [26, 27]. Second, the Illumina EPIC microarray is costly and technically complex [28], making it difficult to implement in large-scale population screening in China. To overcome these limitations, we developed a cost-effective, multiplex PCR-based DNA methylation clock for the Chinese population, which is simple to operate. The performance of our model is consistent with that reported in the literature for this field. Specifically, the Elastic Net model achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 3.39 years (R2 = 0.95) on the training set and 4.04 years (R2 = 0.93) on the test set of the Fuyang cohort. This level of accuracy was maintained in the independent Hefei cohort, with an MAE of 4.11 years (R2 = 0.89). The performance metrics are comparable to those of established epigenetic clocks, such as the Hannum clock (MAE = 3.90 years) [11] and the iCAS-DNAmAge clock (MAE = 4.37 years) [15], thereby validating the high accuracy of the proposed method.

We also demonstrated that finger-prick blood samples produced highly consistent age predictions compared with EDTA venous blood (Supplementary Text 1).

Human aging rates are not constant, as observed in transcriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics, or microbiomics [14, 29]. DNA methylation changes are also non-linear; some genes exhibit altered methylation rates during aging. Zikai Zheng et al. reported methylation peaks at ages 35 and 55 in both males and females [15]. In our study, males exhibited a single peak at ~ 36 years, while females had two peaks at ~ 24 and ~ 46 years. These differences may relate to sex hormones and social stress. The male peak (~ 36 years) likely represents a physiological turning point where basal metabolic rate begins to decline and muscle mass and physical performance start gradually decrease [30], coupled with cumulative social stress. The female dual peaks align with reproductive life stages: 24 years corresponds to peak fertility and hormone levels, while 46 years coincides with perimenopause, characterized by dramatic declines in estrogen and systemic epigenetic remodeling [31]. Collectively, these findings reinforce that aging is a non-uniform process across the lifespan.

Tumors are an important factor driving the increase in epigenetic age [5, 32, 33]. In tumor patients, blood methylation profiles often differ from physiological aging, leading to systematic overestimation of epigenetic age, particularly in leukemia. In this study, all leukemia patients exhibited epigenetic ages significantly higher than chronological age, and solid tumor patients also showed age acceleration, though to a lesser degree. This difference likely reflects the proportion of tumor cells in peripheral blood: leukemia cells directly circulate in blood, affecting DNA methylation broadly, whereas circulating tumor cells in solid tumors are scarce, limiting impact. These findings are consistent with Spolnicka et al. [34], indicating that blood-based epigenetic clocks may have limitations in assessing the aging status of leukemia patients, and interpretations for solid tumor patients should be cautious. Nonetheless, blood epigenetic age remains valuable for cancer risk stratification and mortality prediction [35].

Single-source blood methylation data have limitations for evaluating multi-organ aging, as a systemic intermediary is needed to link epigenetic signals with macroscopic physiological phenotypes. In our study, a clock based solely on limited biochemical and hematological markers showed insufficient predictive accuracy, indicating the need for more comprehensive biomarkers. Nevertheless, our methylation clock captured multi-system aging signals: predicted “Old” and “Young” groups showed significant differences in LYMPH%, NEUT%, Urea, and HbA1c (P < 0.05), reflecting aging in immune, renal, protein metabolism, and glucose metabolic systems, supporting the potential of blood methylation clocks to assess systemic aging.

In conclusion, we developed a cost-effective methylation clock suitable for the Chinese population using multiplex PCR technology. The model demonstrated excellent predictive performance across multiple independent cohorts, highlighting its potential for large-scale screening. Limitations include regional sampling bias (primarily eastern China), limited coverage of target CpG sites, and reliance on blood methylation data, which constrains organ-specific aging assessment. Future studies will expand targeted regions, integrate proteomic and metabolomic data, and investigate dynamic responses to lifestyle interventions to establish a more comprehensive, multidimensional aging assessment system. This approach aims to support China’s transition from “longevity” to “healthy longevity” through precise and practical aging evaluation.
