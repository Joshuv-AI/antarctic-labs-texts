Causal relationship between immune cells and epilepsy mediated by metabolites analyzed through Mendelian randomization
Authors: Jiangwei Chen, Haichun Yu, Huihua Liu, Han Yu, Shuang Liang, Qiong Wu, Xian Zhang, Rong Zeng, Limei Diao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur study investigated the causal relationship between immune cells, metabolites, and epilepsy using two-sample Mendelian Randomization (MR) and mediation MR analysis of 731 immune cell traits and 1400 metabolites. Our core methodology centered on inverse-variance weighted MR, supplemented by other methods. This approach was crucial in clarifying the potential intermediary functions of metabolites in the genetic links between traits of immune cells and epilepsy. We found a causal relationship between immune cells and epilepsy. Specifically, the genetically predicted levels of CD64 on CD14-CD16- are positively correlated with the risk of epilepsy (p < 0.001, OR = 1.0826, 95% CI 1.0361–1.1312). Similarly, metabolites also exhibit a causal relationship with both immune cells (OR = 1.0438, 95% CI 1.0087–1.0801, p = 0.0140) and epilepsy (p = 0.0334, OR = 1.0897, 95% CI 1.0068–1.1795), and sensitivity analysis was conducted to further validate these relationships. Importantly, our intermediate MR results suggest that the metabolite Paraxanthine to linoleate (18:2n6) ratio may mediate the causal relationship between immune cell CD64 on CD14-CD16- and epilepsy, with a mediation effect of 5.05%. The results suggest the importance of specific immune cell levels and metabolites in understanding epilepsy’s pathogenesis, which is significant for its prevention and treatment.Keywords: Epilepsy, Mendelian randomization, Immune cells, MetabolitesSubject terms: Genetics, Immunology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Epilepsy, a chronic neurological condition, is marked by atypical electrical brain activity resulting in recurrent seizures, known as spontaneous recurrent seizures (SRS)1. Clinical manifestations of epilepsy include convulsions, loss of consciousness, myoclonus (sudden muscle jerks), aura, automatisms, hypotonia (decreased muscle tone), altered sensations, and prolonged muscle contractions2–4. According to the 2017 definition by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), epilepsy is categorized as generalized epilepsy, focal epilepsy, combined generalized and focal epilepsy, and unknown epilepsy5. Epilepsy affects people of all ages, regions, and races, although it is more prevalent in children and adolescents. Its prevalence worldwide ranges from 0.5 to 1.0%5. Various factors may contribute to the development of epilepsy, including genetic predisposition, acquired brain injuries, infections, and metabolic or immune disorders2. Despite these known factors, the precise cause of epilepsy often remains unclear.

In the pathogenesis of epilepsy, inflammation and immune responses are vital factors6–8. During epileptic seizures, the secretion of inflammatory mediators and the activation of immune cells can lead to excessive neuronal excitation and abnormal synaptic connectivity9–11. A complex interaction exists between immune cells and neurons, known as neuro-immune modulation12. Immune cells can release various cytokines and chemokines, which may affect neuronal excitability and stability, thereby influencing epileptic seizures8,13. However, the existence of a direct causal relationship between epilepsy and immune cells remains uncertain. Thus, a study design that minimizes biases is necessary to elucidate this potential causality. Moreover, given the intricate relationship between immune cells and metabolites14–16, and findings that certain metabolites, such as circulating metabolites17, can serve as biomarkers for epilepsy, it is conceivable that metabolites may act as potential mediators between immune cells and epilepsy.

Mendelian Randomization (MR) is a research method grounded in Mendel’s Second Law18–20. It utilizes the random allocation of alleles from parents to offspring during the creation of gametes, facilitating the establishment of links between traits of exposure, such as intermediate phenotypes, and outcomes of diseases18–20. This method employs genetic variation as a key variable, serving as an instrument to deduce the impact of exposure factors on outcomes based on observational data18. The inherent randomness of allele distribution in Mendelian Randomization naturally minimizes non-random errors or misleading factors, and prevents causal inversion through the principles of Mendelian inheritance18. Therefore, MR provides a reliable mechanism for drawing causal inferences from observational data. Our objectives were to (i) ascertain if there is a causal link between immune cells and epilepsy, and (ii) evaluate the degree to which metabolites mediate the effects of immune cells on epilepsy.

Methods

Study design

Our analysis was founded on publicly accessible data that have been previously approved by the institutional review boards of the corresponding studies. This meant that no further approvals were necessary for our work. Detailed descriptions of the results from our analysis can be found in both the main article and the supplementary materials accompanying it.

To ensure dependable outcomes, a two-sample MR study must satisfy three fundamental assumptions: (1) The instrumental variables (IVs) ought to exhibit a notable correlation with immune cells; (2) IVs should remain uncorrelated with any confounding variables capable of impacting the connection between exposure and outcome factors; (3) IVs should solely influence outcomes via the pathway of immune cells.

Our study initially utilized a two-sample MR to ascertain the causal association between immune cells, metabolites, and epilepsy. Subsequently, to further investigate the potential impact of immune cells on epilepsy mediated through metabolites, we employed a mediating MR approach, also known as a two-step MR.

Data sources

The immune cell exposure data is sourced from OpenGWAS, which includes 126 billion genetic associations from 14,582 complete GWAS datasets21. The intermediary (metabolite) data comes from GWAS Catalog22. The outcome (epilepsy) data is derived from FinnGen GWAS23 R10, including 12,891 cases and 312,803 controls, with all individuals being of European descent. Sample characteristics include age at first onset, gender distribution, and mortality rate during the follow-up period, and so on.

Selection for genetic variation

For immune cells to outcome, immune cells to metabolites, and metabolites to outcome, we selected instrumental variables (IVs) with a p-value threshold of < 1.0 × 10−5. To ensure independent loci for the IVs, we used the “TwoSampleMR” package, setting the linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold for the 1000 Genomes EUR data to R2 < 0.001, with a clumping distance of 10,000 kb.

For the reverse direction (epilepsy to immune cells), IVs were chosen with a p-value threshold of < 5.0 × 10−6. Similarly, to obtain independent loci for the IVs, we applied the “TwoSampleMR” package with the LD threshold for the 1000 Genomes EUR data set to R2 < 0.001, and a clumping distance of 10,000 kb. The results revealed no evidence of reverse causation. For MR analysis, palindromic and ambiguous SNPs were omitted from IVs24. Furthermore, we computed the F statistic (i.e., the proportion of variability attributed to Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms in each exposure), represented as F=(N-K-1K)(R21-R2), where K stands for the count of genetic variants and N denotes the sample size25. We excluded instrumental variables with low strength (F-statistics < 10)25.

Statistical analysis

Although the primary emphasis of our research was on outcomes using the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method26, it was crucial to confirm that the directions of the results were consistent across all methods used, including IVW. For a thorough secondary analysis, we applied various methods like MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, weighted mode, and MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO)20,27–29, to conduct a sensitivity check of our IVW results.

Pleiotropy and heterogeneity analysis

Our study began with employing the MR-PRESSO29 method to pinpoint and remove outliers. We then reanalyzed the data without these outliers. Next, to investigate the influence of each Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) on the link between exposure and outcome, we executed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, systematically omitting one SNP at a time. Additionally, the MR-Egger regression test was applied to detect horizontal pleiotropy in the Mendelian randomization analysis, particularly focusing on the importance of the intercept term’s statistical significance30. To explore heterogeneity within our findings, we calculated the Cochran Q statistic, considering P = 0.05 as the significance threshold31. All these statistical procedures were performed using R (version 4.2.1), utilizing the MR and MR-PRESSO packages.

Results

Association of immune cells with epilepsy

We first screened for exposure tools related to the outcome (epilepsy) from 731 immune cells. After excluding palindromic SNPs and those identified by MR Steiger filtering as having incorrect causal directions, there are a total of 34 qualified immune cells (Table 1). We then conducted analyses using MR Egger, Weighted Median, IVW, Simple Mode, and Weighted Mode, with results illustrated in forest plots (Supplementary Fig. 1). Research indicates that inflammatory monocytes exacerbate seizures and hippocampal neuropathology32. In recent years, monocytes have been classified into three subtypes: classical (CD14++CD16−), intermediate (CD14 ± CD16 + +), and non-classical (CD14 + CD16 +)33. Therefore, we selected the immune cell subgroup CD64 on CD14-CD16- (id ebi-a-GCST90002001) as our exposure tool variable, and it has the highest significance. In analyses such as MR Egger, Weighted median, IVW, Simple mode, and Weighted mode, we looked for results with p-values below the commonly used threshold for statistical significance (e.g., 0.05). In this case, the IVW method had the lowest p-value at 0.0004, indicating statistical significance in this analysis.

We examined the odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence interval (CI). In the IVW method, the OR was 1.0826, with a 95% CI of 1.0361 to 1.1312, indicating not only a positive direction of effect but also that the confidence interval does not include 1, suggesting an association between CD64 on CD14-CD16- and the outcome variable. Additionally, the OR remained above 1 across different estimation methods, indicating consistent result directions across methods, i.e., the exposure increases the risk of epilepsy. To validate this, we also conducted sensitivity analysis (Fig. 1a). Additionally, our MR analysis showed no evidence of reverse causation between CD64 on CD14-CD16- and epilepsy.

To further validate our findings, we conducted parallel analyses of all significant immune cell populations. Of the 34 eligible immune cells, several populations showed significant results across different methods, and we conducted detailed comparative analyses. The results indicate that, apart from CD64 on CD14-CD16-, several other immune cell populations also show significant associations with epilepsy. Most IVW analyses show positive correlations, including CM DN (CD4-CD8-) AC, CD14 + CD16 + monocyte AC, CD16 + monocyte % monocyte, HLA DR + CD4 + % lymphocyte, B cell % lymphocyte, NK AC, CD28 on CD4 Treg, CD127 on granulocyte, CD25 on CD45RA + CD4 not Treg, indicating that an increase in these cells may be associated with an increased incidence of epilepsy. Conversely, Naive CD8br AC, Granulocyte AC, IgD + CD38- AC, CD33dim HLA DR- AC, Basophil AC, and other cell types showed significant negative correlations with epilepsy, indicating that increases in these cells may be associated with a decrease in epilepsy. Some results, such as those for CD33dim HLA DR- AC, Basophil AC, Naive CD4 + % CD4 + , EM DN (CD4-CD8-) % DN, CD14- CD16- AC, CD39 + CD4 + % T cell, CD28 + CD45RA- CD8dim AC, CD28- CD25 + + CD8br AC, showed slight positive or negative correlations or no discernible correlation with epilepsy. Some results indicate that increases or decreases in these cells may not be significantly associated to epilepsy. These analyses demonstrate that CD64 on CD14-CD16- exhibits the most consistent and significant variation in our dataset. These results further reinforce the evidence of immune cells’ role in the pathogenesis of epilepsy. By performing parallel analyses on all significant immune cell groups, we not only validated the association between CD64 on CD14-CD16-and epilepsy but also revealed the potential roles of other immune cell groups, providing more comprehensive evidence.

Association of metabolites with epilepsy

From 1400 metabolites, we identified exposure tools related to the outcome (epilepsy). After excluding palindromic SNPs and those identified by MR Steiger filtering as having incorrect causal directions, we identified a total of 75 qualified metabolites (Table 2). We then performed analyses using MR Egger, Weighted median, IVW, Simple mode, and Weighted mode, and presented the results in forest plots (Supplementary Fig. 2). We selected the Paraxanthine to linoleate (18:2n6) ratio (id GCST90200982) as the exposure tool for studying the outcome of epilepsy. Among all methods, the IVW method yielded the lowest p-value (0.0334), indicating statistical significance. Across different estimation methods, the OR remained consistently above 1, indicating a consistent association between this ratio and an increased risk of epilepsy. Although some methods had confidence intervals close to 1, the IVW method provided a sufficiently wide confidence interval (1.0068–1.1795), and a narrower interval, suggesting higher precision and enhancing the credibility to the result. We also performed a sensitivity analysis to confirm this result (Fig. 1b).

Discussion

In this research, we employed MR analysis to investigate the causal link between immune cells and epilepsy, and to determine whether this relationship is mediated through metabolites. Our findings suggest a causative link between genetically forecasted levels of immune cells (specifically CD64 on CD14-CD16-) and an increased risk of epilepsy. Under constant conditions, each standard deviation increase in CD64 on CD14-CD16- corresponds to approximately an 8.26% increase in the risk of epilepsy (see Fig. 3b). Notably, approximately 5.05% of this effect appears to be mediated through the ratio of the metabolite Paraxanthine to linoleate (18:2n6).

Studies show that inflammation is a major factor in epilepsy, including genetic epilepsy37,38. Activation of the innate immune system, including cytokines and chemokines, triggers a series of neuroinflammatory responses that affect neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission, thereby increasing the risk of epileptic seizures. For instance, interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) can affect neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission, thereby promoting or inhibiting seizures37,39. In genetic epilepsy, levels of IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α are often elevated; these pro-inflammatory cytokines regulate the blood–brain barrier, allowing more immune cells and cytokines to enter the brain, thus promoting neuroinflammation and epileptic activity40,41. IL-1 and TNF-α can increase neuronal excitability by upregulating NMDA receptors on postsynaptic cells, thereby facilitating seizures42,43. Additionally, TNF-α can enhance glutamate release from microglial cells by upregulating glutaminase and increasing the expression of AMPA receptors, further promoting seizures. IL-6 in the nervous system has multiple functions, including regulating voltage-gated and receptor-mediated ion channels that affect neuronal excitability and inducing neurological diseases in animal models44,45. Other studies46have demonstrated that tumor necrosis TNF-α inhibits epileptic seizures through specific receptors, while IL-1 receptor antagonists have been found to exhibit antiepileptic effects in mice. Overexpression of cytokines, such as IL-6, in the brain is associated with the induction of neurological disorders in animal models. In animal models, inducing neuroinflammation can significantly increase the frequency and severity of epileptic seizures. For example, mice injected with LPS (a pro-inflammatory agent) exhibit higher susceptibility to seizures, along with significantly elevated levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in brain tissue47,48. Clinical studies have also found that epilepsy patients have elevated levels of inflammatory markers in their cerebrospinal fluid and serum, which are closely associated with seizure frequency and disease severity49.

Furthermore, a study discussed the role of the P2X7 receptor in epilepsy and neuroinflammation, focusing on its expression in various brain cells and its involvement in the inflammatory process during seizures13. This suggests a close link between immune responses and neural health. The migration of immune cells to the central nervous system and their roles in neurological disorders represent significant area of research. Additionally, studies50 have investigated the role of the blood–brain barrier in immune functions and dysfunctions, which is crucial for understanding how peripheral immune responses impact the central nervous system. Collectively, these studies emphasize the intricate interplay between the immune system and neurological disorders, especially epilepsy.

When immune cell activity changes, various metabolites may be released, which can influence the risk of epilepsy by affecting the function of neural cells. For example, certain metabolites might alter neuronal excitability or affect neural conduction pathways51. A study employed 1H-NMR and DI/LC–MS/MS technologies to identify and quantify 212 metabolites52. The results showed that among these 212 detected metabolites, 14 exhibited significant concentration changes between epilepsy patients and the control group (p < 0.05, q < 0.05)52. Therefore, by studying these changes in metabolites, scientists can better understand the connection between the immune system and the onset of epilepsy, and how modulating these metabolic pathways might reduce the risk of epilepsy. This study identified that the Paraxanthine to linoleate (18:2n6) ratio may play a certain mediating role in the relationship between immune cells and epilepsy. This suggests that this metabolite could be a crucial factor linking changes in immune activity and epilepsy risk. The variation in this ratio may reflect the activation state of immune cells, impacting the function of the nervous system. Paraxanthine and linoleic acid (18:2n6) are important metabolites: Paraxanthine is a product of caffeine metabolism53, while linoleic acid is an essential fatty acid crucial for inflammatory responses54. Paraxanthine is metabolized through the cytochrome P450 enzyme system in the liver55, whereas linoleic acid undergoes a series of enzymatic reactions to produce arachidonic acid (ARA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA)56,57, both of which are significant signaling molecules and inflammatory mediators. It is hypothesized that Paraxanthine may alter the activity or expression of these enzymes, thereby affecting the levels of linoleic acid and its metabolites. This interaction may influence neuronal excitability and inflammatory responses. Although direct evidence is currently lacking, existing studies suggest that paraxanthine possesses anti-inflammatory properties. Some paraxanthine derivatives can inhibit the chemotaxis of neutrophils and monocytes in human blood and suppress TNF-α production58,59. Additionally, related research has found that linoleic acid and its metabolites are associated with neurological diseases60–62. Animal experiments have shown that linoleic acid, when combined with conventional antiepileptic drugs, may be effective in treating generalized convulsive epilepsy63.

These data preliminarily support our hypothesis about their potential interactions (see Fig. 4). The correlation between metabolite levels and immune response indicates that these factors may play a critical role in the pathophysiology of epilepsy. However, the exact nature of these interactions remains unclear and requires further investigation. Additionally, the analysis of the mediating role of the ratio of the metabolite Paraxanthine to linoleate (18:2n6) in the causal relationship between the immune cell subgroup CD64 on CD14-CD16- and epilepsy also supports this view, although this role is limited (see Fig. 3b). It is still uncertain whether changes in this ratio lead to increased excitability of nerve cells or alterations in neural communication pathways, thereby increasing the risk of epilepsy. The current evidence is mainly correlational and insufficient to confirm the ratio of Paraxanthine to linoleic acid as a direct causal factor for epilepsy. Further research is needed to determine its specific role in the pathological process. Larger-scale clinical trials and long-term follow-up studies are required to validate our hypothesis. Our future research will continue to analyze the changes in the ratio of paraxanthine to linoleic acid in patients with different types of epilepsy and study the specific effects of this ratio on neuroinflammation and the neurotransmitter system. This will help better evaluate the potential application value of this ratio in epilepsy treatment.

Our research further underscores the critical role of neuroinflammation in epilepsy. By analyzing immune cells and metabolites in epilepsy patients, we found that specific immune cells (e.g.,CD64 on CD14-CD16-) and metabolites are significantly elevated in these patients, consistent with inflammatory responses and increased cytokine levels observed in previous studies, including IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α. Additionally, we revealed new aspects, such as significant changes in specific metabolites like the Paraxanthine to linoleate (18:2n6) ratio in epilepsy patients, suggesting that metabolic pathways may play a role in the onset of epilepsy. Our findings enhance the understanding of the role of metabolites in neuroinflammation and epilepsy, offering new insights into the potential of metabolic regulation as a therapeutic approach for epilepsy.

In our research, we were the first to employ mediation MR technology to explore the causal association among metabolites, immune cells, and epilepsy. Through multiple typical sensitivity analyses, we excluded the likelihood of reverse causation. Our initial results indicate a causal link between immune cells and epilepsy and its intermediaries, further supporting the theoretical basis for treating and preventing epilepsy and proposing new methods for its management. For example, the risk of epilepsy onset can be managed by initially regulating specific metabolites through diet, exercise, or other methods. By identifying key immune cells and metabolite markers in specific epilepsy subtypes, personalized treatment plans can be developed. For instance, immunomodulatory therapy can be considered for epilepsy patients with significantly abnormal immune responses. Additionally, by detecting the levels of immune cells and metabolites in a patient’s body, early diagnosis and prediction of epilepsy risk can be achieved, enabling early intervention to prevent the occurrence of epilepsy or reduce its severity.

While our study includes several advantages, such as a substantial sample size and the utilization of various sensitivity analysis techniques to ensure the credibility of our research and mitigate confounding variables, it is essential to acknowledge the presence of unavoidable limitations. Firstly, although the sample size is substantial, the participants are entirely of European descent, potentially limiting considerations of genetic diversity and environmental influences. Secondly, due to the limitations of the database used, our current study analyzes epilepsy as a whole. Future research should conduct a more in-depth analysis of the various subtypes of epilepsy. Thirdly, despite our efforts to identify and remove outlier variables, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of horizontal pleiotropy influencing our findings. Fourthly, while our results suggest a potential mediation effect, the fact that the p-value is greater than 0.05 and the confidence interval includes negative numbers may be due to the presence of unknown moderating variables. Future research should focus on including participants from a more diverse range of ethnicities and regions, expanding the sample size, and conducting experiments at the cellular level, with animal models, as well as clinical trials. Advanced analytical methods or stricter statistical controls should be used to reduce the impact of potential biases. Additionally, the robustness of research results could be further validated by using different statistical models or tools.

Future research will focus on validating the mechanisms we have identified, particularly the specific roles of immune cells and metabolites in the various subtypes of epilepsy. We plan to conduct larger clinical trials to verify the association between immune cells, metabolites, and the different subtypes of epilepsy. Additionally, we will utilize cell and animal models to further investigate the specific mechanisms of these biomarkers. For example, by using gene editing techniques, we can specifically knock out or overexpress certain immune cell surface markers to observe their effects on epileptic seizures. Recognizing the importance of genetic diversity, we will expand our study samples to include participants from more diverse ethnic and regional backgrounds to enhance the generalizability and reliability of our findings.

Conclusion

Our study supports previous findings of a causal role of immune mechanisms in the development of epilepsy, showing that specific levels of immune cells (such as CD64 on CD14-CD16-) are associated with an increased risk of epilepsy. Additionally, the ratio of Paraxanthine to linoleate (18:2n6) appears to exert a moderating influence on this relationship, although its impact is limited. Subsequent investigations should prioritize larger sample sizes, deeper exploration of mechanisms, controlling potential confounding factors, and conducting comparative studies across different populations to validate and extend the current findings. This is significant for understanding the pathogenesis of epilepsy and its prevention and treatment.
