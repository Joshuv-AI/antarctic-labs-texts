Validation of the Emergency Surgery Acuity Score in Patients Who Underwent Emergency General Surgery in South Korea: A Multi‐Centre, Retrospective, Post Hoc Analysis
Authors: Gun‐Hee Yi, Suk‐Kyung Hong, Yang‐Hee Jun, Sungyeon Yoo, Keesang Yoo, Hak‐Jae Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aimed to validate the Emergency Surgery Acuity Score (ESAS) in Korean patients who underwent emergency general surgery (EGS) and evaluate its effectiveness in predicting various outcomes.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingTwo tertiary hospitals in South Korea.ParticipantsThis study included 1629 adults who underwent EGS between July 2014 and December 2019.Primary and Secondary Outcome MeasuresThe ESAS and its abbreviated version, the Physiological Emergency Surgery Acuity Score (P‐ESAS), were calculated for each patient. Predictive performance was assessed for 28‐day mortality, post‐operative complications, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and hospital length of stay (LOS). C‐statistics were used to compare ESAS with P‐ESAS and other scoring systems, including the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Logistic regression models were employed to identify independent predictors of 28‐day mortality, incorporating clinically relevant covariates such as demographic characteristics, comorbidities, physiological parameters, and surgical factors.ResultsThe ESAS and P‐ESAS demonstrated superior predictive abilities compared to the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) across clinical outcomes. For 28‐day mortality, the c‐statistics were 0.917 for ESAS, 0.897 for P‐ESAS, and 0.691 for CCI. Both ESAS and P‐ESAS outperformed CCI in predicting post‐operative complications (c‐statistics: 0.701, 0.677, and 0.637, respectively) and ICU admission (c‐statistics: 0.777, 0.737, and 0.645, respectively). Linear regression analysis revealed a significant relationship between ESAS and hospital length of stay (LOS = 2.96 × ESAS; p < 0.001, adjR 2 = 0.430). Both ESAS and P‐ESAS were identified as significant independent predictors of 28‐day mortality in multivariable analyses.ConclusionThe ESAS is validated as an effective tool for predicting 28‐day mortality, post‐operative complications, ICU admission, and hospital LOS in Korean patients undergoing emergency general surgery. Its predictive performance compared to other scoring systems highlights its potential to improve risk stratification and resource management in emergency surgery settings.

Introduction

Emergency general surgery (EGS) has long been recognized as a significant public health burden. Between 2001 and 2010, a total of 27 688 807 individuals were admitted for EGS in the United States, accounting for 7.1% of all hospitalizations [1]. Patients requiring EGS demonstrate a substantial rise in 30‐day mortality and post‐operative complication rates when compared with patients undergoing elective surgery [2]. This increase has been attributed to clinical and patient factors at the time of surgery, including age, comorbidities, and acute physiologic disturbances. Havens et al. reported that the mortality and major post‐operative complication rates in patients undergoing EGS were 1.39 and 1.31 times higher, respectively, than those in patients without EGS [3]. Notably, EGS is identified as a distinct risk factor for mortality and complications, necessitating timely surgical interventions and accurate risk assessment tools [3, 4].

Predicting post‐operative outcomes in patients undergoing EGS is essential for making effective bedside decisions and optimizing post‐operative care. Accurate risk stratification plays a crucial role in several aspects of patient care and healthcare management, including informed consent, resource allocation, quality improvement initiatives, and performance comparisons across institutions. Various scoring systems have been developed to assess surgical risk, each with its own strengths and limitations. Although the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification is widely used due to its simplicity, it lacks specificity for emergency cases. The Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the enUmeration of Mortality and morbidity (POSSUM) offers a more comprehensive assessment; however, it tends to overpredict mortality in low‐risk patients [5, 6]. Moreover, tools like the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), while valuable, are often less applicable in emergency settings due to their design limitations [7, 8]. More recently, tools specifically designed for emergency surgery have been developed. The Emergency Surgery Acuity Score (ESAS) is a 29‐point score that includes 22 pre‐operative variables to predict 30‐day mortality [9]. Its abbreviated version, the Physiological Emergency Surgery Acuity Score (P‐ESAS), is a 15‐point score focusing on acute physiological derangements, aiming to be more user‐friendly at the bedside [10]. Several studies have reported that the ESAS significantly predicted mortality and post‐operative complications [11, 12, 13].

However, despite its proven utility in Western populations, its validation in non‐Western settings, including South Korea, remains limited. Given these considerations, there is a clear need to validate existing scoring systems, particularly the ESAS, in diverse populations. While the utility of the ESAS has been demonstrated in several studies in Western populations, it has not been studied in Asian populations. Therefore, this study aimed to validate the predictive performance of the ESAS and P‐ESAS for clinical outcomes, including 28‐day mortality, post‐operative complications, and hospital length of stay, in Korean patients undergoing EGS.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals in South Korea: Asan Medical Center (AMC) and Samsung Medical Center (SMC). The characteristics of these hospitals are presented in Table 1. The study included adults (aged 18–99 years) who underwent EGS for acute abdominal conditions or trauma between July 2014 and December 2019 at AMC, and between July 2016 and December 2019 at SMC. EGS was defined as any surgery performed to address life‐threatening conditions requiring urgent intervention, including gastrointestinal obstruction, perforation, ischemia, and trauma. The exclusion criteria were patients aged < 18 years, emergency surgeries performed by non‐general surgery departments, patients initially admitted for conservative management before requiring surgery, and cases involving vascular procedures or organ transplantation. Conservative management was defined as non‐operative initial management, such as medical therapy or observation, for conditions potentially requiring surgical intervention. Patients initially treated conservatively but eventually requiring surgery were excluded to maintain homogeneity in surgical cases. Patients' demographics and medical histories were accessed from the electronic medical records. The study used anonymized retrospective data with no patient interaction, meeting ethical guidelines for waiver of informed consent. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Asan Medical Center (No. 2022‐0680).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was to evaluate the predictive performance of the ESAS for 28‐day mortality. Secondary outcomes included assessing the ESAS's ability to predict other clinical outcomes, such as operation time, length of hospital stay (hospital LOS), length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay for patients admitted to the ICU, re‐operation during hospital stay, and re‐admission within 30 days after hospital discharge. We also assessed post‐operative complications using the Clavien–Dindo classification, categorizing them as no complications (grade 0), mild complications (grades 1–2), and severe complications (grades 3–5). Additionally, we performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to identify prognostic factors influencing 28‐day mortality. These factors included patient characteristics, such as sex, age, operation time, presence of malignancy, CCI, ESAS, P‐ESAS, presence of sepsis or shock on admission, time from the emergency room admission to the operating room, and whether the surgery was performed by the ACS division.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, while continuous variables were analyzed using the student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. C‐statistics were calculated to assess the predictive accuracy of the ESAS, P‐ESAS, and other scoring systems, such as the CCI. Optimal cut‐off values were determined using Youden's index from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. We performed univariate analyses by calculating odds ratios to identify potential prognostic factors for 28‐day mortality. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was then conducted to identify independent predictors of 28‐day mortality. Clinically relevant variables, such as age, sex, comorbidities, physiological derangements, and surgical factors, were selected based on prior studies and clinical importance. To assess the relationship between the ESAS and hospital LOS, we performed a linear regression analysis. Additionally, we conducted separate analyses for each participating hospital to evaluate the consistency of ESAS performance across different settings. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://www.r‐project.org). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Among the 1600 patients included in this study, 939 and 661 were from Hospitals A and B, respectively. The baseline characteristics, such as age and sex distribution, were comparable between the two hospitals (mean age: 57.6 ± 16.0 years in Hospital A vs. 56.9 ± 17.7 years in Hospital B; p = 0.467; male sex: 58.7% vs. 57.3%; p = 0.628). However, Hospital A had a slightly higher CCI score compared to Hospital B (2.2 ± 2.3 vs. 1.9 ± 2.5; p = 0.021), reflecting a higher prevalence of chronic conditions. Open surgeries were more frequent in Hospital A (67.9% vs. 46.7%), while laparoscopic surgeries were more common in Hospital B (29.8% vs. 51.1%). ICU admission rates were significantly higher in Hospital B (26.8% vs. 44.9%; p = 0.001), likely due to institutional practices. The primary causes of emergency surgery included gastrointestinal obstruction (24.0% in Hospital A vs. 7.9% in Hospital B), perforation (35.5% vs. 28.0%), and ischemia or infarction (9.9% vs. 8.0%). Hospital A had a higher proportion of small intestine surgeries (48.6% vs. 23.4%), while appendix surgeries were more common in Hospital B (20.8% vs. 33.7%). These differences highlight the diversity in patient populations and surgical practices across the two hospitals, providing important context for interpreting the study results. Clinical Outcomes

The overall 28‐day mortality rate was 2.6%, with no significant difference observed between the hospitals (3.2% in Hospital A vs. 1.8% in Hospital B; p = 0.123). In‐hospital mortality was also comparable between the two hospitals (4.9% in Hospital A vs. 3.0% in Hospital B; p = 0.084). The mean length of hospital stay was 14.2 ± 17.1 days, with patients in Hospital A staying significantly longer than those in Hospital B (15.7 ± 18.5 vs. 12.0 ± 14.5 days; p = 0.001). Similarly, ICU length of stay was longer in Hospital A compared to Hospital B (8.3 ± 14.6 vs. 5.8 ± 11.8 days; p = 0.030). Re‐operation rates were higher in Hospital A (9.4% vs. 6.1%; p = 0.021), whereas re‐admission rates were higher in Hospital B (3.6% vs. 9.8%; p = 0.001).

Post‐operative complications occurred in 18.9% of patients overall, with no significant difference observed between the hospitals (19.9% in Hospital A vs. 17.4% in Hospital B; p = 0.490). Based on the Clavien–Dindo classification, 81.1% of patients experienced no complications, while mild complications (grades 1–2) and severe complications (grades 3–5) occurred in 6.7% and 12.2% of patients, respectively. Predictive Performance of ESAS and P‐ESAS

The ESAS and P‐ESAS demonstrated superior predictive abilities compared to the CCI across all outcomes. For 28‐day mortality, the c‐statistics were 0.917 for ESAS, 0.897 for P‐ESAS, and 0.691 for CCI (Figure 1a). For post‐operative complications, the c‐statistics were 0.701 for ESAS, 0.677 for P‐ESAS, and 0.637 for CCI (Figure 1b). For ICU admission, the c‐statistics were 0.777 for ESAS, 0.737 for P‐ESAS, and 0.645 for CCI (Figure 1c). In ICU patients, the ESAS and P‐ESAS had c‐statistics of 0.842 and 0.825, respectively, for 28‐day mortality, outperforming APACHE II (0.778, Figure 2a). For post‐operative complications in ICU patients, the ESAS (0.613) also slightly outperformed P‐ESAS (0.595) and APACHE II (0.594, Figure 2b). Optimal cut‐off values determined using Youden's index were 5.5 for ESAS and 2.5 for P‐ESAS for 28‐day mortality. Cut‐off values for ESAS and P‐ESAS were determined through ROC curve analysis using Youden's index, which balances sensitivity and specificity to identify optimal thresholds. These values are not absolute scores but serve as benchmarks to stratify patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups for outcomes such as 28‐day mortality and post‐operative complications. By providing a practical tool for risk stratification, these cut‐offs enable clinicians to prioritize interventions for high‐risk patients, improving resource allocation and clinical decision‐making.

Table 5 presents the univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses for identifying prognostic factors influencing 28‐day mortality. The variables included in the regression analyses were selected based on their clinical relevance and previous studies indicating their potential impact on mortality in emergency surgical patients. The primary aim of this analysis was to evaluate whether ESAS and P‐ESAS could serve as independent predictors of 28‐day mortality after adjusting for other significant factors. The multivariate analysis confirmed ESAS and P‐ESAS as independent predictors, demonstrating their potential as reliable tools for stratifying risk in emergency surgical patients. These findings suggest that integrating ESAS and P‐ESAS into clinical workflows could improve patient management and optimize resource allocation. In the multivariate analysis, female sex (OR: 0.43, p = 0.044), the ESAS (OR: 1.42, p = 0.001), P‐ESAS (OR: 1.43, p = 0.001), and shock on admission (OR: 2.97, p = 0.017) remained significant independent predictors of 28‐day mortality. These results highlight the importance of the ESAS and P‐ESAS as prognostic tools for predicting 28‐day mortality in patients undergoing emergency surgery, even after adjusting for other significant factors.

Discussion

Acute care surgery encompasses a range of interventions, including trauma management, surgical critical care, and EGS [14, 15]. A surgical procedure is considered emergent when it is crucial for addressing an immediate threat to a patient's life, organs, or tissues. Patients requiring EGS present a unique set of challenges, regardless of their age or pre‐existing health conditions. These individuals often exhibit acute physiological disturbances and lack the benefit of pre‐operative optimization. As outlined by the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma, an ideal risk stratification tool should effectively measure morbidity and mortality risk using easily obtainable and objective data. It should be applicable early in the clinical course, prior to surgical intervention, and be relevant for non‐surgical cases. Additionally, it must be easy to implement in everyday clinical practice [16].

Our investigation demonstrates the robust predictive capabilities of the ESAS in a Korean population. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the strong predictive capabilities of the ESAS. Sangji et al. [9] first introduced and validated the ESAS using the ACS‐NSQIP database, reporting a c‐statistic of 0.86 for 30‐day mortality, indicating robust predictive power in emergency surgery settings. Our findings extend these results, demonstrating superior predictive performance with c‐statistics of 0.917 for ESAS and 0.897 for P‐ESAS in predicting 28‐day mortality. This enhanced performance may be attributed to the comprehensive nature of our patient cohort and the standardized emergency care protocols in Korean tertiary hospitals. The high accuracy of these scoring systems in our study is consistent with prior research highlighting their effectiveness across diverse emergency surgery settings [17]. The development of the P‐ESAS aimed to simplify risk assessment by focusing on acute physiological factors; still, the ESAS remains more comprehensive in capturing a wide range of comorbid conditions [10, 16]. The comparative analysis between ESAS and P‐ESAS in our study (c‐statistics: 0.917 vs. 0.897 for mortality, 0.701 vs. 0.677 for complications) demonstrates that both instruments offer comparable clinical utility depending on the specific context. Moreover, in this study, the ESAS demonstrated superior predictive ability for 28‐day mortality, post‐operative complications, and ICU admission compared to the CCI. The high accuracy of these scoring systems in our study is consistent with prior research highlighting their effectiveness across diverse emergency surgery settings [17].

This study is the first to validate the ESAS for patients undergoing EGS in Korea, confirming its effectiveness in this population. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on Western populations, this research verifies the applicability of the ESAS in a non‐Western race, contributing significantly to global emergency surgery risk assessment. Our results indicate that the ESAS predicts 28‐day mortality and morbidity, ICU admission, and hospital LOS with high accuracy. The strong correlation between ESAS scores and hospital length of stay (LOS = 2.96 × ESAS; p < 0.001) provides quantitative evidence for resource planning and patient counseling. This comprehensive predictive ability aids pre‐operative evaluation, informed decision‐making, and optimization of patient care in EGS. The established cut‐off values (ESAS: 5.5, P‐ESAS: 2.5) for predicting 28‐day mortality provide objective thresholds for risk stratification in our population. These parameters, combined with the scores' significant predictive performance for ICU admission (c‐statistics: 0.777 for ESAS, 0.737 for P‐ESAS), facilitate evidence‐based critical care resource allocation and clinical decision‐making. The ESAS could be integrated into hospital resource management systems to optimize ICU bed allocation and surgical scheduling, and serve as a standardized tool for quality improvement initiatives across different institutions in Korea. More specifically, these cut‐off values could help clinicians identify high‐risk patients who might benefit from more intensive perioperative monitoring or earlier ICU admission. The strong predictive performance for both mortality and ICU admission suggests that the ESAS could be particularly valuable in determining the level of post‐operative care required. Furthermore, the score's utility extends beyond individual patient care to institutional resource planning, where it could help optimize emergency surgery scheduling and staffing allocation. In the broader Korean healthcare context, the ESAS could facilitate standardized outcome reporting and inter‐hospital comparisons, potentially leading to improved quality metrics and more efficient resource utilization across different healthcare facilities.

This study has certain limitations; first, as a retrospective study, the data collection may not be systematic. Second, the study was conducted in a limited number of hospitals in Korea, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other countries or regions. Third, while the predictive performance of the ESAS was validated against other emergency surgery risk assessment tools, further research is needed to evaluate its effectiveness in clinical practice. Future research should involve prospective studies across diverse populations and regions to further validate the predictive performance and clinical applicability of the ESAS. Additionally, studies assessing the impact of the ESAS on resource allocation and patient management optimization in clinical practice are necessary.

In conclusion, this study successfully validates the ESAS for patients undergoing EGS in Korea, demonstrating its effectiveness in this population. The ESAS proves to be a reliable tool for predicting 28‐day mortality, post‐operative complications, ICU admission, and hospital LOS, aiding in pre‐operative evaluation and patient management. While the study's retrospective nature and limited geographic scope are notable limitations, the findings contribute significantly to global emergency surgery risk assessment.
