Genetic relationship among grape (Vitis vinifera L.) genotypes grown in inner Anatolia region Turkey analyzed by ISSR and RAPD markers
Authors: Yeter Çilesiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) is extensively cultivated in Turkey and holds significant cultural and economic value. However, genetic characterization of grape genotypes from lesser-studied regions such as Inner Anatolia remains incomplete, limiting conservation and breeding efforts. This study addresses this critical knowledge gap by utilizing ISSR (inter simple sequence repeats) and RAPD (randomly amplified polymorphic DNA) molecular markers to comprehensively assess genetic diversity and relationships among grape genotypes from the Divriği district in Sivas province, Turkey. Eight ISSR and eight RAPD primers were used, revealing considerable genetic variation. The ISSR primers produced a total of 88 polymorphic bands, with primer UBC-810 producing the highest number of bands (15). Similarly, RAPD primers generated 68 polymorphic bands and the highest number of bands (13) were produced by OPA-18 marker. The UPGMA dendrogram analysis using ISSR data revealed two primary genetic clades, with similarity indices between 0.212 and 0.816. Similarly, RAPD analysis also produced two main clusters, exhibiting genetic similarity indices between 0.164 and 0.912. Principal component analysis (PCA) corroborated the dendrogram groupings from both ISSR and RAPD analyses, exhibiting consistent clustering and strengthening the dependability of both marker systems. Significant genetic differentiation was observed among the tested grape genotypes, highlighting the presence of genetically distinct individuals. Genotypes ‘G7’, ‘G1’, ‘G9’, and ‘G10’ demonstrated significant genetic divergence, suggesting them as crucial candidates for conservation efforts. This genetic diversity likely reflects historical cultivation practices, natural selection, mutation accumulation, and potential introgression from wild populations. The results indicate the significance of integrating molecular markers into grapevine breeding and conservation efforts. Both ISSR and RAPD markers proved successful in genetic characterization, yielding significant insights into grapevine genetic resources. Introduction

Grape (Vitis vinifera L.) is a fruit crop belonging to family Vitaceae and the genus Vitis. The genus Vitis comprises about 60 species of grapevines that provide the berry fruit known as grapes1. Grapes had been domesticated ~ 6000 − 800 years ago2. More than 90% of commercially available grape berries originate from the main species V. vinifera1. Grapes are globally recognized for their rich nutritional composition, including several beneficial substances that promote human health3–5. Grapes plays an essential role in healthy and balanced human nutrition and may be consumed in several ways6,7. Grapes display a variety of colours, including white, green, red, purple, and black8,9. The grape berry is rich in sugars, organic acids, vitamins, minerals, and phenolic compounds10,11. It has around 15–30% carbohydrates (glucose and fructose)12 along with vitamins C, A (in the form of β-carotene)13, and K. Vitamin C strengthens the immune system14, while vitamin K is crucial for coagulation and skeletal health15,16. Grapes are notably abundant in K, which helps in the regulation of cellular water equilibrium and blood pressure17,18. Grapes include phenolic compounds, such as flavonoids, resveratrol, and quercetin, which have strong antioxidant effects that aid the body in combating free radicals and oxidative stress3,19. Owing to these beneficial attributes, grapes have a significant global market for both fresh consumption (table grapes) and dried products (raisins), as well as processed products (wine, juice, etc.)6.

Globally, grapes were cultivated on 6.59 million hectares, yielding 72.4 million tons in 202320. China leads in grape production, followed by Italy, the USA, Spain, France, Turkey, and India21. Turkey’s viticulture history extends over millennia, with Anatolia (Asia Minor) serving as the starting point in grape production and a significant migration pathway for Mediterranean grapevines22,23. Turkey produces 4.2 million tons of grapes annually and serves as the leading supplier of raisins, constituting 30% of global exports24. Besides wine, grapes are eaten fresh, dried, or used in traditional items like grape molasses and stuffed vine leaves in Turkey, making them commercially and culturally significant25,26. The national germplasm bank in Turkey has over 1,200 grape varieties, demonstrating the genetic diversity of local viticulture22. Crop resilience, breeding programs, and the conservation of historical varieties rely on the preservation and understanding of this diversity.

Anatolia provides an extensive variety of wild and cultivated grape varieties, since it is the place of grapevine origin27. Wild grapevines (V. vinifera subsp. sylvestris) in Anatolia influenced the cultivated subspecies (V. vinifera subsp. sativa)22. The genetic links of Anatolian grape varieties were poorly identified until recently28. The continental climate and extensive viticultural history in Inner Anatolia have resulted in local varieties. A total of 88 grape varieties from Central Anatolia were examined using 17 simple sequence repeats (SSR) loci, revealing a significant degree of genetic variability (9 alleles per SSR locus), which signifies considerable intra-population diversity28. Furthermore, a recent study confirmed significant intra-regional grape diversity and mixing in Turkey29. Sixty local grape genotypes from north Central Anatolia were examined using ten ISSR and seven iPBS (retrotransposon) markers29. The molecular data revealed significant diversity within the region, i.e., > 89% of the total genetic variation existed among local populations, compared to just 11% within sub-regions29. A high-throughput single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotyping analysis of 341 Turkish grapevine accessions revealed a limited genetic structure by region and a significant level of mixing among Anatolian germplasm23. The results indicated that Turkey’s grape gene pool has considerable diversity (particularly in Inner Anatolia), while mostly being interconnected. This diversity is likely a consequence of natural evolutionary processes in domestication centers and considerable human-mediated spread of grape cultivars throughout time. The findings further emphasize that morphological variation (ampelography) alone may be misleading in classifying varieties within Turkey’s grape collections. DNA markers are essential for accurately evaluating genetic diversity and relationships across significant germplasm.

It is crucial to assess the genetic diversity within local populations to efficiently preserve and exploit grape genetic resources30. Molecular markers, including ISSR (Inter-Simple Sequence Repeat) and RAPD (Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA) are extensively used for the identification and evaluation of genetic variation in plants31,32. The DNA-based approaches could clarify genetic structure and diversity between and among populations, with applications in plant breeding, evaluation of inter-species relationships, parentage analysis, and conservation biology33. The ISSR markers focus on variations in microsatellite region lengths and are efficient at identifying mutations within small or localized populations34. Similarly, RAPD markers use short arbitrary primers to amplify random DNA segments and may identify polymorphisms across the genome35. Both ISSR and RAPD markers have shown efficacy in evaluating genetic diversity in grapevines and in identifying cultivars36–39. Both approaches may be used without prior knowledge of the target organism’s genome sequence and are cost-effective. These markers are crucial for plant breeding and genetic mapping studies23,40,41. Al-Khayri et al.41 used ISSR, RAPD and SCoT markers to assess variation in Kalanchoe genotypes, highlighting their overall relevance in plant diversity.

While inner Anatolia (Sivas province) region in Turkey is not known for extensive viticulture42,43, grape cultivation exists in some regions, including Divriği, Suşehri, and Koyulhisar. Small vineyards are established on plains or hills, depending on the local geography42. These vineyards possess a range of grape genetic resources. Local farmers frequently select grapevines exhibiting superior production and quality characteristics to optimize profitability. Indigenous grape varieties often possess distinct aroma and taste, providing a considerable benefit for local winemaking and direct consumption. Preserving grape genetic variety is crucial for maintaining environmental equilibrium, avoiding the extinction of local genotypes, and ensuring sustainable farming methods for future generations.

Significant genetic diversity can be accurately characterized using ISSR and RAPD molecular markers, offering critical insights for the conservation, sustainable use, and future improvement of grapevine genetic resources in Turkey. Therefore, this study assessed genetic diversity and relationships among indigenous grapevine genotypes cultivated in the Inner Anatolia (Sivas province) using ISSR and RAPD molecular markers. The research aims to provide genetic insights necessary for efficient conservation, preservation of local genotypes, and improvement of sustainable viticulture strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and DNA isolation

Young leaves were collected from 18 different grapevine genotypes acclimatized to Divriği district in Sivas province in Inner Anatolia, Turkey. The leaves were collected from a private-owned form by taking permission from the owner. The genotypes are being cultivated for years; hence, these were identified by the author and the farm owner. There is no need for voucher specimen deposition as only leaves were collected, and genotypes are being cultivated after thorough identification. These genotypes were assigned codes from G1 to G18. A comprehensive morphological characterization of grape genotypes was performed in accordance with the standardized protocols established by IPGRI/UPOV44. The evaluated traits included leaf, berry and seed characteristics and related information are shown in Table 1. The genotypes can be divided into four different groups based on fruit skin color, i.e., yellow (‘G1’, ‘G2’, ‘G5’, ‘G6’, ‘G8’, ‘G9’, ‘G10’, ‘G12’), black (‘G3’, ‘G4’, ‘G15’), colorful (‘G7’, ‘G13’, ‘G16’), and red (‘G11’, ‘G17’, ‘G18’). Regarding leaf traits, genotypes exhibiting small, pentagonal leaves include ‘G1’, ‘G2’, ‘G3’, ‘G4’, ‘G6’, ‘G9’, ‘G10’, ‘G12’, ‘G15’, ‘G17’, and ‘G18’, whereas small, circular leaves are seen in ‘G5’, ‘G8’, and ‘G14’. Likewise, genotypes exhibiting medium, pentagonal leaves included ‘G11’ and ‘G16’, whereas those showing medium, circular leaves are ‘G7’ and ‘G13’. The genotypes are categorized according to berry size and shape into small, round berries in ‘G1’, ‘G2’, ‘G15’, ‘G17’, and ‘G18’, medium, round berries in ‘G3’, ‘G4’, ‘G7’, ‘G11’, ‘G12’, ‘G13’, ‘G14’, and ‘G16’, medium, ellipsoid berries in ‘G5’, ‘G6’, ‘G8’, ‘G9’, and ‘G10’, and large, round berries in ‘G3’, ‘G4’, and ‘G11’. In conclusion, most genotypes exhibit multi-seeded features (‘G1’, ‘G2’, ‘G3’, ‘G4’, ‘G7’, ‘G8’, ‘G9’, ‘G10’, ‘G14’, ‘G16’), while a few had few-seeded traits (‘G5’, ‘G6’, ‘G12’, ‘G13’). This categorization facilitates a precise comparison of the genotypes according to their fruit, leaf, berry, and seed characteristics. Collected leaves were immediately frozen or preserved on ice until DNA extraction.

Genomic DNA was extracted from about 100 mg of leaf tissue per genotype with a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) procedure, with minor modifications to the original method45. The concentration and purity of extracted DNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer (MaestroNano®, Maestrogen, Taiwan), and the DNA samples were diluted to a working concentration of around 5 ng/µL.

ISSR and RAPD marker analysis

A total of eight ISSR primers and eight RAPD primers were used for PCR amplification (see Table 2 for primer sequences and annealing temperatures). These primers were selected due to their effective use in grapes or similar species in previous studies46,47, with the objective of producing unique and polymorphic band patterns. Each PCR reaction (20 µL total volume) included 4 µL of template DNA (about 20 ng), 1 µL of primer, 10 µL of 2× PCR master mix (which included Taq DNA polymerase, dNTPs, Mg²⁺, and buffer), and 5 µL of nuclease-free water. PCR amplifications were conducted in a thermal cycler, starting with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at the primer-specific temperature (48–55 °C for ISSRs; 34 °C for RAPDs – refer to Table 2) for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Data scoring and statistical analysis

Each polymorphic band observed on the gels was classified as a binary character, assigned a score of “1” if present in a genotype and “0” if missing. A binary data matrix including ISSR markers (88 loci) and RAPD markers (68 loci) was created for the genotypes. The genetic similarity between each pair of genotypes was determined using Jaccard’s similarity coefficient. Pairwise genetic similarity matrices were constructed independently for ISSR and RAPD data based on these similarity values.

Cluster analysis was conducted using the unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) algorithm to produce dendrograms illustrating genetic relationships among the genotypes. Clustering was conducted using the MVSP 3.22 program50 with similarity matrices as input. A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the binary data for ISSR and RAPD markers to independently visualize the genetic relationships in a reduced dimensional space. The first two or three main components were analyzed to see genotype clustering. The dendrograms and PCA plots were examined to identify group structures within the grape genotypes.

The cophenetic correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate the accuracy of the cluster dendrograms in reflecting the similarity data. Furthermore, the ISSR and RAPD results were analyzed for concordance, specifically to see whether they exhibited analogous grouping patterns. Basic descriptive statistics (including the count of bands per primer and the proportion of polymorphic bands) were computed for each marker. All analyses were conducted with MVSP and Microsoft Excel.

Results

ISSR marker diversity and clustering

Eight ISSR primers produced 88 polymorphic DNA bands across 18 genotypes. This indicates a high degree of genetic polymorphism detectable by ISSR markers in local grape genotypes. The UPGMA dendrogram based on ISSR data consisted of two main clades (clusters) (Fig. 3). According to the ISSR-based genetic similarity matrix, the lowest pairwise similarity was 0.212 (between genotypes ‘G6’ and ‘G7’), and the highest similarity was 0.816 (between genotypes ‘G4’ and ‘G3’) (Table 3). Clade 1 of dendrogram further subdivides into two subclades. Subclade A contains genotypes ‘G9’ and ‘G10’, while Subclade B contains genotypes ‘G15’, ‘G6’, ‘G18’, ‘G16’, ‘G13’, ‘G11’, ‘G8’, ‘G5’, ‘G14’, ‘G12’, ‘G17’, ‘G4’, ‘G3’, and ‘G2’. Clade 2 comprises the remaining genotypes ‘G1’ and ‘G7’, which are clustered together separate from all others (Fig. 3). This clustering pattern suggests that ‘G1’ and ‘G7’ are the most genetically distinct among tested genotypes (as also reflected by their low similarity to others). It is notable that the ISSR clustering did not correspond to fruit skin color or other obvious traits listed in Table 1. For example, Clade 2 included genotype ‘G1’ (yellow fruit) and ‘G7’ (“colorful” fruit), whereas Clade 1 had a mix of yellow, red, black, etc. This indicates that the genetic relationships inferred by ISSR markers are not simply related to phenotypic traits, rather reflect deeper genomic differences possibly accumulated over time or due to distinct lineage origins.

RAPD marker diversity and clustering

The eight RAPD primers produced 68 polymorphic bands among tested grape genotypes. The number of bands generated by each RAPD primer varied, with primer OPA-18 producing the highest number of bands (13), indicating its significant usefulness within this sample set. The UPGMA dendrogram constructed from RAPD data identified two primary clusters, designated as Clade 1 and Clade 2 (Fig. 5). The RAPD-based similarity matrix indicated that minimum genetic similarity between any two genotypes was 0.164 (between ‘G12’ and ‘G7’), while maximum was 0.912 (between ‘G15’ and ‘G3’) (Table 4). The results are comparable to those obtained from ISSR markers; however, genotype pairings differ. In the RAPD dendrogram, Clade 1 is significant since it contains just one genotype, i.e., ‘G7’ isolated from all others. Clade 2 includes the remaining 17 genotypes and exhibits complexity, with more sub-structural elements. Clade 2 has two main subclades, A and B. Subclade A is categorized as A1, which includes just ‘G9’, and A2, which include ‘G5’, ‘G16’, ‘G14’, ‘G18’, ‘G17’, ‘G13’, ‘G6’, ‘G12’, ‘G11’, ‘G8’, ‘G4’, ‘G15’, and ‘G3’. Subclade B is categorized as B1, which comprises just ‘G10’, and B2, which includes ‘G2’ and ‘G1’. The RAPD analysis indicates that genotype ‘G7’ is significantly different, forming a distinct cluster, while genotypes ‘G1’ and ‘G2’ are grouped together at one end of the variation (subcluster B2). Furthermore, genotypes ‘G9’ and ‘G10’ appear somewhat isolated, each constituting a minor unique branch. Most of the genotypes clustered into a substantial group (A2), indicating a higher degree of similarity.

Discussion

The results indicated significant genetic diversity, with unique genetic clusters identified by both marker systems. While the molecular data revealed significant genetic variation, the phenotypic traits, i.e., fruit skin color, leaf structure, berry size, and seed characteristics did not correspond with the genetic classifications. This highlights the complex nature of grapevine genetic architecture, in which traits may not accurately represent the underlying genetic relationships, hence highlighting the significance of molecular markers in revealing the actual genetic diversity of grape genotypes. The results provide significant insights into the genetic resources of Turkish grapevines, with implications for conservation and breeding efforts.

The results obtained from ISSR data in current study are similar to previous studies of grapevine diversity36–38,40,41. Salimov et al.51 investigated ISSR-based genetic polymorphism in Azerbaijani grape genotypes and identified many unique clusters, demonstrating significant intra-specific variations. Similarly, Ikten et al.52 demonstrated the efficacy of ISSR markers in classifying grapevine genotypes into distinct genetic groups, supporting their utility in genetic diversity analyses. The identification of two main genetic clades among Divriği grape genotypes supports the idea that traditional grape populations might include genetically distinct individuals, even within the same location23. This variation may result from different ancestral origins or prolonged isolation of subpopulations22. Similar patterns of distinct genetic groupings within geographically limited grape populations have been reported in Turkish grape germplasm53. A specific cluster (Clade 2: G1 and G7) may signify a distinct germplasm, maybe an ancient local cultivar or a wild/feral lineage, that is very different from the others. These results highlight the need to examine local grape genotypes, as hidden local genotypes may include distinctive alleles.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is an efficient approach for dimensionality reduction suitable for multivariate genetic data and has been used in multiple studies of genetic variation to enhance grouping54. The PCA findings support the hypothesis that there are many main clusters of genetic diversity within the grape populations included in the current study. Salimov et al.51 revealed that ISSR-based PCA categorized grape genotypes into many groups that aligned with UPGMA dendrogram. The current study suggests that the consistency between PCA and UPGMA supports the absence of any genotype that might skew the clustering, as such distortion would be evident through discrepancies in the PCA grouping. This alignment further confirms the reliability of the observed genetic structure.

The similarities between the RAPD and ISSR clustering results are interesting. In both analyses, genotype ‘G7’ was identified as one of the most unique samples, clustering independently in RAPD and forming a small Clade 2 with genotype ‘G1’ in ISSR. Genotype ‘G1’ was further isolated, being grouped with ‘G7’ in ISSR and with ‘G2’ in RAPD. Genotypes ‘G3’ and ‘G15’ had the greatest RAPD similarity of 0.912 and were also closely associated in the ISSR dendrogram, both being within the same extensive cluster and demonstrating a reasonably high ISSR similarity of 0.75. This consistency indicates that certain genotypes are consistently genetically near (or remote) irrespective of marker type. Both ISSR and RAPD, though focusing on distinct forms of genomic polymorphism, explained the fundamental genetic architecture of studied grape genotypes. The results of the current study align with the prevailing concept that ISSR and RAPD markers often provide consistent patterns in diversity studies. Sevindik et al.55 used RAPD and ISSR to define apricot genotypes, finding that the combination of both markers yielded a reliable assessment of genetic distance. Nonetheless, there are a few differences between the ISSR and RAPD data. RAPD clustering grouped ‘G1’ and ‘G2’ together, whereas ISSR associated ‘G1’ with ‘G7’ and positioned ‘G2’ inside a larger cluster. The differences may arise from the specific genomic areas sampled by each marker. The RAPD markers may amplify distinct repetitive or coding regions that ISSRs are unable to target. The general representation of the two primary clusters stays consistent. The minor adjustments (such as the placements of ‘G1’, ‘G2’, ‘G9’, ‘G10’) need additional examination via amplification of markers or the use of more reliable markers, such as SSRs or SNPs, for validation.

The results of the current study are supported by other grapevine studies investigating several molecular markers. Dev et al.40 used RAPD and SSR markers to evaluate grape mutants, identifying that both marker types exhibited significant diversity and clearly categorized the mutants from the controls. Although SSR markers are often regarded as more repeatable and informative, RAPD markers have the benefit of targeting random genomic areas, potentially revealing variations that SSR may overlook. In the current study, RAPD markers successfully identified an outlier (‘G7’), which was also detectable by ISSR, indicating that both marker types reflected the unique characteristics of that genotype. The significant closeness between ‘G3’ and ‘G15’ in RAPD data (0.912) indicates that these two genotypes may be closely linked, either as clonal variations or siblings. Genotypes ‘G3’ and ‘G4’ showed the highest similarity of 0.816 in ISSR analysis, while ‘G3’ and ‘G15’ demonstrated a higher similarity of 0.912 in RAPD analysis. This suggests that ‘G3’ is genetically similar to both ‘G4’ and ‘G15’ by many metrics, indicating a possible relationship among all three. Similarly, G3’, ‘G4’, and ‘G15’ were grouped in the ISSR dendrogram (subclade B of Clade 1), with ‘G3’ and ‘G15’ clustered together in RAPD indicating that ‘G4’ is less closely associated with ‘G3’ in RAPD but remains within the larger cluster. Collectively, G3’, ‘G4’, ‘G15’, and maybe others like as ‘G5’ constitute a core group of genetically related genotypes. These may signify a regionally preferred genotype or a singular landrace that has undergone minimal divergence. To optimize variety in a collection, one should choose representatives from several clusters, such as include one from ‘G7’, one from ‘G1’/‘G2’, one from ‘G9’/‘G10’, and one from the ‘G3’/‘G4’/‘G15’ group, rather than selecting all from a single cluster.

Both ISSR and RAPD markers demonstrated a moderate to high degree of genetic variation among tested grape genotypes. Although the two approaches include fundamental differences, they validated one another by identifying similar genetic groups. Previous studies indicate that integrating several marker systems may provide a more thorough understanding of genetic variation. Ikten et al.52 integrated iPBS retrotransposon markers with SSRs to evaluate wild grapevines, finding that both marker types exhibited high polymorphism and were effective in elucidating population structure.

Genetic diversity within local grape genotypes is determined by numerous factors. Historical methods for cultivation, accumulation of mutations in long-lived vines, potential introgression from wild grapevines, and the clonal replication of select superior vines all contribute to the phenomenon. Divriği area lacks significance as a viticulture hub, and has mostly isolated, farmer-cultivated grape genotypes. Such lineages can accumulate somatic mutations, resulting in polymorphisms, and diverge genetically over decades or centuries. If seeds from previous generations became wild and hybridized, some sampled vines may be seedling offspring instead of clonal cuttings, significantly increasing variety. The data of current study cannot differentiate between clonal and seedling origins. Nonetheless, low similarities (e.g., 0.164 between ‘G7’ and ‘G12’ in RAPD) indicate that some are markedly dissimilar, exceeding usual clonal variants. This suggests that at least one of them may be a unique variety or a wild wine. Similarity ranges are comparable to those reported in many germplasm studies of grape diversity. Yüksel Özmen et al.56 used 15 SSR markers on 151 Turkish grape varieties, revealing genetic similarity between 0.13 and 0.96, which encompasses a broad spectrum comparable to current findings (0.164 to 0.912). Furthermore, Arnold and Schnitzler57 identified that even wild North American Vitis populations utilized as rootstocks exhibit considerable genetic structure and ecological differentiation, noting that in grapes, wild or local varieties preserve distinct genetic signatures that are of significant value.

Although ISSR and RAPD markers have advantages in assessing genetic diversity, they include limitations, including reproducibility challenges, vulnerability to experimental factors, and band dominance, which confound data interpretation58. These markers also exhibit a deficiency in codominant inheritance, limiting their use in research requiring allele frequency data59. The use of more sophisticated markers such as simple sequence repeats (SSRs), single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and start codon optimized polymorphism (SCoT) markers is advised to overcome these issues60,61. The SSRs provide significant polymorphism and facilitate the identification of both heterozygous and homozygous loci, while SNPs allow for extensive genotyping with high throughput, yielding more accurate and dependable genetic information60. The SCoT markers are recognized for their efficacy in evaluating genetic diversity in plants and provide codominant inheritance, which is especially advantageous for precise population structure and genetic mapping studies61. These sophisticated markers could complement ISSR and RAPD data, improving the accuracy and comprehensiveness of genetic studies.

Overall, ISSR and RAPD markers reveal genetic links with practical significance. The identification of two major genetic clusters by both marker methods indicates that conservation efforts for grape genotypes in Sivas must include individuals from each cluster. Genotypes ‘G1’ and ‘G7’ (Clade 2 in ISSR, Clade 1 in RAPD) exhibit significant divergence; their loss might result in the eradication of an entire lineage of genetic diversity. Simultaneously, genotypes within the extensive cluster may exhibit more interrelation, thus retaining a portion of them (as opposed to the whole) might encompass most alleles found in that group. Conventional grape germplasm collections often emphasize popular cultivars; nevertheless, this study highlights that hidden local genotypes include distinctive alleles that may be beneficial for qualities such as stress resistance and taste profiles. In situ or ex situ conservation of these local genotypes may be essential. This study may inform the prioritization of certain genotypes (e.g., one may prioritize the highly divergent genotypes such as ‘G7’, ‘G1’, ‘G9’, and ‘G10’ in a collection to optimize genetic diversity).

Conclusion

This study investigated the genetic relationships of 18 grape genotypes collected from inner Anatolia (Divriči, Sivas) using eight ISSR and eight RAPD markers. The genotypes were categorized into genetic groups with both marker techniques, demonstrating significant variation. The ISSR and RAPD analyses revealed two main clusters that were consistent across the marker techniques. The ‘G3’, ‘G4’, and ‘G15’ were often the most closely related, whereas ‘G7’, had the greatest genetic divergence. Both clustering and ordination methods grouped genetically similar genotypes together, supporting the results. Both marker systems yielded comparable results, indicating their use for genetic evaluation in the absence of SSRs or genome sequencing. Grape breeders may use this knowledge to choose genetically divergent parent genotypes from different groups to enhance heterosis or distinctive trait combinations. The findings also reveal local genotypes that represent unique genetic lineages (e.g., ‘G1’ and ‘G7’) for conservation purposes. Results indicate that morphological classification alone may not correctly represent genetic differences. Two seemingly independent grapevines may be interconnected. Therefore, integrating morphological and molecular data is essential for proper and thorough characterization of grape diversity. Although studied grape genotypes are closely related, they have significant genetic diversity. Once properly characterized and protected, this diversity may facilitate the development of novel grape varieties exhibiting enhanced traits such as stress resilience or unique quality attributes, hence promoting the genetic viability of viticulture in the region. It is recommended to use molecular markers, such as SNP, SSR or SCoT with phenotypic evaluations to enhance grapevine breeding and conservation strategies that use local genetic resources.
