Clinical prognostic value of homologous recombination repair axis markers and PARP1 expression in breast cancer: a systematic review, meta-analysis, and independent cohort validation
Authors: Kezhen Shen, Lei Cai, Wei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResistance to poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors limits durable clinical benefit in breast cancer. The translational relevance of resistance-associated genes to patient prognosis remains poorly characterized. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the clinical prognostic value of homologous recombination repair axis markers and PARP1 expression in breast cancer.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched from January 2015 to April 2026 for studies reporting survival outcomes associated with PARP inhibitor resistance-associated or homologous recombination repair-related markers. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model, with subgroup, sensitivity, and independent TCGA validation analyses.ResultsEighteen studies (13,641 patients) met inclusion criteria. Primary meta-analysis (five studies, n = 909) showed that HR repair axis positivity was associated with reduced mortality risk (HR = 0.508, 95% CI: 0.411–0.627, p < 0.0001; I2 = 0%), with consistent effects in PARPi-treated (HR = 0.458) and untreated cohorts (HR = 0.557; p for interaction = 0.363). Vote-counting of PARP1 expression (four studies) found three studies reporting high expression as a risk factor, one study protective. TCGA validation suggested an association with worse survival (HR = 1.437, p = 0.013), but this was threshold-dependent and non-significant with an upper-quartile cut-off (p = 0.504). Of note, TCGA assessed mRNA expression, which does not directly measure functional HR deficiency. Evidence for PARP1 as an adverse prognostic marker remains inconclusive. A clinical prioritization framework was developed for HR repair axis markers.ConclusionsHR repair axis positivity is associated with reduced mortality risk in breast cancer, independent of PARPi exposure. High PARP1 expression shows an inconclusive prognostic role. These findings support a conceptual distinction between functional HR deficiency and transcriptional biomarker overexpression. However, primary findings are based on limited studies (k = 5) and require prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16530-0.Keywords: Breast cancer, PARP inhibitor, Homologous recombination deficiency, Prognostic biomarker, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, accounting for approximately 26.7% of newly diagnosed cancer cases (1,959,256 new cases in women aged < 75 years) and representing a leading cause of cancer-related mortality (495,572 deaths, 17.7% of cancer deaths) in 2022 [1]. Despite significant advances in early detection and systemic therapy, therapeutic resistance continues to limit durable clinical benefit [2], particularly in aggressive subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [3], which is characterized by early relapse, visceral metastasis, and poor prognosis compared with other breast cancer subtypes [4].

Poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors have reshaped the treatment landscape for patients with homologous recombination-deficient breast cancer, especially those harboring BRCA1/2 germline mutations [5]. By exploiting synthetic lethality, PARP inhibitors selectively target DNA repair-deficient tumor cells while sparing normal tissues, offering improved specificity and a more favorable toxicity profile compared with conventional chemotherapy [6]. Beyond monotherapy, accumulating evidence suggests that PARP inhibitors can be rationally combined with epigenetic modulators or immunotherapeutic agents to enhance antitumor efficacy [7].

However, the clinical durability of PARP inhibitor therapy is frequently undermined by acquired resistance. Preclinical studies have identified multiple resistance mechanisms, including restoration of homologous recombination repair (e.g., BRCA reversion mutations, RAD51 upregulation) [8], rewiring of cell cycle checkpoints [9], activation of drug efflux pathways, and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment [10]. In response, a growing number of preclinical investigations have proposed diverse resistance-reversal strategies, ranging from epigenetic regulation (e.g., EZH2 inhibitors) [11] to immune activation (e.g., STING agonists) [12] and metabolic interventions [13].

Nevertheless, a critical gap remains in the systematic evaluation of the translational strength of these proposed strategies and, more fundamentally, the clinical prognostic relevance of the homologous recombination repair axis markers and PARP1 expression themselves. Existing narrative reviews have summarized individual resistance mechanisms, but few have systematically assessed whether genes identified in preclinical resistance models actually predict patient survival outcomes. This distinction is crucial because a gene may be mechanistically involved in PARPi resistance in vitro but have no independent prognostic value in patient cohorts, or may even show opposing prognostic directions depending on whether functional deficiency or expression level is assessed.

To address this unmet need, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 studies investigating the prognostic value of homologous recombination repair axis markers and PARP1 expression in breast cancer. Following PRISMA guidelines, we applied the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale [14] to assess risk of bias and performed meta-analyses using random-effects models with subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Critically, we integrated independent validation using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) breast cancer cohort [15] to externally verify our findings. This review aims to quantify the prognostic value of HR repair axis markers - including BRCA1/2 mutations, BRCA1 promoter methylation, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status, and BRCAness phenotype (i.e., BRCA-like genomic characteristics without underlying BRCA1/2 mutations) - in breast cancer, evaluate the prognostic role of PARP1 expression through vote-counting synthesis and TCGA validation, develop a clinical prioritization framework to guide the integration of these markers into prognostic assessment, and highlight the critical distinction between functional HR deficiency and expression-level biomarkers to inform future translational research.

By integrating evidence grading with independent validation and mechanistic interpretation, this study provides a translationally oriented framework to distinguish markers with genuine clinical potential from those supported by fragmented or low-quality data, while also identifying critical knowledge gaps for future investigation.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed across PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases from January 2015 to April 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text words related to breast cancer, PARP inhibitors, drug resistance, survival outcomes, and genetic biomarkers. The complete search strings for each database are provided in Supplementary File 1. All retrieved records were imported into reference management software, and duplicates were removed.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: human studies involving patients diagnosed with breast cancer; evaluation of PARP inhibitor resistance-associated genes or homologous recombination repair-related markers, including but not limited to BRCA1/2 mutations (germline or somatic), BRCA1 promoter methylation, HRD status, BRCAness phenotype, or expression levels of PARP1, RAD51, and other DNA repair genes; intra-cohort comparison based on gene status (e.g., high versus low expression, mutant versus wild-type, impaired versus proficient); reporting of clinical survival outcomes, including overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), progression-free survival (PFS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS), or distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS); provision of hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) or sufficient Kaplan-Meier curve data to enable HR estimation; original research articles, including cohort studies, case-control studies, post-hoc analyses of clinical trials, and single-arm stratified studies; full-text, peer-reviewed articles published in English. While these markers operate at different biological levels, they converge on a common functional endpoint: impaired homologous recombination repair. This convergence justifies their grouping as an ‘HR repair axis’ for prognostic assessment. However, these markers are not clinically interchangeable; sensitivity analyses stratified by biomarker type were performed to assess consistency across definitions. Studies utilizing publicly available transcriptomic or genomic datasets (e.g., TCGA, METABRIC, GEO) were eligible provided they included survival analysis with hazard ratio estimation and involved at least one validation cohort or a multi-cohort design with internal validation.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: preclinical studies (cell line or animal models only, without human patient data); single-arm studies without intra-cohort comparison based on gene status (e.g., descriptive reports of survival in a single gene-defined subgroup only); studies reporting only surrogate endpoints such as pathologic complete response (pCR), objective response rate (ORR), or IC50 without survival outcomes; studies lacking HR estimates or sufficient Kaplan-Meier data for HR calculation; pure bioinformatics studies without independent clinical cohort validation; studies where the gene of interest was analyzed only as part of a multi-gene signature without individual gene-level effect estimates; duplicate publications from the same cohort (the largest or most complete dataset was retained); review articles, editorials, conference abstracts, case reports, or guidelines; non-English publications; full-text unavailable after contacting authors (maximum three attempts).

Review process

Two reviewers (KZS and LC) independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant articles underwent full-text review. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The study selection process was documented following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Data extraction was performed by one reviewer (KZS) using a standardized Excel template and independently verified by another reviewer (LC) for accuracy and completeness. The following information was extracted from each eligible study: first author, publication year, study design, sample size, breast cancer subtype, gene or marker evaluated, comparison groups, association with PARP inhibitor resistance, outcome type (OS, DFS, PFS, RFS, BCSS, or DMFS), effect size (HR and 95% CI), p-value, follow-up duration, PARPi treatment status (Yes/No/Unknown), and quality assessment scores. For studies reporting only Kaplan-Meier curves without HRs, survival data were extracted using Engauge Digitizer (version 12.1), and HRs with 95% CIs were estimated using the Tierney method, which reconstructs HR from time-to-event data using observed minus expected events and variance [16].

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included cohort studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates three domains: selection of study groups (4 items), comparability of groups (1 item, up to 2 points), and ascertainment of outcome (3 items). Total scores ranged from 0 to 9, with scores of 7–9 classified as high quality, 5–6 as moderate quality, and ≤ 4 as low quality. Two reviewers independently performed quality assessments, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Synthesis methods

Meta-analysis was performed using R software (version 4.5.1) with the meta and metafor packages [17]. Due to anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and treatment contexts, a random-effects model with the DerSimonian-Laird estimator for between-study variance (τ²) was used for all pooled analyses. Effect sizes were reported as pooled HRs with 95% CIs. Weights were assigned using the inverse variance method, where each study’s weight was calculated as 1 / (σi² + τ²), giving greater weight to studies with narrower confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with I2 > 50% considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were planned a priori to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including stratification by PARPi treatment status (PARPi-treated versus untreated cohorts). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the leave-one-out approach, sequentially excluding each study to assess the robustness of pooled estimates. For the PARP1 analysis, a vote-counting approach was employed due to the limited number of studies and absence of meta-analyzable data, summarizing the proportion of studies reporting risk versus protective effects [18]. Detailed meta-analysis outputs, including primary, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses, are provided in Supplementary File 3.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Given the limited number of studies in the primary meta-analysis (k = 5), we acknowledge that I² has low statistical power to detect true heterogeneity, and its confidence interval is wide. Therefore, the observed I² of 0% should be interpreted as an absence of detectable heterogeneity rather than definitive evidence of homogeneity. Notably, the leave-one-out sensitivity analyses (Sect. 3.4) yielded consistent pooled HRs across all iterations, supporting the conclusion that substantial heterogeneity was not present in these data. In the absence of statistical evidence for heterogeneity, the random-effects model effectively reduces to a fixed-effects model, but the former was retained for consistency with the primary analysis plan. Consistent with Cochrane guidance, we also present τ² estimates and prediction intervals where appropriate.

Assessment of publication bias

Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plot asymmetry. Formal statistical tests for publication bias (e.g., Egger’s test) were not performed due to the limited number of included studies in the primary meta-analysis (k = 5), as such tests require a minimum of 10 studies for reliable estimation [19]. This limitation is explicitly acknowledged in the interpretation of the results.

TCGA validation

Independent validation was performed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) breast cancer (BRCA) cohort. Gene expression data (RNA-seq, HTSeq-FPKM-UQ) and corresponding clinical survival data were obtained from the TCGA database. For each target gene (PARP1, BRCA1, BRCA2), patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression level. The median cut-off was chosen as it is a standard approach for initial exploratory analyses in the absence of clinically validated thresholds, ensuring balanced group sizes for statistical comparison. To assess the robustness of findings to cut-off selection, a sensitivity analysis using the upper quartile (75th percentile) as an alternative threshold was also performed, as reported in Sect. 3.7. Overall survival was compared between groups using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to estimate HRs with 95% CIs. All TCGA analyses were conducted using R software with the survival and survminer packages.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 181 records were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates (n = 3), 178 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 130 were excluded for not meeting eligibility criteria. Full-text review was conducted for 48 articles, and 30 were excluded for the following reasons: no internal control comparison (n = 8), no OS/PFS/DDFS/BCSS outcome (n = 12), no HR or 95% CI provided (n = 6) and preclinical study (n = 4). Finally, 18 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1) [20–37].

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection

The 18 included studies comprised 13 retrospective cohort studies, 2 post-hoc clinical trial analyses, 1 phase II trial subgroup analysis, 1 multi-cohort bioinformatics study, and 1 small case series (n = 7). Sample sizes ranged from 7 to 6,351 patients, with a total of 13,641 patients across all studies. Six studies (33.3%) involved PARPi-treated cohorts, while 12 studies (66.7%) were conducted in observational cohorts without PARPi exposure. Regarding molecular pathways, 10 studies (55.6%) focused on HR repair/BRCA axis markers, 4 studies (22.2%) on PARP1-related markers, 2 studies (11.1%) on epigenetic/transcriptional regulators (Jab1, LSD1), and 2 studies (11.1%) on novel markers (CXorf56, FLT1). The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale scores ranged from 5 to 8, with 10 studies (55.6%) classified as high quality (NOS ≥ 7) and 8 studies (44.4%) as moderate quality (NOS 5–6). HR repair axis and overall survival: primary meta-analysis

Five studies reported overall survival (OS) outcomes for HR repair axis markers, including BRCA1/2 germline or somatic mutations, BRCA1 promoter methylation, HRD status, and BRCAness phenotype [21, 23, 24, 33, 36]. All studies compared patients with HR repair axis positivity (impaired/deficient/mutation-positive) versus those without (proficient/wild-type).

The pooled analysis using a random-effects model demonstrated that HR repair axis positivity was associated with a significantly reduced risk of death (HR = 0.508, 95% CI: 0.411–0.627, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2A). Heterogeneity was minimal (I2 = 0%, p = 0.66), indicating high consistency across studies. Individual study HRs ranged from 0.380 to 0.620, all favoring the HR repair axis-positive group (Table 2). The complete input data for the primary meta-analysis are provided in Table S1. Given the limited number of studies (k = 5) and the dominant weight of the Fasching et al. cohort (52% of total patients), these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require confirmation in larger prospective datasets.

Subgroup analysis by PARPi treatment status

Subgroup analysis stratified by PARPi treatment status showed that the protective effect of HR repair axis markers was present in both clinical contexts. In PARPi-treated cohorts (k = 2, n = 82), the pooled HR was 0.458 (95% CI: 0.336–0.622) [24, 33]. In untreated cohorts (k = 3, n = 827), the pooled HR was 0.557 (95% CI: 0.416–0.747) [21, 23, 36]. The test for subgroup differences was not significant (p = 0.363), indicating that the prognostic value of HR repair axis markers is not dependent on PARPi treatment exposure (Fig. 2B). Both subgroups showed no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0% for both). However, the PARPi-treated subgroup analysis was based on only two studies (n = 82), and these findings require validation in larger prospective cohorts.

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the primary findings. After sequentially excluding each study, the pooled HRs ranged from 0.447 (after excluding Brianese et al.) to 0.528 (after excluding Xu et al.), all remaining statistically significant (p < 0.0001 for all iterations). The I2 remained 0% across all leave-one-out iterations, indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate (Fig. 3A).

Given that the Fasching et al. study [36] contributed 471 of the 909 patients (52%) in the primary meta-analysis, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding this study to assess its influence on the pooled estimate. After excluding Fasching et al., the pooled HR for the remaining four studies was 0.518 (95% CI: 0.416–0.646, p < 0.0001), which is nearly identical to the primary pooled HR of 0.508 (Table S2). Heterogeneity remained undetectable (I² = 0%), though the 95% confidence interval for I² was wide (0% − 84.7%). We also performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to studies with high methodological quality (NOS ≥ 7). Three of the five studies met this criterion [21, 24, 36]. The pooled HR for these high-quality studies was 0.420 (95% CI: 0.280–0.632, p < 0.0001), indicating a stronger protective association than in the primary analysis (Table S2). Again, I² was estimated at 0%, but with a wide confidence interval (0% − 89.6%).

To assess consistency across different definitions of HR repair axis positivity, we stratified studies by biomarker type (germline BRCA1/2 mutation, HRD score, BRCA1 promoter methylation, BRCAness, and Mutational Signature 3). All comparisons between HR-deficient and HR-proficient status reported HRs below 1.0, with no directionally opposite findings (Supplementary Table S7).

These additional analyses demonstrate that the protective association of HR repair axis positivity is not driven by the largest study or by studies of lower methodological quality. However, the wide confidence intervals for I² underscore the limited statistical power to reliably assess heterogeneity with only five studies.

PARP1 expression: vote-counting synthesis

Four studies evaluated the prognostic value of PARP1 expression in breast cancer [20, 22, 26, 37]. Three of four studies (75%) reported that high PARP1 expression was associated with worse survival, with HRs ranging from 1.183 (95% CI: 1.004–1.394) to 1.730 (95% CI: 1.203–2.488). One study (Ghasemi et al., 2025) reported a protective effect in specific subgroups (HR = 0.599, 95% CI: 0.442–0.812 for OS in TNBC). The consistency in the direction of effect (three of four studies favoring risk) supports a potential adverse prognostic role of PARP1 overexpression in breast cancer (Fig. 3B). The complete dataset for this vote-counting synthesis is provided in TableS3. However, the limited number of studies (k = 4) and the presence of conflicting findings (one protective, three risk) preclude definitive conclusions. Furthermore, as described below (Sect. 3.7), the TCGA association was threshold-dependent and lost statistical significance when an upper-quartile cut-off was applied. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Exploratory analysis of non-OS outcomes

Four studies reported non-OS outcomes for HR repair axis markers. Wang et al. (2024) [21] reported that HRD positivity was associated with improved DMFS (HR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.26–0.90) and OS (HR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.20–0.99). Batalini et al. (2022) [31] demonstrated that Mutational Signature 3 positivity predicted better PFS (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.098–0.77) in PARPi-treated cohorts. In contrast, Jahan et al. (2024) [35] reported that non-BRCA HRR mutations (ATM, CDKN2A) were associated with significantly worse time-to-treatment failure compared to germline BRCA mutations (HR = 11.68, 95% CI: 1.62–84.19). Sueta et al. (2022) [34] found that post-treatment BRCA1-like phenotype was associated with worse BCSS (HR = 5.67, 95% CI: 1.19–29.3). Due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome definitions and comparison groups, these studies were not pooled quantitatively (Figure S1). The detailed data for this exploratory analysis are provided in Table S4.

TCGA Validation

Independent validation using the TCGA-BRCA cohort (n = 1,214 patients with complete survival data) confirmed the prognostic value of PARP1 expression. Of note, the TCGA cohort did not receive PARPi therapy; therefore, the following analyses assess general prognostic associations rather than PARPi resistance-specific predictive value. High PARP1 expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival compared to low expression (HR = 1.437, 95% CI: 1.079–1.912, p = 0.013; Fig. 3C; Table 3). The median follow-up time was approximately 40 months. For BRCA1 and BRCA2, no significant associations with overall survival were observed in this cohort (BRCA1: HR = 0.981, 95% CI: 0.742–1.297, p = 0.876; BRCA2: HR = 1.112, 95% CI: 0.840–1.470, p = 0.458; Figure S2, Table 3).

To assess whether the observed association between high PARP1 expression and worse overall survival was dependent on the choice of median cut-off, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the upper quartile (75th percentile) of PARP1 expression as the threshold (cut-off value = 12.80 FPKM-UQ). In this analysis, high expression was defined as expression above the 75th percentile (Q4, n = 304) and low expression as the remaining patients (Q1-Q3, n = 910). When patients were stratified by the upper quartile (Q4, n = 304 vs. Q1 - Q3, n = 910), high PARP1 expression showed a trend toward worse overall survival, but the association did not reach statistical significance (HR = 1.121, 95% CI: 0.801–1.569, p = 0.504; Figure S2C). Thus, the significant association observed with the median cut-off (Fig. 3C) was not replicated with the more stringent upper-quartile threshold. This discrepancy may reflect that the adverse prognostic signal is driven by a broad subset of patients with moderately elevated PARP1 expression rather than exclusively by extreme overexpressors, or it may be due to reduced statistical power in the smaller high-expression group (n = 304 vs. 607 in the median split). Future studies should systematically evaluate optimal PARP1 expression thresholds using maximally selected rank statistics or time-dependent ROC analysis in larger, independent cohorts.

Qualitative synthesis of novel markers

Five studies investigated novel or less-characterized markers that could not be quantitatively synthesized. Tai et al. (2024) [25] reported that high FLT1 expression was associated with shorter PFS (p = 0.012 for pFLT1; p = 0.005 for total FLT1) in a small PARPi-treated cohort (n = 10). Zhu et al. (2023) [28] demonstrated that high CXorf56 expression was independently associated with worse OS (HR = 3.658, 95% CI: 1.694–7.894, p = 0.001) and promoted HR repair and PARPi resistance mechanistically. Peng et al. (2025) [29] showed that high Jab1 expression was associated with worse OS in both overall (HR = 1.471, 95% CI: 1.196–1.806) and TNBC (HR = 1.407, 95% CI: 1.100–1.796) populations. Nagasawa et al. (2015) [30] reported that high LSD1 expression was associated with worse RFS (HR = 3.688, 95% CI: 1.232–11.040, p = 0.020). Vidula et al. (2020) [27] found no significant survival difference based on cfDNA-detected BRCA1/2 mutations, although in vitro functional assays showed PARPi sensitivity in BRCA1-mutant CTCs. These findings are summarized in Table S5.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot showed that four of the five studies were located to the left of the pooled effect estimate, while one study (Brianese et al.) was located to the right (Fig. 3D). This asymmetry may reflect small-study effects, publication bias, or true biological heterogeneity, though definitive interpretation is precluded by the small number of studies. Formal statistical tests for publication bias (e.g., Egger’s test) were not performed due to the limited number of studies in the primary meta-analysis (k = 5), as such tests require a minimum of 10 studies for reliable estimation. This limitation is acknowledged in the interpretation of the findings.

Clinical prioritization of markers

Based on the integrated evidence from meta-analysis, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and TCGA validation, we summarized the current evidence status and identified research priorities for HR repair axis and PARP1-related markers (Table 4). BRCA1/2 mutations were supported by moderate-level evidence from a five-study meta-analysis and TCGA validation, with consistent protective associations across cohorts; further validation in diverse populations is a moderate research priority. HRD score and BRCA1 promoter methylation showed promising protective signals but were each supported by only a single study (low evidence level), warranting high research priority for prospective validation. PARP1 expression demonstrated conflicting evidence (low-moderate level), with vote-counting synthesis and TCGA validation suggesting an adverse prognostic role in untreated cohorts; standardization of detection methods is a moderate research priority before further prognostic validation. Novel markers (CXorf56, Jab1, LSD1, FLT1) were supported by insufficient evidence and remain hypothesis-generating only, with low research priority until independent validation is available.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis integrated evidence from 18 studies (13,641 patients) with independent TCGA validation. The primary analysis (five studies, n = 909) showed that HR repair axis positivity (BRCA1/2 mutations, BRCA1 promoter methylation, HRD status, or BRCAness phenotype) was associated with significantly reduced mortality risk (HR = 0.508, 95% CI: 0.411–0.627, p < 0.0001; I² = 0%). This protective effect was consistent across PARPi-treated (HR = 0.458) and untreated cohorts (HR = 0.557), indicating that the prognostic value of HR repair deficiency is independent of PARPi exposure. However, the primary meta-analysis included only five studies, and the pooled estimate was substantially influenced by a single large cohort (Fasching et al., n = 471, 52% of total weight). Sensitivity analyses excluding this study yielded consistent results (HR = 0.518, p < 0.0001; Table S2), but the limited evidence base precludes definitive conclusions. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

In contrast, PARP1 expression showed a conflicting prognostic pattern. Vote-counting synthesis (four studies) found that three studies reported high PARP1 expression as a risk factor for worse survival (HR range: 1.183–1.730), while one study reported a protective effect. TCGA validation suggested an association with worse overall survival (HR = 1.437, p = 0.013), but this association was threshold-dependent and non-significant with an upper-quartile cut-off (p = 0.504). Given the limited number of studies and conflicting results, the evidence for an adverse prognostic role of PARP1 expression remains inconclusive.

Based on the integrated evidence, we propose that homologous recombination repair deficiency functions primarily as a treatment-agnostic prognostic marker in breast cancer, conferring a survival advantage through inherent chemosensitivity rather than serving solely as a PARP inhibitor-specific predictive biomarker. We term this conceptual distinction, whereby functional HR deficiency (protective) and transcriptional overexpression of DNA repair genes (risk) exhibit opposing prognostic directions, the “functional–transcriptional biomarker dichotomy.” This framework underscores the importance of distinguishing what a biomarker measures (functional capacity versus expression level) from what it predicts (prognosis versus therapeutic response). A comprehensive summary of all analyses is presented in Table S6.

A conceptual distinction warrants emphasis: the favorable prognosis associated with HR repair axis positivity may reflect, at least in part, enhanced sensitivity to conventional DNA-damaging chemotherapy (which all patients received) rather than intrinsically less aggressive tumor biology. Thus, HR repair axis markers should be interpreted as prognostic of better outcomes in the context of standard chemotherapy, not merely predictive of PARPi response. This distinction is critical: a prognostic marker informs overall disease trajectory, while a predictive marker identifies differential benefit from a specific therapy. The observation that the protective effect was present in both PARPi-treated and untreated cohorts (p for interaction = 0.363) supports the interpretation of HR deficiency as a treatment-agnostic prognostic marker, though formal testing of predictive effects would require randomized trials with a non-treatment arm.

Mechanistic insights and biological plausibility

The protective effect of HR repair axis positivity is mechanistically well-grounded. BRCA1/2 deficiency or HRD compromises the homologous recombination repair pathway, the primary high-fidelity mechanism for DNA double-strand break repair [38]. This deficiency creates synthetic lethality when combined with DNA-damaging agents (e.g., platinum-based chemotherapy) or PARP inhibitors, as tumor cells become unable to repair therapy-induced DNA damage [39]. Consequently, patients with HR-deficient tumors exhibit improved responses to such therapies, translating into better survival outcomes. Notably, the protective effect does not require PARPi exposure; it is also observed in patients receiving conventional DNA-damaging chemotherapy (anthracyclines, taxanes, or platinum agents) [40], to which HR-deficient tumors are intrinsically more sensitive. Thus, HR repair axis markers function as prognostic indicators in the context of standard chemotherapy, with the added property of predicting incremental benefit from PARP inhibition.

The discordant findings for PARP1 expression warrant careful biological interpretation. PARP1 is a multifunctional enzyme involved in DNA damage detection, base excision repair, and PARylation signaling [41]. High PARP1 expression may reflect several underlying biological states. First, it is associated with high proliferative index. A large-scale analysis of 6,351 breast cancer patients from three independent cohorts (TCGA, METABRIC, SCAN-B) demonstrated that high PARP1 expression correlates strongly with increased cell cycle pathway activity and proliferation markers [26], explaining in part the risk signal observed in our TCGA validation. Second, PARP1 upregulation may represent a compensatory response to genomic instability. Highly proliferative tumors experience excessive DNA replication stress, necessitating enhanced DNA repair capacity [42]. PARP1 upregulation reflects the tumor’s adaptive response to maintain genomic integrity, though this same adaptation may promote cell survival under therapeutic pressure. Third, the prognostic meaning of PARP1 expression is treatment-context dependent. In untreated or conventionally treated cohorts (such as TCGA), high PARP1 expression identifies tumors with aggressive biology and worse outcomes [43]. In PARPi-treated cohorts, PARP1 may serve as a predictive biomarker for synthetic lethality in HR-deficient tumors. This context-dependent duality explains why PARP1 expression has been reported as both a risk factor (in unselected cohorts) and a predictor of PARPi sensitivity (in BRCA-mutated, PARPi-treated cohorts).

Importantly, PARP1 overexpression does not equate to HR deficiency - in fact, PARP1 is often upregulated in HR-proficient tumors as part of compensatory DNA repair activation [26, 44]. This biological distinction explains why high PARP1 expression was associated with worse prognosis in our TCGA validation, where most patients did not receive PARPi therapy and had intact HR repair function [45].

The translational gap between HR repair deficiency (protective) and PARP1 overexpression (risk) highlights a critical insight: functional deficiency (assessed by mutation, methylation, or integrated HRD score) is fundamentally different from expression level (assessed by IHC or RNA-seq). The former indicates a targetable vulnerability, while the latter may simply reflect aggressive tumor biology. This conceptual framework is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Comparison with prior research

Our findings align with previous studies demonstrating that BRCA1/2 germline mutations confer better prognosis in triple-negative breast cancer, particularly in early-onset populations. A large-scale meta-analysis of 60 studies (105,220 patients) reported that BRCA1/2 mutation carriers with TNBC had significantly improved overall survival compared with non-carriers (HR 0.49, 95% CI: 0.26–0.92) [46], remarkably consistent with our finding of a 49.2% mortality risk reduction (HR 0.508, 95% CI: 0.411–0.627). At the individual study level, Brianese et al. (2018) [23] first reported that BRCA1 impairment (by germline mutation or promoter methylation) was associated with improved survival in young TNBC patients (≤ 40 years), a finding confirmed in our pooled analysis. Similarly, Wang et al. (2024) [21] demonstrated that HRD positivity predicted better DMFS and OS in carboplatin-treated TNBC, supporting the concept that HR deficiency sensitizes tumors to DNA-damaging agents beyond PARP inhibitors.

However, our study extends prior knowledge in several important ways. Previous meta-analyses focused on BRCA1/2 mutations in isolation or provided limited integration of HRD markers in breast cancer [47]; we integrated multiple HR repair axis markers (BRCA1/2 mutations, BRCA1 methylation, HRD score, BRCAness phenotype), providing a more comprehensive assessment of HR deficiency as a unified prognostic concept. Whereas prior meta-analyses evaluated PARP inhibitor efficacy in treated populations [48], we directly compared PARPi-treated and untreated cohorts, demonstrating that the protective effect is not dependent on PARPi exposure, a clinically relevant finding for patient stratification. Additionally, we provided independent TCGA validation for PARP1 expression, revealing a prognostic pattern opposite to that of HR repair deficiency, which has not been systematically addressed in prior literature.

The prognostic value of PARP1 expression remains controversial. Some studies have reported that high PARP1 expression correlates with worse outcomes, consistent with our findings, while others have suggested a protective role in specific contexts. This controversy likely arises from differences in patient populations, treatment backgrounds, and lack of standardized cut-offs for PARP1 IHC scoring. Our vote-counting synthesis synthesized evidence from four independent studies and addressed a gap in the systematic evaluation of PARP1 expression as a prognostic marker, revealing a consistent risk-associated role (three of four studies reporting adverse prognostic effects).

Clinical implications and translational potential

Our findings have several direct clinical implications. HR repair axis markers, particularly BRCA1/2 mutations and HRD score, may be considered for prognostic assessment in TNBC and early-onset breast cancer patients, though prospective validation is needed before routine clinical implementation. The consistent protective effect across multiple cohorts suggests that HR-deficient tumors have inherently better prognosis, which raises the hypothesis that treatment de-escalation strategies might be considered for responding patients, though this requires prospective validation. The prognostic value of HR repair axis markers is independent of PARPi treatment, meaning that even without PARPi therapy, HR-deficient patients have better outcomes due to enhanced sensitivity to conventional DNA-damaging chemotherapy. This finding supports HRD testing as a general prognostic tool, not merely a predictive biomarker for PARPi therapy. Clinicians should recognize that the favorable prognosis associated with HR repair axis markers reflects enhanced chemosensitivity rather than indolent tumor biology; these markers are prognostic in the context of DNA-damaging chemotherapy (treatment-agnostic) and may have additional predictive value for PARPi benefit, but the two concepts should not be conflated.

PARP1 expression should not be used as a standalone prognostic marker in clinical practice. A critical barrier is the lack of standardized detection methods: the four studies in our vote-counting synthesis used different cut-off definitions (IHC 4-tier scoring, H-score > 10, median expression, H-score > 200). Until standardization is achieved through automated quantitative IHC, validated cut-offs via ROC analysis, and external quality assessment schemes, PARP1 IHC should remain an investigational tool rather than a clinical-grade assay. Clinicians should exercise caution when interpreting PARP1 IHC results for prognostic or predictive purposes.

Based on the integrated evidence, we identified research priorities for future investigation (Table 4). HRD score and BRCA1 promoter methylation represent the highest priority for prospective validation given their promising but preliminary single-study signals. BRCA1/2 mutations, while supported by more consistent evidence, require validation in more diverse populations. Standardization of PARP1 detection methods is a prerequisite for further prognostic evaluation of this marker.

Beyond these specific markers, a broader translational barrier highlighted by this study is the pervasive conflation of distinct biomarker concepts in the PARP inhibitor resistance literature. Our findings underscore the critical importance of distinguishing between functional deficiency (assessed by mutation, methylation, or genomic scar scores) versus expression level (assessed by IHC or bulk RNA-seq), and between prognostic value (outcome independent of therapy) versus predictive value (differential benefit from a specific therapy). The observation that HR deficiency confers a protective prognosis regardless of PARPi exposure, while high PARP1 expression identifies aggressive biology in untreated cohorts, illustrates how failure to disentangle these axes can lead to seemingly contradictory findings and impede clinical translation. We propose that the “functional-transcriptional biomarker dichotomy” described here may serve as a generalizable framework for interpreting and designing prognostic biomarker studies in oncology, particularly when the biomarker of interest can be assessed through both functional and expression-based assays.

Heterogeneity considerations

While the primary meta-analysis showed minimal statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), several sources of clinical heterogeneity warrant discussion. The definition of HR repair axis positivity varied across studies: Brianese et al. [23] used BRCA1 germline mutation or promoter methylation (> 16.1%); Wang et al. [21] used HRD score ≥ 50 or BRCA1/2 mutation; Kawachi et al. [24] used BRCA1 methylation > 80%; Batalini et al. [31] used Mutational Signature 3. Despite these differences, the direction of effect was remarkably consistent (all HRs < 1), suggesting that diverse HR deficiency definitions converge on the same biological phenomenon. Differences in chemotherapy backgrounds (anthracycline/taxane vs. platinum-based) may influence the magnitude of the protective effect, as platinum agents exploit HR deficiency more directly. The lack of significant subgroup difference between PARPi-treated and untreated cohorts (p for interaction = 0.363) suggests that the prognostic value of HR repair axis markers is robust across treatment contexts, though the point estimate favored PARPi-treated cohorts (HR = 0.458 vs. 0.557), potentially reflecting added benefit from PARPi therapy in HR-deficient tumors. Sensitivity analyses stratified by biomarker type confirmed consistent protective signals across germline BRCA1/2 mutation (pooled HR = 0.48), HRD score, BRCA1 methylation, and Mutational Signature 3, though estimates for non-germline markers were derived from single studies and carry greater uncertainty.

Limitations of the Evidence

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the primary meta-analysis included only five studies reporting OS outcomes for HR repair axis markers. This small number of studies limits the statistical power of the meta-analysis and precludes formal publication bias testing. Furthermore, the Fasching et al. study [36] contributed 471 of the 909 total patients (52%), substantially influencing the pooled estimate. While sensitivity analyses excluding this study and restricting to high-quality studies yielded consistent results (Table S2), the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and requiring confirmation in larger, prospective datasets. The low power for detecting heterogeneity also means the observed I² of 0% should be interpreted with caution, as its confidence interval is wide with only five studies.

Second, substantial heterogeneity existed in the definitions of HR repair axis positivity across studies, including different thresholds for BRCA1 methylation (e.g., > 16.1% in Brianese et al.), HRD scores (e.g., ≥ 50 in Wang et al.), and BRCAness phenotyping methods. This conceptual heterogeneity, while statistically justified by the consistent direction of effect, may obscure biological nuances regarding the degree or mechanism of HR impairment. The pooled estimate should therefore be interpreted as reflecting a general protective signal associated with various forms of HR dysfunction, rather than a precise effect size for any single definition. None of the included studies directly compared multiple HR repair axis markers within the same cohort, so the concordance between different definitions and their relative prognostic performance remain unknown. Future studies should prioritize head-to-head comparisons of different HRD assays.

Third, the TCGA validation cohort did not receive PARPi therapy, meaning our validation for PARP1 expression reflects a general prognostic association rather than PARPi resistance-specific predictive value [49]. Only mRNA expression levels were analyzed; we could not assess functional HR deficiency or PARP1 enzymatic activity. The choice of median expression as the cut-off, while standard for exploratory analysis, is arbitrary. Our sensitivity analysis using the upper quartile attenuated the risk signal (HR = 1.121, 95% CI: 0.801–1.569, p = 0.504), which may reflect either a true biological phenomenon (adverse prognosis driven by a broader subset rather than extreme overexpressors) or reduced statistical power due to the smaller high-expression group (n = 304). Future studies should systematically evaluate optimal PARP1 thresholds using maximally selected rank statistics or time-dependent ROC analysis.

Fourth, several included studies had moderate methodological quality (NOS 5–6), with common limitations including lack of blinding in outcome assessment and incomplete reporting of statistical methods. However, sensitivity analysis excluding lower-quality studies did not materially change the results. Beyond funnel plot inspection, several factors suggest potential publication bias. All five studies in the primary meta-analysis reported HRs below 1.0, with no null or opposite-direction effects, raising the possibility of selective publication of favorable results. Small sample sizes in some studies (e.g., Kawachi et al., n = 20; Tai et al., n = 10; Walsh et al., n = 7) increase the risk of effect size overestimation due to small-study effects. Additionally, we could not assess unpublished negative studies, as prospective registration of observational biomarker studies is not standard practice.

Fifth, the exploratory analysis of non-OS outcomes (DMFS, PFS, TTF, BCSS) revealed mixed findings that could not be quantitatively pooled due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome definitions and comparison groups. These results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Sixth, the qualitative synthesis of novel markers (FLT1, CXorf56, Jab1, LSD1) was based on single studies with small sample sizes, precluding any firm conclusions about their prognostic value. Finally, most included studies were conducted in predominantly White or Asian populations, with limited representation of African, Hispanic, and other ethnic groups. The generalizability of our findings to diverse populations requires further investigation.

Future directions

Several priority areas for future research emerge from this study. Prospective multi-center cohort studies with standardized HRD assessment protocols are needed to validate the prognostic cut-offs for BRCA1 methylation and HRD scores. The prognostic value of HR repair axis markers in non-TNBC subtypes (e.g., HR+/HER2- breast cancer) remains underexplored and warrants investigation. Dynamic changes in HR repair function during treatment, including the emergence of BRCA reversion mutations as a resistance mechanism, should be characterized using longitudinal ctDNA monitoring. The biological mechanisms underlying the opposing prognostic directions of HR repair deficiency and PARP1 overexpression merit further mechanistic investigation; spatial transcriptomics and single-cell analyses could elucidate whether PARP1 overexpression in HR-proficient tumors is associated with specific microenvironmental features or clonal subpopulations. The novel markers identified in this review (CXorf56, Jab1, LSD1, FLT1) require independent validation in larger cohorts before any clinical translation can be considered.

Given the limited ethnic diversity in current evidence, international collaborative efforts are urgently needed to characterize the prognostic value of HR repair axis markers in underrepresented populations. Of the 18 included studies, 12 were conducted in East Asian (Chinese, Korean, Japanese) or White (European, North American) populations, with only one study from Brazil (mixed ethnicity) and none from African or indigenous populations. This is particularly concerning because BRCA mutation spectra and HRD prevalence vary significantly across ethnic groups [50]. For example, founder mutations in BRCA1/2 differ between Ashkenazi Jewish, Icelandic, and African populations [51], and the frequency of BRCA1 promoter methylation may vary by ancestry [52]. Future studies should prioritize recruitment of diverse cohorts, including African, Hispanic, Southeast Asian, and Indigenous populations, to ensure equitable generalizability of prognostic biomarkers. Finally, future systematic reviews in this field should consider individual patient data meta-analysis (IPD Meta) [53] to enable more precise subgroup analyses and time-dependent effect modeling.

Hypothesis-generating clinical considerations

Based on the integrated evidence, we offer the following hypothesis-generating considerations for future research and prospective validation. For patients with early-onset TNBC (≤ 40 years) or those with a strong family history, BRCA1/2 germline testing and HRD assessment may identify individuals with better prognosis, but this hypothesis requires prospective validation before clinical implementation. The observation that HR repair axis positivity is associated with better prognosis raises the possibility that HR-deficient patients might be candidates for treatment de-escalation strategies, though this remains an exploratory concept requiring prospective validation. PARP1 IHC should not be used as a standalone prognostic marker outside of standardized research protocols, as its potential clinical utility requires validation in PARPi-treated cohorts with uniform cut-off values. Novel markers including CXorf56, Jab1, LSD1, and FLT1 remain hypothesis-generating and should not guide clinical decisions until independently validated in large-scale, multi-center cohorts.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides consistent evidence that HR repair axis positivity is associated with favorable survival outcomes in breast cancer, independent of PARP inhibitor exposure, though this finding is based on a limited number of studies (k = 5) and requires prospective validation. These findings support a “functional - transcriptional biomarker dichotomy,” wherein functional HR deficiency confers a protective prognosis, while transcriptional overexpression of PARP1 identifies aggressive tumor biology with adverse prognosis. It is important to note that TCGA validation assessed mRNA expression levels, which do not directly measure functional HR deficiency or PARPi resistance biology. This conceptual distinction has important implications for biomarker interpretation: what a marker measures (functional capacity versus expression level) is fundamentally distinct from what it predicts (prognosis versus therapeutic response). Future prospective studies with standardized HRD assessment protocols and diverse population representation are warranted to validate this framework and refine the clinical utility of these markers.
