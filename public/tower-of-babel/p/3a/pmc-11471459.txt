Safety Evaluation and antioxidant potential of new probiotic strain Bacillus subtilis (NMCC-path-14) in Balb/c mice by sub-acute repeated dose toxicity
Authors: Muhammad Usama Mazhar, Sadaf Naz, Jehan Zeb Khan, Sharjeel Khalid, Shakira Ghazanfar, Samy Selim, Muhammad Khalid Tipu, Sumel Ashique, Sabina Yasmin, Mohammed S Almuhayawi, Aziza Alshahrani, Mohammad Yousuf Ansari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProbiotics have recently gained significant interest for their possible therapeutic effects in treating numerous health conditions. Probiotics containing Bacillus subtilis have been shown to have several health benefits, most notably in preventing diarrhea and gastrointestinal problems. A novel probiotic strain, Bacillus subtilis (NMCC-path-14), isolated from the rumen of a Nilli Ravi Buffalo, was evaluated for 28-day repeated dose toxicity in Balb/c mice. The NMCC-path-14 in low dose (1 × 108 CFU/ml) and high dose (1 × 1010 CFU/ml) was administered to the mice through gavage regularly. After 28 days of treatment, it was discovered that the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) for NMCC-path-14 wasgreater than 1 × 1010 CFU/animal/day. This study also revealed no treatment-related changes in clinical parameters, body weight, gross pathology, or histology. Food consumption, hemoglobin, hematocrit, red blood cell counts, and colon length increased, while total/differential leukocyte count and platelets remained unchanged. The administration of NMCC-path-14 also resulted in decreased bilirubin and creatinine levels. Furthermore, NMCC-path-14 also displayed a promising antioxidant potential by increasing the antioxidant enzymes (GST, GSH, and CAT) and decreasing oxidant enzyme (MDA and NO) levels in vital organs like the liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon. TheNMCC-path-14also decreased the pathogenic bacterial population while increasing the beneficial population. Given the lack of adverse effects observed after NMCC-path-14 treatment, this strain is safe and must be considered as a potential probiotic in humans.Keywords: Bacillus subtilis, Probiotic safety study, Repeated dose toxicity, Antioxidant, Colon length, Feed intake

Introduction

The intestinal microbiota has a crucial role in maintaining the host's health since they are involved in various tasks related to nutrition, immunity, and physiology. An imbalance in the microbiota of the gut can lead to several chronic conditions, including inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, cancer, and autism [1]. Probiotics are living microorganisms that, when given in enough amounts, provide a beneficial impact on the health of the host [2]. Various strains of probioticsappear to affect the immune system (like B. subtilisDE111® [3]and BS-Z15 [4]) and reduce intestinal permeability (L. fermentum MTCC-5898 [5]B. subtilisBs 29784 [6]); however, the exact mechanisms by which they do so are still unknown [7]. Probiotics have been utilized in various food products and are recommended as a dietary supplement or treatment for different conditions due to their health benefits [8]. Recent research has expanded the use of probiotics beyond their traditional applications. They are now being explored for their potential in managing lifestyle diseases like metabolic syndrome (such as obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease), influencing the activity of the brain, and exhibiting anti-cancer properties [9,10].

Bacillus, a Gram-positive bacterium, has been shown to have positive probiotic effects. They exhibit pathogen exclusion, antioxidant, antibacterial, immunomodulatory, food fermentation [11], and general improvement of gut flora (by increasing Lactobacillusspp and, at the same time, decreasing the pathogenic bacteria like C. perfringens and E. coli [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Due to its widespread distribution, Bacillus bacteria can enter the human intestine via water, food, or air. Humans harbor a substantial quantity of Bacillus species, averaging 105 colony-forming units (CFU) per gram [16]. Species from the Bacillus genus, e.g.,B. coagulans, B. clausii,B. subtilis, andB. licheniformis, are some of the most commonly used probiotics [11,17]. Multiple studies have assessed the in vivo toxicity of Bacillus species, mostly B. subtilis, B. coagulans, B. licheniformis, B. clausii, and B. amyloliquefaciens, and no harmful effects were seen in the experimental animals. The benefits of probiotics and their pathogenicity, however, vary with strain. Therefore, it is necessary to regularly and separately assess the toxicity of newly isolated strains [18].

B. subtilis is an aerobic, mesophilic, spore-forming, Gram-positive bacteria [19].B. subtilis can be regarded as an ideal and versatile probiotic bacterium for both humans and animals. This is primarily attributed to its adaptability in the growth and utilization of nutrients, as well as its ability to generatea diverse array of enzymes, including peptides, lipopeptides, antibiotics, and bacteriocins. B. subtilis is capable of secreting antimicrobial compounds, producing spores, and can be easily cultivated [[20], [21], [22]].

Throughout history, humans have extensively consumed several strains of B. subtilis. For instance, 108 CFU/g of B. subtilis have been reported to be utilized in the traditional Japanese fermented soybean dish known as natto [23].B. subtilis strains with a concentration of 106–109 CFU per serving have been commercially available in various probiotic products worldwide [24].B. subtilis is also included in the inventory of microbial species with technologically useful roles in fermented food products highlighting its extensive history of use. The International Dairy Federation originally compiled it with the European Food and Feed Cultures Association [25]. In addition, it is worth noting that this particular bacterium has been granted a qualified presumption of safety (QPS) status by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). This designation is based on the fact that the microorganism does not produce any enterotoxins or emetic toxins [22,26]. FAO/WHO guidelines state that probiotics mustbe tested for safety on animal models before being used in humans [17].

However, Bacillus strains can always lead to opportunistic infections in those with weakened immune systems or defenses. In addition, it is essential to acknowledge that the possible hazards related to introducing novel probiotic strains can only be dismissed once they undergo thorough safety and toxicity assessments, as outlined by the FAO/WHO (2002) [27]. Therefore, it is crucial to do a comprehensive safety evaluation for each case, mainly when introducing the strain into the food chain of animals and humans. Due to the absence of specialized recommendations for the safety evaluation of probiotics [28], the commonly accepted practice is to utilize the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) guidelines that are often used for the safety assessment of chemicals in rodents.

The aerobic metabolism typically produces reactive oxygen species (ROS) [29]. Free radicals in moderation can be advantageous, but excess production and residual ROS can lead to oxidative stress, impair bodily functions, speed up aging, and trigger several diseases [30]. A free radical scavenging mechanism balances free radical formation and removal to prevent oxidative damage [31].

This study examined the repeated dose administration of B. subtilis (NMCC-path-14) over 28 days to determine the NOAEL (No Observed Adverse Effect Level). Furthermore, antioxidant potential in various vital organs and fecal microbiota analysis of this specific strain was also performed.

Materials and methods

Probiotic strain and culture condition

The B. subtilis strain (NMCC-path-14) with an accession number of MK355558, consisting of 1196 bp linear DNA, was isolated from the rumen of a Nilli Ravi Buffaloand was provided by the probiotic lab at the National Institute for Genomics and Advanced Biotechnology (NIGAB), located at the National Agricultural Research Centre (NARC) in Islamabad, Pakistan. NMCC-path-14 was cultured using the previously reported method with minor modifications [32]under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 12–14 h. The growth medium used was Luria Bertani (LB) broth, which consisted of 10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, and 5 g of sodium chloride per liter. Pre-cultured vegetative cells of B. subtilis NMCC-path-14 were harvested by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 10 min. After a single PBS wash, the vegetative cell suspension was prepared at a concentration of1 x 108 CFU/ml with LB broth. The bacterial count was accurately measured with a bacteria counter. The cells were isolated through the process of centrifugation of the obtained supernatant. After discarding the supernatant, cell pellets underwent three rounds of washing in deionized water. The cell pellets were stored at a temperature of −20 °C until they were used. Fresh cell suspensions were prepared daily from the frozen stock before administering to the animals. Normal saline (N/S) was used as the diluent to achieve the required concentrations of NMCC-path-14, set at 1 × 108 CFU/ml and 1 × 1010 CFU/ml.

Animals and grouping for 28-days repeated dose toxicity

Balb/c mice (30 healthy Females) weighing 25–30 g (age: 3–4 weeks) were purchased from the National Institute of Health (NIH) in Islamabad, Pakistan, and housed at the animal house, Quaid-i-Azam University in Islamabad, Pakistan. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and principles of laboratory animals provided by the Bioethics Committee of Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad, with approval number BEC-FBS-QAU2021–338. The animals had a 7-day period of acclimatization to adapt to their new environment. They were then randomly assigned into three equal groups (n = 10/group).

•Normal control (NC): Conventional baseline diet + N/S p. o; continued till day 28

Normal control (NC): Conventional baseline diet + N/S p. o; continued till day 28

•Bacillus subtilis Lowdose (BS-LD): Conventional baseline diet + B. subtilis (1 × 108 CFU/mlp. o) continued till day 28

Bacillus subtilis Lowdose (BS-LD): Conventional baseline diet + B. subtilis (1 × 108 CFU/mlp. o) continued till day 28

•Bacillus subtilis High dose (BS-HD):Conventional baseline diet + B. subtilis (1 × 1010 CFU/mlp.o) continued till day 28

Bacillus subtilis High dose (BS-HD):Conventional baseline diet + B. subtilis (1 × 1010 CFU/mlp.o) continued till day 28

The rationale for selecting these doses is based on several studies that indicated a dosage range of 107–109 CFU as the optimal dose for dairy and non-dairy products [33]. Secondly, various strains of B. subtilis are available commercially in a concentration of 106–108 CFU/serving; hence, doses two doses were carefully selected for this study [24]. Treatments were orally administered once every day for 28 days with a syringe with a 16-gauge ball-tipped feeding needle. Since no specific safety criteria for assessing probiotics is currently in place, we evaluated the subacute toxicity in female mice using OECD 407 guidelines with minor modifications. In general, it is widely accepted that females exhibit greater sensitivity to the toxicological impact of chemicals in comparison to males [34]. Furthermore, female mice were vulnerable to pregnancy when toxicity trials were conducted on both male and female mice at the same time. Hence, exclusively female mice were utilized in this study.

Study assessments 2.3.1 clinical signs and mortality

Daily observations were made of all the general characteristics listed in the oral toxicity test, including any changes in breathing, skin and fur color, eye color, behavior pattern, lethargy, sleep, changes in gait, posture, and mortality (if any).

Body weight gain

The animals were weighed before the first day of therapy and on days 7, 14, 21, and 28.

Feed consumption

Equal amounts of feed were given to each cage, and the remaining feed in each cage was measured daily. Animal feed consumption (g/day) was calculated daily for 28 days (g/animal/day), as shown below [11].

Average feed consumption of animal (g/day) = Feed provided (g) to cage – feed leftover (g) in cage/No. of Animals per cage.

Necropsy

On day 28, after weighing, all animals were euthanized under anesthesia using Xylazine + Ketamine (16 mg + 60 mg, i. p.). A cardiac puncture was used to draw blood. For hematology, a portion of the blood samples were delivered in EDTA tubes. In contrast, for the evaluation of serum biochemistry, the remaining blood samples were placed in Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum stored at −20 °C.The liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon tissues were collected and cleaned with saline. These organs were divided into two portions; one portion was fixed in 10 % buffered formalin for histology, and the other portion was kept at −80 °C for homogenate preparation. Cryopreserved tissues (liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon) were thawed and weighed using a digital balance. In a 2 ml round bottom Eppendorf, an identical amount of tissue (0.1g) was diluted with 1 ml of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (pH = 7.4). The mixture was homogenized for 3–6 min at 5800 rpm using a homogenizer (Precellys Evolution, Bertin Technologies). The resulting homogenates were centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at a speed of 4000 rpm to obtain supernatant, which was stored at −80 °C. The supernatant was used to estimate antioxidant and oxidative stress markers [2].

5Gross examination of organs and length of colon

Macroscopic evaluation of the liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon was performed by a trained pathologist who was blind to the treatment protocol. In order to assess the impact of NMCC-path-14 on colon length, the colon was removed from the ileocecal junction and placed on a non-absorbent surface for measurement using a ruler.

6Relative organ weight

The relative organ weight of the liver, kidneys, and spleen was calculated using the following formula [2]:

Relative organ weight = Weight of the organ (g) ÷ Weight of the animal (g) × 100.

Hematology and serum biochemistry

Using an auto hematology analyzer (Sysmex XT-1800i, Sysmex Corporation Kobe, Japan), hematologic variables such as red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), differential leukocyte count (DLC), hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), and platelet count were determined. Additionally, using commercially available diagnostic kits and following the manufacturer's instructions (Tersaco AG), serum parameters such as Alanine transaminase (ALT), Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein, albumin, globulin, albumin-globulin ratio (A/G), creatinine, and direct bilirubin were determined.

Histopathological examination of the liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon

The liver, kidney, spleen, and colon were stored in a 10 % formalin solution for 36 h and then subjected to the standard paraffin-embedding procedure for fixed specimens. The paraffin blocks were sliced into 5 μm thick sections using a microtome (Leica Biosystems). After mounting on glass slides the sections were stained with H & E, and examined under a light microscope (Olympus CX41RF) [2].

The following parameters were observed in the liver specimen: pyknosis, fatty change, necrosis, kupffer cell hyperplasia, congestion, and central vein dilatation. In kidneys, congestion, necrosis, tubular alteration, inflammatory cells, and glomerular change were evaluated. In the spleen, red and white pulps, as well as lymphoid follicles, were analysed. Goblet cells, mucosal layer inflammation, and crypt integrity were all assessed in the colon. Two experienced pathologists examined histopathological sections. Three H &E stained sections per animal (n = 5/group) were randomly chosen, and five non-overlapping fields (40× magnification; scale bar: 20 μm for the liver, kidneys, and colon while 4× magnification; scale bar: 200 μm for the spleen) were chosen for quantification.

Antioxidants and oxidative stress markers

The antioxidant enzymes GST, GSH, and CAT, as well as the oxidative stress markers malonaldehyde (MDA) and nitric oxide (NO), were measured in liver, kidney, spleen, and colon tissue homogenates using a spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad xMarkTMMicroplate Absorbance Spectrophotometer, Serial No. 10573, Japan). Using a method previously published [35], GST's capability of conjugating GSH with 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene was employed to evaluate the enzyme's functionality. The reduced GSH assay was performed using the previously described method [36]. CAT's ability to degrade hydrogen peroxide served as a gauge of its enzymatic activity, which was obtained using the approach previously reported method [37]. Thiobarbituric acid reactive substance analysis is the method that is most frequently used to quantify the MDA, a result of lipid peroxidation. A previously reported method was used to measure MDA [38]. The quantity of NO in tissue homogenates was determined using the Griess reagent method reported previously at 450 nm [39].

Microbiological analysis of fecal content (fecal enrichment)

The fresh cecal content of five mice from the experimental and control groups was collected, and the quantities of Bacillus, Lactobacilli, E. coli, and total aerobic bacteria were enumeratedby following the method of Sung-Ho et al. [40]. Cecal content (100 μm) was mixed with 1 ml of N/S in sterile Eppendorf tubes. The mixture was then vortexed for 1 min to generate the first dilution (10−1). Afterward, it was kept at room temperature for 40–60 min. Following this, a sequence of dilutions of the cecal content in N/S was prepared from the initial dilution to 10−5 and 10−7, and 50 μl of these dilutions were plated on appropriate selective media for the enumeration and culture of various bacterial groups. MacConkey agar was utilized for culturing and quantifying E. coli, whereas MRS (Merck) was employed to cultivate and quantify Bacillus and Lactobacillus. Plate Count Agar was used to cultivate and quantify the overall bacterial growth. Initial observations were documented over 24 h of cultivation under optimal conditions for each category of bacteria, and conclusive outcomes were acquired after 48 h. CFU was counted in each of the single crop dilutions. The arithmetic mean of the number of colonies in the crops of a single dilution or starter sample was calculated. The multiplicity of sample dilution was considered during the calculation. The result was expressed as a log of CFU in 1 g of cecal content.

Statistics

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 for macOS (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) was used to generate the graphs. The statistical package for social sciences (SPSS) version 25 for macOS (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) was used to analyze means. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) is followed by a multiple comparison using the least significant difference (LSD). The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical signs and mortality

No instances of mortality were found at any dosage level for the whole duration of the treatment period. Throughout the study, no clinical manifestations or symptoms associated with the NMCC-path-14 in any of the animals were documented. A comprehensive clinical examination was conducted, and it was determined that there were no noticeable alterations in the animals that could be attributed to the treatment administered for the entire study duration. Ophthalmology examination showed no NMCC-path-14administration-related alterations in both eyes after therapy (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Total body weight and feed consumption

Animals inNMCC-path-14 groups did not exhibit treatment-related changes in body weight (from day 1 to day 28) when compared to animals in the NC (Fig. 1a). Both BS-LD and BS-HD significantly increased (p < 0.05) feed consumption among mice when compared with NC.(Fig. 1b).

AmongNMCC-path-14 groups, comparable change was seen in the case of creatinine, while BS-HD decreased the level of bilirubin more significantly (p < 0.001) than BS-LD (Table 2).

Histopathological analysis

Histopathology of visceral organs revealed that administration of BS-LD and BS-HD maintained the normal architecture of these organs, and no abnormalities (such as necrosis, inflammation, or hyperplastic changes) were observed. Liver histology revealed a normal central vein, bile ducts, hepatic artery/vein, and sinusoids. Histology of kidneys revealed that glomerular space and Bowman's capsule were intact, with normal proximal and distal convoluted tubules. In the spleen, BS-LD and BS-HD lymphoid follicles, white and red pulp were normal. Normal crypts, goblet cells, and no inflammation were seen in colon tissue (Fig. 3).

Liver

BS-LD significantly increased the GST (p < 0.01), GSH (p < 0.001), and CAT (p < 0.05) when compared to NC. Similarly, BS-HD raised the levels of GST, GST, and CAT significantly (p < 0.001) when compared with NC. Among NMCC-path-14 groups, the BS-HD raised the GST and GSH more efficiently (p < 0.01) than the BS-LD. Administration of BS-LD and BS-HD decreased the MDA levels significantly (p < 0.05). BS-LD and BS-HD decreased the NO significantly (p < 0.01) compared toNC.AmongNMCC-path-14 groups, non-significant change in MDA and NO was seen (Fig. 4).

Kidneys

When compared to NC, both BS-LD and BS-HD raised the levels of GST, GSH, and CAT significantly (p < 0.05). Furthermore, oxidant enzymes (MDA and NO) were decreased by both doses of NMCC-path-14significantly (p < 0.01). When BS-LD was compared to BS-HD, it was revealed that a significant increase in GST (p < 0.01) and GSH (p < 0.05) was found in BS-HD. Comparable change in CAT, MDA, and NO was seen among NMCC-path-14 groups (Fig. 4c).

Spleen

Administration of BS-LD and BS-HD resulted in the elevation of antioxidant enzymes significantly (p < 0.01). Similarly, administration of NMCC-path-14led to decreased MDA and NO levels significantly (p < 0.05). Discussion

In recent years, probiotics have been utilized to supplement traditional treatments for different health issues [41]. Today, many food and dairy products contain probiotics. While most probiotics are harmless, the FAO/WHO guidelines advise that novel probiotics must be assessed for toxicity before being released into the market for human consumption [17,42].

For this study, a strain of lab-isolated potential probiotic B. subtilis, NMCC-path-14 was selected due to the well-documented benefits, remarkable resilience, and adaptability of the Bacillus species. Its survival at high temperatures and acidic pH levels supports gut health, produces immunomodulation, and synthesizes antibacterial molecules [19,21]. Its extensive history of safe use in clinical and industrial environments supports its choice for investigating its probiotic effects and possible health advantages [43,44]. Probiotic-containing strains of B. subtilis are extensively researched among Bacillus species [45], and none have been found to be unsafe, even at high doses [[46], [47], [48], [49]]. Also, the consumption of B. subtilis has a well-established history of safe use in the food industry [43,44]. Existing safety evaluation recommendations forpotential probiotics strongly advocate using rodentsto analyze sub-acute toxicity as the fundamental requirement. Various investigators have adopted the same approach to validate the safety profile of their claimed promising probiotic strains [17,50,51]. Currently, there are no specific safety guidelines available for evaluating probiotics; hence, OECD 407 guidelines with minor modifications were used. A commonly accepted range for oral administration is a minimum concentration of 10⁷ cfu/g of the probiotic strain in the intestinal content [52]. A higher dose is typically given to account for the differences among various probiotic bacteria species in their ability to survive physiological conditions and temporarily colonize the gut [53]. However, most probiotic strains tend to occur as functional foods or dietary supplements rather than being utilized as treatments. Consumers often eat these functional foods for extended durations; thus, the probiotic strains employed in these meals must be safe for long-term consumption. A subacute (28-day) oral toxicity test was conducted to assess the potential negative effects of long-term ingestion of probiotic microbes on the host's health. To rule out any unfavourable impacts of novel strain NMCC-path-14, toxicology testing in rodents was performed.

Considering probiotics may affect body weight [54], food intake was monitored daily, and body weight was measured weekly. On administration of NMCC-path-14, the BS-LD, and BS-HD did not show any weight changes throughout the 28 days of its administration (Fig. 1a). Naiduet al.in their study observed that B. subtilis ZB183 spores at the doses of 109, 1010 and 1011 CFU/kg body weight/day did not affect body weight in rats [23]. Similarly, B. subtilis BS 304.04 supplementation did not change weight in rats [17]. NMCC-path-14notably raised the feed intake in mice (Fig. 1b). Our results are in accordance with the finding of Xu. et al. revealed that B. subtilis BS-Z15 significantly enhanced the feed intake in mice by increasing the presence of Lactobacillus in the intestines of mice. Similarly, in our study, NMCC-path-14 increased fecal Lactobacillus levels. This finding aligns with the results reported by Donaldson et al., which indicate a correlation between the increased presence of beneficial intestinal bacteria, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, and weight loss [55]. In brief, bacterial proliferation increases metabolism and stimulates appetite, which promotes regular digestion and the efficient utilization of feed [56]. Since feed intake was increased but weight was not affected significantly, this strain might be explored for human weight management. We did not find gross lesions or morphological alterations in the liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon when comparingNMCC-path-14 treated animals to NC(Fig. 2a and b).

Relative organ weight is utilized as an index for assessing the extent of chemical toxicity [32]. It has been widely used in toxicity tests and is a sensitive measure of chemically induced organ alterations [18]. Ashafa et al. identify increased organ weight as an inflammatory response, while decreased organ weight is linked to cellular constriction [57]. The relative organ weight of essential organs such as the liver, kidneys, and spleen remained unchanged (Fig. 1c). Other stains like B. subtilis ZB183 and B. subtilis BS 304.04 displayed similar results [17,23].

Since probiotics colonize in the colon hence, colon length was measured. Compared to NC, colon length was considerably increased following the administration of NMCC-path-14(Fig. 2b). Similar results were displayed by B. subtilis 168 [58]and B. subtilis B.S‐Dia [59]. Recently, Notch signaling has been shown to regulate the gut progenitor compartment and modulate intestinal inflammation. After intestinal damage, its role in tissue regeneration is unclear. The Notch signaling system and NF-κB pathway regulate the interaction between intestinal epithelial cells and immune cells after pathogenic insults. This connection determines whether the gut will regenerate or suffer significant intestinal damage [60]. Hu et al. proposed that B. subtilis increases the colon length by simulating the intestinal secretory cell differentiation by inhibiting the Notch pathway [58]. Since Inflammatory bowel disease is known to cause contraction of colon length in mice models [61],NMCC-path-14 could be explored for this purpose in the future.

Hematological indicators are commonly employed to assess inflammation and infections, whereas serum biochemistry analysis is utilized to evaluate organ-related issues [62]. NMCC-path-14administrationincreased RBC count, Hb, and Hct (Table 1). The findings of our study align with the already published research. The administration of B. subtilisNS4182-01 effectively reversed the decline in RBC count, Hb levels, and Hct caused by aflatoxin in rats [63]. Prazdnova et al. observed increased RBC count and Hb level in hens and roosters after supplementing with B. subtilis KATMIRA1933 [64]. Korcok et al. attributed this outcome to the activation of hematopoietic organs and certain lactic acid bacteria, such as Lactobacilli, which indirectly enhance the accessibility of dietary iron through a various process, including the reduction of intestinal pH [65]. Prazdnova et al. proposed that this could be attributed to synthesizing metabolites possessing antioxidant, DNA-preserving, and hemopoiesis-stimulating characteristics [64]. WBC count and DLC were not affected byNMCC-path-14 administration. Additionally, plateletcount remained unchanged (Table 1). Similar results were reported previously [17,49,66].

Hepatic transaminases, specifically AST and ALT, indicate liver function [67]. Any drug/chemical that exhibits liver toxicity will have an impact on these transaminases. Hepatic inflammation, cell leakage, and cell membrane damage are caused by the elevation of AST and ALT levels [68]. AST is found in higher levels in the liver, heart, kidney, and pancreas and is released slowly compared to ALT. ALT is a very sensitive indicator of hepatic damage, primarily located in the cytosol of hepatocytes [69]. On the other hand, the activity of ALP is associated with the functioning of liver cells, and an increase in its activity can occur due to increased production or blockage of the biliary system inside the liver ([70]. Bilirubin serves as an additional marker for assessing liver function. Elevated bilirubin levels indicate liver dysfunction. According to the American Association for Clinical Chemistry (AACC, 2001), decreased levels of albumin and total protein serve as reliable indications of impaired liver and renal function [71]. Serum creatinine levels serve as a reliable indicator of renal function [69]. ALT, AST, and ALP remained within the standard range, which is in accordance with previous findings [23,66]. These results indicate that NMCC-path-14 does not produce a toxic effect on hepatocytes. This was further confirmed by histopathological analysis of the liver. NMCC-path-14raised serum total proteins, albumin, and globulin levels while the A/G ratio was unaffected (Table 2). Our results are in accordance with already published studies. In rats and broilers with aflatoxin B1 poisoning, B. subtilis elevated total protein levels and albumin [19,63]. In addition, the treatment of B. subtilis resulted in an increase in total protein, albumin, and globulin levels in hens/roosters [64] and Japanese quail birds [72]. Furthermore, studies by Helal et al. and Ramesh &Souissi have indicated that B. subtiliscan enhance serum levels of total protein, albumin, and globulin in rabbits and fish [73,74]. It is proposed that by inhibiting the pathogen growth,B. subtilis enhances thedietary utilization of proteins. This could be the reason for the rise in serum total protein and albumin [72]. According to Ramesh &Souissi, B. subtilisKADR1 increases the defense molecules, including agglutinins, lectins, lysozyme, immunoglobulins, etc., which may increase the total protein concentration [74]. Daily administration of NMCC-path-14also resulted in a notable decline in creatinine and bilirubin levels (Table 2). Numerous studies have documented that probiotics can reduce creatinine levels to below-normal levels. For example, B. subtilis supplementation in Japanese quail birds [72], Promax® in broilers [75], and Bacillus species in rats infected with Staphylococcus aureus [76]. According to the study by Salim [77], it has been suggested that certainprobioticscan utilize urea, creatinine, and other toxic compounds as sources of nourishment. This could explain the ability of NMCC-path-14 to decrease creatinine.

Additionally, Lempert et al. propose that creatininase activity in probiotics may decrease creatinine levels [78]. Researchers have suggested that probiotics reduce intestinal urobilinogen combinations such as urobilinogen, mesobilirubin, half-stercobilinogen, stercobilinogen, dihydrobilirubin, and dihydromesobilirubin to lower the bilirubin levels. Additionally, higher gut flora and decreased mucosal β-glucuronidase activity are linked to improved bilirubin metabolism [79]. Hosono et al.have hypothesized that probiotics may increase bilirubin metabolism by inhibiting the enterohepatic bile acid reabsorption [80].

Histopathology conducted via H&E staining showed no structural alterations, inflammation, or damage in the liver, kidneys, spleen, or colon. The liver showed normal hepatocytes and portal area with no signs of inflammation or necrosis. In addition, the kidneys displayed normal glomerular and tubular structures. Spleen histology displayed regular white and red pulps with prominent lymphoid follicles. Normal crypts, goblet cells, and colonocytes were seen in colon histology (Fig. 3). Similar results were demonstrated by Bacilluscoagulans SNZ 1969 [11], Bacillus clausii UBBC07 [47], Bacillus Subtilis UBBS-14 [50].

The first line of cellular defense against oxidative injury is the free radical scavenging enzymes. Suppressing these defensive pathways increases the vulnerability to free radical-induced cellular damage [81]. Previously, various strains of B. subtilis have been shown to have antioxidant potential, namely B. subtilis Ch9 [31], B. subtilis xztubd1 [82], B. subtilis B10 [32], B. subtilis PRM102 [83], and B. subtilis ATCC 35854 [72]. In line with the previous findings, NMCC-path-14improves the anti-oxidative capacity of the liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon. The effects of BS-HD were more promising than BS-LD. NMCC-path-14enhanced the levels of GSH, GST, and GST, enhancing the antioxidant capability of these organs (Fig. 4). Similarly, by declining the MDA and NO levels, NMCC-path-14decreased the generation of ROS, protecting against oxidative stress. Tanget al. have suggested that B. subtilis can induce the secretion of antioxidant enzymes or secrete antioxidant enzymes itself. This leads to an increase in the production of antioxidants, which effectively eliminate surplus free radicals and maintain a balanced level of free radicals in the body [31]. Furthermore, Ren et al. have demonstrated that B. subtilis increases the Nrf-2 gene, increasing antioxidant capacity [84]. The results we collected confirmed that NMCC-path-14 had a beneficial effect on the antioxidant capacity of Balb/c mice. This suggests that it could be utilized as an effective antioxidant probiotic.

Understanding the fecal microbiota in probiotic-treated animals is essential because that may explain the influence of probiotics on gut microflora. Such analyses can identify their effect on shifts in gut microbial composition, diversity, and function, which is critical to deciphering the efficacy, probiotic niche as well as working mechanism [71]. The higher abundance of Bacillus species in the fecal matter of the animal treated with NMCC-path-14, compared to animals in the NC, indicates successful colonization of NMCC-path-14 strain in the colon. Pathogenic bacteria, namely E. coli, generate β-glucosidase and β-glucuronidase enzymes. Therefore, these two harmful bacterial enzymes are frequently present in the gastrointestinal tract of animals [71]. The mice that were administered the NMCC-path-14 strain exhibited a notable reduction in E. coli levels, which suggests a decrease in the activity of β-glucuronidase and β-Glucosidase. Administration of MicewithNMCC-path-14 strains exhibited a noteworthy rise in the populations of healthy bacteria, including Bacillus and Lactobacillus, while a reduction in harmful bacteria such as E. coli and total aerobic bacteria was demonstrated. Previous studies have also reported that various strains of B. subtilis can affect microbiota diversity. B. subtilis KN-42 increased lactobacillus and decreased E. coli [85]. In another study, B. subtilis increased Lactobacillus and Bacillus [86].

Limitations of the current study include usingonly female mice and utilizingvegetative cells of NMCC-path-14 instead of the spores. Gender has an impact on drug metabolism, immunological response, and toxicity, leading to limitations in the generalizability of findings due to gender bias. This may fail to include adverse effects that are particular to males and may result in biased safety profiles in studies relating to drugs. Vegetative cells have a reduced capacity to survive under harsh environmental conditions. Spore formation enhances the robustness and long-term survival of probiotics, hence increasing their efficacy. By focusing on vegetative forms, the potential effects and interactions of spore forms and the host microbiota are disregarded. Despite some limitations, this study provides good preliminary data about the safety of this particular strain, which can help conduct a more thorough study on NMCC-path-14.

Conclusions

NMCC-path-14repeated dose experiments were well tolerated and did not elicit harmful clinical signs in female Balb/c mice. Notably, no significant adverse effects were recorded with NMCC-path-14therapy at the dose of up to 1 × 1010 CFU/animal/day.Since NMCC-path-14increased feed intake, increased RBC count, Hb, and Hct level without negatively affecting the overall general health of mice, it also improved the antioxidant capacity and immune system (indicated by a rise in serum globulin levels), and no mortality was seen throughout the study. NMCC-path-14 also elevated healthy bacteria, at the same time decreasing harmful bacterial populations. Thus, the NOAEL for NMCC-path-14after 28 days of treatment in mice was determined to be greater than 1 × 1010 CFU/animal/day. Based on this information and a lengthy history of B. subtilis safety in dietary supplements, we conclude that NMCC-path-14 is safe. However, it is imperative to conduct more extensive chronic oral toxicity studies and clinical trials to establish the safety of this particular strain of B. subtilis as a probiotic for human use.
