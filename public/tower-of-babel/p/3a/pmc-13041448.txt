PDIA6 promotes the cell proliferation of ESCC by enhancing the disulfide bond formation in TRAF4
Authors: Yingying Chen, Weizhe Zhang, Yurong Chen, Xiaoyu Li, Yunshu Shi, Qiang Yuan, Ruixian Han, Yuhan Zhang, Xiaokun Zhao, Yamei Hu, Chenjuan Zhang, Ziming Dong, Mee-Hyun Lee, Myoung Ok Kim, Zigang Dong, Yanan Jiang, Kangdong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProtein disulfide isomerase A6 (PDIA6), a member of the PDI family, catalyzes disulfide bond formation and assists protein folding. However, its function in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains largely unknown.MethodsFunctional experiments, including CRISPR/Cas9 knockout, overexpression, rescue assays, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, were performed to evaluate the role of PDIA6 in ESCC proliferation and tumor growth. Pull down-mass spectrometry assays, co-immunoprecipitation, and protein-protein docking assays were used to investigate PDIA6–tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 4 (TRAF4) interactions and disulfide bond formation. Ubiquitination and cycloheximide chase assays were applied to assess the stability of TRAF4. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting PDIA6 were tested for therapeutic efficacy in vitro and in vivo.ResultsWe found PDIA6 was markedly upregulated in ESCC tissues, with a positive correlation to a poor prognosis. Functional assays demonstrated that PDIA6 significantly promoted the proliferation of ESCC cells both in vitro and in vivo. A pull down–mass spectrometry assay identified TRAF4 as a direct binding partner of PDIA6. Protein-protein docking revealed that PDIA6 interacted with the N-terminal (1-277) domain of TRAF4, enhancing disulfide bond formation at Cys39/Cys42 and Cys83/Cys106. These bonds were indispensable for TRAF4’s E3 ubiquitin ligase activity in facilitating the ubiquitination of AKT. PDIA6 further stabilized TRAF4 by competing with SMAD-specific E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (SMURF1), thereby preventing TRAF4 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. The absence of PDIA6 led to the destabilization of TRAF4, resulting in the inactivation of the AKT/mTOR pathway. Rescue experiments using TRAF4 C42A or C83A mutants failed to restore AKT signaling or tumor growth. Notably, ASOs targeting PDIA6 suppressed ESCC growth in vitro and in patient-derived xenografts.ConclusionsPDIA6 drives ESCC progression by stabilizing TRAF4 and sustaining AKT/mTOR signaling. Background

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a highly malignant and aggressive tumor of the upper digestive system [1], primarily classified into two histological subtypes: esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC). Current treatment modalities for ESCC include radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy [2, 3]. However, despite the existence of available options, patients’ survival rates remain low owing to the limited efficacy of these treatments. Therefore, investigating the underlying mechanisms and identifying new therapeutic targets is crucial for improving the scientific understanding and clinical management of ESCC.

The abnormality of important disulfide bonds in protein structures can lead to several diseases, such as cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, and cardiovascular disease [4–6]. The protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) family plays a pivotal role in facilitating the formation of disulfide bonds and has been associated with cancer progression [7, 8]. Protein disulfide isomerase A6 (PDIA6) is a member of the PDI family and mainly localizes in the endoplasmic reticulum. PDIA6 catalyzes the synthesis of disulfide bonds as an oxidase and functions as a molecular chaperone to facilitate the proper folding of proteins [9–11]. According to previous studies, PDIA6 promotes the proliferation of gastric cancer cells and regulates cisplatin resistance in lung cancer cells, indicating its critical role in cancer progression [12–14]. However, the function and molecular mechanism of PDIA6 in ESCC still need to be determined.

The AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway plays a crucial role in cell growth, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance in ESCC and other cancers [15, 16]. It has been implicated as a potential therapeutic target in ESCC [17, 18]. AKT activation is typically mediated by upstream signals, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA) mutations [19–21]. However, the mechanism underlying sustained AKT1/mTOR pathway activation remains to be thoroughly investigated in ESCC.

In this study, we found that PDIA6 was overexpressed in ESCC and negatively associated with patients’ survival. PDIA6 promoted disulfide bond formation in tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 4 (TRAF4), thereby activating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Furthermore, treatment with antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting PDIA6 reduced the cell proliferation of ESCC by inhibiting the TRAF4/AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway. These findings reveal a novel mechanism by which PDIA6 drives ESCC proliferation, potentially through activation of the AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for ESCC.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

The following antibodies were utilized in this research: anti-PDIA6 (ab227545, Abcam, UK), anti-TRAF4 (sc-390232, Santa Cruz, USA), anti-SMURF1 (sc-100616, Santa Cruz, USA), anti-V5 (13202S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-GAPDH (60004, HuaBio, China), and anti-Flag (F1804, Sigma, USA) antibodies. JetPRIME® transfection reagent (114-07, Polyplus, USA) and Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose (sc-2003, Santa Cruz, USA) were also used.

Cell culture

The Shantou human embryonic esophageal (SHEE) cell line was kindly provided by Prof. Enmin Li (Shantou University, China) [22–24]. ESCC cell lines, including KYSE30 (RRID: CVCL_1351), KYSE70 (RRID: CVCL_1356), KYSE140 (RRID: CVCL_1347), KYSE150 (RRID: CVCL_1348), KYSE410 (RRID: CVCL_1352), KYSE450 (RRID: CVCL_1353), KYSE510 (RRID: CVCL_1354), and HEK293T (RRID: CVCL_0063) cells were obtained from the Department of Pathophysiology, School of Basic Medical Sciences, Zhengzhou University, China [25, 26]. ESCC cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (C3010-0500, Biological Industries, Israel) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (C04001-500, Gibco, Israel) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). HEK293T cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (C3110-0500, Biological Industries, Israel) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and were cytogenetically verified by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling to confirm their authenticity. Moreover, the cell lines were certified mycoplasma-free using the Beyotime Mycoplasma Detection Kit (C0301S, Beyotime, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

The tissue sections were baked at 65 °C for 2 h, dewaxed in xylene, and then fully rehydrated. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inactivated by microwaving the sections for 15 min. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. On the following day, species-specific secondary antibodies were applied. 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate was used for color development, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Sections were then differentiated three times using an acid–alcohol mixture, rinsed in water for 30 min, dehydrated through graded alcohols, and sealed. The staining results were quantified using ImageJ software.

Tissue array

The tissue microarray used in this study (No. JS W-11-01) was purchased from Shanghai Tu Fei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. It consisted of 180 tissue spots: 102 were from ESCC tumor tissues, resulting in 98 unique cases after excluding 4 duplicates, and 78 were adjacent tissue spots, which were collected at a distance of 2 cm away from the cancerous lesion and histologically confirmed to be cancer-free. After removing 2 duplicates and 1 spot lost during immunohistochemistry, 75 cases remained. Ultimately, 73 cases had paired tumor and adjacent tissue, while the others were unpaired.

We analyzed PDIA6-stained tissue microarrays with ImageJ to calculate the PDIA6 positivity rate in epithelial tissues. For duplicates, the positivity rate was averaged. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to evaluate the correlation between patient survival time and PDIA6 expression.

Plasmid construction

The primers used for target gene amplification and for constructing TRAF4 mutants were all designed on the basis of NCBI database sequences and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Nested PCR was performed to obtain the full-length sequences, where the products of the first-round PCR served as templates for the second round of amplification. The resulting PCR products were purified using the UE DNA Gel Extraction Kit (UE-GX-250, USE, China), digested with the appropriate restriction enzymes at 37 ℃, and further purified using the UE PCR Clean-up Kit (UE-PCR-250, USE, China). The purified PCR fragments were then ligated into the enzyme-digested vectors, including pLVX-IRES-Puro-3 × Flag, pcDNA3.1/V5-His A, pCMV-myc, and pcDNA3.1/V5-His A for TRAF4 constructs using T4 DNA ligase (M0202L, New England Biolabs, USA). The sequences of all primers are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

CRISPR/Cas9 knockout cell lines and monoclonal cell lines

PDIA6 and TRAF4 were knocked out using the CRISPR/Cas9 system in ESCC cells. JetPRIME® transfection reagent was used to transfect 293T cells with the target plasmid and packaging plasmids (pMD2.G and psPAX2.0). After 4-6 h of transfection, the culture medium was replaced, and the virus was collected at 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively. The collected viruses were filtered through 0.22-μm filters. KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells were infected with a viral mixture containing 3 mL of viral particles, 7 mL of complete medium, and 8 μg/mL polybrene. The cells were treated with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 72 h. The knockout efficacy was detected by western blot. Then, the stable monoclonal cells were generated using the limiting dilution method [27]. Approximately 2 × 103 sgPDIA6 or sgTRAF4 cells were resuspended in 50 mL of culture medium, and 100 μL of the suspension was seeded into 96-well plates. The oligonucleotide sequences of PDIA6 and TRAF4 single guide (sg) RNA are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell proliferation assay

In total, 2000 ESCC cells were seeded into 96-well plates for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. After incubation, 10 μL of thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Next, 100 μL dimethyl sulfoxide was added to each well, and the optical density (OD) value was measured at 570 nm.

Soft agar assay

The soft agar assay was performed to assess the anchorage-independent growth of ESCC cells [25, 28]. Briefly, six-well plates were prepared. A bottom layer of Basal Medium Eagle (BME) buffer containing 0.6% agar, 10% FBS, and 2 mM L-glutamine was added to the plates, and was allowed to solidify. Then, 8000 cells per well were suspended in BME supplemented with 0.35% agar, 10% FBS, and 2 mM L-glutamine and overlaid onto the bottom layer. The plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 14 days, and colonies were subsequently counted using an IN Cell Analysis 6000.

Colony formation assay

For the colony formation assay, 200 ESCC cells were seeded in six-well plates. Following incubation for 10–14 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and photographed for analysis.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (R0020, Solarbio, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The concentration of cellular protein was measured with the BCA protein assay kit (P0010, Beyotime, China). The protein samples were electrophoresed by 10% SDS–PAGE, then transferred to a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (162-0177, BIO-RAD, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature (RT) for 2 h, then the primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 ℃. The following day, these membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-IgG antibody at RT for 2 h, and subsequently were detected by Tanon 5200 Series Fully Automated Chemiluminescence Imaging Analysis System.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

The total RNA from ESCC cells was extracted using Trizol (15596026CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Then, 500 ng RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A, Takara). The cDNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Real-time fluorescence quantitative qPCR was performed on cDNA using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q714-03, Vazyme Biotech), and GAPDH was the internal control for relative gene expression calculation using 2−ΔΔCt. All reactions were repeated three times. The sequences of primers are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Pull down–mass spectrometry assay

293F cells overexpressing 3 × Flag-PDIA6 protein were collected and lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 300 mM NaCl; 5% glycerol; 1% Tween-20; 2 mM DTT; and 1 mM EDTA). The lysates were precleared with Plus Protein A/G agarose beads (P2055, Beyotime, China) at 4 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, M2 beads (L00432, GenScript, USA) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C to capture PDIA6. After incubation, the M2 beads bound to PDIA6 were washed and added to KYSE70 cell lysates for an overnight incubation at 4 °C. The beads were then boiled at 100 °C for 30 min and centrifuged. The supernatants were resolved by SDS–PAGE until the samples entered the separation gel, and then visualized with fast silver stain kit (P0017S, Beyotime, China). Differential bands corresponding to PDIA6-specific interactions were excised for in-gel digestion.

Gel pieces were washed sequentially with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and acetonitrile, dried, reduced with 10 mM DTT at 56 °C for 45 min, and alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide at RT for 30 min in the dark. The samples were re-washed with 50 mM ABC and acetonitrile and then dried using a SpeedVac. Proteins were digested overnight with 30 µL mass-spectrometry-grade trypsin. Peptides were extracted from the gel using 50% acetonitrile with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid and desalted using homemade C18 tips. Finally, the peptides were analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nano Ultimate 3000 system coupled to an Orbitrap Elite or Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer after dissolving in 10% formic acid. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) was used as a negative control, and Flag-PDIA6 interaction proteins were identified from differential bands with peptide scores above 2.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Cells were lysed with Co-IP buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5% NP40) on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and quantified using a BCA assay, after which the protein concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL. An aliquot corresponding to 1% of the lysate volume was taken as the input. Protein A/G agarose beads (20 μL) were added and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. The supernatant was incubated overnight with antibody (2 μg) or normal IgG as a control at 4 °C with rotation. The next day, Protein A/G agarose beads were washed with Co-IP buffer three times. Finally, 20 μL of 2× loading buffer was added, and the sample was boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. Western blot assays were performed to detect protein binding.

Protein purification

Recombinant Flag-PDIA6 and V5-tagged proteins, including TRAF4 and its mutants (C39A, C42A, C83A, and C106A), were transiently expressed in HEK293F cells [25]. Cells were transiently transfected with the expression plasmid using polyethyleneimine (PEI) buffer and harvested 72 h after transfection. Cell pellets were lysed by sonication on ice in 293F lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 300 mM NaCl; 5% glycerol; 1% Tween-20; 2 mM DTT; and 1 mM EDTA). The lysates were incubated with either the anti-DYKDDDDK Affinity Resin (L00432-1, GenScript, USA) or the anti-V5 Agarose Affinity Gel (A7345, Millipore, USA) for 14 h. The beads were sequentially washed with lysis buffer, wash buffer 1 (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 300 mM NaCl; 5% glycerol; 0.1% Tween-20; 2 mM DTT; and 1 mM EDTA), and wash buffer 2 (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, and 300 mM NaCl). Flag-PDIA6 and V5-tagged proteins were eluted with 3 × FLAG peptide (F4799, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and V5 peptide (HY-P0325, MedchemExpress, USA), respectively. Purified proteins were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

The pBiFC-VN173-PDIA6 and pBiFC-VC155-TRAF4 plasmids were constructed and transfected into 293T cells. After 24 h, the cells were washed twice with PBS, and 3 × 104 cells were seeded onto coverslips in a 24-well plate. Following 24 h of incubation, the cells were stained with BD Pharmingen™ Hoechst 33342 Solution (561908, Abcam, USA). Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (SOPTOP IRX50, Sunny Optical Technology, China) at 40× magnification. The exposure time was kept constant at 1000 ms for all experimental groups.

Immunofluorescence staining

In total, 2 × 104 KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells were seeded into wells containing sterile glass coverslips in a 24-well plate. The cells were incubated overnight with PDIA6 and TRAF4 primary antibodies in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The next day, appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies were added and incubated for 1 h at RT. Cells were then counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (SOPTOP IRX50, Sunny Optical Technology, China). Results were analyzed using Image-Pro Plus v6.0.

Protein–protein docking

The full-length structures of PDIA6 (AF-Q15084-F1) and TRAF4 (AF-Q9BUZ4-F1) obtained from AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) were used for protein–protein docking via the HDOCK SERVER website (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn) [29]. Default docking parameters were applied, and the top-ranking conformation was selected for subsequent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The binding energy of the PDIA6–TRAF4 complex, along with energy components, was calculated using MD simulation trajectories. The docking score reflects the predicted interaction features and geometry of the complex on the basis of the server’s scoring function.

Chemical modification assay

293T cells were transfected with full-length or truncated V5-TRAF4 plasmids and cultured for 24 h. Cells were then divided into three groups: the untreated group, the control group, and the experimental group. Then, the cells were modified with N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) and methoxypolyethylene glycol maleimide (mPEG5000-Mal). All samples were first treated with NEM to block free thiol groups for 30 min. After removing excess NEM, the experimental group was treated with dithiothreitol (DTT) to reduce disulfide bonds, followed by incubation with mPEG5000-Mal to label newly exposed thiols for 1 h. The control group was treated with mPEG5000-Mal only, without DTT reduction. After modification, cells were lysed in buffer containing 10% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, and 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5). Samples were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, boiled to denature the proteins, and analyzed by SDS–PAGE and western blot. Changes in protein molecular weight were used to assess disulfide bond formation.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

Cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes and treated with 50 μg/mL CHX for 0, 6, and 8 h to inhibit protein synthesis. After treatment, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. TRAF4 protein levels were analyzed by western blot.

Ubiquitination assay

Cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding pCMV-MYC-ubiquitin, pLVX-IRES-Puro-3 × Flag-SMURF1, pcDNA3.1-V5-His A-TRAF4, and/or pLVX-IRES-Puro-3 × Flag-PDIA6 as indicated in each experiment. After 48 h, cells were harvested and lysed in ubiquitination lysis buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 250 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 10% glycerol, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.2 mM PMSF. MYC-tagged proteins were immunoprecipitated using an anti-MYC antibody, and the ubiquitination levels of TRAF4 were subsequently analyzed by western blot.

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) assay

ASOs targeting PDIA6 were designed, and their efficacy was assessed by measuring PDIA6 protein and mRNA levels using western blot and qPCR. The effects of PDIA6-targeting ASOs on cell proliferation were evaluated using cell proliferation and colony formation assays. All ASO sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

The antitumor activity of ASO was assessed using an ESCC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model. Female severely immunodeficient CB-17/SCID mice, 4–6 weeks old, were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 21–25 °C and 45–50% humidity. LEG110 ESCC tumor tissues were trimmed to approximately 0.1 g per piece and washed with PBS containing penicillin/streptomycin. After 2 weeks, when the average tumor volume reached 100–150 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 7): an ASO-negative control (NC), an ASO-201, and an ASO-162 group. PDX tumors received intratumoral injections every 5 days for a total of five times, with each injection containing 5 nmol of ASO. To minimize tissue damage from intra-tumoral injections, we administered the injections slowly, avoiding repeated punctures at the same site. We confirmed needle placement with slight aspiration and pressed the site with a sterile cotton swab afterward to reduce leakage and injury [30]. Tumor volumes and body weights were measured every 2 days to generate growth curves. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached about 1000 mm3 in the control group, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data in this study were statistically analyzed using SPSS 21.0 software. Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare significant differences if the sample data variance was homogeneous. The nonparametric test was used for uneven variance. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Experiments were repeated three times.

Results

PDIA6 is highly expressed and is negatively associated with patient survival in ESCC

To investigate the clinicopathological significance of PDIA6 in ESCC, we assessed PDIA6 protein levels in an ESCC tissue array using IHC staining. The results confirmed that the PDIA6 protein was mainly localized in the cytoplasm and was increased in tumor tissues compared with adjacent tissues (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that PDIA6 was significantly upregulated in both unpaired (Fig. 1B) and paired (Fig. 1C) comparisons in ESCC. Further analysis revealed that high PDIA6 expression was associated with poorer prognosis (Fig. 1D).

Consistently, PDIA6 protein levels were elevated in ESCC in unpaired (Supplementary Fig. S1A) and paired (Supplementary Fig. S1B) comparisons based on data from the PRIDE database (PXD021701). The TCGA database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) showed that PDIA6 mRNA expression was upregulated in 14 cancers (Fig. 1E) and remarkably upregulated in EAC and ESCC tissues (Fig. 1F). Moreover, PDIA6 mRNA levels were higher in all ESCC stages than in normal tissues (Fig. 1G).

To further validate the high expression of PDIA6, we examined PDIA6 protein levels in ten pairs of ESCC tissues using western blot analysis. The results showed that PDIA6 protein levels were higher in ESCC tissues in nine out of ten patients (Fig. 1H). The data above suggest that PDIA6 is highly expressed and positively correlated with poor survival rates in patients with ESCC.

PDIA6 promotes ESCC cell proliferation in vitro

To investigate the functional role of PDIA6 in ESCC development, we first examined the PDIA6 expression levels in SHEE and various ESCC cell lines, the result showed that PDIA6 protein levels were higher in ESCC cells than in SHEE (Fig. 2A). We then established PDIA6 knockout cells in KYSE30 and KYSE70 by the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig. 2B). After PDIA6 knockout, cell proliferation decreased as shown by the cell proliferation assay (Fig. 2C), and anchorage-independent growth was reduced as demonstrated by the soft agar assay (Fig. 2D). Moreover, the colony formation assay results also proved that PDIA6 knockout could reduce the number of colonies and inhibit the size of colonies in KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells (Fig. 2E). Together, these data confirm that downregulated PDIA6 could hinder the cell proliferation of ESCC cells.

C The cell viability of stable PDIA6 knockout KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells was detected using cell proliferation assays. D Soft agar assay and quantitative analysis. Conversely, we generated PDIA6-overexpressed (PDIA6-OE) KYSE450 cells (Fig. 2F). Functional assays revealed that PDIA6-OE significantly promoted cell proliferation (Fig. 2G) and increased both the number and size of colonies (Fig. 2H). Consequently, to further confirm these findings, we restored PDIA6 expression in sgPDIA6 KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells and detected ESCC cell proliferation. Western blot results showed that the PDIA6 protein level was successfully rescued in KYSE30 (Supplementary Fig. S2A) and KYSE70 (Supplementary Fig. S2B) cells. The cell proliferation ability was significantly increased after restoring PDIA6, as determined by a cell proliferation assay (Fig. 2I, J). Moreover, rescuing PDIA6 increased the colony numbers and sizes in sgPDIA6 KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells (Fig. 2K, L), indicating that PDIA6 enhanced the cloning ability in ESCC cells. These findings collectively demonstrate that PDIA6 promotes the proliferation of ESCC cells in vitro.

PDIA6 binds with TRAF4 in ESCC cells

To investigate the underlying mechanism, we performed a pull down–mass spectrometry assay in KYSE450 PDIA6-OE cells and identified 30 candidate interacting proteins compared with the control group (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S3). Co-IP assays confirmed that PDIA6 interacted with tumor necrosis factor TRAF4 in 293T cells (Fig. 3B). This interaction was further validated using purified proteins (Fig. 3C). As a member of the TRAF protein family, TRAF4 is involved in proliferation, invasion, drug resistance, apoptosis, and cell cycle regulation. It is frequently overexpressed in tumors and contributes to tumorigenesis [31, 32]. Furthermore, the BiFC assay demonstrated that PDIA6 directly binds to TRAF4 in cells (Fig. 3D). Endogenous Co-IP assays revealed that PDIA6 could bind to TRAF4 in ESCC cells (Fig. 3E, F), and TRAF4 also could bind to PDIA6 (Fig. 3G, H). Our data indicate that PDIA6 interacts with TRAF4 in ESCC.

C In vitro co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays using purified proteins confirmed the interaction between PDIA6 and TRAF4. G, H Endogenous Co-IP in KYSE30 (G) and KYSE70 (H) cells revealed that TRAF4 could bind to PDIA6. I Protein–protein docking predicted the interaction interface between PDIA6 and the N-terminal domain of TRAF4. PDIA6 is shown in pink and TRAF4 in blue. J Schematic of the full-length TRAF4 and truncated domains with the RING finger domain (1–277 aa) or TRAF domain (278–470). Co-IP assays showed PDIA6 bound to the TRAF4’s 1–277 domain

We further investigated the specific binding region between PDIA6 and TRAF4 through protein–protein docking experiments. The predicted results indicated that the binding region was located in the N-terminal domain of TRAF4, with a docking score of -259.89 (Fig. 3I). The TRAF4 protein consists of a RING finger domain, seven consecutive zinc finger domains with 1–277 amino acids (aa), and the TRAF domain (278–470 aa) [33]. On the basis of the computational predictions and the functional region of TRAF4, we designed two TRAF4-truncated plasmids: V5-TRAF4 (1–277) and V5-TRAF4 (278–470). A subsequent Co-IP assay revealed that PDIA6 interacted with the 1–277 domain of TRAF4 (Fig. 3J). Together, our findings suggest that PDIA6 binds to TRAF4 in ESCC cells, with the binding region located in the RING-finger domain of TRAF4.

PDIA6 enhances the formation of disulfide bonds of TRAF4

Given that PDIA6 acts as an oxidase to promote disulfide bond production in the oxidizing environment of the endoplasmic reticulum [7], we further employed a chemical modification assay to determine whether TRAF4 contains disulfide bonds. The results showed that sequential treatment with DTT, NEM, and mPEG5000-Mal led to a significant shift in molecular weight. In contrast, the molecular weight of V5-tagged TRAF4 remained unchanged when treated with NEM or mPEG5000-Mal alone (Fig. 4A), indicating the presence of disulfide bonds in TRAF4. Considering that PDIA6 interacted with the 1–277 domain of TRAF4, as shown in Fig. 3J, we investigated the region where TRAF4 disulfide bonds were formed. The molecular weight of V5-TRAF4 (1–277) increased after DTT, NEM, and mPEG5000-Mal treatment (Fig. 4B), while the molecular weight of V5-TRAF4 (278–470) showed no significant change (Fig. 4C, D). These results were consistent with the formation of a disulfide bond in the TRAF4 RING-finger domain.

To further identify the specific cysteine sites involved in TRAF4 disulfide bond formation, we predicted disulfide bond formation sites using the DiANNA website (http://clavius.bc.edu/~clotelab/DiANNA/) [34]. We selected some sites for mutation plasmids on the basis of the cysteine site score. Clusters of cysteines were observed, with two or three cysteines forming disulfide bonds in a cluster from the full-length TRAF4 structure obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. Subsequently, combined with predicting data, we created V5-TRAF4 plasmids with alanine (Ala, A) mutations replacing cysteine (Cys, C) residues at eight sites (34, 39, 42, 83, 90, 106, 113, and 146) on TRAF4 (Fig. 4E). Chemical modification assays revealed that mutations at C34, C90, C113, and C146 increased protein mass, suggesting that these were involved in disulfide bond formation, while mutations at C42, C83, and C106 did not affect molecular weight (Fig. 4F). Considering the spatial distances between the different sites, we propose that the cysteines at positions 39 and 42, as well as those at positions 83 and 106 in TRAF4, form disulfide bonds.

TRAF4 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that activates the phosphorylated form of AKT and downstream signaling pathways by ubiquitinating it [32]. To investigate whether disulfide bond formation affects the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRAF4, we introduced point mutations at Cys42 and Cys83, two cysteine residues predicted to form disulfide bonds in TRAF4. The results revealed that mutations at 42 and 83 of TRAF4 reduced AKT ubiquitination (Fig. 4G). Together, these findings indicate that disulfide bond formation at C42 and C83 is essential for the E3 ligase function of TRAF4 to ubiquitinate AKT.

Subsequently, we investigated whether PDIA6 can directly regulate TRAF4 disulfide bond formation. We found that DTT eliminated the interaction between PDIA6 and TRAF4 (Fig. 4H), and mutations at the TRAF4 39/42 and 83/106 sites had a similar effect (Fig. 4I). Subsequently, interaction assays using purified proteins further confirmed these findings (Supplementary Fig. S4). These findings clarified that the interaction between PDIA6 and TRAF4 depended on the presence of disulfide bonds. Collectively, these results indicate that PDIA6 enhances the formation of disulfide bonds in TRAF4 at sites 39/42 and 83/106.

PDIA6 increases the stability of TRAF4 by inhibiting the ubiquitination of TRAF4 in ESCC cells

We surprisingly found that the TRAF4 protein levels decreased after PDIA6 knockout in ESCC cells (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S5). At the same time, TRAF4 mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 5B). These data suggested that PDIA6 may stabilize the TRAF4 protein and prolong its half-life through post-transcriptional regulation, without affecting its transcription. Considering that disulfide bond formation contributes to enhanced protein stability and prolonged protein [35], we hypothesized that PDIA6 may stabilize TRAF4 by facilitating disulfide bond formation.

To confirm this hypothesis, we performed a CHX chase assay and found that PDIA6 knockout accelerated TRAF4 degradation in KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells (Fig. 5C, D). To determine the degradation pathway, cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 or the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ). MG132 effectively blocked TRAF4 degradation, whereas CQ had no effect (Fig. 5E, F). These results suggested that PDIA6 stabilizes TRAF4 by preventing its degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway in ESCC.

Since TRAF4 has been reported as a substrate of the SMAD-specific E3 ubiquitin ligase SMURF1 [36], we next examined whether SMURF1 can ubiquitinate TRAF4 in ESCC. Ubiquitination assays confirmed that SMURF1 promoted TRAF4 ubiquitination (Fig. 5G). Moreover, PDIA6 was found to suppress SMURF1-mediated ubiquitination of TRAF4 (Fig. 5H). In addition, a dose-dependent analysis further showed that increasing PDIA6 expression gradually reduced the interaction between TRAF4 and SMURF1 (Fig. 5I).

To further investigate the relationship between disulfide bond formation and TRAF4 stability, the ubiquitination levels of TRAF4 mutants were examined. As expected, mutations at Cys39/42 and Cys83/106 enhanced SMURF1-mediated ubiquitination of TRAF4 (Fig. 5J) and impaired the ability of PDIA6 to suppress TRAF4 ubiquitination (Fig. 5K). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that PDIA6 prevents the ubiquitination of TRAF4, leading to increased TRAF4 stability in ESCC cells.

PDIA6 promotes ESCC cell proliferation through the TRAF4/AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway

Considering that TRAF4 activates phosphorylated AKT through ubiquitination, which contributes considerably to the development of ESCC [32], we hypothesized that PDIA6 could activate the AKT signaling pathway by regulating TRAF4. We initially confirmed that TRAF4 was highly expressed in ESCC cells (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, we established stable TRAF4 knockout KYSE30 and KYSE70 cell lines (Fig. 6B). The cell proliferation (Fig. 6C), soft agar (Fig. 6D), and colony formation assays (Fig. 6E) demonstrated that TRAF4 knockout significantly impaired the proliferative capacity of ESCC cells. We next investigated whether PDIA6 can activate the AKT signaling pathway by modulating TRAF4. We found that TRAF4 knockout led to reduced phosphorylation of AKT1, mTOR, and p70S6K (Fig. 6F). Interestingly, PDIA6 knockout also produced similar reductions in the phosphorylation of these proteins (Fig. 6G). Together, our research indicated that PDIA6 promotes activation of the AKT signaling pathway by regulating TRAF4 in ESCC.

To further verify the role of TRAF4 disulfide bonds in this process, we rescued PDIA6 knockout cells with wild-type (WT) TRAF4, as well as C42A and C83A mutants. Western blot analysis revealed that only the rescue of the WT group restored the phosphorylation levels of AKT1 and mTOR, whereas the C42A and C83A mutants failed to rescue these phosphorylation levels (Fig. 6H). We then assessed the effects of TRAF4 WT and mutants in PDIA6 knockout cells on ESCC proliferation. Cell proliferation (Fig. 6I) and colony formation assays (Fig. 6J) demonstrated that WT TRAF4 fully rescued proliferation and colony formation, whereas the C42A and C83A mutants had significantly diminished effects. Collectively, these results suggest that PDIA6 sustains ESCC cell proliferation by promoting disulfide bond formation in TRAF4, thereby activating the AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway.

ASO targeting PDIA6 inhibits ESCC cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo

Given its high expression and pivotal role in ESCC progression, PDIA6 was investigated as a potential therapeutic target. We designed specific ASOs targeting PDIA6 and assessed their efficacy in vitro and in vivo. Western blot and qPCR analyses confirmed that ASO-201 and ASO-162 significantly reduced PDIA6 protein (Fig. 7A) and mRNA (Fig. 7B) levels. Subsequently, we evaluated the biological effects of PDIA6-ASO treatment on ESCC cells. ASO treatment significantly suppressed cell proliferation (Fig. 7C) and reduced colony formation capacity (Fig. 7D). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that PDIA6 silencing via ASO led to decreased phosphorylation of AKT1, mTOR, and p70S6K (Fig. 7E). These results indicate that ASO treatment targeting PDIA6 inhibits ESCC cell proliferation by inhibiting the AKT signaling pathway.

To evaluate the effect of ASO targeting PDIA6 on tumor growth in vivo, we employed an ESCC PDX model. The LEG110 case, which had high PDIA6 expression, was selected (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). ASO treatment significantly reduced tumor volume (Fig. 7F), inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 7G), and reduced tumor weight (Fig. 7H). IHC analysis further revealed lower Ki-67 and PDIA6 expression in ASO-treated tumors (Fig. 7I; Supplementary Fig. S6C). In line with the in vitro data, phosphorylation of AKT1, mTOR, and p70S6K was markedly decreased in PDX tumors following ASO treatment (Fig. 7J). These findings support that targeting PDIA6 with ASO can effectively prevent ESCC cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo.

Discussion

PDIA6, a member of the PDI family, plays a critical role in maintaining protein homeostasis by catalyzing disulfide bond formation and facilitating proper protein folding within the endoplasmic reticulum [7, 37]. Beyond its canonical role as an oxidoreductase and molecular chaperone, PDIA6 has emerged as a multifunctional regulator of tumor biology. Studies have demonstrated that PDIA6 is highly expressed in multiple malignancies, such as non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, breast cancer, and glioblastoma, and is closely associated with tumor proliferation, metastasis, invasion, and therapeutic resistance [38–41]. While previous studies have shown that PDIA6 promotes gastric cancer progression through the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway or mediates cisplatin resistance in lung cancer via the MAP4K1/JNK axis [13, 14], the molecular mechanism by which PDIA6 acts remains to be elucidated. Notably, our study focuses on the disulfide bond formation functions of PDIA6 and elucidates its molecular mechanisms in ESCC. We reveal that PDIA6 enhances TRAF4 stability by promoting disulfide bond formation, thereby preventing its degradation and activating the AKT1/mTOR pathway, which drives ESCC progression. These findings reveal a novel mode of PDIA6 action in ESCC, expanding our understanding of its role in tumor biology.

The formation of disulfide bonds is essential for maintaining protein organization and controlling their activity in cancer cells [35, 42]. In this study, we identified TRAF4 as a novel substrate of PDIA6 and demonstrated that PDIA6 catalyzes the formation of two specific disulfide bonds, Cys39/Cys42 and Cys83/Cys106, within the RING finger domain of TRAF4. These disulfide bonds are located in the RING finger domain, a region responsible for mediating E3 ligase activity, and are critical for TRAF4’s structural stability and ubiquitination function. While prior studies have primarily focused on TRAF4-mediated ubiquitination [43–45], our study uncovers a novel redox-based mechanism through which PDIA6 enhances the functional stability and activity of TRAF4. This disulfide-bond-mediated regulation adds a new layer of control to the post-translational modification of TRAF4, reinforcing its oncogenic potential in ESCC.

AKT is a key oncogenic kinase involved in regulating multiple cellular processes, including proliferation, survival, metabolism, and migration [46]. Aberrant activation of the AKT signaling pathway is frequently observed in ESCC and is associated with tumor progression and poor clinical outcomes. Functionally, TRAF4 utilizes its E3 ubiquitin ligase-active N-terminal RING domain to post-translationally modify AKT via K63-linked polyubiquitination [32, 47]. This K63-linked ubiquitination is a nondegradative modification that serves as a critical molecular switch for AKT activation [48]. Unlike K48-linked ubiquitin chains, which lead to protein degradation, K63 modification does not target AKT1 for proteasomal degradation [49, 50]. Instead, it promotes AKT phosphorylation, thereby activating the downstream AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway [51, 52]. In our study, increased TRAF4 levels enhance AKT1 ubiquitination, leading to activation of the AKT1/mTOR signaling cascade, as evidenced by elevated phosphorylation of downstream effectors in ESCC. Moreover, the AKT1/mTOR pathway plays a crucial role in driving malignant phenotypes and represents a promising therapeutic target [53]. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which PDIA6 maintains oncogenic signaling in ESCC, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the PDIA6/TRAF4/AKT1/mTOR axis.

Although PDI family members have been explored as therapeutic targets, pan-PDI inhibitors often suffer from poor specificity and significant systemic toxicity, limiting their clinical utility [54–57]. In contrast, antisense oligonucleotide therapy offers a highly selective and durable gene-silencing strategy [58–63]. In the present study, we designed ASOs targeting the human-specific PDIA6 gene, incorporating phosphorothioate (PS) and locked nucleic acid (LNA) modifications to enhance stability and reduce potential adverse reactions [64, 65]. Both in vitro and xenograft-based in vivo studies demonstrated that these ASOs effectively suppressed ESCC cell proliferation and tumor growth. Moreover, we discovered that utilizing ASOs can effectively hinder the PDIA6/TRAF4/AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway. These results provide strong functional evidence that ASO-based targeting of PDIA6 offers a viable therapeutic strategy, particularly for patients with ESCC and high PDIA6 expression, and may overcome the limitations of conventional PDI inhibitors.

To implement an ASO-based treatment strategy for solid tumors more effectively, we must address three main weaknesses: degradation susceptibility, potential toxicity, and poor tissue delivery [66, 67]. Chemical modifications, such as PS backbones and 2’-O-methyl (2’-MOE) modifications, can improve stability, extend half-life, and reduce the frequency of administration [68, 69]. Improving sequence specificity can minimize toxicity and off-target effects, while robust post-marketing surveillance ensures long-term safety [70, 71]. In addition, the use of nanomaterials can enhance ASO delivery [72]. Therefore, we will develop FDA-approved nanomaterials to encapsulate these ASOs, enhancing their ability to target tumors and preparing them for future clinical applications.

Conclusions

PDIA6 expression is upregulated in ESCC and negatively correlates with patients’ survival. PDIA6 promotes ESCC cell proliferation by maintaining the AKT1/mTOR signal pathway in vivo and in vitro. Mechanistically, PDIA6 triggers disulfide bond formation in TRAF4 at cysteines 39/42 and 83/106 and competes with SMURF1 for binding to TRAF4, thereby increasing the stability of the TRAF4 protein. Furthermore, ASO treatment targeting PDIA6 effectively suppresses ESCC growth by inhibiting the TRAF4/AKT1/mTOR signaling pathway. Our study highlights a novel regulatory mechanism for sustaining AKT1/mTOR signal pathway activation in ESCC, offering a promising target for ESCC treatment.
