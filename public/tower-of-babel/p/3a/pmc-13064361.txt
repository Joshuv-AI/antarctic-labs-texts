Pediatric faculty attitudes on point-of-care ultrasound education in residency: a multicenter study
Authors: April Slamowitz, Piyawat Arichai, James Bost, Jeremy Kern, Sonali Basu, Alyssa Abo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoint-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a bedside diagnostic and procedural tool with growing relevance in clinical medicine. While widely integrated into adult emergency medicine and increasingly introduced in undergraduate medical education, its adoption in pediatric residency programs remains limited. There is a lack of standardized curricula, and little is known about pediatric attending physicians’ perspectives on POCUS education — who are key to curriculum development, supervision, and institutional implementation. This study aimed to assess attitudes, training experiences, and perceived barriers among pediatric faculty regarding POCUS integration into pediatric residency training.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was distributed to pediatric attending physicians at two large, urban, tertiary care centers. The survey included demographic data, clinical POCUS use, prior training, perceived importance, and barriers implementing a POCUS program. Data was collected using REDCap and analyzed using descriptive statistics and subgroup comparisons.ResultsOf 1,000 invited faculty, 134 responded (13.4%). POCUS was used in pediatric clinical practice by 28%, primarily in emergency medicine, anesthesia, critical care, and surgery (usage ≥ 75%). Of the 28% of respondents who said they use POCUS, 84% of them stated they received some level of training, meaning 16% of providers who use POCUS report never having formal training. Faculty with fewer years in practice were significantly more likely to have received POCUS education during residency and to express interest in further training (p < 0.05). Overall, 90% of faculty supported integrating POCUS into pediatric residency programs, with the highest support in procedural specialties and among those with prior training. Common barriers included limited faculty availability, lack of equipment, time constraints, and funding needs. Perceived clinical relevance varied by specialty and training background: only half of faculty believed POCUS would have improved clinical care in the past month.ConclusionPediatric faculty broadly support incorporating POCUS into residency education, especially in high-acuity specialties and among more recently trained physicians. However, training gaps and infrastructure barriers persist. These findings highlight a need for structured, specialty-sensitive curricula, faculty development, and institutional investment to align pediatric training with evolving clinical standards and trainee expectations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08838-z.Keywords: POCUS, Ultrasound, Education, Medical education, Faculty, Residency, Pediatrics

Background

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a real-time, bedside diagnostic and procedural tool used by clinicians to answer focused clinical questions or guide interventions [1–6]. Its clinical value is well-established across adult medicine where POCUS has been shown to improve patient outcomes, reduce procedural complications, expedite decision-making, lower healthcare costs, and decrease reliance on ionizing radiation [4–7]. Accordingly, it is widely adopted in adult emergency medicine, critical care, and internal medicine, and is now a core component of many adult residency training programs [1, 8].

In contrast, pediatric residency programs have been slower to adopt POCUS as a standard educational component [1, 9–11]. While subspecialties such as pediatric emergency medicine, anesthesia, and critical care have begun integrating POCUS into practice — supported by specialty-specific fellowships and guidance from the American Board of Pediatrics and the American Academy of Pediatrics — general pediatric training still lacks standardized curricula and competency benchmarks [3, 8, 10–13].

Meanwhile, undergraduate medical education has rapidly expanded POCUS instruction. Increasing numbers of medical schools are introducing ultrasound as part of the core curriculum, providing students with foundational skills and raising expectations for continued training during residency [14]. However, most pediatric residency programs have not kept pace. Although some offer exposure through electives or didactic sessions, these opportunities are not typically standardized for clinical proficiency [14–16]. The disconnect between early exposure in medical school and limited training in pediatric residency creates a widening educational gap.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that pediatric residents strongly support POCUS education and view it as an essential clinical skill [16–19]. Yet, significant barriers to implementation remain, including a lack of structured curricula, limited faculty expertise, and competing educational priorities [10, 19–22]. This fragmented infrastructure risks depriving trainees of a skill that is becoming increasingly standard in other areas of medicine [17, 18, 21, 23].

While residents’ perspectives on POCUS have been explored, far less is known about the views of pediatric attending physicians who play a central role in curricular design, clinical supervision, and institutional prioritization. Their attitudes, training experiences, and perceived barriers are critical to implementing effective and sustainable POCUS programs in pediatric residency training.

This study aimed to address this gap by surveying pediatric faculty at two large academic medical centers. Specifically, we assessed faculty support for POCUS education in residency, described their clinical use and training backgrounds, and identified perceived barriers to curricular integration. These insights are intended to inform institutional strategies and guide the development of a scalable, specialty-sensitive approach to pediatric POCUS education.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of pediatric attending physicians at two large urban tertiary care academic hospitals: Children’s National Hospital in Washington, DC, and Cohen Children’s Medical Center in New York over a 2-month period. Both institutions support pediatric residency programs with more than 30 residents per year. The survey (see Additional File 1) was distributed via institutional listservs and targeted all pediatric faculty members across a broad range of subspecialties.

Survey instrument

The survey instrument was developed in collaboration with pediatric POCUS faculty and residency program leadership to ensure content validity. This survey was only developed for the study and has not been previously published. It included items on respondent demographics, clinical use of POCUS, prior training experiences, attitudes toward POCUS education in residency, and perceived barriers to implementation. Attitudes were rated using a 5-point Likert scale.

Data collection

Survey responses were collected and managed using REDCap, a secure, HIPAA-compliant electronic data capture platform. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and uncompensated. Completion of the survey was considered implied informed consent. All participants were required to affirm their agreement with a standardized definition of POCUS prior to initiation of the survey.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondent characteristics and survey responses. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Comparative analyses were performed to examine associations between faculty characteristics and key outcomes, using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata (Version 17, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA; www.stata.com).

POCUS utilization in practice

Among respondents, only 38 of 134 (28%) reported using POCUS in their routine clinical practice. POCUS use was most common among emergency medicine (75%), anesthesia (75%), pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) (80%), and surgery (80%) in comparison to primary care and other subspecialties (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Of those using POCUS, it was used most for clinical applications (95%), but also for educational purposes (40%) and research purposes (26%). Of those attendings who reported using POCUS in clinical practice, 50% were billing for their scans. There was no statistically significant association between years in practice and current POCUS utilization.

Emergency medicine and anesthesia reported the highest rates of formal POCUS training (90% and 92%, respectively). In contrast, PICU/NICU had relatively high formal training rates (70%) and high clinical use (80%), while cardiology and radiology reported low formal POCUS training (14%) and low clinical utilization of POCUS (8%, p < 0.001; Table 2).

Faculty with formal POCUS training were significantly more likely to use POCUS in clinical practice (62% [32 of 52 respondents] versus 8% [6 of 81 respondents], p < 0.001).

Interest in POCUS training

Faculty with fewer years in practice demonstrated significantly greater interest in additional formal POCUS training (79% versus 61%, p = 0.035). Faculty with formal POCUS training were significantly more likely to be interested in POCUS education (88% versus 56%, p < 0.001).

Interest also varied by subspecialty (p < 0.001), with the highest enthusiasm reported among clinicians in emergency medicine (95%), anesthesiology (92%), surgical specialties (90%), and hospital medicine (89%). In contrast, only 7% of traditional ultrasound users (e.g., cardiology and radiology) expressed interest in additional formal POCUS training. Interest also was higher among inpatient-focused specialties (72%) compared to outpatient specialties (48%, p < 0.001).

Support for POCUS training in pediatric residency

Support for incorporating POCUS education into pediatric residency programs was 90%. Faculty with less than 10 years in practice were significantly more likely to support POCUS education compared to those with more than 10 years of experience (98% versus 85%, p = 0.015). Faculty with previous formal POCUS training were significantly more likely to support POCUS education compared to those without formal POCUS training (98% versus 86%, p = 0.028; Fig. 1).

Subspecialty support varied significantly with 100% support among hospital medicine, anesthesia and surgical specialties (p = 0.032); emergency medicine and NICU/PICU followed closely at 90%. Traditional ultrasound users showed the lowest support at 67%.

POCUS perceived importance

Perceptions of POCUS’s impact on patient care differed significantly across subspecialties. When asked about its importance in the past month, emergency medicine, anesthesia, and surgical specialties had the highest rates of “very important" (p < 0.001). These same groups also rated POCUS as “most important” to overall patient care (p = 0.01). In contrast, inpatient subspecialties and traditional ultrasound users reported significantly lower perceived value in both domains.

Perceptions of POCUS importance differed notably by training background. Faculty with formal POCUS training were significantly more likely to rate POCUS education as “somewhat important,” “important,” or “very important” overall (91% versus 77%, p = 0.015). Similarly, they were more likely to support making POCUS a procedural requirement for pediatric residents (70% versus 56%, p = 0.030). When asked about its relevance in recent patient care, 87% of formally trained faculty rated POCUS as at least “somewhat important,” compared to 63% of untrained faculty (p = 0.010).

Faculty’s perceived barriers to POCUS implementation

Perceived barriers to POCUS education varied significantly across subspecialties. Availability of equipment was most frequently cited among hospital medicine (94%), PICU/NICU (78%), anesthesia (83%), and outpatient specialties (80%) (p = 0.002). Funding to support POCUS programs was a notable concern in hospital medicine (89%) and anesthesia (75%) (p < 0.001). Additionally, the lack of POCUS-trained staff was most often reported by emergency medicine (75%), hospital medicine (72%), and traditional ultrasound users such as cardiology and radiology (65%) (p = 0.040).

When asked for additional barriers using an open-ended format, other answers included: “uninterested residents,” “likelihood that focus on POCUS will further deteriorate pediatric resident physical exam skills,” “should be limited to pediatric fellowship training,” “services being territorial over billing for procedures,” “not sure of benefit in general practice nor time and reimbursement,” “insurance coverage and malpractice,” and “residents are under-trained at routine skills and should focus on those before using POCUS.”

Discussion

90% of the pediatric faculty surveyed supported integrating POCUS into residency training, aligning with the strong interest previously documented among residents [16–19]. This convergence suggests institutional readiness to move from ad hoc exposure toward structured, competency‑based curricula. Nevertheless, the low rate of current clinical use outside high‑acuity/procedural specialties and the sizeable training and infrastructure gaps reveal that implementation will require targeted, specialty‑sensitive strategies.

When thinking about POCUS adoption into pediatric residency, we can apply the framework discussed in Roger’s Diffusion of Innovation framework [24]. POCUS exhibits clear relative advantage (rapid, radiation‑free bedside information), compatibility with evolving bedside diagnostics, and strong observability in high‑acuity settings [24]. However, complexity (skill acquisition, quality assurance, image archiving, credentialing, billing) and constrained trialability (limited faculty and equipment) impede diffusion in general pediatrics and traditional ultrasound specialties [24]. These barriers can be addressed through: (1) phased, milestone‑aligned curricula; (2) faculty development with credentialing pathways and protected time; (3) pragmatic infrastructure (archiving, quality assurance (QA), billing workflows) to normalize practice and fund sustainability; and (4) subspecialty‑tailored use cases to enhance perceived relevance where clinical utility is less obvious.

Only half of respondents believed POCUS would have improved care in the previous month, indicating unrealized potential. This gap may reflect limited access, entrenched practice patterns, and lack of faculty role modeling rather than a true absence of value. Embedding POCUS into routine rounds, checklists, and procedure supervision, coupled with image review and QA, can normalize its use and build local evidence of benefit. The observation that only 50% of users bill for scans emphasizes missed opportunities to support sustainability and institutional acceptance.

Generational trends are encouraging; recently graduated faculty were more likely to be exposed to POCUS during residency or fellowship, to value it, and to want more. These early adopters can be leveraged as champions for curricular design, faculty development, and QA leadership.

Detailed cost analyses for POCUS program implementation are scarce in the literature, as most studies focus on educational outcomes and barriers rather than itemized expenses; however, several key cost domains have been identified. Equipment acquisition is consistently cited as a major barrier, requiring ultrasound machines with appropriate transducers and sufficient availability to maintain low learner-to-instructor ratios [25]. Faculty development represents another significant investment, given that lack of trained instructors is among the most frequently reported obstacles [9, 26]. Additional costs include infrastructure for image review and quality assurance, simulation resources, and educational materials [26]. Programs seeking cost efficiency often leverage external training courses and interdisciplinary faculty, whereas comprehensive in-house curricula demand greater upfront investment but offer more control and sustainability.

Barriers to POCUS integration varied substantially across subspecialties, emphasizing the need for tailored solutions. For example, hospital medicine and critical care faculty frequently cited equipment limitations as the primary obstacle. These groups expressed strong interest in adopting POCUS but lack the basic infrastructure — without adequate machines and transducers, even motivated educators cannot begin implementation. In contrast, specialties such as emergency medicine and radiology typically have ready access to ultrasound equipment; however, their challenge lies in securing dedicated POCUS champions to lead curriculum development, faculty training, and quality assurance. These contextual differences suggest that implementation strategies should be specialty-specific: addressing equipment acquisition and resource allocation for inpatient and critical care settings, while prioritizing leadership and faculty development in departments where equipment is available but structured programs have not yet been established. Faculty support for POCUS integration in pediatric residency aligns with national trends observed in other disciplines. Emergency medicine programs were among the earliest adopters and now include POCUS as a core competency, supported by American College of Emergency Physicians guidelines and near-universal integration into residency curricula [1, 5, 8, 12]. Internal medicine programs have made significant progress toward structured curricula, with expert consensus identifying core diagnostic and procedural applications, though adoption remains variable nationwide [26]. Critical care training has fully embraced POCUS, with the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) requiring competence as part of fellowship training [27]. In contrast, pediatric programs remain less standardized, highlighting a need for targeted strategies to close this gap and align with broader efforts to embed POCUS as a core competency across medical education.

Strengths and limitations

This study adds faculty‑level data to literature dominated by resident perspectives, spans two large academic medical centers, and disaggregates results by subspecialty and career stage. This study is subject to several limitations. The response rate was 13.4%, which raises the possibility of nonresponse bias. Faculty with preexisting interest in POCUS may have been more likely to complete the survey, potentially inflating estimates of support for POCUS education and reported utilization. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and may not fully represent the views of all pediatric faculty. Additionally, both institutions are large, urban, tertiary care centers; results may not generalize to smaller or rural programs. Subspecialty group sizes varied, and self-reported data may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Furthermore, this study did not employ qualitative or mixed methods approaches to explore faculty strategies for integrating POCUS into pediatric residency curricula, advocating for funding, or prioritizing applications for general pediatric practice, which limits insights into implementation pathways. Finally, the survey did not assess clinical competency or the effectiveness of existing POCUS training models, limiting conclusions about educational outcomes. Along with this, the timing and format of POCUS training may influence faculty confidence and utilization. While our survey identified when training occurred, it did not capture details about training structure or intensity. Future studies should explore these factors to better understand how educational experiences shape clinical adoption and perceived relevance.

Future studies

Future studies should aim to include a broader sample of pediatric programs, including community hospitals, to improve generalizability. Qualitative studies also may help clarify specific needs and implementation strategies tailored to various institutional contexts. Future research should consider benchmarking pediatric POCUS integration against adult internal medicine programs, which have established structured curricula and competency standards, to better understand gaps and opportunities for alignment.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates strong and widespread support among pediatric faculty for the integration of POCUS education into pediatric residency training. Support was highest in procedural and acute care specialties, aligning with current clinical usage and perceived relevance. Despite clear interest, significant barriers remain, including limited faculty expertise, equipment availability, and time for skill acquisition.

These findings highlight a critical opportunity and growing need for structured, specialty-sensitive implementation strategies and faculty development initiatives. As POCUS becomes increasingly embedded in medical education and practice, pediatric residency programs are well-positioned to adopt and lead innovative, high-value training models that align with the evolving standards of pediatric care.
