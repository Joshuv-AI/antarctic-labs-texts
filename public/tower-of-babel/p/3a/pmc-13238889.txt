”Not always the magic bullet”—Insufficient seizure control by ketogenic dietary therapies in Glut1 Deficiency Syndrome
Authors: Joerg Klepper, Eva Runkel, Lucia Kiesel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveKetogenic dietary therapies (KDTs) are the treatment of choice for Glut1 Deficiency Syndrome (Glut1DS), providing dietary ketones as an alternative fuel to the brain and effectively controlling seizures. Recent evidence indicates insufficient seizure control in Glut1DS patients despite adequate KDT and ketosis.MethodsFifty‐three patients, diagnosed with Glut1DS and treated by KDT, were followed in a single‐center outpatient clinic from 2000 to 2023. Epilepsy, present in 44 patients, was analyzed for seizure control, EEG changes, and potential correlations to clinical and genetic features. Epilepsy response to KDT was defined as a >90% seizure reduction on KDT monotherapy.ResultsOn KDT monotherapy 27/44 (61%) patients became >90% seizure free, rated as responders. 17/44 were non‐responders. Within this group, 10/17 patients responded to KDT plus antiseizure comedication. In 7 patients, KDT plus antiseizure comedication failed to control seizures. No correlations of seizure control to gender, age at start or type of KDT, or SLC2A1 variants were observed. In responders (n = 27), EEG epileptic activity, evident in 15/27 patients, improved on KDT in 5 patients. In non‐responders (n = 17), EEG epileptic activity was evident in 14/17 and improved on KDT in 9 patients. EEG background slowing prior to KDT normalized in KDT in all responders (4/27), but in none of the non‐responders (4/17).SignificanceEpilepsy is a dominant feature of Glut1DS. KDT provides efficient seizure control, but failure to control epilepsy is more common than expected. KDT epilepsy response did not correlate with seizure type, clinical, or genetic features, emphasizing the complexity of this entity. Add‐on antiseizure medication can be effective in some patients, without individual drug superiority. Epileptic activity on EEG did not prove a good marker for outcome, but reversible EEG background slowing on KDT might be predictive for favorable seizure control.Plain Language SummaryImpaired glucose transport into the brain causes a brain energy crisis termed Glut1 Deficiency. Ketogenic diets mimic fasting, provide ketones as an alternative fuel, and effectively restore brain function. In our study, this worked for ca. 60% of patients. To our surprise, epilepsy persisted in about 40% of patients despite ketogenic diets for reasons unknown. Additional medication against epilepsy randomly helped in some patients. We analyzed many parameters without finding obvious explanations. Electrical brain activity improvement on ketogenic diets might be a future marker for efficient seizure control.

INTRODUCTION

Glut1 Deficiency Syndrome (Glut1DS) is caused by impaired glucose transport into brain and results in complex epileptic encephalopathy. Major clinical features are intractable childhood epilepsy, global developmental delay, and movement disorders with constant and paroxysmal elements of ataxia, dyskinesia, and spasticity. Age‐specific seizure types are cyanotic spells, early‐onset absences, myoclonic and generalized seizures, often starting in late infancy and generally unresponsive to antiseizure medication (ASM). 1 , 2 , 3 Ketogenic Dietary Therapies (KDT) remain the gold standard for treatment since 1991, following the concept that ketones serve as an alternative fuel to the brain. 4 In Glut1DS, seizures generally respond very well to KDT. However, with growing numbers of patients there are increasing reports on failure of KDT to control epilepsy despite adequate ketosis. 5 , 6 Potential contributing factors discussed recently include genotypic variability in SLC2A1, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired blood–brain barrier transport, hormonal influences, and epigenetic regulation. 7

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Glut1DS was diagnosed in 53 patients based on clinical features, hypoglycorrhachia, and/or pathological SLC2A1 variants as described 1 (Table 1A). SLC2A1 variants were classified in category A mutations (missense) and category B mutations (nonsense, frameshift or splice site). 2 , 7 All patients were followed on 6–12 month‐intervals at a single‐center outpatient clinic (Aschaffenburg Childrens' Hospital). Consent was obtained from patients and parents/caregivers for data analysis and publication. At each visit patients were seen by a single physician (JK) obtaining the patient's history, clinical examination, laboratory blood analysis (full blood count, electrolytes, liver/kidney parameters, 3‐hydroxy‐butyrate [3OHB], lipid status and blood levels of ASM). By two neuropediatricians (JK and ER). Data was collected, pseudo‐anonymized, and evaluated within a three‐year time‐period (January 2020 to December 2023) (Table 1B).

The data were then processed using R statistical software, version 4.1.3. Continuous variables were checked for normal distribution using the Shapiro test. If the distribution was normal, the t‐test was used to test for a significant difference. If the variable was not normally distributed, the Mann‐Withney test was used. For qualitative or dichotomous characteristics, significance was calculated using the chi‐square test and for small sample sizes (n < 5 in a subgroup) Fisher's exact test was used. The significance level was set at 5%. To describe the data, adequate localization and dispersion measures were calculated in the respective groups. Metric variables are described with median and range; nominal data are presented in absolute and relative proportions.

Ketosis varies with age and may range from 0.3–6.0 mmol/L 3‐hydroxy(3OHB) per definition. 8 Accordingly, in our study adequate ketosis was defined as 3OHB values of 1.0 mmol/L to 6.0 mmol/L depending on age and type of KDT. In infants and preschool children, minimal ketosis was defined as >2 mmol/L, 7 whereas in school‐age children, adolescents, and adults minimal 3OHB values of >1.0 mmol/L were acceptable. Patients checked capillary 3OHB usually once daily, but at least once a week at the same time of day. Measurements reflect both parents'/patients' 3OHB values in diaries as well as 3OHB values measured at the outpatient appointments in our institution.

Epilepsy response to KDT was defined as a >90% seizure reduction on KDT monotherapy. Seizures were based on clinical history, mobile phone video from parents, and video‐EEGs if available. Seizure types reflect all seizures types within the time period given (2000–2023). Epileptic EEG discharges were indicative for epilepsy, EEG background slowing indicative for developmental delay. EEGs were recorded in wakefulness only and routinely recorded for a minimum of 40 min. Provocation methods (photic stimulation and hyperventilation) were applied routinely unless contraindicated. Clinical seizures were not captured in any patients. Non‐automated EEG analysis for epileptic activity (seizure control) and background slowing (developmental delay) was performed by two neuropediatricians (JK and ER) and described according to standard EEG evaluation guidelines. 9

RESULTS

The diagnosis of Glut1DS was made in 53 patients as described. 1 Epilepsy, either isolated or in combination with movement abnormalities and/or developmental delay, was seen in 44 patients (17 males/27 females) (Figure 1A). Mean patients' age was 12.8 years (range 2–23 years) at the time of the last follow‐up. The mean age at diagnosis matched the mean age at KDT initiation (nine months). Glut1DS phenotype distribution is shown in Figure 1B. All patients attended clinics every 6–12 months and none were lost to follow‐up.

Lumbar puncture

CSF data closely matched reference values for Glut1DS as reviewed by Leen et al. 10 CSF glucose levels ranged from 17 to 50 mg/dL (Leen et al.: 16.2 to 50.5 mg/dL). CSF/blood glucose ratios ranged from 0.20 to 0.63 (Leen et al.: 0.19 to 0.59). CSF lactate levels ranged from 0.4 to 1.3 mmol/L (Leen et al.: 0.6 to 1.5 mmol/L), excluding CSF data on patient #43 as the lumbar puncture was reportedly contaminated with blood and, as such, invalid (Table 1A).

SLC2A1 variants

Heterozygous, autosomal‐dominant SLC2A1 variants were identified in all but one patient (pat. #41). Mutations were classified as type A mutations (missense mutations) and type B mutations (nonsense and frameshift mutations, deletions, insertions, intronic, and splice‐site mutations), 4 as shown in Table 1A. Neither the location of the SLC2A1 variant nor the severity of the mutation (type A or type B) was correlated to epilepsy control (Figure 2).

KDT

All patients remained on various KDT with adequate ketosis throughout follow‐up. Start and type of KDT as well as the epilepsy response to KDT in monotherapy or in combination with antiepileptic drugs (ASM) are shown in Table 1B.

Epilepsy

Age at first seizure, seizure type, EEG findings, and epilepsy response are shown in Table 1B. Seizure onset did not differ significantly between responders (1.8 years) and non‐responders (1.6 years). Seizure types varied with isolated or combined focal, generalized tonic–clonic, and/or typical and atypical absence seizures. Combined seizure types were present in 21/44 patients—in patient #13 seizures remained unclassified. No specific seizure type was predominant and no significant difference of seizure types was observed between responders and nonresponders to KDT.

Epilepsy response to KDT and ASM. In patients #01–27 (Table 1B) a >90% seizure reduction was achieved by KDT monotherapy (“responders”). In patients #28–44 (Table 1B) seizure control was insufficient by KDT alone (“nonresponders”). In Table 1B we only listed the AEDs which were applied in combination/following KDT, and in therapeutic doses or plasma levels. Discontinuation due to side effects was neglectable; in general AEDs were discontinued due to inefficacy to control epilepsy. In nonresponders, multiple ASMs were used in combination with KDT, predominantly ethosuximide and levetiracetam, without evidence of a single drug providing superior seizure control (Figure 3).

EEG epileptic activity varied, mainly as a combination of 2.5‐ to 4‐Hz spike–wave discharges, focal and generalized tonic–clonic epileptiform discharges as described previously. 11 Single seizure types were seen in 20/44 patients (generalized tonic–clonic: n = 9; absence: n = 7; focal: n = 4).

In nonresponders, epileptic discharges, evident in 14/17 patients before KDT, resolved in 5 patients on KDT (Figure 5B). DISCUSSION

Cardinal Glut1DS features are epilepsy, movement disorders, and developmental delay. Our results confirm previous reports that the predominant Glut1DS phenotype is epileptic (44/53 patients) in combination with other clinical features. 12 Isolated epilepsy, evident in 2/44 patients, seems rare (Figure 1a). Our results are encouraging as on average Glut1DS diagnosis and KDT initiation were made early at the mean age of nine months, indicating that with the diagnosis of Glut1DS physicians are aware of KDT as the therapeutic gold standard as described. 1

When analyzing treatment efficacy in this entity, KDT in Glut1DS is considered a prototype of precision medicine, indicating that the pathogenic mechanism is met by specific treatment targeting the cause of the disease. 13 For over 30 years, KDT remains the gold standard of Glut1DS therapy, but not every symptom of Glut1DS responds equally well to KDT. “Failure of KDT” in the literature is obscuring as it may relate to epilepsy, movement, or development abnormalities. Many studies do not clearly state which clinical features improved and which KDT was used. Also, studies are limited due to the complex and age‐dependent Glut1DS phenotype.

Epilepsy is the most prominent symptom in Glut1DS and rarely an isolated clinical feature—73% of patients in our cohort presented with the classical combined phenotype (Figure 1b). Only 9/53 patients presented without epilepsy. 61% responders to KDT monotherapy reflect previous reports: 67% responders in 87 patients described retrospectively by Pong et al., 14 and 62% responders in 37 patients summarized by Leen et al. 2 It is lower than the 80% reported for Glut1DS by Kass et al., 6 but significantly higher than estimated for KDT efficacy in intractable childhood epilepsy. 15

KDT failure for epilepsy was recently brought back to attention by a retrospective study of Bekker et al. describing seven Glut1DS patients with refractory epilepsy. Potential causes given by the authors were KDT inefficacy, intolerance, and an inability to attain ketosis, a special cohort with advanced age at seizure onset, female sex, and advanced age at diagnosis and initiation of KDT. 5 Our results in a much larger cohort confirm insufficient seizure control by KDT in one third of Glut1DS patients; however, without correlations to the above parameters.

Pharmacoresistance

Epilepsy in Glut1DS is considered pharmaco‐resistant, but most reports relate to ASMs given prior to KDT. There is poor data on the recurrence of seizures in Glut1DS patients on KDT plus ASMs. In our study, KDT plus ASM was effective in 23% of non‐responders to KDT monotherapy, indicating that here ASM comedication is an option. Data on the combination of KDT and antiepileptic drugs is limited. 16 In‐vitro data identified barbiturates and valproic acid as Glut1 transport inhibitors to be avoided. 17 , 18 On KDT, valproic acid comedication may partially inhibit beta‐oxidation of fatty acids. Acetazolamide, topiramate, and zonisamide inhibit carbonic anhydrase and may potentiate the metabolic acidosis in combination with KDT. 4 , 19 Concomitant lamotrigine decreased the efficacy of KDT in childhood refractory epilepsy. 20 Malagá et al. reported a 50% seizure reduction by acetazolamide in 10/17 Glut1DS patients—however, this drug was ineffective in patient #44 of our cohort (Figure 5). 21 In our study, ethosuximide was most frequently used in both responders and non‐responders, followed by levetiracetam and valproic acid (Figure 5). Levetiracetam and ethosuximide were the most used ASMs, but we could not identify superior seizure control for either drug. In non‐responders to KDT, ethosuximide controlled seizures in 5/17 patients, whereas levetiracetam controlled seizures in 3/17 (Figure 3A,B). In the same group, ethosuximide also failed to control seizures in 5/17 patients, whereas levetiracetam failed to control seizures in 2/17 patients (Figure 3C,D). These results and Figure 3 illustrate the difficulty to assess ASM impact on epilepsy in Glut1DS. As no particular medication proved superior no recommendation for individual ASMs can be drawn from our study.

EEGs on KDT

Unlike previous reports 5 , 22 EEG epileptic activity in our study did not prove a good marker for seizure control by KDT. The degree of EEG background slowing (18%) matched previous data of 11%–21%, described in 20 untreated Glut1DS patients. 11 EEG slowing might be due to the energy deficit in the brain, as remarkably, EEG background slowing normalized in all responders (4/4), but not in non‐responders (0/4). Although numbers are small, this novel observation indicates that reversal of EEG background slowing could potentially serve as an EEG‐marker for epilepsy control by KDT in Glut1DS.

SLC2A1 variants

Our results did not show that type B mutations cause a milder epileptic phenotype, 2 , 4 but the effects on movement abnormalities or developmental delay were not studied. As such, phenotype–genotype correlations could not be identified in our study.

CONCLUSION

Over 30 years, epilepsy in Glut1DS has been reported as being effectively controlled by KDT. However, the substantial number of nonresponders in our study emphasizes the need for concomitant and novel treatments. The crucial question—why do KDT fail to control epilepsy in individual patients—could not be answered despite a wide search for potential parameters or correlations. Limitations of the study are the complexity of Glut1DS phenotype and genotype, the variable KDT applied, and the isolated focus on epilepsy. A novel finding of our study is the reversal of EEG background slowing that may indicate favorable seizure control by KDT in Glut1DS. Response to KDT in Glut1DS needs to be specified according to symptoms—here we focused on epilepsy, as seizures can be monitored well by protocols and EEG. The focus on epilepsy, however, is misleading, as effects of KDT on development and movement are just as crucial, but more prolonged and more difficult to monitor. As such, controlling seizures in Glut1DS is only the “tip of the iceberg”. The need to elucidate further pathogenic mechanisms, identify those at risk for KDT failure, and develop alternative treatment strategies is compelling.
