Dynamic weight and sensitive factor-based geological suitability evaluation of underground space development in Yangtze delta’s Hangjiahu plain
Authors: Liang He, Liting Ma, Shuhan Shi, Yun Huang, Wenke Liu, Chao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes a comprehensive evaluation model integrating dynamic weighting (DW) and sensitive factor evaluation (SFE) to assess underground space development in the Hangjiahu Plain. The model constructs a refined index system by analyzing the engineering geological conditions across shallow (0–10 m), middle (10–30 m), and deep (30–60 m) layers, and introduces variable weight theory to dynamically adjust the weight, thus addressing key limitations of the traditional fixed-weight model. The results showed that the area of the forbidden development zone of Grade IV in the shallow layer increased substantially and was mainly affected by the thickness of the soft soil. The area of Grades I and II in the middle layer decreased from 97.42% to 51.49%, while the area of Grade IV in the deep layer increased to 81.96%. All sensitive factors are located in the Grade IV area, which is further expanded after weight adjustment, indicating that the model can effectively identify high-risk areas. The model was further examined through a rail transit planning and route selection case in the study area, providing contextual support for its applicability at the regional planning stage. This study provides a theoretical basis for sustainable urban underground space utilization and a reference for similar geological regions.Keywords: Underground space development, Engineering geological conditions, Evaluation model, Variable weight model, Hangjiahu PlainSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Natural hazards, Space physics, Risk factors

Introduction

With the continuous improvement in the global urbanization level, the demand pressure on urban space capacity is increasing. Urban underground space refers to areas below the earth’s surface that can be utilized for urban development, including but not limited to underground commercial facilities, transportation tunnels, parking facilities, storage areas, and utility corridors1. These spaces serve a variety of functions, such as supporting urban transportation systems, providing storage solutions, facilitating commercial activities, and offering residential or mixed-use purposes2. During urban modernization, underground space has become increasingly obvious as an important resource for urban space expansion. Underground space is not renewable3; therefore, a reasonable and effective method must be applied before the development of such a space. Blind or fragmented development can easily cause serious problems such as the chaotic layout of underground space buildings4, low effective utilization ratio of underground space resources, environmental pollution, ground subsidence, and stratum deformation5. The establishment of a scientific, effective method for urban underground space development can effectively protect reserve resources and promote the sustainable use of urban underground space resources.

Engineering geological conditions, including topography, formation lithology, geological structure, and hydrology, are key factors that determine the feasibility and safety of underground space development methods6. These conditions are directly related to the stability, construction difficulty, and cost of underground structures. Engineering geological conditions directly affect underground space development7, and a dynamic balanced relationship of mutual restriction exists between them. If this effect exceeds the balance range that engineering geological conditions can bear, a series of engineering geological problems such as ground collapse and groundwater pollution will occur8. Therefore, during the development of underground spaces, engineering geological conditions must be considered, and their suitability needs to be evaluated.

In studies that evaluate the suitability of engineering geological conditions for urban underground space development, appropriate evaluation indexes must be selected and these indexes must be assigned values to realize an effective evaluation9. At present, commonly used index weighting methods include the analytic hierarchy process10, entropy weight method11, negative list method12, and neural network method13. Most of these methods rely on fixed weights for the evaluation criteria, which limits their ability to adapt to dynamic conditions. These methods also fail to incorporate sensitive factors that may have a disproportionate impact on underground space suitability, such as active faults or groundwater conditions14. Moreover, some sensitive engineering geological conditions, such as active faults and earthquakes, have not been sufficiently considered in the selection of indexes. In addition, given the complexity of the engineering geological conditions of underground space, this space exhibits substantial differences at different depths15, which need to be studied at different levels. However, only a few studies have evaluated the suitability of urban underground engineering geological conditions at different levels, and problems such as imperfect evaluation index systems and lack of unity and pertinence, persist.

In parallel with geological suitability evaluation, recent studies have increasingly explored digital twin and BIM-based frameworks to enhance underground construction through real-time monitoring, design–construction integration, and construction-stage optimization16. These intelligent systems have demonstrated strong potential in improving structural stability analysis, excavation control, and construction efficiency17. However, such approaches primarily focus on the construction and operation stages of underground engineering, and their effectiveness relies on detailed site-specific data and project-level models. In contrast, the planning-stage evaluation of underground space suitability still depends largely on geological condition assessment at a regional scale, highlighting the continued need for robust and interpretable evaluation frameworks to support early-stage decision-making. Recent studies have also highlighted the importance of uncertainty characterization in geoscientific and engineering-related modeling18. These studies further indicate that uncertainty-aware and interpretable parameter-response mechanisms are increasingly important in regional geoscientific evaluation frameworks, particularly under conditions of strong spatial heterogeneity and multi-factor geological interactions.

Existing underground space evaluation methods often rely on fixed weights and fail to account for dynamic changes in factors or the influence of sensitive factors. These limitations can lead to inaccurate suitability assessments and hinder the effective utilization of underground space. The variable weight model is a method of dynamically adjusting weights based on the actual situation, and it can flexibly assign weights in accordance with the actual situation of different evaluation indexes and their importance differences in different environments. Hence, it can overcome the shortcomings of traditional methods in weight distribution to a certain extent19. The dynamic weight adjustment method of the variable weight model can accurately reflect the real influence of each index on the suitability of engineering geological conditions while fully considering the relationship between the indexes and their changes in different situations20. For example, the influence of groundwater conditions varies with depth and site conditions. In areas with shallow groundwater tables and poor drainage, groundwater-related risks are more pronounced, particularly for shallow and mid-depth developments, and the corresponding weight is increased. In contrast, in areas with deeper groundwater tables or deeper development layers, the relative importance of groundwater conditions is reduced.

This study adopted Jiaxing City, the core city of the Hangjiahu Plain in the Yangtze River Delta in China, as an example. The area’s geological complexity, including varying soil types and groundwater conditions, presents unique challenges for underground space development, making it an ideal case study21. Additionally, Jiaxing’s role as a key city in the region and its ongoing infrastructure development make it particularly relevant for studying the suitability of underground space, as it is facing pressure to optimize the use of limited land resources. In this study, a new comprehensive evaluation model that combines dynamic weighting (DW) and sensitive factor evaluation (SFE) was proposed to evaluate the suitability of engineering geological conditions for underground space development. This model, called DW-SFE, establishes a suitability evaluation index of engineering geological conditions for shallow (0–10 m), middle (10–30 m), and deep (30–60 m) space development and constructs a corresponding evaluation index system. Considering the topography, geotechnical and engineering geological conditions, hydrogeological conditions, and adverse geological effects of each level, the factors were divided into common and sensitive factors to accurately construct a comprehensive evaluation model. The contribution of this study lies in integrating dynamic weight adjustment for common factors with an explicit sensitive-factor evaluation procedure within a GIS-based suitability framework. Rather than proposing an entirely new weighting theory, the primary methodological contribution of this study lies in integrating dynamic weight adjustment with an explicit sensitive-factor evaluation procedure within a GIS-based suitability framework. The proposed framework is intended to provide a more constraint-sensitive and planning-oriented extension of conventional AHP-GIS evaluation for underground space development.

Materials and methods

Overview of the study area

The study area includes Nanhu District, Xiuzhou District, Haining City, Pinghu City, Tongxiang City, Jiashan County, and Haiyan County. The urban area covers an area of 3,915 km2. The geographical coordinates are 30°21’–31°02′N and 120°18′–121°16′E. The geographical location is shown in Fig. 1. The urbanization process of Jiaxing City is accelerating, and the urbanization rate has reached 74.2% by 2023. The population continues to grow and the demand for urban spaces is increasing22. In recent years, Jiaxing has actively promoted the use of underground spaces, especially in central urban areas and emerging development zones. On October 21, 2024, the Special Planning for Underground Space of Jiaxing High-Speed Railway New City was officially opened. The planning scope was from the east to the Fengyu branch line, from the south to the Chajiasu high-speed railway, from the west to the Shanghai–Hangzhou railway, and from the north to Changshui Road. The total area was approximately 50 km2, revealing the potential for underground space development in Jiaxing City.

The engineering geological conditions in Jiaxing City are complex and diverse, covering various geological structures and stratigraphic types23. The stratum 60 m below the surface in the study area is mainly composed of sediments from the Middle and Late Quaternary, and the soil types and engineering characteristics in different depth ranges vary considerably. From a vertical perspective, the subsurface stratigraphic structure of the study area exhibits clear depth-dependent characteristics typical of Quaternary sedimentary plains. The shallow layer (0–10 m) is predominantly composed of soft soils such as muddy silty clay, characterized by high water content, low strength, and high compressibility, which poses notable constraints on shallow underground space development. In the middle layer (10–30 m), sandy silt and silty clay are widely distributed, and the mechanical properties of the soil gradually improve compared with the shallow layer. This depth range often exhibits alternating soft and relatively stiff layers, reflecting moderate heterogeneity and providing more favorable conditions for certain types of underground structures under appropriate engineering measures. The deep layer (30–60 m) is mainly composed of denser silty clay and sandy silt formed under long-term consolidation. Although the overall bearing capacity is higher at this depth, the coexistence of thick soft soil layers and confined groundwater introduces additional challenges for deep underground space development. These vertical variations in stratigraphic composition and engineering properties form the geological basis for the depth-dependent suitability evaluation adopted in this study. The shallow–middle–deep evaluation structure adopted in this study also reflects the broader concept of multiscale index construction in complex geological systems, where engineering constraints and geological responses vary significantly across different spatial and stratigraphic scales. Based on the occurrence conditions, hydrophysical properties, and hydraulic characteristics of groundwater, groundwater in this area can be divided into three types: pore groundwater, karst water, and bedrock fissure water.

Construction of the comprehensive evaluation model

A DW-SFE model that combines DW and SFE is proposed. The engineering geological factors affecting the development of underground spaces are divided into common and sensitive factors24, and their evaluation models have been studied. A flowchart of the construction of the comprehensive evaluation model is shown in Fig. 2. The proposed underground space suitability evaluation framework is implemented through a structured, GIS-based workflow. First, geological, hydrogeological, and engineering datasets are collected and preprocessed, including data screening, standardization, and unification to a consistent coordinate system and spatial resolution. Second, evaluation factors are selected and classified according to their relevance to underground space development, and proximity-based factors are further processed using standard GIS buffer analysis to represent their spatial influence ranges. Third, all factor layers are converted into raster format and standardized based on their classification criteria to ensure comparability. A dynamic weighting scheme is then applied to adjust factor weights according to their relative importance under specific conditions, while sensitive factors are evaluated separately and integrated to reflect their disproportionate influence on suitability. Finally, all weighted raster layers are overlaid and integrated within the GIS environment to calculate a comprehensive suitability index, which is visualized as suitability maps to reveal spatial differences in underground space development potential. All spatial processing, buffer analyses and raster calculations were performed in ArcGIS 10.8 (ESRI, USA).

Selection of evaluation factors

Based on extensive existing research results, this study comprehensively analyzed the geological environmental factors that affect the suitability of underground space development in the study area. Five evaluation factors were selected: topographical conditions, geotechnical and engineering geological conditions30, hydrogeological conditions, adverse geological conditions, and structural geological conditions. The selection of the five evaluation factors was guided by three main considerations. First, these factors have been widely adopted in previous underground space suitability studies and are generally recognized as key constraints for underground development. Second, they are closely related to the dominant geological and hydrogeological conditions of Jiaxing City, which is located in a typical soft-soil plain area31. Third, reliable and continuous datasets for these factors are available at the urban scale, ensuring the feasibility and consistency of spatial evaluation32. Therefore, the selected factors are both scientifically representative and practically applicable for underground space suitability assessment in the study area.

(1) The topography is directly related to soil stability, drainage capacity, and construction difficulty33. Studies have shown that areas with high topographic relief and steep slopes are likely to experience soil erosion and landslide risks, which negatively affect the safety of underground projects. Topographic features also affect the flow and distribution of groundwater, thereby influencing the potential development of underground spaces.

(2) The engineering geological characteristics of rock–soil mass directly determine the difficulty of underground cavern excavation and the stability of the surrounding rocks. Relevant studies have shown that the inhomogeneity of the rock–soil mass is the main cause of uneven settlement of structures, which easily generates serious safety problems34. In addition, the existence of soft layers may lead to additional construction difficulties and cost increments. In particular, during the development of urban underground space, the high compressibility and low intensity of soft layers substantially increase the difficulty of underground structure construction35.

(3) Hydrogeological conditions, especially the groundwater level and water quality, considerably influence the development of underground spaces36. The presence of groundwater affects the mechanical properties of soil and may cause water disasters, such as water gushing and leakage. High groundwater levels may generate stability problems in underground structures, and corrosive water quality may accelerate the degradation of structural materials.

(4) Adverse geologic actions such as soil liquefaction and ground subsidence pose a potential threat to engineering projects and the environment. Saturated sand is prone to liquefaction under the action of earthquakes and strong artificial vibration and is often accompanied by large-scale ground subsidence deformation, slumps, ground fissures, water spraying, and sand shooting, resulting in the destruction of subway stations, tunnels, and other projects37. Ground subsidence causes damage to ground buildings, and the settlement of the foundation reflects its stability, which has a direct effect on the safety and use of buildings38.

(5) Structural geological conditions considerably influence the stability of engineered buildings. Common structural geological issues include folds, fractures, and fissures, which cause certain deformations or displacements of the rock mass and destroy the integrity of the original rock mass, especially in fault fracture zones, often leading to the collapse of the surrounding rock of the cavern. Engineering geological conditions involve many factors, besides those mentioned above. However, some factors were not included in this study. For example, in terms of adverse geological conditions, although common conditions, such as soil liquefaction and ground subsidence, are considered, some unique local geological disasters, such as karst collapse, landslides, and debris flows, were not examined in this study because the relevant conditions in Jiaxing City are not prominent or have little effect on the overall engineering geological conditions.

Weight determination

Initial weight

Based on the established evaluation index system, a questionnaire containing judgments of different index weights was designed. The questionnaire evaluates the importance of indexes from topographical conditions to the geological structure. The questionnaire was distributed to experts who had been working in the field of geological environment for many years, and they had rich research and practical experience in geotechnical engineering, hydrogeology, engineering geology, and other related fields. In accordance with the characteristics of the engineering geological conditions in Jiaxing City, the indexes in the questionnaire were scored and weighted. Based on the feedback results, the judgment matrix of the target and criterion layers of the index system for shallow (0–10 m), medium (10–30 m), and deep (30–60 m) underground spaces was obtained using the analytic hierarchy process. Suitability changes across different depth layers

The corresponding area proportions of Grades I–IV under the initial and adjusted weighting schemes are summarized in Fig. 7. Overall, after weight adjustment, the spatial distribution of suitability grades showed significant changes, reflecting the enhanced sensitivity of the proposed DW-SFE model to key geological constraints.

Area proportions of Grades I–IV under the initial and adjusted weighting schemes for the shallow, middle, and deep layers.

For the shallow layer (0–10 m), which corresponds to the construction zone for basement levels, underground road tunnels, and station halls of rail transit, the initial weight evaluation indicated that Grade II areas accounted for 92.03% of the study area, while Grade III covered only 1%. Grade IV zones, caused solely by sensitive factors such as active faults, occupied 2.55%. After weight adjustment, the area of Grade IV increased substantially, Grade III expanded slightly, and Grade II decreased markedly. This shift is attributed to the thick soft soil layers distributed in central Haiyan County, eastern Pinghu City, northern Xiuzhou District, and parts of Haining City, where the overall geotechnical performance is poor, causing their suitability to downgrade from Grade II to Grades III and IV after weight adjustment. Large-scale underground development or the construction of major underground facilities should be avoided in these zones. Where construction is necessary, strict engineering measures and feasibility assessments must be implemented. In a few other areas with favorable index values, the evaluation improved from Grade II to Grade I. In summary, after weight adjustment, most of the shallow space in the study area fell into the moderately suitable category (Grade II), covering 1,842.21 km² and accounting for approximately 46.22% of the total area.

In the middle layer (10–30 m), which typically accommodates three underground levels, transportation tunnels, rail transit lines, and utility corridors, the initial evaluation showed that most areas belonged to Grades I and II, together occupying 97.42% of the region. After weight adjustment, the areas of Grades I and II decreased substantially, while those of Grades III and IV increased. This shift mainly reflects the combined influence of thick soft soil and shallow confined groundwater conditions, which reduced the overall suitability in several southeastern areas of the study region. Therefore, stricter engineering measures are required in these zones40. Overall, the suitability of the middle space remained moderate, with Grades I and II together accounting for approximately 51.49% after adjustment.

The deep layer (30–60 m) represents underground space reserved for future development. The initial evaluation indicated that most areas belonged to Grades II and III, covering 2,617.01 km² and 1,253.51 km², respectively, corresponding to 65.66% and 31.45% of the study area. After weight adjustment, the areas of Grades II and III decreased significantly, while Grade IV expanded to 3,266.71 km², accounting for 81.96% of the region. This dominant shift mainly reflects the combined influence of confined groundwater, soft soil, and soil heterogeneity under the dynamic weighting scheme. However, this result should be interpreted as a planning-level screening outcome rather than absolute engineering infeasibility. In areas classified as Grade IV, underground development should be approached with caution and supported by detailed site-specific investigation, feasibility assessment, and appropriate engineering measures before implementation.

Vertical comparison and mechanism interpretation

From a vertical perspective, after weight adjustment, Grades I–II were most extensive in the shallow and middle layers, whereas Grade IV was dominant in the deep layer. Overall, the middle layer shows better suitability than the shallow layer, while the deep layer exhibits the strongest geological constraints. Consequently, suitability generally decreases with depth under the dynamic weighting scheme. This depth-dependent differentiation also indicates that underground space suitability evaluation cannot be adequately represented by a uniform weighting structure across all stratigraphic levels. This extensive Grade IV coverage should be interpreted as a conservative regional screening result reflecting unfavorable deep geological conditions under the weighting scheme, rather than as a definitive exclusion of all engineering development in these areas.

From the perspective of value distribution, the comprehensive evaluation scores obtained with the adjusted weights show better discreteness compared to those with the initial weights, which facilitates a clearer division of suitability grades. Overall, all areas containing sensitive factors were classified as Grade IV (unsuitable zones). This result suggests that the proposed framework is capable of preserving the non-compensatory influence of critical geological constraints, which is difficult to achieve under conventional fully compensatory weighted-overlay approaches. The Grade IV areas identified with the initial weights remained unchanged, as they were solely determined by sensitive factors. In contrast, the adjusted weight evaluation resulted in a substantial expansion of Grade IV areas due to the poor values of certain common indexes, demonstrating the model’s ability to dynamically capture multi-factor constraints.

Discussion

Advantages of the comprehensive evaluation model in suitability evaluation

The model, which is based on variable weight theory, overcomes the problem that the traditional fixed-weight model cannot accurately reflect the effect of internal differences on the overall evaluation through the dynamic weight adjustment of engineering geological condition factors. It exhibits advantages when applied to Jiaxing City. For example, in the evaluation of the engineering geological conditions of underground space development at different depths in Jiaxing City, the model flexibly adjusts the weights in accordance with the actual situation of each layer, such as soft soil thickness and soil uniformity, to make the evaluation results realistic. Moreover, the model comprehensively considers common and sensitive factors. Common factors were evaluated by the analytic hierarchy process combined with variable weight theory, and sensitive factors were determined using GIS technology. The model prevents the omission of important factors and comprehensively covers all aspects of engineering geological conditions, thus providing a scientific and accurate evaluation basis for underground space development. In addition, the model evaluation results can be directly applied to urban planning to provide guidance to planners regarding the suitability of underground space development in different regions, which is conducive to the rational planning of underground space development, reduction of geological risks, and sustainable utilization of underground space resources.

Comparison with conventional AHP–GIS and variable-weight models

Conventional AHP–GIS suitability evaluation generally relies on fixed global weights and linear overlay of all factors. Under this logic, the relative importance of each factor remains unchanged across evaluation units, and sensitive geological constraints are usually incorporated as ordinary indicators within the same weighted summation framework. In contrast, the proposed DW–SFE framework introduces two additional mechanisms. First, the weights of common factors are dynamically adjusted according to the standardized state of each factor in each evaluation unit, allowing the model to reflect local geological heterogeneity. Second, sensitive factors are evaluated independently through GIS-based buffer/exclusion analysis and then integrated with the common-factor evaluation, rather than being treated as ordinary weighted variables. Therefore, the framework combines gradual suitability differentiation with explicit restriction of critical geological conditions.

The quantitative effect of this distinction can be observed in the zoning results. After dynamic adjustment, the proportion of Grades I–II in the middle layer decreased from 97.42% to 51.49%, indicating that areas previously regarded as broadly suitable under initial weighting became more differentiated once unfavorable factor states were penalized. In the deep layer, Grade IV expanded to 81.96%. Moreover, all areas affected by sensitive factors were classified as Grade IV, showing that the explicit sensitive-factor module imposes a stricter geological constraint than a conventional weighted overlay alone. These results suggest that DW–SFE should be understood not as a completely new weighting theory, but as a more constraint-sensitive and planning-oriented extension of existing AHP–GIS and variable-weight evaluation logic. From a broader methodological perspective, the proposed framework may also be interpreted as a regional planning-oriented complement to recent developments in uncertainty-aware geoscientific modeling, multiscale index construction, and Digital Twin–based underground engineering systems. Unlike project-scale intelligent underground systems that rely on high-density real-time monitoring and engineering feedback, the DW–SFE framework emphasizes interpretable regional suitability differentiation under comparatively limited geological information conditions.

Model application and validation

To verify the effectiveness of the comprehensive evaluation model, the rail transit planning route in Jiaxing City was selected as an example for analysis. Based on the 3D geological survey and borehole data of Jiaxing City, the engineering geological conditions of different areas along the rail transit line were analyzed and compared with the obtained suitability evaluation figure. These areas were preliminarily classified as Grade IV. The area in Fig. 8(a) is mainly Grade IV, which is consistent with the evaluation results. In the middle range, the influence of soft soil thickness is small, and the comprehensive performance of the soil is good, which meets the conditions of the Grade I area. Compared with the area in Fig. 8(b), the area in the figure is mainly Grade I, which is consistent with the evaluation results. Under the effect of the inhomogeneity of soil mass along the southwest line, according to the data of Jiaxing City, the area along the line has three to four alternate layers of soft and hard soil within the vertical and 200 m plane range, and five layers of areas are alternately distributed sporadically; thus, it is preliminarily judged as a Grade III area. Compared with the area in Fig. 8(b), the area in the figure is mainly Grade III, which is consistent with the evaluation results.

According to the suitability zoning results for underground space development in Jiaxing City, the planned route selection area is located mainly within zones assessed as suitable for rail transit development and construction. Comparison with the available engineering geological survey information indicates that the evaluation results are generally consistent with the actual engineering geological characteristics of Jiaxing City. This agreement provides contextual support for the applicability of the proposed framework at the regional planning stage. Therefore, the validation presented in this study should be understood as a consistency-based assessment using representative infrastructure planning and geological evidence, rather than a large-sample quantitative benchmark evaluation.

Limitations and future works

Despite the effectiveness of the proposed dynamic weight–based evaluation framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, some local geological hazards, such as sinkholes and landslides, were not explicitly included in the current evaluation system. This omission is mainly due to the highly localized nature of these hazards and the limited availability of continuous and reliable datasets at the urban scale. Although such hazards may play an important role in underground space development at specific sites, incorporating them into a city-wide suitability assessment remains challenging. Second, the evaluation factors and weight adjustment mechanism adopted in this study were designed to support regional-scale planning and strategic decision-making. As a result, the model is not intended to replace detailed site-specific investigations required at the engineering design stage. Local-scale conditions, such as small-scale stratigraphic variations or construction-specific risks, still need to be assessed through detailed geological surveys.

Future research may focus on improving the proposed framework by incorporating additional geological hazard factors when higher-resolution and more comprehensive datasets become available. Although the binary exclusion strategy produced relatively stable regional suitability patterns under moderate buffer-distance variation, more refined graded or probabilistic representations of geological influence may further improve the realism of sensitive-factor evaluation. Moreover, the integration of time-dependent data and multi-source monitoring information may further enhance the ability of the model to capture dynamic changes in underground environmental conditions. These improvements would allow the framework to better support both regional planning and site-level decision-making for underground space development.

Conclusion

This study proposed a Dynamic Weight–Sensitive Factor Evaluation (DW–SFE) framework for assessing the suitability of engineering geological conditions for underground space development at the regional scale. By integrating variable-weight adjustment, sensitive-factor exclusion, and GIS-based spatial analysis within a unified workflow, the framework was designed to improve the representation of geological constraints and spatial heterogeneity in underground space suitability evaluation under soft-soil plain conditions. Application of the framework to Jiaxing City in the Hangjiahu Plain revealed clear spatial and depth-dependent differences in underground space suitability. After dynamic weight adjustment, suitability in the shallow and middle layers became more differentiated, while the deep layer exhibited extensive Grade IV zones mainly associated with confined groundwater conditions, soft-soil distribution, and stratigraphic heterogeneity. These results indicate that geological constraints become increasingly significant with depth under the regional planning-scale evaluation framework adopted in this study. However, the identified Grade IV zones should be interpreted as conservative planning-level screening results rather than absolute engineering infeasibility, and detailed site-specific investigation remains necessary before engineering implementation.

The comparison between the suitability zoning results and the available rail-transit planning and geological survey information showed general consistency, providing contextual support for the applicability of the framework in regional underground space planning and route-selection analysis. The proposed framework therefore demonstrates potential as a practical decision-support tool for identifying relatively suitable and restricted development zones under complex geological conditions. Rather than proposing a fundamentally new weighting theory or optimization algorithm, the DW–SFE framework should be understood as a planning-oriented methodological integration and refinement of existing AHP–GIS and variable-weight evaluation approaches. Its methodological value lies primarily in combining dynamic factor weighting with explicit non-compensatory treatment of sensitive geological constraints within a multi-depth evaluation structure.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The framework was developed mainly for regional planning-scale evaluation and does not replace detailed engineering geological investigation required for project-level design. In addition, parameter selection and sensitive-factor treatment in this study were designed to maintain interpretability and conservative screening capability rather than statistical optimization. Future studies may further improve the framework through quantitative sensitivity analysis, uncertainty characterization, graded sensitive-factor modeling, and incorporation of higher-resolution geological and monitoring datasets. Overall, this study provides a practical and interpretable reference framework for underground space suitability evaluation in soft-soil plain regions and may support sustainable underground space planning in rapidly urbanizing cities facing increasing land-use pressure.
