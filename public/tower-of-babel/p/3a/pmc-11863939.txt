Development and validation of a novel combinational index of liquid biopsy biomarker for longitudinal lung cancer patient management
Authors: Min-Yi Liao, Yun-Jie Hao, Ching-Shan Luo, Ching-Mei Chen, Po-Hao Feng, Hsin-Yu Yang, Da-Jeng Yao, Kang-Yun Lee, Fan-Gang Tseng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMany cancer biomarkers such as the circulating tumor cells/microemboli (CTCs/CTM) have been reported significant associations with clinical outcomes. However, different biomarkers have different sensitivities and specificities for cancer types and cohort patients, and synergistic effects between certain biomarkers have also been observed, leading to the inaccurate, fluctuating, and even controversial results when multiple biomarkers are analyzed together. In this paper, a novel combinational index, P-score, was developed for monitoring and predicting the disease condition of lung cancer patients during follow-up visits.Materials and methodsThere were totally 13 return patients with 54 blood samples involved in this study to examine the number of CTC and CTM. Information from one group of 7 patients including 27 blood samples with published clinical data was employed to develop while those from another group of 4 patients containing 14 blood samples with unpublished clinical data were used to validate the P score in prediction. Enumerations were based on immunofluorescent staining images. Distributions of CTC/CTM and their frequencies in stratified patients were carefully examined and analyzed the ROC curve and AUC value to develop the P score and P score-based prediction model.Results and conclusionWe found that the predictive power of P-score was not only comparable to the traditional cancer marker, in comparison with individual CTC/CTM, more false positives could be corrected by using P-score, thereby to improve the accuracy of analysis. From our preliminary validation tests, the prognosis and disease progression monitored longitudinally by P-score were further confirmed by clinical outcome data from physicians and its sensitivity was even better than those from individual biomarkers. We believe that this novel combinational indicator could be a promising tool to interpret clinical outcomes more accurately from multiple factors, particularly useful for the early prognosis and longitudinal monitoring in cancer patient management.Keywords: Lung cancer, Circulating tumor cells/microemboli, Combinational index, Disease monitoring and prognosis, Drug screening

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the top reasons of cancer-involved mortality worldwide [1], though there have increasing advances in targeted treatments and precision medicines recent years. There has also been revealed in clinical trials that comparing to no screening, lung cancer screening could facilitate the reduction of cancer-related mortality in aspect of improving the early detection and prognosis, stratifications of patients in risk model and treatment response, and researches such as the artificial intelligence assistance, and predictive biomarker discoveries [2]. It is generally believed that accuracy and timeliness in diagnosis and managing clinical cancer patients are arduous challenges, having critical impacts on patient survival [3]. But the heterogeneity of lung cancer and traditional sampling related screenings such as low dose computed tomography (LD-CT), are often restricted in the examination scale and timeframe when they are applied in diagnosis and making decision on targeted therapy for lung cancer patients such as those with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [4]. Liquid biopsy technique could collect numerous types of biomarkers from bodily fluids harmlessly and less invasively than the traditional way dealing with tissue biopsy and more cost-efficient than examinations by large expensive instruments [5]. This approach could conveniently facilitate lung cancer study to identify more molecular mutations not only at specific time points in real time, but also throughout a long-term period, having a great potential to promote the early screening and diagnosis, disease monitoring and prognosis, patient stratification, and targeted treatment [[6], [7], [8]].

Liquid biopsy biomarkers, including various circulating nucleotides, tumor-related micro-vesicles, tumor-educated entities, circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and circulating tumor microemboli (CTM), are expanding attentions in cancer studies [5,9]. In lung cancer studies, the clinical significance and biology of the prevalence and number of CTC and CTC cluster, regarding the diagnostic and prognostic potentials in prediction [[10], [11], [12], [13]], the specific immune microenvironment for targeted treatment M, the mechanism of metastasis and the molecular characteristics [14,15], have been investigated [16]. In combination with advanced molecular technologies and materials, such as next generation sequencing (NGS)-based assessments, multi-parameter flow cytometry, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), long noncoding RNAs (IncRNAs), and artificial intelligence [17], the abnormalities of various targetable oncogenic drivers including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), v-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1 (BRAF), proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase 1 (ROS1), metabolic equivalent (MET) genes, Ki-ras2 Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), some immune checkpoint inhibitors and DNA methylations have also been evaluated in such liquid biopsy biomarkers as the circulating-tumor DNA (ctDNA), cell-free DNA (cfDNA), CTCs, extracellular vesicle (EV), displaying their values in clinical applications [18].

However, few of them were well-established and confirmed the utility and validity to decode clinical data for early diagnosis, prognosis after treatment, and disease surveillance in cancer management [19]. Underlying reasons might involve that different biomarkers have different predictive powers regarding the sensitivity and specificity for a specific type of cancer or the individual cancer patient in the cohort at the specific spatiotemporal checkpoint [20], and potential synergistic effects between biomarkers or factors of interest have also been observed in clinical studies [21]. In addition, multiple key factors/biomarkers are gradually prone to be integrated into one analytical model to stratify patients, to more accurately predict the effectiveness of medical treatments and to monitor disease progression [22]. However, how to maximize the efficacy and balance the weights of different factors/biomarkers in the combined analytical model is still quite challenging, which to a certain extent may determine whether clinical data can be truly translated into meaningful clinical treatment outcomes.

Owing to increasing knowledges of bioinformatics and biometry, from the full model study to the combined analysis of multiple models [[23], [24], [25]], involving linear/logistic/lasso regressions, modified, or together with different variable selection algorithms, weighing approaches to correct and calibrate, such as Bernoulli likelihood weights, AIC weights, absolute prediction error weights, generalized degrees of freedom weights and standardized residual weights, as well as the corresponding criteria to justify [26], biomarker-based combination models for predicting survival [27] and risk [28], diagnosis [29] and prognosis [30,31], have increasingly been employed in cohort studies. However, those approaches are either too complicated to handle, requiring specific data processing or relying on enough experience to reduce bias.

Here we developed a combinational index, by which multiple key factors or biomarkers of interest could be easily integrated into one prediction model with appropriate corrections to avoid overfitting. From our preliminary test and validation, predictions resulting from this index were comparable, and might have even better sensitivity without losing specificity, to traditional serum protein marker of cancer, CTC or CTM individually, due to more misjudged data could be corrected in analysis, bringing about an improved rate of accuracy and consistent outcomes with clinical prognosis. Hence, we believe that this novel index would be a convenient tool for clinicians to analyze multiple key factors together and especially useful for the early prognosis and longitudinally monitoring patients after treatment in cancer management.

Material and methods

Patient recruitment

This study was approved and registered under the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shuanghe Hospital in New Taipei City, Taiwan (IRB number: TMU-JIRB No.: N201702026). All patients involved were agreed to and signed their individual informed consent for the IRB.

Sample preparation and CTCs/CTM detection

Plasma preparation and CTCs/CTM enrichment

Blood samples kept in the BD Vacutainer® tubes with K2EDTA (Plymouth, UK) were collected by Shuanghe Hospital and handed over to the research team in National Tsing Hua University for further process within 24 h. All plasma were prepared as mentioned in previous studies [32]. In brief, the Lymphopre™ Density Gradient medium (STEMCELL technology) was firstly mixed with 2 mL whole blood in specific separation tubes (Bio-Check Laboratories ltd, New Taipei City, Taiwan) before performing centrifugation to remove red blood cells (bottom layer) from the mononucleated cell layer with the enriched CTC/CTM. The supernatant plasma-containing layers above the Lymphopre™ medium were then centrifugated to separate cells from plasma. Finally, cell pellets were gently collected, washed, and resuspended in Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution (Corning INC., Brooklyn, NY, USA) to be further examined.

CTCs/CTM identification and enumeration

The method involved in this study to detect CTC/CTM was immunofluorescent staining-based [33], in which cells in suspension were stained by 1 μg/ml Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Taiwan Co., Ltd, Taipei, Taiwan), 1:400 diluted anti-epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) conjugated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) (BioMab Inc., Taipei, Taiwan) and anti-CD45-PECy7 (1:200) (Beckman Coulter Inc., California, USA) at room temperature in dark, respectively. Then, the cell suspension with labelled CTC/CTM was loaded to the microfluidic-based cell self-assembly array chip (SACA) [34], to be identified and enumerated on the chip-based imaging platform (CytoSCM Imaging System, Cell Envision Inc., Taiwan) by processing the captured images as mentioned previously [35]. The whole process and our criteria to identify CTCs/CTM were displayed in Supplementary figures (Fig. S1).

Statistical analysis

The distribution, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the related area under the ROC Curve (AUC) of biomarkers were analyzed and plotted by R Studio (Integrated Development Environment for R, Boston, MA, USA), in which p values were calculated by Wilcoxon-Mann-Witney test. The rest figures were all drawn by Origin 2021b (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

In this study, ROC Curves were employed to assess the predictive power of biomarkers. For instance, when considering two biomarkers (e.g. the CTCs and CTM in this study), their predictive powers could be described by their individual cut-off points and AUC values ((1), (2)). When analyzing the effect of grouped biomarkers on the final prediction model (equation (4)), a new combinational index (the P score) could be further defined by individual predictive factors (equation (3)) in the regression analysis.

CTCs and CTM were the enumerated values. A1 and A2 were the best AUC values resulted from the ROC curve under the best cut-off position of the related specific biomarker CTC and CTM respectively. B1 and B2 were the best cut-off position calculated in the distribution curve of the specific biomarker CTC and CTM respectively, considering the sensitivity and specificity. The P score-related prediction model was formed by P score plus an additional constant C to adjust the final value. The constant C was the offset constant when analyzing grouped biomarkers to adjust the final index of samples in the test group.

The criteria of the prediction by assessing P score were when the P score in the prediction model was dropped into an area lower than 0, the corresponding sample was predicted as stable disease (SD); when the P score was dropped into an area higher than 0, the corresponding sample was predicted as progressive disease (PD). Schematic images of the related mechanisms were displayed in Supplementary Figure (Fig. S2).

Results

Design of this study

There were 13 return patients (7 with clinical data, 4 without clinical data, 2 dead during the time of this study) and totally 54 blood samples (3 poor samples without data, 27 with clinical data and 14 without clinical data) from March 2019 to June 2022 enrolled in this study (Table 1). All patients were followed at least three times and their blood samples were collected during follow-up visits (usually every three months). Most patients were male with advanced, metastatic lung cancer. The design of this study was presented in Fig. 1.

Distribution and correlation of enumerated CTC/CTM with clinical prognosis

Except deaths of two patients during this study and another three poor samples failed to detect CTC and CTM, 27 blood samples from 7 patients prognosed with either PD or SD and 14 samples from 4 patients with unpublished clinical data, were finally analyzed (n = 41). Distributions of the enumerated CTC and CTM in 7 patients grouped by the prognosed statue of disease and correlations of the enumerated CTC and CTM with the corresponding prognoses of patients were preliminarily investigated (n = 27), where there were 12 and 15 samples from patients prognosed with PD (n = 12) and SD (n = 15), respectively. For samples from 4 patients (n = 14) without published clinical data, their status of disease predicted in this study was checked by the doctor thereafter for validation test in this study.

The number of CTC and CTM was detected by the SACA-chip based platform (CytoSCM imaging@cellenvision), and the optimal threshold numbers of CTCs and CTM were analyzed at 35 and 7.5, respectively. From our results (Fig. 2), in both PD and SD samples, more CTCs were identified than CTM, and more CTC/CTM were observed in samples from patients diagnosed with PD than SD (Fig. 2A). There were statistically significant differences between the distribution of CTC and that of CTM in both PD (p = 0.0018) and SD (p = 0.000048) samples, as well as significant differences in both distributed numbers of CTC (p = 0.0011) and CTM (p = 0.00065) between the SD and PD patients (Fig. 2B).

A newly defined combinational index and the index-based prediction model

Based on the individual distributions of biomarkers CTC/CTM in stratified samples and the analyses of their respective ROC and AUC in every sample of return patients, a novel combinational indicator for analyzing multiple biomarkers of interest (in this study the CTC and CTM) to avoid overfitting, namely the P score was developed (Fig. 3A). The P score-based model to predict the statue of disease in tested samples were also established (Fig. 3B). Based on this prediction model, there was an improved accuracy in predicting recurrences, but it was also found that the most accurate predictions were from P scores within the <0 and >1 ranges (almost 100 %) (Fig. 3C). All P scores and the P score-based predictions for the tested 27 samples were listed in combination with their enumerated values of CTC and CTM (Fig. S3). Compared with individual biomarkers (CTC/CTM), regarding the sensitivity and specificity (Fig. 4A and B), a better ROC curve with higher AUC value was resulted from the P score (Fig. 4C and D). The potential reason might be more misjudged data points were corrected when the analysis was using P score (Fig. S3).

Validation of the P score in predictive analyses

To validate the P score-based model for predicting the status of disease clinically, 14 blood samples from another four return patients without released clinical data were examined. After enumerating CTC and CTM, P score was calculated and applied to predicting the status of disease of these patients, which results were further checked by the doctor. By tracking this new indicator during follow-up visits, the status of disease of all four patients were monitored and plotted in Fig. 5. For patient 10, 12 and 13, their P scores (Fig. 5A, C and 5D) were kept at a lower level (<1), showing clearly declining trends, whose status of disease predicted by the P score were stable, consistent with their prognoses from the doctor later, as SD (Fig. 5E). The status of disease of patient 11 (Fig. 5B), predicted by the P score as progressive, was also in line with the later prognosis by the doctor (Fig. 5E). However, there were only one month in between the second and third follow-up visits for patient 11 and 12, those predictions may request further verifications in future studies.

Comparation of the predictive analyses by monitoring CEA and P score

For two patients whose clinical data were fully recorded the level of the traditional cancer marker carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) at all follow-up visits, predictions of the status of disease of each patient by monitoring the level of serum CEA and P score were analyzed and compared. From results (Fig. S4), predictions for all two patients by monitoring their levels of serum CEA and the P scores, throughout the follow-up period, displayed a similar and comparable trend. Interestingly, based on monitoring curves, an earlier response from the P-score (at least three months earlier) than that from the CEA was noticed in the p-score based monitoring curve of case 2 (Fig. S4B), but due to the long interval (6 months) between visits of case 1 in p-score based monitoring curve, the earlier responses only at least one month in advance, was observed (Fig. S4A).

Patient managements by monitoring CTC, CTM and P score

Prediction of the status of disease of return patients after treatments by monitoring CTC and CTM

The level of CTCs and CTM of all patients during follow-up visits were monitored and plotted with time axis parallelly labelled with the type of therapy, surgery, prescriptive medication, chest X-ray photos and the Computed Tomography (CT) images at specific follow-up visits which information were all based on the published clinical data. As an example, the analyzed figure for one patient was provided (Fig. 6). By monitoring the number of CTCs and CTM detected in this study, correlations of medical treatments with changes of these biomarkers were clearly displayed, which could be used to facilitate doctors to manage individual patients and make decisions on their further treatments. For example, from the monitoring curves (Fig. 6), at the beginning, the number of CTC and CTM were both increased after the first visit, which were reached to the highest level and declined after being treated with the immunotherapy and surgery. As treatments ongoing, they were steadily kept in a lower level, serving as a reliable indicator. Predictions of the status of disease in patient by monitoring serum levels of CTC and CTM, were also comparable to changes of the lymphadenopathy (the primary tumor was resected after concurrent chemoradiotherapy) shown in the CT scanning images of this patient at follow-up visits. From the CT scanning images, the lesion in focused area was shrunk after treatment and not further enlarged, which could be also reflected by monitoring biomarkers (CTC/CTM) in a less invasive but more cost-effective way.

Discussions

Despite the rarity and fragility, among various circulating tumor biomarkers, both solitary and clustered circulating tumor cells (CTCs/CTM) become more attractive, due to their biogenesis inherently associated with the primary or metastatic tumor entity [36]. Correlations of the level and detection rate of CTCs, with clinical outcomes have already been broadly evaluated in various cancer studies [37,38] and a small number of clinical trials [16], where CTC was typically analyzed as an independent variable with mutational and metastatic information [16,39]. But controversial results were also observed [17]. And ever since many studies observed and explored CTC clusters, a higher possibility of the metastasis prompted by CTC cluster than individual CTC was strongly supposed [40].

In our previous cohort studies of colorectal cancer (CRC), we have observed that, CTCs and CTM had different distributions in stratified patients, presented their individual relationships with categories of the TNM stage, and might play important roles in early predicting the disease progression [32,33]. In particular, the CTM, not only correlated with the metastasis significantly, but could also be seen as an independent factor in predicting patient survivals, and comparable to the traditional serum protein cancer marker [35]. What is more, synergistic effects between these key factors were also noted [21,41,42]. In fact, due to the complicated relationship between such biomarkers as CTCs and CTM [43], and between circulating biomarkers and the physiological condition of the host body [[44], [45], [46], [47]], it is usually difficult to distinguish and identify their individual power of indication in clinical samples [20,[48], [49], [50], [51]]. This might be one of the reasons why more and more novel biomarkers were emerged in cancer study, but few of them had been well-confirmed, completely validated and successfully utilized in clinics [19,[52], [53], [54], [55]]. The analysis relying on any individual biomarker alone, such as CTCs or CTM, or depending on simple combinations of multiple biomarkers without correction or being overfitted by excessively employing various statistical algorithms, will be inefficient and prejudicial, leading to certain inaccuracies and misunderstandings.

In this longitudinal study of return patients with lung cancer, by mapping and comparing distributions of two potential biomarkers CTCs/CTM in stratified patients, the underlying dynamics of individual biomarkers in relation to clinical prognoses were identified. These dynamics may contribute to the inaccuracy of the individual or combined biomarker-based prediction models [[56], [57], [58]]. For instance, distributions of the number of CTCs and CTM were all relatively scattered in PD patients than those in SD patients, and compared with CTCs, distributions of CTM in both SD and PD patients, were more concentrated and specific (Fig. 2). There were more overlapped areas between the two true positive areas of SD and PD samples in the distribution map of CTM than those of CTCs (Fig. 3). These overlapped areas in curves determine the final selection of the best cut-off point for CTCs or CTM individually, which would further have impact on the biomarker-based prediction (Figs. S2 and S3). From these, it was obvious that the accuracy of prediction based on the individual biomarker either CTCs or CTM would be reduced after analysis, and a better prediction model should consider both two biomarkers. However, when analyzing multiple biomarkers, it is usually difficult to balance their individual powers of indication especially when their distribution maps with apparently different. To maximize the indicative power of individual biomarker of interest (in this study, the CTCs and CTM) and to reduce the potential bias by referencing individual biomarker-based prediction model, we firstly figured out the weighted power of indication for each biomarker by referencing their best cut-off points and AUC values, before being incorporated into the evaluation function to determine a composite index (the P score). The P score was defined as the integrated indicator with the balanced evaluation function, in which the input was the predictive powers of individual biomarker, and the output was the predicted clinical outcome of patients. ROC curves were analyzed to assess the effectiveness of individual or composite index in prediction, regarding the sensitivity and specificity.

From our preliminary results, the P score-corrected prediction model displayed a higher accuracy rate (90 %) than those analyzed by CTC (88.5 %) and CTM (89.7 %) respectively in the ROC curve, although the best sensitivity and specificity of P-score might not be selected, comparing to those of CTC/CTM analyzed individually (Fig. 4). In addition, it was found that, patients with higher P scores had the highest recurrence rate (100 %) (Fig. 3). Comparing to individual biomarker-based models, the P score-based model comprehensively assessing quantitative changes of individual biomarkers (CTC/CTM), demonstrated a high correlation with the clinical condition of the patient, and presented the potential to provide more accurate results to longitudinally evaluate the effects of medical treatments on the clinical status of return patients with lung cancer (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6).

In clinical practice, the serum level of CEA is a commonly used indicator of cancer, whose level is tended to be elevated in lung cancer patients [59]. This kind of biomarker has also been used to assess the effectiveness of treatment [60,61] and in lung cancer management [62]. However, many other physiological conditions than lung cancer, such as the inflammation and infection could also influence the serum level of this kind of marker, suggesting that CEA may not be reliable for monitoring patients in the long term [[63], [64], [65], [66]]. In this study, the prediction model of P score was also compared with that of CEA in some return patients with CEA data. Both two indicators displayed comparable trends in predicting the disease progression of patients (Fig. S4), but P score-based model might be at least 3 months earlier to predict the outcome of treatments, suggesting the potential value of P score for clinical applications.

Furthermore, the P score was preliminarily used to assess clinical treatments of prescriptive medicines in five return patients during their long-term follow-ups in this study. Regarding the quantification and comparison, it was observed that when the value of P score was less than 0 or more than 1, its prediction would be almost 100 % accurate, however, when the value was less than 1 but more than 0, accuracy of its prediction was reduced to about 60 %, requiring further refinement (Fig. 3). But it could be seen that the fluctuation of P score was reliably consistent with those of diagnosed disease status of cancer patients, suggesting that the P score, having comparable sensitivity and better accuracy would be a promising indicator in longitudinally predicting the disease progression of individual patient, and evaluating the screening efficiency of new drugs in treatment.

Conclusions

Although there were limitations to improve. For instance, the number of our return patients and the total samples examined in this study was limited and the P score was currently established in lung cancer study by considering only two circulating biomarkers, the type of cancer could be expanded to more other types, more and various biomarkers or factors of interesting in clinical settings, should be considered to form the P score and prediction model. Further optimization and external validation of this combinational index is also necessary, particularly those values dropped within the intermediate range. To improve the sensitivity of P score, a regular interval between observations and an increased frequency of observations are highly recommended in future works. The P score demonstrates a promising way to analyze multiple biomarkers of interest together by simply avoid overfitting. We believe that this novel index would be an easy but effective tool for clinicians to prognose and monitor lung cancer patients longitudinally, to evaluate medical treatments of individual patients and even to screen new drugs in a less invasive but more cost-effective way.
