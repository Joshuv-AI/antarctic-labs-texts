Male survivors of childhood sexual abuse: perceived social support as a correlate of imposter phenomenon — an exploratory study
Authors: Shubham Sharma, Seema Singh, Kanika Ahuja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMale survivors of childhood sexual abuse (CSA) often grapple with lasting self-doubt and feelings of fraudulence, hallmarks of imposter phenomenon. We investigated how perceived social support from family, friends, and significant others (SO) relates to imposter phenomenon (IP) in this group, expecting higher degrees of support to link with lower imposter feelings. MethodsUsing a cross-sectional design, 85 male participants aged 20–35 years from Delhi NCR, participated through purposive and snowball methods, completed the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support and Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale.ResultsAnalyses indicated significant negative associations between Imposter Phenomenon and the Perceived Social Support (PSS) (r = –.567, p < .01) and its domains of Significant Other (r = –.609, p < .01), Friends (r = –.490, p < .01), and Family (r = –.426, p < .01). Hierarchical Regression analysis showed that age and education accounted for 3.8% variance in IP, PSS total with age and education explained 32.9% where R2 = .329, F (3,81) = 13.24, p < .001, while PSS sub-domains with age and education explained 38.1% of the variance in perceived social support, R2 = .382, F (5, 79) = 9.74, p < .001.ConclusionsInverse links between social support and imposter phenomenon were confirmed, pinpointing significant other as particularly protective, followed by friends and family. These insights strengthen the need to provide tailored support strategies for male survivors and call for expanded research despite constraints of sample size and cross-sectional data in India.

Male childhood sexual abuse in India

The shadow of CSA looms large over the life paths of survivors, especially within the Indian context, where male survivors have to contend with not only the burden of stigma within their families, who would expect them to conform to traditional masculine ideals, but also the lack of discussion within society about the vulnerability of boys. Having conducted research on trauma within Delhi NCR, the authors have personally witnessed how male survivors often struggle with the feeling that they are not only successful or have achieved stability within their lives, yet still manage to feel that they are not deserving of it, that sooner or later, their inadequacies will be outed to the world. This is similar to the imposter phenomenon, which was first described by Clance [6] as the feeling of inadequacy within individuals who feel they have achieved success not due to their own competence, but due to trickery or due to chance. While the imposter phenomenon is an area that is heavily explored, especially within high-achieving groups or within groups such as academia or medicine, the link between the imposter phenomenon, trauma, and especially CSA, especially within males, is an area that is not explored. Betrayal Trauma Theory [20] proposes that when abuse is perpetrated by a caregiver or trusted authority figure, psychological adaptation may demand a form of motivated unawareness — what Freyd termed "betrayal blindness" — that preserves the dependency relationship at the cost of full conscious processing of the violation. Smith and Freyd [36] extended this argument to show that betrayal by trusted institutions further entrenches non-disclosure and worsens survivor outcomes, particularly when the social environment reinforces silence. In India, meaning of social support is shaped by collectivist family structures, strong obligations and responsibilities, and culturally script of masculinity. Indian families often operate as a primary site of identity and social standing, but they can also become spaces where silence, honour concerns, and stigma discourage disclosure of sexual victimization. Furthermore, hegemonic masculine norms may further discourage help-seeking because disclosure can be seen as weakness or loss of status. In contrast, friends, and significant others may provide relatively less judgemental, more validating relationships. This is certainly worrying within the context of Indian males, who are already an underrepresented group within the larger context of trauma research, where CSA has already affected their identity, creating feelings of fear of judgment, which is similar to the imposter phenomenon, where discloser percentage of CSA is already low, cultural salience of family honour, collectivist obligations, and rigid constructions of masculine stoicism does not merely provide a backdrop for these processes, it actively determines which disclosures are permissible, which relationships carry protective potential, and how relational acknowledgement is sought and withheld.

Imposter phenomenon's trauma-linked manifestations

The impostor phenomenon is characterized by specific cognitive-affective cycles of over-anxiety before a task, discounting of one's success, and the persistent feeling of being discovered. These processes are closely related to the internalized feelings of shame and distrust that are commonly reported by CSA survivors. Sakulku and Alexander [33] suggest that the root cause of the impostor phenomenon is the whimsical validation from parents, which is closely related to the feelings of shame and distrust reported by CSA survivors. In the case of male CSA survivors in India, the hegemonic masculinity construct is closely related to the feelings of shame and the reported feelings of weakness that are associated with the expression of emotions around Indian culture. Male CSA survivors in India are likely to report feelings of maladaptive coping and social incompetence, which sustain the impostor phenomenon cycle. The literature indicates that the impostor phenomenon is closely related to CSA and other traumatic experiences. Quantitative data related to the impostor phenomenon in male CSA survivors is scarce, and the majority of the literature is focused on female CSA survivors and the association between the impostor phenomenon and general feelings of self-esteem. Quantitative data related to the impostor phenomenon in male CSA survivors is virtually non-existent, and the majority of the literature is focused on female CSA survivors and the association between the impostor phenomenon and general feelings of self-esteem [3, 18]. In the case of non-Western cultures, the impostor phenomenon related to feelings of shame and distrust within the family is closely related to the feelings of collectivism that are commonly reported by people from non-Western cultures.

Perceived social support as trauma buffer

Perceived social support (PSS), which is the subjective availability of emotional, informational, and tangible support from family, friends, or significant others, is a well-established protective factor against related precipitate. Measured by instruments such as the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; [44], the construct of PSS allows for the differentiation of social support sources into subtle categories, where family social networks can serve to reinforce cultural resilience by virtue of shared cultural values but are complicated by the experience of abuse coupled with stigma,peer social networks bid avenues for the disclosure of shame in a non-judgmental manner; and romantic partners are essential in the repair of trust necessary for intimacy in the aftermath of abuse. Longitudinal research has demonstrated the protective effects of PSS in CSA survivors, reducing the trajectory of post-traumatic stress, depression, and relationship difficulties, where social support from friends is seen to have positive outcome and social support from family is complicated by the experience of invalidation [12, 37, 42]. In Indian culture, the construct of PSS serves as a cultural mediator where community social networks provide against feelings of isolation, although disclosure of abuse is complicated by the experience of rejection, thereby requiring source-specific analyses [40].

Critical gap: PSS-IP link in male CSA survivors

The exact nature of the intersection between PSS subdivisions and impostor phenomenon has not been studied within the exact sample. IP is distinct from general trauma related shame, low self-worth, or depression, in that it focuses on achievement related doubt despite evidence of competence [5]. Similar research among non-trauma samples has found a negative correlation between PSS and IP, through lower levels of anxiety and increased levels of self-efficacy, thus illustrating the impact of social support in alleviating inner criticism [15, 33]. While CSA related shame and fear of exposure often contribute to low self-regard or anxiety, IP more specifically reflects an internalized sense of “intellectual fraudulence” in one’s accomplishments. For male CSA survivors, this construct compliments rather than duplicates existing work on CSA, shame, and attachment by highlighting how success itself can be experienced as threatening within a patriarchal, success driven construct. Building on work showing that minority status and perceived discrimination interact with imposter experiences and social support [8], this study treats IP as a specific, meaningful outcome, in male CSA survivors, not merely a proxy for general distress. In Indian male survivors of CSA, there is a complete lack of quantitative research using the foundational measures of Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) and subscales of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [22, 32, 38], thus limiting intervention development to include counselling strategies to improve friendship rather than reconciliation within the family. Adjacent literatures have examined shame and self-concept damage following trauma [17], disrupted attachment as a consequence of caregiver abuse [21], masculinity norms as barriers to help-seeking among male survivors [13], and perceived social support as a moderator of post-traumatic distress more broadly [29]. However, none of these lines of inquiry converge on imposter phenomenon as a discrete outcome, nor do they account for how source-specific social support differentially attenuates fraudulence-linked self-perception within a culturally specific context of stigmatised male victimisation. Complementary insights emerge from meta perception research, which demonstrates how experiences of harm reshape not only how individuals perceive themselves but also how they anticipate being judged or valued by others. Borinca, Stathi, and Gkinopoulos [2] found that distressing harm-related memories intensified negative meta-perceptions, while relational recognition partially counteracted these effects. Though situated in intergroup contexts, this mechanism translates meaningfully here: perceived social support among male CSA survivors may operate less as generalized reassurance and more as relational validation, directly counteracting the fear of exposure and disbelief that structurally defines imposter phenomenon.

Aim and hypothesis

This research aims to explore the link between social support received from family members, friends, and significant others and the degree of imposter phenomenon among male survivors of CSA, while also determining which specific social support is better associated with lower levels of imposter phenomenon. To study the same, study advances two hypotheses concerning the relationship between perceived social support and the endorsement of imposter phenomena. (H1) PSS total scores will correlate negatively with IP scores. (H2) PSS from friends will show the strongest negative correlation with IP, followed by significant others and Family. The study further tries to incorporate 2 research questions – (R1) How do PSS total and subscale scores relate to IP among male CSA survivors? And (R2) To what extent do PSS subscales predict variance in IP scores among male CSA survivors? To help further the understanding and potential of this research.

Method

Research design

This research used a cross-sectional quantitative research design to explore the relationship between perceived social support and imposter phenomenon among male survivors of CSA in India. The data collection process began from January 2025 to February 2026.

Participants

The participants included 85 adult males who had experienced CSA. These participants were from Delhi NCR, along with the surrounding regions. The inclusion criteria for the participants included their reported history of experiencing sexual abuse, either penetrating or non-penetrating, prior to the age of 18. In addition, they had to be at least 20 years old, male, and proficient in English. The exclusion criteria included participants who had not completed the survey with fewer than 20% incomplete responses or failed the attention checks. They had to not have had a diagnosed mental illness or cognitive or developmental issues.

Initial recruitment yielded 88 participants, of whom three were removed for having missing data, leaving a set sample of 85 participants (N = 85; response rate = 96.6%). Participants’ ages stretched from 20 to 35 years, with a mean of 22.72 and standard deviation of 2.55. Participants’ educational levels included having a bachelor’s degree or currently pursuing one (60%), having a master’s degree or currently pursuing one (34.12%), having an undergraduate diploma (4.7%), and having a doctoral degree (1.18%). Using snowball and purposive sampling to access the hard-to-reach population is in line with best practice for accessing child sex abuse (CSA) survivor samples, where the stigma of the issue makes it impossible to use probability sampling techniques [10, 41].

Measures

Perceived Social Support – The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; [44] measured subjective social support received from three sources: family, friends, and significant others. Participants used a 7-point Likert scale to rate items, and subscale and total scores were computed as means. Higher scores indicated more perceived social support. The MSPSS possesses good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88–0.95) and validity in multicultural and trauma-experiencing populations [27, 28]. Imposter Phenomenon – The Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS, [6] measured IP experiences in terms of their intensity using 20 items on a 5-point Likert scale. Scores range from 20 to 100, with those above 60 representing more common IP experiences. The CIPS possesses good reliability (α = 0.86–0.91) and convergent validity in relation to other related constructs such as anxiety and self-esteem in clinical populations [19]. Demographics and Covariates – A simple demographic measure was used to obtain data on age, education level, employment status, and religion, used as covariates in data analysis.

After approval was obtained from the ethics committee, recruitment was carried out through purposive approaches to practitioners and support groups in the Delhi National Capital Region. An initial cohort of "seeds" (n = 15) were chosen on the basis of diversity in terms of age and history of abuse; they were further used to recruit other participants through snowballing techniques. Willing respondents were able to access an online survey via a secure online survey system through shared links; anonymity was ensured by not capturing IP addresses or other identifying features [30]. Those willing to participate in face-to-face meetings were also able to do so. The identification of CSA history relied on participant self-disclosure rather than standardized trauma inventories. This approach was necessitated by the extreme cultural stigma surrounding male victimization in India. Given the lack of institutionalized support networks for male survivors in the region, the study utilized a 'Low-Barrier' recruitment strategy. Probing for specific trauma characteristics (e.g., severity or perpetrator identity) was avoided to prevent re-traumatization and to encourage participation from a demographic that rarely engages in public disclosure. While this limits the ability to conduct subgroup analyses based on trauma type, it was deemed an essential ethical safeguard for this exploratory study. The average time spent on the survey was 12 min. There were no monetary or other incentives provided, and participants were aware that they could withdraw at any point in time.

Data analytics

Analyses employed SPSS v20 software (IBM 2020). Missing values were listwise deleted following MCAR assumption, using Little’s test, where p > 0.05. Internal consistency reliability utilized Cronbach’s alpha, where alpha should be above 0.70. Descriptive statistics summarized the sample and score distributions. Normality tests (Shapiro–Wilk) rejected normality for all variables. Bivariate Spearman’s Rho correlation examined zero-order relationships between PSS subscales/total, IP, and control variables.

Hierarchical linear regression was executed with bootstrapped standard errors (5000 samples) to address non-normality, in an attempt to examine PSS's ability to predict IP. Step 1: Covariates of age and education Step 2: PSS total; Step 3: PSS subscales to examine unique contributions of subscales to the PSS total. Assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity (VIF < 5) and normal residuals checked using Durbin-Watson. ΔR2 and f2 reported to examine effect size [7]. Alpha was set at 0.05,no adjustments for multiple comparisons given exploratory aims. Power analysis (G*Power 3.1) confirmed N = 85 detected medium effects (f2 = 0.15) at 85% power for regression [16].

Result

Preliminary analyses suggested data suitability for non-parametric tests. The missing value analysis indicated presence of no missing data. All measures demonstrated acceptable internal consistency: MSPSS Family (α = 0.80), Friends (α = 0.78), Significant Others (α = 0.82), Total (α = 0.83); CIPS (α = 0.83). Regression assumptions held: no influential outliers (Cook's D < 1), VIFs ranged 1.82–2.19, residuals approximated normality (Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.86 > 0.05), and scatterplots evidenced linearity and homoscedasticity [1]. Discussion

This study investigated the relationship across perceived social support (PSS) and imposter phenomenon (IP) among 85 male survivors of childhood sexual abuse (CSA) in India. Findings presented that higher PSS—both total and across subthemes—were prominently linked with lower IP intensity, with comfort from significant others promising as the strongest predictor. These figures extend the existing literature on PSS-IP dynamics into an understudied trauma population and carry meaningful implications for clinical intervention in the Indian context.

One striking finding—total PSS is linked strongly and negatively with IP (rs = –0.57, p < 0.001)—corresponds with results seen outside trauma-exposed groups, suggesting that stronger social support tends to soften feelings of being an impostor [15, 34]. Since constant self-doubt and internalized shame erode confidence in one’s abilities, supportive connections gain importance as tools to interrupt the repeating pattern of impostor thoughts [3]. Especially within India, low awareness about mental well-being paired with societal disapproval around seeking aid means personal networks often fill roles usually taken by professional services, making perceived support even more crucial for emotional stability [26].

The primacy of significant others support

Contrary to H2, the support from significant others and not friends, that emerged as the strongest predictor and correlated with lower IP level (β = −0.47, p < 0.001), meanwhile, friends and family did not add distinct predictive value. While friends support was assumed to be the primary validator given disclosure dynamics, the present findings may reflect several alternative explanations. Relationship-status composition within the sample may have elevated the salience of significant other support, consistent with evidence that committed partnerships provide qualitatively distinct emotional validation among trauma survivors [24]. Further, the term “significant other” in MSPSS refers to any highly important person and may include, but is not limited to, romantic partners. The core value of IP leans on fear of exposure of intimacy which translates to the dread that those closest to a person will eventually debunk their perceived fraudulence [5, 33]. On the grounds that significant others are those people who represent the relational audience most feared in this exposure narrative, their consistent validation holds greater power to disrupt self-doubt. When a person whose good opinion is most coveted offers sustained affirmation, it penetrates the internalized inadequacy in ways that broader peer networks rarely achieve [43].

Attachment theory provides additional explanative framework. CSA essentially interferes with early attachment bonds, especially when perpetrated by acquainted adults, inducing what Freyd [20] termed betrayal trauma—a pervasive erosion of trust in familiar relations that grips resulting relational functioning. This approach may be employable for Indian male survivors, whose cultural conditioning, assuming stoic self-sufficiency turns genuine relational validation from close relations, and be reasonably impactful [35].

Family support: ambivalence over efficacy

Family support produced a negligible and nonsignificant regression coefficient (β = 0.02, p = 0.878), a pattern which was unexpected but aligned theoretically within this sample. A notable segment of CSA cases involves intrafamilial perpetration or takes place in midst of indifferent family atmospheres, where abuse is downplayed or rejected [20, 25]. The weak family support association may reflect theoretical concerns about intrafamilial abuse or stigma, tough this was not directly measured in the present study. The breach of trust by or within the family, provokes deep hesitation in survivors' experiences of family-sourced support, where acts of familial warmth may simultaneously elicit proximity and threat, upsetting their buffering capacity [11]. Silence wraps around men’s pain in India, shaped by traditions where speaking up brings doubt instead of comfort. When stories emerge, they often meet raised eyebrows or quiet dismissal—responses that twist truth into something unrecognizable. Support from loved ones might exist on paper, yet feel hollow when it comes with strings attached. Without real belief behind it, care loses power to ease deep wounds. Even if people show up, their presence may do little when acceptance isn’t full. Numbers can suggest help is working, but feelings tell another story entirely.

The subtheme—Friends support yielded a meaningful zero-order correlation with IP (rs = −0.49, p < 0.001) but did not independently project it once significant others support was included in regression. This arrangement likely reflects shared variance: participants with strong significant others support tend to describe higher peer support as well, suppressing friends' distinct contribution [15]. It also exhibits the essence of impostor experiences themselves. Friends’ support, while emotionally forthcoming, often loses the depth and rigor required to address IP's hardened self-narratives,friendships can treat self-doubt as normal instead of questioning its validity, giving solace while leaving imposter views intact [31, 43]. Still, IP shares a relationship with peer ties, these connections matter—especially for men surviving childhood sexual abuse who lack deep bonds so far.

Imposter phenomenon in Indian male CSA survivors

Sample's mean IP score (M = 59.53, SD = 23.02) classed survivors near Clance's [6] threshold for common impostor experiences, echoing research documenting elevated IP among trauma-exposed populations and in Indian samples broadly [9, 25]. What stands out in Indian cultural settings is how group-oriented values shape impostor phenomenon in men: success often demands a show of constant certainty. When personal uncertainty clashes with that expectation, the gap deepens feelings of being a fraud—yet speaking up rarely fits the script [23]. For the sample manoeuvring this cultural statute, the exposure to IP is further rendered invisible—discounted externally by masculinity scripts and internally by shame-based self-silencing [35, 42]. The analysis, drawn from the Indian sample using validated measures, represent a rare empirical documentation of this juncture.

Conclusions, practical implications and limitations

Findings hold clinical relevance for trauma-informed practice in terms of social support with male CSA survivors in India. Clinicians may consider masculinity sensitive therapeutic approaches that reframe help-seeking as consistent with-rather than contrary to- male identity norms [14]. The family support contribution, must be taken up from case-to-case basis, given past harm within families. A study reported over half the perpetrators to be family members in India [4], automatic inclusion of family members could do damage—each situation demands scrutiny before involving them [25]. Measurement overlap between emotional intimacy and significant other support cannot be ruled out, and future studies should examine whether relationship status moderates these source specific associations. Several limitations temper these conclusions. The small, cross-sectional sample, absence of trauma severity and mental health covariates, relationship status, employment, time since abuse and correlational design means that these results do not establish enhancing significant other support would reduce imposter feelings, and must be treated as tentative, although the correlational analysis does suggest a relation between the two [39]. The study did not differentiate between intrafamilial and extrafamilial abuse. This is a significant limitation, as survivors of familial abuse may experience 'Betrayal Trauma' more acutely, potentially associated with the non-significant role of family support found alongside. Future research should investigate if severity of trauma moderates the relationship between social support and imposter feelings. The exclusion of survivors with diagnosed mental health conditions and non-English speaking sample likely resulted in a selective, 'high-functioning' sample. Given the high prevalence of PTSD, depression, and anxiety among CSA survivors, findings may not be generalizable to the broader survivor population currently navigating clinical levels of distress. However, this exclusion was a deliberate choice to maintain construct clarity for the IP, as this demographic is uniquely positioned to experience the 'success-performance' pressure central to the Imposter Phenomenon. Future studies should include clinical populations to examine how the relationship between social support and IP might shift when comorbid with trauma-related disorders. A cross-sectional study does not allow for causal reasoning since longitudinal research would be necessary to find out if PSS comes before the decrease in IP or if low IP enables individuals to seek help. Snowball and purposive sampling methods are suitable but create a selection bias issue beyond Delhi NCR [10]. Sample size was relatively small, the investigation could not analyse group differences based on the form, duration, or relationship between the individual being abused and their abuser—all potential moderators in the relationship between PSS-IP. Future studies should increase sample sizes to explore these interactions.
