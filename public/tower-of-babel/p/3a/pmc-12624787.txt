MarK, a Novosphingobium aromaticivorans kinase required for catabolism of multiple aromatic monomers
Authors: Benjamin W Hall, Jeanette C Neri, Craig A Bingman, Avery C Vilbert, Jacob B Eckmann, Kevin S Myers, Bryan S Sibert, Elizabeth R Wright, Brian G Fox, Patricia J Kiley, Daniel R Noguera, Timothy J Donohue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aromatic compounds used in a variety of industrial products are currently obtained from nonrenewable petroleum sources. Alternatively, the plant polymer lignin is an abundant renewable source of aromatics, and its depolymerization generates a variety of products that can include acetovanillone, a vanillin derivative containing an acetyl side chain. The Alphaproteobacterium Novosphingobium aromaticivorans DSM12444 can metabolize several chemically modified aromatics in deconstructed lignin, but not acetovanillone. In this work, adaptive laboratory evolution identified a single amino acid change in the previously uncharacterized gene product Saro_1862 that is necessary and sufficient for N. aromaticivorans growth with acetovanillone as a sole growth substrate, as well as other aromatic monomers not metabolized by wildtype cells. We show that a glutamate (E) to lysine (K) substitution at amino acid residue 16 of Saro_1862 results in a ∼1600-fold increase in the rate of ATP-dependent acetovanillone phosphorylation. We also find that recombinant Saro_1862 E16K phosphorylates several other aromatic compounds in vitro, defining the first reported catalytic activity for the widespread UPF0261 protein domain contained in Saro_1862. Thus, we propose naming Saro_1862 MarK, for multiple aromatic kinase. A 1.57 Å crystal structure of MarK E16K predicts that the E16K substitution lies in a potential ATP binding site, suggesting how this amino acid change increased catalytic activity. A search for homologs of MarK and other proteins required for acetovanillone degradation predicts that this pathway for aromatic metabolism exists throughout the bacterial phylogeny.Keywords: lignin degradation, phosphorylation, bacterial metabolism, enzyme, enzyme structure, kinase, acetovanillone, aromatic metabolism, Novosphingobium

Aromatic compounds are found in cellular components such as proteins and nucleic acids and are also used to produce industrial chemicals, including solvents, dyes, fragrances, polymers, agrochemicals, and pharmaceuticals. The large number and quantities of aromatic products needed by society and the environmental impact of their industrial production has led to an interest in developing methods to use lignin as a potential renewable source of these chemicals (1, 2, 3, 4). This work expands upon the knowledge needed to use lignin-derived aromatics as a renewable source for chemical production.

Lignin is a heterogeneous polymer of aromatics that helps plant cell walls maintain their structure (5), and it represents the second most abundant biopolymer on the planet (6). However, to convert lignin into industrial chemicals, it must be depolymerized. Many chemical methods for lignin depolymerization exist, most yielding mixtures of aromatic compounds, whose identities and chemical modifications to the aromatic ring depend on the parameters of the deconstruction process and the plant material (3, 7).

One aromatic monomer that is often present in significant concentrations after lignin depolymerization is acetovanillone, a vanillin derivative that contains an acetyl side chain on the aromatic ring (3). Acetovanillone can represent the second or third most abundant aromatic monomer after oxidative depolymerization of purified lignin (8, 9), black liquor (10), kraft lignin (11, 12, 13), or sulfite lignin (14). In addition, acetovanillone is a product of microbial oxidation of ethylguaiacol, a product of biomass hydrogenolysis that can represent up to 10% of aromatic monomers (15). Microbes that are able to generate chemicals from these abundant biomaterials should therefore have the ability to metabolize acetovanillone and other chemically modified aromatics into valuable products. However, multiple bacterial strains studied for lignin conversion, including Pseudomonas putida, Pseudomonas fluorescens, Novosphingobium aromaticivorans, and Rhodococcus opacus, are unable to metabolize acetovanillone (9, 13, 15, 16).

We have developed strains of N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 that convert aromatic monomers and dimers from deconstructed biomass into 2-pyrone-4,6-dicarboxylic acid (17), cis,cis-muconic acid (18), or other products (19). However, N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 is incapable of metabolizing acetovanillone (15). Because acetovanillone is structurally similar to vanillin and other aromatics metabolized by N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 (20, 21), we hypothesized that adaptive laboratory evolution could generate strains able to utilize this chemically modified product of lignin deconstruction.

Here we identify a single amino acid substitution of Glu to Lys at residue 16 in a previously uncharacterized gene product, Saro_1862, that is both necessary and sufficient for growth of N. aromaticivorans on acetovanillone as the sole growth substrate. The E16K substitution in Saro_1862 increases the in vitro ATP-dependent rate of acetovanillone phosphorylation by the purified recombinant protein over 1600-fold. We call the Saro_1862 protein MarK for multiple aromatic kinase, as MarK E16K phosphorylates numerous related aromatic monomers in vitro and is required for microbial growth on a structurally related subset of these compounds. The crystal structure shows MarK E16K is a homodimer of the widely distributed yet poorly understood UPF0261 domain and suggests how the E16K substitution increases aromatic phosphorylation. These data, combined with results of bioinformatic analyses, predict that MarK family members are involved in metabolism of aromatic compounds by many other bacteria.

Results

Laboratory evolution of acetovanillone-metabolizing strains of N. aromaticivorans DSM12444

While N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 metabolizes vanillin and a variety of structurally similar aromatic compounds, it cannot catabolize acetovanillone (15). Thus, we hypothesized that adaptive laboratory evolution could yield mutations that lead to acetovanillone metabolism by N. aromaticivorans. To test this, we grew the parent N. aromaticivorans strain in three parallel minimal media cultures with a mixture of acetovanillone and vanillin as the sole growth substrates. We initially grew cultures in medium containing 0.34 mM acetovanillone and 1.36 mM vanillin for three passages and increased the acetovanillone:vanillin ratio in subsequent passages until the cultures only contained acetovanillone as the sole growth substrate (Fig. S1). At the end of this procedure, we obtained cultures that showed growth and metabolism of acetovanillone as the sole growth substrate after 48 h.

We plated the three evolved cultures onto solid medium containing acetovanillone as the sole growth substrate and sequenced genomic DNA from four isolated colonies from each plate. Analysis of the genomic DNA revealed that all 12 isolates had a single G to A mutation in the first position of codon 16 of Saro_1862 (SARO_RS09325, Dataset S1), yielding a glutamate to lysine substitution (E16K) in the protein. We introduced the same single nucleotide mutation into the genome of the parent strain by homologous recombination, and the newly created strain (TD606) grew on acetovanillone as a sole growth substrate with properties comparable to the evolved strains (Fig. 1). Thus, the MarK E16K substitution is necessary and sufficient to confer acetovanillone metabolism to N. aromaticivorans DSM12444.

Saro_1862 is a part of a predicted acetovanillone metabolism operon

The N. aromaticivorans genome sequence shows that Saro_1862 is the first gene in a transcription unit (Saro_1862-1858) that encodes homologs of the acvCDEF/hpeCBAD acetovanillone metabolism genes in Sphingobium sp. SYK-6 and Rhodococcus rhodochrous GD02, respectively (22, 23) (Fig. 2). In these two other organisms, AcvC/HpeC and AcvF/HpeD catalyze essential steps in acetovanillone metabolism, so we created N. aromaticivorans strains lacking the homologs of acvC/hpeC (Saro_1861) and acvF/hpeD (Saro_1858) in the acetovanillone-metabolizing TD606 strain. As predicted, these mutant strains (TD625 lacking Saro_1861 and TD634 lacking Saro_1858) no longer grew on acetovanillone (Fig. 1). Based on amino acid identity, we propose annotating Saro_1861-1858 as acvCDEF (Table S2).

Saro_1862 encodes an acetovanillone kinase

N. aromaticivorans lacks genes encoding homologs of the heterodimeric, ATP-dependent acetovanillone kinases AcvAB/HpeHI in Sphingobium sp. SYK-6 or R. rhodochrous GD02, respectively. However, we hypothesized that Saro_1862 encodes a kinase that phosphorylates acetovanillone, since the predicted gene product is a member of the UPF0261 domain family best characterized by the tomato Tm-1 protein, which harbors an ATP-binding domain. To test this, we expressed and purified MarK E16K and incubated it with acetovanillone and ATP. High-pressure liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) analysis showed the disappearance of acetovanillone and the accumulation of a compound with the correct m/z ratio for phosphorylated acetovanillone (Fig. 3A). No phosphorylated acetovanillone was observed in control assays lacking either MarK E16K or ATP (Fig. 3, D and E). Additional HPLC analysis identified ADP as the stoichiometric nucleotide product of MarK activity (Fig. 4). These results validate that MarK E16K, encoded by Saro_1862, is a previously undescribed form of acetovanillone kinase.

To further characterize this kinase, we used a spectrophotometric assay that measures conversion of acetovanillone to phospho-acetovanillone (23) by the E16K and wildtype forms of MarK. Under identical reaction conditions with 50 μM acetovanillone, MarK E16K phosphorylated acetovanillone at 64 ± 5 min−1 (n = 3 preparations, Fig. S3), while MarK E16 had a reaction rate of 0.041 ± 0.003 min−1 (n = 3 technical replicates). This ∼1600-fold increase in rate provided by the E16K substitution is likely responsible for the ability of evolved strains and TD606 to grow on acetovanillone.

MarK E16K phosphorylates aromatic ketones and aldehydes in vitro

Since neither MarK nor its homologs have been characterized, we tested whether representatives from the three major classes of lignin-derived aromatic monomers (e.g., syringyl, guaiacyl, and p-hydroxyphenyl) are substrates of this enzyme. After 1 h in vitro incubation, MarK E16K phosphorylated all three classes of aromatics (Fig. 5), with a possible preference for those with aldehyde or ketone substituents and showed incomplete phosphorylation with syringyl substrates (Figs. MarK E16K is required for growth on ketone-substituted aromatics

We also tested the N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 and TD606 (containing MarK E16K) for growth on the aromatics that were phosphorylated by purified MarK E16K. TD606 was able to grow using guaiacylpropanone or p-hydroxyacetophenone as a sole growth substrate, while the parent strain was not (Fig. 6). Both parent and evolved strains grew equally well on vanillin, p-hydroxybenzaldehyde, vanillic acid, and methylguaiacol, suggesting that MarK E16K is not essential for their metabolism (Fig. S21). In addition, although many other aromatics were phosphorylated by MarK E16K, they did not support growth of TD606. In sum, MarK E16K supports growth of N. aromaticivorans on some aromatics that contain an acetyl or propanone side chain.

Each MarK monomer consists of N- (residues 4-178) and C-terminal (residues 186-406) subdomains with ATP-Grasp-like αβα sandwich folds connected by a seven amino acid linker. The homodimer has a C-shape with an interface of 1527 Å2 provided by the N-terminal subdomains; the two C-terminal subdomains have no interactions in the observed structure.

The MarK E16K structure was determined using crystals obtained from crystal growth solution containing citrate, acetovanillone, and cacodylate. In this structure, acetovanillone is bound in a well-defined pocket in both C-terminal domains (Fig. 7B), with hydrogen bonds provided by Gly195 N and His219 NE2 to the phenolic oxygen and His348 to the ketone oxygen. The His304 sidechain provides a π-stacking interaction on one side of the bound substrate, while Thr196, Phe283, and Ile285 provide a hydrophobic surface on the other side. K16 in MarK E16K is located on an exposed surface of the N-terminal domain, and citrate and two Na are bound nearby (Fig. 7C).

MarK has the closest structural homology to the tomato protein Tm-1 (PDB: 3WRY-B, Z-score 31.0; rmsd 5.3 Å, (25)). In fact, Tm-1 is the only solved protein structure that shares similarity with both the N- and C-terminal domains of MarK; 1000 subsequent hits in a Dali structure search show structural similarity with either the N-terminal or C-terminal domain but not both (25). Tm-1 provides resistance to tomato mosaic virus by binding tightly to the viral replication complex, bridged by an ATP molecule. In the Tm-1 structure, ATPγS is bound to the N-terminal domain. Alignment of the MarK E16K and Tm-1 structures shows that citrate and ATPγS are placed in a similar location of the protein fold (Fig. 7C). Moreover, MarK E16K corresponds to Tm-1 Lys20, which Ishibashi et al. (26) showed makes an essential contact with the α-phosphate oxyanion of bound ATPγS. The ∼25 Å separation between a well-defined acetovanillone binding site and putative ATP binding site in MarK is considered in the Discussion.

Presence of MarK homologs across phylogeny

MarK is a member of the UPF0261 domain family, which is widely distributed in Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukaryota but had little prior experimental validation of its possible function(s). The kinase function assigned to this ubiquitous domain by this work, and its essential role in aromatic metabolism, raises a question of whether homologs of genes needed for utilization of these carbon sources are present in other bacteria. Thus, we searched 17,510 genomes in the NCBI representative prokaryotic genomes database for homologs of either monomeric (MarK) or heterodimeric (AcvAB or HpeHI) aromatic kinases along with the presence of genes required for later steps in acetovanillone metabolism, namely acvCDEF and vceAB or hpeCBAD and hpeEF from Sphingobium sp. SYK-6 and R. rhodochrous GD02, respectively (22, 23). This search identified 266 genomes encoding a MarK homolog and 570 genomes encoding AcvAB or HpeHI homologs along with other required genes somewhere in the genome (Dataset S2). In 27 of these genomes, the marK homolog is colocalized with acvCDEF/hpeCBAD homologs in a similar arrangement as in N. aromaticivorans (Fig. 2A, Dataset S3), while 25 genomes contain heterodimeric kinase acvAB/hpeHI homologs colocalized with acvCDEF/hpeCBAD as in Sphingobium sp. SYK-6 or R. rhodochrous GD02 (Fig. 2A, Dataset S3). The presence of such homologs in bacterial genomes suggests that many bacteria could metabolize acetovanillone or related aromatic compounds via a similar pathway. Organisms containing these genes are well-distributed across the bacterial phylogeny (Fig. 8), including Alphaproteobacteria, Actinomycetota, and Bacillota. Genes encoding MarK homologs are more abundant in Alphaproteobacteria than homologs of the heterodimeric kinases AcvAB or HpeHI (Fig. 8). However, ∼25% of the genomes with at least one aromatic kinase gene (179 of 657) encode homologs of both MarK and AcvAB or HpeHI, predicting that numerous bacteria contain two enzymes to phosphorylate acetovanillone or other aromatic substrates.

Discussion

The use of lignin as an abundant, renewable source of chemicals requires strategies to generate these chemicals from both native and chemically modified aromatics. While acetovanillone is abundant in biomass depolymerized by several methods, it cannot be used as a sole growth substrate by N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 and other aromatic-utilizing bacteria (15). We used adaptive laboratory evolution to isolate N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 mutants that grow on acetovanillone due to a point mutation in Saro_1862, encoding a previously uncharacterized protein. Our studies show that Saro_1862 is a previously undescribed multiple aromatic kinase, now named MarK. This is the first catalytic function determined for proteins in the ubiquitous, sparsely characterized UPF0261 family.

MarK can phosphorylate multiple aromatics

Our analysis of homodimeric MarK and prior work on heterodimeric kinases AcvAB and HpeHI (22, 23) show that all phosphorylate acetovanillone. However, the rate of acetovanillone phosphorylation by Mark E16K (64 ± 5 min−1) is higher than reported for either pure R. rhodochrous GD02 HpeHI (4.1 ± 0.4 min−1, (23)) or cell extracts containing Sphingobium sp. SYK-6 AcvAB (22). Thus, heterologous engineering of acetovanillone metabolism may benefit from using MarK E16K based on its higher rate of catalysis. The simplicity of optimizing homodimeric versus heterodimeric expression may also have some advantage.

MarK E16K, AcvAB (22), and HpeHI (27) can all phosphorylate multiple aromatic compounds, including acetovanillone, acetosyringone, vanillin, and p-hydroxybenzaldehyde. Based on relative peak areas in the HPLC-MS assay used in this study, MarK E16K is more active with acetovanillone than acetosyringone, similar to HpeHI (27), while AcvAB displays the opposite pattern (22). Moreover, MarK E16K did not phosphorylate guaiacol, while Sphingobium sp. SYK-6 AcvAB is active with this aromatic (22). Since phosphorylation is not required for growth of N. aromaticivorans or other bacteria on vanillin, p-hydroxybenzaldehyde, vanillic acid, or methylguaiacol (Fig. S21, (21, 28, 29, 30)), these are adventitious reactions of MarK E16K. More studies are required to determine if MarK synthesis or activity is regulated to avoid unnecessary consumption of ATP via MarK kinase activity in the presence of these aromatic substrates.

MarK supports growth on keto-substituted aromatics

N. aromaticivorans transforms ethyl- and propyl-substituted aromatics generated by reductive lignin depolymerization into acetovanillone, acetosyringone, guaiacylpropanone, and syringylpropanone (15), while these compounds can be generated directly by oxidative depolymerization (3, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13). Here, we show that MarK E16K uniquely endows growth on acetovanillone, guaiacylpropanone, and p-hydroxyacetophenone (Figs. 1 and 6). Metabolism of these aceto- and propanone-substituted aromatics likely proceeds by an ATP- and biotin-dependent carboxylation adjacent to the keto group of a phosphorylated aromatic substrate (22, 23). Owing to its ability to phosphorylate multiple aromatics, MarK E16K could be important for metabolism of other keto-substituted aromatics as well.

In contrast, we found that cells expressing MarK E16K could not grow on acetosyringone (Fig. S22), even though this aromatic is phosphorylated by MarK E16K in vitro and the predicted downstream product syringic acid (22) can be used by N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 as a sole growth substrate (17). Since S-type aromatics such as acetosyringone are abundant in depolymerized lignin from hardwoods (11, 15), additional studies are needed to generate N. aromaticivorans strains that can metabolize acetosyringone.

Structural insights into MarK kinase activity

We propose that the multiple carboxylate groups of citrate in the MarK structure may be a proxy for the negatively charged phosphotriester portion of a bound ATP as seen in Tm-1. The overlap between citrate in the MarK structure and ATPγS in the aligned Tm-1 structure (Fig. 7C) suggests that this region represents the ATP-binding site in MarK, and AlphaFold three docking of ATP to MarK E16K also supports this (31). In addition, the correspondence of MarK E16K to Tm-1 K20, which directly contacts ATPγS, supports this placement of the ATP binding site in MarK. Lysine residues provide stabilization of the negative charge during nucleotide binding in many kinases (32, 33, 34, 35), which could explain the importance of the E16K mutation in the function of evolved MarK.

Acetovanillone is bound to the C-terminal subdomain in a site that provides hydrophobic and π-stacking interactions and a critical hydrogen bond between His219 NE2 and phenolic oxygen. We used Rosetta docking (36, 37, 38, 39) to explore how these interactions contribute to the different reactivities shown in Fig. 5 and found that molecules with a phenol O group that could hydrogen bond with Gly195 N and His219 NE2 gave docked poses with best total scores similar to acetovanillone (Table S1). Hydrophobic interactions also provided a strong component of the binding, such as with p-cresol. Removal of the methoxy group or introduction of changes at the C-1 position (except for large extension such as in ferulate or p-coumarate) had lesser impact on predicted binding to the C-terminal subdomain.

In the MarK E16K structure, the putative ATP binding site is ∼25 Å from the observed acetovanillone binding site, raising a question of how this and other aromatics could be phosphorylated. One possibility is that the 7-residue linker between the N- and C-terminal domains of MarK could allow inter-domain movements which bring ATP and acetovanillone closer together, as these residues have high B-factor values. The set of Tm-1 structures shows a variety of arrangements of its N- and C-terminal subdomains in the presence of binding partners and ATP (26), supporting the possibility of inter-domain movements in MarK. We used hard-body docking of the MarK E16K homodimer and the separated N- and C-terminal subdomains in the absence of ATP (40, 41) and negative-stain cryo-EM to explore possibilities for alternative arrangements that might bring the two binding sites into closer proximity, but none were satisfactory (Fig. S25). Use of AlphaFold 3 (31) predicted that the MarK crystallographic dimer bound to ATP could dimerize in a way that places the γ-phosphate of ATP within 12 Å of His219 from the opposing dimer, with a possibility that different rotamers of Lys16 and His219 may reduce this distance even further. In other protein fold families, large-scale domain motions have been used to achieve phosphoryl transfer over extended distances (42, 43, 44), including for the acetovanillone kinase HpeHI (27).

One notable difference between MarK and HpeHI is the nucleotide product they create: MarK produces ADP upon aromatic phosphorylation (Fig. 4), while HpeHI is a dikinase that produces AMP (27). Thus, MarK has a distinct mechanism for ATP hydrolysis leading to acetovanillone phosphorylation relative to HpeHI. Additional structure-function studies of UPF0261 domain family members like MarK are needed to reveal additional details of their mechanism of ATP hydrolysis and substrate phosphorylation.

Diversity of bacterial aromatic kinases

After searching for acetovanillone metabolism genes across the bacterial tree of life (Fig. 8), we found that homologs of these genes are present in multiple highly divergent clades. This analysis also revealed that many bacterial genomes contained homologs of both MarK and AcvAB/HpeHI, opening the possibility that some organisms can use different kinases to increase the number and types of aromatic substrates that are phosphorylated and metabolized.

Sequence alignments of MarK homologs revealed that a number of N-terminal residues are highly conserved (>90%), including D14, T15, and K16 which are likely involved in ATP binding. We also note that among all Novosphingobium genome sequences in the NCBI representative prokaryotic genomes database, N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 is the only one having E16. All other Novosphingobium genomes encode a lysine (K) at the same MarK residue. The presence of a lysine at this residue in all other MarK homologs is consistent with the role of lysine 16 in catalysis by MarK E16K shown here and the reduced in vitro rate of acetovanillone phosphorylation by MarK with a glutamate at this position. Thus, it appears our N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 strain contains a rare, mutant form of MarK with insufficient kinase activity to support growth on acetovanillone and some other aromatics. Other amino acids that interact with acetovanillone in MarK E16K (G195, H219, and H304) are also highly conserved across homologs (>90%), suggesting this is a conserved aromatic phenol binding site and that phosphorylation of these substrates is a widely shared capability in Bacteria.

In conclusion, we used adaptive laboratory evolution to create N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 strains capable of metabolizing acetovanillone, an aromatic monomer that can be found in or derived from lignin depolymerized by oxidative and reductive methods. We found that a single E16K amino acid change in a previously uncharacterized MarK was both necessary and sufficient for acetovanillone metabolism by N. aromaticivorans DSM12444 and recapitulates the presence of a comparable lysine residue seen in other Novosphingobium strains. Biochemical and genetic characterization of MarK revealed that this kinase phosphorylates several aromatic monomers and is required for metabolism of selected aromatics with ketone side chains. Our data also predict that MarK homologs are found throughout bacteria, and structural analysis shows high conservation among residues that bind ATP and aromatic monomers. This work provides the first evidence for catalysis by the widespread UPF0261 protein domain and highlights the potential importance of aromatic phosphorylation as an initial step in metabolism of these abundant chemicals by many existing or engineered microorganisms.

Experimental Procedures

Bacterial strains and growth media

For these experiments, we used the parent strain N. aromaticivorans 12444Δ1879 (45), in which the putative sacB gene has been deleted to allow markerless genomic modification by variants of the pK18mobsacB plasmid (46). N. aromaticivorans cultures were grown in standard mineral base (SMB) (45) at an initial pH of 7.0, supplemented with indicated carbon sources, and shaken at ∼200 rpm at 30 °C. For routine growth and manipulation, N. aromaticivorans strains were grown in SMB + 10 mM glucose. Where needed to select for the presence or absence of plasmids, media were supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin or 10% sucrose (w/v).

Escherichia coli strains used for cloning and conjugation were grown in lysogeny broth and shaken at ∼200 rpm at 37 °C. Strain WM6026 was grown in lysogeny broth supplemented with 300 μM diaminopimelic acid. Where needed to select for the presence of plasmids, media were supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin.

Adaptive laboratory evolution

Media for the adaptive laboratory evolution experiment were made by mixing SMB-acetovanillone (1.7 mM) with SMB-vanillin (1.8 mM) in different ratios (schematically described in Fig. S1). Three replicate cultures were started in 10 ml of 20% SMB-acetovanillone/80% SMB-vanillin using parent N. aromaticivorans 12444Δ1879 colonies from an SMB-glucose plate. After 72 h, 20 μl of each culture was used to inoculate a new 10 ml 20% SMB-acetovanillone/80% SMB-vanillin culture. These cultures were incubated for 48 h, followed by inoculating new cultures for 48 h, and followed by inoculating new cultures for 72 h. Then, these 72 h cultures were used to inoculate 10 ml 50% SMB-acetovanillone/50% SMB-vanillin. We followed the same pattern of 48 h, 48 h, and 72 h incubation of these cultures at this aromatic ratio. Next, these 72 h cultures were used to inoculate 10 ml 80% SMB-acetovanillone/20% SMB-vanillin, which again followed the same pattern of 48 h, 48 h, and 72 h incubation at this aromatic ratio. Finally, these 72 h cultures were used to inoculate 100% SMB-acetovanillone cultures, which were incubated for 72 h before freezing aliquots from the three cultures, denoted TD561, TD562, and TD563. We plated these stocks on SMB-acetovanillone to isolate single colonies, which were designated strains TD585-596 (Table 2, Fig. S1). All cultures were monitored intermittently for cell density using a Klett–Summerson photoelectric colorimeter with a red filter, and they each showed increases in cell density before each passage (Fig. S2).

Whole genome sequencing

Evolved strains TD561-563 and TD585-596 were grown in 5 ml SMB-acetovanillone, and the parent strain N. aromaticivorans 12444Δ1879 was grown in 5 ml SMB-glucose. Cell pellets were obtained by centrifuging 2.4 ml culture for 2 min at 15,000g. Genomic DNA was isolated using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen), quality checked using a Nanodrop ND1000, and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA BR assay. Genomic DNA (1000 ng) was sheared using a Covaris S220 Focused-ultrasonicator using the manufacturer’s protocol for 300 bp target fragment size. DNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. Prepared sequencing libraries were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA BR assay, and fragment sizes were measured using a TapeStation D1000. Pooled libraries were sequenced using paired end 2 × 50 bp reads in an Illumina NextSeq 1000 following the manufacturer’s protocol.

DNA sequence data analysis

Paired end Illumina sequencing reads were processed with Breseq (v0.36.1) (47) using default parameters. Reads were analyzed for genomic differences compared to the N. aromaticivorans DSM 12444 reference (Assembly Accession Number GCF_000013325.1). The mutations, primarily SNPs and INDELs, identified by Breseq in greater than 80% of sequencing reads were used for further analysis and are included in Dataset S1.

Generation of defined N. aromaticivorans mutants

N. aromaticivorans genomic mutations were created by homologous recombination using variants of the pK18mobsacB plasmid (46), with detailed methods described in Supporting Information. Briefly, plasmids containing regions of homology near the desired genome modification site were transferred into N. aromaticivorans by conjugation using E. coli S17-1 or WM6026 cells. Transconjugants that had recombined the plasmid into the genome were selected for plates containing kanamycin. Kanamycin-resistant colonies were streaked onto SMB + 10 mM glucose + 10% sucrose (w/v) to select for double crossovers that result in the plasmid looping out of the genome. Colonies on sucrose-containing plates were patched onto kanamycin and sucrose plates separately to screen for kanamycin sensitivity and sucrose resistance. Such colonies were then screened by colony PCR to check for the desired genetic mutation, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Bacterial growth curves

Starter cultures of N. aromaticivorans strains were grown overnight in SMB-vanillate (4 mM). The following morning, these starter cultures were diluted 1:1 in fresh SMB-vanillate (4 mM) and shaken at 30 °C for an additional hour. Then, these cultures were used to inoculate 1.5 ml of SMB media containing a designated aromatic carbon source in a 24-well plate at a starting A600 of 0.05, as measured in a 1 cm cuvette. Various SMB media containing different aromatics were prepared fresh by adding a 1:100 dilution of 100× aromatic dissolved in methanol to the SMB media. All aromatics were tested at 1 mM and 2 mM, but only data for more robust growth are shown. The plates were incubated at 30 °C and shaking for 1 min every 15 min. Each condition was tested in triplicate cultures. Growth of all N. aromaticivorans strains in SMB-vanillate (2 mM) is reported in Fig. S23.

MarK expression and purification

A gBlock of Saro_1862 encoding MarK was codon optimized for E. coli (Integrated DNA Technologies) and cloned into pVP68K (48), which adds an N-terminal His8-tagged maltose-binding protein (MBP) to the protein coding sequence. Primers were designed to introduce a TEV protease cleavage site (ENLYFQS) directly upstream of the gene encoding MarK. This plasmid was then transformed into in E. coli B834 cells. Protein expression was induced by growth in autoinduction medium ZMS-80155 (49) supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin. The ZMS-80155 medium contains 1% N-Z Amine AS, 50 mM phosphate, 20 mM succinate, 0.8% glycerol, 0.015% glucose, 0.5% α-lactose, 2 mM MgSO4, a trace metals mix (49), and a vitamin mix (50). Cultures were grown at room temperature for 24 h (A600 > 10), at which point they were chilled on ice. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 6000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were then resuspended in 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, and 300 mM NaCl. Cell suspensions were sonicated using a Branson Sonifier 450 for 30 min at 25% duty cycle on the max power setting for the microtip attachment. Cell debris was separated from the cell lysate by centrifugation at 20,000g for 30 min at 4 °C.

The cell lysate was passed through a Cytiva ÄKTA pure 25 chromatography system with a Cytiva HisTrap HP 5 ml column kept at 4 °C for protein purification. The column was preequilibrated with 20 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, and 20 mM imidazole, which was also used as the wash buffer following sample injection. Proteins were eluted with a five column volume gradient from 0% to 100% elution buffer, which contained 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, and 300 mM imidazole. Elution buffer was held at 100% for 10 column volumes until returning to 0%. Eluted protein was concentrated and buffer exchanged using a Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit with 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, and 300 mM NaCl. Protein was treated with approximately 0.015 mg/ml TEV protease for 24 h at room temperature. Next, cleaved protein was again run through the Cytiva ÄKTA pure 25 chromatography system with a Cytiva HisTrap HP 5 ml column for separation of cleaved MarK from the His8-MBP. Using the same method, cleaved MarK eluted at ∼40% elution buffer while His8-MBP eluted at ∼90% elution buffer, as confirmed by enzyme activity assays and SDS-PAGE. Cleaved MarK was concentrated and buffer exchanged using a Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit with 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, and 300 mM NaCl. Protein concentration was determined using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay, using bovine serum albumin as a standard. Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Size-exclusion chromatography

Size-exclusion chromatography was used to estimate the oligomerization state of purified recombinant MarK E16K and MarK E16 proteins. A Phenomenex bioZen size-exclusion column (SEC-2 1.8 μm 150 Å 150 × 4.6 mm) connected to a Shimadzu Nexera XR HPLC system set to 0.7 μl injections and a 0.3 ml/min flow rate was used for chromatography. The mobile phase was 30 mM Tris, pH 8, containing 100 mM NaCl. Protein retention times were determined by absorbance at 280 nm as measured by a Shimadzu SPD-M20A photodiode array detector. A standard curve was created by plotting log10 molecular weight versus retention time for standards in the Supelco Protein Standard Mix 15 to 600 kDa and for bovine serum albumin. The apparent molecular weight of the MarK E16K and MarK E16 proteins was calculated from the linear fit of this standard curve.

In vitro aromatic phosphorylation assays

To test the ability of MarK protein to phosphorylate acetovanillone or other substrates, purified recombinant enzyme was mixed with substrate and ATP in a reaction buffer. A typical 100 μl reaction contained 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 2 mM MgCl2, 200 μM MnCl2, 1 mM ATP, 200 μM aromatic substrate (from a 2 mM stock in water), and 0.01 mg/ml MarK protein. The reaction was incubated at 30 °C for 1 h before analysis by HPLC-MS. Reaction products were separated by a reverse-phase Phenomenex Kinetex F5 column (2.6 μm pore size, 2.1 mm ID, 150 mm length) attached to a Shimadzu Nexera XR HPLC system. The mobile phase was a binary gradient (Fig. S26) of solvent A (0.2% formic acid in water) and solvent B (methanol) flowing at 0.4 ml/min. To detect reaction products, absorbance was measured between 190 and 500 nm using a Shimadzu SPD-M20A photodiode array detector. The eluent from the HPLC was also analyzed via mass spectrometry using a Shimadzu triple quadrupole mass spectrometer LCMS-8045. We used negative mode Q3 scans from 100 m/z to 500 m/z around the retention times of HPLC peaks to obtain mass spectra of compounds eluting at such times.

Determination of the nucleotide product of MarK activity

To determine the nucleotide product of MarK activity, in vitro reactions were carried out as described above for the aromatic phosphorylation assays. Reaction products were analyzed using reverse-phase HPLC on a Kinetex 2.6 μm C18 100Å column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, Phenomenex; catalog #00F-4462-AN) connected to a Shimadzu Nexera XR HPLC system. Chromatographic separation was performed isocratically using 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) at a flow rate of 0.35 ml/min with the column oven maintained at 30 °C.

The mobile phase was prepared by dissolving 3.70 g of potassium phosphate monobasic (KH2PO4) and 3.98 g of potassium phosphate dibasic (K2HPO4) in 1 L of deionized water, adjusting the pH to 6.0 as needed. ATP, ADP, and AMP standards (10 mM) were prepared in reaction buffer (20 mM Hepes, 0.2 mM MnCl2, and 2 mM MgCl2, pH 7.5) and serially diluted to generate a standard curve. Nucleotide species were detected by UV absorbance at 254 nm, identified by comparing retention times to those of authentic standards, and quantified by comparing 254 nm absorbance to that of standards.

Measurement of the rate of acetovanillone phosphorylation by MarK

The rates of acetovanillone phosphorylation by the MarK E16K variant enzyme and the MarK wildtype enzyme were measured as described in (23), with two modifications: the reaction buffer used 20 mM Tris pH 8 instead of 20 mM HEPPS, pH 8, and the enzyme concentration was approximately 40 nM (MarK E16K) or 40 μM (MarK wildtype) instead of 1 μM to ensure measurements were obtained under initial velocity conditions. Otherwise, the buffer similarly contained 50 μM acetovanillone, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM MnCl2, 2 mM DTT, and 1 mM ATP. The reaction was monitored by absorbance at 340 nm, and a blank-adjusted reaction rate was calculated based on the difference in extinction coefficients for acetovanillone and phospho-acetovanillone at 340 nm (23) and a measured path length of 0.62 cm based on a 200 μl reaction in a 96-well plate (Fig. S3). The assay was performed in technical triplicates. The rate reported for MarK E16K represents the average of three separate preparations of MarK E16K protein.

Crystallization, data collection, and structure determination of MarK E16K

Crystallization screening experiments using wildtype or E16K MarK alone, with acetovanillone, with ADP and ATP, and a combination of acetovanillone and ATP or ADP were conducted using commercial general screens and a SPT Labtech Mosquito crystallization robot. Diffracting crystals were obtained from 2 mM acetovanillone, 1.0 M trisodium citrate, and 0.1 M Na-cacodylate, pH 6.5. Crystals were harvested using Mitegen MicroMounts after supplementing the crystal growth solution with 20% glycerol and cryopreserved by immersion in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at NSLS-II AMX Beamline (17-ID-1) (51) on a Dectris Eiger X 9 M detector. The structure was solved and refined against a dataset collected at 0.9201 Å, containing 2000, 0.1° frames. Usable diffraction data from the primitive orthorhombic lattice extending to 1.58 Å was reduced with XDS/XSCALE (52). Structure determination used the Phenix suite of programs (53). The most likely composition of the asymmetric unit was two copies of the protein and a solvent content of 54%. The space group was assigned as P212121 when the structure was solved by molecular replacement using Phaser (54). A search model segmented into domains corresponding to residues 5 to 177 and 187 to 406 of AlphaFold model AF-Q2G771-F1-v4 (55) gave a log likelihood gain of 3923 and a translation function z-score of 43.9. The final structure, validated (56) and deposited in the Protein Data Bank (57) with accession code 9MPY, was obtained by alternating rounds of map fitting in Coot (58) and refinement using phenix.refine (59).

Searching for genes encoding an acetovanillone kinase and other reported acetovanillone metabolism proteins across bacteria

The conversion of acetovanillone to vanillic acid requires an acetovanillone kinase (MarK or AcvAB/HpeHI), a carboxylase (AcvCDE/HpeCBA), a phosphatase (AcvF/HpeD), a CoA ligase (VceA or HpeE), and a beta-ketoacyl-CoA hydrolase (VceB or HpeF) (22, 23). Thus, we searched NCBI for genomes that contained homologs of MarK (ABD26302.1), AcvA (WP_014074980.1), or HpeH (UZF45677.1), AcvB (WP_014074979.1) or HpeI (UZF45676.1), AcvC (WP_014074978.1) or HpeC (UZF45675.1), AcvD (WP_014074977.1) or HpeB (UZF45674.1), AcvE (WP_014074976.1) or HpeA (UZF45673.1), AcvF (WP_014074975.1) or HpeD (UZF45672.1), VceA (BAK67171.1) or HpeE (UZF45671.1), and VceB (BAK65920.1) or HpeF (UZF45670.1). We used tBLASTn in BLAST + version 2.14.1 (60) against the ref_prok_rep_genomes database downloaded from NCBI on September 12, 2023. We used the default tBLASTn parameters and kept all sequences that met the E-value cutoff of 0.01. Next, we used the taxonomy ID of each BLAST hit with TaxonKit software (61) to obtain lineage information for each species. The tBLASTn hits for all proteins, including genomic locations of the hits, can be found in Dataset S2. Finally, we compared the list of tBLASTn hits for each protein to assemble two separate lists of species with acetovanillone metabolism gene homologs: first, those that encode homologs of MarK, AcvC or HpeC, AcvD or HpeB, AcvE or HpeA, AcvF or HpeD, VceA or HpeE, and VceB or HpeF; second, those that encode homologs of AcvA or HpeH, AcvB or HpeI, AcvC or HpeC, AcvD or HpeB, AcvE or HpeA, AcvF or HpeD, VceA or HpeE, and VceB or HpeF (Dataset S2).

To assess whether homologs are colocalized in the genomes of these species, we compared the starting positions of tBLASTn hits for the kinase (MarK or AcvAB/HpeHI), carboxylase (AcvCDE/HpeCBA), and phosphatase (AcvF/HpeD). We ignored the CoA ligase (VceA or HpeE) and beta-ketoacyl-CoA hydrolase (VceB or HpeF) because they are not colocalized in multiple organisms that can metabolize acetovanillone (Fig. 2A). Homologs were determined to be colocalized if their starting positions were within 500 bp of the ending position of the previous homolog. The list of species containing gene clusters similar to those in Figure 2A is provided in Dataset S3, along with their genomic locations. A few genomes have homologs arranged in a different order; these are detailed in Dataset S3.

To display the findings in a tree representing the diversity of bacteria, we started with the phylogenetic tree of all classes of bacteria in the GTDB available in AnnoTree (62). We removed all unnamed nodes, collapsed some classes into phyla, and pruned any classes that were not present in the NCBI representative prokaryotic genomes database to yield a tree that best matched the subgroups contained in the NCBI representative prokaryotic genomes database. Some subgroups in the NCBI database have different designations in the GTDB, so these subgroups were renamed to their GTDB counterparts for a more accurate representation in the tree. For example, the 1028 Betaproteobacteria genomes in NCBI are labeled as Gammaproteobacteria in the GTDB and thus Figure 8 as well. Finally, we used lineage information obtained for each species that contained BLAST hits to label the distribution of our hits in the bacterial phylogenetic tree. Tree manipulations were performed using TreeViewer (63).

Ligand docking studies

Rosetta was used to dock aromatic ligands into the crystal structure of MarK E16K. The ligands investigated are listed in Table S1. Ligand definition files were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information PubChem; 50 conformers were calculated for each using the BCL::Conf server (64). The ligands to be docked were roughly aligned to the position of acetovanillone as a starting pose, and 1000 additional poses were calculated using rosetta_scripts.static.linuxgccrelease and scripts provided in Supporting Information.

Negative stain grid preparation

Carbon-coated copper 200-mesh TEM grids (CF200-Cu; Electron Microscopy Sciences) were glow discharged for 30 s with 20 mA current in a GloQube glow discharge system (Quorum Technologies). Purified recombinant MBP-MarK E16K was diluted to 9 μg/ml with dilution buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM MnCl2, 2 mM DTT, and 1 mM ATP) immediately prior to application of 4 μl of sample to the TEM grid. The protein was allowed to incubate on the grid for 1 min prior to two washes with 20 μl dilution buffer, two washes in 20 μl 1% uranyl acetate solution (Electron Microscopy Sciences), and a 1-min incubation in 1% uranyl acetate solution. The grid was side-blotted with #1 Whatman filter paper (Cytiva) between each wash and after the final incubation. The grid was allowed to further air dry for approximately 1 min and was stored in a desiccator until imaging.

Negative stain transmission electron microscopy

Imaging of the negative stain grids was done on a Talos L120C 120 kV transmission electron microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific) equipped with a CETA CMOS camera (ThermoFisher Scientific). Grid screening and data acquisition were done using standard low dose techniques in SerialEM 3.8 (65) software. Images were collected at a nominal magnification of 45 kx (3.2 Å/pixel) and 73 kx (2.0 Å/pixel) using a range of defocus values from −0.5 μm to −2.0 μm in 0.25 μm intervals.
