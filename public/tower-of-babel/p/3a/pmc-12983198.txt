Dynamic Evolution and Degradation of Silicon–Electrolyte Interfaces under Cycling via Chemical Potential–Controlled Molecular Dynamics
Authors: Sart Ratanaporn, Kiettipong Banlusan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silicon is a promising high-capacity anode material for next-generation lithium-ion batteries, but its large volume change and unstable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) cause rapid capacity fading. To uncover the atomistic origins of this degradation, we develop a chemical-potential-controlled reactive molecular dynamics framework that enables explicit multicycle lithiation-delithiation simulations of silicon anodes. By tuning the electronegativity of Si atoms, the chemical potential of lithium is modulated to drive spontaneous insertion and extraction, mimicking charging and discharging. The simulations capture the key electrochemical behaviors, including Li migration, anode expansion-contraction, and SEI evolution. Under fast charging, accelerated Li insertion induces severe Si dissolution, volume loss, and reduced lithium retention. Concurrently, ethylene carbonate (EC) decomposes at the Si surface through ring opening, releasing C2H4 and forming carbonate fragments that bond with Si. Repeated cycling promotes detachment of Si–C–O species into the electrolyte, linking interfacial decomposition with mechanical failure. This study provides a direct atomistic picture of coupled electrode–electrolyte degradation in Si-based batteries and introduces a transferable simulation approach for exploring charge–discharge processes in alloying and intercalation materials.

Introduction

Silicon has emerged as a promising anode material for next-generation lithium-ion batteries due to its abundance, low cost, and exceptionally high theoretical specific capacity (∼4200 mAh/g). − Despite these advantages, silicon suffers from significant limitations. Upon full lithiation, it undergoes a volumetric expansion exceeding 300%, , leading to mechanical stress, electrode cracking, and continuous growth of the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI). These effects cause the progressive loss of active material, − reducing Coulombic efficiency and cycle life.

To address these challenges, researchers have explored two complementary strategies: nanostructure modification of the electrode and electrolyte design. Nanostructuring approaches, such as silicon nanoparticles, porous frameworks, or hybrid architectures, help buffer volume changes and improve mechanical resilience. For example, Ezzedine et al. developed a hybrid anode of silicon nanoparticles anchored on vertical carbon nanotubes, which buffer expansion and contraction during cycling. In addition, Azib et al. investigated Ni–Sn alloy matrices as buffering materials for silicon anodes, achieving reduced capacity loss and higher Coulombic efficiency. On the electrolyte side, progress has centered on optimizing solvents, lithium salts, and functional additives. Ethylene carbonate (EC) remains widely used for its high dielectric constant and ability to form protective interphases, though its high viscosity necessitates blending with lower-viscosity carbonates. − Ether-based solvents offer excellent ion-transport properties, − while lithium salts critically influence ionic conductivity, thermal stability, and interfacial chemistry. , Additives play a decisive role in regulating electrode–electrolyte reactions and enhancing long-term stability. In particular, vinylene carbonate (VC) and fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) serve as effective SEI-forming agents, − whereas sulfur- and phosphorus-based compounds enhance electrolyte stability and suppress side reactions. ,

While experimental studies have provided valuable insights into these degradation processes, a detailed atomistic understanding of the underlying mechanisms during cycling remains elusive. Several atomistic simulation approaches have been developed to probe fundamental processes in electrochemical energy storage systems. For example, kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) simulations have been widely used to investigate ionic transport kinetics in battery materials, , while molecular dynamics methods provide complementary insight into structural and chemical evolution. Reactive molecular dynamics (ReaxFF-MD) simulations offer the ability to probe structural and chemical transformations with bond-breaking and bond-forming resolution, yet their application to realistic electrochemical cycling has been restricted. − A major challenge is that conventional ReaxFF charge equilibration (QEq) methods equilibrate local charges but do not intrinsically impose a macroscopic electrochemical driving force. To overcome this limitation, Onofrio and Strachan introduced the electrochemical dynamics with implicit degrees of freedom (EChemDID) method, which dynamically modifies atomic electronegativities to represent applied voltages across metallic electrodes. This pioneering approach successfully reproduced voltage-driven Cu filament growth and dissolution in electrometallization cells. Building on this idea, subsequent studies have extended the EChemDID method to Li-metal systems, enabling the multicycle simulations of lithium stripping, plating, and SEI evolution. For instance, Yang and Pao applied the method to probe SEI microstructural changes during repeated cycling and revealed the role of electrolyte additives in stabilizing the interphase. More recently, Lee et al. employed the approach to dynamically visualize dendrite nucleation and growth at Li-metal electrodes, showing how additives such as HF promote LiF formation and suppress dendritic instabilities.

Despite these advances, all prior applications of electronegativity-switching methods remain confined to metallic Li electrodes, where lithium deposition and dissolution dominate the electrochemical dynamics. These frameworks cannot be directly applied to alloying- or intercalation-type anodes, such as silicon, where lithium insertion competes with solvation in the electrolyte.

Here, we extend this framework to Si alloying anodes. Instead of modifying Li parameters, we adjust the electronegativity of Si atoms to modulate the relative chemical potential of lithium between the electrode and the electrolyte. This adjustment enables lithium to spontaneously migrate into or out of the Si anode under different electronegativity conditions, effectively mimicking charge and discharge. Using this chemical potential switching method, we achieve multicycle atomistic simulations of lithiation-delithiation in Si anodes. Our simulations reveal the mechanistic origin of Si dissolution under fast charging, and the dual degradation of both electrode and electrolyte, with Si bond breaking and EC decomposition. Together, these results provide a new atomistic framework for understanding Si-anode degradation and suggest design principles for more durable high-capacity batteries.

Computational Methods

ReaxFF Force Field

Reactive (ReaxFF) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out using the LAMMPS software package with an integration time step of 0.25 fs. ReaxFF incorporates bond order to dynamically modify covalent bonding and apply the charge equilibration (QEq) , method to update partial charges, thereby accounting for reactive phenomena and environment-dependent charge distribution. Within the QEq scheme, charge redistribution is obtained through self-consistent minimization of the total electrostatic energy: Eele=∑i(Ei0+χi0Qi+12Jii0Qi2)+12∑i<jJijQiQj where E i 0, χ i 0, and J ii 0 are the atomic energy, electronegativity, and self-Coulombic repulsion of atom i and J ij is the interatomic Coulomb interaction. Here, χ i 0 = (∂E ele/∂Q i )0 and J ii 0 = (∂2 E ele/∂Q i 2)0, such that atomic electronegativity can be regarded as the chemical potential for charge transfer.

The ReaxFF parameters for the Si–Li–C–O–H systems were taken from ref This parameter set was originally developed for the Si–Li–O systems , to investigate lithiation mechanisms in Si-based electrodes and was subsequently combined with previously established parameters for the C–H and O–H systems. , To further optimize the force field, Yun et al. incorporated additional training data obtained from DFT calculations of bond dissociation energies and equations of state for various molecules and crystals relevant to this system (e.g., Si–C, Li–C, O–C). This refined parameter set has been successfully applied to study the atomistic evolution of the solid-electrolyte interphase in Si anodes.

Systems of Interest

Our simulations represent repeated lithiation-delithiation processes of a silicon anode in the presence of the ethylene carbonate (EC) electrolyte. Figure a illustrates the initial configuration used for cycling simulations, consisting of an amorphous silicon slab enclosed by EC+Li layers. The silicon anode was constructed by first creating a crystalline structure of 1600 Si atoms (10 × 10 × 2 unit cells), which was then transformed into the amorphous structure through annealing and quenching. Annealing was performed under an NPT ensemble at 2300 K and 1 atm for 100 ps, followed by cooling to 300 K over 200 ps to form amorphous silicon. The resulting amorphous silicon slab was subsequently equilibrated under an NVT ensemble at 300 K for 100 ps within a 55 × 55 Å box.

Validation of the chemical-potential tuning scheme. (a) Initial equilibrated configuration of the Si anode with EC electrolyte and Li, simulated with unmodified ReaxFF parameters. Atomic color coding: C, gray; H, white; O, red; Li, magenta; Si, orange. (b) Formation energies of Li x Si and Li x EC calculated with standard and modified Si electronegativities (ΔχSi = 0, 5, 7). (c) Corresponding insertion voltages derived from the formation energies.

The initial configuration of EC molecules was generated using the Packmol software, with 400 molecules equally distributed above and below the Si slab at a volume consistent with the bulk density of EC. A nonperiodic boundary condition was applied along the z-axis to spatially separate the upper and lower regions. Lithium atoms were then gradually inserted above and below the simulation box, up to twice the number of Si atoms and eight times the number of EC molecules (3200 Li atoms in total). At each insertion step, 400 Li atoms (200 per side) were added into the designated zones near the box edges, and the z-dimension of the box was slightly expanded to maintain zero stress. Each insertion was followed by a 10 ps NVT equilibration to allow Li diffusion and stabilization. The final structure was further equilibrated under the NVT ensemble at 500 K for 100 ps. This elevated temperature was chosen for production simulations to enhance Li diffusivity and reduce computational time.

Charge–Discharge Simulations

To simulate charge–discharge cycling, we modified the ReaxFF force field by adjusting the electronegativity (χ) parameter of Si atoms. This adjustment alters the relative chemical potential of lithium between the electrode and the electrolyte, thereby driving lithium insertion (charging) or extraction (discharging). Two charging conditions were examined: ΔχSi = 5 and ΔχSi = 7, which were chosen to raise the Si electronegativity above that of EC atoms. A higher ΔχSi value enhances the driving force for lithium migration into silicon, with ΔχSi = 7 corresponding to faster lithiation than ΔχSi = 5. Each step of charge or discharge consisted of a 50 ps NVT simulation at 500 K. Charging was simulated with the modified ReaxFF parameters, while discharging was simulated using the unmodified ReaxFF force field. A total of 10 full charge–discharge cycles were carried out, during which lithium ions were observed to reversibly migrate between the Si anode and the EC electrolyte, enabling direct tracking of lithiation-delithiation dynamics.

Chemical Potential Tuning for Charge–Discharge Switching

Figure a illustrates the equilibrated structure prior to cycling, simulated with the unmodified ReaxFF parameters. Under these conditions, Li atoms predominantly reside in the electrolyte, with only a small fraction penetrating into the Si slab, even after prolonged equilibration. This observation suggests that, thermodynamically, Li insertion into pristine Si is less favorable than solvation in EC. To quantify this preference and validate our electronegativity-tuning approach, we computed the formation energy: Ef=ELixSi(orLixEC)−(ESi(orEC)+xELi)

and the corresponding insertion voltage: V=−1edEfdx where x denotes the Li/Si or Li/EC ratio. Additional NPT simulations were performed on a series of Li x Si and Li x EC structures using both unaltered and modified Si parameters (see Supporting Information, Figures S1–S2). As shown in Figure b, EC exhibits a more negative formation energy than Si with standard ReaxFF, indicating that Li insertion is thermodynamically more favorable in EC. The associated voltage profiles (Figure c) further confirm the higher chemical potential of Li in EC relative to Si. Increasing the Si electronegativity shifts this balance: ΔχSi = 5 makes Si insertion moderately more favorable, while ΔχSi = 7 drives a pronounced stabilization of Li x Si and a substantial rise in the insertion voltage. Together, these calculations demonstrate that tuning the Si electronegativity parameter effectively modifies the Li chemical potential landscape, enabling Li to migrate between electrolyte and electrode. This establishes the basis for the charge–discharge cycling simulations presented below.

Nonselected atoms were omitted for clarity. Trajectory and surface analyses were performed using the OVITO software package. Longer simulation times result in further Li migration into Si. Upon restoring Si electronegativity to its original value, the migration direction reverse; Li atoms exit the Si slab and preferentially redistribute into the electrolyte.

To rationalize how electronegativity switching controls Li migration, Figure c,e present schematic diagrams of the competing chemical potentials at different operation states. As described in the Method section, the simulated structure has an initial Li content (Li/EC) of x EC 0 = 8. Prior to cycling simulation (Figure c), this chemical potential is balanced with that of a low-Li Li x Si alloy (see also Figure c), resulting in only a small amount of Li observed within the Si slab. As shown in Figure d, increasing the electronegativity of Si raises its chemical potential, driving lithium to spontaneously migrate from EC into silicon. Equilibrium is reached when the two potentials become equal, V(x Si C) = V(x EC C). Consequently, some fraction of Li is expected to remain in EC at the end of charging. Upon restoring Si electronegativity, the chemical potential of Si decreases below that of EC, reversing the migration direction and forcing Li to diffuse back into the electrolyte (Figure e). During this process, the voltage of Si rises as lithium leaves, while the EC voltage decreases due to Li accumulation. The reduced voltage difference at later stages results in a weaker driving force, which can leave residual lithium trapped in Si at the end of discharge (x Si DC). These results confirm that electronegativity switching provides a tunable thermodynamic lever for directing Li migration, capturing the essential features of charging and discharging in Si anodes.

To further validate the physical meaning of the electronegativity tuning scheme, we analyzed the electrostatic response of the system using QEq-derived charge density. From the charge distribution, the electric field and electrostatic potential profiles were obtained by solving Maxwell’s and Poisson’s equations (see Supporting Information, Figure S3). Increasing ΔχSi lowers the electrostatic potential of the silicon region relative to the electrolyte, creating a potential gradient that drives lithium migration into the anode. This behavior is consistent with the effect of an applied charging bias in electrochemical systems.

To demonstrate the applicability of the chemical potential switching method for cycling simulations, the prepared system was subjected to ten consecutive charge–discharge cycles. Figure a shows atomistic configurations obtained at different stages of cycling with ΔχSi = 5. The snapshots clearly illustrate the reversible insertion and removal of lithium, accompanied by marked dilation of the Si slab during charging and partial contraction during discharging. Notably, some Li remains trapped inside the anode after each discharge, indicating incomplete delithiation.

Multicycle lithiation–delithiation of Si anodes simulated using the chemical potential switching method. (a) Atomistic configurations during representative charge (C)–discharge (DC) cycles with ΔχSi = 5. (b) Number of Li atoms inside the Si anode as a function of time for ΔχSi = 5 and ΔχSi = 7.

A quantitative view of this behavior is provided in Figure b, which plots the number of Li atoms inside the Si anode as a function of time under two charging conditions, ΔχSi = 5 and ΔχSi = 7. In both cases, Li rapidly migrates into or out of Si within ∼10 ps, followed by slower adjustments that reasonably converge within ∼50 ps for each step. The stronger driving force of ΔχSi = 7 results in more Li insertion during charging compared with ΔχSi = 5. However, with increasing cycle number, the total amount of stored Li progressively decreases, particularly under the fast-charging condition (ΔχSi = 7, blue curves). This trend indicates structural degradation of the Si anode, which reduces its lithium-storage capability. During discharging, a further distinction emerges; the system cycled with ΔχSi = 7 retains fewer Li atoms inside Si compared to ΔχSi = 5 (red vs magenta curves in Figure b), especially at later cycles. As shown in subsequent analyses, these effects originate from the progressive dissolution of silicon during repeated lithiation-delithiation.

Electrode and Electrolyte Degradations during Cycling

To analyze structural changes in the anode, we calculated the surface area and solid volume of the Si slab using the Construct Surface Mesh modifier in the OVITO software, which employs the α-shape method. In this approach, atoms are treated as point-like particles, and tetrahedralization of the atomic positions classifies regions as solid or open depending on whether the circumsphere radius is smaller or larger than a reference probe sphere. The solid region is then tessellated to generate the surface mesh. A probe radius of 3.2 Å, corresponding to the interatomic distance of silicon in the lithiated structure (Figure S4), was used in this study. Figure shows the reconstructed surfaces of the Si slab during cycling with ΔχSi = 5 and ΔχSi = 7, with all atoms omitted for clarity. The analysis reveals alternating expansion and contraction of the slab and progressive roughening of its surface during repeated lithiation-delithiation. Figure a shows a clear increase in roughness with cycle number. More roughness is observed during lithiation compared to delithiation due to large expansion as Li enters the slab. Under faster charging conditions (ΔχSi = 7, Figure b), the slab undergoes greater expansion because of increased Li uptake. This results in a more abrupt rise in roughness, particularly in the early cycles. Such roughening is critical for battery stability, as it promotes heterogeneous SEI formation.

Surface mesh analysis of the Si anode during cycling with (a) ΔχSi = 5 and (b) ΔχSi = 7.

Figure quantifies the structural evolution of the Si slab during repeated lithiation-delithiation by tracking volume and surface area changes relative to the pristine state. As shown in Figures a and b, charging under ΔχSi = 7 induces significantly larger volume expansion than ΔχSi = 5, reflecting the higher chemical potential that drives more lithium into the anode. During discharge, both conditions exhibit incomplete contraction, with the electrode volume remaining slightly above the initial value. This residual swelling originates from lithium atoms trapped inside the silicon matrix, consistent with the incomplete delithiation observed in trajectory analyses. Over successive cycles, however, the amplitude of expansion diminishes, and under ΔχSi = 7 the delithiated electrode eventually contracts below its original volume. This irreversible shrinkage indicates progressive loss of active silicon. Figure c,d reveal that the electrode surface area also evolves markedly during cycling. The surface area is consistently larger during charging than discharging, and it increases progressively with cycle number. The effect is stronger at ΔχSi = 7, where rapid charging exacerbates surface roughening. These results quantitatively support the morphological observations in Figure . Notably, a sharp drop in surface area is observed during the tenth charge cycle at ΔχSi = 7, suggesting surface collapse and loss of structural integrity.

Additional mean square displacement and Arrhenius analyses of lithium diffusion under different ΔχSi conditions are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S5–S7 and Table S1). The results show modest changes in activation energy and diffusivity with ΔχSi, indicating that the dominant degradation mechanisms identified in this work arise from structural and interfacial instability, rather than intrinsic Li mobility limitations.

Our simulation results closely mirror experimental observations of silicon electrode degradation during cycling. , Zhao et al. visualized the 3D morphological evolution of nanoporous silicon anodes using synchrotron X-ray nanotomography, revealing that volume expansion and contraction during cycling gradually lead to delamination and loss of electrode thickness. Importantly, their study showed that higher-capacity cycling accelerates delamination and structural failure, paralleling our finding that ΔχSi = 7 promotes more severe volume change and surface instability. Together, the simulations and experiments highlight that fast charging, while enhancing short-term lithium uptake, induces morphological instability, including volume loss, surface roughening, and eventual delamination, that compromises long-term electrode durability.

Figure illustrates the chemical evolution at the Si-electrolyte interface and the solvation structure during repeated cycling under ΔχSi = 5. To probe this process, we tracked the molecular transformations of EC molecules initially adsorbed on the Si surface. Only atoms chemically bonded to the selected atoms are displayed in Figure a. As shown in the figure, the selected EC molecules undergo progressive ring-opening decomposition catalyzed by surface Si atoms. The inset traces the major reaction pathway. The interaction with under-coordinated surface Si induces ring opening, followed by the release of C2H4 and the formation of a surface-bonded [CO3] fragment. This mechanism is consistent with previous theoretical studies of EC reduction on Si surfaces and hard X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (HAXPES) experiments, which identified CO3-containing species on cycled Si anodes. To further validate the ReaxFF-predicted EC decomposition pathway, we performed complementary DFT calculations of EC ring opening on a Si(111) surface. The results confirm that EC decomposition into C2H4 and surface-bound [CO3] species is thermodynamically favorable (Supporting Information, Figure S8), consistent with the reaction mechanisms observed in our reactive MD simulations.

Beyond the single-cycle reactions previously reported, our multicycle simulations reveal dynamic interfacial instability. As the anode undergoes alternating expansion and contraction, Si bonds rupture, liberating Si–C–O radicals that dissolve into the electrolyte. Over repeated cycles, the interfacial region becomes increasingly heterogeneous, populated by increasing numbers of C2H4 molecules and Si–C–O fragments. These observations extend earlier findings by Martínez de la Hoz et al., Yun et al., and Jeong & Johnson, demonstrating that the SEI species formed at the Si surface are not static but evolve dynamically under electrochemical cycling. The simulated dissolution of Si–C–O species offers an atomistic picture of silicon loss and SEI destabilization during repeated charging–discharging, highlighting a chemical pathway that couples interfacial decomposition with electrode degradation. Figure b visualizes these environments. Near the Si surface, carbonate fragments bond to Si forming an amorphous interphase, while in the electrolyte region, a mixture of intact EC, C2H4 molecules, and Si–C–O fragments dominates. Minor [C2H4]-containing species are also observed both at the interface and in the electrolyte. Together, these results bridge the gap between prior single-event reduction studies and the dynamic degradation processes governing the long-term instability of Si-based anodes.

Our simulations enable direct tracking of key molecular species within the simulated system, including the number of Si atoms in the anode, dissolved Si species in the electrolyte, and Li atoms stored in the electrode. Figure a,b reveal that the number of Si atoms remaining in the electrode decreases progressively with repeated charge–discharge cycles under both ΔχSi = 5 and ΔχSi = 7. This continuous decline arises from interfacial reactions leading to SEI growth and Si dissolution, as previously visualized in Figure . The reduction is markedly more pronounced for ΔχSi = 7, demonstrating that rapid charging accelerates structural degradation of the silicon anode. The Si loss occurs primarily during discharging, when Li extraction is accompanied by Si–Si bond rearrangement and mechanical contraction, whereas partial structural recovery is observed upon relithiation during charging. This cumulative Si depletion directly impacts lithium storage capability. Figure c,d show a gradual decrease in the number of Li atoms inserted during charging and remaining after discharge. The effect is especially severe at ΔχSi = 7, where diminished Li retention correlates with accelerated structural breakdown of the active material.

Consistently, Figure c,d show a steady accumulation of C2H4, which is one of the main decomposition products identified in Figure . Taken together, these results demonstrate that fast charging not only amplifies mechanical and chemical degradation of the silicon anode, which are manifested as Si loss and reduced lithium retention, but also accelerates electrolyte decomposition, producing gaseous byproducts and unstable interphase species. The coupled deterioration of both electrode and electrolyte underscores the necessity of designing Si anodes and electrolyte formulations that can mitigate bond rupture and suppress interfacial reactivity under high-rate operation.

Conclusions

This work presents the atomistic demonstration of multicycle lithiation-delithiation simulations of a silicon anode using a chemical potential switching method based on ReaxFF molecular dynamics. By systematically tuning the electronegativity of Si atoms, the chemical potential of lithium between the electrode and electrolyte was modulated, allowing spontaneous and reversible Li migration, effectively mimicking charge and discharge processes. The simulations reveal that under repeated cycling, the Si anode experiences progressive structural and chemical degradation. Expansion and contraction during lithiation-delithiation induce surface roughening, Si–Si bond rupture, and dissolution of Si into the electrolyte. The degree of degradation intensifies under high-rate charging (ΔχSi = 7), leading to volume loss, reduced lithium storage capacity, and surface instability. Concurrently, the electrolyte undergoes decomposition initiated by Si-catalyzed ethylene carbonate (EC) reduction, producing gaseous C2H4 and Si–C–O surface fragments. These interfacial reactions destabilize the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI), promoting continuous Si loss and chemical heterogeneity. Together, these findings establish a direct atomistic link between fast charging, interfacial decomposition, and mechanical failure in Si-based anodes. The proposed chemical potential switching framework offers a powerful and generalizable approach for exploring charge–discharge phenomena in alloying and intercalation materials, guiding the rational design of more stable anodes and electrolyte systems for next-generation high-energy batteries.
