The Role of Deep Cerebral Tracts in Predicting Postoperative Aphasia: An nTMS‐Based Investigation of the Corticothalamic Fibers
Authors: Zixu Bao, Haosu Zhang, Maximilian Schwendner, Axel Schröder, Bernhard Meyer, Sandro M. Krieg, Sebastian Ille
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPostoperative aphasia (POA) is a common complication in patients undergoing surgery for language‐eloquent lesions. This study aimed to enhance the prediction of POA by leveraging preoperative navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation (nTMS) language mapping and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)‐based tractography, incorporating deep learning (DL) algorithms. One hundred patients with left‐hemispheric lesions were retrospectively enrolled (43 developed postoperative aphasia, as the POA group; 57 did not, as the non‐aphasia (NA) group). Fiber tracking was performed at fractional anisotropy threshold (FAthres) of 0.10 and 0.15, analyzing the total fiber volume (Vfibertotal), average fractional anisotropy (FAwhole), and number of visualized tracts. Additionally, the visualization ratio (VR) and FA‐sensitive visualization were assessed for individual tractography. The NA group demonstrated significantly higher Vfibertotal (FAthres = 0.10: 61.1 vs. 51.7 cm3, p = 0.029; FAthres = 0.15: 36.9 vs. 29.6 cm3, p = 0.008), higher FAwhole (FAthres = 0.10: 0.38 vs. 0.35, p = 0.006; FAthres = 0.15: 0.42 vs. 0.39, p = 0.006), and greater tract numbers (FAthres = 0.10: 6.1 vs. 5.7, p = 0.111; FAthres = 0.15: 5.6 vs. 4.8, p = 0.004). Among individual fiber tracts, the corticothalamic fibers (CtF) showed significantly higher VR in the NA group (86.0% vs. 58.1%, p = 0.003), whereas FA‐sensitive visualization of CtF was higher in the POA group (11.6% vs. 0.0%, p = 0.013). A binary DL model developed to predict POA achieved a sensitivity of 72.3% and specificity of 85.3%, with an area underthecurve (AUC) of 0.82. Our findings demonstrate the potential of nTMS‐based tractography to predict POA by integrating DL. The CtF showed the most significant potential in predicting aphasia risk and understanding the complexity of the language network, whereas their individual predictive contribution within the model remained limited.

Introduction

The primary focus in the surgical treatment of cerebral lesions is maximal resection while preserving neurological function (Eyüpoglu et al. 2013; Stummer and Kamp 2009). This requires comprehensive preoperative assessments and precise localization of eloquent brain functions to balance functional preservation and extent of resection (Duffau and Mandonnet 2013; Hervey‐Jumper and Berger 2016).

As an advanced and complex brain function, language function is particularly vulnerable. It can be impaired to a certain extent even when lesions are not considered language‐eloquent according to conventional anatomical understanding (Chang, Raygor, and Berger 2015). Navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation (nTMS) allows for a non‐invasive assessment of language function before surgery (Sollmann, Zhang, Fratini, et al. 2020). Combining nTMS language mapping with diffusion tensor imaging fiber tracking (DTI FT) enables the combined visualization of cortical and subcortical structures, providing valuable insights for surgical planning and reducing postoperative functional deficits (Zhang et al. 2020). Previous studies have demonstrated that nTMS exhibits high sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV) in identifying cortical language regions, as well as substantial overlap with intraoperative direct cortical stimulation and high positive predictive value (PPV) in mapping network centralities (Picht et al. 2013; Ille et al. 2024). Despite the application of advanced preoperative functional diagnostics, some patients still show surgery‐related language impairments after surgery, referred to as postoperative aphasia (POA) (Nass et al. 2000; Wilson et al. 2015; Andrews et al. 2023).

Recently, structural network analysis has facilitated our understanding of language function (Zhang et al. 2022). For patients with left‐hemispheric lesions, studies have highlighted several tracts to be associated with language performance, including the inferior fronto‐occipital fasciculus (IFOF), the frontal aslant tract (FAT), the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) and the arcuate fasciculus (AF) (Sollmann, Zhang, Schramm, et al. 2020). However, the contribution of individual fiber tracts to the language network is dynamic and underlies neuroplastic processes. In addition to fiber tracts considered components of the classic dual‐stream language model, additional fibers can also contribute to language performance (Dick et al. 2014). For instance, commissural fibers (CF) facilitate connections between brain regions across hemispheres, thereby mediating plasticity and reorganization of language function in patients with language‐eloquent tumors. The cortico‐nuclear tract (CNT), connecting the motor cortex and the brainstem, is suggested to contribute to language production and speech motor functions (Ohlerth et al. 2021). Fibers reaching from the thalamus to the anterior temporal and lateral frontal regions linked by the cortico‐thalamic fibers (CtF) are thought to be involved in lexical and semantic processing (Nishio et al. 2014). Additionally, previous research has mostly focused on changes in different language‐related tracts in patients with language‐eloquent tumors based on a single fractional anisotropy threshold (FAthres), lacking systematic comparisons across different thresholds to assess how varying FAthres influence the visualization of specific tracts (Ille et al. 2018; Sollmann et al. 2016). As the FAthres increases, both the volume and density of visualized tracts decrease, resulting in fewer tracts being visualized. This reduction may lead to the absence of certain tracts, suggesting their vulnerable characteristics (Sollmann, Zhang, Fratini, et al. 2020). Such visualization change induced by FAthres, however, is also influenced by the inherent tradeoff between specificity and sensitivity in current deterministic tractography: higher FA thresholds reduce false‐positive streamlines but may omit anatomically valid fibers, whereas lower thresholds improve sensitivity at the cost of including unplausible fibers (Schilling, Nath, et al. 2019; Schilling, Daducci, et al. 2019). To account for this, we selected two representative FA thresholds (0.10 and 0.15) that provide a stable and clinically applicable range, balancing anatomical validity the while enabling the detection of clinically relevant changes (Negwer et al. 2017).

Furthermore, DTI‐based tractography using a deterministic algorithm is increasingly being replaced in research fields by more advanced and accurate reconstruction methods, such as constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) models, which were suggested to better identify crossing fibers (Tahedl et al. 2025). However, deterministic DTI‐based fiber tracking remains routinely used in neurosurgical planning due to its integration into clinical platforms, compatibility with nTMS‐based mapping, and robust reproducibility (Sollmann et al. 2018, 2019). In our previous research, a machine learning model was constructed using structural connectome features derived from nTMS‐based tractography, aiming to improve the accuracy of predicting neurological function prognosis (Zhang et al. 2023; Ille et al. 2022). However, fully automated deep learning (DL) models have notable limitations, especially when depending only on imaging data. For subcortical structures, factors like tumor compression and edema can disrupt white matter tract visualization, potentially reducing the accuracy of these models. Manually corrected and analyzed fiber tracking (FT) data has not been routinely applied in DL models before. In this study, instead of using raw FT data, we incorporated the identification of individual fiber tracts by physicians and several tract‐related parameters to train the neural network model to minimize systematic errors and improve the model's performance.

The present study aimed to enhance the prediction of POA in patients with language‐eloquent lesions by employing DL algorithms centered on preoperative nTMS‐based tractography analysis for the general properties of the language network and specific language‐related fiber tracts.

Materials and Methods

Study Hypothesis

The current study investigated the hypotheses that (1) the properties of language networks obtained from nTMS‐based tractography differ between patients with and without POA; (2) the visualization of different language‐related tracts varies between the NA and POA groups; (3) a DL model can be trained to help predict the postoperative language disorders.

Ethics

This study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Technical University of Munich (registration numbers: 222/14 and 192/18). All research was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from patients.

Patient Inclusion Criteria

This post hoc study analyzed patients undergoing preoperative nTMS language mapping and nTMS‐based FT in our department (Figure 1). German‐speaking patients aged over 18 years diagnosed with left‐hemispheric perisylvian lesions impacting language‐related cortical or subcortical structures were included in this study. Exclusion criteria included further neurological or psychiatric diseases and contradictions for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and nTMS, such as cardiac pacemakers. The Edinburgh Handedness Inventory was applied for handedness (Oldfield 1971). Patients were retrospectively selected based on data integrity from 2018 to 2022, including complete nTMS data, preoperative MRI, and postoperative follow‐up records. To ensure outcome reliability, only patients with documented language assessments at approximately 1 month after surgery were included. This time point was used to distinguish postoperative aphasia (POA) from transient perioperative language disturbances. The study group contained 43 patients who developed postoperative aphasia persisting for more than 1 month, classified as the POA group. To support the development of a robust deep learning model, 57 patients without any postoperative aphasia were randomly selected from our institutional database using a uniform sampling method without replacement and without any manual filtering or stratification. These individuals met the same inclusion criteria as the POA group. The resulting dataset of 100 patients was determined based on prior experience in deep learning model development at our center to ensure sufficient convergence and feature stability while maintaining a reasonable POA‐to‐NA ratio for model generalizability.

MRI Imaging Data

Preoperatively, MRI (Ingenia 3T, Philips) was performed, including a diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) sequence with 32 optimized gradient directions uniformly distributed over the sphere (repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) 5000/78 ms, voxel‐size 2 × 2 × 2 mm3, b‐value 1000 s/mm2) and T1‐weighted images with contrast agent (TR/TE 9/4 ms, 1 mm3 isotropic voxel size).

nTMS Language Mapping

Preoperative nTMS language mapping was performed using the Nexstim eXimia NBS system (version 5.1.1; Nexstim, Finland) according to our clinical routine (Ille et al. 2015a, 2015b, 2021). A head tracker was attached to the patient's forehead to enable navigation. For anatomical coregistration between the patient's scalp and enhanced T1‐weighted images, the nasion and the crus of the helix of both ears were used as predefined anatomical landmarks. This process was followed by software‐guided registration of nine scalp‐surface regions. According to the manufacturer's specifications, the software permits a maximum stereotactic error of up to 5 mm. However, when following this coregistration procedure, the typical tool‐tracking accuracy is better than 1.5 mm, and the overall coregistration accuracy is within 2 mm. This level of accuracy is generally considered sufficient for language‐eloquent cortex mapping in neurosurgical planning (Krieg 2017).

According to the cortical parcellation system (CPS) originally proposed by Corina et al. (Corina et al. 2005, 2010), the dominant hemisphere was divided into 21 anatomically and functionally defined language‐related regions, encompassing a total of 46 predefined cortical stimulation targets. These target sites were subsequently stimulated using repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) at 110% of the individual resting motor threshold, delivering 5 pulses at a frequency of 1 Hz (Sollmann et al. 2014; Krieg et al. 2016).

The stimulation was performed using an object naming (ON) task in German. Two baseline sessions without stimulation were conducted prior to mapping to assess and confirm task performance. During the actual mapping, each of the 46 cortical targets was stimulated three times using rTMS synchronized with image presentation. Task performance was recorded via audio and video, and subsequently analyzed post hoc using the Nexstim NexSpeech Analyzer software (version 2.0.1; Nexstim, Helsinki, Finland). Naming performance under stimulation was compared to each participant's baseline performance. Naming errors were identified and classified into four categories based on established protocols: (1) performance errors, (2) semantic errors, (3) no responses, and (4) hesitations (Lioumis et al. 2012; Krieg et al. 2017). To minimize subjective bias, all mapping results were independently reviewed by two experienced neurosurgeons, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Stimulated sites associated with any error types were defined as language‐positive sites and exported in digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format at three depths (15, 20 and 25 mm to the scalp). These sites were then used as seed points for subsequent tractography.

nTMS‐Based Tractography

Tractography was performed based on a deterministic tracking algorithm in the Brainlab Elements platform (version 3.1.0; Brainlab AG, Germany), using preoperative DTI data and nTMS language mapping. Prior to tract reconstruction, diffusion MRI data were preprocessed within the Brainlab Elements Distortion Correction module (version 3.1.0; Brainlab AG, Germany), including Eddy current and head motion correction, as part of the standard processing workflow (Tahedl et al. 2025; Sollmann et al. 2017). To ensure accurate spatial alignment across modalities, contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted images were automatically fused with the diffusion images using the Elements Image Fusion module (version 3.1.0; Brainlab AG, Germany), which applied a mutual information algorithm correction. Additionally, nTMS‐derived language‐positive stimulation sites were registered to the anatomical space using cranial landmarks‐based calibration. As a result, the nTMS stimulation sites and diffusion data were coregistrated in the same spatial space as the structural image for subsequent tractography.

All language‐positive stimulation sites were each expanded to a radius of 5 mm and served as a single seed region for deterministic fiber tracking following the all‐spots strategy. Because the number and distribution of language‐positive sites varied across patients, the resulting seed regions were individual for each patient. All tractography was performed using a minimum fiber length (FL) of 100 mm and an FAthres of 0.10 and 0.15, with default angular thresholds and step sizes as per manufacturer recommendations (Brainlab Elements, version 3.1.0, Brainlab AG, Germany).

Subsequently, the individual language‐related tracts, consisting of the AF, CF, CNT, CtF, SLF, inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), and IFOF were identified in each patient (Kuhnt et al. 2012; Axer et al. 2013; Gierhan 2013). All tractography results were independently reviewed by two experienced neurosurgeons, all of whom were blinded to the patients' clinical outcomes to ensure unbiased assessment. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion.

The brain and tumor volume were segmented using the Elements platform and its integrated SmartBrush tool (version 3.1.0; Brainlab AG, Germany). The volume of the left brain was calculated without the tumor region. The visualization ratio (VR) of each tract was analyzed (Zhang et al. 2022; Ille et al. 2022). Additionally, the total number of visualized tracts (n vis), the total fiber volume (Vfibertotal), and the average fractional anisotropy (FAwhole) of the entire network for each FAthres setting were analyzed. Fiber tracts only visible at an FAthres of 0.1 and not at an FAthres of 0.15 were defined as FA‐sensitive tracts, suggesting their sensitivity to the FA threshold. Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses between the NA and POA groups were performed with GraphPad‐Prism (Version 8.4.3). Patient‐ and lesion‐related parameters were compared using Fisher's exact test and independent t‐test. Chi‐square test was employed to compare the cortical distribution of language‐positive sites. Tractography properties were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test since the Shapiro–Wilk normality test indicated a non‐normal distribution. The comparison of VR and RatioFAsens of individual language‐related tracts between the two groups was conducted by Fisher's exact test.

Deep Learning Model

The above‐collected data was loaded and processed using the Pandas Library (Version 2.2.2, https://pandas.pydata.org), which is a Python‐based data analysis tool that facilitates efficient data importation and preparation for further analysis. Features of tractography properties (n vis, Vfibertotal, FAwhole, Vfiberavg, and Fiber‐Brain‐Ratio) and individual tract properties (VR and RatioFAsens) were directly imported after data processing. A feature selection was performed, using correlation analysis to retain features with an absolute correlation value above 0.2 with the target variable (“POA”), and a Spearman correlation test to retain features with a p value under 0.05.

For each fold of cross‐validation, the dataset was randomly split into training (80%) and testing (20%) datasets. Standardization was performed using StandardScaler (Version 1.5.2, https://scikit‐learn.org), where the scaler was fitted on the training set and then applied to the testing set, ensuring unbiased model evaluation and preventing data leakage. To stabilize training, accelerate convergence, and potentially enhance model generalizability, batch normalization (BN) layers were integrated into the neural network architecture. By reducing internal covariate shift, BN can improve training efficiency and indirectly mitigate overfitting.

A deep neural network (DNN) was constructed using Keras Sequential API (Version 3, https://keras.io/) in Python (Version 3.8, https://www.python.org/) with TensorFlow backend (Version 2.16.1, https://www.tensorflow.org/), consisting of an input layer, two hidden layers with the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, dropout layers to reduce overfitting, and a sigmoid‐activated output layer for binary classification (Figure 4A). The model was compiled with the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer (https://www.tensorflow.org/python/tf/keras/optimizers/Adam) and binary cross‐entropy loss function and evaluated on sensitivity and specificity (Kingma and Ba 2014). It was trained over 50 epochs with a batch size of 100, using multiple random splits for robustness and averaging performance metrics across five iterations. To further interpret predictions, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed using GradientExplainer (Version 0.45.0, https://shap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/shap.GradientExplainer.html#shap.GradientExplainer), a tool that provides insights into the algorithms feature importance (Lundberg et al. 2020). Additionally, a SHAP summary plot to visualize the impact of each feature was created.

Results

Patient‐Related Data and Language Mapping Characteristics

There were no significant differences between the two groups regarding gender and handedness (Table 1). Patients were aged 57.6 ± 17.1 years in the NA group and 58.9 ± 14.2 years in the POA group (p = 0.693). Pathological diagnosis showed high‐grade glioma (HGG) in 43 (43%) cases (21 in the NA group and 22 in the POA group), low‐grade glioma (LGG) in 32 (32%) cases (17 in the NA group and 15 in the POA group), and other lesion types (including metastasis and cavernoma) in 25 (25%) cases (19 in the NA group and 6 in the POA group) (Table 1). The distribution of lesions in the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes between the two groups revealed no significant differences (p = 0.999). The lesion size was 22.9 ± 29.4 cm3 in the NA group and 16.5 ± 19.3 cm3 in the POA group (p = 0.222).

Analysis of General Properties in the Language Network

When the FAthres was set to 0.1, there was no significant difference in the n vis between the two groups (6.1 vs. 5.7; p = 0.111). However, when the threshold was increased to 0.15, n vis was higher in the NA group (5.6 vs. 4.8; p = 0.004) (Table 2, Figure 3A). The Vfibertotal of the visualized language network in the NA group was significantly higher than that in the POA group for both FA thresholds (FAthres = 0.1: 61.08 vs. 51.74 cm3, p = 0.029; FAthres = 0.15: 36.88 vs. 29.57 cm3, p = 0.008) (Table 2, Figure 3B). Likewise, the average FAwhole also showed a significant increase in the NA group compared to the POA group at both thresholds (FAthres = 0.10: 0.38 vs. 0.35, p = 0.006; FAthres = 0.15: 0.42 vs. 0.39, p = 0.006) (Table 2, Figure 3C). In Vfiberavg, a significant difference was only observed at a threshold of 0.15 (FAthres = 0.10: 10.07 vs. 9.02 cm3, p = 0.073; FAthres = 0.15: 7.30 vs. 5.77 cm3, p = 0.024) (Table 2, Figure 3D). Furthermore, the NA group demonstrated a higher Fiber‐Brain Ratio at both FA thresholds (FAthres = 0.1: 6.69 vs. 5.58, p = 0.013; FAthres = 015: 4.02 vs. 3.17, p = 0.004) (Table 2, Figure 3E).

Visualization Ratio (VR) and Ratio of FA‐Sensitive Tract (RatioFAsens ) of Individual Language‐Related Fiber Tracts

Comparing the visualization ratio for the two groups, only the VR of the CtF with an FAthres of 0.15 was significantly higher in the NA group compared to the POA group (86.0% vs. 58.1%, p = 0.003) (Table 3). In comparison, the analysis of RatioFAsens for each language‐related fiber tract revealed a significantly higher ratio of FA‐sensitive visualization of CtF in the POA group (11.6% vs. 0.0%, p = 0.013) (Table 3).

Analysis of Deep Learning Model

The trained DL model was conceived using an 80:20 split, with 80% of the patients used for training and 20% for testing, achieving a sensitivity of 72.3%, specificity of 85.3%, Positive Predictive Value (PPV) of 82.8%, and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) of 75.2%. The corresponding area under the curve (AUC) of the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was 0.82 (Figure 4B). The detailed cross‐validation results are summarized in Table S3. Among all the language network features in this model, the identification and evaluation of CtF at an FAthres of 0.15 contributed most significantly to the model's prediction accuracy with the highest SHAP value of 0.05 (Figure 4C).

Discussion

This research investigated the general properties of preoperative nTMS‐based language networks and analyzed the features of individual language‐related fiber tracts in patients with and without POA. In general, we clarified that the properties of language networks in the NA group were superior compared to the POA group, indicating that those patients had a more intact and solid language network before surgery. The individual fiber tract analysis suggested a significant difference in the CtF feature between the NA and POA groups. Furthermore, a DL model was constructed using metrics derived from preoperative nTMS‐based tractography, achieving satisfactory performance in predicting the risk of POA, with a sensitivity of 72.3%, specificity of 85.3%, PPV of 82.8%, NPV of 75.2%, and an AUC of 0.82.

The Difference of General Properties in Language Network

The n vis, volume, and FAwhole of the visualized language network can reflect the general characteristics of the language network in patients with left‐hemispheric lesions. In general, patients with POA exhibited lower Vfibertotal and FAwhole of the language network at both FA thresholds (FAthres = 0.10: p = 0.029 and 0.006, FAthres = 0.15: p = 0.008 and 0.006, respectively). The Fiber‐Brain ratio, a parameter indicating the relative volume of the language network, was also significantly reduced in the POA group at both thresholds.

These significant reductions in nTMS‐based tractography parameters may be attributed to preexisting impairments in the language network before surgery. For instance, factors such as tumor‐induced edema and direct infiltration can disrupt the integrity of white matter tracts within language networks, leading to a decrease in average FA, reduced density of fiber bundles, and lower tract volumes (Jellison et al. 2004a; Henderson et al. 2020; Henderson Jr. et al. 2021). These factors could explain the reduced network volumes and decreased FA observed in POA patients. However, the n vis and the Vfiberavg were significantly lower only at the FAthres of 0.15 in the POA group since lower FA thresholds tend to generate redundant fibers, resulting in abnormally higher tract volumes and tract numbers (Negwer et al. 2017).

Previous studies have demonstrated that a decreased average FA in complex networks is an indicator of impaired performance. For example, a decreased average FA in global white matter is a superior predictor of cognitive impairment in patients with white matter hyperintensities (Xing et al. 2021). Similarly, a reduced average FA in language‐supporting neural circuits is highly associated with stuttering symptoms, indicating possible structural connectivity deficits (Chang, Zhu, et al. 2015). Moreover, a higher preoperative FA of the AF is a useful predictor of better postoperative language recovery in brain tumor patients (Kinoshita et al. 2014). Additionally, both the Vfibertotal and the Fiber‐Brain Ratio were shown to be associated with lexical processing level, indicating the robustness of functional regions (Pliatsikas et al. 2014; Ehling et al. 2019; Pasquini et al. 2022).

The findings suggest that patients who do not experience postoperative aphasia likely have a more resilient and stable preoperative language network. In contrast, patients with lower average fractional anisotropy (FA), reduced network volume, and a lower Fiber‐Brain Ratio in their language networks may face a heightened risk of developing postoperative language deficits. Thus, the general properties of language networks derived from nTMS‐based tractography could be a potential predicting factor to evaluate the risk of POA.

Comparison of Different Subcortical Language‐Related Tracts

Language‐related fiber tracts, including the AF, CF, CNT, CtF, SLF, IFL, and IFOF, were manually identified based on DTI FT. (Axer et al. 2013; Gierhan 2013) Only the VR of the CtF was significantly lower in the POA group only for a FAthres of 0.15 (p = 0.003), suggesting that the CtF is significantly less likely to be visualized and identified in nTMS‐based tractography for patients with postoperative language deficits. Additionally, the RatioFAsens of the CtF was significantly higher in the POA group when the FAthres increased (p = 0.013), indicating that the CtF is highly sensitive to threshold changes. Notably, the VR of AF and SLF in patients with POA also showed a trend toward reduction compared to the NA group (p = 0.129 and 0.156). The RatioFAsens of the SLF showed a similar trend as the CtF in the POA group (p = 0.136). However, those differences were not statistically significant. The VR represents the percentage of patients whose fiber tracts had average FA values exceeding the selected threshold, whereas the RatioFAsens refers to the percentage of patients with fiber tracts having average FA values between both thresholds (0.10 and 0.15 in this study). In impaired networks, FA values for white matter tracts may show different alteration trends depending on various pathological conditions (Henderson et al. 2020). For instance, under tumor‐related effects, such as vasogenic edema and glioma infiltration, typically reduce FA, whereas bulk displacement of white matter tracts may not significantly alter absolute FA values (Jellison et al. 2004b). Conversely, in neurodegeneration conditions like Alzheimer's disease, selective degeneration of crossing fibers may paradoxically lead to increased FA in certain regions due to loss of perpendicular diffusion components or fiber microstructural reorganization (Pierpaoli et al. 1996; Acosta‐Cabronero et al. 2010). Therefore, direct interpretation of FA values should be applied cautiously, as changes may reflect a combination of structural damage, reorganization, and fiber architectural complexity. However, in this study, FA was not directly used as an absolute comparative measure. Instead, we employed the VR and RatioFAsens, which indirectly reflect tract visibility under different FAthres and allow comparisons of tract integrity across patient groups without relying solely on raw FA values. Compared to the NA group, the CtF in the POA group exhibited significantly lower VR and higher RatioFAsens, highlighting the most pronounced difference of CtF both between the two groups and across visualization‐required FA thresholds. Indeed, this indicated that the CtF is the most vulnerable to tumor‐induced impairments, and its individual FA value has the potential to be a key indicator for postoperative language function.

The Potential Role of CtF in Language Plasticity

Most studies describing the relationship between subcortical structures and language function focused on tracts such as AF and ILF that are included in the hypothesized dual‐stream model (Nieberlein et al. 2023; Caverzasi et al. 2016; Tuncer et al. 2021), aiming to investigate the structural basis of functional plasticity. Notably, the role of CtF in language processing networks has also been identified, although it is not included in the classic language model (Chang, Raygor, and Berger 2015). Its origin, the thalamus, is considered a central monitor in coordinating language‐specific cortical activities and interacts with the basal ganglia in both perceptual and productive linguistic operations (Civier et al. 2013). Furthermore, the CtF is involved in spatiotemporal gating for the information exchange between basal ganglia and cortical areas for functional maintenance (Klostermann et al. 2013). Functional neuroimaging studies also suggested that various thalamic and cortical regions were corporately involved in several linguistic tasks, including object naming and lexical decision (Llano 2013). A recent study by Maldonado et al. using subcortical stimulation during awake surgery, diffusion MRI, virtual dissection, and post‐mortem fiber dissection, identified four key fiber components connecting the thalamic pulvinar and temporal regions: the anterior component (Arnold's bundle), the optic radiations‐like component, the lateral component, and the auditory radiations‐like component (Maldonado et al. 2024). Notably, the functional relevance of CtF may extend beyond direct information transfer and involve higher‐order mechanisms in language support. According to a recurrent circuit model proposed by Crosson et al. cortico‐thalamo‐cortical circuitry is essential for maintaining stable semantic representations during language processing, enabling iterative interactions between lexical and semantic systems (Crosson 2021). This mechanism supports word retrieval, auditory‐verbal comprehension, and semantic feedback through dynamic interplay between cortical and thalamic regions. Therefore, the observed reduction in CtF integrity in POA patients may not only reflect structural disruption but also indicate impaired thalamic regulation within the broader language network. Although CtF showed relatively low SHAP values in predicting postoperative language status, its mediating role may be critical under linguistically demanding conditions. Although current surgical strategies have prioritized the preservation of major deep white matter tracts within the dual‐stream model, it may be equally important to explicitly assess the involvement of cortico‐thalamic pathways during surgical planning. In particular, the pre‐ and intraoperative identification of these circuits may impact surgical approaches for deep frontal or temporo‐thalamic tumors (Duffau 2014, 2015).

Overall, our findings, together with previous studies, support the notion that CtF is structurally and functionally connected to language‐related regions and contributes to the reorganization of speech function (Dick et al. 2014; Civier et al. 2013; Argyropoulos et al. 2013).

Prediction of POA Based on DL Model

Classical statistical analysis methods are limited in comprehensively analyzing potential relationships between various variables. Therefore, it is essential to introduce new computational technologies in medical analysis to aid in this process. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of applying DL models to elucidate language processing mechanisms and predict prognosis through complex functional and structural connectivity (Zhang et al. 2023; Ille et al. 2022). Moreover, the role of medical professionals in preprocessing the data is currently essential to provide accurate and clinically relevant data before applying DL models (Chukwujindu et al. 2024). This approach helps to avoid inexplicable results, thereby enhancing the feasibility of clinical applications.

One of the main aims of this study was to explore an effective combination of nTMS‐based tractography and advanced computational techniques to improve the prediction of postoperative functional changes. In this study, PyTorch (version 1.8.0, https://pytorch.org/, Python 3.8) was used to establish a new DL model using clinical and network properties to predict the risk of POA. The feature attribution method using SHAP analysis indicated that the identification of the CtF in nTMS‐based tractography at an FAthres of 0.15 had the most significant impact on the prediction efficiency, highlighting its potential for predicting functional outcomes in patients with language eloquent lesions. To further evaluate whether cortical mapping data may confound model predictions, we specifically analyzed the characteristics of nTMS‐derived language‐positive sites at the cortical level. Although cortical stimulation consistently shows robust negative predictive value, previous studies have highlighted its limited specificity and high false‐positive rate, potentially due to trans‐synaptic signal propagation and oversensitive identification of subtle speech errors (Jeltema et al. 2021; Muir et al. 2022). Moreover, several stroke studies have indicated that subcortical disconnections, rather than cortical damage alone, are more critical for predicting long‐term language outcomes (Billot et al. 2022; Salvalaggio et al. 2020). Consistent with these findings, our data revealed no significant differences between the NA and POA groups in terms of the number of positive stimulation sites, the number of positive cortical regions, or their spatial distribution. Furthermore, when these mapping‐related features were tested as input variables during the DL model development, they failed to pass the correlation‐based feature selection step. Even when manually included, cortical mapping features neither enhanced predictive performance nor meaningfully contributed to feature importance. Collectively, these findings support that the cortical characteristics of nTMS language mapping alone should be further investigated for identifying its relationship with POA. However, given the relatively small dataset and the unbalanced characteristic in histopathological findings, the risk of overfitting should be acknowledged, as it can lead to poor generalization, unreliable predictions, and reduced clinical applicability of the model. To mitigate this, we applied batch normalization, dropout regularization, and k‐fold cross‐validation during model training. Batch normalization stabilizes learning by normalizing layer inputs, reducing internal covariate shift, and accelerating model convergence (Ioffe and Szegedy 2015). Dropout regularization randomly removes a subset of neurons during each training iteration, effectively reducing neuron co‐dependency and preventing complex co‐adaptations on training data (Srivastava et al. 2014). K‐fold cross‐validation systematically partitions data into multiple subsets to ensure that the model's performance generalizes beyond the training set, thus improving robustness against overfitting (Lopez‐Del Rio et al. 2019).

In addition to the strategies implemented in this study, there are other well‐established methods, such as early stopping, weight decay, and data augmentation, that have also shown efficacy in reducing overfitting in deep learning models. These techniques operate through mechanisms like halting training based on validation performance, penalizing large weights, or expanding dataset diversity through synthetic variation (Krogh and Hertz 1991; Zhang et al. 2021; Shorten and Khoshgoftaar 2019). Although not applied in the current analysis, such methods still have high potential and could be considered in future studies with similar modeling frameworks and data characteristics.

Furthermore, to promote clinical translation, future studies may benefit from developing standardized workflows for applying deep learning models in neurosurgical practices. Such frameworks may include consistent criteria for image acquisition (e.g., imaging modalities, data preprocessing, and tractography parameters), preprocessing pipelines, input feature definitions, and model validation approaches. It could promote wider adoption and improve cross‐institutional applicability of predictive DL models for preoperative planning.

Methodological Considerations in Tractography

In the present study, the clinically approved platform (Brainlab Elements) was employed to perform deterministic tractography based on diffusion tensor imaging data, primarily due to its compatibility with navigated TMS data, seamless integration into neurosurgical workflows, and visualization. Although other widely used open‐source toolboxes (e.g., MRtrix3, FSL) may enhance methodological transparency and improve comparability with the broader research literature, the use of this mature clinical platform was prioritized to ensure clinical feasibility and interpretation (Sollmann et al. 2018, 2019).

DTI‐based deterministic tractography enables clear and consistent visualization of major white matter tracts using clinically feasible data and offers straightforward interpretability for surgeons. However, a well‐known limitation of DTI‐based fiber tracking is its inability to adequately resolve complex fiber orientations such as crossing fibers, thus potentially underestimating the true anatomical complexity and connectivity (Schilling, Nath, et al. 2019; Maier‐Hein et al. 2017). Furthermore, the sensitivity–specificity tradeoff is still an inherent issue in deterministic DTI‐based fiber tracking, since higher FA thresholds may improve specificity but risk ignoring anatomically valid fibers (false negatives), whereas lower thresholds can increase sensitivity at the expense of introducing unrealistic streamlines (false positives) (Schilling, Daducci, et al. 2019; Farquharson et al. 2013).

To mitigate these limitations, constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) has been employed as a more advanced reconstruction method, which enables tracking multiple fiber orientations per voxel, thus significantly improving anatomical accuracy in regions with complex fiber architectures (Tournier et al. 2007). When combined with deterministic algorithms, CSD typically yields a more accurate reconstruction of crossing fibers compared to DTI‐based fiber tracking (Dell'Acqua and Tournier 2019). Probabilistic tractography, which can also be applied to CSD models, further addresses these limitations by assigning uncertainty measures to fiber orientations, producing probabilistic streamlines reflecting the inherent uncertainty in diffusion measurements, especially in the reconstruction of the corticospinal tract (Radwan et al. 2024).

Despite the promising technical advantages of CSD model‐based tractography, whether using either deterministic or probabilistic algorithms, several practical and methodological challenges still limit their widespread clinical adoption. Firstly, CSD models typically require high‐quality, multi‐shell, high‐angular‐resolution diffusion imaging (HARDI) data, involving significantly longer acquisition times and advanced scanner capabilities, making them less feasible in routine clinical practice (Jeurissen et al. 2014). Secondly, the complexity of the data processing pipeline required for CSD‐based methods necessitates specialized expertise and computational resources, creating extra barriers to clinical integration (Tahedl et al. 2025). Additionally, while previous studies have demonstrated encouraging results for CSD‐based tractography of several major language‐related tracts under optimal imaging conditions, its performance still varies across structures and subjects compared to standard DTI models, limiting confidence in its clinical reproducibility (Zhao et al. 2016; Boukadi et al. 2018).

Given these considerations, deterministic DTI‐based tractography was selected for the current study as a clinically pragmatic and interpretable approach. This decision allowed us to align with routine neurosurgical protocols and ensure reproducibility while focusing on tract‐level differences related to postoperative language status for patients with language‐eloquent tumors. Future studies involving optimized acquisition protocols and systematic validation of CSD‐based methods are still essential to refine the clinical utility of tractography.

Limitation

One primary methodological limitation of this study is the use of a commercially available tool (Brainlab Elements), which is well‐established in clinical routine, yet may reduce methodological transparency and comparability with other studies using open‐source tools, which should be addressed in the future.

In addition to this technical limitation, several dataset‐related factors should also be acknowledged. Firstly, although all patients underwent basic language evaluation adopted from the Aachener Aphasia Test to confirm sufficient baseline function for nTMS language mapping, no detailed and complete standardized and quantitative language assessments were performed prior to surgery. Besides, the preoperative nTMS‐based tractography for each language‐related tract was not conducted individually; all the properties were derived from the entire language network, lacking specific tract‐level analysis. Secondly, the current DL model did not explicitly incorporate tumor‐related histopathological features such as glioma grade, extent of peritumoral edema, or infiltration. Although edema was qualitatively considered during lesion segmentation, these variables were not modeled as separate predictors due to the limited sample size and the potential confounding effects of non‐glioma pathologies. Thirdly, nTMS mapping and tractography were performed only on the tumor‐affected left hemisphere, reflecting standard preoperative workflows. However, considering the language lateralization and the presence of left‐handed patients, future studies should consider systematic bilateral mapping and hemisphere‐specific tractography to improve prediction across handedness groups and provide additional insights into compensatory mechanisms (Gatto et al. 2024). Fourthly, the study did not include postoperative nTMS‐based fiber tracking data, meaning the postoperative functional organization and its relevance to individual tracts and DL features were not investigated. Additionally, categorizing postoperative language function into binary outcomes (with or without surgery‐related aphasia) ignored the variations in aphasia types and severity. Finally, despite the significant contribution of the CtF on the DL model's output, the actual SHAP value of this feature was relatively low, suggesting that the language function network is too intricate and variable to be predicted solely based on a single factor or a limited set of network properties. Hence, the practicality and stability of this DL model in clinical works still require more research to verify. Further studies should incorporate individual fiber tract analysis, standardized pre‐ and postoperative language behavioral assessments, and stratification by tumor characteristics. Integration of structural and histopathological variables, such as glioma grade, edema volume, and white matter infiltration, may further enhance the robustness and clinical interpretability of DL‐based predictive models for postoperative aphasia.

Conclusion

In this study, we observed inferior general properties of the entire nTMS‐based tractography in the POA group, indicating an impaired language network before surgery. The corticothalamic fibers in the POA group exhibited a more unstable and impaired condition, highlighting their vulnerability in patients with postoperative language deficits. Furthermore, combining network properties with artificial intelligence allowed more detailed insights. A DL model identified the properties of CtF as an indicator to predict the risk of postoperative language deficits in patients with language‐eloquent lesions.
