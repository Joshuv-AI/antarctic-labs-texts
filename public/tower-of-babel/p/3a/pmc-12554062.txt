Estimating the health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy in the United States: a population-based analysis
Authors: Steffany Vucetich, Kelly DeToy, Shilah F Waters, Nicole La Ruta, Yeonsoo Baik, Carolina Cardona, Carolina Duque, Robert H Gilman, Bryan N Patenaude
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundChagas disease (ChD) presents a growing concern in the United States (US), stemming largely from Chagas cardiomyopathy, a severe complication linked to heart failure, arrhythmias, and sudden death. This condition not only has health impacts in the form of morbidity and premature mortality but also carries significant economic burden related to its cost of care. This study aims to evaluate the current health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US using a health system perspective.MethodsWe estimated the number of adults infected with Trypanosoma cruzi in the US using 2018–2022 data from the American Community Survey Public Use Microdata Sample (ACS-PUMS) and prevalence estimates from prior literature. To assess the disease's broader impact, we incorporated proportions of hospitalized patients with concurrent cardiac conditions from prior literature and estimated healthcare costs using 2019 Healthcare Costs and Utilization Project, National Inpatient Sample (HCUP–NIS) data. The estimated economic burden was $4.96 billion (2019 USD) (95% CI: $4.91–$5.01), with 1.176 million DALYs (95% CI: 1.165–1.186). Research in context

Evidence before this study

Since its discovery over 100 years ago, Chagas disease (ChD) has remained in the spotlight due to its significant links to poverty and its devastating effects on health, economies, and societies. Most global public health efforts to address ChD have focused on reducing vector-borne transmission in Latin American endemic countries, resulting in decreased prevalence and incidence of acute cases. However, migration patterns and the persistence of chronic illness in adults, which increase years lived with disability, make it essential to assess the health and economic burden of Chagas disease's chronic cardiac effects in non-endemic countries such as the United States, where 30% of patients with Chagas develop cardiomyopathy. We searched Embase, Web of Science, and PubMed for English language research articles with search terms including “chagas disease”/“cardiomyopathies”/“prevalence”/“disability-adjusted life years”/“cost of illness”/“united states” as well as their synonyms with no year restrictions. Our search revealed a striking scarcity of studies on this topic in the US. The only study quantifying and evaluating the economic burden of ChD in the US considers all stages of the disease but does not differentiate the burden across immigrant populations focusing on cardiomyopathies or provide a detailed perspective from the US health system. Existing evidence highlights the need for enhanced attention to ChD in the US, including improved screening programs, expanded access to treatment, and greater research investment, as well as the need of updated estimates for prevalence and burden for this specific disease. While some estimates of Chagas cardiomyopathy prevalence exist, no studies quantify the cost of illness during severe cardiomyopathy stages while considering the distinct challenges and barriers of the US. Moreover, updated, population-based research is notably absent, particularly studies that contrast immigrant ChD prevalence with severe cardiomyopathy outcomes in the US context, which are critical to shaping effective public health responses and mitigating the disease's impact.

Added value of this study

As indicated by the current evidence, our study is the first to report estimates of the health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy on its severe stage among Latin American immigrants in the United States, highlighting its impact and comparing its burden with that of other diseases. To do so we first estimated the number of adults affected by ChD to generate population-level prevalence data by country. Using age-specific prevalence rates, we identified the subset of adults with Chagas cardiomyopathy. To account for the complexity of Chagas cardiomyopathy and its related cardiac complications, we incorporated the proportions of hospitalized patients with overlapping cardiac conditions. Age-specific distributions were further accounted to calculate associated healthcare costs and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for each condition by age group. These estimates were then aggregated to determine the total DALYs and total healthcare costs, reflecting the overall health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy. Furthermore, our study highlights the significant disparity between ChD's profound burden and the limited attention and funding it receives by evaluating the financial resources allocated to ChD research and advocacy.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings highlight the significant health and economic burden posed by Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US while revealing a stark disparity between the disease's impact and the resources allocated to address it. The historical lack of attention to ChD reflects decades-long systemic gaps in addressing its health and economic burden. If current patterns persist, the growing prevalence of Chagas cardiomyopathy among high-risk immigrant populations will significantly strain healthcare systems. The complex drivers of the current landscape of the disease require a multisectoral approach, fostering collaboration and partnerships between endemic and non-endemic countries which will be helpful to reducing gaps among vulnerable populations. Our results also stress the need for improved data collection and surveillance systems in high-income countries such us the US. Structural changes could include increased research funding for diagnostics and treatment. Moreover, improving access to antiparasitic and advanced cardiac care treatments will be critical. However, a strategy focusing on prevention, early detection, and public health infrastructure is essential to mitigate the impact of this neglected disease.

Introduction

Chagas disease (ChD) contributes to substantial morbidity and mortality across the Americas yet remains underprioritized in the United States (US) health care system. The disease, caused by a chronic parasitic infection with Trypanosoma cruzi, has several clinical presentations but most commonly manifests as cardiomyopathy in approximately 30% of infected individuals.1 Chagas cardiomyopathy usually presents decades after initial infection and is associated with arrhythmias, heart failure, sudden cardiac death, and other life-threatening or disabling conditions.2, 3, 4

Infection is primarily acquired through triatomine vectors infected with the parasite T. cruzi (T. Cruzi), which are endemic throughout Latin America. However, the pathogen can also spread congenitally, as well as through blood transfusions and organ transplants,5 allowing transmission cycles to persist across generations, even where the vector is absent. Although historically seen as a problem of low-income communities in South and Central America, ChD has recently garnered attention as a significant public health challenge in the US and other traditionally non-endemic countries of the Global North due to migration.6 Previous estimates suggest that approximately 300,000 individuals are living with Chagas disease (ChD) in the US. ChD is one of the most significant parasitic infections disproportionately affecting marginalized populations in the US, with 2.5% of individuals infected with any parasitic infection in the country living with ChD. Furthermore, it is the most prevalent parasitic disease among Hispanic or Latin American immigrants, with infection rates 1.78 to 7.32 times higher than cysticercosis.7 However, these figures remain speculative due to the absence of nationwide screening programs, limited awareness and knowledge of the disease, and active case surveillance.

Despite the limited data to estimate ChD prevalence, the economic burden caused by ChD is known to be considerable.8 In addition to the productivity lost from early mortality or long-term disability, the chronic nature of the disease and the difficulty of treatment contribute to accruing costs related to medications, hospitalizations, and interventions such as pacemaker implantations and cardiac transplantation. Previous studies have estimated the economic burden of ChD globally, but only a few have focused in the US.8,9 Nonetheless, these commonly fail to capture the full scope of the impact, as treatment approaches vary depending on the concurrent conditions associated with Chagas cardiomyopathy. Further, these studies often rely on estimates from countries outside of the US where data directly associated with ChD are more readily available. Therefore, the current economic and health burden in the US remains unclear.

Additionally, the funding levels and prioritization of ChD in the US relative to other similar diseases is speculated to be low but, to our knowledge, has not been thoroughly quantified. The toll of underfunding ChD in the US is significant, as it serves as a structural and systemic barrier that limits the development of better diagnostic tools and access to treatment, creating barriers to timely care. Marginalized populations, such as Latin American immigrants, often face unique challenges such as lack of healthcare access and awareness, which worsens due to inadequate resources. Without sufficient funding, efforts to scale up testing, treatment, and healthcare provider education in high-burden states remain insufficient to meet the needs of these vulnerable communities.10,11

In this paper, we aim to evaluate the current health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US using a health system perspective. Our study relies upon large, nationwide databases and secondary data sources to provide a comprehensive and robust calculation of US-specific healthcare expenditures for cost analysis, together with prevalence and disease burden estimates. Subsequently, we compare these estimates to those of other conditions in the US and assess the proportionality of funding through the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Methods

Overview

Our methodological approach to estimate the health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy in the United States (US) involved several key steps. First, we calculated the number of adults infected with Chagas disease (ChD), generating population-level estimates to capture the disease's prevalence by country. Next, we applied age-group-specific prevalence rates to determine the subset of adults with Chagas cardiomyopathy. Subsequently, to capture the multifaceted nature of Chagas cardiomyopathy and its associated cardiac complications, we integrated proportions of hospitalized patients with concurrent cardiac conditions. We further analyzed age-group-specific distributions and calculated associated healthcare costs, alongside disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for each condition by age group. Subsequently, these estimates were aggregated to calculate the total DALYs and total healthcare costs, representing the health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy. We adjusted our results for multimorbidity and performed a probabilistic sensitivity analysis. Finally, we compared our final health and economic burden to other diseases in the US.

Data inputs

Our analysis used a range of data inputs (Supplementary Table B1). Age-group-specific prevalence rates of ChD by country and Chagas cardiomyopathy by age-group were sourced from Irish et al. (2022), which provided estimates of T. cruzi infection and cardiomyopathy prevalence among Latin American migrants in the US.12 The population estimates of total number of adult Latin American-born persons in the US, stratified by age group, were derived using the 2018–2022 American Community Survey Public Use Microdata Sample (ACS-PUMS) 5-year estimates, a publicly available dataset provided by the US Census Bureau used to supplement the decennial census.13 Proportions of hospitalized patients with concurrent conditions were retrived from Lima et al. (2021), which analyzed hospitalization trends and comorbidities among Chagas cardiomyopathy patients in the United States over a 15-year period.14 Inpatient information (age-group-specific distributions and healthcare costs) were obtained from the 2019 Healthcare Costs and Utilization Project, National Inpatient Sample database (HCUP–NIS) from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, which is a large database containing ICD-10 classifications and costs of inpatient hospital stays around the US.15 Finally, to calculate DALYs we used age-specific life expectancy data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC),16 in conjunction with condition-specific survival rates sourced from the literature,17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 and disability weights from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study.23

Measures

Infection and disease prevalence

To estimate the infection and disease prevalence of ChD we followed the methodology described by Irish et al. (2022), updating their estimates by using the 2018–2022 ACS-PUMS 5-year data instead of the 2014–2018 ACS-PUMS 5-year data originally utilized. This adjustment accounts for demographic and migration pattern changes between the two periods, which is important given that the data from the 2018–2022 period included1 population controls informed by the 2020 Census with changes to the weighting methodology implemented to address the nonresponse bias, and2 improvements in the question regarding Hispanic origin.24 First, we obtained the population of adult Latin America-born persons living in the US by age group and country of origin (place of birth for Belize, Brazil, and Guyana) using 5-year data files from the ACS-PUMS. While ‘place of birth’ refers to the physical location where an individual was born, ‘country of origin’ reflects a broader concept. For many immigrants, their country of origin may be the country where they grew up, their family's ancestral homeland, or a place where they spent a significant portion of their life before migrating to another country. Using country of origin allows us to capture not only individuals born in these endemic countries but also those who may have lived much of their lives in these regions, or whose parents were from these endemic countries.

Second, to estimate the prevalence of T. cruzi infection and the number of those with ChD, we applied the age- and country-specific seroprevalence estimates provided by Irish et al. (2022) to our population estimates. These estimates were derived using a hybrid approach: for countries with sufficient US-based screening data (e.g., Mexico, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, and Colombia), Irish et al. calculated weighted prevalence rates directly; for countries with limited US data, they scaled El Salvador's age-specific prevalence pattern using correction factors informed by World Health Organization regional estimates. These seroprevalence values represent five-year period prevalence estimates for the 2014–2018 period, as derived from ACS-PUMS microdata, and are not annualized or cumulative figures. We adopted this same approach but applied the seroprevalence rates to the updated 2018–2022 ACS-PUMS population. Standard errors (SEs) were approximated using the ACS replicate weights methodology to account for complex sampling design and were used to construct confidence intervals (CIs) around our infection estimates.25 These intervals capture uncertainty in population size but not in seroprevalence or disease progression, for which SEs were not available.

Irish et al. (2022) provided a breakdown of the estimated number of infected adults by age group (18–34, 35–49, and >50), and the corresponding number and percentage of individuals with Chagas cardiomyopathy in each age category. They estimated 4%, 10%, and 25%, respectively, to be infected with cardiomyopathies.12 In this study we used these estimations and the estimated adults infected with ChD to calculate the number of adults with Chagas cardiomyopathy.

Concurrent cardiac conditions

Chagas cardiomyopathy is a multifocal disease, often manifesting as one or multiple cardiac diagnoses. To estimate the number of infected individuals with concurrent cardiac conditions, we accounted for several conditions identified by Lima et al. (2021), who provided proportions of hospitalized patients with these conditions.14 The conditions included in our analysis were sinoatrial dysfunction (SID), atrioventricular node block intraventricular conduction abnormalities (AV), atrial tachyarrhythmias (AT), ventricular arrhythmias (VA), heart failure (HF), and stroke (S). Rather than relying on prevalence data from the general population, we used estimates of severe concurrent conditions observed specifically among hospitalized Chagas cardiomyopathy patients. Because the Chagas-specific sample size in the HCUP-NIS was insufficient to support reliable cost estimation, we then applied average hospitalization costs for each condition derived from the general population in HCUP-NIS.

Cardiac diagnoses and associated health costs

Using the HCUP-NIS database, we identified age-group-specific proportions for each concurrent condition linking them to their specific ICD-10 codes. We included ICD-10 codes for Chagas disease with heart involvement, acute Chagas disease with heart involvement, and chronic Chagas disease with heart involvement, consistent with previous methodologies.14 These ICD-10 codes were used both to identify cases of Chagas cadiomyopathy and to classify concurrent conditions. Hospitalized patients were included in our analysis if any of their diagnoses were coded with these ICD-10 codes, and we then multiplied these proportions by the number of individuals with each concurrent condition to estimate the number of infected individuals by age group. We do not assume that all individuals with Chagas cardiomyopathy are hospitalized; instead, we use the prevalence of severe concurrent conditions observed among patients hospitalized with Chagas to estimate the number of severe cases.

Additionally, we calculated the average cost by age-group for each concurrent condition (detailed calculations in Supplementary Table B4). Pulmonary hypertension was excluded in our analysis as it is considered a manifestation of heart failure, thus its inclusion would result in double-countingmultimorbodity cost and health effects. Furthermore, procedures performed were not included in our analysis as for the scope of this analysis, they are considered treatments for the concurrent conditions rather than independent health outcomes. Further, we ensured to account for the cost-to-charge ratio provided in HCUP-NIS, which normalizing charges that vary by hospital, type of service, and inflationary costs. Our cost estimates are presented in 2019 USD, based on data from the HCUP-NIS, which reflects hospitalization costs during that period without adjustment for inflation.

Disability-Adjusted Life Years

Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) are a commonly used metric in public health research to quantify disease burden by combining years of life lost due to premature mortality (YLL) with years lived with disability (YLD); the equation used is provided in the Supplementary Equation 1. For this calculation, we combined the CDC age-specific life expectancy for the overall Hispanic population in the US with survival rate curves for each of the concurrent conditions considered in this study and their respective disability weights (DW). The survival rates and disability weights can be found in Supplementary Table B2. Additionally, we estimated discounted DALYs by applying a 3% annual discount rate, which adjusts for the time preference of health interventions. This discounting is applied over each individual's remaining life expectancy, reducing the weight of future health outcomes compared to present ones.

Multimorbidity adjustment

Multimorbidity was considered to avoid double-counting for both our DALYs and cost estimates. We identified the proportion of patients with concurrent conditions (Supplementary Table B3) using the 2019 HCUP-NIS database. The proportions of concurrent conditions were assumed to be consistent with those of the general population due to the limited and non-representative sample size of individuals coded with ChD. Percentages of patients with only one concurrent condition were applied to our final estimates in the probabilistic sensitivity analyses below.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

We conducted a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) to assess the robustness of our findings regarding the health and economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US. We applied beta distributions to represent the uncertainty in the prevalence of Chagas cardiomyopathy and the probability of each concurrent condition. Additionally, we used a gamma distribution to model the uncertainty in the number of individuals infected with ChD. We selected 95% CIs to define the uncertainty levels for both the probability of concurrent conditions and the number of individuals infected with ChD.

After assigning our parameter distributions, we conducted Monte Carlo simulations to propagate uncertainty through the model and generate our costs and DALYs outcomes. Each simulation involved randomly sampling values from the assigned probability distributions. We performed 1000 simulations to ensure stable estimates of the model outputs. Our results were summarized using the mean and the 95% CIs to characterize the central tendency and variability of the outcomes.

Health burden & available funding comparison

The final calculated health burden, in DALYs, for ChD and the available funding were compared to other diseases in the US using data available from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study,26 and the NIH RePORT database,27 respectively. The NIH RePORT database includes funding amounts from grants, contracts, and other funding mechanisms used across the NIH, and the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). We identified funding amounts for Chagas disease for each year and compared its proportion for the total funding amount in the respective year. The diseases or conditions selected for comparison were informed by common clinical categorization or similarities in DALYs.

Ethics approval

This study utilized publicly available data from the American Community Survey, the Global Burden of Disease Results, and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Report on Funding for Various Research, Conditions, and Disease Categories. Additionally, we analyzed data from the 2019 Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) National Inpatient Sample (NIS), obtained from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality through a one-time purchase.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Prevalence and infection

Table 1 shows the estimated prevalence and number of Latin America–born adults infected with T. cruzi in the United States by country of origin and age groups. Our estimated prevalence of ChD among Latin America-born adults in the US is 1.3%, which is equivalent to 427,145 people above age 18 between 2018 and 2022. Among those infected, 12% (50,668 individuals) were between 18 and 34 years old, 23% (98,082) were between 35 and 49 years, and the majority, 65% (278,395 individuals), were over 50. Mexico-born individuals have the highest number of infected individuals at 268,922, followed by El Salvador at 45,483, and Bolivia at 29,320. These estimates reflect a substantial burden of disease among these populations. Among the infected adult population, we estimated that 82,264 individuals in the US were living with Chagas cardiomyopathy between 2018 and 2022. Of these, 2.46% (2027) were between 18 and 34 years of age, 11.92% (9808) were between 35 and 49 years of age, and 84.60% (69,598) were over 50.

Costs and DALYs

Table 2 presents the number of observations, mean costs, total costs, total DALYs, and total discounted DALYs for each concurrent condition associated with Chagas cardiomyopathy disaggregated by age groups. The estimated number of individuals affected by each concurrent condition was as follows: 3949 with SID, 11,928 with AV, 22,293 with AT, 20,566 with VA, 53,142 with HF, and 4031 with S. The total economic costs across all age groups, expressed in 2019 USD, were: HF ($3.08 billion), VA ($1.75 billion), AT ($0.86 billion), S ($0.57 billion), AV ($0.29 billion), and SID ($0.20 billion). Similarly, the total DALYs for each condition were: HF (912,041 DALYs), VA (233,823 DALYs), AV (191,073 DALYs), AT (110,599 DALYs), SID (61,385 DALYs), and S (48,318 DALYs). While individual DALYs were higher for younger age groups, total DALYs (both raw and discounted), as well as DALYs per 100,000 Hispanic and overall populations, were higher among older groups. This pattern reflects he fact that cardiomyopathy predominantly affects older adults, resulting in greater prevalence and higher total DALYs in these groups, despite lower DALYs per person in comparison to younger individuals.

DALYs and available funding compared to other diseases in the US

According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, the DALYs for ChD in the US was estimated to be 0.000686 million in 2019. In contrast, our estimate of 0.886 million ranks Chagas cardiomyopathy as the 18th highest contributor to the overall disease burden in the US. These DALYs are comparable to those associated with interpersonal violence and neonatal disorders. Discussion

Our study highlights the substantial health and economic burden of chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US and contextualizes this burden with that of other diseases. Our estimates indicate that 427,145 persons are living with T. cruzi infection in the US and, of these, 82,269 have Chagas cardiomyopathy. The total DALYs are 0.886 million, adjusted for multimorbidity, meaning that an individual who develops ChD loses, on average, 10.77 years of life. The adjustment for multimorbidity highlights the considerable health and economic burden associated with Chagas cardiomyopathy, even after factoring in variability. Additionally, these findings demonstrate the significant impact of ChD on both healthcare expenditures and health outcomes in the US, with the total DALYs representing 1.176 million healthy life years lost due to Chagas cardiomyopathy. The economic burden of disease was found to be nearly $5.0 billion, yet funding available for research through the National Institutes of Health (NIH), and the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), was only $14.61 millions in 2023.

Previous estimates, including those upon which we modeled our methods (i.e., Irish et al.), calculated an infection prevalence of ∼300,000 people.12 However, our estimate of ∼430,000 is higher due to several key factors. Our estimates incorporate more recent data accounting for demographic shifts between the 2014–2018 period used in previous estimates and the 2018–2022 period used in our simulations. Second, changes in the Census Bureau's weighting methodology in 2020, aimed at addressing nonresponse bias, were incorporated into our model. Third, improvements in questionnaires regarding Hispanic origin further refined the accuracy of our prevalence estimates. Although our estimated prevalence rate (1.3%) is slightly lower than the 1.64% estimated by Irish et al., the larger Latin American population size yields a greater absolute number of infected individuals, despite the lower prevalence rate.

In contrast to earlier models, our analysis integrates more recent data and a broader set of assumptions to address the heterogeneity of ChD across populations. For instance, in Los Angeles, the prevalence of ChD among Latin American immigrants (1.24%) was found to be similar to our estimate, but with significant variation by country of origin: El Salvador had a prevalence of 3.45%, while Mexico had 0.79%.28 These figures differ from our estimates of 2.87% for El Salvador, and 1.10% for Mexico, highlighting the necessity of accounting for regional and demographic variability in ChD burden assessments. Furthermore, our estimated prevalence of 1.3% among Latin American migrants in the US is higher than rates reported in a systematic review of studies conducted between 2004 and 2014. This review identified much lower prevalence rates, including 0.2% among blood donors (Custer et al., 2012), 0.3% among pregnant women (Edwards et al., 2014), and 0.3% among Canadian Blood Services donors (O'Brien et al., 2013).29 Notably, a recent study reported a higher seroprevalence of ChD among at-risk populations in Washington, D.C. (3.8%), further supporting the likelihood that previous studies underestimated the true burden of disease.30 These disparities suggest that earlier studies likely underestimated the true burden of ChD, possibly due to sampling bias, underreporting and lack of comprehensive surveillance.

To the best of our knowledge, the study by Lee et al. (2013) represents the only extensive analysis of the economic burden of ChD. They estimated the total burden in the US to be 1.123 million DALYs, while our estimates suggest a total of 0.886 million DALYs, likely statistically comparable to the estimate by Lee et al., demonstrating that the burden remains substantial. A notable difference, however, is that their analysis included DALYs calculations for Chagas-related megaesophagus and megacolon, conditions that are particularly challenging to quantify. Additionally, while their analysis assessed the global burden of ChD across all stages, our study specifically focuses on the US population and Chagas cardiomyopathy, as this stage is the most significant contributor to both morbidity and mortality in affected individuals. Despite these differences in focus and methodology, both studies highlight the persistent and substantial economic and health burden that Chagas cardiomyopathy continue to impose on public health in the US.

Our health and economic burden estimates for Chagas cardiomyopathy rely on hospitalization data, capturing severe cases but excluding milder forms managed in outpatient settings. This may underestimate costs for non-hospitalized patients. A key limitation of our study is that we applied the proportions of concurrent conditions from hospitalized patients to prevalence estimates derived from outpatient data. While this introduces uncertainty, we assumed that outpatient individuals with Chagas cardiomyopathy will eventually require hospitalization and incur similar inpatient costs due to the progressive nature of the disease. Further research incorporating outpatient data is needed for a more comprehensive assessment of Chagas cardiomyopathy's health and economic impact in the US.

Our study used GBD data for disability weights and for comparisons with other diseases, but not for prevalence estimates. While the GBD provides its own prevalence estimates, particularly through the GBD 2019 study, it has been noted that GBD estimates for non-endemic regions tend to be lower than those from other data sources, likely due to methodological differences and challenges in estimating cases in migrant populations.31 The GBD 2021 release further adjusted its prevalence estimates, which could affect reproducibility in future iterations. However, since disability weights remained unchanged from GBD 2019 to GBD 2021, our DALY estimates remain robust. Prior GBD analyses have also reported a significant global decline in both Chagas disease and Chagas-related heart failure (Chagas HF) prevalence, particularly in Latin America, where vector control programs, improved blood screening, and public health interventions have contributed to reduced transmission.32 While these trends reflect progress in endemic areas, our findings suggest that the burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US remains substantial. This persistence is likely due to the long-term progression of previously acquired infections among Latin American migrants. Additionally, persistent gaps in diagnosis and treatment in non-endemic settings may contribute to an underestimation of disease burden. These findings highlight the need for further research, improved surveillance, and expanded healthcare access to accurately assess and address the burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy, particularly in migrant populations.

Our research found that the economic burden of Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US is $4.96 billion, which is lower than the $6.7 billion estimated by Lee et al. (2013) for Chagas disease in both the United States and Canada. The difference reflects both the geographic scope and methodological approach, as our study focuses specifically on cardiomyopathy cases in the US and uses more recent, hospitalization-based cost estimates in 2019 USD. Unlike Lees's study, which assesses the burden of all states of ChD, our analysis specifically focused on the burden of disease in one stage of ChD—cardiomyopathy. Unlike dynamic models such as those used by Lee et al. (2013), which simulate annual progression using Markov modeling (4% probability of annual progression to cardiomyopathy), our approach applies fixed age-specific proportions based on cumulative progression estimates to reflect long-term outcomes. This static approach was appropriate given our objective to estimate the present burden rather than to forecast progression over time. We incorporated more granular, age-specific progression rates based on Irish et al.: 4% for individuals aged 18–34, 10% for those aged 35–49, and 25% for individuals over 50 years old. However, these progression rates remain highly debated, as they vary widely across countries. Furthermore, our research integrated findings from Lima et al. (2021), which provide detailed proportions of specific conditions among Chagas heart disease patients. Although Lima et al.’s estimates are derived from 2002 to 2017 HCUP-NIS data, they represent the most detailed and nationally representative condition-specific comorbidity information currently available for Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US. It is important to mention that this data reflects inpatient populations with more severe disease outcomes, highlighting the lack of community-based data that would help characterize the broader, multifocal development of ChD—an absence particularly evident in the US. Consequently, these rates may overestimate the burden compared to the general population with Chagas cardiomyopathy. Importantly, this is not an estimate of total economic burden—it is an estimate of healthcare provider financial burden reflective of the population who was observed to seek care. We agree that not everyone will be hospitalized, which is why the proportions used to calculate our estimates are based on observed hospitalizations. The fact that not all affected individuals will have access to care or receive inpatient care is important and is captured in our sensitivity analysis. These estimates may therefore overestimate the costs due to the realities of care-seeking and care-receiving behaviour in the affected population. In this context, the major limitation of our work remains the scarcity of community-based research on ChD in the US, which constrains a complete understanding of its economic and health burden. This limitation is shaped by several factors. First, there is limited availability of robust epidemiological data that capture the spectrum of Chagas cardiomyopathy in outpatient settings, particularly in underrepresented and uninsured Latin American immigrant populations.33 Second, our reliance on the HCUP-NIS database introduces potential biases, as these data reflect hospitalized cases and may not represent the full burden of disease in the community.14 Furthermore, we were unable to incorporate indirect costs such as productivity loss, informal caregiving, or long-term disability-related expenses, which may significantly add to the true economic burden.8 Lastly, substantial regional variation in access to care and diagnostic practices across the US remains unaccounted for in our estimates, despite its potential influence on outcomes and healthcare costs.10 Addressing these gaps will require targeted community-based studies, enhanced surveillance systems, and more inclusive data collection practices that capture the lived experiences and health trajectories of at-risk populations.

In consideration of the assumptions discussed above, we anticipate our estimates of disease burden to be overestimated. Nonetheless, it is unquestionable that ChD is contributing to a significant but largely unseen burden in the US, particularly within a labor force that supports the economy through manual labor. This disease disproportionately affects individuals from Latin American countries who engage in essential and physically demanding jobs. The health outcomes of these individuals, especially those suffering from Chagas cardiomyopathy, remain underreported, intensifying the economic and social challenges posed by this neglected tropical disease.6 Unfortunately, treatment for individuals who have already progressed to the cardiomyopathy stage is difficult and often less effective, especially given the late diagnosis, fragmented care, and limited access to advanced therapeutic options.34

Looking through the NIH RePORT database, which includes funding amounts from grants, contracts, and other funding mechanisms used across the NIH, as well as disease burden data published by the NCHS at the CDC,27 we estimate that the amount of research funding available for ChD in the US was ∼$14.61 million USD in 2023 which represents 0.005% of the budget. In comparison, HIV/AIDS, Tuberculosis, Malaria are estimated to cause 52.3%, 2.8%, and 11.3% of the DALYs in US respectively, but receives 225×, 22×, and 48× higher the funding respectively. Even if our burden estimates are 10× higher than reality, the disease is still being underfunded. Understanding the burden imposed by Chagas cardiomyopathy is crucial and our findings stress the importance of implementing effective early diagnosis strategies to prevent unnecessary adverse health and economic consequences.

Despite the significant burden caused by Chagas cardiomyopathy in the US, funding for research, prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of ChD remains disproportionately low compared to other diseases. Additionally, funding for care related to this specific disease frequently depends on ad hoc and often informal mechanisms initiated by individual physicians or researches invested in the disease whom usually utilize their own funds or research grants to study the disease.10 This low amount of funding inhibits the ability to develop effective strategies for early diagnosis, comprehensive treatment, and public health interventions, which are vital in mitigating the severe health and economic consequences associated with ChD. Early detection through increased investment in diagnostic tools is essential for timely treatment and prevention of severe complications.35 Increased investment in ChD is essential to address the unmet needs in managing ChD and to reduce its impact on public health and healthcare costs.
