Blood transfusion: Not always as safe as you think
Authors: Naomi Rahimi‐Levene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Commentary on: Proudlove et al. Efficiency and Safety Gains from Bedside Electronic Transfusion Checks in four hospitals in England. British Journal of Haematology 2026; 209:1311‐1320.

In this issue, Proudlove et al. compare the manual procedure of group and screen (G&S) sample taking and transfusion administration to a bedside electronic transfusion check (BETC) system, where barcode confirmation of patient identification is used with a form to submit to a third paperless system. 1 They found the paperless procedure to be more efficient, conserving resources including time, staff, forms, etc. An even more important finding is patient safety; transfusion errors such as described above can be reduced to a minimum with an electronic identification process.

Death due to transfusion errors is a ‘zero tolerance’ event in medical care. Errors can potentially occur at several stages in the transfusion cycle and must be prevented. The two clinical steps, starting at drawing a G&S sample and ending in transfusing the blood component to the patient, are most prone to errors. Identification is manual in both; the patient's wristband is compared with the sticker on the G&S samples or on the component before administration and the patient is asked to identify himself if possible. The sticker can belong to another patient; the staff may be busy, treating a few patients in parallel and mixing them up with one another, misidentification being the most common error.

Errors in the blood bank are much rarer than bedside ones as the work performed in the laboratory is computerized in many sites worldwide. Samples are scanned into the system, units are cross‐matched and prepared using barcode identification and are released via the computer system.

In 1990, Sazama reported on 355 transfusion associated deaths occurring over 10‐years through 1985, of them at least 131 were due to blood groups A, B and O (ABO) incompatibility, possibly even more. 2 The deaths were attributed to misidentification of the patients.

A summary of 27 years of haemovigliance in the United Kingdom, dealing with ABO incompatible transfusions, demonstrated that 6.3% (0.04 per 100 000) of reported cases resulted in patient death, and 23.9% (0.16 per 100 000) were linked to major morbidity. 3 Over 50% were clinical errors, 45.9% administration errors and 29.6% collection errors, laboratory errors being less frequent. More than 70% were pre‐transfusion errors. There was a significant improvement in safety over the years, but they stated the need for more robust safety systems to further reduce these errors. Storch et al. reviewed Federal Drug Administration (FDA)‐reported ABO incompatible transfusions between 2010 and 2019, and although there has been a reduction in mortality, 2 cases a year are still reported. 4 Both the Serious Hazards of Transfusion (SHOT) summary and the FDA‐report, suggest using BETC technology.

In the Prevention of bedside errors in transfuion medicine (Probe‐TM) study, Murphy et al. 2007 suggested a method to prevent transfusion to the wrong patient by attaching a tag to the blood component reminding the member of staff performing the transfusion to check the patient's wristband. This was manual and failed to improve the safety of bedside transfusion practice. 5

Chan et al. in 2004 published their experience with a barcode identifier in the transfusion process. They demonstrated effectiveness and a small amount of error reduction compared to the manual process. 6 In their manuscript, they emphasize the need to approach the correct patient when taking G&S samples for the whole process to apply to the patient needing transfusion.

Ohsaka et al. developed a computer‐assisted transfusion management system; between 2002 and 2006, 49 974 blood components were transfused without a single mistransfusion. 7

To prevent human errors, barcode scanning with bedside electronic transfusion systems and association of the sample or the unit with the patient has been investigated.

Oyekan et al. summarized their experience in the UK in implementing BETC. 8 , 9 They aimed to identify facilitators and barriers, and to provide guidance for future users. They concluded that safety can be improved significantly if the staff is properly trained to use BETC, if the systems are integrated into clinical workflows and there is strong governance. The authors also summarized staff satisfaction and concluded that the staff perceived improvement of safety, efficiency in terms of time saving and less staff needed. The workflow and trackability were better, and the staff preferred this method to the older manual one.

In summary, there is a need for worldwide adoption of bedside electronic checking systems at both ends of the transfusion process. No excuse can condone misidentification errors in transfusion. The barriers to implementing ‘dual end’ checks are complex. First, this issue must be elevated to the top of the ‘must change’ list, recognizing the fact that relying on staff complying 100% with standard operating procedures is an illusion. Second, once there is recognition, institutions must be ready to make a change. Last, but not least, funding needs to be allocated, recognizing the fact that lives will be saved and in the long run, also money.
