A Bayesian multilevel joint model for predicting neonatal head circumference and body mass index: a case study in Dalahu County, Iran
Authors: Sahar Fallah, Bahare Andayeshgar, Payam Amini, Behzad Mahaki, Soodeh Shahsavari, Amir Hossein Hashemian, Andrew Fournier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNewborn anthropometry is an essential research tool for assessing newborn growth and studying the factors that influence inadequate or excessive fetal growth. Newborn Body Mass Index (BMI) and Head Circumference (HC) are among the anthropometric measurements that are important to monitor from early infancy. This study aimed to identify the maternal, paternal, and pregnancy-related factors associated with newborn BMI and HC in Dalahoo County, Iran.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included all pregnant women who attended primary health centers in Dalahoo County, located in the central regions of the western half of Kermanshah Province, Iran, from March 21, 2022, to May 19, 2024. The response variables included newborn HC as a continuous variable and newborn BMI as an ordinal variable. BMI was categorized based on sex-and age-specific reference values provided by World Health Organization (WHO). Newborn BMI and HC were examined concurrently to assess factors influencing newborn development using a Bayesian multilevel joint modeling approach.ResultsFor each additional week of gestation, the odds of being in a higher BMI category increased (OR 1.45; 95% HPD 1.31–1.65). Newborns with smoking fathers had lower odds of being in a higher BMI category than those with non-smoking fathers (OR 0.41; 95% HPD 0.36–0.47). Newborns of mothers with Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) had higher odds of being in a higher BMI category than those of non-diabetic mothers (OR 2.56; 95% HPD 2.18–2.92). Male newborns had higher odds of being in a higher BMI category than females (OR 1.40; 95% HPD 1.22–1.60). For head circumference, each additional week of gestation increased the mean by 0.44 cm (posterior mean, 0.44; 95% HPD, 0.42–0.45). Male newborns had a larger mean head circumference (posterior mean difference, 0.24 cm; 95% HPD, 0.05–0.38). All associations were interpreted while holding other variables constant.ConclusionsIn this cohort study, gestational age (per week), Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM), and male sex were associated with higher odds of being in a higher newborn BMI category, whereas paternal smoking was associated with lower odds. Gestational age and male sex were associated with a larger mean Head Circumference (HC). Joint modeling of BMI and HC within a Bayesian multilevel framework allowed simultaneous estimation of both outcomes while accounting for outcome correlation, supporting the assessment of BMI and HC together in newborn growth. However, given the limitations of external validity, these findings should be interpreted with caution and are not intended as direct clinical recommendations.

Introduction

Newborn anthropometry reliably measures fetal development quality and indicates health, nutritional status, performance, future well-being, and survival [1–3]. As a measurement science, anthropometry is an essential research tool for evaluating infant growth and studying factors that determine inadequate or excessive fetal growth [4, 5]. Measurements such as height, weight, Head Circumference (HC), Body Mass Index (BMI), body circumferences, and skinfold thickness are part of anthropometry [6].

Birth weight is used to evaluate intrauterine fetal development and monitor newborn health. Both low and high birth weights contribute significantly to the global disease burden. Low birth weight (under 2500 g) is a strong predictor of infant mortality [7, 8], with affected infants at higher risk of poor growth, cognitive deficiencies, and early death [9]. Conversely, infants with high birth weight (more than 4000 g) may face severe complications during childbirth, including macrosomia, which increases risks of shoulder dystocia, birth injuries, and maternal complications [10].

BMI, calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²), is a widely used indicator for assessing nutritional status and growth patterns in newborns and infants [11]. It helps identify underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese infants. Monitoring BMI from early infancy is crucial as it can predict future health outcomes, including obesity and metabolic disorders in later life [12].

HC is another critical anthropometric measure that reflects brain growth and development during the prenatal and early postnatal periods [13, 14]. Abnormal HC measurements, whether microcephaly or macrocephaly, can indicate underlying neurological conditions or developmental disorders [15, 16]. Regular monitoring of HC is essential for early detection and intervention of potential developmental issues.

Several factors influence newborn anthropometry, including maternal characteristics (age, pre-pregnancy BMI, gestational weight gain, gestational diabetes), paternal factors (smoking status), and infant characteristics (sex, gestational age) [17–22]. Understanding these relationships is crucial for developing targeted interventions to improve maternal and child health outcomes.

Traditional statistical methods often analyze anthropometric measures independently, potentially missing important correlations between outcomes. Joint modeling approaches allow simultaneous analysis of multiple correlated outcomes, providing more comprehensive insights into factors affecting newborn development [23, 24]. Bayesian multilevel joint models offer additional advantages, including flexibility in handling complex data structures, incorporation of prior information, and robust estimation of parameters and their uncertainties [25, 26].

Therefore, the primary objective of this study is methodological rather than mechanistic or purely clinical. Specifically, we aim to demonstrate the application and added value of a Bayesian multilevel joint modeling framework for the simultaneous analysis of correlated newborn anthropometric outcomes—Body Mass Index (BMI) and Head Circumference (HC)—using routinely collected population data. The secondary objective is to illustrate how this framework can be used to estimate associations between maternal, paternal, and pregnancy-related factors and these outcomes. Clinical interpretation is provided only to contextualize the results, rather than to propose direct clinical decision rules.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on all pregnant women referred to primary health centers in Dalahoo County, located in the central region of the western half of Kermanshah Province, Iran. Information about pregnant women and newborns is recorded in the SIB system, a comprehensive electronic integrated health system. The data, collected by physicians and healthcare providers (Behvarz), are collected from individuals and recorded in the system [27]. From March 21, 2022, to May 19, 2024, a total of 803 pregnancies were registered in four primary health centers in Dalahoo County: three urban centers (Kerend-e Gharb, Rijab, and Gahwareh) and one rural center (Zaweleh Olia). These centers represent the first level of care in the Iranian healthcare system. Pregnant women were not referred from higher levels of care; rather, they were registered and followed directly at these primary health centers as part of routine antenatal care.

Out of these, 537 pregnant women (198 residing in urban areas and 339 in rural areas) with complete and detailed data were included in this study. The exclusion criteria were incomplete information, stillbirths, and multiple pregnancies. The Pregnant women included in this study were routine antenatal follow-ups at primary health centers, where most pregnancies were uncomplicated and reached term. High-risk pregnancies and very preterm births are typically referred to secondary or tertiary hospitals and, therefore, fall outside the sampling frame of the present study. Consequently, the study population is not expected to include a large proportion of preterm or high-risk pregnancies. Gestational age was determined based on the last menstrual period or first-trimester ultrasound and represented the completed weeks of gestation at the time of delivery. Gestational age was partly based on the last menstrual period, which may introduce measurement error due to recall bias or irregular cycles. All measurements were performed by experienced midwives. Newborn HC was measured using a tape measure from the midpoint of the frontal bone above the eyebrows to the most prominent point of the occipital bone at the back of the head. Newborn weight was measured using a digital scale (grams), and newborn height (in centimeters) was measured in the lying position using a graduated board.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (code IR.KUMS.REC.1402.034) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and national guidelines and regulations.

Predictor and response variables

Parental information included the mother’s age (years), education, occupation, gestational health problems, pre-pregnancy BMI (kg/m²), gestational weight gain (kg), gestational age (weeks), delivery type, abortion history, number of previous births (parity), father’s age (years), occupation, and smoking status. To ensure the validity of statistical analyses, categorical variables with sparse data across multiple categories were carefully evaluated. In particular, parity was recategorized into two groups (nulliparous vs. multiparous), and mother’s education was recategorized into two levels: Low (illiterate/primary) and High (secondary/university). For the variable “Mother’s occupation” statistical comparisons were not performed due to the very small sample sizes in the “employed “category, as such analyses would not yield reliable or valid results. Newborn information included sex (female or male), birth weight (kg), height at birth (cm), and HC at birth (cm).

The choice of newborn BMI as an anthropometric indicator was driven by two considerations: First, BMI categories based on WHO sex- and age-specific references are widely used and interpretable in public health and clinical settings. Second, from a modeling perspective, BMI categories allow the use of a proportional odds framework, which naturally accommodates ordinal outcomes and enables direct interpretation of cumulative odds. The proportional odds model estimates the cumulative odds of being in a higher BMI category (normal or overweight vs. underweight; overweight vs. normal/underweight). A single odds ratio assumes proportionality across thresholds. The proportional odds framework models cumulative contrasts rather than category-specific effects.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive analyses, independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used for continuous variables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Spearman or Pearson correlation coefficients were used for continuous predictors.

In this study, we used a Bayesian multilevel joint modeling approach to simultaneously analyze two related newborn health outcomes: head circumference (HC) and BMI. As these two measures are correlated, analyzing them separately may lead to biased results. Using a joint model, we accounted for their relationship and improved accuracy. Additionally, given that our data were obtained from different health centers, we incorporated a multilevel structure to control for center-specific variation. The Bayesian approach allows us to quantify uncertainty more effectively and handle data limitations, making it a robust choice for this study.

Bayesian analysis provides accurate inferences that are conditional on the data and do not rely on asymptotic approximations. Moreover, Bayesian methods do not require strict assumptions, and in small sample sizes, they produce more accurate estimates compared to classical methods.

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data. Quantitative variables were summarized using measures of central tendency and dispersion indices (mean ± standard deviation (SD)), with minimum and maximum values reported to describe the observed range. Qualitative variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The distribution of newborn HC across health centers was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which confirmed approximate normality.

Before modeling the combined responses of newborn HC and BMI, independent samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and Spearman rank correlation analyses were conducted to identify candidate predictor variables. For the newborn BMI outcome, gestational age (weeks), gestational weight gain (kg), sex, paternal smoking status, and gestational diabetes were selected. For newborn HC, gestational age (weeks), gestational weight gain (kg), and sex were selected. These predictors were then included simultaneously in the joint model. Newborn BMI and HC were modeled jointly due to their positive and significant correlation (ρ = 0.319, P < 0.0001). Joint modeling has gained increasing attention, as separate analyses may be inappropriate or inefficient when outcomes are correlated [29].

Joint modeling improves prediction accuracy, reduces bias, and provides more reliable parameter estimates [25, 30]. As pregnant women were selected from different health centers (537 women across 4 centers), and each center has unique characteristics, the women are not homogeneous across centers. To more accurately estimate the effects and reduce errors, we modeled the heterogeneity between health centers using a multilevel approach [31]. In this study, the two levels were as follows: Level 1 — pregnant women nested within Level 2 — health centers.

Random effects are the most prevalent approach for developing a model. The response variables were combined using random intercepts. Random effects here capture variation between individuals in the response variables. A linear mixed model was used for newborn HC, and a generalized linear mixed model with a logit link function was used for newborn BMI. Given that individuals are nested within different health centers, center-specific random effects were incorporated to account for between-center variability and to jointly model the response variables. The random intercepts in the cumulative logit regression and linear mixed models were assumed to follow normal distributions. Bayesian inference

The Bayesian framework is highly flexible for joint modeling of multilevel data, as it can manage complex hierarchical structures and incorporate prior information to improve parameter estimation, especially with sparse or small datasets. It provides a coherent method to quantify uncertainty via posterior distributions and effectively handles missing data, making it ideal for complex multilevel models [36].

Parameter estimation was performed using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). Diagnostics confirmed proper convergence, adequate mixing, and low autocorrelation, with sufficient chain length, appropriate burn-in, and no sensitivity to priors, ensuring the validity of the analysis. Weakly informative normal priors (mean 0, large variance) were specified for regression parameters to ensure minimal prior influence while maintaining computational stability. Sensitivity analyses using alternative prior specifications were explored and did not materially affect the results (results not shown). An inverse-Wishart prior was specified for the variance–covariance matrix of random effects. While commonly used, this choice assumes a particular dependence structure; however, given the relatively large sample size, posterior estimates were primarily driven by the data. The inverse Wishart distribution (2, S) was used for the prior distribution of the variance-covariance matrix. In this study, we used the MCMC procedure, where an array defines the random intercepts. Therefore, the random intercept vector has a variance-covariance matrix. We assumed the prior distribution IWish (2, S) for this matrix. The value 2 indicates the dimensions of matrix S, (2 × 2), and S represents prior information obtained from the separate models. Before combining the models, we ran each model (once for the ordinal response and once for the continuous quantitative response) to estimate the variance of the random intercepts and construct matrix S assuming zero correlation.

Statistical software

Some statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The multilevel Bayesian joint model was fitted using PROC MCMC in SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), which is widely used for joint modeling. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study included 537 pregnant women, comprising 198 urban and 339 rural residents, who provided detailed information. Participants visited two health centers in Kerend-e Gharb (n = 155, 28.9%), one in Rijab (n = 167, 31.1%), one in Gahwareh (n = 113, 21%), and one in Zawele Olia (n = 102, 19%).

Maternal age was centered in the early thirties. The mean pre-pregnancy BMI was in the overweight range, and gestational weight gain was, on average, around 10 kg with wide variation from loss to substantial gain. Gestational age categories included 3 (0.6%) very preterm (28 to 32 weeks), 36 (6.7%) moderate preterm (32 to37 weeks), 494 (92%) term (37 to 42 weeks), and 4 (0.7%) post-term (≥ 42 weeks). Among comorbidities, thalassemia minor was the most frequent, followed by gestational diabetes and anemia. More than half of the births (56.6%) were vaginal. Most mothers were homemakers (98.7%) and had secondary education (60.1%).

Comparison with separate models

To evaluate the performance of the proposed Bayesian multilevel joint model, we compared its results with those obtained from separate models for HC and BMI using the same dataset (Table 5). Overall, the estimates from both approaches were broadly consistent in terms of the direction and statistical significance of the effects. However, the joint model generally provided more efficient estimation by leveraging correlation between outcomes, as reflected by smaller posterior standard errors and narrower 95% HPD intervals for most of the predictors (e.g., paternal smoking: 0.2941 vs. 0.0947).

Discussion

Main findings

In the final joint Bayesian multilevel model, gestational age and male sex were consistently associated with higher odds of being in a higher newborn BMI category and with a greater mean head circumference. In this study, BMI refers to newborn BMI calculated from birth weight and length, unless explicitly stated otherwise (e.g., pre-pregnancy maternal BMI). Maternal gestational diabetes was associated with higher odds of being in a higher BMI category, whereas paternal smoking was associated with lower odds of being in a higher BMI category. Gestational weight gain was not independently associated with either outcome after adjustment. Taken together, these findings highlight the value of jointly evaluating newborn BMI and head circumference as complementary indicators of early growth and neurodevelopment. This study primarily demonstrates the use of a Bayesian multilevel joint modeling approach for analyzing correlated newborn anthropometric outcomes within a real-world primary care dataset. The associations observed between gestational age, sex, gestational diabetes, paternal smoking, BMI, and head circumference are largely consistent with existing literature and are presented to illustrate the interpretability of the joint model rather than to establish novel biological mechanisms.

Interpretation in context of existing literature

Anthropometric measurements obtained at birth are cost-effective, non-invasive, and practical indicators for assessing fetal growth and nutritional status and for identifying newborns who may require closer monitoring [14, 16]. Among these measures, Body Mass Index (BMI) provides information beyond birth weight alone by accounting for the proportionality between weight and length. Previous studies have shown that BMI may be a more accurate predictor of later obesity risk than birth weight alone [37], supporting the use of standardized BMI growth charts from early life [38, 39]. In addition to traditional anthropometric indices, BMI improves the precision of newborn growth assessment by identifying disproportionate growth patterns that may not be evident from weight alone [40–42].

In the present study, we applied a Bayesian multilevel joint modeling framework to simultaneously analyze newborn BMI and head circumference (HC), the latter being a widely accepted proxy for brain growth and neurological development. This approach accounted for the correlation between outcomes and the hierarchical structure of the data, with newborns nested within health centers, an aspect that is rarely addressed in previous studies. Consistent with the existing literature, greater gestational age was associated with higher odds of being in a higher newborn BMI category. Although this relationship is well established [43–45], our study confirms and quantifies it in this population using a joint Bayesian framework. These findings further support the recommendation to use multiple anthropometric measurements simultaneously for a comprehensive evaluation of newborn growth [14]. Several studies have also demonstrated that BMI is a more informative indicator of neonatal adiposity than alternative indices, such as the ponderal index [46, 47]. Variations in the distribution of newborn BMI were observed across health centers. In all centers, the normal BMI category was the most frequent, and among the non-normal categories, underweight was more common than overweight, although both categories were relatively uncommon. These differences may reflect geographic and socioeconomic disparities between urban and rural regions of Kermanshah Province, including variability in access to healthcare services and maternal nutritional status [24].

Regional differences in gestational weight gain, particularly lower weight gain among mothers from Zaweleh village, are consistent with previous reports identifying Dalahu County as an underserved area for maternal and newborn health center [24]. Sex-based differences were evident in the anthropometric outcomes. Male newborns had higher odds of being in higher BMI categories and a larger mean head circumference than female newborns. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting greater birth size and anthropometric dimensions among male newborns [48, 49]. Although female newborns are typically lighter and shorter at birth, evidence suggests that sex-specific differences in body composition may exist, with female newborns sometimes exhibiting a higher relative fat mass despite their smaller size [50]. Although no women reported smoking during pregnancy, paternal smoking was associated with lower odds of being in a higher newborn BMI category, likely reflecting exposure to second-hand smoke. Previous studies have consistently linked prenatal exposure to tobacco smoke with impaired fetal growth, low birth weight, and preterm birth [51–53].

Given the rural residence of much of the study population and the potentially limited awareness of the risks of passive smoke exposure, these findings underscore the importance of targeted interventions aimed at reducing household smoke exposure during pregnancy. In the adjusted joint model, gestational weight gain was not independently associated with newborn BMI or head circumference. While gestational weight gain remains an important determinant of maternal and newborn health [54–57], its effects may be mediated by other factors, including gestational age and maternal metabolic conditions. Previous studies have reported associations between gestational weight gain and newborn BMI z-scores [19], suggesting that the relationship may depend on population characteristics and the analytic approach. Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) was associated with a higher likelihood of being in a higher newborn BMI category. This finding aligns with extensive evidence linking GDM to increased neonatal adiposity and obesity [58, 59]. Potential mechanisms include altered intrauterine glucose exposure, metabolic programming, and changes in the maternal and neonatal gut microbiomes [60]. GDM has also been associated with adverse outcomes among newborns classified as Small for Gestational Age, emphasizing the need for early detection and effective management during pregnancy [61]. Head Circumference increased with increasing gestational age, a finding that is expected and consistent with previous research demonstrating a strong association between gestational age and head growth [62–64]. Male newborns also had a larger mean head circumference than females, in agreement with prior studies [12, 62, 65]. HC is influenced by multiple factors, including genetics, ethnicity, and maternal health conditions, and remains an essential component of routine newborn assessment.

Methodological contribution

From a methodological perspective, various statistical approaches have been used to analyze infant growth outcomes, including logistic regression, correlation analyses, and separate mixed-effects models [66–68]. The comparison with separate models further supports the use of joint modeling, as it allows for more efficient estimation by incorporating the correlation between outcomes. Recently, joint modeling frameworks for correlated ordinal and continuous outcomes have been developed, particularly within Bayesian hierarchical settings [69–72]. However, most existing applications focus on clinical or longitudinal disease outcomes rather than on newborn anthropometric indicators at birth. In contrast, the present study applies a Bayesian multilevel joint modeling framework to simultaneously analyze newborn BMI and head circumference, accounting for both outcome correlation and the hierarchical structure of the data, with newborns nested within health centers.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Approximately 33% of registered pregnancies were excluded, primarily due to referral to higher-level care or incomplete data. This likely introduces selection bias toward lower-risk pregnancies and may limit generalizability, particularly for preterm or high-risk populations. This selection may attenuate variability in both outcomes and predictors, potentially leading to conservative estimates of associations. However, the primary objective of this study was methodological rather than population inference, and thus the impact on demonstrating the joint modeling framework is limited. The study population was limited to healthcare centers in Dalahu County, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other regions. Smoking and alcohol use during pregnancy may have been underreported because of cultural stigma. Direct measures of household smoke exposure, socioeconomic status, maternal diet, physical activity, and certain maternal comorbidities were not available in the SIB database. Future studies incorporating these factors and broader geographic settings are warranted to improve external validity.

Conclusion

Gestational age, maternal Gestational Diabetes Mellitus, infant sex, and paternal smoking were associated with newborn anthropometric outcomes in this study. By jointly modeling Body Mass Index (BMI) and Head Circumference (HC), this study demonstrates the value of a Bayesian multilevel framework for the simultaneous analysis of correlated newborn anthropometric outcomes while accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data and between-center variability. Within our specific dataset from primary health centers in Dalahoo County, the model identified associations consistent with previously reported patterns and reflect the types of relationships this framework is designed to capture. Rather than relying on birth weight alone, this methodological approach provides a more coherent and statistically efficient framework for evaluating newborn anthropometric outcomes and their associated factors. The proposed framework is primarily intended to demonstrate the methodological advantages of joint multilevel modeling in multicenter settings. Consistent with the study limitations, particularly regarding selection bias and unmeasured confounders, the external validity of these findings is limited. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to other populations or healthcare settings and should not be considered direct clinical evidence or recommendations.
