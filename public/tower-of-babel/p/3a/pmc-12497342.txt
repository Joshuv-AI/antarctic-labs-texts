Predictors of dental caries among inmates in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Suliman Shahin, Balgis Osman Gaffar, Muhammad Ashraf Nazir, Ibrahim AlHussain, Wazeer Alghamdi, Jehan Alhumaid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOral health is a neglected aspect of healthcare, especially among prison inmates who face barriers in accessing dental services. Dental caries is a prevalent and preventable disease that affects inmates’ general health and quality of life. This study evaluated dental caries and its predictors among inmates in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted as part of an ongoing oral health program by the College of Dentistry, Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University(IAU), Dammam, targeting inmates in prisons across Dammam, Khobar, Dhahran, Jubail, and AlHasa in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. This prison outreach program is conducted annually from January to April. Male and female inmates who voluntarily consented were included without exclusion criteria. Data collection involved oral examinations using the DMFT index and Simplified Oral Hygiene Index (OHI-S), following World Health Organization’s (WHO) criteria, and structured interviews based on the WHO Oral Health Questionnaire for Adults. Calibrated examiners performed the examinations using portable dental units. Statistical analysis included t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsThe sample consisted of 230 participants with 86.1% males and 13.9% females. In the study, 98.3% of the sample had untreated decay and 99.1% of participants had DMFT score ranging from 1 to 28, with a mean score of 14.41 ± 7.89. About 25.2% of participants never cleaned their teeth using any tool, 23.5% did not use toothpaste, and 23.5% consumed soft drinks several times a day. Dental attendance, defined as being seen by a dentist at prison, within the last year was reported by 37% of participants and 15.2% never received dental care. Most participants (85.2%) reported dental pain during the last year. The study showed a statistically significant correlation between the simplified oral hygiene index and the mean decayed score (r = 0.456, P < 0.001). The mean DMFT of the sample significantly increased with advancing age (P = 0.023). Male participants (15.12 ± 7.94) demonstrated significantly higher mean DMFT score than females (10.03 ± 6.05) (P = 0.001). Similarly, significantly higher DMFT was found in Saudi (15.74 ± 7.91) versus non-Saudi participants (10.45 ± 6.41) (P < 0.001). Those who used toothpaste (12.82 ± 6.95) had significantly lower mean DMFT score than those who did not use toothpaste (19.59 ± 8.58) (P = 0.006). A statistically significant relationship was observed between soft drink consumption and caries experience (P = 0.043).ConclusionUntreated decay was highly prevalent among inmates in this study. A statistically significant correlation was found between oral hygiene and caries experience, with advancing age, male gender, Saudi nationality, and frequent soft drink consumption being significantly associated with higher caries levels. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted oral health promotion strategies and improved access to dental care within correctional facilities. Policy implications include the integration of oral health education into prison health programs and the establishment of routine dental screenings. Future research should explore the effectiveness of mobile dental services and behavioral interventions tailored to this high-risk population.

Introduction

Dental caries represents a prevalent public health concern on a global scale. It is identified as the most predominant non-communicable ailment, and as of 2019, there were 3.09 billion cases of untreated dental caries in the world [1]. This multifactorial disease results in the destruction of the hard tooth structure due to the interaction of bacteria and fermentable carbohydrates [2]. The prevalence of dental caries in Saudi Arabia (SA) is high compared to other countries, despite declining caries rates in the country [3]. The burden of dental caries is always higher in economically disadvantaged communities and underserved populations such as prison inmates, as they face challenges in maintaining oral hygiene and often struggle to receive dental care due to cultural and socioeconomic barriers [3–6]. Factors like limited availability of dental clinics, financial constraints, age-related changes, and physical limitations contribute to this issue [4].

Prison inmates live in a restricted environment that affects their medical, psychological, social, and their overall wellbeing and poor oral health is not an exception [7]. This population has a different lifestyle, with a lack of routine dental care and oral hygiene practices [8] as well as diets composed greatly of bread and rice, which collectively leads to an increased risk of dental caries [9]. Many studies highlighted the burden of oral and dental diseases among prison inmates and that the length of imprisonment affected the severity of dental caries experienced [10–17]. Good oral health requires regular oral hygiene and checkups, the use of fluoride, and a nutritious diet, which can be challenging within the prison environment [14]. On the other hand, inadequate oral hygiene habits, improper diet, smoking, substance abuse, and limited oral health awareness are commonly cited as factors contributing to the poor oral health of inmates [18, 19].

Health is a fundamental human right, nevertheless, inmates face more health issues than the general public with very little access to healthcare services and programs [7, 8]. National data and reports often do not include information on the health status of inmates, resulting in a lack of organized health and dental care services by the appropriate health authorities [7, 8, 20]. Oral health is often overlooked among inmates, and this lack of data hinders the development of evidence-based policies and preventive programs. While international studies have consistently highlighted the high prevalence of dental caries among incarcerated populations [7, 8, 11, 13, 14], limited research exists in the Middle East, particularly within the Gulf region.

This study addresses a critical gap by examining predictors of dental caries among inmates in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia and might reveal culturally specific factors that are less prominent in Western contexts. Additionally, the study underscores systemic barriers to dental care access within prisons, a challenge similarly observed in correctional facilities across the United States [16], Europe [17], and Africa [14]. By contributing region-specific data and highlighting context-specific risk factors, this research provides a foundation for culturally tailored oral health interventions within correctional settings.

The study used the Social Determinants of Health (SDH) framework to examine the impact of systemic and structural factors on oral health outcomes in incarceration [21]. It focuses on age, gender, nationality, education level, dietary environment, and healthcare access. The Health Belief Model (HBM) is used to assess individual-level behavioral predictors of dental caries, such as inmates’ perceptions of their susceptibility to dental disease, severity of problems, perceived barriers to care, and confidence in managing oral hygiene [22]. These frameworks provide a comprehensive lens for evaluating both systemic and individual contributors to dental caries. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate caries and its predictors among inmates in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia to answer the following question: What is the prevalence of dental caries in inmates, and which sociodemographic factors influence its distribution within this population?

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study is part of a sustainable annual program implemented by the College of Dentistry Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, Dammam, which targets inmates in prisons of Dammam, Khobar, Dhahran, Jubail, and AlHasa, Eastern province of Saudi Arabia. The program is conducted from January to April each year with 2 visits per week (one for a female prison and one for a male prison).

Ethical consideration

The study protocol and program content were reviewed and approved by the Deanship of Scientific Research at the Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, Dammam (IRB-2024-02-346). Details of the program and study protocol were published previously [10]. Ethical approval was granted based on the protocol fulfilling all ethical principles and that the study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declarations. Approvals to enter the prisons were received from the Ministry of Interior and prisons’ administrations after a criminal background and security check for all team members.

Study participants

The study participants were males and females detained in the prisons, and those who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study were examined and interviewed. No exclusion criteria were applied. Announcements were sent by the administration of prisons to inmates about the visit of the dental team. A list of inmates who wanted to be examined and interviewed was prepared by the prison administration. As the program aims to provide examination and treatment to all inmates, the selection of participants in the current study was not based on sample calculations but on consent by the inmates and logistic constraints.

Data collection tools and procedure

Data were collected using (1) The World Health Organization’s Oral Health Questionnaire for Adults to assess oral health practices including, dental attendance and consumption of sugary drinks [23]. (2) Oral health examination was performed according to the WHO criteria, using two well-known epidemiological indices, namely, the DMFT (Decayed, Missing due to caries, and Filled Teeth) index for dental caries [24] and simplified oral hygiene index (OHI-S) for the oral hygiene status [25]. Six surfaces (four posterior and two anterior teeth) were examined for the OHI-S. All team members had to attend training and calibration sessions. Clinical examinations were conducted by four calibrated dental examiners, and inter- and intra-examiner reliability were assessed individually using Cohen’s kappa statistics, with values not less than 0.85. Trained data recorders were responsible for the clinical data, documenting findings in real time during examinations and ensuring standardized data entry, thus enhancing the accuracy and consistency of the collected information. The examination was carried out on participants who were seated in portable dental units using headlights, a regular mouth mirror, a WHO periodontal probe, and a dental explorer, with adherence to all disinfection protocols. Following the oral examination, each participant was interviewed using a structured questionnaire. All inmates received brushing kits after the program completion regardless they were examined or not.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corporation. Calculations were performed for descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Shapiro-Wilk Test was used to test the normality of data, and accordingly parametric tests were performed. Mean scores of continuous variables (such as DMFT index, SOH index, etc.) were compared between two categories through an independent samples t-test and among three or more categories through a one-way ANOVA test. Pearson correlation assessment and multiple linear regression examination were conducted to investigate the connections between oral hygiene condition and caries occurrences. Median split was used for DMFT and OHI-S data to create low and high categories. The significance level for statistical analysis was established as 5%.

Oral hygiene behaviors

About one quarter of participants never cleaned their teeth, did not use toothpaste, and consumed soft drinks several times a day. Dental attendance within the last 12 months was reported by 37% of participants and 15.2% never received dental care (Table 1). According to Fig. 1, most participants reported using toothbrush for cleaning teeth, and this was followed by miswak and wooden toothpicks. Only 8.50% of participants used dental floss.

Caries experience

In the study, 98.3% of the sample had untreated decay and 99.1% of participants had DMFT score ranging from 1 to 28 with mean score of 14.41 ± 7.89. Missing teeth was recorded in 63.90% of participants and only 22.20% had filled teeth (Fig. 2). The mean decayed teeth of the sample was 11.35 ± 7.23, missing teeth 2.69 ± 4.26, and filled teeth 0.37 ± 0.91.

The prevalence of zero caries was 0.9% of the study population. About 13% of participants had a DMFT score of 0–5 (low risk), 24.4% had a score of 6–10 (moderate risk), 21.7% had a score 11–15 (high risk), and 40.9% had a score of 16–28 (very high risk), indicating that most participants experienced significant caries (Fig. 3).

Predictors of caries

The mean DMFT score significantly increased with advancing age (P = 0.023). Male participants (15.12 ± 7.94) demonstrated significantly higher mean DMFT score than females (10.03 ± 6.05) (P = 0.001). Similarly, significantly higher DMFT was found in Saudi (15.74 ± 7.91) versus non-Saudi participants (10.45 ± 6.41) (P < 0.001). Significantly higher mean decayed teeth was found in males (P < 0.001), Saudis (P < 0.001), and participants without formal education (P = 0.037). Older adults and participants with medical conditions exhibited significantly higher mean missing teeth than adults and younger adults (P < 0.001) and those without medical conditions (P = 0.001). The mean filled teeth were significantly higher among females (P = 0.034) and college/university educated participants (P = 0.006). Significantly lower mean score of Simplified oral hygiene index (SOHI) was found in females than males (P = 0.007) (Table 2).

Relationship between caries experience and oral hygiene status among study participants

The association of study variables with increased caries experience (high DMFT) was assessed in bivariate and multivariable analyses. Bivariate analysis included age, gender, education, nationality, years in prison, teeth cleaning, use of fluoride toothpaste, consumption of soft drinks, and simplified oral hygiene index score. The study variables, such as age, gender, nationality, teeth cleaning, use of fluoride toothpaste, and simplified oral hygiene index score showed significant association with increased caries experience in bivariate analysis and were included in the multiple logistic regression backward model. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values. All VIFs were below 2, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a concern in this model. The age (OR = 0.36, P = 0.004), nationality (OR = 2.21, P = 0.026), teeth cleaning (OR = 3.24, P = 0.001), and simplified oral hygiene index score/poor oral hygiene (OR = 1.84, P = 0.035) remained significantly associated with increased caries experience (Table 5).

Discussion

Globally, there is a lack of continuous monitoring of oral and dental problems among prison inmates which raises more concerns about the impact on their general health and wellbeing. This study evaluated caries experience and its predictors among inmates in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia as a part of a sustainable oral health promotion program which is the first to be established in the kingdom and in the Gulf area. The study found a high prevalence of untreated dental caries, which was significantly correlated with poor oral hygiene and past caries experience, highlighting the need for continuous oral health education and promotion within prisons.

The mean DMF score in the current study was 14.41 ± 7.89, considerably higher than studies conducted worldwide, for example from India [26], Korea [27], South Africa [28] Finland [29] and Kosovo [30]. While a higher DMF score (17.17 ± 8.23) was observed in prison inmates in Portugal [31]. The current study also found that less than a quarter of the participants had their teeth filled, which indicates the unavailability of dental treatments in prisons. This observed difference in DMFT scores in the current study may be due to dietary patterns, access to fluoridated water, dental care services, and prison healthcare standards. Cultural practices and limited preventive dental services may also have contributed to higher scores. The findings support the World Health Organization’s recommendation for integrated oral health strategies in prisons, contributing to more equitable care for incarcerated populations. Dental caries is a multifactorial disease that reflects the interplay between host factors, bacterial, and unhealthy dietary habits in addition to poor oral health behaviors, which are usually aggravated in strict environments such as prisons. These results can be utilized for assessing present public health efforts and for directing upcoming programs and policies implemented within prison settings. The outcomes could also establish a structure for continuous and forthcoming public health monitoring initiatives in the country.

In the current study, the mean DMFT score significantly increased with advancing age similar to what was reported by Balkrishna et al. [32] Another study also identified age as a risk factor for poor oral health, especially among inmates [33]. Similarly, aging was proposed as one of the major driving factors for common non-communicable diseases in modern-day society [34]. The association between age and dental caries was found to be more prominent in older individuals [35]. The increased prevalence of dental caries among participants in the current study in older individuals can be attributed to the cumulative exposure to risk factors and past caries experience. Older individuals are also resistant to behavioral change [36], which makes them benefit less from oral health education and interventions.

In our study we found that male participants demonstrated significantly higher mean DMFT score than females, contradicting the findings by Balkrishna et al. [32], but in line with what was reported by Zajmi et al. [30]. A recent review concluded that males were more likely to ignore their oral health, have poorer oral hygiene habits, have irregular dental visits, and experience higher rates of periodontal disease, oral cancer, and dental trauma. On the other hand, more positive attitudes about dental visits, greater oral health literacy, and better oral health behaviors were observed among females [37].These gender differences could be further explained by body structure and function, immune system, and sex hormones [37], which highlights the need for gender-specific oral health interventions and promotional programs to improve oral health and reduce gender-related disparities. The findings of the current study that male participants, Saudi nationals, and older inmates have higher DMFT scores can be related to behavioral, socioeconomic, and systemic factors [8]. Similarly, older inmates accumulate more dental disease due to risk factors and limited restorative care [28]. In the same context, Saudi nationals may have different dietary habits and healthcare-seeking behaviors compared to other nationalities [6]. From a social determinants of health perspective, factors such as nationality and gender influence caries risk through differences in socioeconomic background, health literacy, cultural practices, and access to care [21]. In a prison context, these determinants may affect diet, hygiene resources, and healthcare utilization, thereby shaping oral health outcomes.

The Health Belief Model explains the influence of oral hygiene behaviors through individual perceptions—how inmates assess their susceptibility to caries, the seriousness of its consequences, and the benefits versus barriers of regular oral hygiene. For example, inadequate perceived risk or low self-efficacy in maintaining oral care under prison conditions can result in poorer hygiene practices and higher caries prevalence [22]. The present study showed a statistically significant relationship between teeth cleaning and increased caries experience. Inmates who never cleaned their teeth demonstrated the highest caries experience, which decreased progressively among participants who cleaned monthly, weekly, once daily, and twice daily. In addition, logistic regression analysis revealed a statistically significant relationship between poor oral hygiene and dental caries experience among our participants. The literature shows an established relationship between tooth brushing and caries experience, highlighting the vital role of oral hygiene habits in dental caries prevention [38, 39]. Geleto et al. reported that participants who did not brush their teeth were 3.5 times more likely to have caries than those who brushed their teeth [38]. Similarly, Oscarson et al. showed a statistically significant relationship between brushing twice a day and caries free status [39]. It is known that tooth brushing is an effective method of removing dental plaque and sugary food debris from teeth, thus preventing microbiota from colonizing teeth and producing acid to cause demineralization, leading to caries [40].

Toothpaste is one of the methods of supplying fluoride to the tooth structure and preventing caries and improving the oral health of individuals [41]. Moreover, the global drop in caries prevalence over the past few decades is attributed to the use of fluoridated toothpaste in addition to water fluoridation and positive oral health behaviours [40, 42]. A previous study by Bukhari et al. did not find a significant difference in the use of toothpaste between inmates and the comparison group in Saudi Arabia [43]. Despite this, improved oral health demonstrated through significantly reduced caries experience was found in our sample of participants who used toothpaste compared to those who did not use it. According to a systematic review, the use of fluoride toothpaste reduces decayed, missing, and filled surface increments in adults, therefore highlighting the importance of fluoride toothpaste in the prevention of caries [44].

Additionally, a recent study reported that regular toothpaste use often indicates greater awareness of oral hygiene, more consistent brushing habits, and potentially a higher likelihood of engaging in other preventive behaviors such as dietary moderation and regular dental care, all of which contribute to better oral health outcomes [45].

Dental literature consistently reports increased caries experience associated with the consumption of dietary sugars [39, 46, 47]. Kmietowicz suggested that sugar should be reduced to 3% of total daily energy intake to lower the risk of caries [47]. In the present study, the frequency of soft drink consumption was significantly associated with increased caries experience. These results are in accordance with the findings of a previous study, which reported a high prevalence of caries among individuals who consumed soft drinks [39]. Another study reported 3.65 greater likelihood of caries among participants who consumed sugar [38]. Conversely, Soares et al. reported high consumption of sugary food among inmates in Portugal, but found no statistically significant relationship between caries and sugar consumption [31]. Oral microorganisms use sugars and other fermentable carbohydrates as a substrate. This bacterial activity lowers the pH of saliva and plaque and increases acidity, leading to the development of carious lesions [46]. Saudi prisons can be influenced by various factors including nationality, gender, cultural, religious, and systemic factors. Cultural dietary habits increase sugar exposure, while religious practices can be protective. Gender norms and institutional practices can also affect access to oral hygiene resources, creating risk profiles for male and female inmates [7, 8]. Systemic barriers such as limited dental workforce, lack of portable dental units, and overcrowding hinder timely diagnosis and treatment [4–6, 11, 13]. On the other hand, language barriers, lower health literacy, and limited access to dental services further limit oral healthcare for non-Saudi inmates [6–8]. Viewed through the Social Determinants of Health framework, these factors highlight structural inequities, while the Health Belief Model underscores how cultural and environmental contexts shape inmates’ oral hygiene.

The current study has certain limitations. First, its cross-sectional design makes it difficult to draw any causal inference, in addition to concerns about sampling bias from self-selection of participants as healthier or more interested inmates may have volunteered to be examined. Second, some of the collected information was based on inmates’ reporting and may therefore be subject to social desirability and reporting biases. Thirdly, even though the examiners were effectively trained and calibrated, it was important to agree on the number of surfaces to classify as previously decayed in a tooth undergoing extraction when using the DMF index, especially in studies addressing oral health disparities. Given that the highest value is expected to result in an overestimation of disparities, whereas omitting the ‘M’ component would exclude a crucial aspect of inequalities [48]. Also, the lack of a portable dental unit limited the quality of clinical examinations, potentially leading to underdiagnosis of caries. This may have resulted in an underestimation of caries prevalence and affected the accuracy of associations with predictive factors. In the same context, the small proportion of female inmates (13.9% of the sample) limited gender-specific analyses, potentially underrepresenting female oral health patterns and needs. Lastly, there are factors related to the prison environment itself, such as ethical concerns about the inmates (being a vulnerable population), accessibility, and the ability to implement rigorous evaluation measures. Also, it is likely that studies investigating or reporting human behaviors in prisons to be subjected to Hawthorne effect [49].

In Saudi Arabia, the Ministry of Health has successfully deployed mobile health clinics to serve remote populations, reducing the need for permanent infrastructure and lowering operational costs [50]. Adapting these mobile units to serve prisons could offer a scalable, cost-effective approach to delivering dental care. These units can rotate between facilities, ensuring continuity of care while maintaining manageable resource allocation. Such models align with the Saudi Vision 2030 goals of equitable healthcare access and could significantly improve oral health outcomes among prison inmates. Beyond the establishment of dental clinics, health promotion and preventive strategies play a critical role in improving oral health among inmates. Interventions such as structured oral health education sessions, peer-led workshops, and routine oral hygiene instruction can be implemented with minimal resources [51]. Additionally, incorporating oral health into broader health literacy programs and training prison staff to reinforce key behaviors can have a lasting impact. Such preventive approaches align with public health priorities and represent sustainable, cost-effective measures to reduce the burden of dental disease in prisons [51].

Conclusion

This study showed that untreated dental caries is high among inmates. A statistically significant correlation was observed between oral hygiene and caries experience. Advancing age, male gender, Saudi nationality, and consumption of soft drinks were significantly related to higher caries experience. Governmental authorities should enhance oral health in prisons through scalable strategies such as mobile dental units with regular visits, preventive and promotive measures, and structured education programs supported by ongoing monitoring and evaluation. Ministry of Health should consider the integration of dental care into national prison health programs. These measures are feasible and cost-effective in the long term, reducing the burden of advanced dental disease and emergency care. Future research should be conducted to identify risk indicators over time, potentially using prospective or case-control study designs. It is also crucial to utilize oral epidemiological studies to determine the oral health of inmates in order to inform policy creation and decision-making.
