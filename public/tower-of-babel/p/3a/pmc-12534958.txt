Inhibitory effects of berberine on Fonsecaea monophora in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Liya He, Yong Zhu, Xin Mei, Bing Yu, Sha Lu, Dongdong Mo, Ruiqiang Fan, Xinsheng Chen, Liyan Xi, Ting Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChromoblastomycosis (CBM) is a chronic and difficult-to-treat infection caused by dematiaceous fungi, primarily affecting the skin and subcutaneous tissues. In Southern China, Fonsecaea monophora is recognized as a predominant pathogen agent of CBM cases. Effective therapeutic strategies remain limited for this mycosis. However, berberine, a bioactive compound extracted from various traditional medicinal herbs, has attracted considerable attention due to its potent antimicrobial properties.MethodsThe study evaluated the antifungal efficacy of berberine against F. monophora through a series of in vitro studies, including antifungal susceptibility testing and time-kill curve analysis. Moreover, a Galleria mellonella infection model of F. monophora was also established for the comparative analysis of berberine’s efficacy.ResultsBerberine exhibited inhibitory activity against all tested strains. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of berberine against F. monophora strains ranged from 16 to 64 µg/mL, with a geometric mean of 49.74 µg/mL. In the Galleria mellonella model, berberine treatment significantly prolonged the survival time of infected larvae.ConclusionThese findings suggest that berberine has the potential, as a complementary treatment, for CBM. However, the current research is limited to in-vivo and preliminary in-vitro model involving a small number of fungal strains. Therefore, further validations through additional studies, such as involving murine model and other advanced experimental systems, will be necessary. Keywords: Berberine, Fonsecaea monophora, Chromoblastomycosis, Antifungal drug, Galleria mellonella

Introduction

Chromoblastomycosis (CBM) is a chronic disease emanating from subcutaneous, deep-seated fungal infection. It is caused by the pathogenic dematiaceous fungi that invade and cause granulomatous inflammation of the skin and subcutaneous tissues [1]. As previously noted, traumatic environmental exposure during travel and implantation from surrounding sources is a common etiological factor for CBM, which is considered to be a disease primarily located in tropical and subtropical regions [2]. In southern China, the Fonsecaea species are more prevalent, with Fonsecaea monophora being the most commonly identified pathogen [3]. The treatment of CBM remains a significant clinical challenge, necessitating long-term systemic administration of antifungal drugs, often used in combination. This problem is exacerbated by low cure rates, high recurrence rates, prolonged treatment durations (ranging from six months to two years or even longer), and negative reactions to systemic antifungal drugs [4]. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic agents or strategies is urgently needed.

Berberine is an almost colorless quaternary ammonium salt that is obtained from the herb Coptis chinensis, a staple in traditional Chinese medicine. Owing to its diverse range of pharmacological and biological actions, it exhibits marked antimicrobial, antitumor, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic effects [5]. More recent studies have demonstrated the inhibitory impact of berberine on several pathogenic fungi like Candida albicans [6], Trichosporon asahii [7], Aspergillus fumigatus [8], Trichophyton mentagrophytes [9], Microsporum canis [10], and Talaromyces marneffei [11]. So far, no studies have been reported on inhibition of F. monophora with berberine alkaloid.

The study has evaluated the inhibiting effects of berberine on F. monophora through in vitro experiments. Moreover, it has explored the impacts of berberine on F. monophora in vivo by constructing an infection model of Galleria mellonella.

Materials and methods

Strains media and preparation of the inoculum

This study has retrieved a total of 11 F. monophora strains from the Department of Dermatology of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. Among them, 10 patients’ isolate strains from China were obtained alongside F. monophora strain CBS 296.37 (Table 1). The identification of these strains was achieved through comprehensive analysis of molecular sequencing data, targeting the ITS region of the ribosomal DNA [12]. For control purposes, Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22,019 was used. After cultivating the clinical strains on Sabouraud’s glucose agar (SGA) and Potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 26 °C for 2 weeks, the cells were harvested. After harvesting the cells, they were suspended in sterile water and vortexed. Cells were counted after serial dilution in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) using a hemocytometer.

Agents

The agents included fluconazole (FLZ), itraconazole (ITZ), amphotericin B (AMB), terbinafine (TRB), and berberine (BBR). All of them were obtained as pure powders from Macklin, China.Prepare stock solutions by dissolving ITZ, AMB, and TRB in 100% DMSO at a concentration of 1600 µg/mL. Prepare BBR separately at 20 mg/mL in 100% DMSO. FLZ was made in sterile distilled water. After dilution in the RPMI 1640 medium, these stock solutions were further serially diluted twofold. Finally, the strength of solution for the experiment four times was obtained.

Antifungal susceptibility test

A broth microdilution protocol of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M38-A2 with modifications was employed for evaluating the antifungal susceptibility of berberine on F. monophora. In addition, 96-well tissue culture plates (Corning, USA), which were incubated for five days with the temperature of 35 °C, were utilized for the antifungal susceptibility testing. Briefly, in the RPMI 1640 medium, the initial concentration of F. monophora meant 0.5–5.0 × 104 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL, and the final concentration ranged from 0.5 to 1024 µg/mL for BBR, 0.0625–128 µg/mL for FLZ and AMB, 0.0078–16 µg/mL for ITZ and TRB. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of FLZ, ITZ, AMB, TRB, and BBR were determined in accordance with the CLSI M38-A2 protocol [13]. These tests were performed three times using quality control C. parapsilosis ATCC 22,019.

Time-kill curve analysis of berberine on F. monophora

To further investigate the antifungal activity of berberine against F. monophora, the next research objective involved conducting time-kill curve studies in RPMI 1640 medium buffered with MOPS as previously mentioned [14]. Cultures without the drug served as negative controls. The final concentrations were 32, 64, and 128 µg/mL for BBR, 0.5 µg/mL for ITZ, and 4.0 × 104 CFU/mL for F. monophora (strain CBS 296.37). Cultures were shaken continuously at 35 °C after different treatments. From each suspension, 50 µL aliquots were collected at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. These samples were diluted in sterile phosphate balanced solution (PBS). After incubating 50 µL of the diluted fungal suspension on PDA plates at 35 °C for 5 days, colonies were counted.

Influence of berberine on Galleria mellonellamodel of F. monophora infection

For the study, larvae with weights between 250 and 300 mg were meticulously selected, with each group consisting of 20 individuals. These larvae were kept in the dark and at a constant temperature of 16 °C until the start of the experiment. As described previously, the infection processes followed the established protocols [15]. Each larva was inoculated with 10 µl PBS or a conidia suspension (F. monophora type strain CBS 296.37) containing a density of 1 × 107 per ml via the last left proleg. Subsequently, the larvae were administered a 10 µL injection into the last left proleg of either PBS, BBR at 64 µg/mL, BBR at 128 µg/mL, or itraconazole (ITZ) at 0.5 µg/mL. It should be noted that the control group was not infected with fungi and received no drug treatment; the model group was infected with fungi and received no drug treatment. After inoculation, the larvae were transferred to plastic containers and maintained at a constant temperature of 35 °C. They were subjected to daily observation for a period of 10 days. The larvae were considered dead and removed after exhibiting no response to touch stimulation. This experiment was performed three times on different days.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses in this study were conducted using SPSS 22.0 software. The data are presented as mean values with standard deviations (SD) as error bars. When there were more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was used. For the statistical analysis, the graphs pertaining to the survival rates of the larvae were created using GraphPad prism 6 software (GraphPad software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Moreover, these survival data were tested against Kaplan-Meier survival curves with log-rank comparisons. Statistically significant differences were defined as values of P < 0.05.

Results

Initially, we evaluated the MIC values of BBR on the strains of F. monophora, including the quality control C. parapsilosis ATCC 22,019. In every instance, berberine had an inhibitory effect to some degree on all of the strains tested. As shown in Table 1, the MIC of BBR for the strains of F. monophora was from 16 to 64 µg/mL. It was calculated that the geometric mean of these MICs was 49.74 µg/mL. As a quality control strain, we obtained a MIC value of 16 to 32 µg/mL for berberine against C. parapsilosis ATCC 22,019. Furthermore, the dynamic antifungal effects of the drug could be observed through the time-kill curve assays for F. monophora CBS 296.37 strain (Fig. 1). From these results, BBR had potent antifungal activity against F. monophora.

Time–kill curves of F. monophora type strain CBS 296.37

We also studied the inhibitory impact berberine has on G. mellonella infected with F. monophora. For the laboratory group without drug administration, all experimental larvae died by day 8. However, in the case of BBR 64 µg/mL dose group, noted survival rate by day 10 was a remarkable 45%. Further increasing the dose to 128 µg/mL of BBR improved survival rate by the same period to 55%. At the same time, the group treated with ITZ at a 0.5 µg/mL dose showed a 30% survival rate by day 10 (Fig. 2). The results demonstrated that berberine treatment enhanced the survival rate of G. mellonella infected with F. monophora. Analysis showed overall, there was a highly significant difference in model group and both BBR treatment groups regarding surviving count per each group (P = < 0.001 for both the 64 µg/mL and 128 µg/mL BBR dose groups). However, the difference in survival rates among these groups and ITZ 0.5 µg/ml group was not significant.

Discussion

Chromoblastomycosis is a fungal infection that indeed poses numerous challenges to clinicians in its treatment [16]. The intricate treatment procedure is sometimes challenging since this condition needs long-term care and the fungus is resistant to several antifungal medications. Furthermore, the prolonged utilization of antifungal drugs may precipitate adverse reactions in patients, exacerbating the risks and complexities associated with treatment [17]. Given these challenges, the pursuit of innovative pharmaceuticals and therapeutic approaches becomes increasingly important. Traditional Chinese medicine has a long history in the study of antifungal effects. Some traditional Chinese medicines have already demonstrated significant clinical efficacy [18]. Researchers have found that berberine has inhibitory effects on a variety of pathogenic fungi, including C. albicans [6], T. asahii [7], A. fumigatus [8], and T. mentagrophytes [9]. This investigation established the MIC of berberine against F. monophora to be between 16 and 64 µg/mL. This discovery corresponds with the MIC values recorded for berberine against many pathogenic funguses.

Specifically, the MIC for T. asahii spans from 32 to 128 µg/mL [7], while for A. fumigatus, the range is broader, from 4 to 256 µg/mL [8]. Similarly, the MIC for T. marneffei was noted to be between 32 and 64 µg/mL [11]. Based on these findings, berberine has the potential in serving as antifungal agent with a consistent spectrum of activity across various fungal species. Furthermore, the research has established that berberine has substantial inhibitory effects on F. monophora both in vitro and in vivo, a finding that has not been previously documented.

The time-kill curve experiments provide insight into the interaction between berberine and fungal cells over time, aiding in dose and treatment duration decisions. In this study, we observed that the MIC of berberine, when used alone, was significantly higher than that of conventional antifungal drugs such as ITZ and TRB, revealing its limited efficacy as a standalone treatment for chromoblastomycosis. Although previous research has reported synergistic inhibitory effects when berberine is combined with other antifungal drugs against fungi like C. albicans [19], Our study did not reveal a synergistic effect when berberine was combined with ITZ or TRB against F. monophora in vitro. Checkerboard assays, performed in accordance with CLSI M38-A2 guidelines, demonstrated no synergy between berberine and either ITZ or TRB against F. monophora, as evidenced by fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) indices greater than 0.5. To more effectively harness the antifungal potential of berberine, future research should focus on molecular-level modification and structural optimization to enhance its clinical efficacy.

Berberine shows in vitro antifungal activity against F. monophora with an MIC of 16–64 µg/mL. However, its therapeutic potential is limited due to poor pharmacokinetics. Animal studies reveal low systemic exposure after oral administration; In normal rats administered berberine intragastrically at a dose of 120 mg/kg, the peak plasma concentrations (Cmax) barely reaches 32.6 ng/mL [20], which is insufficient to inhibit F. monophora. This is mainly because berberine has extremely low oral bioavailability (< 1%), caused by poor absorption and strong first-pass metabolism [21]. As a result, conventional oral dosing can’t achieve therapeutic plasma concentrations. To address skin and subcutaneous infections, it is necessary to develop localized delivery systems like nanoformulations and topical formulations [22].Additionally, the experimental work of this study utilized the G. mellonella infection model for in vivo experiments, which has gained increasing attention due to its innate immune response similarities to mammals. The circulating hemocytes in the hemolymph of G. mellonella possess the ability to immobilize and kill microorganisms, making them valuable in microbiological research [23]. Although the G. mellonella model is relatively new in application, its potential in the study of infectious diseases and pathogen virulence has been widely recognized [24, 25]. Our experimental observations provided strong in vivo experimental evidence for the antifungal activity of berberine by proving that berberine played an important part in prolonging the survival time of G. mellonella larvae infected with F. monophora. Moreover, we observed black melanization regions on the cuticle of larvae following fungal infection. These regions are not only indicative of host immune activation but also serve as a visual marker reflecting infection severity and treatment efficacy. However, the current lack of standardized criteria for evaluating melanization in larvae limits the ability to precisely compare the effectiveness of different treatments. Future research could incorporate image analysis technology or develop unified visual scoring standards to quantify melanized regions. This would allow for a deeper investigation into the relationship between melanization, infection severity, and treatment efficacy. Additionally, exploring the commonalities and differences in melanization across various insect species can enhance our understanding of insect immune mechanisms.

While our study demonstrates berberine’s efficacy, the mechanism of action against F. monophora remains unclear. Based on established knowledge, the disruption of fungal cell membrane integrity and function [26], potentially involving ergosterol [27], stands as a compelling candidate mechanism worthy of future investigation. However, the findings are currently limited to non-mammalian systems, a small number of fungal strains were evaluated, and the underlying mechanism of action remains unexplored. Further validation using murine models and advanced experimental systems is deemed necessary and justified to address these primary limitations.

Conclusion

The observed MIC range indicates that berberine holds promise as an antifungal agent against F. monophora infections, supporting its potential inclusion in targeted treatment strategies. The G. mellonella infection model was shown to be a useful and dependable in vivo system for assessing antifungal efficacy, further confirming its usefulness in preclinical testing of novel compounds. These results also demonstrate the therapeutic potential of berberine and lay the groundwork for future studies into its development as part of antifungal therapy.
