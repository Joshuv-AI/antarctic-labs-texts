The evolving landscape of neuromodulation for pain care
Authors: Jean-Pascal Lefaucheur, Yinghui Fan, Bifa Fan, Perianen Ramasawmy, Andrea Antal, Dimos D. Mitsikostas, Cecile De Vos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Here, we present five different insights on the use of neuromodulation strategies for effective pain management in clinical practice. Experts briefly illustrate the various techniques available and the complexities involved in evaluating their effectiveness. Additionally, they highlight the challenges for widespread adoption in clinical practice and explore some future directions. These viewpoints underscore the evolving landscape of neuromodulation in pain management and the ongoing efforts to optimize these treatments for diverse patient needs.

Main text

Which neuromodulation strategies are currently used for effective pain management in clinics?

Jean-Pascal LefaucheurClinical Neurophysiology Unit, Paris-Est Creteil University

Jean-Pascal Lefaucheur

Clinical Neurophysiology Unit, Paris-Est Creteil University

To treat chronic pain, invasive (surgically implanted) or non-invasive neuromodulation techniques can be proposed. At the cerebral level, the use of invasive approaches (cortical “motor” or deep brain targets) remains limited in clinical practice. Non-invasive brain stimulation, in particular, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, is currently more developed, but the need for iterative stimulation sessions is a major constraint. At the spinal level, the most common approach is invasive spinal cord stimulation (SCS), including various paradigms, which may or may not trigger paresthesias. At the peripheral level, it also mainly concerns non-invasive approaches, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation and various stimulation techniques at the cranial level (supraorbital, occipital, or transauricular stimulation). Brain stimulation can be proposed to treat diffuse pain syndromes of central neuropathic origin (post stroke or spinal cord injury) or non-neuropathic origin (fibromyalgia). Spinal cord stimulation can be proposed to treat peripheral neuropathic pain in the limbs (persistent spinal pain syndromes, neuropathies, or complex regional pain syndrome). Peripheral nerve stimulation can be proposed to treat focal pain, particularly of neuropathic origin, but also visceral pain (abdominal pain due to menstruation) or migraine and other headaches. Neuromodulation techniques certainly represent a major advance for the treatment of chronic pain in clinical practice, combining efficacy and a much lower risk of side effects than medications.

What factors influence the effectiveness of SCS on its own or alongside other pain management methods?

Yinghui Fan and Bifa FanShanghai Jiao Tong University and China-Japan Friendship Hospital

Yinghui Fan and Bifa Fan

Shanghai Jiao Tong University and China-Japan Friendship Hospital

The overall effect of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) comes from the summation of spatial and temporal influences, similar to acupuncture. Mild electrical pulses stimulate the nervous network producing analgesia, accompanied by complex activation involving the autonomic nervous system and the cortex. The superiority of a novel stimulation system is often reported in comparison to conventional medical management, which may result in decreased opioid requirement.

Outcome measures in pain clinical trials depend on subjective evaluations. Non-drug treatment, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, might improve treatment satisfaction and promote coping skills against negative pain experiences. Synergistically, the placebo effect and the potential anti-depressive role of SCS may contribute to pain alleviation. Understanding the relative effectiveness of SCS in relieving pain is inherently complex.

Whether supraspinal mechanisms of SCS would impact the brain’s reward system remains elusive. Neurotransmitters, such as endorphins and serotonin, could be modulated by SCS and differentially regulated by the activity of the opioid receptors. The success or failure of SCS therapy can be affected by cognitive disorders or substance addictions. Reducing opioid use does not necessarily indicate pain relief, nor does removing the stimulation system signify treatment failure. A more holistic view of the pain pathways would help clarify these complexities. Nevertheless, closed-loop SCS systems with wireless remote programming, which adjust stimulation in real time based on body feedback, are transforming clinical practice. Exploring and revealing neural decoding will likely lead to the development of more effective strategies for pain management.

What are the main challenges of using neurostimulation for pain management in clinical practice, and how can they be addressed?

Perianen Ramasawmy and Andrea AntalUniversity Medical Center Göttingen

Perianen Ramasawmy and Andrea Antal

University Medical Center Göttingen

While non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) techniques such as transcranial direct current stimulation and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation hold promise in chronic pain management with fewer adverse effects than medication, there are some challenges impeding their successful implementation in clinical practice. Using standardized stimulation protocols for all chronic pain patients, independently from the type of pain, may be suboptimal due to differences in brain anatomy, neurophysiological state, and pain severity across individuals. Developing reliable predictive biomarkers to more accurately identify patients likely to respond to NIBS would enable more targeted and effective use of these therapies in pain relief. Chronic pain patients frequently experience comorbid conditions like cardiovascular diseases and psychiatric disorders, which require adjunct medications that may interfere with mechanisms underlying NIBS-induced analgesia, potentially modifying treatment efficacy. Daily NIBS application over weeks limits treatment accessibility and places a burden on patients and clinical staff. At-home neurostimulation under remote supervision could address this issue. To sustain the benefits of NIBS in pain relief over time, clinicians should plan the therapy, including individualized maintenance sessions, based on patient response and recommended clinical guidelines. Addressing these challenges through personalized approaches and ongoing research will be crucial for optimizing NIBS as a chronic pain management tool in clinical practice.

How accessible are migraine neurostimulation treatments, and what barriers exist to their widespread adoption?

Dimos D. MitsikostasAeginition Hospital, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens

Dimos D. Mitsikostas

Aeginition Hospital, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens

Several neuromodulation techniques targeting central or peripheral sites of the trigeminal vasculature have been tested for the symptomatic and prophylactic treatment of migraine, leading to the commercialization of a number of devices: gammaCore, Nerivio Migra, NEMOS, VITOS, TNM, sTMS, CEFALY, etc. All share good safety profiles and moderate-to-good evidence for efficacy and merit alternative treatment options along with physical and bio-behavioral therapy when pharmacological treatments are conflicting, poorly tolerated, or unwanted by people with migraine. However, there are some barriers to their use in practice, particularly because of reluctance by professionals and people with migraine to use both. Another significant barrier is the limited evidence of efficacy due to poor study design. For example, blinding in trials investigating the use of neuromodulation devices was imperfect, and participants may have been able to predict the treatment they received. In addition, most of these trials were non-inferiority studies; thus, prioritizing safety and tolerability, but not efficacy outcomes, as trials of superiority over placebo is usually done in pharmacological treatment studies, hindering reimbursement approval. In Europe, trials with neuromodulation devices are not reviewed by the European Medicines Agency and are not officially licensed to treat medical conditions, further reinforcing payer exclusion. Because of these limitations, most of the aforementioned devices are not covered by government or private insurances. Thus, the accessibility to neuromodulation techniques for management of migraine is poor because of personal preferences and of the cost of use.

What are the future directions in the field of neurostimulation for pain management?

Cecile De VosCenter for Pain Medicine, Erasmus University Medical Center

Cecile De Vos

Center for Pain Medicine, Erasmus University Medical Center

Increasingly, clinical studies on pain treatments focus on including a wider range of relevant outcome measures, like quality of life and patient satisfaction, to more comprehensively assess the treatment’s effects.

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is the most common application of neuromodulation for the treatment of chronic pain. Generally, successful treatment has been achieved in over 60% of patients across studies on several major pain conditions. Until now, most studies on SCS, as well as reimbursement criteria in most countries, define treatment success as a minimum of 50% reduction in self-reported pain intensity. However, by focusing solely on self-reported pain intensity as the outcome parameter, researchers and practitioners neglect other important aspects of SCS treatment effects. This approach fails to capture the complexity of chronic pain and a patient’s overall health status. Moreover, significant physiological effects of SCS treatment are missed.

The working mechanisms of SCS are still not fully understood and different stimulation settings may involve different pathways. Therefore, including objective physiological outcome measures alongside pain reduction is key to better understanding the effects and mechanisms of SCS. For instance, in patients with complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) treated with SCS, vasomotor disturbances have been quantified using infrared thermography. Additionally, quantitative sensory testing has shown a reduction in allodynia and hyperalgesia in CRPS patients, as well as restored sensation in patients with painful diabetic polyneuropathy.

I hope and expect that objective physiological outcome measures will play a more prominent role in future studies, guiding us to further optimize neuromodulation treatments for pain of various etiologies.
