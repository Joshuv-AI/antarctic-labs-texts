Sequential reagents release from layered effervescent tablets for simplifying multi-step sample preparation and extraction of metal ions from environmental samples
Authors: Vasiliki Aggeli, Efthimia Toti, Dimosthenis L Giokas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSample preparation typically involves multiple steps to create conditions for separating analytes from the sample matrix. These steps involve the preparation of reagents and materials, their addition into the sample, mixing by external energy input, and timing of each step. All these steps increase analysis time, reduce sample throughput, and require benchtop laboratory equipment and trained personnel. In this work, we report layered effervescent tablets to simplify multistep sample preparation. The tablets consist of layers that dissolve sequentially at a controlled rate, releasing stored, pre-quantified reagents at specific time intervals. As a proof-of-concept demonstration, we designed tablets for the extraction of copper ions, as model analyte, from aqueous solutions. The tablets autonomously perform a series of steps that involve adjustment of pH, dissolution and dispersion of a chelating agent, incubation, mixing and extraction of the metal chelates in a magnetic sorbent by the action of effervescence. The preliminary analytical application of the method to the determination of copper in environmental samples revealed analytical features, which are comparable to standard laboratory practices (recoveries > 80%, RSD < 8%). In this manner it is demonstrated that layered effervescent tablets can be a promising tool for simplifying multistep sample preparation by reducing reliance on human and technical resources and allowing extractions to be performed with minimal intervention and basic infrastructure.Graphical abstract Keywords: Simplified sample preparation, Autonomous reagent release, Effortless extraction, Heavy metals

Introduction

Extraction is an integral part of many sample preparation procedures employed in environmental, food, and biochemical testing [1, 2]. Typically, extraction is performed by adding an extraction medium (solid or liquid), mixing with the sample to facilitate the mass transfer of the analytes from the sample to the extraction medium, collection of the extraction medium and elution of the extracted analytes for their introduction into the appropriate detector [1, 2]. On the basis of this principle, a plethora of extraction and microextraction methods employing dispersed or immobilized extraction phases have been reported that accomplish to streamline the extraction process, improve mass transfer between the sample and the extraction medium, reduce extraction time and facilitate the elution of the analytes [3–9].

Before extraction, however, several initial steps must be performed to modulate the physicochemical conditions of the sample and modify the properties of the analytes to facilitate their extraction. These steps may include the adjustment of pH and ionic strength, the addition of reagents for performing derivatization or complexation reactions, the addition of internal standards to reduce errors, etc. All these steps require weighting, dissolving, and pipetting of the reagents into the sample at a pre-defined sequence and mixing of each reagent for specific time periods to ensure completion of the reactions. Therefore, these steps must be performed manually by trained users in laboratory facilities. As a result, despite the large progress on extraction procedures, the initial steps for sample treatment remain a bottleneck in sample preparation because they require considerable human and technical resources.

At present, methods for the simplification of sample pretreatment steps involve manifolds that can perform these steps on-line such as flow or sequential injection analysis (FIA/SIA) devices, and automated workstations that can perform a series of steps that are suited to a specific extraction method. FIA/SIA devices, and their most advanced lab-on-valve (LOV) configurations, use peristaltic or syringe pumps to withdraw a specific volume of sample, mix it with the appropriate reagents and deliver the sample to an extraction medium [10, 11]. They also enable the on-line elution of the analytes and their delivery to the appropriate detector. This approach may alleviate the need for human intervention but it often requires relatively complex device setups that involve multiple flow streams, peristaltic pumps and mixing coils which much be precisely adjusted to perform multiple steps, in a timely manner and at a specific sequence [12–14]. Therefore, they are suited for benchtop applications and for handling one sample at a time. Automated workstations use bulk and sophisticated instruments employing robotic liquid handlers (RLH) that can accurately automate the dispensing, transporting, and mixing of liquids [15]. By integrating these devices with other devices (shakers, ultrasonicators, magnets, etc.) several steps associated with sample pretreatment and extraction can be automated [16, 17]. However, the purchase and ongoing maintenance of such apparatus is very high, and they need specialized configurations to enable their adaptation to different sample preparation procedures and workflows. Moreover, depending on the complexity of the procedure, technical limitations such as carryover contamination, challenges in achieving precise sample dilution, and difficulties in handling insoluble components have been reported [18].

In this work, we report a different approach for simplifying multistep sample preparation that does not rely on instrumental manifolds, but it is based on effervescent tablets. Effervescence reactions have been used to disperse and dilute pharmaceutical active compounds for decades and are based on a simple reaction between a carbon dioxide donor and a proton donor to produce carbon dioxide bubbles in situ in the aqueous sample solution. By exploiting this reaction, Valcarcel and co-workers developed effervescence-assisted microextraction by formulating a tablet that consisted of a sorbent medium mixed with sodium carbonate or bicarbonate and weak acids [19]. Upon addition into the aqueous sample, the effervescence reaction disperses the sorbent into the sample to accomplish the extraction of the target analytes. By this approach manual mixing is avoided, extraction is streamlined and on-site sample treatment may be facilitated [20]. Over the years, a plethora of applications exploiting the advantages of effervescence assisted microextraction have been developed. Through these studies it was also revealed that the composition of effervescence reagents imparts a significant influence on other important parameters such sample pH, ionic strength, viscosity, extraction time, etc., which may require further optimization though additional experimental steps that are customarily performed manually before extraction [21, 22]. A similar rational but without involving extraction was recently adopted by Chan and co-workers who developed tablets that can sequentially dissociate in the sample and dissolve reagents in a pre-defined and time-controlled manner [23, 24]. By this approach, multi-step colorimetric assays could be performed with minimum user intervention and laboratory resources while offering improve reagent stability, easier handling and faster turnaround times since there is no need to prepare reagents, pipette them into the sample and mix them at each step in a timely controlled manner.

Inspired by tablets designed for controlled release of reagents for performing colorimetric assays [23, 24] and effervescence-assisted microextraction [19, 20, 22], we report the development of layered effervescent tablets that enable the controlled release of reagents and materials for performing both sample preparation and extraction and showcase their application, as proof-of-concept, for the extraction of metal ions from water samples. The designed tablets are composed of three layers (outer, internal and core) which dissolve sequentially into the sample and perform a series of actions in a predefined sequence and at specific time intervals. Specifically, the outer layer serves to adjust the pH of the sample and release a chelating agent to complex metal ions. The internal layer acts as a coating that encapsulates the extraction medium and protects it from contact with water for a specific time thus, affording adequate incubation time for the chelating agent to complex metal ions. The third layer (core) contains the extraction sorbent which sorbs the metal complexes. The outer layer and the core were mixed with effervescent reagents which spontaneously generate CO2 bubbles upon contact with water, enabling the dispersion of reagents and their mixing without external energy input. Once the procedure is completed, the sorbent is collected by an external magnetic field and eluted to release the metal ions. Therefore, except for the elution of the analytes from the sorbent, which still needs to be performed manually, the procedure simplifies all other steps associated with sample treatment and extraction which are performed with pre-stored reagents, minimal user intervention and rudimentary laboratory resources and facilities.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Sodium bicarbonate, disodium hydrogen phosphate monohydrate, L(+)-ascorbic acid,, L(+)-tartaric acid, sucrose and starch were obtained by Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Ultra-high purity cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate and iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate, (99.999% trace metals basis), sodium hydroxide monohydrate (99.9% trace metals basis), HNO3, HCl, disodium hydrogen phosphate heptahydrate and high purity sodium dihydrogen phosphate (99.99%, Suprapure®) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Mg-stearate and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, M.W. 22,000 ≥ 98%, hydrolysed) were from VWR Chemicals. Stearic acid was procured from APC Pure (Cheshire, UK). Microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) powder, and sodium oleate were from Thermo Scientific. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP K15, Aver. MW 10.000) and sodium alginate were obtained from TCI Chemicals. An axially magnetized rectangular Neodymium (N42) bar magnet (3 × 3 × 20 mm thick), obtained from Magnet Expert (https://www.magnetexpert.com, Tuxford, UK), was used for the magnetic separation of the nanoparticles from solution.

Apparatus

Tablets were prepared in a stainless-steel manual tablet press using 6 mm and 12 mm dies. The determination of metal ions was performed using a GBC 932 Plus flame atomic absorption spectrometer with a Photron multielement hollow-cathode lamp. Measurements were performed in acetylene-air flame using the peak area mode at 324.7 nm for Cu2+ with D2 background correction. Powder XRD (PXRD) diffraction patterns were recorded on a Bruker D2 Phaser X-ray diffractometer (CuKα radiation, wavelength = 1.54184 Å). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and Energy Dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) were performed with a Phenom Pharos G2 Desktop FEG-SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on Cr sputtered specimens (Q150T ES Plus automatic sputter coater, Quorum Technologies Ltd). IR spectra were recorded on a Perkin Elmer Spectrum Two attenuated total reflectance-IR (ATR-IR) spectrometer. Vacuum drying of reagents and materials was performed in a MMM Vacucell 55 ECO oven.

Preparation of the tablets

Before use, all reagents and materials, except Mg-stearate, were vacuum-dried at 40 °C for 6 h to remove excess moisture. First, the core of the tablets was prepared by mixing 65 mg NaH2PO4, 35 mg Na2CO3 and 150 mg of CoFe2O4@stearic acid MNPs. The powdered reagents and material were poured into a die mold of 0.6 cm diameter and compressed. The cores were stored in a desiccator until use.

For the preparation of the tablets, the reagents comprising the bottom external layer (140 mg NaH2PO4, 70 mg Na2CO3, and 1.5 mg of sodium gluconate) were mixed and poured into a 12 mm stainless steel die and pressed. Then, the reagents formulating the bottom part of the internal layer were added as a mixture (120 mg microcrystalline cellulose (MCC), 60 mg polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and 10 mg Mg-stearate) and pressed. The tablet core was added, and the reagents of the upper internal layer (120 mg MCC, 60 mg PVP, and 10 mg Mg-stearate) were added again and compressed. Finally, the reagents of the upper external layer (140 mg NaH2PO4, 70 mg Na2CO3, and 1.5 mg of sodium gluconate) were poured on top. The die was pressed to formulate the tablet, which was ejected with the aid of the compression piston by applying gentle force. After preparation, the tablets were stored in a desiccator until use (usually for 2–3 days).

Synthesis of CoFe2O4@stearic acid magnetic nanoparticles

CoFe2O4@stearic acid magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) were prepared by alkaline hydrolysis of iron and cobalt salts in the presence of stearic acid and characterized in detail according to the procedures described in our previous work [22]. The nanoparticles were stored in a desiccator in a glass vial.

Real samples

Genuine water samples were collected in amber glass containers from the Kalamas River and Lake Pamvotis (Epirus, NW Greece). Tap water was obtained from the local water supply network, while bottled water was purchased from local stores. All samples were filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters to remove suspended solids and stored in the dark at 4 °C before analysis. Spiking of the examined metal ions was performed on the filtered samples before analysis.

Experimental procedure

To 10 mL of the water sample and a tablet was added. The external layers of the tablet induce intense effervescence for 1 min adjusting the pH of the solution and releasing the chelating agent. Afterwards, effervescence stops and an incubation period begins during which, the internal layer gradually hydrates. After 10 min, water reaches the core and effervescence is initiated dispersing the sorbent for approximately 5 min. Once the sorbent is dispersed in water, a neodymium magnet is held at the bottom of the vial to collect the magnetic sorbent and discard the aqueous sample. Then, 2 mL of 1 M HNO3 was added to elute the metal ions using vortex agitation for 5 min. The magnetic nanoparticles were collected on the vial walls with the magnet, the extract was filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane filter and aspirated into the nebulizer of flame AAS. Results and discussion

Characterization of CoFe2O4@stearic acid magnetic nanoparticles

The successful synthesis of CoFe2O4@stearic acid magnetic nanoparticles was verified with pXRD, SEM-EDX and ATR-IR analysis, which revealed similar properties to those observed in our previous work [22]. The pXRD pattern (Fig. 2a) shows index peaks at 2θ = 18.53, 30.3, 35.74, 37.26, 43.25, 57.35, 62.94, corresponding to (111) (220) (222) (311) (400) (511) and (440) Bragg reflections, which are characteristic of magnetic nanoparticles [25]. The SEM images (Fig. 2b) show the cubic shape of the nanoparticles, while EDX analysis (Fig. 2c) shows stoichiometric ratio of Co: Fe: O 1 : 1.94 : 3.52, which is in excellent agreement with the expected structure. The presence of carbon at equal percentage to Co, verifies the presence of stearic acid on the surface of the MNPs, which was further confirmed by the IR bands at 2977 cm− 1 (C-H stretching), 2860 cm− 1 (methylene mode), 1094 and 1052 cm− 1 (C-C and C-O stretching vibrations, respectively) and 1436 cm− 1 (symmetric stechting vibation of COO-) (Fig. 2d). The fact that all IR bands were weak indicates the partial coating of the magnetic nanoparticles with stearic acid. This is also evidenced by the water contact angle of 75 ± 4o, indicating that the MNPs are wettable by water; therefore, they are hydrophilic, which favors their dispersion into water.

Characterization of the CoFe2O4@stearic acid magnetic nanoparticles. (a) powder XRD pattern, (b) SEM image, (c) EDX spectra and (d) ATR-IR spectra of the nanoparticles

Finally, the stability of the nanoparticles was investigated by recording the pXRD and ATR-IR spectra after 2 months of storage at ambient conditions. Both spectra were similar to those recorded from freshly prepared nanoparticles, indicating their high stability under normal conditions.

Operational principles and design of layered effervescent tablets

To demonstrate the utility of multilayer tablets for releasing reagents and materials at specific times from sequential layers, three-layered tablets were formulated as prototypes. The main idea is that a sequential release profile from each layer will allow reagents to react with the target analytes in solution over a specific incubation time, followed by the release of the sorbent material to extract the analytes, mimicking the sample preparation steps before and during extraction. A tablet design that aligns with these considerations is shown in Fig. 3.

The external layers (white pearled layers) at the top and bottom of the tablet contain effervescence reagents and the chelating agent. The internal layer (shown transparent for improved viewing) contains polymers that dissolve in water gradually over time, and the core (black disk) contains effervescence reagents mixed with the sorbent. Pictures are not scaled to real size

According to this design, the outer layers (top and bottom) dissolve first by the action of effervescence releasing stored reagents, mixing them in the solution and adjusting the pH of the solution. The addition of effervescence reagents both at the top and bottom layers ensures uniform dispersion, eliminating the need to orient the tablet (upright or horizontal). Then, water comes in contact with the internal layer which is composed of polymers that resist immediate dissolution in water thus offering adequate incubation time for the reagents to react with the target analytes. Once the internal layer is hydrated, water reaches the core which releases the sorbent material by the action of effervescence which aids the dispersion and mixing of the sorbent without external energy input.

A significant challenge in the operation and manufacturing of such tablets is the fabrication of the internal layer because it serves (a) as a scaffold for encapsulating the core which contains the extraction sorbent, (b) as a substrate for depositing the external layers which contain the reagents necessary for extraction and (c) as a aqueous barrier layer that dissolves slowly in water, thereby mimicking the incubation time of reagents released from the outer layers. Hence, it should be rigid enough to ensure the stability of the tablets, resist immediate dissolution in water and contain inert reagents that do not react with other tablet components or the analytes. Therefore, various polymers and compounds, commonly used in tablet formulation in the pharmaceutical industry, were tested as substrates for formulating the internal layer and accomplish various dissolution times. The tested compounds (Table 1), either alone or in mixtures, enable to control the release profile of the internal layer due to their different hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity. As be seen, some of these compounds were not appropriate either because they resulted in poor tablet stability or increased layer dissolution time or caused interferences during atomic spectroscopy analysis.

Based on these observations, the tablets’ internal layers were prepared using MCC which is a hydrophilic polymer that is insoluble in water due to many intra- and inter-molecular hydrogen bonds between the individual chains of cellulose [26]. Moreover, MCC acts as a binding agent, promoting the layer’s structural integrity and improving its compressibility, both of which are important for maintaining the stability of the tablets [27, 28]. Because there is no difference in the strength of water-water and water-cellulose bonds, water can cause hydrolytic scission of the glucosidic bonds, leading to a lowering of the degree of polymerization and loss of physical stability [29]. This property makes MCC particularly useful for the intended application, as it resists immediate dissolution but it can gradually absorb water and swell, leading to its disintegration and collapse. In the compressed form of the tablet, this occurs by the appearance of cracks on the tablet surface. These cracks allow water to slowly penetrate and reach the core, initiating an effervescence reaction from the reagents stored in the core (see below). However, as these cracks were formed slowly (> 20 min), the water penetrating the internal layer and reaching the core was low. As a result, effervescence was low and consequently, the sorbent was slowly released from the core and precipitated since mixing was not intense enough to disperse the sorbent.

To mitigate this problem, and reduce MCC disintegration time, PVP was added because it absorbs more water than MCC through capillary flow action [30]. Moreover, PVP acts as a binder, improving tablet stability and increasing its hardness [31]. The relative percentage of PVP compared to MCC in the composition of the internal layer was investigated at three values (10, 30 and 50%, w/w). At 30% (w/w) of PVP the tablets exhibited good stability and reproducible disintegration after ~ 10 min which was deemed as a good compromise between incubation time and total analysis time. At higher percentage the tablet was less stable, and disintegration occurred faster due to the higher hydrophilicity of PVP, while at 10% (w/w) PVP no change in the disintegration time was observed compared to the absence of PVP. Based on these observations, the internal layer was fabricated by 0.14 g of MCC and 0.06 g of PVP. The mass of each reagent, which is crucial in determining the release rate of the layer, was determined empirically to provide full coverage of the tablet core while offering enough space in the die mold to add the external layers. However, using advanced tableting apparatus it would be possible to further adjust the mass of reagents and thereby accomplish more precise control of dissolution rate. The mass of reagents also affects the thickness of the layer. However, this can also be controlled by the compression force and may be used to modulate the dissolution time of the layer by applying a stronger compression force to closely pack the polymer powder and decrease porosity [24, 32].

Finally, the addition of a small amount (~ 5%, w/w) of Mg stearate as a lubricant was found to be necessary to lubricate the rotary press die walls. This was necessary to reduce friction between the tablet and the die’s metal surface during ejection, thereby facilitating tablet ejection from the mold [33].

Optimization of tablet composition for the extraction of metal ions

For the extraction of metal ions, that is demonstrated as proof-of-concept in this work, experiments targeting the optimization of the solution pH, the concentration of the chelating agent, the amount of sorbent, and the elution conditions were performed using 10 mL aqueous solutions containing 0.8 µg mL− 1 of Cu2+. All measurements were performed against reagents blank (solutions without metal ions to subtract any background signals from reagent impurities). For this reason, high purity reagents and metal salts were used to mitigate background signals.

The selection of the chelating agent was the first factor considered. Since no external energy is applied for mixing, and the chelating agent is present in powder form in the outer layers of the tablet it should be readily soluble in water and dissolve almost spontaneously during the effervescence without special conditions. In that regard, various water-soluble chelating agents were tested. The efficiency of these chelating agents was examined as proof-of-concept for in the extraction of Cu2+, by preparing tablets containing effervescent reagents (i.e. mixtures of acids-bases) that produce a pH as close as possible to the optimum pH for each chelating agent, as reported in previous studies [34–37]. For sodium gluconate, the pH was adjusted to 7.5 because it is known to act as a pH buffer, generating aqueous solutions with a pH of 6–8, which is the pH range with the greatest buffering capacity [38, 39]. According to these experiments (Table 2), sodium gluconate exhibited the highest extraction efficiency, possibly due to its higher water solubility compared to other chelating agents (such as dithiocarbamates, dithiophosphates, etc.). The high solubility of sodium gluconate stems from the presence of multiple hydroxyl (-OH) groups and a carboxyl (-COOH) group. These groups form strong complexes with many metal ions, including alkaline earth metals. The formation of water-soluble metal complexes precludes the formation of insoluble precipitates and ensures their homogeneous distribution in the solution, which is important to facilitate extraction without external energy input.

Following the selection of the chelating agent, the composition of effervescent reagents was investigated as means to adjust the solution pH to approximately 7.5. Three combinations of effervescence reagents were examined for that purpose, namely NaH2PO4 - Na2CO3 at a mass ratio of 2:1, tartaric acid - Na2CO3 at a mass ratio of 1:1 and Na2HPO4 - NaHCO3 at a mass ratio of 1:1. The results are summarized in Fig. 4a. One-way ANOVA of these results shows that there is no significant difference among averages at the p = 0.01 probability level, indicating that all combinations can effectively be used to adjust the pH of the solution. We selected to continue our work with NaH2PO4 - Na2CO3 at a mass ratio of 2:1 because (a) Na2CO3 is less hygroscopic [40], thus affording improved stability to the tablets and (b) the 2:1 acid-base mass ratio causes a more violent effervescent reaction than 1:1 mixtures as a result of the larger amount of acid [22]. Hence, it provides faster dissolution of the chelating agent and consequently longer incubation time for complexing metal ions before the next step of the process. The mass of NaH2PO4 - Na2CO3 was 0.42 g (equally distributed in the upper and bottom layers) and composed of 0.28 g NaH2PO4 and 0.14 g Na2CO3. This mass was the maximum amount of mass that could be inserted in the 1.6 cm die mold when all tablet layers were present. We preferred to use the maximum mass of effervescence reagents to ensure intense effervescence for fast dissolution and good buffering capacity. These factors are also important in the analysis of real samples because natural waters have buffering capacity and contain other metal ions. Therefore, it is necessary to ensure the reproducible adjustment of sample pH and adequate incubation time to ensure full complexation of the metal ions.

The optimum mass of sodium gluconate was then investigated by increasing the mass from 3 to 10 mg, equally distributed between the top and bottom layers of the tablets (i.e., 1.5-5 mg in each layer). The results of Fig. 4b show that the mass of sodium gluconate does not affect the extraction of Cu2+ ions when present at concentrations equal to or higher than 3 mg. That is because gluconic acid forms 1:1 metal chelates with Cu2+ [37, 41], therefore 3.0 mg of sodium gluconate (i.e. 2.68 mg gluconate ions / 10 mL) is already in significant excess (~ 335-fold) compared to Cu2+ ions. Lower mass of sodium gluconate was not examined because it was not feasible to manually weigh and handle smaller amounts. Nevertheless, the excess of sodium gluconate is beneficial for the analysis of real samples, accounting for the presence of co-existing species in natural waters, especially alkaline earth metal ions, which are typically in large excess relative to other species and are efficiently complexed by gluconate ions.

The optimization of the composition of the tablet core was made by blending effervescence reagents with CoFe2O4@stearic acid MNPs as an adsorbent. Specifically, the core was prepared by mixing 0.065 g NaH2PO4, 0.035 g Na2CO3 and increasing mass (25–200 mg) of CoFe2O4@stearic acid MNPs (Fig. 4c). The extraction efficiency of Cu2+ increased with increasing mass of MNPs up to 100 mg, remained constant up to 150 mg and declined with higher mass. This observation was attributed to the inadequate dispersion and mixing of the MNPs with increasing mass. However, increasing the amount of salt in the core to generate more intense effervescence and improve the dispersion of MNPs would result in larger core size and consequently in a change in tablet design characteristics. Therefore, the mass of MNPs was maintained at 150 mg for further work.

Since the zero-point charge of the nanoparticles is close to 7 [22], the surface charge at the working pH should be slightly negative. Therefore, we reasoned that the sorption of Cu-gluconate complexes occurs with exposed (not covered with stearic acid) nanoparticle surface sites through inner-sphere coordination to Fe–OH/Fe site [42]. However, stearic acid coating was maintained for stability reasons since non-coated magnetic nanoparticles are less stable and exhibit higher tendency to aggregate in aqueous media, reduced colloidal stability, loss of size-dependent properties (such as superparamagnetism) and are influenced by environmental factors such as pH, electrolyte concentration, and oxidation [43–45].

Optimization of the elution step

The desorption of the metal-gluconate complexes from the magnetic nanoparticles’ surface was optimized by testing methanol as well as aqueous and methanolic solutions containing HNO3 and HCl at two concentration levels (1.0 and 2.0 M), mixing conditions (stirring and vortex agitation), and elution times (2–45 min). All desorption experiments were performed with 2.0 mL of elution solvent to ensure adequate volume for aspiration into the nebulizer of FAAS. The results show that methanol is inadequate to elute the metal complexes, possibly due to the low solubility of gluconate in methanol [46]. At the same time, methanolic and aqueous solutions of 1.0 M HNO3 are equally effective in extracting the metal ions with either vortex agitation or stirring for 5 min (Fig. 4d). An increase in HNO3 concentration or elution time, and other mixing methods did not influence the extraction efficiency. Therefore, elution was performed with 2.0 mL of 1.0 M HNO3 under vortex agitation for 5 min. However, after elution with HNO3, the sorbent could not be reused due to the instability of CoFe2O4@stearic acid magnetic nanoparticles in the acidic conditions of the elution solvent, an observation which is agreement with previous reports [47].

Technical evaluation of tablets

By this design, once the tablets are added to water, an intense effervescence from the external (top and bottom) layers is observed for approximately 1 min. After that time, effervescence reduces significantly but does not completely halt, which facilitates mixing. The internal layer starts to crack and disintegrate after about 10 min. Once water reaches the core, it takes approximately 5 min to disperse the core through mild effervescence. This is advantageous compared to the rapid effervescence employed in classic effervescence-assisted microextraction because, as the sorbent is gradually released, it provides adequate incubation time to extract the metal chelates without the need for additional energy input (i.e. mixing). Overall, analysis is completed in less than 20 min with minimal or no user intervention. Photographs of the first four steps of the process are shown in Fig. 5.

Photographs of the first four steps of the process and duration of each step. The faint yellow coloration is due to the addition of dye (8-hydroxyquinoline sulfonic acid), to facilitate the visual observation

The reproducible preparation of the tablets was evaluated by determining the reproducibility (intra-day precision) and the repeatability (inter-day precision) of the total analysis time, determined by timing of each step separately. The sequential release of reagents was captured by video recording using a digital camera (Sony G, 50 Mpixel). The time of external layer release was visualized by a yellow dye release (8 hydroxyquinoline sulfonic acid) from the top layer and black solid release (i.e. magnetic nanoparticles) from the core of the tablet. The reproducibility of the tablet disintegration time was 7.8% (n = 6) while the repeatability was 11.7% (n = 7) which are both satisfactory. Further improvement and more accurate timing of each step could likely be accomplished by using automated tablet pressing equipment such as rotary tablet press, powder bed-based 3D printing, high-shear granulators, etc. to enable more precise control over compression force, layer thickness, reagent density per layer, number of layers, size of tablets, etc.

Selectivity

The selectivity of the method was examined by extracting aqueous solutions containing 1 µg mL− 1 of Cu2+ and equal concentrations of various metal ions (Pb2+, Fe3+, Co2+, Ni2+, Zn2+, Cd2+) as well as 100 µg mL− 1 of alkaline earth metals (Ca2+, Mg2+) and determining the recovery of Cu2+ as target analyte. In addition, the concentration of each metal ion in the final extract was also determined by flame AAS. Due to the excess of the chelating agent (300 µg mL− 1), no interference in the extraction efficiency of Cu2+ was observed (recovery 55%) while all metal ions could be determined in the HNO3 extract solution, yielding recoveries of 45% for Ni2+, 30% for Zn2+, 23% Co2+, 40% Fe3+, and 15% for Cd2+ (alkaline earth metals were not determined). Therefore, the method is not selective for specific metal ions, paving the way for the further development of the method for multi-elemental analysis especially when combined with ICP devices. These results also indicate that the method does not accomplish the exhaustive extraction of metal ions but the non-exhaustive (equilibrium-based) extraction as with most microextraction methods [1, 48, 49].

Analytical merits

Method calibration was performed by extracting aqueous standard solutions containing increasing concentrations of Cu2+. The dose-response curve for Cu2+ exhibited a linear increase with increasing concentration for more than an order of magnitude (0.05–1.5 µg mL− 1, y = 0.381x + 0.0106, R2 = 0.998). Based on this curve, the minimum concentration that could be determined was defined as the lowest concentration of the calibration curve (0.05 µg mL− 1). The working range of Cu2+ and the LOD are below the WHO limit (2 µg mL− 1) and EPA limit (1.3 µg mL− 1) for drinking water. Higher sensitivity can be accomplished by eluting the analytes to smaller volume of HNO3 to increase the preconcentration factor or by extracting larger sample volumes. The latter entails the fabrication of larger tablets or tablets with higher reagent intensity to enable stronger and more prolonged effervescence depending on sample volume. However, this requires advanced tableting instruments (e.g. rotary tablet press, powder bed-based 3D printing, hot-melt extrusion, etc.) which were not available in our laboratory. Alternatively, more sensitive detectors such as ETAAS or ICP-MS may be employed depending on the needs of the analysis.

Although at this proof-of-concept stage the system has not yet been fully optimized for analytical sensitivity, the results showcase the advantages of the proposed approach that lie in its operational simplicity and integration of pretreatment and extraction into an autonomous workflow, which differentiates it from existing methodologies and highlights its potential for decentralized or on-site analysis. Analysis of real samples

As a proof-of-concept demonstration, the method was applied to the determination of Cu2+ in spiked water samples at concentrations below the WHO and EPA limits for drinking water. Matrix effects were weak (< 10%) in all freshwater samples (Table 4) and moderate in seawater (~ 34%) (results not shown). These matrix effects may be related to (a) the large excess of chloride anions which may strongly compete gluconate for the complexation of Cu2+, (b) the large excess of Ca2+ and Mg2+ that may effectively be complexed by gluconate, and (c) the compression of the electrical double-layer of the MNPs which is involved in extraction. Therefore, spiking experiments to assess the method’s accuracy were performed in freshwater samples.

Since the method is based on equilibrium (non-exhaustive) extraction, relative recoveries were calculated (defined as the ratio of the concentration found in spiked water samples to that determined from the extraction of an aqueous standard solution of the same concentration). The results gathered in Table 4 show that the method affords satisfactory relative recoveries which are comparable to standard laboratory practices and within the acceptable limits suggested by IUPAC (70–120%). Similar recoveries were also observed in other effervescence-assisted microextraction methods [54, 55] while further improvement can likely be accomplished by using automated tablet pressing machines to improve the design of the tablets. The RSD of the measurements (n = 3) in real samples was lower than 8%, which is also satisfactory.

Conclusions

In this study, we report a new approach to simplify multistep sample preparation by encapsulating the required reagents and extraction sorbent materials into layered effervescent tablets that allow sequential dissolution and release of each layer. This approach enables the definition of the number of experimental steps, the release rate of the reagents or materials, and the reaction time of each step. Using three-layered tablets, we show that the use of preformulated tablets can optimize the analytical workflow and streamline sample preparation by (a) storing reagents, alleviating the need for preparing working solutions or weighing solid reagents and materials and (b) enabling analysis without technical skills and equipment. The analytical results from a proof-of-concept demonstration show satisfactory recoveries from spiked water samples, comparable to those obtained with standard laboratory practices. By further developing and improving tablet design, improved analytical merits, more complex reactions and additional steps may be introduced to the analysis making controlled-release layered effervescent tablets a springboard for the next-generation sample preparation methods that afford labor-efficient operation as well as on-site sample pretreatment capabilities even by non-experts. Further efforts are devoted to this endeavor.
