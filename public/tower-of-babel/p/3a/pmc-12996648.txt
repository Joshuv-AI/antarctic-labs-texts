Lafora disease gene therapy: EPM2A but not EPM2B overexpression results in Lafora body formation
Authors: Esther O Alao, Mehrnaz Sheibani, Jun Wu, Ummay Marriam, Doretha Evans, Sahba Kasiri, Mayank Verma, Silvia Nitschke, Felix Nitschke, Steven J Gray, Sharmistha Mitra, Berge A Minassian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLafora disease (LD) is a fatal teenage-onset neurodegenerative epilepsy caused by loss-of-function mutations of the genes encoding the laforin phosphatase-malin E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. This complex regulates glycogen structural integrity, through yet unclear mechanisms. Deficiencies of the complex lead few glycogen molecules at a time to misshape, precipitate and cumulate into toxic Lafora bodies (LBs) that drive LD. We developed an intrathecal AAV9-based gene therapy for malin-deficient LD and obtained preclinical efficacy without toxicity. A similar gene therapy for laforin-deficient LD also afforded efficacy, but with a relatively narrow therapeutic window. When overexpressed, laforin paradoxically led to LB generation and did so at a rate higher than in LD. The phenomenon was invariably observed first and dominantly in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and occurred irrespective of where in the CSF axis the viral vector was delivered. Laforin overexpression-mediated LB formation represents novel biology, which we characterize and show that it occurs independently of laforin and malin's enzymatic activities. The accumulations are time-dependent, which toward clinical development will necessitate longer preclinical and clinical safety monitoring. The effect is strongest with species mismatch (i.e., expression of human laforin in mice), which may require matched-species preclinical development. Finally, the DRG are sentinel, and their pathology and neurophysiology can serve to monitor the iatrogenic toxicity. Our work suggests a clear gene therapy development path for malin-deficient LD, and sets important guardrails for gene therapy for laforin-dependent LD. It also opens a new avenue to understanding the basic biologies of glycogen quality control and LD.Keywords: EPM2A, EPM2B, Gene therapy, Intrathecal, Dorsal root ganglia

Introduction

Lafora disease (LD) is an extremely severe autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disease of early adolescence caused by loss-of-function mutations of the EPM2A [1] or NHLRC1 (EPM2B) [2] genes. Until then healthy young teenagers develop seizures that gradually become intractable and, over time, in wakefulness near-constant. This is accompanied by hallucinations and progressive cognitive decline. As teenage years conclude most are wheelchair bound, and soon dementia and a vegetative state set in. Death occurs in most cases approximately 10 years from onset through lost airway control and status epilepticus [3]. Intense therapeutic research activity is underway [4] including recent publication of promising preclinical efficacy results with gene replacement therapy [[5], [6], [7]].

The primary pathology of LD is a lifelong constant slow (over months in mouse models and years in affected dogs and humans) formation, precipitation and accumulation of glycogen molecules with overlong branches. In the brain, when these accumulations (called Lafora bodies; LBs) reach a threshold (in humans at around age 14 years) they start driving a complex multifaceted neuropathology including disturbed cellular metabolism, inflammation and neurodegeneration, which collectively underlie the clinical manifestations of the disease [4,8]. Of note that the primary disturbance in glycogen structure affects very few molecules at a time as evidenced by the fact that the amounts of normally branched glycogen in the LD mouse models are always normal [9]. Consistent with this, there is no cell-wide disturbance of the canonical enzymes of glycogen metabolism. Instead, the basic pathology appears to be in the quality control of glycogen construction, where small numbers of poor-quality species generated precipitate, escape catabolism by the digestive enzymes that operate in solution, and slowly and steadily accumulate into the toxic LBs [[10], [11], [12]].

As mentioned, LD is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the EPM2A or NHLRC1 (EPM2B) gene encoding, respectively, laforin and malin which function as a complex. Laforin has a phosphatase domain with which it dephosphorylates glycogen [10,11], but this function appears to be dispensable in preventing LB formation as complementation in Epm2a−/− mice with phosphatase-inactive laforin completely prevents LB formation and LD [[13], [14], [15]]. Laforin binds glycogen through a dedicated carbohydrate binding domain and localizes the lion's share of malin to glycogen [16]. Absence of laforin leads malin to degrade and disappear [16]. Malin is an E3 ubiquitin ligase [17] whose authentic target is not yet known, and given the abovementioned results to date appears to be key to understanding the role of the laforin-malin complex in regulating glycogen structure quality control.

In the present work we performed preclinical gene therapy experiments in mouse models of the EPM2A and EPM2B forms of LD and obtained efficacy, described below, in both cases. However, in experiments assessing safety we discovered that EPM2A (but not EPM2B) overexpression results, paradoxically, in LB formation. This effect is in part due to species incompatibility, raising important considerations for translational therapeutic development. The phenomenon is driven neither by laforin's phosphatase domain nor malin's ubiquitin ligase activity, unveiling the potential existence of functions in this enzyme complex beyond their known enzymatic activities. Finally, dorsal root ganglia (DRG) of the spinal cord are exquisitely sensitive to this toxic effect irrespective of where in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space the EPM2A gene therapy is administered, rendering them and their neurosensory functions ‘canaries’ in the mining for a safe gene therapy for the EPM2A form of this devastating disease.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction and viral packaging

We cloned codon-optimized human EPM2A and EPM2B under the JeT promoter into self-complementary AAV9 expression plasmids [18] (AAV9-JeT-EPM2A and AAV9-JeT-EPM2B). We likewise cloned codon-optimized mouse Epm2a and human EPM2A under the CBh promoter (AAV9-CBh-Epm2a and AAV9-CBh-EPM2A). Finally and in the same fashion, we cloned mutated forms of these last two transgenes which express phosphatase-inactive laforin. The mutations were c.794G > C; p. C265S for the murine and c.797G > C; p. C266S for the human for final vectors AAV9-CBh-Epm2api and AAV9-CBh-EPM2Api. Viruses were produced at the Baylor Gene Vector core except AAV9-JeT-EPM2B, which was produced at the University of Texas Southwestern Translational Gene Therapy core.

Mice

Epm2a−/− and Epm2b−/− mouse models were described previously [19,20]. Sexes were used in equal proportion. Procedures were carried out according to NIH guidelines and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee regulations at the University of Texas Southwestern [21,22].

Virus administration, mice sacrificing and histochemistry

All lumbar intrathecal (IT) dosing was done at P10. Vectors were diluted in 5 μL PBS containing 1:5 dilution of Evans Blue for visual injection confirmation. Mice were anesthetized under isoflurane and administered 2 mg/kg meloxicam subcutaneously. Mice were placed in the prone position, and a small incision was made over the lumbar spine. The incision site was cleaned with 70 % ethanol, and a 28 G Hamilton syringe needle was inserted perpendicularly beveled down into the lumbar spinal canal. A total of 5 μL of either vector solution or PBS was slowly injected into the IT space. Following injection, the needle was gently withdrawn to prevent backflow and the incision site closed using surgical glue. Mice were placed on a warming pad during recovery and subsequently returned to their home cages. Animals were monitored periodically to ensure proper recovery and activity post-procedure.

All intracerebroventricular injections (ICV) were done at P2. Vectors were diluted in 3 μL PBS. Procedure was done as previously described [23]. Briefly, a 10-μL Hamilton syringe fitted with a 28 G needle was sterilized using 70 % ethanol and vector or PBS solution carefully drawn. Mice were anesthetized on ice for ∼5 min. The injection site was marked ∼0.7–1.0 mm lateral to the sagittal suture and ∼0.7–1.0 mm caudal to neonatal bregma. The needle was inserted at the marked site perpendicularly to a depth of ∼2 mm and 1.5 μL of solution was slowly injected into each lateral ventricle. To minimize backflow, the needle was left in place for ∼1 min before being withdrawn. Mice were placed on a warming pad for 10 min to recover and returned to their home cages with their mothers.

All Intramuscular Injections, dosing was done at P10. Vector was diluted in 10 μl PBS and injected into both GP muscles (10μl/GP) using a 31G Becton Dickinson (BD) SafetyGlide insulin needle.

Mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Tissues including brain, spinal cord, heart, liver, and gastrocnemius plantaris muscle (GP) were harvested. One brain hemisphere and GP muscle was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for biochemical analyses, the other fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin for histology.

Frozen tissues were ground to fine powder for western blotting, quantitative real-time PCR(qRT-PCR) and glycogen quantification. Fixed tissues were stained with periodic acid–Schiff with (PASD) or without (PAS) diastase pre-digestion to assess for Lafora body, or normal glycogen, accumulation, respectively. Diastase is amylase, which readily and completely digests normal soluble glycogen. The malstructured, insoluble and aggregated glycogen that constitutes LBs is relatively resistant to diastase. Thus, inclusion of diastase (D) in the staining distinguishes LBs from accumulations of well-structured glycogen.

To evaluate transgene expression, slides were either immunostained or RNAscoped as below.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC), fiber typing and immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Paraffin-embedded brain and muscle tissue samples were sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were mounted on glass slides, deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. For IHC experiments, antigen retrieval was performed on tissue sections using citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Sigma) to unmask epitopes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with BLOXALL solution (Vector Laboratories) for 10 min. Tissue sections were incubated in a 2.5 % normal horse serum blocking solution for 30 min. Thereafter, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 2.5 % normal horse serum: a human-specific mouse anti-EPM2A antibody (Fig. 1, Fig. 3; BioLegend, Cat. #858002, Clone N84/37; 1:150 dilution). The following day, sections were incubated sequentially with an Amplifier Antibody and ImmPRESS Polymer Reagent (Vector Laboratories, Catalog # MP-7602), followed by development with ImmPACT DAB EqV substrate (Vector Laboratories) until the desired staining intensity was achieved. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, the slides were then dehydrated, and cover-slipped using synthetic mounting media.

Laforin and glycogen synthase (GYS1) immunofluorescence staining was performed as follows, but first a note on the structure detected by GYS1 staining in Fig. 1, Fig. 3f. The GYS1 antibody detects glycogen synthase, much of which is located on glycogen. In the physiological condition, a glycogen synthase signal, if detected, is a faint diffuse signal corresponding to soluble glycogen diffusely spread throughout the cytosol. When LBs are present, glycogen synthase becomes concentrated in the bodies and manifests as bright puncta with immunofluorescence. Punctal GYS1 staining is therefore a proxy for LBs [24]. Following antigen retrieval and endogenous peroxidase quenching, tissue sections were incubated for 1 h in a blocking solution made with 5 % normal donkey serum and 0.5 % Triton. Next, slides were incubated with rabbit anti-Gys1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technologies, Cat. #S886S; 1:200) and mouse anti-Laforin (Biolegend, Cat. #858002, Clone N84/37; 1:200) in a 5 % normal donkey serum and 0.1 % Triton solution overnight at 4 °C. Slides were then washed with PBS and successively incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti mouse IgG (ThermoFisher, Cat# A21202; 1:400) and Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ThermoFisher, Cat# A10042; 1:400) and DAPI.

For myofiber typing, paraffin embedded muscle tissues were serially cut for PASD staining and typing using antiserum MHC-I (NOQ7.5.4D; IgG1; 1:1200; Sigma-Aldrich; St Louis, MO). Primary antibody was detected with peroxidase-conjugated secondary (anti-mouse IgG; A8924 Sigma-Aldrich) and color developed with diaminobenzidine substrate (Dako-Agilent; Santa Clara, CA).

RNAscope In Situ hybridization

The RNAscope red chromogenic assay was performed by the University of Texas Southwestern Metabolic Phenotyping Core to evaluate the biodistribution of human EPM2B mRNA in 5 μm paraffin-embedded sections. Slides were deparaffinized and pretreated according to the manufacturer's protocol (Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACD) Inc., California). Briefly, sections were boiled in Target Retrieval solution, rinsed with distilled water, dehydrated in 100 % ethanol and digested with a protease solution at 40 °C for 30 min. Hybridization was carried out following ACD's RNAscope® 2.5 HD Detection Kit (RED; Cat. #322360) protocol. Custom-designed probes specific for our codon optimized human EPM2B (RNAscope™ Probe–Mm-Nhlrc1, Cat. #587431) were applied to the sections and incubated at 40 °C for 2 h. Subsequent amplification steps were performed per manufacturer instructions. Chromogenic signal detection was achieved using a 1:60 mixture of Fast RED-B and Fast RED-A at room temperature for ∼10 min. Slides were rinsed with distilled water, counterstained with hematoxylin, and mounted using EcoMount (BioCare Medical) before coverslipping.

Microscopy and image analysis

RNAscope, PASD, and IHC stained slides were scanned using the Hamamatsu Nanozoomer 2.0 HT digital slide scanner using its 40× objective and IF stained slides were scanned using the Olympus BX41 microscope at 60x resolution. In PASD stained slides, percentage area occupied by Lafora bodies (LBs) in the cerebellum, cortex, and hippocampus were quantified using HistoQuant software (3DHISTECH), which detects LB signals based on pixel color. Results are reported as the percentage of tissue area covered by LBs. For IF stained sections, FiJi was used to obtain merged RGB images.

Measurement of insoluble glycogen

Frozen brain tissues were ground and aliquots (40–50 mg) incubated at room temperature for one hour, which results in degradation of soluble glycogen and not insoluble glycogen as previously described [25]. Incubated samples were boiled at 95 °C in 30 % KOH, and glycogen was precipitated in 70 % ethanol with 57 mM sodium sulfate. Three additional rounds of precipitation and suspension in 67 % ethanol with 15 mM LiCl were performed, after which the pellet was resuspended in 100 mM sodium acetate pH 4.5 and digested with amyloglucosidase (Megazyme) in a 55 °C oven for one hour along with digestion of two blank controls. Glucose amount resulting from this digestion was determined in triplicate for each sample using a modified version of Lowry and Passonneau. Briefly, samples and glucose standards were mixed with 170 μL of G6PDH reaction mix (200 mM Tricine buffer/10 mM MgCl2 pH 8, 0.66 mM NADP; 1 mM ATP; 0.5 units G6PDH). Absorbance at 340 nm was acquired before (Abs1) and after (Abs2) addition of hexokinase (0.6 units in 4 μl of 200 mM Tricine/KOH, pH 8, 10 mM MgCl2). Abs2 minus Abs1 of glucose standards was used to derive glucose quantity in samples. Following normalization to fresh weight, glucose quantity represents the amount of insoluble glycogen.

Results

Overexpression of human EPM2A, but not EPM2B, in wild-type mice generates Lafora bodies

Toward developing a gene replacement therapy for the EPM2A form of LD, we packaged codon-optimized human EPM2A driven by the Chicken Beta hybrid intron (CBh) promoter using a self-complementary vector genome design in AAV9 [Fig. 1a], injected the virus at high dose (5 × 1011 vg) into wild-type (WT) mice via IT lumbar puncture at postnatal day 10 (P10), and sacrificed the mice one month later for analysis. PBS injection was used as control. Immunohistochemistry with a laforin antibody exclusively detecting the human protein showed abundant presence of human laforin in tissues of AAV9-CBh-EPM2A, but not PBS, injected mice, including brain, spinal cord (not shown), dorsal root ganglia, heart and liver [Fig. 1b]. H&E staining showed no tissue structural abnormalities or cellular infiltration, but detected inclusions. PASD staining showed that the inclusions were LBs. In the nervous system these were present in neurons close to the injection site (DRG and lumbar spinal cord), but not further afield (i.e. cervical spinal cord or brain) [Fig. 1c]. Inclusions were also present in the liver. These stained positive with PAS but not PASD indicating that their constituents more closely resemble normal glycogen than the malstructured glycogen that constitutes LBs (see methods for the difference between PAS and PASD) [Fig. 1d].

Formation of LBs as a result of reintroduction of laforin, a protein that normally prevents LB formation was surprising. Their occurrence in the vicinity (DRG and lumbar spinal cord) of the injection site (lumbar IT) suggests that they resulted from transgene overexpression, especially considering that DRG are known to take up 10 and 100 times more virus than hindbrain and forebrain cells respectively following IT gene therapy [26]. To determine whether neurons in the brain could also form LBs with laforin overexpression, we injected the same vector into cerebral ventricles (WT mice at P2; ICV; 1 × 1011vg; mice sacrificed at 3 months) [Fig. 1e–f]. LBs formed in brain proximate to the injection site, i.e. in the regions of the cortex and hippocampus surrounding the ventricles [Fig. 1g], with laforin localizing at the LBs [Fig. 1h]. LBs also formed in the cerebellum but in much lower amounts [Fig. 1g]. These results showed that laforin overexpression-associated LB formation is not a particularity of DRG and other spinal cord cells. At the same time, despite their distance from the injection site LBs did form in the DRG with cerebral ventricular administration [Fig. 1g], suggesting that these cells have particular permissiveness to AAV9 and/or are otherwise prone to LB formation with laforin overexpression [Fig. 1g]. There were no LBs in peripheral organs including the liver.

To reduce the vector-mediated EPM2A transgene expression, we replaced the strong CBh promoter with the weak JeT promoter [27] and repeated the above lumbar IT experiment in WT mice, this time with two doses, high: 5 × 1011 vg and low: 1.25 × 1011 vg [Fig. 2a]. Western blotting for human laforin from whole brain lysates showed that at the same high dose, laforin expression driven by JeT was approximately 3 times lower than by CBh [Fig. 2b]. LBs formed in the vicinity of the injection site (DRG and spinal cord) with high but not low dose AAV9-JeT-EPM2A [Fig. 2c–d]. In peripheral tissues, there were no LBs with either dose.

To develop a gene replacement therapy for the EPM2B form of LD we took the results with the EPM2A gene into account and proceeded directly with the JeT promoter. We replaced EPM2A with human EPM2B (again codon-optimized and in self-complementary genome configuration) to obtain AAV9-JeT-EPM2B, dosed WT mice by lumbar IT puncture at P10 with the high dose of 5 × 1011 vg and sacrificed the mice one month later for analysis [Fig. 2e]. Due to lack of a suitable antibody for malin immunohistochemistry, we employed RNAscope to assess the biodistribution of EPM2B transcripts. Expression was present in all tissues tested including the brain, heart, spinal cord, and liver [Fig. 2f]. Staining with H&E revealed no abnormalities (data not shown) and with PASD no LBs in any tissues [Fig. 2g]. These findings suggest that high dose IT AAV9-JeT-EPM2B does not result in LB formation.

EPM2A and EPM2B gene therapies are effective and reduce Lafora bodies and neuroinflammation in Epm2a−/− (LKO) and Epm2b−/− (MKO) mice respectively

In the preceding sections AAV9-JeT-EPM2A, and not AAV9-JeT-EPM2B, induced the formation of LBs in WT mice. To determine whether these gene therapies reduce LB formation in knockout mice, we administered them at P10 by lumbar IT injection at high (5 × 1011 vg) and low (1.25 × 1011 vg) dose in corresponding knockout mouse models and sacrificed the mice 3 months later for tissue studies. For the brain, one hemisphere was used for histochemistry, the other for biochemical analyses [Fig. 3a and 4a]. Brain laforin protein levels were analyzed by western blots using an antibody reactive to mouse and human laforin. Compared to untreated WT mice, AAV9-JeT-EPM2A treatment resulted in approximately two-fold increased laforin protein with the high dose and approximately seven-fold decreased levels with the low dose [Fig. 3b and c]. In the case of AAV9-JeT-EPM2B, again because of unavailability of a suitable antibody, we relied on quantitative RT-PCR. With the high dose human EPM2B expressed five times higher in treated knockout mice than the expression of murine Epm2b in untreated WT mice, while with the low dose expression was comparable to endogenous murine levels [Fig. 4b and c].

In Epm2a−/− (LKO) mice treated with high dose AAV9-JeT-EPM2A there were significant reductions in LB formation in cerebellum (30 %) and hippocampus (57 %) but not cortex [Fig. 3e]. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed that cells expressing laforin lacked LBs, while cells not expressing laforin had LBs [Fig. 3f]. Reduction of total hemispheric glycogen (a biochemical proxy for total LBs) [19,20,26] was 37 % [Fig. 3g]. Cxcl10 expression is a marker of neuroinflammation [28]. Hemispheric expression of this gene trended toward reduction but did not achieve significance [Fig. 3h]. With low dose AAV9-JeT-EPM2A, none of the above measures were significantly reduced, although there was a trend of LB reduction in hippocampus [Fig. 3d–e].

In Epm2b−/− (MKO) mice treated with high dose AAV9-JeT-EPM2B there were significant reductions in LB formation in cerebellum (36 %), hippocampus (40 %), cortex (45 %), total hemispheric glycogen (37 %) and Cxcl10 expression (50 %). With low dose, the only significant diminution was a 20 % reduction in cerebellar LBs [Fig. 4d–g].

Strikingly, in both cases, the abundant LBs normally present in paraspinal muscles at three months of age completely disappeared with the lumbar IT gene therapies [Fig. 4h; see also Fig. 6e], indicating that brain-directed gene therapy for LD is potently collaterally effective against LB accumulations in peripheral organs.

EPM2A, but not EPM2B, overexpression generates LBs in DRG in corresponding KO mice

In the previous section we showed that high-dose (5 × 1011 vg), IT-delivered, weak (JeT) promoter-driven, self-complementary human EPM2A or EPM2B had significant impacts on LB reductions in their corresponding KO mice. In both cases, total brain glycogen diminished by 37 % and LBs by 30–57 % in different brain regions. These percentages predict potentially meaningful clinical impacts on the human disease upon translation. Results with a lower dose (1.25 × 1011 vg) were much more modest for EPM2B and not significant for EPM2A. To address the question whether the potentially meaningful (high) dose could have toxicity, we performed PASD studies on the sentinel DRG and found that there is no toxicity with EPM2B [Fig. 4d], but there is with EPM2A [Fig. 3d]. These results indicate that a potentially clinically meaningful dose of EPM2A, at least delivered IT, may have iatrogenic toxicity that needs to be considered and monitored. Meaningful IT EPM2B dosing, on the other hand, appears to be safe.

Lafora body generation by laforin overexpression is not due to increased laforin phosphatase activity and not solely due to species incompatibility

To investigate the mechanism of LB generation by laforin overexpression we asked two questions: 1) whether the effect was related to the laforin ortholog utilized (human gene in mouse) and 2) whether it was driven by laforin's known enzymatic (phosphatase) activity. To address these two questions we constructed three new vectors: AAV9-CBh-Epm2a (expressing WT murine laforin), AAV9-CBh-Epm2api (expressing phosphatase-inactive murine laforin) and AAV9-CBh-EPM2Api (expressing phosphatase-inactive human laforin), all identical to the above AAV9-CBh-EPM2A (expressing WT human laforin) except for the indicated changes [Fig. 5a]. We repeated the ICV experiment of Fig. 1e (WT mice; administration at P2; 1 × 1011vg; mice sacrificed at 3 months) with each of the four vectors. Expression levels in the brain from all four proved similar [Fig. 5b]. To compare laforin protein levels, we selected mice with the most similar expression levels across the four groups and performed Western blotting with the antibody that recognizes human and mouse laforin. For both human and mouse laforin, phosphatase-inactive laforin levels were always higher [Fig. 5c]. A similar phenomenon had been noted previously in mouse models constitutively expressing phosphatase-inactive laforin [15,29], which confirm that this form of laforin is longer-lived.

All four groups formed LBs in the brain in the following acutely descending order: AAV9-CBh-EPM2Api (highest amount), AAV9-CBh-EPM2A (second-highest), AAV9-CBh-Epm2api (much lower) and AAV9-CBh-Epm2a (minimal to negligible) [Fig. 5d–f]. Insoluble glycogen quantities, biochemical proxies for LBs, followed a similar pattern [Fig. 5g]. These results indicate a major role for species incompatibility in laforin overexpression-driven LB formation. However, we did see LB formation with the murine vectors, suggesting that overexpression of the natural ortholog could also induce LBs to at least some extent. To test this, we analyzed the DRG, which in our opening experiments had shown particular susceptibility to laforin overexpression-induced LB formation, in the four groups. The DRG from all four constructs had formed LBs, including the group treated with AAV9-CBh-Epm2a, and the quantity of LBs in the latter was not very different from that of AAV9-CBh-EPM2A, for example [Fig. 5h]. This result reemphasizes the particular susceptibility of DRG to laforin overexpression LB formation. It also shows that a species’ natural laforin can generate LBs when overabundant.

Results displayed in Fig. 5d-f suggest that phosphatase-inactive laforin is more LB-genic than WT laforin. However, such a conclusion could be confounded by the invariably higher protein levels of phosphatase-inactive laforin compared to WT laforin for the same amounts of expression. To correct for this, from the AAV9-CBh-EPM2A and AAV9-CBh-EPM2Api treated groups we handpicked mice that had accumulated similar levels of insoluble brain glycogen (i.e. had formed similar amounts of LBs) [those are mice 3 to 8 in Fig. 5g]. We performed laforin Western blots on brain extracts from these mice and found that they had similar levels of laforin [mice 3 to 8 in Fig. 5i]. In other words, mice with similar amounts of human laforin, whether phosphatase-active or inactive, generate similar amounts of LBs, i.e. LB-genicity of overexpressed laforin in WT mice is not mediated through the phosphatase activity, but is rather a function of the amount of laforin protein.

Rate of LB generation due to laforin overexpression depends on the level of overexpression and can outpace LB formation caused by laforin absence

The rate of LB formation and growth in LKO and MKO mice (and probably LD dogs and humans with similar mutations) is constant and invariant [30,31]. In these mice, at 1 month of age LBs are barely visible by light microscopy, and have a certain amount at 3 months and gradually and constantly more every passing month. To determine whether the rate of LB generation by laforin overexpression tracks with the rate in LKO mice, we dosed WT mice lumbar intrathecally with AAV9-CBh-EPM2A (5 × 1011 vg) at P10, sacrificed the mice at 1 month and studied the DRG. Surprisingly, the AAV9-injected WT mice had plentiful LBs in the DRG at this early stage [Fig. 6a–b]. The figure shows a representative example of the DRG of an LKO mouse (uninjected) at the same age with negligible (a single body in this field) LB formation.

In another experiment, we dosed LKO mice intrathecally at P10 with AAV9-JeT-EPM2A (5 × 1011 vg) and studied the DRG at 3 months. The LKO treated mice had drastically greater LB production than the untreated LKO mice [Fig. 6c and d]. As mentioned, LBs form not only in nervous tissue but also in muscle. Remarkably, in this last experiment while AAV9-JeT-EPM2A dramatically increased the rate of LB formation in the DRG of LKO mice, it completely prevented any LB formation in their paraspinal muscles [Fig. 6e]. In other words, AAV9-JeT-EPM2A extensively corrected LD in the muscle, but accelerated it in the DRG.

Laforin overexpression–induced LB formation occurs independently of malin

As reviewed in the Introduction, laforin's role in preventing LB formation does not depend on its glycogen phosphatase function, but does depend on its partner malin's E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. As we showed in a previous section of the present work, LB formation by laforin overexpression also does not depend on its phosphatase function. We now ask whether it is mediated by malin.

We injected 5 × 1011 vg of AAV9-CBh-EPM2A or AAV9-CBh-Epm2a into the gastrocnemius plantaris (GP) muscle of WT mice at P10 and allowed the mice to age until seven months [Fig. 7a]. This is an age when in LKO or MKO mice there are plentiful LBs, which could serve as a comparator to what might occur in the injected WTs. PASD-staining of injected GP showed that it had formed abundant LBs, with either vector [Fig. 7b]. LBs that form in GP or other skeletal muscles in LKO or MKO mice are of two types, ‘pebbles’ or ‘boulders’, the first being multitudinous small bodies while the latter single or several very large ones. ‘Pebbles’ form in Type II (fast-twitch glycolytic) fibers while ‘boulders’ in Type I (slow-twitch oxidative) fibers [32]. In the present experiment, the laforin overexpression-induced LBs in WT GP were likewise in the dual ‘multitudinous pebbles’ and ‘single/several boulders’ conformations [Fig. 7b, pebbles(∗∗), boulders(∗)]. In summary, LBs formed as a result of laforin overexpression follow the same unknown metabolic determinants of structure as natural LBs resulting from absent laforin or malin.

Following laforin overexpression, western blots of GP extracts showed that malin levels had increased [Fig. 7c–d]. This finding is notable because in previous unpublished cell culture experiments using lentiviral transduction and strong promoters we could not upregulate malin protein levels despite large increases in its mRNA amounts, suggesting the presence of tight post-transcriptional regulation of cellular levels of this protein.

The facts that malin is the effector molecule in the laforin-malin complex, that its cellular levels are strongly kept low, and that these here increased suggested the possibility that the laforin overexpression-driven LB formation was mediated by malin overactivity. To test this, we administered AAV9-CBh-EPM2A (5 × 1011 vg) via lumbar puncture at P10 into MKO mice, sacrificed the animals 30 days later and examined the DRG [Fig. 7e]. The MKO mice overexpressing laforin exhibited a dramatic accumulation of LBs in the DRG compared to practically none in MKO mice treated with PBS (as expected at this age) [Fig. 7f], indicating that laforin-driven LB formation does not occur via increased malin levels or via malin at all.

Discussion

Approximately one third of the lafoin protein is a phosphatase that dephosphorylates glycogen and another third is a carbohydrate binding domain with which it binds glycogen. Finally, through its binding with malin, laforin scaffolds most of the cell's malin to glycogen [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. While several different functions have been suggested for the laforin and malin enzymes, the most important appears to be prevention of glycogen branch over-elongation and resultant LB formation [4]. In fact, in the mouse models, downregulation (e.g. with an antisense oligonucleotide) of the glycogen branch elongating enzyme glycogen synthase, or digestion of the LBs themselves by amylase, leads to corrections of the neuroinflammatory, neurometabolic and behavioral phenotypes of the disease [20,[33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38]]. These approaches have sufficiently been developed to being tested in human clinical trials [clinicaltrials.gov numbers: NCT06609889 and NCT05930223].

While the above approaches are trialed, it is important to explore the therapeutic potential of gene replacement therapy for LD, because, in principle, the ideal therapy for a disease due to a lost gene function is replacement of that function. In LD, where clinical manifestations are delayed and progressive, early gene replacement could in theory prevent disease onset altogether.

For diseases of the brain the usual administration route for viral gene therapy is the CSF because 1) this fluid bathes the brain, allowing first-pass viral diffusion in its parenchyma, 2) CSF is relatively immune-privileged, 3) the approach avoids the challenge imposed by the blood-brain barrier (compared to alternative intravenous administration) and 4) it avoids the requirement of very high viral doses and associated risks of direct or immune-mediated toxicity associated with intravenous administration, not to mention cost [26,39,40]. The downside is that the virus does not distribute uniformly. It spreads from high concentration at the site of injection to low in distant regions, resulting in potentially excessive local transduction around the site of injection and risk of overexpression toxicity to lack of transduction in farther regions. For our experiments we set the high dose at 5 × 1011 vg per mouse, which is very close to the equivalent highest human dose used to date in human clinical trials in other diseases (e.g. 1 × 1015 vg per patient for Neuronal Ceroid Lipofuscinosis 7) [41]. With the EPM2B gene driven by the JeT promoter this led to no toxicity including in the DRG and to meaningful efficacy with LB reductions in the 36–45 % range in the major brain regions (hippocampus, cortex, cerebellum) with an average of 37 % across the brain based on biochemical (glycogen) quantification, as well as 50 % reduction in the Cxcl10 marker of brain inflammation. Our CSF-administered AAV9-JeT-EPM2B vector is thus primed for further development for gene therapy for the EPM2B form of LD. As a note regarding peripheral organs, as seen in Fig. 4, Fig. 6, this brain-directed therapy appears to be even more strongly effective outside than in the nervous system. Muscle, liver and heart form plenteous LBs in LD but remain mostly clinically unaffected in the shortened lifespans of these patients. Successfully treating the brain would extend lifespan, but it appears at the same time to prevent peripheral disease that might otherwise manifest.

While this work was in progress another group published murine gene therapy results for EPM2B [7]. In their AAV9 vector, the EPM2B gene was not packaged in self-complementary fashion or codon-optimized, which would be expected to result in lower transduction and expression, respectively, compared to our construct. Their promoter was the CAG promoter (similar to the CBh promoter) which would be expected to result in higher expression compared to our JeT promoter. Administration was ICV with a dose approximately 100 times less than the dose used in our study. They obtained no reductions in LBs in any brain region, possibly because of overall lower transduction and/or expression compared to our experiments. They did obtain several, albeit transient, inflammatory and behavioral improvements, possibly by correcting phenotypes not related to LBs. Taking their findings together with ours, it is clear that there is a relatively open path forward to developing safe and effective CSF-delivered AAV9 based gene replacement therapy for the EPM2B form of LD.

For EPM2A, our initial strong CBh promoter led to dramatic and rapid LB formation in the DRG, and was not utilized further. With the weak JeT promoter and lumbar IT high-dose (5 × 1011 vg) viral delivery we obtained significant reductions in LBs of 30 % in the cerebellum and 57 % in the hippocampus, but no significant reduction in the cortex. Total brain glycogen was reduced by 37 %. There was not a significant reduction in Cxcl10. This dose did result in the accelerated LB formation in the DRG. The 4 times lowered dose of 1.25 × 1011 vg was not effective in reducing brain LBs, but also was not toxic to the DRG. With these results we seemed to have found a tenuous range for possibly obtaining some efficacy without toxicity.

The group that published the above-reviewed ICV gene therapy for EPM2B obtained the following results for gene therapy with EPM2A (promoter, construct, route of administration and dosing were the same as for their EPM2B experiments except EPM2A replaces EPM2B in the construct). There was 30–40 % reduction in LBs in the hippocampus (which is immediately adjacent to the cerebral ventricles), but not elsewhere. They did not report on brain-wide glycogen or on the state of the DRG [5]. These results suggest that low dose with overall low expression delivered in the CSF far from the DRG can be safely effective in proximate brain regions, but not farther afield. The DRG could potentially be spared from EPM2A overexpression-driven LB formation despite their particular sensitivity to this effect. Taking all the CSF directed EPM2A gene therapy results together, it is clear that CSF delivery is less than an ideal route for this form of LD because of the unavoidable high local transduction and expression required (with resultant toxicity) for sufficient viral diffusion to much of the rest of the brain for efficacy. It appears that an intravenous (IV) approach providing uniform viral distribution should be considered. This was actually carried out by the same group mentioned so far. They packaged each of their above EPM2A and EPM2B vectors in the AAV9P31 virus, delivered 1.9 × 1012 vg/kg and obtained 35–50 % LB reduction in the hippocampus and cortex for EPM2A and 40–45 % reduction only in the hippocampus for EPM2B. They did not report on total brain glycogen or the state of the DRG [6]. AAV9P31 is a recombinant strain that facilitates blood-brain barrier (BBB) passage in mice [42,43]. However, as the group who used it for these studies notes, this does not translate to rats and nonhuman primates [6,44], and is therefore unlikely to sufficiently cross the BBB in humans. Notwithstanding, there presently are AAV9 strains designed specifically to cross the human BBB [45], which should allow further exploration of the IV route for LD, particularly EPM2A, gene therapy.

The paradoxical EPM2A overexpression-driven LB formation has the following particularities. While it can occur elsewhere in the nervous system, and for that matter in the rest of the organism (heart, skeletal muscle, liver), it predominates in the DRG. This is not solely due to the proximity of lumbar intrathecal administration, as it preferentially happened in the DRG even with cerebroventricular dosing. The DRG and its function can therefore serve the purpose of sentinel for toxicity studies in preclinical and clinical gene replacement therapy development. DRG toxicity in preclinical studies following high dose AAV gene therapy has been reported widely before, and in general appears to be primarily due to transgene overexpression and not the viral load [[46], [47], [48], [49]]. Usually, the toxicity includes axonal or neuronal degeneration/necrosis, mononuclear cell infiltration and inflammation, and satellitosis [46,47,50]. With limited microscopic inspection so far, we do not see these pathological features. We also do not note overt physical weakness, sensory loss or incoordination during basic mouse handling and observation. However, this could relate to a limitation of our study, namely its short follow-up. Given the known time-dependent inflammation and neurodegeneration in the brain in LD, the rapid LB formation in the DRG in the present case is certainly concerning. Longer term studies including detailed neuropathological analyses and formal behavior testing should clarify these matters, and, as a potential general advance in understanding LD, help establish temporal, mechanistic and behavioral connections between LBs and LD neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration and symptomatology. For practical purposes in developing a gene therapy for the EPM2A form of the disease, preclinical safety studies need to be sufficiently long, and clinical development may require including monitoring nerve conduction studies.

As mentioned, a feature of laforin-overexpression driven LB formation is that its rate in mice is much higher with the human versus native murine ortholog of the gene. Development of EPM2A gene therapy may require basing preclinical studies on the natural ortholog with bridging studies to translate to humans. The rate of LB formation is dependent on high cellular quantities of laforin irrespective of how this occurs including through the unexplained effect of the C > S mutation that inactivates laforin phosphatase's catalytic domain. What cellular levels laforin needs to reach before triggering LB formation is not possible to determine from our current results for two reasons: 1) the phenomenon is time-dependent and our experiments went only to three months, and 2) as mentioned, CSF administration results in graded protein levels from very high to very low. If we were to grossly estimate from the totality of our data we would venture a guess that approximately hundredfold higher than normal cellular levels are needed for laforin to switch from LB prevention to generation. Given LD's severity as a disease, perhaps local toxicity could be an acceptable side effect. Still, it seems preferable for gene therapy for the EPM2A form of LD to seek an approach with uniform (IV) versus graded (CSF) delivery.

Astride their significance to gene therapy development our serendipitous results afford potential insights into the unknown mechanisms of LB formation in LD, which in turn could open new therapeutic entry points. As mentioned, regular glycogen metabolism and overall cellular activities of the classical enzymes of this metabolism are not affected in LD. One leading hypothesis is that the laforin-malin complex is dedicated to regulating a tiny fraction of glycogen that for various reasons (e.g. occasional outpacing of glycogen branching enzyme by glycogen synthase) acquire overlong branches and the associated risk of precipitation and accrual into LBs [30]. Possibly, such long branches are marked by phosphorylation and the laforin-malin complex detects and binds them through the laforin carbohydrate domain's known preference for longer glucan chains [[51], [52], [53]] and the laforin phosphatase domain's known affinity for phosphorylated glucans [10,11,54,55]. Malin would then inhibit enzymes involved in branch elongation such as glycogen synthase or its multiple activators. Having halted the overelongation, malin could ubiquitinate and remove laforin to allow reshortening of the long branches. The enzyme that would perform this last task would be glycogen phosphorylase, which is known to stop at phosphorylated glucans [30], necessitating laforin to remove the phosphate mark before being removed itself and allowing phosphorylase to resume. Many parts of this hypothesis need confirmation, but one thing is clear: the only two enzymatic activities of the laforin-malin complex, namely laforin's phosphatase and malin's E3 ubiquitin ligase, must have roles, yet to be uncovered, in preventing LB formation in health. In the present work we tested whether either is operative in overexpression-induced LB formation and found that neither is: laforin overexpression can form LBs while lacking phosphatase activity and its partner malin. Possibly, the over hundredfold laforin levels first overwhelm malin and then scaffold glycogen synthase (plus/minus its activators) to glycogen sites devoid of malin where branch overelongations take place with pursuant glycogen precipitation. To speculate slightly further, while LBs are considered insoluble because they separate from the aqueous phase of tissue extract preparation, recently it was reported that accumulating glycogen can form intracellular liquid droplets through liquid-liquid phase separation [56]. Conceivably, these microenvironments rich in laforin-scaffolded glycogen synthase become rapidly expanding sites of abnormal glycogen generation and accumulation manifesting as these LBs forming at much greater rates than the LBs of laforin or malin deficiency (i.e. of LD).

We mentioned that malin levels are tightly controlled by unknown post-transcriptional mechanisms. This is favorable for gene therapy and may be the reason we observed no signs of toxicity with AAV9-JeT-EPM2B even at high dose. Regarding laforin, our results suggest there is cellular need to maintain its amounts also regulated, but that this would occur at the protein level, because we are readily able to raise its levels through overexpression. Ubiquitin mediated proteasomal degradation is the leading protein level regulation, and if this is the mechanism for laforin, its interactor malin would be the candidate ligase. In fact, even at the establishment of malin's N-terminus as an E3 ligase domain 20 years ago it was demonstrated, in in vitro experiments, that malin ubiquitinates laforin and directs it to proteasomal degradation [17]. Subsequent cellular work brought evidence of LB-like accumulations in cell cultures overexpressing laforin [57]. These observations remained unpursued because it remains counterintuitive how in an autosomal recessive disease one disease gene product, malin, would have the function of destroying the other, laforin. The present in vivo results should revive these early observations and trigger considering laforin-malin levels as finely regulated as the fine function the complex serves, namely maintaining the architecture of the cytosol's by far largest macromolecule, glycogen, in near-perfect sphericity and resultant solubility. Finally, it is also remarkable how at a macro level, i.e. as the malstructured precipitated glycogen molecules accumulate into LBs, these bodies take specific structures (‘boulders’ or ‘multitudinous pebbles’) irrespective whether formed by laforin deficiency or overexpression.

In summary, our results suggest that CSF-directed gene therapy for the EPM2B form of LD is eminently possible. Greater caution is needed for the EPM2A form of the disease and more refined approaches warranted. These could include a feedback-regulated construct [58], such as is in clinical trial presently for Rett syndrome, or constructs regulating expression in the most susceptible cells (DRG) as previously published [59]. Alternatively, an intravenous approach may be sought utilizing viral vectors that cross the blood-brain barrier through facilitated transport [45], to afford uniformity of viral distribution and avoid the local over-transduction that characterizes CSF routes. At the molecular level our results uncover and begin to characterize a new mechanism of LB generation, the further understanding of which may elucidate the finer aspects of glycogen metabolism, LB formation and LD.
