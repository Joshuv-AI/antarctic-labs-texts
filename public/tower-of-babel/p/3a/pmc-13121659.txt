Computational multi-omics integration suggests an association between LAMA3 expression, immune suppression, and metabolic programs in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Ruixiang Li, Yuheng Gu, Hua Yang, Shangke Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal malignancy in which immune dysregulation and metabolic remodeling are common features. Laminin subunit alpha 3 (LAMA3), a component of laminin-332 involved in cell–ECM adhesion and integrin-linked signaling, has not been systematically characterized in PDAC in a multi-omics context.MethodsWe conducted an in silico integrative multi-omics analysis using bulk and single-cell transcriptomic datasets, together with WGCNA, functional enrichment, immune deconvolution, CNV inference, pseudotime trajectory analysis, and regulatory network modeling. Candidate upstream regulators were explored computationally, and molecular docking was performed for hypothesis generation.ResultsLAMA3 showed higher expression in PDAC compared with non-tumor controls and was associated with pathways related to cell adhesion, extracellular matrix organization/remodeling, and immune-related programs. In bulk deconvolution analyses, higher LAMA3 expression was associated with increased macrophage-related signatures and lower estimated CD8⁺T-cell infiltration. Single-cell analyses indicated predominant expression in epithelial populations and higher CNV activity in LAMA3-high states; LAMA3 expression also tended to increase along inferred pseudotime trajectories, consistent with more advanced malignant epithelial programs. Enrichment analyses further linked LAMA3-high tumors to cell-cycle-, p53-related, and metabolic gene sets. Regulatory modeling prioritized EP300 as a putative upstream transcriptional regulator, and docking analyses suggested a potential interaction between Prostaglandin J2 and EP300, supporting an indirect, hypothesis-generating therapeutic avenue.ConclusionThis computational multi-omics study implicates LAMA3 as a candidate biomarker and a potential contributor to adhesion-associated tumor programs and an immunologically constrained microenvironment in PDAC. These findings are association-based and require experimental validation (e.g., functional perturbation and orthogonal immune profiling) before causal or therapeutic claims can be made.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04804-9.

Introduction

PDAC is a highly aggressive malignancy with an exceptionally poor prognosis, characterized by a 5-year survival rate of less than 10% [1, 2]. It has emerged as one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. The asymptomatic nature of PDAC during its early stages, pronounced molecular heterogeneity, and marked resistance to conventional chemoradiotherapy have collectively imposed significant challenges on its early detection and effective treatment [1]. In recent years, the rapid evolution of multi-omics technologies has progressively unraveled key molecular regulatory networks underlying tumorigenesis, thereby offering new avenues for biomarker discovery and precision oncology in PDAC [3].

Among the major biological events implicated in PDAC progression, ECM remodeling, cell adhesion dysregulation, immune evasion, and genomic instability have been identified as central drivers of tumor evolution. As a critical component of the ECM, the laminin family plays a fundamental role in maintaining epithelial polarity, basement membrane integrity, and tumor cell dissemination [4, 5]. LAMA3, a key constituent of the Laminin-332 complex, has been increasingly recognized in epithelial-derived malignancies—such as lung, gastric, and head and neck squamous cell carcinomas—as a pro-migratory, pro-invasive molecule potentially involved in immune modulation [6–9]. However, the expression profile, functional dynamics, and clinical implications of LAMA3 in PDAC remain insufficiently characterized.

This study aims to comprehensively investigate the expression patterns, biological functions, and potential clinical utility of LAMA3 in PDAC. By integrating WGCNA, differential expression profiling, GO and KEGG enrichment, survival correlation analysis, immune infiltration evaluation, single-cell transcriptomic characterization, CNV inference, pseudotime trajectory reconstruction, and multi-layered regulatory network modeling involving TFs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs, this work systematically delineates the regulatory role of LAMA3 in PDAC pathogenesis.

In addition, potential regulatory axes involving LAMA3 and druggable upstream targets were explored to provide mechanistic insight and identify novel intervention strategies. Collectively, the present study seeks to elucidate the multidimensional regulatory landscape of LAMA3 in PDAC, uncover its functional roles in tumor heterogeneity, immune microenvironment remodeling, and metabolic reprogramming, and offer a theoretical foundation and data support for target-oriented therapeutic development and biomarker discovery.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Bulk transcriptomic data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession number GSE62452, comprising 61 normal pancreatic tissue samples and 69 tumor samples from patients with PDAC. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were retrieved from dataset GSE278688, which included 13 PDAC tumor samples and 5 adjacent normal tissue samples [10]. Summary-level genetic association data for summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) analysis were derived from the FinnGen consortium release titled “finngen_R12_C3_PANCREAS_ADENO_DUCTAL_EXALLC”.

Schematic overview of the analytical framework

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

To identify gene modules significantly associated with PDAC, WGCNA (v1.73) was implemented using R. Hierarchical clustering was first performed to detect and eliminate outlier samples [11]. The appropriate soft-thresholding power (β) was selected using the pickSoftThreshold function to construct an adjacency matrix, which was subsequently transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). Based on TOM-derived similarity measures, genes with similar expression profiles were clustered using average linkage hierarchical clustering into distinct modules. The correlation between each module and the PDAC phenotype was then computed to identify key modules with putative functional relevance.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

DEG screening between PDAC and normal tissues was performed using the limma package (v3.58.1) in R [12]. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change (logFC)| > 1 were defined as significantly differentially expressed. To maintain analytical sensitivity, genes with nominal p-value < 0.05 were also retained for downstream analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional characterization of DEGs was conducted via GO annotation (including biological processes and cellular components) and KEGG pathway enrichment using the clusterProfiler package (v4.10.1) in conjunction with org.Hs.eg.db (v3.18.0) [13–15]. Visualization was achieved through the enrichplot (v1.22.0) and ggplot2 (v3.5.1) packages. Additionally, GSEA (vWin_4.4.0) was performed using the c2 curated gene set as the reference to detect significantly enriched pathways [16]. Enrichment was considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

To address immune regulation specifically, we pre-specified four KEGG immune pathways—Antigen processing & presentation, Proteasome, Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and Chemokine signaling—and performed immune-focused pre-ranked GSEA on the LAMA3-high vs. LAMA3-low ranked list. We summarize enrichment using NES and FDR q-values (significant at FDR q < 0.05; exploratory at 0.05–0.25); NES > 0 denotes enrichment in the LAMA3-high group. For sample-level visualization we computed ssGSEA (GSVA) scores for these pathways. Because PDAC is rich in stroma, ESTIMATE Purity/Stromal scores were included in regression/stratified analyses to mitigate confounding.

Protein–protein interaction network construction

Potential PPI relationships among identified genes were analyzed using the STRING database. The resulting interaction network was visualized in Cytoscape software. Core subnetwork modules were identified using the MCODE plugin, which enabled clustering of highly interconnected gene groups. To determine central regulatory genes, the cytoHubba plugin was employed, which ranks nodes based on topological features using multiple scoring algorithms to identify hub genes within the network.

Machine learning and clinical correlation analysis

All machine-learning analyses were conducted in R using glmnet (v4.1-8) for LASSO, randomForest (v4.7-1.1) for RF, and neuralnet (v1.44.2) for ANN, with NeuralNetTools (v1.5.3) for model interpretation. Full session information and pinned package versions are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Unless otherwise noted, all resampling procedures and model fits used a fixed random seed of 123. Models were evaluated on a patient-level non-overlapping held-out test cohort. All preprocessing and feature selection (normalization, feature filtering, and LASSO variable selection) were performed within training folds only; the held-out test set remained isolated until final evaluation.

To identify core genes associated with PDAC while improving predictive accuracy and robustness, we applied a multi-algorithm pipeline. First, LASSO regression (glmnet) was used for feature selection and dimensionality reduction. Next, a random forest classifier (randomForest) was trained to assess the relative importance of candidate genes in distinguishing PDAC from normal tissues. Finally, artificial neural network models (neuralnet, assisted by NeuralNetTools) were fitted to further validate and optimize the selected gene signature [17].

Diagnostic performance was quantified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the ROC curve (AUC). For clinical relevance, we examined associations between gene expression and clinicopathological features (e.g., tumor stage, lesion size). We then fitted univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the independent prognostic significance of the core genes in PDAC.

Immune infiltration and single-cell transcriptomic analysis

Immune cell infiltration characteristics in PDAC tissues were inferred using the CIBERSORT algorithm, which deconvoluted bulk RNA-seq data to estimate the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes across samples. Subsequent analysis involved Pearson correlation calculations to evaluate associations between the expression levels of key genes and the abundance of specific immune cell populations.

We processed data with Seurat (v5). After creating Seurat objects, we applied unified QC thresholds: we retained cells with 300–7,000 detected genes and nUMI > 1,000, and discarded cells with mitochondrial RNA fraction > 20% or hemoglobin gene fraction > 5% (HBA1, HBA2, HBB, HBD, HBG1, HBG2). To avoid systematically removing highly proliferative tumor cells solely due to high UMI counts, we did not hard-remove the top 3% UMI cells; instead, putative doublets were identified with DoubletFinder (pK optimized via sweep; expected doublet rate set by library size/CellRanger estimates) or Scrublet, and only doublet-flagged cells were excluded. Ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX with cross-validation by DecontX. After QC, the top 2,000 highly variable genes were used for PCA/UMAP and downstream analyses. We performed a sensitivity analysis by re-running the entire workflow under stringent (mt ≤ 15%, Hb ≤ 3%) / main (mt ≤ 20%, Hb ≤ 5%) / lenient (mt ≤ 25%, Hb ≤ 10%) thresholds and with/without removing the top-3% UMI cells.

Focusing on epithelial cell populations within tumor samples, subcluster annotation was performed, followed by trajectory inference using the Monocle package (v2.30.1) [18]. Genes exhibiting significant differential expression across subclusters (q-value < 0.01) were selected as ordering genes to guide pseudotime reconstruction. Principal component analysis was applied for dimensionality reduction, and Discriminative Dimensionality Reduction with Trees (DDRTree) was used to embed the expression matrix in a low-dimensional space, generating a continuous trajectory of cell state transitions. The shortest path algorithm was utilized to assign pseudotime values to each cell, reflecting their progression along the differentiation trajectory.

Gene expression dynamics along pseudotime were modeled using generalized linear models (GLMs). Genes showing statistically significant variation (adjusted q-value < 0.05) were identified via the Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing and visualized along the trajectory to reveal dynamic transcriptional changes associated with cell-state transitions.

Rationale for immune analyses and confounder control. We used deconvolution-based correlation to quantify associations between LAMA3 and inferred immune-cell fractions (cellular composition). In parallel, we performed immune-focused GSEA (KEGG: Antigen processing & presentation, Proteasome, Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, Chemokine signaling) to test pathway-level program differences between LAMA3-high and LAMA3-low tumors. These approaches address complementary questions (composition vs. pathway activity). Given PDAC’s desmoplastic stroma, we considered ESTIMATE Purity/Stromal scores as potential confounders in regression or stratified analyses.

Somatic copy number variation analysis

Large-scale somatic copy number variation (CNV) features in tumor epithelial cells were investigated using the inferCNV package (v1.6.0) in R [19].Required inputs—raw count matrices, cell annotations, and gene–chromosomal coordinate mappings—were prepared according to the official specification. Adjacent epithelium was used as the reference population. We ran infercnv::run with cutoff = 0, denoise = TRUE and noise_filter = 0.2 (i.e., a boolean denoise switch with a numeric de-noising threshold), window_length = 101, cluster_by_groups = TRUE, HMM = FALSE, and seed = 123. Segment-level CNV scores were derived from the denoised, reference-normalized intensities by summarizing the mean absolute deviation within each contiguous genomic segment; when a single per-cell burden was required, we summed segment scores to obtain a quantitative measure of somatic structural variation at the single-cell level.

Molecular regulatory network construction of key genes

Upstream regulatory mechanisms of key genes were elucidated by first predicting potential transcription factors (TFs) using the HTFtarget database. Candidate miRNA–mRNA interactions were inferred through integrated analysis across PITA, microT, and TargetScan databases, with only those consistently identified by all three tools retained for high-confidence interpretation [20]. Regulatory associations between miRNAs and lncRNAs were subsequently identified using miRNet, StarBase, and LncBase v3, based on consensus across platforms [21–24]. Furthermore, potential TFs interacting with candidate lncRNAs were predicted via LncACTdb 3.0, thereby completing the upstream regulatory landscape of key genes.

SMR colocalization analysis, drug–gene interaction, and protein–protein docking

SMR analysis was conducted based on the FinnGen R12 PDAC-associated dataset (finngen_R12_C3_PANCREAS_ADENO_DUCTAL_EXALLC), in conjunction with European-ancestry genotype and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data from the 1000 Genomes Project [25–27]. The SMR software (v1.3.1-win) was used with the following parameters: HEIDI test P > 0.05, F-statistic > 10, and exclusion of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region (chr6:26–34 Mb) to improve causal inference specificity.

Potential therapeutic strategies were explored by querying candidate small-molecule compounds targeting key genes from the DSigDB database. Protein–protein interaction docking simulations were subsequently conducted using the CB-Dock2 online server [28]. Three-dimensional conformations of the resulting protein complexes were rendered and structurally analyzed in PyMOL to interpret potential binding mechanisms.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in RStudio (v4.3.3) using appropriate R packages. Group differences in continuous variables were assessed via the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while correlations between variables were calculated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Unless otherwise specified, statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, and only results meeting this threshold were interpreted further.

Results

To identify gene modules associated with PDAC, WGCNA was applied to construct a scale-free gene network. The optimal soft-thresholding power (β) was determined using the pickSoftThreshold function. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A, a power of β = 11 achieved a satisfactory scale-free topology fit. A total of 10 distinct modules were subsequently identified through dynamic tree cut clustering and were labeled as black, blue, brown, green, greenyellow, grey, magenta, purple, red, and tan (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The number of genes within each module is depicted in Fig. 2A.

Module–trait associations were evaluated by correlating each module eigengene with sample phenotype (tumor vs. normal). A heatmap of Pearson correlation coefficients revealed that the blue module exhibited the strongest positive correlation with PDAC samples (r = 0.66, P = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁷), indicating robust statistical significance (Fig. 2B). Based on this association, the blue module was selected for further downstream analyses.

The relationship between module membership and gene significance for PDAC was further confirmed by scatterplot analysis. Genes within the blue module demonstrated a high degree of intra-modular connectivity and phenotype relevance (cor = 0.972, P = 1.4 × 10⁻³¹²; Fig. 2C), supporting the biological relevance of this module to PDAC progression.

Identification of differentially expressed genes between normal and PDAC tissues

Gene expression data from the GSE62452 dataset were first normalized across samples (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between PDAC and normal tissues were then identified using the limma package. The overall distribution of DEGs was visualized using a volcano plot (Fig. 2D). The top 100 DEGs ranked by statistical significance were used to construct a heatmap, illustrating distinct expression patterns across tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 2E).

Further refinement of candidate genes for downstream analysis was achieved by intersecting the top 200 DEGs with those assigned to the blue module. This integrative approach resulted in the identification of 94 genes that were both significantly differentially expressed and co-expressed within the PDAC-associated module (Fig. 2F). These genes were subsequently designated as core candidates for functional annotation and mechanistic investigation.

Biological processes and signaling pathways potentially regulated by the identified DEGs were explored through functional enrichment analysis based on the GO and KEGG databases.

GO biological process analysis (Fig. 3A) revealed significant enrichment in terms such as skin development, epidermis development, integrin-mediated signaling pathway, cell-matrix adhesion, and cell-substrate adhesion. Notably, LAMA3, LAMB3, and COL17A1 were highly involved in epidermis development (Fig. 3B).

In the GO cellular component category, enriched terms included integrin complex, basal part of cell, cell-substrate junction, and other adhesion-related protein complexes (Fig. 3C). Among these, COL17A1, LAMA3, ITGB6, and ITGB4 showed prominent enrichment in cell-substrate junctions (Fig. 3D).

Intersection analysis between the two methods yielded four robust diagnostic genes: COL17A1, LAMA3, ITGB4, and LAMC2 (Fig. 5E).

Immune infiltration profiling in PDAC

Comprehensive characterization of the PDAC immune microenvironment was achieved using the CIBERSORT algorithm, which estimated the relative abundance of 22 immune cell subtypes. Compared with normal tissues, PDAC samples exhibited significantly elevated infiltration of resting CD4 memory T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and resting mast cells (Fig. 7A).

A heatmap of immune cell correlations was generated to assess intercellular relationships (Fig. 7B). Based on these findings, representative immune cell subsets with strong and statistically significant correlations were selected for comparative visualization using boxplots (Fig. 7C–F), highlighting immune infiltration dynamics between groups.

Focusing on the prioritized candidate LAMA3, its association with immune cell infiltration was analyzed. Correlation scatterplots revealed negative associations between LAMA3 expression and multiple immune cell types (Fig. 7G). More specifically, LAMA3 expression was negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T cells (r = − 0.30, p = 6.45 × 10⁻³), resting mast cells (r = − 0.25, p = 2.61 × 10⁻²), and monocytes (r = − 0.33, p = 2.74 × 10⁻³). These findings suggest an immunosuppressive profile associated with high LAMA3 expression in PDAC.

CNV inference and pseudotime analysis of tumor epithelial cells at the single-cell level

Following rigorous quality control, a total of 104,913 high-quality single cells were retained for downstream analyses. Using a clustering resolution of 0.8, 32 distinct cell clusters were identified. Based on classical marker gene expression profiles, UMAP-based spatial distribution, and bubble plot marker visualization, manual annotation was performed, resulting in the classification of six major cell types: B cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, and T cells. The spatial distribution of all cells in two dimensions was visualized via UMAP (Fig. 8A).

Comparison of cellular composition between adjacent normal and PDAC tissues revealed a significant increase in the proportion of epithelial and myeloid cells in tumor samples (Fig. 8B). Functional clustering of individual cell subsets was conducted using the ClusterGVis package. Heatmaps of module gene expression across cell types, along with corresponding pathway enrichment plots, were generated (Fig. 8C). The enriched pathways spanned multiple key biological processes involved in the tumor immune microenvironment, including immunoglobulin-mediated adaptive immune responses, ECM remodeling, antimicrobial peptide production, B/T cell activation, leukocyte chemotaxis, myeloid cell migration, endothelial differentiation, and angiogenesis.

Epithelial cell heterogeneity was further dissected by isolating this cell type for subcluster annotation, followed by two- and three-dimensional visualization using UMAP (Fig. 8D, E). Large-scale CNV inference was then applied across all epithelial subpopulations to distinguish potential malignant clones (Fig. 8F). Violin plot analysis of CNV scores revealed that subclusters Epi_1, Epi_2, Epi_3, Epi_5, Epi_6, Epi_7, and Epi_8 exhibited elevated CNV activity, indicative of malignant phenotypes. In contrast, Epi_4 displayed relatively low CNV levels and was inferred to represent normal or low-malignancy epithelial cells (Fig. 8G).

Temporal dynamics of LAMA3 during epithelial cell fate transitions were examined using pseudotime trajectory analysis across epithelial subclusters. A progressive increase in LAMA3 expression was observed along the pseudotime axis (Fig. 8H–J). Integration of pseudotime trajectories with LAMA3 expression gradients onto UMAP projections further revealed a clear spatial–temporal alignment between gene upregulation and epithelial progression (Fig. 8K, L).

Molecular regulatory network of LAMA3

The potential immunologic programs associated with LAMA3 in PDAC were investigated using immune-focused, pre-ranked GSEA (Fig. 9A). Tumors with high LAMA3 expression showed significant enrichment of Antigen processing and presentation (NES = 1.670, FDR = 0.008), Proteasome (NES = 2.368, FDR < 0.001), Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction (NES = 1.542, FDR = 0.002), and Chemokine signaling (NES = 1.657, FDR = 0.001), indicating activation of antigen-presentation machinery together with inflammatory/chemotactic signaling in the LAMA3-high state (NES > 0 denotes enrichment in LAMA3-high, per Methods). These pathway-level changes align with an inflamed-but-constrained tumor immune microenvironment described elsewhere in the manuscript.

A multilayered TF–mRNA–miRNA–lncRNA regulatory network was then constructed to delineate the upstream and downstream interactions of LAMA3 (Fig. 9B, C). TF prediction identified KLF4 and EP300 as potential upstream regulators of LAMA3. Downstream, several miRNAs—hsa-miR-1290, hsa-miR-106a-5p, hsa-miR-106b-5p, and hsa-miR-1279—were predicted to target LAMA3, potentially influencing lncRNAs such as LINC01106 and RP11-369C8.1 through post-transcriptional regulation.

Moreover, SMR analysis was performed to assess the genetic regulatory relationship between LAMA3 and eQTLs. The results revealed significant colocalization signals, with multiple loci showing strong association (pSMR < 0.01), supporting the presence of a genetically regulated expression mechanism for LAMA3 in PDAC (Fig. 9D).

As a structural subunit of the Laminin-332 complex, LAMA3 lacks a stable independent folding domain and does not form a well-defined ligand-binding pocket in its isolated state. This structural limitation reduces its feasibility as a direct target for small-molecule docking or receptor modeling, thereby restricting its druggability.

To overcome this limitation, focus was shifted to the upstream regulatory TF EP300. Using the DSigDB database, Prostaglandin J2 (CTD:00001744) was identified as a candidate small-molecule ligand potentially interacting with EP300. A protein–ligand docking simulation was conducted to assess the structural compatibility of this interaction (Fig. 9E). The results indicated that Prostaglandin J2 binds stably to the functional domain of EP300, forming favorable interactions via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts with surrounding amino acid residues. These findings suggest high binding affinity and conformational compatibility, highlighting EP300 as a plausible indirect pharmacological target for modulating LAMA3-associated pathways in PDAC.

Discussion

LAMA3 encodes the alpha3 chain of the laminin family and, together with LAMB3 and LAMC2, forms the heterotrimeric laminin-332 complex [29, 30]. Laminin-332 is mainly localized to the basement membrane of epithelial tissues and contributes to extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion, signal transduction, maintenance of epithelial polarity, and cell migration [31, 32]. As a ligand in integrin-mediated signaling, LAMA3 has been implicated in embryonic development, tissue repair, and cancer-related processes [33]. In epithelial-derived malignancies, altered LAMA3 expression has been repeatedly associated with invasive phenotypes, metastatic dissemination, and adverse outcomes, although the direction and causality of these relationships may vary across tumor contexts.

In the present study, we used an integrative in silico multi-omics framework - including WGCNA, differential expression analysis, functional enrichment, survival association analyses, immune deconvolution, single-cell transcriptomics, CNV inference, pseudotime reconstruction, and regulatory network modeling - to characterize LAMA3-related patterns in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Across bulk co-expression and differential analyses, LAMA3 showed consistently higher expression in tumor samples and was prioritized as a core candidate within the analyzed gene sets. We further observed that higher LAMA3 expression was associated with features commonly linked to aggressive disease, including unfavorable prognosis, immune-microenvironment differences, epithelial states along inferred trajectories, and elevated chromosomal instability signals. Importantly, these associations are derived from computational analyses of public datasets and do not establish causality; experimental validation will be required to determine whether and how LAMA3 contributes to PDAC progression.

Functional enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG) indicated that the selected core genes were overrepresented in tumor-relevant processes and pathways, particularly those related to cell adhesion, membrane-associated structures, and ECM organization. Enrichment of canonical KEGG pathways such as ECM-receptor interaction and focal adhesion is consistent with a potential involvement of these genes in physical and biochemical interactions between tumor cells and the surrounding stroma [34].

Among these candidates, LAMA3 was repeatedly highlighted across multiple enriched terms and pathways and mapped to adhesion-related subcellular annotations, including cell-substrate junctions and the integrin complex. These observations are consistent with a putative role for LAMA3 in epithelial-basement membrane attachment and integrin-linked signaling, which may influence invasive behavior. In addition, protein-protein interaction (PPI) topology analyses ranked LAMA3 as a highly connected node (hub) using several centrality algorithms (MCC, Degree, EPC, DMNC, and MNC), indicating that it resides in a densely connected interaction neighborhood in curated PPI resources. As with all network-based inferences, such hub status reflects database-supported connectivity and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of functional centrality in vivo.

Collectively, our results support the interpretation that LAMA3 is more than a structural ECM component and may be linked to signaling programs within the tumor microenvironment. Through reported interactions with proteins such as ITGB4, ITGB6, and COL17A1, LAMA3 could participate in an integrin-associated interaction module that integrates adhesion cues with downstream responses. Based on prior literature, laminin-332/integrin signaling and ECM remodeling are plausible mechanisms through which LAMA3-associated programs might relate to invasion and metastasis; however, direct mechanistic claims require dedicated functional assays [35–37].

Regarding immunity, we observed an inverse association between LAMA3 expression and several antitumor immune cell estimates in bulk deconvolution. Specifically, CIBERSORT-based analyses suggested that higher LAMA3 expression correlated with lower estimated infiltration of CD8 + T cells, monocytes, and resting mast cells [38]. These findings are compatible with (but do not prove) a relationship between LAMA3-high tumor states and an immunologically constrained microenvironment. Because deconvolution results can be influenced by tumor purity, stromal content, and reference signatures, orthogonal validation (e.g., spatial/IF/IHC quantification and functional assays) will be needed to clarify whether LAMA3 expression affects immune recruitment, activation, or both.

Notably, single-cell RNA-seq comparisons between tumor and adjacent tissues did not indicate a uniform reduction of T-cell proportions in tumors; in some samples, T-cell abundance appeared higher. This suggests that immune suppression in PDAC may reflect functional impairment (e.g., exhaustion or checkpoint-mediated inhibition) rather than simple quantitative exclusion. The observed negative association between LAMA3 expression and CD8 + T-cell estimates therefore motivates hypotheses in which LAMA3-associated epithelial/stromal programs may coincide with T-cell dysfunction via indirect mechanisms (e.g., checkpoint signaling, altered antigen presentation, or an exhausted phenotype) [39], which warrant future validation.

We also observed a positive association between LAMA3 expression and M0 macrophage estimates. Given that M0 macrophages can differentiate toward inflammatory (M1) or immunosuppressive (M2) phenotypes depending on context [40], this pattern may indicate that LAMA3-high tumors tend to co-occur with myeloid-enriched states. Based on established biology, ECM remodeling and integrin-linked signaling could influence monocyte/macrophage trafficking and polarization, potentially via the integrin-FAK/PI3K/AKT axis [41, 42]. Nevertheless, the present analyses cannot determine polarization direction or causality, and dedicated macrophage phenotyping and perturbation experiments will be required. Taken together, our data support a model in which LAMA3 expression is associated with a macrophage-skewed landscape and constrained cytotoxic programs in PDAC, rather than providing definitive evidence that LAMA3 directly induces these immune phenotypes.

Consistent with a more aggressive epithelial state, pseudotime analyses suggested that LAMA3 expression tended to increase along inferred epithelial trajectories and was more prominent in CNV-active subpopulations. These spatial-temporal patterns are compatible with the hypothesis that LAMA3 marks (or potentially contributes to) malignant evolution; however, trajectory inference and CNV calling from scRNA-seq are indirect and should be interpreted cautiously.

At the pathway level, tumors with higher LAMA3 expression exhibited enrichment of antigen processing and presentation, proteasome, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and chemokine signaling gene sets (e.g., Antigen processing and presentation: NES = 1.670, FDR = 0.008; Proteasome: NES = 2.368, FDR < 0.001). These shifts may reflect an inflamed-but-constrained context in which antigen presentation and upstream inflammatory ligands are engaged without a proportional increase in effective cytotoxic programs, consistent with bulk deconvolution and single-cell patterns [43–45]. As above, these findings are descriptive of transcriptomic states and require functional validation.

We also observed enrichment of cell-cycle related signatures, suggesting that LAMA3-high tumors may co-occur with proliferative programs involving cyclins, CDKs, and cell-cycle inhibitors (e.g., p21) [46]. Similarly, although LAMA3 is not a canonical component of the p53 pathway, integrin-FAK signaling and ECM remodeling may intersect with apoptosis-related programs, potentially influencing the expression of apoptotic regulators (e.g., BAX, PUMA, MDM2) [47, 48]. These are plausible cross-talk hypotheses rather than demonstrated mechanisms in the current work.

To explore upstream and downstream regulation, we constructed a multi-layer in silico regulatory model integrating predicted TF-mRNA, miRNA-mRNA, and lncRNA-miRNA interactions using public databases and prior literature. KLF4 and EP300 were identified as candidate upstream transcription factors, with EP300 acting as a well-known epigenetic coactivator. Several miRNAs (e.g., hsa-miR-1290, miR-106a-5p, and miR-106b-5p) were predicted to target LAMA3, potentially linking to downstream lncRNAs such as LINC01106 and RP11-369C8.1. While this network provides a coherent set of testable hypotheses, the predicted edges require experimental validation (e.g., ChIP-qPCR/ChIP-seq, luciferase assays, and loss-of-function perturbations).

As additional genetic support, SMR colocalization analyses suggested that LAMA3 expression may share regulatory signals with multiple eQTL loci [49]. These results are consistent with the possibility that inherited variants contribute to inter-individual variation in LAMA3 expression; however, colocalization does not prove a causal gene or mechanism and should be interpreted as supportive evidence for prioritization.

Finally, regarding therapeutic implications, direct targeting of LAMA3 is challenging because ECM components often lack classical small-molecule binding pockets [50]. We therefore explored an indirect strategy by prioritizing the upstream regulator EP300 [51, 52]. Prostaglandin J2 (CTD:00001744) emerged as a candidate compound in database-driven screening, and docking simulations suggested a stable binding pose within an EP300 functional domain. Docking results are hypothesis-generating and do not establish biochemical inhibition; nonetheless, they motivate future studies to evaluate whether EP300 modulation can affect LAMA3 expression and related phenotypes in PDAC models.

Despite the comprehensive multi-omics approach employed in this study, several limitations remain. First, the analyses were primarily based on publicly available datasets, with limited sample diversity and a lack of multicenter validation. Second, the conclusions were largely derived from computational inference and await experimental validation via in vitro or in vivo functional assays. Third, the immunological and regulatory network components identified require deeper mechanistic dissection, and several regulatory relationships remain putative. Finally, the therapeutic targeting strategies proposed remain at the predictive stage and require rigorous pharmacological and preclinical validation. Future studies integrating large-scale omics data with functional experimentation across diverse clinical cohorts are warranted to advance these findings toward translational relevance.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive, multi-dimensional dissection of the biological roles of LAMA3 in PDAC, revealing its critical involvement in cell adhesion, ECM remodeling, immune microenvironment modulation, metabolic reprogramming, and cell cycle progression. Beyond functioning as an adhesion molecule, LAMA3 emerges as a central integrator of multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, potentially driving the malignant evolution of tumor cells. Its association with immune cell dysfunction—such as polarization and exhaustion—further implicates it in facilitating immune evasion. The presence of LAMA3 within CNV-active epithelial subclusters, along with its complex regulatory network and upstream genomic influences, underscores its potential as both a biomarker and an indirect therapeutic target. These findings provide a solid theoretical framework for future research aimed at exploiting LAMA3-centered axes for diagnostic and therapeutic innovation in pancreatic cancer.
