Ethical use of AI in infectious diagnostic decision and therapeutic stewardship
Authors: Prasan Kumar Panda, Sukanya Ghosh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly reshaping healthcare, offering transformative potential in infectious diagnostics and antimicrobial stewardship through enhanced accuracy, efficiency, and predictive capabilities. However, its integration into clinical practice raises significant ethical challenges. These include transparency of decision-making, protection of patient privacy, algorithmic fairness, accountability, and the preservation of human oversight. Global and national bodies have developed guidance to address these concerns: the World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes autonomy, inclusiveness, and equity; the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates adaptive AI as medical devices; the European Union AI Act classifies medical AI as “high-risk”; and the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) highlights accountability, data security, and cultural sensitivity. Drawing on these frameworks, this perspective discusses the ethical imperatives of deploying AI responsibly in infectious diagnostic and therapeutic stewardship. Best practices are outlined to ensure that innovation enhances patient trust, safety, and equity while mitigating risks of misuse or bias.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being applied across the spectrum of healthcare, from diagnostic imaging and pathology to treatment planning and antimicrobial stewardship. The appeal lies in its ability to process vast amounts of clinical and biological data quickly, providing decision support that can improve accuracy, efficiency, and timeliness of care 1, 2. For example, AI-enabled image interpretation has demonstrated performance comparable to radiologists in detecting early cancers [3], while predictive models for sepsis and hospital-acquired infections can support faster triage and intervention [4].

However, AI’s rapid integration into medicine raises profound ethical and regulatory challenges, especially in high-stakes areas such as diagnostics and antimicrobial stewardship. Ethical concerns span transparency, accountability, data privacy, and fairness of algorithms, with growing recognition that AI systems must not only perform well technically but also inspire trust among clinicians, patients, and society 5, 6.

Global agencies have responded with frameworks to guide responsible AI deployment. The World Health Organization (WHO) issued its 2021 guidance on ethics and governance of AI in health, emphasizing autonomy, accountability, inclusiveness, and sustainability [7]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has adopted a regulatory framework for “Software as a Medical Device” (SaMD), with ongoing work on adaptive AI models [8]. The European Union AI Act (2024) places medical AI systems in the “high-risk” category, mandating strict requirements for transparency, safety, and human oversight [9]. In India, the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) published national ethical guidelines for AI in biomedical research in 2023, with particular focus on accountability, data protection, and cultural sensitivity [10].

Given the global burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), AI is also being explored in antimicrobial stewardship programs, where predictive models can help optimize antibiotic prescribing and reduce misuse [11]. Yet here too, ethical questions arise: What if algorithmic bias leads to under-treatment in marginalized populations? How should patient consent and autonomy be respected when predictive analytics guide therapy? These issues demand close alignment of technological innovation with robust ethical frameworks.

This editorial critically examines the ethics of AI use in diagnostic decision-making and therapeutic stewardship, synthesizing insights from global and national guidelines, and highlighting best practices for responsible implementation.

Why AI is indispensable in diagnostics and antimicrobial stewardship

AI promises to transform diagnostics through enhanced accuracy, speed, and personalization. In radiology, AI systems have shown performance comparable to expert radiologists in detecting pneumonia on chest radiographs, lung nodules on CT scans, and breast cancers on mammography 3, 12. In pathology, digital slide analysis using deep learning can identify malignancies with remarkable precision [13]. Beyond imaging, AI models are being trained to interpret electrocardiograms, predict arrhythmias, and stratify cardiovascular risks [14].

In antimicrobial stewardship, AI has equally significant potential. Predictive algorithms can identify patients at risk of sepsis, guide empirical antibiotic selection, and optimize de-escalation strategies [15]. Machine learning models using electronic health records (EHRs) have been applied to predict multidrug-resistant infections, enabling more targeted interventions [11]. Furthermore, natural language processing (NLP) systems can mine clinical notes to flag inappropriate antibiotic use [16].

Despite these advances, clinical translation remains uneven. One barrier is the “black box” problem—many high-performing AI models, such as deep neural networks, are opaque to clinicians and regulators. When an AI system suggests a diagnosis or recommends withholding antibiotics, healthcare providers must trust the rationale. Without transparency, both clinical adoption and patient confidence are undermined 5, 9. Ethical frameworks must therefore bridge this gap between performance and explainability.

Ethical challenges of AI in diagnostics and stewardship

The ethical dilemmas of AI in healthcare can be grouped into four main domains:

Transparency and Explainability – Clinicians and patients must be able to understand how an AI system arrives at a decision. “Black-box” models raise concerns about accountability, trust when errors occur. For example, if an AI model wrongly flags a tuberculosis case as negative, who is responsible—the clinician, the developer, or the institution 6, 9?

Data Privacy and Consent – AI systems require large datasets, often including sensitive health information. Ensuring compliance with frameworks such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe and India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) is vital 9, 10.

Bias and Fairness – AI models trained on biased datasets may perpetuate health inequities. A U.S. study found that an AI tool used to predict healthcare needs underestimated the severity of illness in Black patients because it relied on healthcare spending as a proxy for health status [17]. Similar risks exist in antimicrobial stewardship, where marginalized populations may be underrepresented in datasets.

Human Oversight and Clinical Judgment – Over-reliance on AI can erode clinician autonomy. WHO stresses that AI should augment, not replace, human decision-making [7]. In stewardship, this means AI can suggest antibiotic regimens, but final judgment must remain with clinicians who weigh contextual factors such as patient preferences and comorbidities.

Global and national ethical guidelines

WHO

The WHO’s six ethical principles for AI in health include: protecting autonomy, promoting human well-being and safety, ensuring transparency, fostering responsibility, ensuring inclusiveness and equity, and promoting sustainable use [7]. These provide a global baseline for ethical AI deployment.

United States

The FDA’s approach to AI in healthcare emphasizes a “total product lifecycle” regulatory framework, ensuring continuous oversight as adaptive AI models evolve [8]. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) also underscores accountability, particularly in patient data governance.

European Union

The EU AI Act classifies medical AI as high risk, requiring rigorous conformity assessment, documentation, and human oversight [9]. This aligns closely with GDPR obligations for data protection and patient consent.

India

The ICMR’s 2023 guidelines are among the first in Asia to address ethical AI specifically in healthcare and biomedical research, highlighting the issues of responsibility, accountability, accessibility, safety, and cultural appropriateness, enuring that AI is tailored to the diverse Indian healthcare contexts [10]. For antimicrobial stewardship, this is particularly relevant given India’s high AMR burden.

Examples of AI in diagnostics and stewardship with ethical concerns

Radiology: An AI model for detecting vertebral fractures in spinal X-rays achieved high accuracy but performed worse in elderly patients, raising concerns about age-related bias [12].

Tuberculosis (TB) Screening: Automated chest X-ray interpretation tools have been piloted in TB programs, but WHO cautioned against sole reliance without human oversight, given variable accuracy across populations [7].

Sepsis Prediction Models: The “AI Clinician” model showed potential in optimizing sepsis therapy [4], but ethical concerns arose when clinicians questioned whether patients or families had given meaningful consent for AI-driven care.

Antibiotic Prescribing: AI tools that recommend narrower-spectrum antibiotics can reduce resistance [15], but if misapplied, they may under-treat patients with resistant infections, raising questions of liability and patient safety.

These examples highlight the balance required between innovation and ethics—AI must improve efficiency without undermining trust, fairness, or safety.

Conclusion

AI has the potential to transform diagnostic and antimicrobial stewardships by enabling earlier detection, more accurate diagnosis, and rational antimicrobial use, including transforming healthcare with a particular focus on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) diagnosis and delivery of healthcare in low-income countries and remote areas. However, its ethical and regulatory dimensions are equally significant. Transparency, accountability, inclusiveness, and respect for patient autonomy must be integrated into AI development and deployment.
