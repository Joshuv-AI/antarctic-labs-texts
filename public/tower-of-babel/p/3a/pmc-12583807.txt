Investigation of floor blasting for rock burst prevention in high-stress roadway under dynamic loading
Authors: Geng Li, Anye Cao, Guifeng Wang, Xianxi Bai, Guowei Lyu, Yujie Peng, Yongqin Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Floor blasting has been demonstrated to reduce roadway floor heaving by interrupting the transmission of floor stress, thereby preventing floor rock burst. This study establishes a roadway floor blasting similarity model test system, which incorporates a swing impact testing facility, a high-speed digital image correlation apparatus, and an ultra-dynamic data acquisition instrument. The initial comparison was made between the effects of floor pressure relief measures on roadway dynamic load response characteristics under high-stress section dynamic load conditions. Furthermore, numerical analyses using the RHT constitutive model within LS-DYNA thoroughly investigated the influence of in-situ stress levels, the intensity of dynamic loads, and their placement. The experimental and numerical findings suggest that floor blasting significantly reduces the peak acceleration and stress amplitude of roadway rock mass under dynamic load conditions. Roadway that employ floor blasting for pressure relief have been shown to maintain an intact floor after strong dynamic loads. Additionally, the horizontal-to-vertical stress ratio exerts a predominant influence on crack propagation patterns, and a lateral pressure coefficient that exceeds 1.6 exacerbates asymmetric damage. The influence of dynamic load intensity and application location on damage asymmetry is significant, with the wavefront side exhibiting higher crack complexity.

Introduction

As the extraction of coal resources in China intensifies, the risks associated with roadway instability, structural failure, and rock burst have become increasingly significant. These challenges are at the forefront of discussions concerning deep resource extraction and mining safety1. Roadway floor rock burst, characterized by a sudden deformation and the failure of coal and rock strata under extreme pressure, is a perilous dynamic hazard that can imperil miners and the integrity of roadways. A variety of effective measures have been implemented to prevent rock bursts in the floor. Compared to alternative methods, floor blasting for pressure relief, prevention of floor heaving, and enhancement of roadway stability offers a simpler configuration, requires less labor, and significantly reduces the extent of floor heaving2,3. When the parameters are carefully selected, this method can effectively block the transmission of floor stress and prevent rock burst4,5. However, the interaction between in-situ stress, mining tremors, and floor blasting can lead to a more complex stress environment around the roadway, potentially triggering more severe floor deformation or even rock burst.

Significant progress has been made in understanding how rock bursts form when static and dynamic forces interact. As mining operations proceed, the static stress in the overlying coal seams steadily increases. When this buildup interacts with seismic activity or other sudden disruptions, the resulting pressure frequently leads to dramatic rock burst6. This theory suggests that an increase in burial depth leads to higher stress concentration in the surrounding rocks. Mining-induced seismic events, propagating as stress waves during the rupture process, exert tensile forces on the free surface. The interaction of these features has been demonstrated to have a substantial impact on the susceptibility of rock bursts in the surrounding strata of the roadway7. Furthermore, researchers have examined the stress progression in the adjacent rock strata during extraction and the energy discharge patterns tied to the fracturing of rock layers8. The “dynamic-static superposition” concept provides a theoretical framework for comprehending the phenomenon of floor rock bursts. These studies emphasize the importance of monitoring stress levels and implementing mitigating strategies, particularly in mining environments where such events pose a substantial risk9–12.

Laboratory experiment and numerical simulation methods are imperative for enhancing the comprehension of the theory of static and dynamic load superposition, particularly in the context of complex engineering problems. However, Controlled explosion experiments are plagued by limited replicability, imprecise data, and challenges in monitoring stress and energy variations. Numerical modeling has therefore become an important means for researching rock blasting performance. LS-DYNA, a widely used software in the field of rock blasting analysis, is renowned for its proficiency in modeling the intricate, non-linear dynamics of rock behavior under explosive loads. Its advanced simulation capabilities make it a go-to tool for researchers and engineers looking to assess blast-induced damage with precision. The RHT (Riedel-Hiermaier-Thoma) model is an extensively used analytical tool for simulating phenomena such as blast effects and infiltration numerically. Studies validate the precision of this model in depicting the response of fragile substances, such as stone and cement, to rapid, high-impact forces. This tool has been demonstrated to be a reliable means of predicting their mechanical response in high-stakes scenarios13,14. Zhao et al. observed that sustained in-situ stress amplified compressive forces along the borehole’s radial axis while diminishing tensile forces. Furthermore, irrespective of fluctuations in the lateral pressure coefficient, the fracture zone exhibited a consistent orientation parallel to the maximum principal stress direction15. Li et al. employed three-dimensional modeling to analyze the damage characteristics of rock blasting in conditions of high in-situ stress. The researchers found that stress significantly affected the resulting fragments’ size and shape16. A wealth of pivotal resources concerning blast modelling can be found in the substantial findings derived from the RHT model in rock blasting simulations17–20. There is limited research on the mechanical behavior of the RHT model in coal seam rock under mining conditions. Consequently, few studies have used LS-DYNA simulations to analyze blast-induced damage in these high-stress, dynamic scenarios, which are common in such settings. The absence of comprehensive data in this area creates a significant gap in our understanding of how coal seam rock responds to explosive forces under extreme pressure.

Recognizing that blasting cannot be studied in isolation from the surrounding stress environment, scholars have increasingly focused on the interactive effects of static and dynamic loads. Dynamic disturbances, such as seismic waves and nearby blasting, can alter the initial stress field of the roadway, thereby influencing the effectiveness of long-hole destress blasting. Consequently, a substantial body of research has been devoted to investigating the interactive effects of dynamic load variations and their detrimental impact on rock blasting. Petr Konicek et al. systematically planned and designed a pressure-reducing blasting method, which effectively reduced the scope and intensity of stress concentration induced by mining in front of mining face21. Fu et al. combined directional energy focusing blasting with non-directional blasting technology, achieving pressure release and control of the surrounding rock mass, effectively reducing stress and dynamic load disturbances on both sides of the roadway22. Additionally, Zhou et al. posited that the implementation of controlled blasting may serve as a mitigating strategy for abutment stress and seismic disturbances in the context of longwall mining gobs23. Xue et al. observed that the implementation of deep-hole fracturing in coal strata leads to a reduction in dynamic and static stress effects, thereby mitigating the occurrence of rock burst incidents24. Therefore, research into the damage caused by long-hole blasting under high static and dynamic loads has become an irreversible trend.

Recent studies on the stability of coal mine roadway surrounding rocks primarily concentrate on the resultant behavioral changes due to stress reallocation caused by extraction activities. However, there is a notable absence of systematic elucidation of the dynamic interplay between microseismic perturbations and rock mass failure mechanisms. Underground excavations are subject to multidirectional dynamic loading during extraction activities. Rock breakdown is primarily caused by stress wave interactions from multidirectional sources. The underlying reasons for the susceptibility of roadway floors to damage from directional blasting and the resultant seismic vibrations remain a significant research challenge for scientists. The phenomenon of rock weakness exhibiting differential patterns across different directions continues to pose a substantial research obstacle.

Building on these issues, a series of laboratory experiments were conducted on roadway models, which were subjected to cyclic dynamic loads under pressure relief measures. Digital image correlation (DIC) technology was utilized to observe the damage patterns of roadway rock mass under dynamic loads. Furthermore, an ultra-dynamic data acquisition instrument was utilized to collect data on surface acceleration and displacement deformation during multiple loading cycles. Subsequently, a model was developed through numerical analysis with the assistance of the LS-DYNA finite element software. Comprehensive parameter studies evaluated the impact of dynamic and static load interactions on the effectiveness of pressure-relief blasting. A thorough examination and evaluation of the surrounding rock’s structural damage and internal energy shifts post-blasting were conducted. The findings reveal the pivotal role of dynamic load parameters in determining the outcomes of floor blasting in high-stress environments, thereby unveiling the underlying mechanisms that underpin these outcomes.

Physical model experiments

Roadway swing impact testing facility

A test setup was developed to study how roadway rock reacts to dynamic forces. The investigation focused on the weakening effect of floor blasting measures. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the system consists of modular cells, a steel gantry, vertical and horizontal hydraulic actuators, and a swing impact hammer. The dimensions of the roadway model are 650 mm (Length) × 400 mm (Width) × 650 mm (Height). To simulate the dynamic stress disturbance formed by the superimposition of advance support pressure and residual floor stress during working face advancement, a hydraulic loading apparatus was affixed to the beam, while the horizontal stress was introduced by hydraulic loading device strategically positioned along the model’s sides. The dynamic forces were initiated with a sudden impact, as a 30-kg hammer was forcibly dropped from the right-hand side of the structure. By adjusting the height of the 30-kg pendulum at the moment of release, different levels of energy discharge could be simulated. The maximum documented drop was 1.6 m, and the greatest possible impact energy was measured at 472 J. To prevent the pendulum from swinging multiple times and thereby affecting the experimental results, a braking device was added to stop the pendulum from impacting the incident bar again. The pre-set horizontal plate in the experimental chamber facilitates the impact load from the pendulum to the entire lane within the chamber through the incident bar. The methodology produces fluctuating pressure waves in both the vertical and lateral axes.

Experimental model and procedure

The roadway’s dynamic response offers valuable insights into the stability of the surrounding rock formation. By tracking acceleration patterns and displacement measurements, engineers can effectively assess how the rock mass behaves when subjected to dynamic stress conditions. This approach provides a pragmatic methodology for assessing structural integrity under real-world loading conditions1. In this study, data collection was performed using a DH5960 ultra-dynamic signal testing system, which can accurately capture the acceleration response and displacement change characteristics of road structures under dynamic loads. Six piezoelectric acceleration sensors (1A518EB) were strategically positioned on the roadway floor, roof, and both roadway sidewalls25. Two eddy current displacement transducers were affixed to the roadway floor. Both components are linked to the DH5960 via a signal amplifier. Advancements in ultra-high-speed camera technology have enabled the precise documentation of microscopic dynamics, including crack propagation, fragmentation, penetration, and exfoliation of materials, with sub-second temporal resolution and high-definition image quality26. To this end, a rapid-acquisition camera was utilized to record the floor’s dynamic deformation and failure due to load, as observed from a frontal perspective. The RP-C18.3-ST thin-film single-point pressure sensor is embedded in the roadway floor. The sensor has the capacity to convert pressure load in the monitoring area into a resistance signal. The external load of the sensor can be calculated based on the “pressure-resistance” calibration curve.

Experimental results

Influence of floor blasting on roadway section failure under dynamic loading

The experiment systematically monitored the dynamic load response of the road using an ultra-dynamic signal testing system (DH5960). The data demonstrated a direct correlation between the efficacy of floor blasting pressure relief measures on the floor and the load energy. In scenarios where the load energy was less than 118 J, the variation in vibration acceleration amplitude between the treated and untreated roadways was less than 5%, suggesting that the fracture network formed by blasting had not yet exerted a substantial effect at this stage. However, when the load energy exceeded 118 J, corresponding to a pendulum height of 0.4 m, the difference in vibration acceleration amplitude between the treated and untreated roadways increased significantly, indicating that the fracture network had begun to exert a substantial effect. As shown in Fig. 3, the peak acceleration in the treated roadway demonstrated a substantial decrease, with a maximum reduction of 97.13%, thereby validating the efficacy of the floor blasting technique in attenuating dynamic loading energy. The specific test data reveal that the peak acceleration of the untreated roadway floor under dynamic loads reaches 155.21–483.43 m/s², while after the implementation of blast pressure reduction, the peak stabilizes at a level below 233.69 m/s². The experimental results indicate a vibration reduction efficiency ranging from 41.34% to 97.13%.The analysis of the acceleration time-history curves reveals that the vibration energy in the treated area exhibits rapid decay characteristics. This finding suggests that the fracture system formed by floor blasting enhances the floor structure’s rockburst resistance through stress wave reflection and friction energy dissipation mechanisms. This characteristic is particularly prominent under conditions of high-energy dynamic load.

By embedding multiple sets of stress sensors in the experimental model beforehand, the stress increase in the model after each dynamic load can be visually displayed. Figure 4 shows the change in stress in the model roadway under different energy dynamic loads. As the dynamic load energy increases from 59 J to 472 J, the internal stress amplitude in the model—both with and without pressure relief measures—exhibits a consistent upward trend. However, the impact of dynamic loading is significantly more pronounced in roadways lacking floor pressure relief. Thus, the implementation of floor pressure relief techniques has been demonstrated to be an effective strategy for mitigating stress concentrations caused by impact loading. Furthermore, the stress amplitude on the side closest to the dynamic load will be visibly higher than on the opposite side, which is farther from the impact source. As shown in Fig. 4, when the pendulum delivers an impact load of 59 J, the roadway model with pressure relief measures exhibits a peak stress of just 147 kPa—far lower than the 249 kPa observed in the untreated model. However, as the dynamic load intensifies to 472 J, the stress in the unmodified roadway increases to 479 kPa—a substantial 230 kPa (92.37%) increase compared to the initial 59 J impact. These findings demonstrate an inverse correlation between proximity to the dynamic load source and peak stress in nearby geological structures, suggesting that decreasing proximity to the load source may result in a reduction of peak stress.

Roadway failure progression

The experiment employed a high-speed camera (MemrecamGX-3) to record the entire process of the unfortified roadway’s failure under strong dynamic loads, as shown in Fig. 5. The study demonstrated a significant correlation between roadway failure patterns and load energy. As the pendulum height increased from 0.2 m (59 J) to 1.6 m (472 J), the severity of failure exhibited a gradual escalation. Critical value tests indicated that the roadway maintained stability when the load energy fell below 413 J (pendulum height of 1.4 m). However, once this threshold was exceeded, the untreated area experienced instantaneous rock burst and dust eruption. This outcome confirms that strong dynamic loads are a necessary condition for triggering rock burst. Comparative experiments showed that the front 200 mm roadway section treated with blasting pressure reduction remained structurally intact under multiple 472 J loads, with only limited deformation. However, the untreated roadway section exhibited through-going cracks and extensive rock burst damage. This finding underscores the efficacy of floor blasting pressure reduction measures in suppressing crack propagation by enhancing rock stress distribution, thereby ensuring the roadway ‘s structural stability under substantial dynamic loads. The vibration reduction effects observed exceed 90%, further substantiating the effectiveness of floor blasting.

Numerical modelling of floor blasting

Numerical model

In the field of rock mechanics research, three predominant methodologies have been identified: numerical simulation, laboratory experiments, and theoretical analysis. Through similarity simulation experiments, we qualitatively confirmed that floor pressure relief measures can significantly enhance the impact resistance of roadways. However, due to the material opacity in the similarity simulation, high-speed cameras were only able to capture the surface crack distribution of the model, while failing to reveal the internal crack propagation characteristics and the evolution of surrounding rock damage. As a substantial element of contemporary computational mechanics, numerical simulation methods have the capacity to bypass the temporal and spatial limitations inherent to conventional experiments, thereby offering a novel approach to understanding the mechanical behavior of rock materials under dynamic loads. This study employed LS-DYNA, an explicit dynamics solver, to perform three-dimensional simulations of dual-borehole floor blasting, with model dimensions of 32 m × 20 m × 5 m, thereby accurately reproducing the geometric characteristics of the field blast test. The diameter of the blasthole is 90 mm, and the diameter of the explosive is 60 mm. The angle of the blasthole was measured at 45°. Moreover, the two blastholes on the roadway cross-section are arranged in a symmetrical manner. During the construction of the model, four fundamental material components were given full consideration: bedrock medium, explosive charge, coupling layer, and sealing material, as vividly illustrated in Fig. 6. The material parameters for each component were obtained through a combination of laboratory testing and prior research27. In the context of mesh partitioning, the 8-node hexahedral element was utilized to achieve a refined discretization. The region of the explosion was modeled with a densified mesh having a precision of 2 mm, while the rock mass region was modeled with a mesh that was gradually refined to a precision of 10 mm. This process culminated in the formulation of a highly precise computational model, comprising 5.62 million elements. The numerical model delineates the coupled interactions among rock mass, explosives, air, and coupled media through CONSTRAINED_LAGRANGE_IN_SOLID. The arbitrary Lagrange-Eulerian (ALE) algorithm was employed to accurately capture fluid-structure interactions for explosive products and coupled media, while the Lagrange algorithm was applied to describe the solid mechanics behavior of rock mass and sealing materials28. The LS-DYNA solver, equipped with 12 NCPUs and 60 GB of memory, is utilized to execute numerical models. The execution of a typical model typically requires approximately 30 h. In order to accurately model real-world blasting conditions, the front and back surfaces were treated as unrestrained boundaries, while the X and Y axes were constrained by in-situ stress fields to replicate underground engineering stress conditions. The simulation of roadway excavation under prestress involves a two-stage analytical approach. Initially, the lateral boundaries of the model are subjected to in-situ stresses during the static analysis phase. Subsequently, dynamic loads are applied to the boundaries, as stipulated. The subsequent phase entails the transfer of the static stress results from each element into the explicit dynamic model. After this, the detonation of explosives is initiated within each borehole, in accordance with the prescribed blasting sequence.

Determination of numerical simulation parameters

The efficacy of the numerical simulation methods is contingent upon two pivotal factors: First, the constitutive relationships of the research objects must be accurately characterized. These objects include rock materials, explosives, and their coupled media. Secondly, the precise calibration of model parameters through systematic experimentation is imperative. This study will methodically expound on the constitutive model selection methodologies applicable to rock dynamics problems, taking into account both the theoretical underpinnings and the applicable conditions.

The RHT constitutive model

In the domain of geotechnical engineering, LS-DYNA is extensively utilized due to its comprehensive suite of constitutive models, which are specifically tailored to rock mechanics studies. These models possess distinct characteristics, making them valuable tools for researchers and practitioners in the field. In contrast, the RHT model establishes a more comprehensive dynamic constitutive theory framework for rock masses by coupling strain rate effects, confining pressure sensitivity, and a three-stage evolution mechanism of progressive damage softening29. This model employs pressure-dependent strength envelopes and cumulative plastic strain damage criteria, enabling precise reproduction of complex mechanical behaviors such as strength degradation, damage accumulation, and crack propagation in rock materials under blasting loads. Utilizing its foundation, this study employs the RHT model to investigate rock fracture under the combined effects of dynamic blast and high-pressure confinement. The constitutive model has been enhanced with an enhanced strain rate sensitivity coefficient and the triaxial stress ratio has been incorporated into the damage evolution equation. The proposed adjustments are expected to provide a clearer representation of how deep rock formations progressively deteriorate when subjected to explosive shockwaves. The refined model is poised to serve as a reliable instrument for quantifying blast-resistant structural solutions in underground construction projects. By systematically comparing numerical simulations with real-world blasting data, engineers can develop accurate dynamic failure benchmarks for rock masses at practical scales.

The RHT constitutive model elucidates the pressure response through the use of the Mie-Gruneisen equation of state, which incorporates two fundamental elements: the Hugoniot polynomial curve and the P–α compaction behavior27. As illustrated in Fig. 7 (a), the p-α state equation curve accurately depicts the mechanical response characteristics of porous materials during dynamic compression. In the initial loading stage, prior to the attainment of the pressure threshold required to disrupt pore structures, the material exhibits conventional linear elastic behavior. During this stage, the stress-strain correlation follows Hooke’s law precisely. However, when the external pressure surpasses the threshold for pore rupture, the internal pore structure of the material undergoes an irreversible collapse. This microscopic structural change leads to a significant reduction in the macroscopic volume stiffness of the material, resulting in a decrease in the effective volume modulus. It is noteworthy that during this stage, pressure and volume strain exhibit a distinct nonlinear relationship, reflecting the unique compressive characteristics of porous materials. During the unloading process, the material exhibits a rebound effect along the predefined elastic stiffness trajectory. However, due to plastic deformation, permanent volume strain remains even when the material is fully unloaded to zero pressure. To quantitatively describe this complex pore collapse behavior, the model introduces the key internal variable α, which characterizes the material’s porosity state and is defined as the volume ratio of dense matrix material to porous material. As the compression process continues, the α value undergoes a gradual decrease from the initial porosity, and this process of change exhibits significant path dependence. Specifically, when the pressure reaches the pore rupture pressure, α = 0, and when it continues to increase to the fully compacted pressure, α = 1. At this point, the material attains its theoretically fully dense state.

The RHT constitutive model employs three-stress limit surface theory to delineate alterations in material strength characteristics under varying stress conditions. This innovative approach effectively accounts for the reduction in material strength due to anisotropy and strain rate effects27. The initial elastic yield surface denotes the point at which a material begins to deform, which is frequently referred to as the material’s original breaking point. Next is the failure surface, which represents the maximum load the material can withstand before failing. Ultimately, the post-failure friction residue demonstrates the material’s durability after it has been completely compromised. This residue appears once the debris has cleared. As shown in Fig. 7 (b), when the material is loaded, it first behaves elastically, meaning it returns to its original shape. During this phase, the stress remains within the initial yield surface. However, once the stress pushes past that surface, the material begins to deform permanently, accumulating plastic strain that cannot be undone. The model incorporates plastic strain and hardening parameters to create a yield surface that changes as the process continues. This dynamic surface is created by blending the initial yield and failure surfaces smoothly using interpolation27,30.

Rock parameter determination through the application of the RHT constitutive model

This research employs the RHT constitutive model to analyze rock materials. This sophisticated elastoplastic damage model accounts for several variables simultaneously. These variables include confining stress, rapid strain rates, strain hardening, and material softening due to damage27. This makes it particularly well-suited for modeling how rock reacts to blasting loads and high-stress. The model incorporates 38 material parameters, 18 of which are deemed to be key and are determined on the basis of experimental data. The minimum damage residual strain was obtained through inverse analysis of blasting tests, while the remaining 19 parameters, which have a relatively minor impact on the computational results, were referenced from values reported by Borrvall and Riedel27 and other literature31,32. The rock parameters employed in this study are primarily derived from field measurement data from the Yanbei mining area and the systematic calibration results of Li et al.33, with some auxiliary parameters based on the research findings of Meng, et al34. and Qiu et al.35. When applied together, these parameters ensure the model accurately depicts the evolution of rock degradation from onset to total collapse.

Numerical results and discussions

Impact of in-situ stress field variations

Horizontal stress is recognized as the dominant force that undermines the structural integrity of coal-rock layers in mine floor roadways. Abnormally high horizontal stress at floor level can lead to either concentrated stress points or a complete reshuffling of pressure within the surrounding rock formations due to the dynamic interplay between blast-induced stress waves and pre-existing horizontal forces. In rock mechanics, the in-situ stress field is fundamental in determining both rock fracture patterns and the success of controlled blasting in floor excavation. This influence is manifested in the spatial distribution patterns of the stress field, variations in the horizontal-to-vertical stress ratio (σh/σv) substantially modify rock failure mechanisms, with particularly pronounced effects observed in layered floor structures subjected to bidirectional constraints. This study examines how in-situ stress affects the progression of blast-induced rock damage. The damage level is measured on a scale from 0 (intact) to 1 (fully failed), with elements that have damage levels above 0.9 considered fully damaged. To assess post-blasting rock damage, distinct zones were classified based on defined thresholds of the D-value. As illustrated in Fig. 10, the investigation examined blast-induced crack propagation patterns from 45° bottom boreholes under pressure coefficients ranging from 1.0 to 2.0. Figure 11 compares blast-induced fracture regions near the borehole and analyzes the damaged and crack propagation zones.

With vertical stress held steady and horizontal stress increased while adding dynamic loading, the size of the crack extension zone is significantly impacted. However, the damage zone around the blasting holes barely registers. Interestingly, when the vertical-to-horizontal stress ratio is one-to-one and a dynamic load is applied to the left side of the model, the crack spreads unevenly on the corresponding side of the floor. As the ratio increases to 1.8, relative to the ratio of small cracks in the roadway floor, further development, the expansion range increases significantly. Concurrently, the crack asymmetric expansion caused by the dynamic load becomes less evident. At this juncture, the larger horizontal stress becomes the predominant factor in crack expansion.

Numerical simulation results indicate a direct relationship between the static load stress and propagation of blasting cracks. Increasing horizontal stress while maintaining consistent vertical stress and introducing dynamic force notably affects crack propagation. However, the area around the blast site doesn’t show much damage. When the vertical and horizontal stresses are balanced and a dynamic load hits the left side, however, the crack forms unevenly along the matching edge of the floor. This phenomenon is accompanied by a gradual decrease in cracks that expand horizontally within the coal seam of the roadway floor. Concurrently, cracks that expand vertically gradually increase, ultimately forming a new broken zone.

Impact of dynamic load strength

To study the influence of dynamic load strength on crack propagation, we kept the lateral pressure coefficient at 1.6 and gradually increased the dynamic load from 5 to 10 MPa, then to 15 MPa, and finally to 20 MPa. The resulting crack growth patterns are shown in Fig. 12. Next, we performed a detailed quantitative evaluation to determine the relationship between load intensity and fracture development. This evaluation involved processing numerical blasting data through ImageJ software to measure the extent of material damage and fractal dimensions. The fractal dimension calculation method used in this study is the box-counting method. The findings from this evaluation are presented in Fig. 1338,41.

It was observed that as the intensity of the dynamic load applied to the roadway side increased, a significant asymmetry in crack expansion after blasting occurred, and the cracks at the bottom corners of the roadway developed further, with a gradual increase in the area of damage. Furthermore, the area of roadway floor damage is affected by the dynamics of the carrier wave, which displays progressive widening on the side facing the wave. Fractal dimension is a quantitative metric used to describe the self-similarity and irregularity of complex structures. It is employed to characterize the complexity of crack propagation or the extent of damage areas in rock or geological structures. A comparative analysis was conducted on the crack extensions of two roadway sides located at the bottom corner of the roadway, following the application of varying dynamic load intensities. This analysis was use of ImageJ software statistics. As the dynamic load intensity increases, the damage area to the floor of the roadway facing the wave increases gradually, while the damage area on the other side remains relatively constant. The fractal dimension of the roadway floor on the wave-facing side increases with dynamic load intensity, while the opposite side demonstrates a decreasing trend. This finding suggests that dynamic loads induce asymmetric stress redistribution in the roadway floor, thereby triggering premature crack formation. The aforementioned fissures have been observed to concentrate blast energy in specific directions, thereby expanding the damage zone.

In LS-DYNA numerical simulations, the study of energy conversion mechanisms during rock blasting processes is of significant importance. The utilization of the *CONTROL_ENERGY keyword facilitates the effective acquisition of the time-history evolution curves of kinetic energy and internal energy. As demonstrated in Fig. 14, the numerical simulation results indicate that under a dynamic load of 10 MPa, the hourglass energy consumption ratio during the rock blasting process is 7.4%, which meets the calculated requirement of 10%29,38. When the shock wave from an explosion acts on the borehole wall, the rock’s kinetic energy undergoes a typical pattern of rapid increase, reaching a peak, and then sharp decay before stabilizing. The evolution of internal energy shows an initial rapid accumulation to a maximum value, followed by a sustained slow growth trend. As the dynamic load intensity increases, both system kinetic energy and internal energy exhibit a significant growth trend. This phenomenon is closely correlated with the expansion process of the rock damage zone.

Impact of dynamic load position

The occurrence of microseismic events in various locations along the roadway is primarily attributable to the rock burst of mining operations at the working face. For instance, in the event of a roof collapse or key layer fracture, micro seismic events have been observed to concentrate at the site of the roadway roof. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that mining can result in elevated levels of stress concentration within the floor plate or the both sides of the roadway, leading to plastic deformation or shear slip of the coal body. Consequently, this may precipitate a mining seismic event. The present investigation sought to explore the impact of the positioning of the dynamic load on the effect of the blasting of the floor. In order to achieve this objective, the roadway side, floor, and roof were applied to the strength of the 20 MPa dynamic load. The damage maps resulting from the blasting process are illustrated in Fig. 15, while Fig. 16 demonstrates the alterations in kinetic energy and the extent of the damage. An extensive examination was performed to evaluate the influence of the changing load position of the floor post-blast. The visuals depicted significantly supported the analysis.

Subsequent to the impact of dynamic loads at varying locations, the floor demonstrates a range of damage and a corresponding degree of damage. It has been demonstrated that the dynamic load applied to a roadway’s floor results in significant damage and crack expansion in the area following blasting. Additionally, the direction of expansion and the propagation of the dynamic load diagonal intersection manifest a discernible trend. When the force of a blast is evenly distributed across a roadway’s floor, the resulting cracks tend to be symmetrical around the tunnel’s center. Because of this, the damaged area is much smaller than it would be if the damage were uneven. Furthermore, the direction of expansion of the damaged area is found to be perpendicular to the propagation of dynamic loads. Following the detonation of the roadway floor and the application of a dynamic load on that side, the subsequent implementation of the floor following the blasting of the roadway floor reveals significant asymmetric distribution. The damage and crack extension area manifest a considerable lateral asymmetry, distinguished by a notably broader area on the side subjected to the waves, in contrast to the other, less impacted side. The distribution of damage in this area displays a diagonal pattern, which is consistent with the direction of wave propagation.

As demonstrated by the preceding analysis, the positioning of dynamic waves will result in substantial variations in the damage patterns observed on the roadway floor following blasting. The rock’s surface exhibits predominantly compressive damage, characterized by crack extension and the propagation of dynamic waves in a diagonal intersection pattern. Conversely, the posterior aspect of the wave demonstrates a preponderance of tensile damage. The direction of crack extension and the direction of propagation of the dynamic wave are perpendicular or parallel. Consequently, the project must be evaluated in conjunction with the geological conditions and the characteristics of the dynamic load. Consequently, in the context of practical projects, it is imperative to take into account the geological conditions and dynamic load characteristics. Properly distinguishing between pressure relief and protective measures is crucial for effective design. The goal is to minimize uneven structural damage resulting from dynamic forces.

Conclusions

This study examined the dynamic behavior and extent of damage to the surrounding rock following blasting on a roadway floor using experimental and computational approaches. The analysis focused on the interaction between high natural ground pressure and microseismic vibrations. The combined effect of these elements gave rise to considerable variations in post-blasting crack propagation, rock blast damage, and rock energy changes. The following conclusions were drawn:

Similar experiments have shown that, in comparison with roadway in which no measures have been taken, the implementation of floor blasting can significantly mitigate the destructive effects of strong dynamic loads and high stress on the roadway rock mass structure. This assessment should inform the design of differentiated pressure release measures and protective measures, with the objective of minimizing the risk of asymmetric damage caused by dynamic loads.
