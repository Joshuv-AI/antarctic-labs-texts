Accelerated enhanced recovery after colorectal surgery with early discharge: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhen-Hao Yu, Li-Ming Wang, Li-Yang Chan, Jeng-Fu You, Geng-Ping Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate whether accelerated enhanced recovery protocols increase rates of readmission, emergency department (ED) revisits, reoperation, or complications following colorectal surgery.MethodsThe protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024621268). A systematic search was perfomed in major databases including MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane databases. Primary outcomes was 30-day readmission rates. Secondary outcomes were ED revisits, reoperations, and complications. Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool.ResultsThirteen studies involving 40,589 patients were included, with 1,783 patients in the aERPs group and 38,806 in the ERPs group. The meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in 30-day readmission rates (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.55–1.29, p = 0.43), ED revisits (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.42–2.18, p = 0.91), reoperations (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.61–1.54, p = 0.90), or complications (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.17–1.32, p = 0.15).ConclusionsAccelerated ERPs with early discharge strategies, appear safe and feasible for selected patients undergoing minimally invasive colorectal surgery, with no increased risk of readmissions, ED visits, reoperations, or complications. Future high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to refine patient selection criteria and optimize implementation of aERPs.Keywords: Accelerated ERAS, Same-day discharge, Colorectal surgery, Minimally invasive surgery, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Minimally invasive colorectal surgery, including laparoscopic and robotic-assisted techniques, has significantly transformed colorectal surgery over the past decades. These techniques have consistently shown advantages over traditional open surgery, such as reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, faster recovery, and fewer surgical site infections, all without compromising oncological outcomes or increasing postoperative morbidity. By minimizing the physiological stress of surgery, minimally invasive approaches have become the preferred method for managing both benign and malignant colorectal conditions [1–4]. The widespread adoption of these techniques has made colorectal surgery safer and more efficient, especially when combined with Enhanced Recovery Protocols (ERPs).

The introduction of ERPs or Enhanced Recovery after Surgery protocols (ERAS) has further revolutionized colorectal surgery by improving outcomes through a structured, evidence-based perioperative care pathway [5–8]. ERPs aim to minimize the surgical stress response, maintain postoperative physiological function, and promote accelerated recovery. Key components of Enhanced Recovery Pathways (ERPs) include early mobilization, multimodal analgesia, avoidance of prolonged fasting, and the early initiation of postoperative nutrition [9, 10]. These practices have been shown to reduce hospital length of stay (LOS) and postoperative complications without increasing readmission rates. When integrated with minimally invasive techniques, ERPs has consistently demonstrated enhanced recovery and reduced morbidity in patients undergoing colorectal surgery [11].

The concept of accelerated enhanced recovery protocols (aERPs), first proposed by Gignoux et al. in 2015 and also referred to as “hyper-ERAS” or “same-day discharge (SDD),” has gained significant attention in colorectal surgery, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic [12]. The pandemic highlighted the necessity of minimizing hospital stays to mitigate the risk of nosocomial infections and optimize the allocation of healthcare resources. Studies have shown that selected patients can safely undergo colorectal surgery and be discharged within 24 h without increasing the risk of postoperative complications or readmissions [10, 13]. The rapid evolution of aERPs supported by advancements in telemedicine and home monitoring, highlights the potential for redefining perioperative care and further shortening recovery times in colorectal surgery [14].

However, real-world data suggests higher complication rates compared to those reported in aERPs studies [12, 15]. Besides, the potential delay in addressing postoperative complications, along with the risks of emergency department (ED) revisits, readmissions, and reoperations, remain significant concerns with this proactive perioperative care approach [16]. This comprehensive analysis aims to evaluate whether the aERPs approach is associated with higher rates of readmission, ED revisits, complications, and reoperations comparing to standard ERPs.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and study selection

This study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024621268). The study was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (version 6.2, 2021) and the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [17]. Institutional review board approval was not required, and there was no external funding or conflicts of interest to declare. In consultation with our institution’s librarian, we developed an exhaustive permutation of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords, including “colorectal surgery,” “same-day discharge,” “enhanced recovery,” “ERAS,” “ enhanced recovery protocols,” “minimally invasive surgery,” and “accelerated recovery.” Using these terms, we conducted a systematic electronic search across the MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane databases. The search was limited to articles published in English between 2000 and 2024, with the final search performed on October 27, 2024.

Inclusion criteria

The studies included in this systematic review met the following criteria: (1) involvement of adult patients (age > 18 years) undergoing minimally invasive colorectal surgery, including laparoscopic or robotic approaches, with the implementation of either a standard Enhanced Recovery Pathway (ERP) protocol or an augmented ERP (aERP) approach; (2) comparison of outcomes between aERPs and standard ERPs, with at least one patient in each treatment arm; and (3) reporting of postoperative outcomes, such as complication rates, readmission rates, or length of stay (LOS). To ensure data uniqueness, when multiple publications from the same or overlapping cohorts were identified, the most recent publication or the one with the largest sample size and most relevant information was selected.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria included non-human studies, pediatric patients, studies not available in full text, and articles without sufficient outcome data.

Outcomes of interests

The primary outcome of this meta-analysis was 30-day readmission rate. Secondary outcomes were 30-day ED revisit, re-operation, and complications.

Study selection and data extraction

The bibliographic details of all retrieved publications were stored in EndNote, and duplicate entries were removed. Two independent reviewers, Lin and Wong, assessed the remaining citations and recorded the details of selected articles using a predefined form. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus or, if necessary, through consultation with a third reviewer, Yu.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

An independent reviewer (You) assessed the risk of bias in the included study using the Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [18]. The assessment evaluated domains including confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as having low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias, and an overall risk rating was summarized.

aERPs and erps

The definition of SDD protocols varied among studies; for this analysis, SDD was defined as patients being discharged on the same calendar date as their surgery, without an overnight stay [19]. Patients discharged within postoperative day 1 (either on the same day as surgery or the following calendar day) were classified as having undergone accelerated recovery with early discharge and included in the aERPs group. Due to variability in the implementation of ERPs, no restrictions were placed on the specific ‘standard’ elements included. To ensure consistency in ERPs care despite variations in adherence across institutions, the control group (ERPs) was required to implement at least seven ERPs elements. This threshold aligns with the Cochrane review by Spanjersberg et al. [20]

Data synthesis

Among the studies we reviewed, several categorized patients based on their postoperative length of stay (LOS), such as postoperative day (POD) 1, 2, 3, or beyond. We synthesized the outcomes of interest, including patient demographics, perioperative outcomes, readmission rates, ED revisits, reoperations, and complications. Patients discharged on POD 2 or later were designated as the control group (ERPs), while those discharged on POD 0 or POD 1 were classified as the intervention group (aERPs) for comparison. For calculations and analyses, medians were converted to means using the method described on the website provided by Abbas et al. [21]

Statistical analysis

We extracted all data from the original studies for analysis. Statistical analysis was conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) version 8.10.0 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, Copenhagen, Denmark). Outcome measures, including mean with standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR), were recorded. Binary outcomes were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) estimated using the Mantel-Haenszel (M-H) method. When means and SDs were not reported, they were estimated from the study data using medians and IQRs, following the methods described by Abbas et al. (2024) [21].

Pooled estimates of differences were calculated using a random-effects model where necessary to account for inter-study heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic, with values > 50% indicating considerable heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses were performed when appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Search overview

A total of 424 articles were identified, with 87 duplicate studies removed. During the title and abstract screening, 297 articles were excluded, including 276 non-relevant articles and 21 focused on non-colorectal surgery. Subsequently, 40 articles deemed potentially relevant were reviewed in full. Of these, 21 were excluded due to the absence of a comparison group, along with 3 review articles and 2 editorials. Ultimately, 13 articles met the inclusion criteria. Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flowchart outlining our search strategy.

The included studies were conducted across multiple centers, primarily in North America, with a focus on the United States and Canada, spanning the period from 2006 to 2024. The majority of studies utilized retrospective or prospective cohort designs, with one study employing a small-scale randomized controlled trial (RCT). A total of 40,589 patients were included in the qualitative and quantitative analyses, with 1,783 patients (4.4%) in the aERPs group and 38,806 in the ERPs group. Bednarski et al. contributed the fewest patients to the aERP group (0.7%), while McKenna et al. contributed the most (50.8%). Among the studies, the mean age ranged from 47.2 to 67.0 years in the aERPs group and 56.5 to 71.0 years in the standard ERPs group. The proportion of female patients ranged from 34% to 65% in the aERPs group and from 37.5% to approximately 60% in the ERPs group. Surgical indications varied across studies, with malignancy, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and diverticular disease being the most common indications. Both traditional open approaches and minimally invasive surgeries were included, though the number of open surgeries was very limited. The categorization of surgical procedures varied across countries, potentially influenced by differences in reimbursement systems. Primary and secondary outcomes

There was no statistically significant difference between the groups for the primary outcome of the 30-day readmission rate (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.55–1.29, p = 0.43), as shown in Fig. 3. Similarly, no significant differences were observed between the groups for the 30-day ED revisit rate (Fig. 4A; OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.42–2.18, p = 0.91), 30-day reoperation rate (Fig. 4B; OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.61–1.54, p = 0.90), or total complication rate (Fig. 5A; OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.17–1.32, p = 0.15). Additionally, there were no significant differences between the groups for specific complications, including anastomotic insufficiency, ileus, and surgical site infections, as illustrated in Figs. 5B–5D. In shorts, the outcomes we analyzed were all statistically non-significant (Table 3).

Study quality

Figure 2 presents a summary of the risk of bias assessment for all 13 studies using the ROBINS-I tool. Most studies were assessed to have an overall moderate risk of bias and were considered methodologically robust. However, two studies were categorized as having a serious risk of bias due to issues with patient selection, where unaccounted confounders between groups may have led to inaccurate results. Only one study was classified as having a low overall risk of bias, which was a small RCT. The process of randomization effectively minimized between-group confounding factors in this study.

Discussion

In this review, we analyzed a total of thirteen articles comparing aERPs and ERPs in patients undergoing colorectal resection or stoma reversal surgery, focusing on readmission rates, ED revisits, reoperations, and complications. This study may be the first to conduct a meta-analysis on this topic. The risk of bias was moderate in most studies, with only one RCT classified as low risk. Our meta-analysis demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the aERPs and ERPs groups for any of the analyzed outcomes, suggesting that discharging patients within POD1 is a safe and feasible strategy for appropriately selected patients according to these real-world data [22]. Our study can be classified as Level 2 evidence according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) or as moderate-quality evidence under the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system [23, 24].

The included literature was primarily composed of North American cohort studies, dominated by a large dataset from Mckenna et al. using the National Surfical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) database [25], alongside a single phase II RCT by Bednarski et al. [14]. Previous systematic reviews [26, 27] have suggested the potential for early discharge following major colorectal surgery; however, high heterogeneity and a lack of randomized data previously prevented a definitive meta-analysis. Additionally, the discrepancy between trial data and real-world data presents a challenge for surgeons in adopting this proactive perioperative care approach. By incorporating newly published, higher-quality studies [28–30],, we aimed to bridge the gap between controlled trial data and real-world applicability, providing greater clarity on the safety of this proactive approach.

Healthcare utilization: The “safety net” fallacy

Evidence from the published literature suggests that aERPs may be feasible and cost-effective for selected patients [31–33]. However, a persistent concern in the surgical community is that early discharge simply shifts the burden of care from the inpatient ward to the emergency department (ED). Critics often argue that keeping a patient in the hospital for 2–3 days acts as a “safety net” to capture early complications. Our meta-analysis challenges this assumption. The results indicated that patients undergoing aERPs did not experience higher rates of readmissions (p = 0.55) or ED revisits (p = 0.42) compared to those with longer stays.

This “null result” is clinically profound. It implies that the complications leading to readmission—such as anastomotic leaks or deep surgical site infections—typically manifest later in the postoperative course, often between days 5 and 7, well after even a standard 3–4 day hospital stay would have concluded. Therefore, extending hospitalization for “observation” in an otherwise stable patient does not prevent these complications; it merely exposes the patient to the risks of prolonged hospitalization, such as nosocomial infections and decreased mobility, without offering additional protection against major surgical adverse events.

While individual studies showed variability, the aggregate data reinforces the stability of these outcomes. For instance, Bednarski et al. [14] reported a high odds ratio for readmission (OR 6.60), but this was driven by a small sample size where only 2 of 14 aERP patients were readmitted. Conversely, larger datasets like McKenna et al. [25] which comprised the majority of our sample, reported an odds ratio of 0.83, closely aligning with our meta-analysis finding of 0.85. Similarly, the readmission rate for aERPs in our analysis was 6.8%, comparable to the 5% reported by Siragusa et al. [31]. This consistency across vast populations suggests that the “safety net” of a standard hospital stay is largely redundant for appropriately selected patients.

Clinical safety and the physiology of early recovery

Our meta-analysis found no significant differences in 30-day postoperative complications, including specific issues like anastomotic leakage, postoperative ileus, and surgical site infections. A critical driver of this safety profile appears to be the evolution of multimodal analgesia. Successful aERPs are inextricably linked to the use of opioid-sparing analgesia, which directly addresses the physiological barriers to discharge.

Studies by Robitaille et al. [34] and Paradis et al. [28] highlighted that the use of transversus abdominis plane (TAP) blocks was instrumental in facilitating early discharge. By reducing reliance on systemic opioids, these protocols minimize postoperative ileus and nausea—the two most common reasons for delayed discharge. When pain is managed via multimodal pathways involving TAP blocks, bowel function returns sooner, and the patient returns to their baseline physiological state more rapidly, enabling safe discharge on POD 1. Furthermore, the reoperation rate for the aERP group was 2%, comparable to the 1.3% in the standard ERP group, and consistent with the 1% reported by Siragusa et al. [31]. This reinforces that early discharge does not lead to “missed” complications requiring surgical intervention. The data suggests that the success of aERPs is not merely about “sending patients home early”; it is a marker of a highly effective perioperative pain management protocol that preserves physiological function.

Barriers to implementation: The social determinants of recovery

Despite the statistical safety shown in this meta-analysis, the transition to same-day discharge is fraught with “real-world” challenges that cannot be captured by p-values alone. Social support remains a critical, often non-negotiable, prerequisite for aERPs, and its absence is a major barrier to widespread adoption.

The feasibility of discharging a patient mere hours after major abdominal surgery relies heavily on the “hospital-at-home” concept described in SAGES guidelines [19]. This requires a capable, physically present caregiver who can assist the patient with mobility, manage a complex medication schedule, and recognize early warning signs of deterioration. In our analysis, outcomes were favorable precisely because the included studies utilized strict selection criteria. However, in the broader population, patients with limited social networks, those living alone, or those with low health literacy may face catastrophic failure if discharged too early.

Furthermore, geographic distance plays a significant role. Patients living far from a tertiary medical center may not have the luxury of a quick ED revisit if a minor complication arises. For these populations, the “safety” of aERPs is theoretical rather than practical. If a patient cannot guarantee a safe home environment with reliable support, aERPs are contraindicated, regardless of their surgical recovery speed. Consequently, future implementation of aERPs must include a rigorous “social stress test” alongside medical screening, ensuring that the burden of care is not unsafely shifted to unprepared family members or caregivers.

Barriers to implementation: medical comorbidities vs. surgical success

A second major hurdle identified in the synthesis of these studies is the distinction between surgical success and medical readiness. It is a common clinical scenario for a patient to have a flawless surgical procedure—minimal blood loss, intact anastomosis, stable vital signs—yet be medically unfit for discharge on POD 1.

This is particularly prevalent in the elderly and those with significant comorbidities (ASA III-IV). While the surgical site may be stable, these patients often grapple with non-surgical issues that prevent discharge. Common medical hurdles include urinary retention, which is a frequent side effect of anesthesia and pelvic dissection; orthostatic hypotension, which makes early mobilization dangerous in frail patients; and unmanaged diabetes or renal insufficiency. As noted in the literature, elderly patients often require longer observation to ensure renal function and hydration status are stable before leaving the hospital.

Therefore, the low utilization rate of same-day surgery (< 5%) noted in the context of these studies is not merely a reflection of surgeon hesitancy, but a recognition of patient complexity. A patient with multiple comorbidities may have a surgically perfect colectomy but still requires a 3-day stay for medical optimization. Expanding aERPs to this demographic without enhanced home-monitoring capabilities would likely lead to the very readmissions we are trying to avoid. Implementation of aERPs requires a strict selection process that evaluates not only the surgical procedure but the patient’s “whole-person” readiness, including their physiological reserve [19, 33].

The role of technology and future directions

To bridge the gap between the strict selection criteria of current studies and the broader patient population, technology must play a central role. The RecoverMI trial by Bednarski et al. [25], though small, pioneered the use of “TeleRecovery,” utilizing remote monitoring to track patient progress.

The future of aERPs likely lies in the integration of wearable biosensors and telemedicine. If clinicians can remotely monitor a patient’s heart rate, temperature, and oxygen saturation in real-time, they may be able to safely discharge “medically complex” patients earlier. Artificial Intelligence (AI) could further aid in this by predicting which patients are at high risk for readmission based on intraoperative data, allowing for personalized discharge planning. Based on our analysis, which primarily includes cohort studies, the findings reflect ‘real-world data’ and indicate no significantly higher rates of readmission, reoperation, or complications in the aERPs group. Consequently, this proactive perioperative care approach shows promise for replication and broader implementation to help reduce healthcare burdens.

Limitation and strength

This study has several limitations. First, the included studies exhibited heterogeneity in the definition of accelerated ERP protocols, patient selection, length of follow-up, surgical indications, and procedures performed. However, this variability may reflect real-world conditions, thereby enhancing the generalizability and reproducibility of the findings. Second, limited information was available regarding the standard ERP protocols, making it challenging to assess the extent to which elements of accelerated ERP protocols were implemented in the control groups. Nonetheless, the core principles of ERPs remain deeply ingrained in colorectal surgical practice and continue to evolve alongside advancements in surgical techniques, telemedicine technology, and the integration of artificial intelligence into the medical field. Third, the available information on quality of life, long-term outcomes—both functional and oncological—and adherence to or implementation of the protocols was relatively limited. As a result, the true impact of accelerated ERPs may be either under- or overestimated.

A key strength of this study is the comprehensive literature search, offering an up-to-date synthesis of current evidence on augmented Enhanced Recovery Pathways (aERPs). Furthermore, we may be among the first to conduct a meta-analysis on these moderately heterogeneous results, incorporating the latest available studies compared to prior systematic reviews. With acceptable levels of heterogeneity, our findings represent a comprehensive and updated dataset in this field.

Future large-scale clinical trials are needed, incorporating well-defined intervention and control groups. These studies should clearly outline complications, include a follow-up period of at least 30 days, and address key factors such as quality of life, protocol adherence and implementation. Such research could pave the way for the development of a standardized accelerated ERP framework.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive evaluation of the outcomes associated with accelerated enhanced recovery protocols (aERPs) compared to standard ERPs in minimally invasive colorectal surgery. The findings suggest that aERPs, including same-day discharge and early postoperative discharge strategies, are not associated with increased 30-day readmission rates, emergency department visits, reoperations, or complications. However, it must be noted that same-day colorectal surgery is currently performed in a very small proportion of patients (< 5%). Future high-quality RCTs are needed to better define the specific patient populations most likely to benefit from aERPs while ensuring safety and efficacy.
