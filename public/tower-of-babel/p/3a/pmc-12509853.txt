Multimodal imaging in the diagnosis of myocardial abscesses in disseminated aspergillosis
Authors: Carlos-Igor Morr-Verenzuela, Guillem Balagué, Mariana Benegas, Rafael Jimenez, Clement Lau, Andreea Sorina Afana, Kamil Stankowski, Subhi Akleh, Kenichiro Otsuka
Published: 2025

Case description

A 64-year-old male, who had undergone hepatorenal transplantation, was admitted with fever, dyspnoea, abdominal pain, and right lower limb oedema. Physical examination revealed an erythematous plaque on the right leg. A skin biopsy was performed, yielding colonies of Aspergillus fumigatus, and the serum galactomannan antigen test was positive.

As part of the diagnostic work-up, a thoracoabdominal computed tomography (CT) scan was performed, which revealed pulmonary and hepatic nodules suggestive of abscesses. Additionally, CT identified multiple incidental hypodense myocardial lesions (Figure 1A–C)

(A and B) Thorax computed tomography without IV contrast (HRCT), revealed the presence of hypodense myocardial lesions (*). (C) Contrast-enhanced thorax computed tomography revealed hypodense lesion in the interventricular septum (*) without contrast uptake. (D and G) SSFP CMR-cine four-chamber and short-axis images showing intramural lesions, located in the septum and apical lateral walls, areas with intermediate myocardial signal intensity (*). (E) Cardiac magnetic resonance four-chamber first-pass myocardial perfusion sequence, showing non-perfused intramural lesions (*) in the interventricular septum and apical lateral walls. (F and H) Cardiac magnetic resonance four-chamber and short-axis early gadolinium enhancement images showing two intramural lesions with hypoenhanced core (*) surrounded by a hyperenhancement ring-like halo (arrow). (I) Late gadolinium imaging showing an apical lateral myocardial abscess with epicardial involvement as a hyperenhancement ring halo (arrow) with hypoenhanced core (*). (J–N) Follow-up at 2 months cardiac magnetic resonance showing significant reduction in lesions size (arrow). (*), the location of the intramyocardial lesions.

For accurate characterization, a cardiac magnetic resonance was performed, which revealed multiple intramural myocardial lesions, with the largest located in the mid-septum (21 × 17 mm), mid-inferolateral wall, and apical lateral wall. On SSFP cine sequences, the lesions appeared as areas of intermediate myocardial signal intensity (Figure 1D and G; Supplementary material online, Videos S1–S3). First-pass perfusion imaging showed no contrast uptake (Figure 1E; Supplementary material online, Videos S4 and S5). Early and late gadolinium enhancement sequences (EGE, LGE) revealed a hypoenhanced core surrounded by a ring-like peripheral hyperenhancement (Figure 1F, H, and I).

These imaging features were consistent with myocardial abscesses, as described in similar previous reports.1–3

The option of performing a cardiac biopsy was rejected due to potential serious complications and the risk of extracardiac dissemination. Similarly, surgical resection was ruled out due to the patient’s frailty, significant comorbidities and prohibitive surgical risk.

A conservative management was adopted. Early systemic antifungal treatment with voriconazole and liposomal amphotericin B was initiated, with long-term therapy planned for 6 month to 1 year.

Follow-up cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) at 2 months demonstrated a marked reduction in the size of the lesions (Figure 1J–N).

Intramyocardial aspergillosis is a rare and life-threatening manifestation of disseminated fungal infection. Its diagnosis is challenging, and high clinical suspicion is needed.

It typically affects immunosuppressed individuals but has been reported in critically ill immunocompetent patients. Clinical manifestations are non-specific, including symptoms of myocardial infarction, arrhythmias, pericarditis, and myocarditis, making diagnosis even more challenging. Blood cultures are usually negative, requiring biopsy or post-mortem confirmation. Pathological findings often include haemorrhagic lesions, small-vessel thrombosis, and myocardial necrosis.2, 3

Mortality rates are extremely high. Early recognition is therefore essential to allow timely reduction or even discontinuation of immunosuppressive agents when feasible and early initiation of antifungal treatment in an effort to improve outcomes.3

Multimodal imaging, especially CMR, can help guide non-invasive diagnosis and monitor disease progression.1–3
