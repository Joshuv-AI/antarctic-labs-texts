Laparoscopic type 3 radical hysterectomy with enclosed colpotomy using the Bakay technique for early-stage cervical cancer: a retrospective case series
Authors: Gamze Savci, Eda Adeviye Sahin, Hanifi Sahin, Mehmet Faruk Olcenoglu, Kadir Bakay, Turan Sahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent developments in cervical cancer treatment have highlighted concerns about minimally invasive surgery. Our team explored an innovative approach that might address these concerns while preserving the patient benefits of laparoscopic procedures.MethodsWe studied 11 women with early-stage cervical cancer who underwent surgery using our modified approach. Instead of using traditional uterine manipulators, we employed the Bakay technique—creating a protective seal around the tumor site before removal. This approach aims to prevent cancer cells from spreading during surgery, addressing a key concern with minimally invasive procedures.ResultsOur diverse patient group included women across different age ranges with predominantly squamous cell carcinoma. Two patients needed additional radiation therapy due to specific risk factors. No recurrence was observed in any patient during follow-up (median 35 months; range 8–48 months). The 5-year OS rate, estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method, was 100% in this small series.ConclusionsThis small retrospective series demonstrates that fully laparoscopic tumor containment using the Bakay technique is technically feasible and was associated with no recurrences over a median follow-up of 35 months. These preliminary findings justify evaluation in larger prospective studies designed to determine whether this approach can achieve oncological outcomes comparable to open radical hysterectomy.Keywords: Early‑stage cervical cancer, Enclosed colpotomy, Laparoscopic radical hysterectomy, Bakay technique

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide, highlighting the pressing need for effective treatment strategies [1]. Despite advancements in medical technology and the development of various treatment modalities, open radical hysterectomy has consistently been recognized as the gold standard for managing early-stage cervical cancer, as endorsed by the latest guidelines from prominent organizations such as the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO), the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO), and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [2–4]. These guidelines emphasize the importance of open radical hysterectomy due to its well-established oncological outcomes and long-term efficacy.

The publication of the LACC trial by Ramirez et al. in 2018 fundamentally altered the landscape of minimally invasive surgery for cervical cancer. That prospective randomized study demonstrated that patients undergoing minimally invasive radical hysterectomy had significantly lower disease-free survival (86.0% versus 96.5% at 4.5 years) and higher rates of regional recurrence compared with those treated by open abdominal radical hysterectomy [5].

The mechanisms underlying the inferior oncological outcomes associated with minimally invasive surgery have been the subject of considerable investigation. A narrative review by Fusegi et al. identified intraoperative cancer cell spillage as a central explanatory factor, attributing it to tumor exposure, uterine manipulator use, and direct handling of the cervix during colpotomy — none of which were adequately controlled in the LACC trial [6]. Consistent with this hypothesis, a propensity score-matched analysis by Kamata et al. subsequently demonstrated that patients who underwent tumor spillage prevention during laparoscopic radical hysterectomy had significantly longer disease-free survival than those who did not (HR 3.54; 95% CI 1.23–10.23), with prevention also associated with lower rates of local recurrence [7].

Two main tumor-containment strategies have been described in the literature. Köhler and colleagues reported a vaginally assisted technique in which the cervical tumor is encased transvaginally prior to laparoscopic dissection [8]. The no-look no-touch technique described by Kanao et al. avoids uterine manipulators and bags the specimen vaginally after completion of laparoscopic dissection [9]. While both approaches have demonstrated favorable oncological results, each requires vaginal contact with the tumor at some stage of the procedure.

In 2018, Bakay described a novel technique for laparoscopic cuff closure using a circumferential purse-string suture placed entirely through laparoscopic access [10]. In the present study, we adapted this technique to achieve complete laparoscopic enclosure of the cervical tumor before any vaginal entry, thereby eliminating both uterine manipulator use and vaginal tumor contact. To our knowledge, this is the first description of fully laparoscopic tumor containment using the Bakay circular suturing technique in cervical cancer surgery.

Methods

This retrospective case series included all consecutive patients who underwent laparoscopic type 3 (Wertheim-Meigs) radical hysterectomy with enclosed colpotomy using the Bakay technique between January 2018 and March 2023.

Inclusion criteria

FIGO 2018 stage Ib1, Ib2, or Ib3 cervical cancer; tumor size ≤ 4 cm on preoperative PET-CT imaging; absence of parametrial involvement on bimanual examination under general anesthesia and PET-CT; no evidence of lymph node metastases or distant disease; and medical fitness for laparoscopic surgery.

Exclusion criteria

FIGO stage IIA or higher; tumor diameter > 4 cm; radiological or clinical evidence of parametrial involvement; prior pelvic radiation; or inability to undergo general anesthesia.

Primary endpoint

Disease-free survival (DFS), defined as the time from surgery to first documented recurrence (confirmed by biopsy of a suspicious lesion identified on PET-CT, MRI, or vaginal examination) or death from any cause.

Secondary endpoints

Overall survival (OS), defined as time from surgery to death from any cause; and surgical complication rate, graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification [11].

Follow-up protocol

Patients were followed at 3-month intervals for the first 2 years, at 6-month intervals from years 2 to 4, and annually thereafter. Each visit included clinical examination, vaginal cuff smear, MRI, and PET-CT. The median follow-up duration was 35 months (range: 8–48 months).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Karamanoglu Mehmetbey University Faculty of Medicine (Decision date: 30.05.2024, no: 06-2024/04), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed by a single experienced laparoscopic surgeon (H.S.) a gynecologic oncologist specializing in minimally invasive gynecological oncology. All the operations were performed under general anesthesia. Nasogastric intubation and urinary catheter were applied to the patients. Operations were performed with four trocars. Primary trocar was used for a 10-mm laparoscope (Karl Storz, Germany) placed usually in the umbilicus. Two lateral 5-mm trocars and one midline 10-mm trocar were used. The middle port was placed midway between the umbilicus and pubis. Haemostasis was usually performed using bipolar forceps (Karl Storz Robi, Germany), whereas dissection was performed using the LigaSure™ (Covidien, Medtronic, USA) Maryland jaw laparoscopic sealer/divider, bipolar forceps and scissors. All patients received systemic pelvic and paraaortic lymphadenectomy. Paraaortic lymphadenectomy was performed routinely in this series as part of the institutional protocol in place during the study period (2018–2023). The dissection was carried out up to the level of the inferior mesenteric artery. Paraaortic lymphadenectomy to the level of the inferior mesenteric artery was performed as part of the institutional protocol. Current NCCN guidelines acknowledge paraaortic lymphadenectomy as an acceptable staging procedure in patients with cervical cancer stage Ib1 and above, and given that our cohort included patients up to stage IIa, this approach was considered appropriate to identify occult nodal disease that would modify adjuvant treatment decisions.

Radical hysterectomy was performed as per the routine method. Uterine manipulation was achieved by placing two seperated sutures using 31-mm Vicryl polyglactin 910 sutures (Ethicon, Inc., Somerville, NJ) passing through the uterine fundus. Before cutting off the vagina, enclosed colpotomy was performed below the lesion of the cervical cancer. Enclosed colpotomy was based on the principle of closing the vaginal canal with a single circular purse suture below the cervical tumor line, after developing the bladder flap, a single 31-mm Vicryl polyglactin 910 suture (Ethicon, Inc., Somerville, NJ) was placed on the line between the cervical tumor and the bladder starting at either 4 to 3 o’clock or 8 to 9 o’clock, as anchor sutures and was continued circumferentially in a full-thickness purse-string fashion through 3, 1, 10, 8, 7, and 5 o’clock and tied completing a circle (Bakay Technique).

Lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) was generally negative in most cases, which is a positive indicator of the tumor’s potential for local spread. However, two patients had positive LVSI, suggesting a higher risk for tumor dissemination. Stromal invasion, which assesses the depth of tumor penetration into the cervical stroma, varied among patients. Most patients had negative or superficial invasion, while two cases showed deeper stromal invasion, specifically into the middle and deep third of the stroma.

Adjuvant treatment, such as External Beam Radiation Therapy (EBRT), was administered to two patients who exhibited positive stromal invasion and LVSI. This treatment decision was guided by the Sedlis criteria, which are used to determine the need for additional therapy based on tumor characteristics. The remaining patients did not require adjuvant treatment, indicating that their tumors were managed effectively with surgery alone.

In terms of postoperative outcomes, no recurrence of cancer was reported in any of the patients. Complications were minimal, with only one patient experiencing a ureterovaginal fistula (Clavien–Dindo IIIb), a rare but serious complication.

Discussion

There exist many studies in the literature where the findings reveal that the rates of survival without the disease and overall survival have been similar when minimally invasive surgery is compared with open surgery among the patients selected during the early stages of cervical cancer cases [12, 13]. However, the rate of disease-free survival in MIS was found to be lower when compared with open surgery as a result of the large-scale LACC trial published by Ramirez et al. in 2018. In that prospective randomized study, the rates of disease-free survival and overall survival concerning the patients on whom minimally invasive radical hysterectomy was administered in the early-stage cervical cancer cases were lower compared to those of the patients on whom open abdominal radical hysterectomy was administered [5]. DFS was 86% in the minimally invasive surgical leg in 4.5 years, while it was 96.5% in the open surgery leg. In addition, the regional recurrence rates were higher [5]. After a result of the large scale LACC trial, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society of Gynecological Oncology (ESGO) changed their treatment guidelines for early-stage cervical cancer [2, 14].

The LACC trial has brought about much discussion and adjustments, and yet, the outcomes of MIS and open surgery have been obtained in a similar fashion in some studies published subsequently [15, 16].

The study of Gil-Moreno et al.in 2019 determined a higher overall total survive (OS) in MIS than ARH [17]. Based on the analyses from the Korea database, Kim JH et al. in 2019 demonstrated that laparoscopic surgery was to be associated with better OS than abdominal operation (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.64–0.85; p < 0.001) [18]. In a retrospective study of Italian origin that included only stage IB1 patients, 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) was longer for the ARH compared to that of the MIRH group (p = 0.03), and there was no significant difference in OS (p = 0.69) [19]. In another retrospective series, the MIRH group had a worse 5-year DFS ratio (p < 0.001), and the multivariate analysis results showed that laparoscopic radical hysterectomy (LRH) was an independent bad prognostic factor (HR 2.883, 95%CI 1.711–4.859; p < 0.001) [20]. In an extensive retrospective series based on Korea database involving stage IB1/IIA patients, worse DFS was observed in the LRH group than in the ARH (HR 2.738, 95% CI 1.326–5.650; p = 0.005); however, OS was not significantly different between the two groups (HR 0.592, 95% CI 0.071–4.922, p = 0.624). Moreover, the recurrence rate was found higher for the MIRH (13.3%versus 4.8%) [21].

Likewise, it was reported in the large scale CIRCOL study published in 2022 that there was no difference between the rates of disease-free survival and overall survival [22].

Notwithstanding, it was stated in the study published by Ramirez et al. that the use of uterine manipulator and the adverse effects of the tumor cells’ spread due to the Co2 gas contacting with the tumor were among the reasons for low survival rates in the MIS group [5].

Consistent with the findings of this report, a very extensive multicenter study (n = 389) reported by Kohler et al. in Germany draws the attention to a combined vaginally assisted laparoscopic radical hysterectomy where the cervical tumor is encased vaginally before resection. Based on the results obtained by this combined technique, the 4.5- and 10-year DFS rates were found to be 95.8%, and 93.1%, respectively, while the 4.5- and 10-year OS rates were 97.8% and 95.8%, respectively. Consequently, the results were reported to be good at excellent level [8].

Our technique takes a different approach from these methods. The Köhler technique works through the vagina to seal the tumor before doing the laparoscopic dissection, which limits visibility and requires vaginal manipulation. The no-look no-touch technique avoids manipulators but bags the specimen vaginally after finishing the laparoscopic work. Our method does the tumor sealing entirely through the laparoscope before opening the vagina at all. This gives us better visualization during the critical sealing step and keeps the abdominal cavity sealed throughout. Whether this fully laparoscopic approach translates to improved oncological outcomes compared with other containment strategies remains to be established in prospective comparative studies.

As an example of new approaches, in the retrospective study conducted with the no-look no-touch technique (n = 163; avoiding the uterine manipulator, minimum invasion of the cervix and bagging the specimen) used in the LRH to prevent tumor spillage, the oncological outcomes were found to be similar between the two approaches (locoregional recurrence 6.3% versus 9.6%, p = 0,566; DFS 94.4% versus 90.9%, p = 0.591; OS 100% versus 96.5%; p = 0.188) [9]. In a study by Uppal, in patients with tumor size measured ≤ 2 cm before the operation, 4.1% and 6% recurrences (p = 0.34) were shown in the ARH and MIRH groups, without negative effect for the MIRH group in the multivariate analysis [23]. Contrary to these studies, Paik et al. found the rate of DFS to be lower in the LRH group of patients with tumor size < 2 cm (HR 12.987, 95% CI 1.451-116.244, p = 0.003); but no difference was found in terms of OS [21]. In a retrospective study conducted by Kim et al. in 2021, it was reported that survival rates were similar between the ARH and MIRH groups after 42.1 months of follow up. However, in cases with tumor size > 2 cm, the recurrence rate was found to be high (22.5% versus 0%; p = 0,008) and the DFS rate was low (p = 0.017) in the MIRH group. As a result, MIRH for < 2 cm tumors was described as not causing any change in oncological outcomes [24].

It is in evidence that there is further need for randomized controlled trials comparing MIRH and ARH. The preliminary data of another retrospective trial, Europe Retrospective Cohort Observation, SUCCOR (Surgery in Cervical Cancer Observational Retrospective), which compares LRH/RRH and ARH, reveal the 4.5-year DFS (82% versus 93%; HR 3.48, %95 CI 1.17–9.48; p = 0.023) and 4.5-year OS outcomes (88% versus 96%, p = 0.016) of the patients in the MIRH group [25]. While the use of uterine manipulator during MIRH is associated with a worse DFS (HR 2.3, 95% CI 1.32–4.29), it has been observed that MIRH does not yield worse oncological outcomes than ARH without manipulator use and protective maneuvers. Our study adds three specific findings to this ongoing debate. First, we show that complete tumor containment using laparoscopy alone is technically achievable across a range of tumor sizes (1.8 to 4 cm) and stages. Second, the absence of recurrence in our series is consistent with the hypothesis that preventing intraoperative tumor spillage may contribute to favorable oncological outcomes, though this cannot be confirmed without a comparative study. Third, unlike techniques that require vaginal access to seal the tumor, our approach stays completely laparoscopic, which may be technically easier and maintain better visibility. These findings help us understand which technical steps might be necessary to make minimally invasive surgery as safe as open surgery for cervical cancer. Moreover, a significant statistical difference was not detected in DFS between the two approaches concerning the patients who had conization previously and < 2 cm tumor size [26].

Accompanied by recently added literature, NICE reported that MIS can be preferred for < 2 cm tumors and there is no difference in the DFS and OS rates [27].

Motivated by the same concerns and considerations, we, in our study, adopted the method of removing the tumor without the use of a manipulator, inspired by the Bakay technique by its being thoroughly packaged without any contact with the abdomen before the colpotomy through placing a vaginal round suture in MIS. In our study, a follow-up period of 48 months was reached in one stage 1b1 patient and 2 stage 1b2 patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery, and no recurrence was detected. Moreover, one of the stage 1 b2 cases had lymphovascular invasion positive preoperatively and had stromal 1/3 invasion. One of the two patients with stage 1b3 and a tumor diameter of 4 cm had a 24-month recurrence-free follow-up period, and the other patient had deep stromal invasion preoperatively and is only in the 8th month postoperatively. The principal limitation of this study is its small sample size of 11 patients. No definitive conclusions regarding oncological safety or long-term efficacy can be drawn from such a series. The median follow-up of 35 months, with one patient having only 8 months of observation, is insufficient to assess long-term disease-free or overall survival for cervical cancer. The absence of a control arm further precludes any comparison with open or other minimally invasive approaches. From a clinical standpoint, the Bakay technique should not currently be offered as an alternative to open radical hysterectomy outside the context of a formal prospective study. The value of this series lies in demonstrating technical feasibility and providing proof-of-concept data to support the design of adequately powered comparative trials.

The fact that surgical procedures that would prevent the spread of cancer cells was not mentioned in the LACC study is an incomplete evaluation of minimally invasive surgery. Our series raises the hypothesis that complete intraoperative tumor containment during MIRH may be a critical determinant of oncological outcomes. However, whether such outcomes are truly comparable to those of open radical hysterectomy cannot be established from this small, single-arm series.

Not using a uterine manipulator and the administration of enclosed colpotomy with the Bakay technique has been described in our study for the first time in the literature.

This surgical approach seems to be safe and applicable for the patients who have early-stage cervical cancer. A larger number of cases and a longer period of follow-up are required in order to understand whether this surgical approach will be really impactive on the prognosis. Hence, the long-term results need to be evaluated in the future prospective studies with adequate number of case series. The routine inclusion of paraaortic lymphadenectomy to the level of the inferior mesenteric artery reflects our institutional protocol and may limit the generalizability of our surgical experience; it does not form part of the Bakay technique itself.

In conclusion, this is the first reported series demonstrating that fully laparoscopic tumor containment using the Bakay technique is technically feasible in patients undergoing type 3 radical hysterectomy for early-stage cervical cancer. No recurrences were observed over a median follow-up of 35 months across a range of tumor sizes and stages, and the complication profile was acceptable. These findings are encouraging but must be interpreted within the constraints of a small, retrospective, single-arm series without a comparator group and with limited follow-up duration. No conclusions regarding oncological equivalence with open radical hysterectomy can be drawn at this stage. The primary contribution of this study is to establish feasibility and to provide the technical basis for future multicenter, prospective, comparative trials.
