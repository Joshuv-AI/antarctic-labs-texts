Mechanistic Elucidation and Optimization of Alkali Activation for Si–Al Recovery from Coal Fly Ash
Authors: Quang Tuan Lai, Ji Whan Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coal fly ash (FA), a major byproduct of coal-fired power plants, remains underutilized despite its high content of silica (SiO2) and alumina (Al2O3). This study investigates alkali activation as a route for selective Si and Al recovery, combining experimental leaching, thermodynamic modeling, and response surface methodology (RSM). Parametric analysis revealed that NaOH concentration, temperature, and reaction time critically influenced dissolution behavior. Optimal conditions5 M NaOH, 140 °C, and 1 hachieved up to 50.4% Si dissolution while minimizing Al solubilization (<3%). Mechanistic evaluation identified a three-stage process: (i) initial extraction, (ii) concurrent dissolution–precipitation governed by zeolite and phillipsite formation, and (iii) diffusion-limited Si release under surface passivation. XRD, SEM-EDS, and equilibrium modeling confirmed sodalite-type precipitation as the principal limitation to Al recovery. RSM-derived regression models (R 2 > 0.94) accurately predicted Si and Al dissolution across variable conditions, enabling process optimization with reduced experimental burden. Beyond resource recovery, selective removal of Si and Al facilitates enrichment of rare-earth elements in the residue, enhancing downstream extraction potential. Recovered silica and alumina can be valorized in advanced alloys, ceramics, adsorbents, and photovoltaic applications, positioning alkali activation as a viable pathway for circular utilization of FA within low-carbon materials innovation.

Introduction

In the contemporary context, with the rapid evolution of the global energy landscape, coal constitutes approximately 40% of the world’s electricity generation. Simultaneously, there exists a substantial and growing demand for electricity, and the absence of a reliable alternative energy source currently underscores the persistently high and escalating reliance on coal. This reliance on coal has been well-documented.

On a global scale, an annual production of the FA ranges between 600 to 1000 million tons. Notably, FA has historically been regarded as the largest industrial waste product in the United States, with an output of nearly 130 million tons, a significant portion of which remains underutilized.

The composition and characteristics of FA are contingent upon the specific raw materials and other variables inherent in the production process. Presently, approximately 70% of the FA is employed in low-value-added sectors as documented by Ding et al., with another 10% being refined into high-value materials, in accordance with Ji et al., while the remaining 20% predominantly accumulates due to suboptimal management practices. In light of these circumstances, industrial entities have begun to focus on the high-value utilization of FA, emphasizing the extraction of beneficial constituents from FA to create advanced, high-value alloys. Notably, Al–Si alloys are extensively applied in critical domains like aviation and transportation owing to their superior ductility and wear resistance. ,

Traditionally, raw materials such as pure silica, waste cutting materials, and simulated volcanic rocks have been employed for Al–Si alloy production, with limited attention dedicated to the conversion of aluminum–silicon mixtures within FA. Furthermore, the predominant method for manufacturing aluminum–silicon alloys is the direct energization electrolysis method, characterized by subpar current efficiency (averaging around 40.7%), resulting in inefficient production.

Previous studies on FA valorization have explored both acid and alkaline routes for Si and Al extraction. Acid leaching systems are effective for aluminum recovery but often suffer from silica gel formation, which hinders solid–liquid separation and reduces process efficiency. At 100 °C for 2 h with 300 g/L NaOH and an L/S ratio of 5 mL/g, coal fly ash showed 33.81% Si dissolution but only 1.37% aluminum extraction. In contrast, alkaline activation using NaOH enables selective dissolution of amorphous silica phases. A comparative study by Murmu et al. demonstrated that NaOH exhibited higher desilication efficiency than KOH for high-silica Indian coal fly ash, achieving up to 55% silica removal under optimal conditions (20 wt % NaOH, 100 °C, 2 h, L/S = 5:1), whereas KOH reached 43% under more severe conditions (28 wt %, 150 °C, 2 h, L/S = 10:1). However, it is frequently accompanied by secondary precipitation of aluminosilicate phases such as zeolites and sodalite. , Recent studies have also investigated combined alkali–acid processes to enhance overall recovery. Yan et al. demonstrated that alkaline leaching enhanced with ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) effectively improved silicon recovery, achieving up to 50% Si dissolution from coal fly ash. However, these approaches increase process complexity and chemical consumption. Despite these advances, a clear mechanistic understanding of the dissolution–precipitation interplay governing selective Si and Al recovery remains limited.

This study was initiated to devise an efficacious approach for extracting silicon, aluminum, iron, and calcium components from the coal FA. Given that the FA constitutes a heterogeneous waste product containing a multitude of elements, it is a viable candidate as a source of raw materials for ongoing research applications. Recovered silicon, for instance, finds utility in various research endeavors such as nanosilica production, contributing to self-compacting concrete, antireflection coatings for solar cells, and photonic devices. − Meanwhile, reclaimed aluminum in the form of Al2O3 serves as a valuable resource for the fabrication of heavy metal adsorbents, ceramic fiber production, modern aircraft and gas turbine manufacturing, and the automotive industry. −

A secondary rationale for this endeavor lies in the removal and recovery of silicon and aluminum from FA, which simplifies the concentration and isolation of the trace elements present in FA (Figure ), a concept emphasized by Lin et al. These trace elements, particularly the rare-earth elements, also hold multifaceted industrial applications.

Despite extensive research on coal fly ash utilization, selective recovery of silica and alumina remains challenging due to competing dissolution–precipitation reactions and limited mechanistic understanding. This study addresses this gap by integrating experimental investigation, thermodynamic modeling, and statistical optimization to elucidate the governing mechanisms and enable controlled, selective Si recovery while suppressing Al dissolution

Materials and Methods

Materials

The FA samples utilized in this study were sourced from coal-fired power facilities in South Korea, specifically from the Boryeong plants. Prior to the incorporation of alkaline materials, the samples were subjected to sieving, with a particle size cutoff of 100 μm.

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was employed as the leachant, and it was procured from the Junsei Chemical Company, Japan. To enhance mixing efficiency and maintain precise environmental temperature control, a PARR autoclave was utilized. The leachate was subsequently separated from the filtrate using vacuum filtration, facilitated by Advatec filter paper (5C, 110 mm).

The contents of Si and Al in the leachate were determined through the application of inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) as the analytical technique.

Method

Alkaline Activation

The leaching experiments were designed to systematically evaluate the effects of NaOH concentration, reaction time, and temperature on the dissolution of Si and Al from coal fly ash (FA). In each experiment, 15 g of FA was mixed with 300 mL of NaOH solution, corresponding to a solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL). The mixture was placed in a sealed reactor and heated using a temperature-controlled system. The reactor was first brought to the target temperature under static conditions to ensure thermal equilibrium. Once the desired temperature was reached, mechanical agitation was initiated at a constant speed of 400 rpm to start the reaction timing.

After the specified reaction time, the slurry was immediately subjected to solid–liquid separation via vacuum filtration. The filtrate was collected and analyzed for Si and Al concentrations using ICP-OES. The leaching efficiencies of Si and Al (%) were calculated according to eq , based on the measured concentrations in the leachate and the initial composition of the FA. All experiments were conducted under controlled and consistent conditions, with NaOH concentration, temperature, and reaction time varied systematically to assess their individual and interactive effects on Si and Al dissolution behavior. 1 Si,Aldissolution⁢(%)=C2×VC1×M×100 where C 2 is the ICP-analyzed result of Si/Al, mg/L; V is the final volume, L; C 1 is the content of Si/Al in the raw sample, mg kg–1; and M is the mass of sample used in the reaction, kg.

Thermodynamic Modeling

In this investigation, the Medusa–Hydra chemical equilibrium software was employed to generate predominance area and species distribution diagrams. This software package incorporates a comprehensive database of logK values at a temperature of 25 °C. The chemical species were designated within the software’s database. Subsequently, the program identified the suitable complexes and solid phases within the system and established the corresponding equilibrium (formation) constants. Subsequent to this step, the chemical equilibrium diagrams were constructed, founded upon the equilibrium system delineated in the former step.

Response Surface Method

Response surface methodology (RSM), pioneered by Box and Wilson in 1951, was developed with the primary aim of enhancing the production process within the chemical industry. Its key objective was to identify optimal factor settings for chemical reactions, thereby achieving a high yield and purity at a minimal cost. This was achieved by conducting a series of experiments that explored the various factors. Response optimization, a fundamental component of RSM, involves predicting responses based on experimental data and determining the factor combinations that optimize the conditions for achieving the desired response.

In this research, experimental tests were meticulously planned using the Box–Behnken statistical experimental design technique, a component of RSM integrated into the Minitab version 16. The experimentation focused on alkali activation tests, specifically investigating the impact of various factors, including reaction time, alkaline concentration, temperature, and CaO content, on the dissolution of Si and Al from FA. The pulp density and agitation speed were consistently maintained at 1:20 (g/mL) and 400 rpm, respectively. The experimental results were subsequently subjected to statistical analysis within the software to assess the effects and interactions of these factors on the Si and Al dissolution.

Furthermore, this analysis allowed for the prediction of Si and Al leaching efficiencies within unexplored regions of the experimental domain, where no experiments had been conducted. Ultimately, the RSM was employed to identify the optimal conditions for achieving the yield of Si and Al dissolution from FA at minimal cost.

Effects of Process Parameters on Si and Al Dissolution

Effect of Reaction Time on Si and Al Dissolution

The investigation employed two different NaOH concentrations, specifically 3 and 5 mol/L, while maintaining a constant temperature of 80 °C. The liquid-to-solid ratio (v/w) was set at 10, and the rotational speed (rpm) was maintained at 400. The leaching duration spanned a range from 15 min to 12 h. Figure illustrates the results pertaining to the influence of leaching duration on the efficiency of leaching Si and Al under these conditions.

Effect of leaching time on leaching efficiency of Si and Al in 3 and 5 M NaOH at 80 °C.

With an increasing leaching time, the leaching efficiency of Si displayed a gradual increment. Notably, the employment of a 5 M NaOH leaching solution resulted in a higher leaching efficiency for Si in comparison to the utilization of a 3 M NaOH solution. The highest Si leaching efficiency was achieved after a leaching period of 12 h. In contrast, the dissolution of Al exhibited an initial increase within the first 2 h of leaching and subsequently tapered off as the leaching duration continued to extend.

The initial use of a 5 M NaOH leaching solution resulted in higher levels of aluminum dissolution compared to a 3 M NaOH solution within the first 4 h of leaching. However, beyond this 4 h period, the aluminum concentration in the 5 M NaOH leach solution decreased more rapidly than that in the 3 M NaOH leach solution, leading to a lower aluminum concentration after 6 h of leaching. As the leaching time extended, the aluminum concentrations from both the 5 and 3 M NaOH leaching solutions converged to similar levels.

Figure depicts XRD analysis results for the FA residue after 2, 6, and 12 h of leaching in 3 M NaOH at 80 °C. Quartz and mullite were identified as major mineral constituents. Notably, the 12 h leaching residue revealed the emergence of phillipsite and zeolite, explaining the decreased Al leaching rate with prolonged reaction time (Figure ). The precipitation and surface deposition of newly formed aluminosilicate phases, phillipsite, and zeolite minerals, on FA particles are further confirmed by SEM analysis (Figure ).

Effect of NaOH Concentration on Si and Al Dissolution Behavior

To assess the impact of NaOH concentration on the dissolution of Si and Al, experiments were conducted under fixed conditions: time (4 h), temperature (80 °C), liquid/solid ratio, and rpm (400). NaOH concentrations ranging from 3 to 10 M were investigated. Results detailing Si and Al dissolution with varying NaOH concentrations are summarized in Table S2 and illustrated in Figure .

Effect of NaOH concentration on Si and Al efficiency at 80 °C for 4 h.

Increasing NaOH concentration from 3 to 5 M led to a rapid rise in Si and Al leaching, from 16.5 and 10.8% to 25.7 and 21.4%, respectively. Beyond 5 M, Si leaching showed insignificant growth (25.78 to 26.97%), while Al leaching gradually decreased from 21.4 to 14.17%. Higher NaOH concentrations enhanced Si and Al dissolution; however, Si reacted with dissolved Al to form a precipitate. Consequently, from 5 to 10 M NaOH, Si in the leach liquor slightly increased, and Al gradually decreased. Subsequently, 5 M was chosen as the optimal NaOH concentration for further tests.

The mineral composition of the alkali-activated residue FA under varying NaOH concentrations revealed the formation of sodalite (Na6(Al6Si6O24)­NaF·H2O) as a new mineral due to Si and Al precipitation in Na-rich solutions. This phenomenon contributes to the explanation of the declining Si and Al dissolution with increasing NaOH content (Figure ).

XRD analysis of the FA residue postleaching in 3 M, 5 M, and 7 M NaOH at 80 °C for 4 h.

Effect of Temperature on Dissolution Kinetics of Si and Al

To assess the impact of temperature on the dissolution of Si and Al, experiments were conducted under fixed conditions (time = 3 h, NaOH concentrations = 5 and 7 M, liquid/solid ratio = 10, and rpm = 400). The temperature ranged from 80 to 140 °C. The outcomes of these experiments, detailing the effect of temperature on Al and Si leaching from FA, are presented in Table S3 and Figure .

Effect of temperature on the leaching efficiency of Si and Al.

At elevated temperatures (80 to 105 °C), Si concentration in the leach liquor increased by 5.3% (5 M) and 6.20% (7 M), while Al decreased by 3.98% (5 M) and 14.96% (7 M). This shift results from the reaction of dissolved Si and Al, leading to the formation of precipitates like sodalite (Na8Al6Si6O24(OH)2·2H2O) (Figure ). Beyond 105 °C, Si and Al concentrations remained stable, likely due to precipitate formation on the FA particle surfaces hindering further OH- penetration. Therefore, 105 °C is identified as the optimal temperature for Si leaching from the FA in 5 M NaOH for 3 h.

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) for Process Optimization

In the optimization study using RSM, the Box–Behnken methodology was applied to design experiments for enhancing the dissolution of Si and Al from PC FA. The investigation focused on three key factors: time, temperature, and NaOH concentration. Based on the observed effects of these factors on Si and Al dissolution, the central point for RSM was set at 3 h leaching, 105 °C temperature, and 5 M NaOH concentration. Subsequently, the ranges for reaction time, temperature, and NaOH concentration were varied between 1 and 5 h, 80–120 °C, and 3–7 M, respectively. The main effects plot for Si and Al dissolution from the PC FA is depicted in Figures S1 and S2, respectively. It reveals a positive correlation between all three factors (reaction time, temperature, and NaOH concentration) and Si dissolution, while displaying a negative impact on Al dissolution. Increased values of reaction time, temperature, and NaOH concentration enhance Si dissolution and reduce Al dissolution. Conversely, the interaction of factors (time*temperature, time*NaOH concentration, and temperature*NaOH concentration) exhibits an adverse effect on the Si dissolution rate and a positive effect on the Al dissolution rate. Similarly, at the highest NaOH concentration and temperature (7 M and 140 °C), the optimum Si dissolution occurs with the lowest reaction time (1 h). Stage 2, lasting from 2 to 6 h (5 M NaOH) or 2 to 9 h (3 M NaOH), exhibits a gradual Si leaching during the initial 2 h. Simultaneously, there is a decrease in the Al concentration in the leach liquor, indicating the concurrent dissolution and precipitation of Si and Al. SEM analysis further validates the precipitation of Si and Al. Stage 3 is characterized by the precipitation of Al and the inward diffusion of NaOH into FA to facilitate Si dissolution. During this phase, the concentration of Al in the leach liquor remains constant, while the Si concentration steadily increases. This increase in Si concentration may result from either Al depletion within the FA particles or the presence of minor amounts of Al reacting with Si to form zeolite crystals. Simultaneously, hydroxyl ions (OH–) diffuse through microcracks in the FA particles, attacking the inner regions and promoting further Si dissolution. However, the efficiency of Si dissolution is relatively modest in this stage, likely because of precipitation processes on the particle surfaces impeding diffusion mechanisms. The SEM image of the activated residue in this stage illustrates comprehensive coverage of the FA particles by the resultant precipitate (Figure S7). Elemental composition analysis via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy EDS reveals that the surface precipitate predominantly consists of Si, Al, Na, and O (Figure S8). Importantly, these elemental components closely align with those found in phillipsite (Na4KAl5Si11O32·10H2O) and zeolite (Na3.6Al3.6Si12.4O32·14H2O), as identified in the previous XRD analysis (Figure ).

Thermodynamic Modeling and Mechanism

Within the alkali activation of the CaO-low system, chemical reactions outlined in eqs – take place. The vitreous phase is primarily characterized by Si–O–Si, Al–O–Al, and Al–O–Si covalent bonds. OH– ions facilitate the dissolution of Si4+ and Al3+ by hydrolyzing their Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al bonds, resulting in the occurrence of tetrahedral SiO4 – and AlO4 – ions in the leaching solution. Over time, the abundance of tetrahedral SiO4 – and AlO4 – ions increases, and they bond together through the sharing of Na+ ions. The Na+ cation acts as a network former, counterbalancing the negative charge of tetrahedral SiO4 – and AlO4 – ions, thereby preventing the reverse dissolution of Si and Al into the solution. As the reaction progresses, tetrahedral SiO4 – and AlO4 – ions become more condensed, leading to crystallization on the surface of the FA particles. As more precipitation occurs, the available surface area for further reaction decreases. Beyond this point, the reaction is governed by a diffusion mechanism, where the reaction takes place within microcracks, dissolving the inner part of the FA particles until all of them are covered by the precipitate.

Although heavy-metal leaching was not the primary focus of this study, it is recognized as an important environmental consideration. Under strongly alkaline conditions (pH > 13), most trace metals are expected to precipitate as hydroxides or become incorporated into secondary aluminosilicate phases, thereby limiting their mobility. Nevertheless, a comprehensive evaluation of heavy-metal behavior remains necessary and will be addressed in future work through targeted leaching tests and environmental assessments.

In addition, while alkali activation is effective for selective Si recovery, NaOH consumption represents a key economic and environmental factor for large-scale applications. In this study, process optimization toward shorter reaction times and moderate alkali concentrations helps mitigate the chemical and energy demand. Furthermore, the implementation of alkali recovery and recycling strategies will be essential to enhancing overall process sustainability and feasibility.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates that alkali activation of coal fly ash provides a controlled and efficient approach to recover high-value silica and alumina, while simultaneously concentrating trace rare earths for subsequent beneficiation. The dissolution of Si was maximized (50.4%) under short-duration, high-temperature, moderate-alkalinity conditions (1 h, 140 °C, 5 M NaOH), whereas Al leaching was constrained by secondary precipitation into zeolitic phases. The mechanistic framework established herelinking dissolution kinetics, precipitation pathways, and diffusion-limited transportoffers predictive insight for process scale-up. Statistical optimization through RSM confirmed that leaching conditions can be tuned to balance selective Si recovery with minimized Al loss, achieving reproducibility and cost efficiency.

Importantly, this work further provides a mechanistic framework for controlling Si and Al behavior during alkali activation, enabling selective recovery and an improved process efficiency. Beyond bulk material recovery, the removal of Si and Al contributes to the enrichment of rare-earth elements in the residue, offering an integrated pathway for critical mineral recovery.

The broader significance lies in enabling coal ash, a persistent global waste stream, to serve as a sustainable feedstock for high-performance materials. Potential applications of recovered Si and Al span Al–Si alloys for transport and aerospace, nanosilica for self-compacting concrete and solar coatings, and alumina for ceramics and environmental adsorbents. Moreover, the removal of bulk Si and Al enhances the concentration of rare-earth elements in the solid residue, providing a dual benefit of critical mineral recovery. These findings support the development of sustainable and circular strategies for coal fly ash valorization and low-carbon materials production.

Future research should focus on pilot-scale demonstrations, integration with rare-earth recovery schemes, and techno-economic assessments to evaluate industrial feasibility, including considerations of alkali consumption, recycling, and environmental risk. Overall, this study advances coal ash valorization within the framework of sustainable materials development and circular economy strategies.
