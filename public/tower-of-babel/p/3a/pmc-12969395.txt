From phase 1 to approval: lymphoma’s long road
Authors: Mark Hertzberg
Published: 2026

The development and regulatory approval of novel therapeutic agents remain formidable challenges in medicine, particularly in oncology and hematologic malignancies, in which high attrition rates and long timelines persist. In this issue of Blood Advances, Luo et al1 provide a comprehensive analysis of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval rates of investigational agents in lymphoma over a 20-year period (2000-2019). The findings offer a valuable lens through which to assess the evolving landscape of lymphoma drug development and the barriers that persist to impede progress.

Luo et al evaluated 651 distinct agents tested in B-cell and T-cell lymphomas, including chronic lymphocytic leukemia across 1032 phase 1 trials, 1027 phase 2 trials, and 140 phase 3 trials. They showed that the overall approval rate of agents was only 6.1%, with an average time to approval of 7.9 years from initiation of phase 1 trials (see figure).1 Of these, 200 agents proceeded from phase 1 to phase 2, and 46 agents proceeded from phase 2 to phase 3 trials, corresponding to relative transition rates of 30.7% and 23.0%, respectively. In addition, the pathway to approval can vary. For example, 22 agents received accelerated approval after phase 2 trials, of which 8 subsequently achieved full (regular) approval after successful phase 3 studies. The authors note that the true rate of approval may have been slightly underestimated, because some trials initiated near the end of the study period may not yet have reached later phases or undergone regulatory evaluation.1

Notwithstanding some encouraging findings, the overall success rate of transition between phases remains low, with less than one-third of agents advancing at each stage. Among therapeutic classes, bispecific antibodies demonstrated the highest transition rate (53.3%), whereas chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies, though highly innovative, showed a more modest 22.5% transition from phase 1 to phase 2.1 Previous studies in the United States of the clinical approval success rate of investigational compounds, as well as the phase transition probabilities, differ significantly by therapeutic class.2 Importantly, when preclinical development is included, the full drug development cycle for lymphoma agents still averages ∼10 years.

Across all 651 distinct agents, 21 broad mechanistic classes were identified.1 The most common approved classes included monoclonal antibodies (n = 6), Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (n = 4), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase inhibitors (n = 4), and CAR T cells (n = 4), with other categories, such as bispecific antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates, represented by slightly fewer agents.1 These data reflect a decade of therapeutic evolution in lymphoma, from chemotherapy-dominated regimens to increasingly targeted, immune-based, and cell-directed therapies.3, 4, 5, 6

The reasons underlying the failure to progress between trial phases remain incompletely understood and still represent a critical area for future investigation. Although Luo et al did not examine these factors directly, the analysis underscores that attrition remains a dominant driver of inefficiency and cost in drug development.1,2 Notably, the authors observed a shortening in median time from phase 1 initiation to FDA approval by ∼2.4 years when comparing 2011-2019 to 2000-2010, suggesting that trial design, regulatory mechanisms, and translational science have incrementally improved over the past decade. This acceleration may reflect earlier identification of nonviable candidates, more flexible licensing pathways, and the expanding use of predictive biomarkers for patient selection.7

Taken together, these findings highlight the persistent unmet need to deepen our understanding of lymphoma biology to guide rational drug development and combination strategies. Reliance on traditional prognostic indices, such as the International Prognostic Index and cell of origin, is no longer sufficient. Instead, future progress will depend on integrating disease biology, such as genetic subclasses (eg, MCD, BN2) or genetic signatures (eg, molecular high grade), as well as markers reflective of disease burden, such as total metabolic tumor volume and circulating tumor DNA, into trial design, enabling a shift from prognostic to a more predictive precision medicine.

The authors suggest that there is a dramatic need for improvement in trial design and in prediction of clinical response through preclinical studies. However, this remains a formidable challenge. For example, the developmental design of T-cell–engaging antibodies illustrates the complexity of balancing efficacy and toxicity. Both the T-cell receptor binding domain and the tumor B-cell antigen target must be finely tuned. Minor modifications in posttranslational processing or binding-site conformation can dramatically alter antigen affinity. In turn, changes in antigen affinity affect clinical outcomes, influencing both efficacy and toxicity profiles, such as cytokine release syndrome, immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome, and chronic immunosuppression and infection.6,7 Robust preclinical assessment of these determinants will be essential to guide safer and more effective translation.

Clinical trials account for nearly two-thirds of all research and development expenditures, representing the largest component of overall drug development costs.8 Failures during these phases substantially inflate total capitalized costs. Therefore, strategies to enhance the probability of successful phase transition, such as optimized preclinical models, adaptive trial designs, and the incorporation of biomarkers as surrogate end points, may yield significant cost savings. Moreover, greater transparency regarding pharmaceutical expenditures, profit margins, and company structure would improve understanding of the economic drivers that influence innovation and regulatory policy.9

Luo et al provide an important and timely overview of the protracted journey from first-in-human testing to regulatory approval in lymphoma drug development. Although modest improvements have been achieved, overall success rates remain low and timelines long. Continued progress will require synergistic efforts across preclinical discovery, robust translational science, and innovative trial design.10 By integrating biological insights, predictive biomarkers, and adaptive methodologies, the field can move closer to realizing the promise of faster, more efficient, and more successful therapeutic development for patients with lymphoma.
