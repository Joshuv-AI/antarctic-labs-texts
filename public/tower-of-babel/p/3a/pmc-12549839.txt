Reimagining patient-reported outcomes in the age of generative AI
Authors: Laurent Boyer, Sara Fernandes, Pascal Auquier, Bruno Falissard, Trishan Panch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenerative artificial intelligence, particularly large language models, offers an opportunity to rethink how patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are assessed and implemented in health systems. Despite decades of psychometric and digital innovation, PROs remain conceptually limited and underused in both clinical practice and AI models. Rooted in top-down, predefined instruments and assumptions of unidimensionality, traditional PROs struggle to capture the fluctuating and multidimensional nature of lived health experiences. In contrast, generative AI supports bottom-up, narrative-based approaches that process language in a flexible and context-aware way. Our viewpoint supports two distinct directions: one that refines current psychometric models through generative artificial intelligence integration, and another that embraces a more disruptive shift toward language-native tools capable of synthesising patient narratives. Realising this potential will require addressing key challenges, including validation, clinical actionability, equity, and trust. Bridging these gaps could make PROs a true lever for more personalised, meaningful, and inclusive care.Subject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly shaping healthcare by enhancing diagnostic, prognostic, therapeutic, and healthcare delivery processes. Its applications range from clinical decision support, patient triage, medical imaging, and risk prediction to administrative automation, workflow optimization, and real-world data integration. This transformation relies on AI’s ability to uncover and learn associations with predictive value from multi-omics data, clinical records, imaging, numerical, and medico-administrative data1. These developments have largely emerged from task-specific or rule-based AI systems designed to process structured data. More recently, a disruptive shift has occurred with the advent of generative AI, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), which have expanded AI’s ability to process and generate unstructured human language at scale.

Yet, one critical dimension remains largely peripheral in this data-driven evolution: Patient-Reported Outcomes (PROs)2. While PROs capture essential aspects of symptoms, function, and quality of life, they remain fragmented, inconsistently collected, and largely absent from AI-driven models. Unlike multi-omics, imaging data, and digital biomarkers (e.g., wearable sensors, remote monitoring), which have benefited from massive investments enabling large-scale collection and structured integration, PROs lack both the infrastructure and the strategic focus needed for widespread adoption, both in clinical routine and large-scale biomedical research. This absence of systematic collection has far-reaching consequences. AI models rely primarily on clinician-validated ground truth rather than PROs, reinforcing a medical paradigm that prioritizes biological markers over patient-reported health status3. At the same time, the lack of large-scale PRO datasets not only limits their integration into AI-driven models but also disproportionately excludes socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. Barriers such as digital exclusion, literacy challenges, and physical impairments further restrict PROs’ accessibility, compounding existing health disparities and introducing an additional layer of algorithmic bias1.

In this Perspective, we examined how generative AI, and specifically LLMs, may enable a fundamental rethinking of how PROs are conceptualized, collected, and operationalized in healthcare. We explored the limitations of current psychometric approaches, the promise and risks of language-native AI tools, and the translational challenges that must be addressed to realize the full potential of generative AI for patient-centered measurement.

The persistent underutilization of PROs despite the removal of key barriers

Despite decades of research and scientific and technological advances, PROs remain underused in both clinical practice and large-scale biomedical research.

From a scientific perspective, the validity of PROs is now well established. Advances in psychometrics, particularly Item Response Theory (IRT)4,5, have strengthened measurement precision, while neuroimaging studies have demonstrated a clear neurophysiological substrate underlying the constructs they assess6–9. Large-scale cohort studies have further shown that PROs can predict clinical outcomes, treatment response, and long-term health trajectories, highlighting their potential to capture aspects of health beyond traditional clinical markers10,11.

From a structural perspective, logistical and feasibility concerns have been significantly addressed. Digital health innovations, including ePROs and Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT), have improved data collection, reducing patient burden while maintaining measurement precision12–14. These tools facilitate seamless integration into digital platforms, making PROs collection more scalable and accessible15,16.

Yet, despite this progress, PROs are still often overlooked, pointing to deeper issues in how they are conceptualized, structured, and interpreted within current measurement frameworks. Addressing these conceptual and methodological limitations requires reimagining PROs along three axes: making them more accurate, through true personalization that better reflects each patient’s unique experience; more meaningful, by enabling qualitative insights that capture the richness of lived health narratives; and more actionable, by producing clinically relevant information that can be seamlessly integrated into care.

Beyond reductionism: the need to rethink PROs assessment

Historically, the measurement of PROs has relied on questionnaires, a gold standard established in 1950s clinical trials to ensure simplicity, objectivity, and standardization17. Despite methodological refinements such as IRT and CAT, which improved measurement precision and flexibility by tailoring questions to individual responses, this approach remains fundamentally unchanged: questionnaires are still the primary tool for capturing PROs (Table 1).

aCronbach42 introduced Classical Test Theory (CTT), which assumes that all test items have the same weight and contribute equally to a total score, without considering differences in item difficulty or their ability to discriminate between individuals. The total score is treated as a direct measure of ability or trait level.

bItem Response Theory (IRT) models (1980s–2000s) based on latent trait theory, the idea that an unobservable trait (e.g., ability, health status) drives an individual’s responses to test questions. Unlike CTT, IRT models account for question difficulty (1PL models) and discrimination (2PL models), meaning that some questions provide more information than others about an individual’s abilities. Scores are estimated using probabilistic models rather than simple sum scores, making measurement more precise and comparable across populations. More advanced models (e.g., 3PL or Multidimensional IRT) have been developed to address item guessing and multiple traits, but they still impose predefined structural assumptions that may not reflect real-world complexity.

Still, these methods are conceptually constrained. IRT is based on the assumption of unidimensionality4,5,18, that complex health experiences can be reduced to a single latent trait, such as pain, fatigue, or anxiety, and captured independently of other interacting factors. While this framework has provided psychometric rigor and comparability, it imposes a rigid structure that often fails to reflect the fluctuating, multidimensional, and interconnected nature of lived health experiences. Advances in multidimensional IRT allow for the simultaneous measurement of multiple latent traits, offering greater flexibility19. Yet even these models struggle to capture the full complexity and dynamic interplay of symptoms, functional status, and well-being. These methods reflect a form of historically necessary reductionism, an effort to make subjectivity measurable in an era that lacked tools for engaging with narrative complexity. Alternative approaches, such as utility theory20 and network models21, have attempted to move beyond these structural assumptions, but they remain marginal in clinical applications.

This conceptual rigidity limits the clinical use of PROs. For patients, filling out a questionnaire to generate a score can feel disconnected from the complexity of their actual experience, forcing them to fit their reality into predefined categories. For clinicians, a single numerical score often offers little actionable insight to support personalized care. Psychiatrist German Berrios notably criticized such quantitative evaluations, questioning how much semantic content and qualitative essence are lost when translating personal experiences into scaled scores22. Reducing rich, nuanced narratives into rigid numerical values weakens their relevance for real-world decision-making.

Generative AI may offer an opportunity to move beyond incremental refinements and fundamentally rethink how PROs are captured, shifting from static questionnaires to adaptive, real-time, and clinically meaningful assessments.

Transforming PROs with generative AI

While AI has been widely applied to enhance diagnostic and prognostic capabilities in healthcare, its potential for PROs assessment remains underexplored. Recent advancements in generative AI introduce three breakthroughs in how PROs could be captured and understood.

First, generative conversational AI enables a shift from static questionnaires to interactive dialog. Traditional PROs rely on fixed, predetermined questions, limiting flexibility and engagement. AI-driven conversational agents allow for real-time, dynamic interactions, adapting questions based on patient responses and capturing more personalized insights. Additionally, generative conversational AI can facilitate PROs completion through voice or video interfaces, improving accessibility for individuals with literacy challenges, language barriers, or motor impairments23,24. By broadening access and engagement, these innovations could help mitigate algorithmic bias over time, ensuring that PROs better represent diverse populations and healthcare needs.

Second, LLMs enhance qualitative analysis, moving beyond scores. Rather than reducing PROs to numerical values, LLMs can analyze free-text responses, extract key health-related themes, and generate clinically meaningful summaries. This technology is already used in healthcare, for instance, to extract information from clinical notes25. These AI-generated insights could help clinicians better integrate PROs into decision-making, offering a more nuanced and personalized understanding of patient experiences.

Third, generative AI may enable a deeper epistemological shift, moving beyond the structural reductionism that has long shaped PROs’ measurement. Unlike psychometric models grounded in predefined latent traits, LLMs process language in a non-linear, multidimensional way, opening new possibilities for capturing the complexity of real-life health experiences26. In doing so, they offer a holistic, bottom-up impression of a patient’s condition, akin to the impression a clinician might intuitively form in practice, but derived directly from the patient’s own words. For instance, rather than scoring depression through a fixed top-down symptom checklist, a language model might synthesize a narrative reflecting how emotional numbness, physical fatigue, feelings of disconnection, and temporal disorientation interact in the person’s daily life27. This could help bridge the gap between standardized measurement and the lived, multidimensional nature of patient experiences.

However, significant challenges remain. Generative AI systems can generate incorrect or nonsensical outputs (“hallucinations”), potentially disrupting dialogs and posing safety concerns28. LLMs lack validation as measurement tools, as they were not designed to provide standardized, psychometrically rigorous assessments29. Beyond these issues, ethical challenges must also be addressed, such as biased or inequitable outputs, context drift, data security concerns, and the risk of undermining patient autonomy. A recent framework, Trustworthy and Responsible Ethical Generative AI in Healthcare, outlines nine guiding principles, including non-maleficence, autonomy, equity, transparency, and security for the ethical use of generative AI in health care30. This framework could serve as a valuable reference for the future development of AI-powered PROs.

From promise to practice: the validation challenge of generative AI in PROs

Realizing the transformative potential of LLMs in PROs will depend on addressing their most pressing limitation: the absence of rigorous validation frameworks tailored to PROs. Traditional PROs are grounded in decades of psychometric theory, offering well-established benchmarks such as construct validity, reliability, and sensitivity to change. In contrast, LLMs generate insights through opaque, probabilistic mechanisms that currently lack comparable scientific standards. Unlike task-specific AI models, which can be evaluated using standardized metrics such as AUC, c-index, or F1-score, LLMs perform a wide range of generative tasks, including summarization, free-text generation, and interactive dialog. This task diversity complicates efforts to design unified evaluation strategies31, particularly for PROs, where models are expected to capture subtle subjective cues through adaptive dialog, interpret and synthesize free-text data, potentially infer structured outcomes, and generate insights that can meaningfully inform clinical decisions.

Addressing this complexity will require the development of new validation methodologies tailored to the properties of LLM-based assessments. Simply applying legacy psychometric techniques may be insufficient.

Emerging proposals suggest a three-tiered framework that could serve as a starting point: general validation (e.g., robustness, consistency, readability), task-specific validation (e.g., summary fidelity, scoring accuracy), and clinical validation (e.g., real-world outcomes or workflow impact)29. This framework may offer a useful starting point for designing validation strategies specific to PROs, which must combine multiple generative tasks with psychometric rigor.

Explainability, defined as the ability to make model reasoning understandable to humans, will play a key role, and recent work has emphasized the need to adapt explainability techniques to the specific challenges of LLM applications in healthcare32.

Hybrid approaches combining LLMs with traditional machine learning techniques may offer promising solutions. Recent studies suggest that these architectures can enhance predictive performance, mitigate clinical risks, and improve the interpretability of outputs, particularly in complex and constraint-driven healthcare tasks33,34. For PROs specifically, where semantic nuance and reliability are both essential, such combinations may help bridge the gap between narrative complexity and structured evaluation.

Crucially, validation must be co-developed with patients themselves, not only to ensure relevance and accuracy, but to avoid repeating the historical pattern of building tools solely based on expert consensus.

A dedicated research agenda is needed, one that includes comparative studies matching LLM-generated insights with standardized PRO scores, and analyses of how well these outputs align with both patient perspectives and expert clinical assessments. Equally important is testing whether LLMs can reliably detect clinically meaningful change over time. Methodologies will also need to assess test–retest reliability, inter-model consistency, and multiple forms of validity, including construct, content, and criterion-related validity.

Finally, proactive and rigorous bias audits must become a non-negotiable part of this validation process. Evaluating potential demographic, socioeconomic, and cultural biases in how LLMs interpret patient experiences is critical to ensuring equity and fairness35.

Without this new generation of validation science, AI-driven PROs risk remaining speculative and promising, but ultimately unfit for integration into safe, actionable, and equitable clinical care.

Refining or redefining PROs assessment?

To fully harness AI’s potential while addressing its limitations, two major research avenues are emerging.

The first is a refinement approach that seeks to modernize current psychometric frameworks by integrating AI tools. In this hybrid model, established methods such as IRT and CAT continue to provide the conceptual foundation and scoring structure36, while AI-driven chatbots enhance engagement through real-time, adaptive interviews37. LLMs could further enrich these interactions by generating qualitative summaries alongside traditional scores, offering a more comprehensive picture of the patient’s condition. While this approach preserves the structure and comparability of current instruments, it does not fully resolve their conceptual limitations but it represents a pragmatic step forward, enhancing flexibility and personalization within an established framework (Fig. 1).

The second avenue is a more disruptive paradigm shift, moving beyond predefined questionnaires and traditional psychometric models altogether. Rather than relying on latent trait theory and scoring systems, this approach uses LLMs to conduct open-ended, real-time conversations with patients. These models can dynamically extract and synthesize relevant clinical information from free-text narratives, capturing the multidimensional, contextual, and evolving nature of health experiences without imposing rigid predefined categories. By embracing the non-linear, high-dimensional properties of LLMs, this approach opens the door to assessing more holistic constructs, such as a person’s overall sense of health or well-being, that have traditionally eluded psychometric formalization. It may even approximate the kind of global clinical impression long used informally by clinicians, but with systematic and scalable support from AI.

From innovation to large-scale implementation: making PROs clinically actionable

Whether through refined hybrid models or a paradigm shift powered by generative AI, rethinking how PROs are captured is only the beginning. To fulfill their potential, PROs must become not only more accurate and more meaningful but also truly actionable in real-world practice. This means ensuring that AI-generated insights can be interpreted, trusted, and seamlessly integrated into clinical workflows. Generative AI may support this transition by producing risk flags or tailored recommendations. However, making these outputs genuinely actionable remains a major hurdle, requiring robust decision-support tools (e.g., AI-assisted summaries, alerts, or recommendations), clinician training, and workflow adaptation to accommodate novel forms of patient-reported information, all of which may involve significant computational resources and staffing investments for scalable deployment.

Achieving this will also depend on broader systemic transformation. Persistent structural barriers, such as workflow fragmentation, lack of data interoperability, misaligned incentives, and clinician burden, must be addressed. These challenges go beyond technical feasibility to involve organizational priorities, resource allocation, and the cultural norms of healthcare systems38,39.

Equally essential is ensuring the social acceptability and trustworthiness of these tools by patients, clinicians, and society at large40. This requires fair, inclusive implementation aligned with patient values. Key to fostering trust are proactive bias mitigation, inclusion of diverse populations in design and validation, and transparency in how AI-generated outputs are produced and used.

Conclusion

Only by addressing the scientific, systemic, and social dimensions of AI-enhanced PROs assessment can we fulfill their original promise: making what matters to patients a true driver of care decisions and system transformation. While PRO LLM-based systems are particularly well-suited for personalized, context-rich interpretation at the individual level, their large-scale deployment could also generate substantial volumes of narrative data, opening new opportunities for population-level insights, public health monitoring, and research. In this way, AI-enhanced PROs may serve as a bridge between personalized, patient-level measurement and broader epidemiological surveillance, complementing existing approaches based on traditional machine learning applied to social media or other digital trace data41.
