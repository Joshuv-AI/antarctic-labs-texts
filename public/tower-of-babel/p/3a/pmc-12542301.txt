Immunoglobulin G Fc engineering for localized therapy: Eliminating neonatal Fc receptor interactions and ensuring purification using protein A
Authors: Akifumi Kato, Asami Toshima, Gennosuke Takekawa, Hikaru Miyagi, Ai Kubota, Sayaka Hori, Yasunori Uemura, Rinpei Niwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) have transformed therapeutic strategies for various diseases. However, administering them can lead to unwanted adverse effects and insufficient delivery to local disease sites. Recent research has shown that substitutions in the immunoglobulin G (IgG) Fc region, designed to eliminate binding to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which accelerates clearance from systemic circulation, may improve the safety of antibody therapies intended for localized use. A major concern with these substitutions is their ability to impair binding to protein A, which is considered the gold standard for large‐scale antibody purification. In this study, we investigated multiple amino acid substitutions at residues I253, H310, H435, and Y436—positions known to affect FcRn or protein A interactions—and found that H435F eliminates FcRn binding while maintaining protein A affinity. Importantly, the H435F substitution resulted in accelerated clearance and a shorter serum half‐life in human FcRn transgenic mice. Moreover, purification using protein A chromatography proved to be effective without significantly compromising yield. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of the H435F substitution to optimize the pharmacokinetics of antibody therapeutics for localized use, reducing systemic side effects. This study paves the way for the use of antibody‐based therapeutics in localized treatments.Keywords: antibody engineering, IgG, localized therapy, neonatal Fc receptor, protein a, shorter serum half‐life

INTRODUCTION

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) are a remarkably evolving class of therapeutics for various diseases. Currently, antibody therapeutics have a higher approval success rate than small‐molecule drugs. Nearly 200 antibody therapies have received marketing approval, and over 1100 are in development by numerous companies (Crescioli et al., 2024). Most of these products are administered in high doses into the systemic circulation, either directly through intravenous injections or indirectly through intramuscular or subcutaneous injections (Prašnikar et al., 2024; Reichert Janice, 2024). Systemic administration of therapeutic antibodies is well suited for widespread ailments. However, administering drugs into the systemic circulation for diseases confined to a small area of a specific peripheral tissue is considered inefficient. This method can lead to undesired systemic adverse effects (Jones & Martino, 2016). Therefore, alternative non‐systemic approaches for the targeted localized use of therapeutic antibodies can potentially overcome the inherent limitations of systemic antibody delivery. Local delivery methods include local injection (Zhao et al., 2022), inhalation (Farinha et al., 2023; Focosi & Maggi, 2023), nasal administration (Fan et al., 2022; Ku et al., 2021), topical (Jacobse et al., 2021), and oral (Abramson et al., 2022) administration. A clear example of the targeted localized use of antibody therapeutics is direct intravitreal injection into the eye to treat ocular diseases. The use of high systemic concentrations of therapeutic antibodies is generally not applicable owing to undesired systemic effects. Instead, applying local low‐dose antibody by local injection at approximately 1/100th of the systemic dose has been established in clinical treatment for neovascular age‐related macular degeneration using vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) neutralizing antibodies, including Bevacizumab (Avastin) and Ranibizumab (Lucentis) (Martin et al., 2011). In these applications, antibodies may leak from the injected site of disease into the systemic circulation and become diluted within the large blood volume; however, the risk for undesired side effects still remains. To address this issue, a recent study has shown that the ratio of antibody concentration in target tissues to systemic circulation can be improved by eliminating FcRn (neonatal Fc receptor for immunoglobulin (Ig)G) binding from the Fc region (Regula et al., 2019).

The long half‐life of IgG is regulated by FcRn‐mediated recycling, which binds strongly at acidic pH and has little to no binding at neutral pH. Cellular uptake occurs through pinocytosis of IgG, followed by FcRn transportation to acidic early endosomes (pH <6.0). This results in the binding of IgG to FcRn and its recycling back to the cell surface. Exocytosis of the IgG‐FcRn complex releases IgG from FcRn due to negligible binding affinity by exposure to the physiological conditions (pH 7.4) of the extracellular space (Pyzik et al., 2023). The residues involved in the IgG‐FcRn interaction have been identified through site‐directed mutagenesis (Kim et al., 1999; Shields et al., 2001), crystallography (Burmeister et al., 1994; Martin et al., 2001; Oganesyan et al., 2014; West Jr & Bjorkman, 2000), and structural modeling (Booth et al., 2018). The CH2 (Ile253 and His310) and CH3 domains (His435) are essential in this interaction. Reports also show that the FcRn‐binding site on IgG overlaps with the binding site of staphylococcal protein A (DeLano et al., 2000). Therefore, mutagenesis, which causes the loss of binding to FcRn, could also influence the binding to protein A (Jendeberg et al., 1997). Protein A chromatography is most commonly used for the initial purification step for IgG‐based protein therapeutics, such as mAbs, bsAbs, and Fc fusion proteins. Approximately 90% of therapeutics that have been approved or are under clinical trials are purified using protein A (Miesegaes et al., 2012). The application of protein A chromatography is rapidly expanding, and its performance is continually improving (Bolton & Mehta, 2016). This makes protein A purification advantageous in reducing the cost of goods (COGs) of IgG‐based therapeutics.

In this study, we investigated substitutions in the Fc region of human IgG that could eliminate binding to human and monkey FcRn while maintaining binding to protein A, using site‐directed mutagenesis, and identified the H435F (His435 to Phe) substitution. This study showed that the H435F substitution did not significantly affect the yield of protein A chromatography. It resulted in accelerated clearance and shortened serum half‐life in human FcRn transgenic mice.

RESULTS

Design of engineered Fc varians that eliminated binding to FcRn while retaining protein A binding

It is well established that FcRn does not bind to the IgG Fc region in the extracellular environment (pH 7.0–7.5); however, it binds to internalized IgG in the early endosome (pH 6.0–6.5). This binding enables the recycling of IgG back into the bloodstream, helping to maintain its concentration in systemic circulation (Roopenian & Akilesh, 2007). The residues that are critical for the binding of the Fc region to FcRn at pH 6.0 were identified as Ile253, His310, His435, and Tyr436, based on observations showing a significant reduction in binding activity when these residues were substituted with alanine (Dall'Acqua et al., 2002; Petkova et al., 2006; Shields et al., 2001). These residues are located near the interaction site of the Fc region with protein A (Figure 1a,b). Thus, substitutions at these positions potentially influence the interaction between Fc and protein A. It has been shown that the I253A substitution in the Fc CH2 region leads to a loss of binding to protein A (Petkova et al., 2006).

We aimed to identify substitutions that would simultaneously achieve a loss of binding to FcRn while maintaining binding to protein A to optimize antibody therapeutics for targeted localized use. This would enable the production using current common protein‐based processes. The IgG1 variants L234A/L235A/G237A (LALAGA) were selected, which completely abolished immune effector functions, including the model Fc region in bispecific antibody 1 (bsAb1) and the symmetric bispecific antibody composed of tetravalent Fabs termed REGULGENT™ (N‐term) (Saito et al., 2024). All four arms of bsAb1 were designed to recognize two human protein antigens that do not exist in mice. This characteristic allowed us to observe the pure antigen‐independent pharmacokinetics of bsAb1, eliminating the effects of antigen‐dependent absorption. A series of Fc variants with single amino acid substitutions (Table 1) was designed to explore the effects of changes in the molecular size and electric charge of the side residues at amino acid positions 253, 310, 435, and 436.

Productivity of bsAb1 Fc variants and quality analysis

BsAb1 Fc variants designed above were expressed transiently using the Expi293™ expression system and purified using CaptureSelect™ IgG‐CH1, which recognized the heavy chain CH1 domain to avoid the impact of the substitutions introduced in the Fc region. The expression titers of the BsAb1 Fc variants were analyzed based on the purification yield. BsAb1, whose Fc region is an IgG1 LALAGA wild‐type (WT), served as the parental control. The purity and the corresponding molecular weights after purification were confirmed through capillary electrophoresis (Figure S1). On day 3, the expression titer of the BsAb1 parental control was 5.2 mg/L, and the titers of the Fc variants were similar to those of the parental control (Table 1). Size exclusion chromatography revealed that the monomer contents of BsAb1 Fc variants were over 98% for all variants, similar to that of the parental control, 99.3% (Table 1 and Figure S2). These results suggest that the amino acid substitutions introduced into the Fc variants had no significant effect on the productivity and properties evaluated based on monomer content.

Identification of Fc substitutions that abolished FcRn binding at pH 6.0

A Biacore™ was used to conduct the Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) analysis to measure the interaction between the Fc region and FcRn. His‐tagged recombinant human or cynomolgus monkey FcRn was immobilized on a sensor chip through an anti‐His‐tag antibody, and each bsAb1 Fc variant was run in the solution. As shown in Figure 2a, bsAb1 IgG1 LALAGA WT bound to recombinant FcRn with very rapid association and dissociation rates, precluding accurate kinetic analysis. The binding of bsAb1 Fc WT to recombinant human FcRn reached a steady state at the tested concentrations during the 1‐min sample injection, although it was unstable. Therefore, the KD values were calculated from the equilibrium analysis using the steady‐state affinity binding model provided by the Bender (Biacore T200 Evaluation Software, Figure 3a).

We confirmed that bsAb1 WT IgG1 LALAGA showed binding affinity to human FcRn at pH 6.0 in the sub‐μM range, consistent with previous reports on normal human IgG1 (Zheng et al., 2012), and that the I253A variant showed no binding affinity to FcRn (Figure 2b). Similarly, variants with amino acid substitutions introduced at Ile253, His310, and His435 showed a complete loss of FcRn binding affinity (Figures 2, 3, and S3). Nevertheless, the variant with an amino acid substitution at Tyr436 retained a weak binding affinity (Figure S3). Similar results were observed using the cynomolgus monkey FcRn (Figure S4).

H435F substitution retained binding affinity to protein A

Subsequently, the binding affinities of the BsAb1 Fc variants to protein A were analyzed. Protein A derived from Staphylococcus aureus was directly immobilized on a sensor chip using amine coupling chemistry. Conversely, the WT IgG1 LALAGA showed a binding affinity of approximately 10 nM for protein A (Figure 4a and Table 1). The insertion of amino acid substitutions at Ile253, His310, and His435, which were shown to abolish binding affinity to FcRn, also resulted in the loss of binding affinity for protein A (Figures 4b and S5). The substitution at Y436 resulted in a partial loss of FcRn binding; nonetheless, binding to protein A was retained. However, the IgG1 LALAGA H435F variant retained binding affinity for protein A comparable to that of the WT (Figure 4c and Table 1).

H435F substitution was applicable to the purification using protein A

Protein A elution is normally conducted at pH 3.0 to elute bound antibodies from protein A resins. Generally, the mechanism of pH sensitivity is conserved in histidine residues, which function as pH changes under slightly acidic conditions (pH ~6.5) near the pKa of the histidine side chain. The dissociation under acidic conditions is driven by electrostatic repulsion in the presence of a histidine residue at the binding interface. We conducted protein A purification of bsAb1 Fc variants bearing the H435F substitution and analyzed the yield to determine the effect of the H435F substitution.

Each 1 mg of purified bsAb1 Fc variants H435F and WT was diluted in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and subsequently loaded onto a 200 μL resin volume of MabSelect Sure™ column by gravity flow, followed by washing and elution using 20 mM citrate (pH 3.0). No critical difference was observed in the yield of purified bsAbs (Figure 4d), showing that the H435F substitution applies to protein A purification, which is the most widely used process for antibody therapeutics (Miesegaes et al., 2012).

H435F variants showed a short half‐life in vivo

Then, we examined whether the FcRn‐binding characteristics of the bsAb1 Fc variants observed in vitro could lead to differences in systemic clearance in vivo. For in vivo PK studies, human FcRn transgenic mice (Tg32) with appropriate gene expression under the control of their endogenous promoter sequence were selected to preserve the species‐dependent interaction between hIgG1 and FcRn. Previous reports have shown the fidelity of this preclinical model as a translational surrogate for evaluating PK of humanized IgG‐based biologics, including mAbs and bsAbs (Avery et al., 2016; Betts et al., 2018; Proetzel & Roopenian, 2014; Tam et al., 2013).

Reportedly, the physicochemical characteristics of mAbs, particularly the isoelectric point and hydrophobicity, influence their pharmacokinetics (Igawa et al., 2010; Sharma et al., 2014). Thus, we confirmed that the H435F substitution did not significantly affect these factors using isoelectric focusing (IEF) and hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) (Figure 5a). PK studies were conducted on the Tg32 strain, and the elimination kinetics of the bsAb1 Fc variants were evaluated. The elimination curves of the bsAb1 H435F variants were distinct from those of the WT (Figure 5b). As predicted by its incapability to bind hFcRn in vitro, substitution of His435 to phenylalanine in the H435F variant led to enhanced clearance and shortened systemic half‐life compared with WT (t 1/2=1.11 ± 0.14 days vs. 5.28 ± 0.87 days, p < 0.01) (Figure 5b and Table 2). These results align with those of a previous report on pharmacokinetic analysis of the I253A substitution in Tg32 mice (Petkova et al., 2006).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified an IgG Fc variant (H435F) that abolishes FcRn binding while preserving Protein A binding through targeted mutagenesis. H435F maintained Protein A capture yields and exhibited accelerated clearance in human FcRn transgenic mice, resulting in a shortened serum half‐life.

His435 is a critical residue for the pH‐dependent binding of IgG to FcRn, along with His310. At an acidic pH (pH 6.0–6.5, corresponding to the environment in early endosomes), the His435 residue (imidazole side chain pKa is between 6 and 7) is protonated and thus positively charged, inducing an electrostatic interaction with the negatively charged residue in FcRn. A structural model of human WT Fc in complex with human FcRn showed that His435 is in close contact with the FcRn residue Asp130 (Booth et al., 2018). The variant His435 was substituted with alanine in this study, which reportedly resulted in no detectable binding to human or mouse FcRn at pH 6.0 in vitro, thereby inducing lysosomal accumulation without extracellular salvage (Firan et al., 2001; Ober et al., 2004). The side chains of histidine and phenylalanine showed structural similarity because they contained aromatic rings (Table 1 and Figure S6). However, their protonation behaviors were significantly different. The side chain of phenylalanine did not protonate even under acidic conditions. Therefore, the substitution of His435 with phenylalanine ablated the binding of Fc to FcRn at pH 6.0 despite the structural similarity of the side chain. This indicates that the interaction is highly charge‐driven, which aligns with previous studies (Figure 6a).

Conversely, crystallographic analysis of human IgG Fc region in complex with protein A revealed that His435 was located directly opposite His137 of protein A (Figure 6b) (Deisenhofer, 1981; Loew et al., 2012). Both residues were uncharged at a neutral pH; thus, unlike the interaction between Fc His435 and FcRn Asp130, the interaction was not electrostatic. Therefore, the structural change of the side chain from the imidazole of His435 to the benzyl of Phe would not affect the binding affinity of Fc to protein A. Interestingly, other substitutions tested here, such as His435 to alanine, glutamic, or threonine, showed significant reductions in affinity for protein A. Those results indicate that those substitutions introduce new steric hindrance or reduce binding affinity between the Fc and protein A. Previous research using molecular simulations has revealed that several residues, including I253 and H310, are important “hot‐spots” for the binding of IgG‐protein A complex (Huang et al., 2011); however, H435 is not included in this list, suggesting that the substitution from His to benzyl of Phe was tolerated.

The interaction between IgG and protein A depends on pH, with the dissociation of the IgG‐protein A complex occurring at low pH to facilitate elution. Molecular simulation studies have reported that this dissociation is primarily driven by the strong electrostatic repulsion between positively charged residues, such as His137, Arg146, and Lys154 of protein A, and positively charged residues, such as His310, Lys317, His433, and His435 of IgG at pH 3.0 (Huang et al., 2011, 2012). Therefore, substituting His435 with phenylalanine may impair electrostatic repulsion, compromise the dissociation of the IgG‐protein A complex, and reduce the elution yield. However, we analyzed the yield of bsAb1 bearing the H435F substitution and found no significant reduction in the amount of bsAb1 eluted. This result showed that the repulsion between positively charged His310, Lys317, and His433 of IgG‐Fc and positively charged His137, Arg146, and Lys154 of protein A is sufficient for dissociating the IgG‐protein A complex at pH 3.0 (Figure 6b).

We demonstrated that the systemic half‐life of the H435F variant is considerably shorter than that of the parent Fc in the human FcRn transgenic mice (Tg32) after intravenous administration; the t1/2 was 1.11 days. This finding aligns with those of previous reports that used Tg32 mice to evaluate I253A and I253A/H310A/H435A (IHH or AAA variants) with no detectable binding to FcRn, suggesting that the H435F substitution ablated FcRn binding affinity in vitro and in vivo (Bennett et al., 2012; Bryniarski et al., 2024; Petkova et al., 2006).

In addition to its IgG recycling function, FcRn is essential in maintaining the tissue distribution of IgG through transcytosis, which involves moving IgG across polarized endothelial cells and numerous types of epithelial cells. FcRn mediates IgG trafficking in gastrointestinal, retinal, urogenital, and respiratory epithelia (Qi & Cao, 2021). For instance, reports show that FcRn transports intravitreally administered full‐length IgGs across the blood–retinal barrier into the systemic circulation in mice (Ng, 2016). Furthermore, reports show that FcRn transports IgGs and Fc‐bearing molecules from the alveolar epithelium to systemic blood circulation. In fact, erythropoietin (EPO)‐fused IgG1 Fc was delivered to the systemic circulation through the human lung using the FcRn‐mediated transport pathway in a phase 1 clinical study (Dumont et al., 2005). Interestingly, a pre‐clinical study in non‐human primates showed that EPO‐Fc IHH variants that ablated FcRn binding significantly decreased transfer to systemic circulation (Bitonti et al., 2004), suggesting that the H435F substitution may similarly enhance tissue localization by reducing transport from the administered local tissue to systemic circulation through the loss of FcRn binding.

While this study demonstrates that the H435F substitution in the Fc region eliminates FcRn binding and retains protein A binding, thereby enabling accelerated systemic clearance and suitability for targeted local use of antibodies, an important limitation is that tissue distribution and retention after local administration were not directly evaluated. Although enhanced systemic clearance was confirmed in vivo, it remains unclear whether the H435F substitution improves antibody localization or prolongs residence time at the target tissue. These factors are critical for ensuring therapeutic efficacy and minimizing systemic exposure. Future studies should address tissue‐level pharmacokinetics following local administration to fully elucidate the benefits of H435F‐engineered antibodies in localized therapeutic contexts.

CONCLUSION

This study showed, for the first time, that the H435F substitution in the Fc region of IgG1 LALAGA maintained its binding affinity to protein A while eliminating its binding affinity to human and monkey FcRn. This H435F substitution accelerated clearance from systemic blood circulation, making it suitable for targeted local use of antibodies. Furthermore, this enabled the use of protein A chromatography, which is the gold standard for large‐scale antibody production.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Modeling of protein structure

The MOE software (MOLSIS, Inc.) was used to conduct the protein structure analysis. Protein structural information was dependent on the complex structure registered in the Protein Data Bank, the engineered human antibody Fc region with human FcRn (PDB: 4N0U) (Oganesyan et al., 2014), and the human antibody Fc region with protein A (PDB: 1FC2) (Deisenhofer, 1981).

BsAb1 Fc variants and production

The expression vectors of bsAb1 Fc variants encoding heavy chains with human IgG1 Fc variants incorporating the LALAGA (L234A/L235A/G237A) substitution and light chains were synthesized using FASMAC (Atsugi, Kanagawa, Japan), cloned into a mammalian expression vector, and expressed recombinantly using the Expi293™ Expression System Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mammalian expression vectors encoding the heavy and light chains were mixed and added in a 2:1 ratio to the cells, and transfection was conducted following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were cultured in Expi293™ expression medium in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C, 125 rpm for 3 days. The antibody‐containing cell supernatant was recovered and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Thereafter, it was sterile‐filtered using a 0.22 μM filter (Corning). The antibodies were subsequently purified from the cell supernatant using a CaptureSelect IgG‐CH1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The supernatant was loaded onto a CaptureSelect IgG‐CH1 column and washed with five column volumes of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; Gibco). It was then eluted using elution buffer (100 mM citrate, pH 3.5) and immediately neutralized with neutralization buffer (1 M sodium phosphate, pH 7.0). Afterward, buffer exchange with PBS was conducted rapidly using an NAP column (Cytiva).

Analysis of purity and physicochemical properties of bsAb1 Fc variants

The purity of the bsAb1 Fc variants was analyzed using a LabChip GX Touch capillary electrophoresis device (PerkinElmer). For quality control of antibody size and monomer purity, analytical SEC was conducted using an ACQUITY™ Premier protein column (1.7 μm particle size; 4.6 mm × 300 mm, Waters) and Shimadzu Nexera X2 UHPLC System (Shimadzu), with 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), 500 mM NaCl, and 5% EtOH at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Overall, 10 μg of antibody was injected onto the column, and the elution time was analyzed using data analysis software (Shimadzu).

Isoelectric focusing (IEF) was conducted to determine the isoelectric points (pI) of the bsAb1 Fc variants using iCE3 (Protein Simple) following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. From the obtained electrophoresis patterns, the isoelectric points of bsAb1 Fc variants were analyzed using iCE3 CFR software version 4.3.1.1352 (Protein Simple). The hydrophobicity of bsAb1 Fc variants was analyzed using MAbPac™ HIC‐20 column (4.6 mm × 100 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and LC‐10Avp System (Shimadzu) with 2 M ammonium sulfate, 20 mM Tris (pH 7.0) and 20 mM Tris (pH 7.0) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Overall, 20 μg of antibody was injected onto the column, and the elution time was analyzed using data analysis software (Shimadzu).

Binding affinity analysis of Fc variants to FcRn

The affinity of bsAb1 Fc variants to FcRn at pH 6.0 was determined using SPR. Measurements were conducted using Biacore™ T100 and T200 instruments (Cytiva). Anti‐His Antibody (Bovine Serum Albumin‐Free, QIAGEN) diluted to 20 μg/mL in Acetate 4.5 (Cytiva) was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva) using the amine coupling chemical N‐hydroxysuccinimide/1‐ethyl‐3‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide, following the manufacturer's instructions. Human or Cynomolgus monkey His‐tagged FcRn proteins were prepared as previously described (Booth et al., 2018) and diluted to 10 μg/mL in HEPES‐buffered saline with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and surfactant P20 (HBS‐EP+) buffer (pH 7.4, Cytiva). They were then injected at a flow rate of 10 μL/min for 120 s. Subsequently, the above bsAb1 Fc variants diluted in HBS‐EP+ buffer at pH 6.0 were injected at a flow rate of 30 μL/min. The binding reaction with FcRn was monitored for 60 s, followed by a dissociation reaction for 150 s. The equilibrium value analysis method was used for this analysis. The obtained sensorgrams were analyzed using Bia Evaluation Software (Cytiva), and the binding affinity (KD) of each Fc variant was calculated.

Binding affinity analysis of Fc variants to protein A

The affinity of the bsAb1 Fc variants for protein A was determined using Biacore T100 and T200 as previously described. Protein A derived from Staphylococcus aureus (Nacalai Tesque), diluted to 1.0 μg/mL in Acetate 4.5 (Cytiva), was immobilized to the CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva) at 75 RU using the Amine Coupling Kit (Cytiva). Then, the bsAb1 Fc variants were diluted in HBS‐EP+ buffer (pH 7.4, Cytiva) using a five‐step serial dilution starting from 100 nM with a dilution factor of 4 and were injected at a flow rate of 30 μL/min. The binding to protein A was measured for 120 s, followed by a dissociation reaction for 120 s.

Measurements were conducted using the single‐cycle kinetics method. BIA Evaluation Software (Cytiva) was used to analyze the obtained sensorgrams, and the kinetic constants for each antibody were calculated using the bivalent analyte model. Association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and binding affinity (KD) were determined.

Purification yield analysis using protein A resin

Each 1 mg of purified bsAb1 Fc variant H435F and WT, as previously described, was diluted in citrate buffer (10 mM citrate and 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.0, prepared in‐house) and subsequently loaded onto a 200 μL volume of MabSelect SureTM resin (Cytiva) packed in an open column. The flow‐through obtained was collected and reapplied to the same column. The columns were washed with 10 column volumes (cv) of Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline (D‐PBS, pH 7.5), and antibodies were eluted using 4 cv of elution buffer (20 mM citrate and 50 mM NaCl, pH 3.0, prepared in‐house). The eluted antibodies were then immediately applied to an NAP column (Cytiva) to exchange the buffer with citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The amount of eluted antibodies was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm using a NanoDrop OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific). The elution steps were repeated three times. The yield analysis was conducted in three independent experiments using three columns for each sample.

Animal experiments

All animal studies were conducted following the Standards for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments at Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd. In vivo elimination of Fc variants in mice

The elimination of bsAb1 Fc variants in vivo was tested using human FcRnTg mice. B6.Cg‐Fcgrt tm1Dcr Tg(FCGRT)32Dcr/DcrJ (Tg32) has the mouse Fcgrt knock‐out substitution and a transgene expressing the human FCGRT gene under the control of its native promoter (The Jackson Laboratory) (Petkova et al., 2006). Male mice, 11 weeks old, with an average body weight of approximately 27 g were used, and the groups were balanced for body weight on the day of the experiment. The transgenic mice (n = 4 for each group) were injected with a single intravenous dose of 0.5 mg/kg of the bsAb1 Fc variants diluted to a final volume of 5 mL/kg in sterile 10 mM L‐sodium glutamate, 262 mM d‐sorbitol, and 0.05 mg/mL polysorbate 80 (pH 5.5). Blood samples were obtained from the tail vein at several time points using non‐heparinized capillary pipettes. Then, the samples were centrifuged (3000 rpm, 15 min, 4°C) to obtain serum.

Determination of Fc variant concentration in serum samples using an electro‐chemiluminescent immunoassay method

The concentrations of bsAb1 Fc variants in mouse serum samples were determined using an electrochemiluminescence assay. The mouse anti‐human IgG monoclonal antibody (clone D4, prepared in‐house) was labeled with biotin using a Labelling Kit‐SH (Dojindo) following the manufacturer's instructions for the capture antibody. Polyclonal anti‐human IgG (prepared in‐house) was labeled with ruthenium using MSD Sulfo‐TAG NHS Ester (Meso Scale Discovery) following the manufacturer's instructions for detecting antibodies. The wells of a microtiter plate, MSD Gold 96‐well Streptavidin Plate (Meso Scale Discovery), were blocked with 1% (w/v) casein‐PBS and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The wells were washed three times with 0.05 w/v% Tween 20‐PBS. Subsequently, the capture antibody, biotinylated anti‐human IgG diluted to 1 μg/mL in 1 w/v% casein‐PBS, was added to the wells and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Then, 50 μL of the prepared serum sample was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The plate was washed three times, followed by the addition of detection antibody, Ruthenylated pAb diluted to 0.25 μg/mL in 1 w/v% casein‐PBS. After incubation at room temperature for 1 h, the plates were washed three times. Thereafter, MSD GOLD Read Buffer A (Meso Scale Discovery) was added to each well, and electrochemiluminescent signals were detected using a MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 (Meso Scale Discovery).

The lower limit of quantification was set at 150 ng/mL. SOFTmax® PRO (Molecular Devices Japan) was used to create a standard curve capable of generating a four‐parameter logistic curve fit.

Calculation of PK parameters

Each animal's PK parameters were calculated using non‐compartmental analysis (IV bolus model) using Phoenix WinNonlin (Certara). The slope of the elimination phase (λz) was calculated using the concentrations of the last three sampling points (72 to 168 h) after the administration. The following PK parameters were reported: t 1/2: Elimination half‐life, AUC0‐7d: Area under the concentration‐time curve from 0 to 7 d after the dosing, AUC0‐∞: Area under the concentration‐time curve from zero to infinity, the predicted value, CL: Total clearance, the predicted value, V ss: Volume of distribution at steady state, the predicted value.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). Results are reported as mean ± SD. Statistical assessment was carried out by Welch's t‐test. The p value <0.05 was considered significant and indicated as ns = non‐significant, *p <0.01, **p <0.001, ***p <0.0001.
