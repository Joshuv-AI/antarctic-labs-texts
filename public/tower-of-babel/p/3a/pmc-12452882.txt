Single-cell transcriptome combined with genetic tracing reveals a roadmap of fibrosis formation during proliferative vitreoretinopathy
Authors: Mengyu Liao, Zhisheng Ye, Yumeng Zhao, Jiaying Nan, Yuming Liu, Xueming Yao, Tianjing Yang, Ju Guo, Xin Jin, Hong Zhang, Caiyun You, Heping Xu, Jinguo Yu, Yupeng Chen, Xiaohong Wang, Hua Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceOcular fibrosis, a severe consequence of excessive retinal wound healing, can lead to vision loss following retinal injury. Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR), a common form of ocular fibrosis, is a major cause of blindness. In this study, we mapped the landscape of fibrosis formation during PVR using single-cell RNA sequencing. Coupled with genetic tracing, we showed retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) as the major source of PVR-associated fibroblasts (PAFs). Notably, we identified Thbs1 as a key hub gene driving RPE cell fate transitions during PVR, which was attenuated with the application of therapeutic antibodies targeting Thbs1. These findings unveiled the cellular origin of PAFs and hold significant promise for treating retinal fibrosis–related diseases.

Ocular trauma poses a significant public health concern with global implications, with approximately 203,000 cases of open globe injury (OGI) annually reported (1). With advancements in vitreoretinal surgical techniques, even severe cases with no light perception have been gradually treated (2). Nevertheless, 36.7 to 57.8% of OGI eyes remain at risk of postoperative failure, primarily due to the development of proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR), which is triggered by excessive wound healing, and characterized by the formation of epi/subretinal proliferative fibrosis membranes, secondary retinal contraction, and redetachment, resulting in poor visual outcomes even permanent blindness (3, 4). The development of PVR was regarded as a complex process involving multiple factors and multicellular events, including postinjury immunocyte infiltration, fibroblast accumulation, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling (5). The treatment for PVR entails surgical removal of the formed membranes, yet no proven pharmacologic approaches are available to halt the progression of intraocular fibrosis (6). Hence, identifying the source of PVR-associated fibroblasts (PAFs) and characterizing their transcriptional dynamics during fate transitions may offer new insights into PVR pathogenesis and uncover potential targets for prevention and treatment of ocular trauma.

In the past, it was believed that outside fibroblasts (from Tenon’s layer or dermal tissue) might directly initiate PVR formation due to the penetrating trauma (7). Meanwhile, intraocular cells may undergo cell fate transitions and gain fibroblast markers, as surgically excised PVR membranes from patients have been found to contain retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, macrophages, glial cells, and myofibroblasts, among others (8, 9). Recent studies indicated that the major contributors to PVR membranes were derived from either RPE (62%) or Müller cells (43.6%), while others reported 71.9% of αSMA+ cells expressing CD45, suggesting that they are derived from myeloid cells (10, 11). Controversy persists due to the limitations of nonspecific labeling of cell lineage. Thus, alternative genetic strategies are needed to specifically target these distinct cell populations. Moreover, the precise mechanisms underlying how these cells participate in the disease progression of PVR are still not fully elucidated.

Cell fate pertains to the forthcoming identity of a cell and its offspring. Reconstructing the lineage relationships and cell fate mapping contributes to a better understanding of disease mechanisms (12). Single-cell sequencing technologies provide the potential to study transcriptional information and map cell fates at the individual cell level, while genetic lineage tracing is considered the gold standard for deciphering cell fate transitions (13, 14). The combined approach of scRNA-seq and genetic lineage tracing allows for the induction of cell labeling at any time point in specific tissues and has been used to investigate the origins of fibroblasts in heart, lung, and other fibrosis diseases (15, 16). Here, we used scRNA-seq combined with genetic lineage tracing to dissect the multiple cell fate transitions during PVR pathogenesis.

Results

Single-Cell Transcriptional Profiling in a Mouse PVR Model.

To investigate the cellular population involved in PVR-associated ocular fibrosis, we performed a droplet-based single-cell transcriptomic analysis in a PVR mouse model that we established before (17). At PVR Day 7, the subretinal αSMA+ lesion could be clearly observed, suggesting successful model establishment (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). We then isolated the cells from the eyecup tissues using trypsin from 10 eyes at PVR Day 7 and subjected to 10× Genomics for single-cell sequencing (Fig. 1A). Upon data processing, a preliminary filtering and cleaning process yielded a dataset of 13,887 single cells, with an average sequencing depth of 2,733 genes per cell. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization unveiled an 8-cluster representation of the cells in PVR (Fig. 1B). Based on the known markers (18, 19), we assigned the cell identities to seven clusters, corresponding to RPE cells (Rpe65 and Lrat), immune cells (Ptprc and Lyz2), photoreceptors (Rho+ rods and SagHi cones), Müller glia cells (MGCs, Slc1a3 and Aqp4), PAFs (Col1a1 and Col1a2), interneurons (Pcp2+ bipolar cells and Nrxn3+ amacrine cells), and endothelial cells (ECs, Cdh5 and Vwf) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). RPE cells constituted the largest fraction, representing 37.9% of the total cell population, while the immune cells comprised approximately 19%, forming the second largest cell cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

Given that PVR is characterized by cellular proliferation, we performed EdU incorporation assays. EdU+ cells were uniformly distributed throughout the PVR lesion but were rarely found in the neuroretina and choroid (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). To further characterize the proliferating cell types, we performed G2M-phase gene set scoring using the single-cell data (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). The analysis revealed that cells with high G2M scores and elevated expression of proliferation markers such as Mki67, Top2a, and Ccnb1 were primarily localized to the PAF and immune cell clusters, indicating active proliferation within these populations.

RPE Cells as the Major Source of PAFs.

Given that RPE cells represent the largest fraction, we first analyzed their characteristics. To assess the heterogeneity of RPE cells after PVR, we extracted a total of 5,312 RPE cells from our single-cell data (Fig. 1C), and integrated them with an external dataset (20) (GSE203138) as the normal control. After integrated analysis and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identification, we identified five RPE subclusters (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A and Table S1 and Fig. 1C). DEGs were identified using a threshold of log2 fold change > 0.5 and adjusted P < 0.01. The top DEGs for each subcluster in Fig. 1C, ranked by log2 fold change, are presented in Fig. 1D. RPE-1 was classified as quiescent RPE (qRPE), expressing well-known RPE markers such as Rpe65, Lrat, and Ttr, which are known to be essential for the physiological function of RPE cells (21). RPE-2, which showed detectable expression of Rho, Sag, Gngt1, was termed as RhoHi RPE (rRPE). RPE-3 was defined as active RPE (aRPE), as they express genes such as A2m, an activation marker in liver fibrosis research (22), and Osmr, a marker of RPE postretinal detached damage (23). RPE-4 were identified as transitioned RPE (tRPE), as they show increased expression of Acta2 (encoding αSMA), Tagln (encoding Transgelin), and Vim (encoding Vimentin). Finally, RPE-5 express both RPE-specific markers and inflammation-related genes, such as Lyz2, C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, and Ctss. We defined RPE-5 as macrophage-like RPE (mRPE) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A and Fig. 1D).

The normal control group constitutes 99.1% qRPE. However, the tRPE and mRPE subclusters are PVR-specific, accounting for 12.1% and 6.1%, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Notably, as RPE cells acquire mesenchymal or macrophage-like characteristics, their expression of core RPE genes, such as Rpe65, diminishes significantly (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).

To further investigate the contribution of RPE cells to fibrosis in PVR, we generated genetic tools for tracing RPE cells by crossing an inducible RPE-specific Cre line, BEST1-CreERT2 mice (24), with Rosa26-LSL-tdTomato (R26-tdT) reporter mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). Without tamoxifen treatment, we did not detect any tdT+ cells in RPE, suggesting no leakiness of BEST1-CreERT2, while tamoxifen administration labels approximately 90% of RPE cells marked by tight junction marker ZO-1 in RPE flat mounts (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). The immunofluorescence staining in sagittal sections also showed a colocalization of RPE marker RPE65 with tdT+ cells, indicative specific labeling across the retina (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Next, we injected tamoxifen, induced PVR one week later, and analyzed at Day 0, 7, and 14. The tdT+ cells were predominantly localized within αSMA+ PVR lesion (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). The EdU incorporation assay further revealed that EdU+ tdT+ cells accounted for 41.1% and 34.6% of all tdT+ cells at Day 7 and Day 14, respectively, suggesting RPE cells constitute a substantial fraction of the proliferating population during PVR progression (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G).

To further validate these findings, we crossed BEST1-CreERT2 mice with another reporter line, Rosa26-CAG-LSL-ZsGreen (R26-ZsG), to label the entire RPE cell (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). We induced PVR and observed that αSMA+ cells constituted 61.0% (Day 7) and 80.4% (Day 14) of all ZsG+ cells post-PVR, suggesting that a large proportion of RPE cells became fibroblastic (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Next, we quantified the percentage of ZsG+ cells within PVR lesions and their percentage within αSMA+ areas. We found that RPE cells account for 61.1% (Day 7) and 68.1% (Day 14) of the total lesion area, and 79.3% (Day 7) and 78.3% (Day 14) of all αSMA+ cells, suggesting RPE cells are the major source of PAFs (Fig. 1 E–H).

Previous literature has reported that during the process of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), RPE cells progressively lose their epithelial markers while acquiring mesenchymal markers (such as αSMA) (25). Using genetic lineage tracing, we specifically tracked the lineage conversion of RPE-derived cells by permanently labeling RPE cells and their descendants. Immunostaining at PVR Day 7 showed three types of ZsG+ RPE-derived cells, illustrating their fate transitions (Fig. 1I). Layer 1 (L1) is a monolayer epithelial structure with high RPE65 expression, representing relatively quiescent RPE cells. This progresses to an intermediate state, Layer 2 (L2), characterized by multilayered cells with αSMA expression and reduced RPE65 levels. Finally, the cells reach Layer 3 (L3), a highly fibrotic state with complete loss of RPE markers (Fig. 1I).

To further investigate the temporal dynamics of this transition, we performed immunofluorescence staining in BEST1-CreERT2; R26-ZsG mice at 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 d post-PVR (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). At steady state (Day 0), RPE cells form a monolayer (L1) characterized by RPE65Hi ZsG+ αSMA− expression. By Day 1, the retina begins to show subtle wrinkling, and RPE cells adopt a multilayered architecture while retaining high RPE65 and initiating low αSMA expression. On Day 3, αSMA expression increases, particularly in the multilayered regions, forming L2 (RPE65+ ZsG+ αSMA+). By Day 5, L2 thickens, suggesting expansion of these transitional cells. Concurrently, RPE65 expression continues to decline, and a subset of cells loses RPE markers entirely, defining L3. By Day 7, L3 expands further, with a greater number of RPE65− ZsG+ αSMA+ cells observed, contributing to fibrotic membrane formation (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D).

Myeloid Cells as the Major Immune Cell Type that Infiltrates the PVR Lesion.

Besides RPE cells, immune cells represent the second largest population (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). To investigate the role of immune cells in PVR, we extracted 2,532 immune cells from our single-cell data and identified five subclusters (Fig. 2A). Clusters 1 to 4 expressed myeloid cell markers, Itgam (encoding CD11b). Based on the expression of macrophage-associated genes Adgre1, Cd68, and Lgals3, as well as microglia marker genes Tmem119 and P2ry12, clusters 1 to 3 were classified as macrophage/microglia (MP/MG). Cluster 4 was identified as neutrophils, as they express S100a8 and S100a9, while cluster 5 was classified as T/B cells (Cd3d and Trbc2 for T cells, Cd79a and Cd79b for B cells) (Fig. 2B). Among the three MP/MG clusters, MP/MG-1 was characterized by high Lyz2 expression, MP/MG-2 by the expression of MHC-II class genes (Cd74, H2-Ab1, and H2-Aa), and MP/MG-3 was identified as proliferative based on the expression of Mki67, Top2a, and Pcna (Fig. 2B). The three MP/MG subgroups comprise over 90% of all PVR-associated immune cells, with Lyz2Hi MP/MG accounting for the largest proportion (75.2%) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). UMAP plots showed that Aif1 (IBA-1), Adgre1 (F4/80), and Cx3cr1, marker genes expressed by both macrophages and active MGs, constituted a significant fraction of immune cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). For MG markers, 338 cells (13.3%) expressed Tmem119, and 213 cells (8.4%) expressed P2ry12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B).

To investigate the contribution of MP/MG in PVR, we generated Lyz2-CreERT2; R26-tdT mice. After Tamoxifen administration, flow cytometry analysis revealed that the labeling efficiency of tdT+ cells driven by Lyz2 promoter in CD11b+ myeloid cells was approximately 73% in peripheral blood (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). Under normal conditions, no IBA-1+ tdT+ cells were observed in retinal flat-mounts or eyeball sections (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). However, after the induction of PVR, a substantial number of tdT+ cells could be observed in the lesion, accounting for 34.4% and 36.5% of the entire PVR lesion area (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F and Fig. 2 C and D). When we induced PVR using a Lyz2-CreERT2; R26-ZsG mouse line, similar results were observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Furthermore, we observed a colocalization of IBA-1 and CD68 with a portion of ZsG+ cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). This finding suggests that the macrophages derived from peripheral circulation is an important source of immune cells that infiltrate into the subretinal space during PVR. Furthermore, EdU incorporation assay revealed that EdU+ tdT+ cells account for 13.1% (Day 7) and 10.2% (Day 14) of the EdU+ cells, suggesting that MP/MGs are also an important part of proliferative cells during PVR (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). However, as active microglia also express Lyz2 (26), we cannot rule out the possibility that some tdT+ cells in the PVR lesion are activated microglia. Indeed, when we utilized Tmem119-EGFP transgenic mice (27), which labels microglia in the retina, we observed that a portion of EGFP+ cells colocalized with lysozyme at PVR Day 7 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D and E). Our results further corroborated the participation of MP/MG in PVR.

A previous study has reported that macrophages undergo macrophage-myofibroblast transition (MMT) in retinal fibrosis diseases (28). We then explored whether MP/MGs could also undergo this transition. Indeed, a small proportion of tdT+ cells in PVR lesions exhibited elongated morphology and expressed αSMA, indicating myofibroblast transition (Fig. 2C). Yet, this proportion only accounts for 0.9 and 0.8% of αSMA+ cells (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that myeloid cells are the major immune cell type that infiltrates the PVR lesion, with a very small portion for source of PAFs (Fig. 2F).

MGCs Mainly Contribute to Gliosis in PVR But Are Nondominant Source of PAFs.

MGCs are another key cell population implicated in the formation of PVR membranes. While fibroblasts transitioned from RPE cells are considered as a key source of PAFs, MGCs have also been implicated in PVR by proliferating and secreting ECM components, contributing to membrane formation and fibrotic contraction (10, 29). From our single-cell data, 1,029 MGCs were extracted and compared to 390 MGCs from normal mouse (GSE271262) (30) (Fig. 3A). The results revealed that MGCs in PVR downregulated Müller cell key identity-controlling genes, such as Nfia and Nfib, and other markers of resting glia cells, such as Slc1a3, Aqp4, Rlbp1, Glul, Espn, and Sox9. In contrast, genes related to cell cycle (Ccnd1 and Ccnd3), glia cell activation (Gfap and Manf), and inflammation (Serpina3n and Stat3) (18, 31) were increased in PVR MGCs (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further showed PVR MGCs were enriched in metabolic processes, whereas normal control MGCs were associated with physiological functions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B).

To understand the contribution of MGC-derived cells in PVR pathogenesis, we generated a GLAST-CreERT; R26-tdT mouse line for tracing MGCs. Without tamoxifen, we did not detect any tdT+ cells in the retina. After tamoxifen administration, almost all the tdT+ cells colocalized with SOX9 (MGC marker) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). After PVR induction, we found that tdT+ cells exhibited abnormal morphology and migrated toward subretinal space. Interestingly, only a minor portion of tdT+ cells were present at the fibrotic lesion (less than 2%), and nearly no tdT+ cells colocalized with αSMA+ cells (less than 0.2%), suggesting MGCs do not contribute to the αSMA+ fibroblast population (Fig. 3 C–F). Similar results were observed using a GLAST-CreERT; R26-ZsG mouse line (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C).

EdU incorporation assay showed EdU+ cells were mainly present in the αSMA+ fibrotic lesion, while no tdT+ EdU+ were detected, suggesting MGC is active but not proliferative in PVR (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Furthermore, we also performed immunostaining in sections and found that GS+ glia cells were limited to the neuroretina, adjacent to but spatially distinct from αSMA+ fibrotic lesion in PVR mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). Costaining for F4/80 and αSMA in RPE flat-mounts from GLAST-CreERT; R26-ZsG mice revealed that the αSMA+ fibrotic region was surrounded by a ZsG+ glial area, with F4/80+ MP/MGs infiltrating both regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S7F). These findings suggest that MGCs contribute to PVR through forming a glial scar which surrounds the fibrotic lesion (SI Appendix, Fig. S7G).

Transcriptional Dynamics Define RPE Stages During Fibrotic Progression.

The above results suggest that among the three cell types, RPE are the major source of PAFs. During the transition, they might eventually lose the RPE marker such as RPE65. To capture the full dynamics of this process, we extracted all EMT-related transcriptomic data, including PAFs that no longer express RPE65, for pseudotime analysis (Fig. 4A). Among the total of 6,249 EMT cells, the high expression of Rpe65 was mainly overlaid on RPE, while Mki67 and Col1a1 increased in PAFs. Both RPE cells and PAFs expressed Acta2, indicating their transition state (Fig. 4B).

Using multiple computational approaches, including Monocle3 for pseudotime inference, which visualized the transition from RPE to PAF by color (Fig. 4C), CytoTRACE (32) for simulating the dedifferentiation gradient during RPE remodeling, we confirmed that the pseudotime trajectory follows a path from less differentiated to more differentiated status, consistent with the transient and reversible nature of EMT (Fig. 4D). Moreover, we utilized the Slingshot analysis to further reconstruct the trajectory, among which the lineage covering over 90% of the cells help to define the four stages during EMT. Notably, rRPE cells were excluded from this lineage. The remaining RPE cells were categorized into four distinct subclusters, representing EMT Stages 1 to 4 (Fig. 4E).

Cells undergoing EMT through various stages along the EMT spectrum include epithelial, partial EMT, and mesenchymal cell states. To further characterize the gene expression profiles of these stages, we conducted temporal gene module enrichment and differential gene expression analyses. As shown in Fig. 4F, four distinct clusters were identified along with a single pseudotime trajectory. Cluster-1 (226 genes) included known RPE marker genes such as Rpe65 and Lrat, which were highly expressed in Stage 1 and declined in later stages. GO analysis revealed enrichment in RPE cell polarity-associated terms, as well as visual system-related terms (Fig. 4F). Cluster-2 (72 genes) demonstrated high consistency with Stage 2, with genes related to substance metabolism such as Atp1a1 and Atp1b1 (Fig. 4F), and pigment synthesis regulators like Tyr and Dct (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A), showing a transient upregulation followed by rapid decline, suggesting that RPE cells at this stage undergo pigment regulation disruption and metabolism changes. Cluster-3, which includes 346 genes related to proliferation (such as Mki67 and Top2a), was highly expressed in Stage 3. The expression of these genes peaked in the mid-to-late stages of RPE remodeling, followed by a slight decline. Cluster-4 (682 genes) primarily consisted of ECM-associated genes, such as Col1a1 and Col1a2 (Fig. 4F), displaying a peak expression at the endpoint of RPE remodeling. Moreover, contractility-associated genes such as Acta2 and Tagln were widespread high expressed in Stages 2 to 4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). Additionally, the gene set scores on biological processes including visual perception, ATP metabolism, proliferation, and EMT showed consistent trends with Clusters 1 to 4, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C).

To determine whether this transition pattern is conserved in human PVR, we analyzed published scRNA-seq data from membranes of PVR patients (33). Pseudotime analysis revealed a trajectory originating from RPE cells, progressing through EMT/FB/RPE-1 and EMT/FB/RPE-2, and culminating in the EMT/FB state (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). Along this trajectory, expression of RPE markers such as RPE65 and BEST1 gradually declined, while contractile genes ACTA2 and TAGLN remained highly expressed in both EMT/FB/RPE-1 and -2 subpopulations. EMT/FB also shows the highest expression of ECM-related genes such as COL1A1 and COL1A2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8E). SI Appendix, Fig. S8 F and G displays that dynamic expressions of key marker genes during RPE-to-EMT are conserved between the human dataset and our mouse model (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 F and G).

To further elucidate the underlying gene regulation in RPE transcriptional reprogramming, we utilized the high-dimensions weighted gene coexpression network analysis (hdWGCNA), a comprehensive framework for constructing and analyzing coexpression networks in single-cell data, in combination with pseudotime analysis (34). By hierarchical clustering, nine consensus modules were identified, and visualized as the coexpression UMAP (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A), and gene sets of module 2 (M2), M9, M6, M5 were significantly expressed in Stages 1 to 4, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). GO term analysis showed M2 was linked to homeostatic RPE functions, while M5, M6, and M9 were enriched for ECM-related processes, protein synthesis, mitosis, and cell cycle (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D). For therapeutic intervention, we analyzed the hub genes in each module. The module eigengenes (MEs) for M9 exhibited a transition status with ECM deposition signature, suggesting it as a potential candidate for therapeutic intervention, where we identified genes including Thbs1, Cd9, and Igfbp2 as hub genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E).

Genetic Lineage Tracing Reveals the Heterogeneity of RPE Cells Post-PVR.

We next explored the potential role of fibroblast-related genes in RPE cell fate transitions. PDGFRα and PDGFRβ are expressed on fibroblasts in most adult organs under homeostasis and play a critical role in fibroblast differentiation during organ fibrosis (35). Interestingly, single-cell RNA seq analysis revealed that a small subset of RPE cells (3.45%) express Pdgfrb, whereas Pdgfra+ RPE cells were rare (0.03%) (Fig. 5A). For further validation, we generated Pdgfra-CreERT; R26-tdT and Pdgfrb-CreERT2; R26-tdT mouse lines. Following tamoxifen treatment, we did not observe tdT+ signals in RPE flat-mounts or eyeball sections from Pdgfra-CreERT mice (Fig. 5B). However, in Pdgfrb-CreERT2 reporter mice, a subset of ZO-1+ RPE cells expressed tdT, displaying the typical hexagonal structure of healthy RPE cells (Fig. 5C). Staining in sagittal sections further confirmed coexpression of RPE65 and tdT in the RPE layer (Fig. 5C). Post-PVR, the subset of Pdgfrb+ cells increased to approximately 22.32% (Fig. 5D).

When a single RPE cell is damaged, adjacent RPE cells expand in size and migrate toward the lesion site, resulting in altered RPE morphology characterized by enlarged and deformed cell shapes (36). At PVR Day 3, we observed tdT+ cells exhibiting pronounced irregular morphology (Fig. 5E). By analyzing the shape and size using Cellpose (37), we found tdT+ cells displayed greater variability in shape and were larger in both area and perimeter post-PVR (Fig. 5F).

Besides fibroblast-like RPE, the single-cell data analysis also identified a subset of mRPE post-PVR (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), which is characterized by the upregulation of macrophage- and phagocytes-associated markers and down-regulation of RPE signature genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). When analyzing the integrated (containing RPE cells from control and PVR, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) RPE subclusters, we observed a portion of RPE cells expressed Lyz2 post-PVR (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B), while only a few Lyz2+ cells (nine in total) were detected in normal RPE (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). To confirm this, we used Lyz2-CreERT2; R26-tdT mice. In control mice, tdT+ cells were barely found; however, post-PVR, many of the tdT+ cells are pigmented (Fig. 5G), suggesting this subpopulation are Lyz2+ RPE cells. GSEA further showed that gene sets related to inflammatory response signatures were upregulated, while eye morphogenesis and vision function signatures were downregulated (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D and Fig. 5H). Additionally, myeloid cell markers, including Itgam, Cx3cr1, H2-K1, and H2-D1, were also highly expressed in mRPE (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E).

To further characterize this population, we evaluated the functional gene set scores for mRPE, non-mRPE, and MP/MG clusters using a previously described method (38). We analyzed the gene set scores related to macrophage/phagocyte signatures and found mRPE with functional scores between non-mRPE and MP/MG (SI Appendix, Fig. S10F). When compared with the other RPE subtypes, mRPE exhibited higher scores of these genes (Fig. 5I). These results revealed mRPE not only expressed macrophage-associated genes but also acquired macrophage-like functions. Designating mRPE as the sender cell, the communication networks showed that mRPE had the strongest interactions with PAF-1, followed by PAF-2 and tRPE (SI Appendix, Fig. S10G and Fig. 5J), suggesting mRPE may influence proliferation and EMT process. The ligand–receptor analysis further highlighted Thbs, Tgfb, and Pdgf signaling as key mediators of communication among these cell populations (Fig. 5K).

Thbs1 as a Therapeutic Target for Ocular Fibrosis in PVR.

To further explore the signaling pathways driving intercellular communication, we assessed the strength of outgoing and incoming signaling interactions among different RPE subtypes (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, Thbs (encoding thrombospondin, TSP) emerged as a prominent signaling molecule, particularly in RPE that were undergoing transition (aRPE and tRPE). We also assessed the signaling interactions among RPE-EMT-FB cell populations from human PVR, which consistently indicated that THBS is a prominent signaling molecule, particularly in RPE undergoing transition (EMT/FB/RPE-2 and 1) (Fig. 6B). Specifically, tRPE from mouse PVR serves as the strongest source of both sender and receiver, while the strongest THBS signals originate from EMT/FB/RPE-2 interact with EMT/FB/RPE-1 in human PVR (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–D). The THBS family includes THBS1-5 (39). Here, Thbs1/THBS1 showed the highest contribution, mediating key ligand–receptor interactions in both mouse and human PVR (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 E and F). Thbs1 is also the highest expressed in EMT Stage 2 in the mouse and EMT/FB/RPE-2 in the human (Fig. 6C). In addition, hdWGCNA also identified Thbs1 as a hub gene in the coexpression networks of M9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E). Communication networks in mRPE further highlighted strong interactions involving Thbs1, particularly between mRPE and both tRPE and PAFs (Fig. 5K). These findings suggest that Thbs1 might serve as a potential therapeutic target for PVR.

Supporting this, the THBS1/Thbs1 expression was significantly increased in both human PVR membranes (GSE179603) (11) and mouse RPE post-PVR (GSE244812) (17) (Fig. 6D). Immunostaining confirmed that TSP-1 was markedly elevated in RPE cells from PVR patients and mice (Fig. 6 E and F).

Based on these data, we aim to see whether TSP-1 serves as a potential therapeutic target for PVR. For this, we utilized TSP-1 neutralizing antibody (aTSP-1, Clone A6.1). For safety evaluation, fundus and OCT imaging revealed that both the retinal structure and thickness remained normal following aTSP-1 administration (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B). Electroretinogram analysis showed no significant difference in a-, b-, or c-waves, suggesting no changes in visual function (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 C–E). Considering that TSP-1 is involved in inflammation under certain conditions like age-related macular degeneration (AMD) models (40), we qualified the number of IBA-1+ cells with RPE flat-mounts and found no difference in myeloid cell accumulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S12F). ZO-1 staining further demonstrated a healthy RPE structure and morphology (SI Appendix, Fig. S12G).

We then injected the aTSP-1 in PVR mice to evaluate the therapeutic effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A). Fundoscopy and OCT imaging results showed that aTSP-1 mitigated retinal detachment, contraction, and fibrosis formation (SI Appendix, Fig. S13B). Histological assessments demonstrated that aTSP-1 treatment reduced the PVR score from 7 (6 to 9) to 5 (3 to 7), indicating alleviated disease severity (Fig. 6G). EMT-related genes, including Acta2, Fn1, and Vim, were also reduced following aTSP-1 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S13C). Also, aTSP-1 reduced αSMA+ areas (Fig. 6H) as well as αSMA protein levels, highlighting the inhibition of intraocular fibrosis (Fig. 6I). To assess whether aTSP-1 affects immune cell infiltration and inflammation, we performed flow cytometry for macrophages/microglia, neutrophils, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, and NK cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S13D). At PVR Day 7, aTSP-1 treatment significantly reduced the infiltration of macrophages/microglia, CD4+ T cells, and neutrophils. While decreases were also observed in CD8+ T cells, B cells, and NK cells, these changes were not statistically significant (SI Appendix, Fig. S13E). Expressions of Tnf, Il1b, and Mcp1 were also reduced, indicating that intraocular inflammation was mitigated (SI Appendix, Fig. S13F).

Given the protracted and unpredictable disease course of PVR, we further tested the therapeutic efficacy by injecting the antibody on PVR Day 7 and assessing the outcome on Day 14 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A). Fundus and OCT imaging showed a marked reduction in PVR membrane formation following aTSP-1 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S14B). Histological analysis revealed a decrease in the PVR score from 9 (7 to 11) to 7 (5 to 9) (Fig. 6J). Fibrosis and intraocular inflammation were also significantly reduced (Fig. 6 K and L and SI Appendix, Fig. S14 C and D), suggesting targeting TSP-1 attenuated PVR progression also in therapeutic settings.

To more comprehensively assess the therapeutic efficacy of aTSP-1, we employed an additional PVR model induced by intravitreal injection of RPE cells (41). The development of posterior vitreous detachment, as well as the presence of vitreous and preretinal cells/membranes, was confirmed by fundus photography and OCT (SI Appendix, Fig. S15A). Immunostainings revealed that TSP-1 was markedly upregulated within the fibronectin-positive preretinal membranes in this model (SI Appendix, Fig. S15B). Following model establishment, aTSP-1 was administered to assess its therapeutic effects. Fundus and OCT imaging demonstrated that aTSP-1 significantly attenuated PVR progression, as evidenced by reduced retinal folds and fewer preretinal cells/membranes (SI Appendix, Fig. S15C). Histological analysis further confirmed a reduction in the PVR score (SI Appendix, Fig. S15D). RT-qPCR analysis and immunostainings showed a significant reduction in fibrosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 E–I). The expressions of Tnf, Il1b, and Mcp1 were also reduced following aTSP-1 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S15J). Collectively, these findings underscore the potential therapeutic value of targeting TSP-1 in PVR development and progression.

Discussion

The development of PVR, characterized by the formation of epi/subretinal proliferative fibrosis membranes, is a primary cause of severe vision impairment or even permanent blindness. Yet, a critical unresolved issue is why certain eyes can achieve anatomic success and visual recovery, whereas others fail and develop PVR, which culminates in long-term poor visual outcomes (3). Recent studies suggest that RPE, glia cells, and macrophages may serve as the primary sources of PAFs postinjury (10, 11). However, the specific roles of these cells in PVR pathogenesis and the dynamics of their transcriptional expression during cell fate transitions remain unclear. This study utilized scRNA-seq combined with genetic lineage tracing to investigate cell type contributions and fate transitions in PVR.

Using scRNA-seq combined with lineage tracing, we found that RPE cells constitute the largest fraction of PVR lesions and serve as the primary source of PAFs. Recent research has demonstrated the high plasticity of RPE cells under homeostatic conditions, such as their morphological heterogeneity in different areas and molecular heterogeneity at various developmental stages (42). Additionally, the diverse heterogeneity may influence disease phenotypes, though the impact is not uniform across all RPE-related diseases. For instance, AMD primarily affects RPE in the macular foveal area, whereas certain degenerative eye diseases affect RPE at the periphery (43). Notably, our study unveiled multiple layers of heterogeneity in RPE cells resulting from pathological changes during PVR. This includes RPE cells with high metabolic activity and matrix-producing capacity at different stages of fibrosis, as well as those exhibiting proliferative and inflammatory responses.

A particularly noteworthy finding is the identification of mRPE post-PVR. A previous study reported low levels of Lyz2 expression in RPE cells under normal conditions (44). Consistently, our scRNA-seq analysis identified a small population of Lyz2+ RPE cells in homeostatic conditions. However, after PVR, the proportion of these Lyz2+ RPE cells significantly increased, indicating that they can acquire macrophage-like phenotypes and functions. This aligns with previous reports providing histological evidence that RPE cells can express macrophage surface markers, such as CD68, under certain conditions (45).

PDGFRα and PDGFRβ play crucial roles in the development of organ fibrosis (35, 46). Our analysis shows that a small fraction of normal RPE cells exhibit baseline expression of Pdgfrb, but not Pdgfra. Zhang et al. reported that Pdgfrb expression was detected in RPE cells, which may receive signals from ligands such as PDGFD to induce a fibroblast-like transition in RPE cells (47). However, the precise expression pattern of Pdgfrb in RPE had not been previously elucidated. Our data demonstrated a heterogeneous distribution of Pdgfrb expression in RPE cells and further confirmed that Pdgfrb+ RPE represents a subpopulation with a mosaic distribution in the healthy state. Interestingly, we found that these cells are more prone to undergo morphological changes post-PVR.

Based on scRNA-seq analysis, we observed that immune cells, including macrophages/microglia, T/B lymphocytes, and neutrophils, constitute a significant portion of the PVR cell population, with macrophages/microglia being the most abundant cell type. Although it is challenging to distinguish the precise contributions of macrophages or microglia to PVR using the Lyz2-CreERT2 reporter mice—resulting in a limitation of this study—single-cell analysis of marker genes suggests that macrophages may be more prevalent than microglia. This finding aligns with previous studies, which propose that macrophages play a major role, particularly in the inflammatory stage of traumatic PVR, while tissue-resident microglia are considered more important in PVR subsequent to rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (48, 49). However, these studies are also based on cell type markers, such as CD68 and IBA-1. Therefore, more precise lineage tracing approaches, such as employing a microglia-specific CreER mouse line, would be valuable for addressing this gap in future research.

MGCs, the predominant glia cells in the retina, are crucial for the homeostatic and metabolic support of retinal neurons (50). Glial cells show high plasticity after spinal cord injury, forming scar borders that sequester inflammatory and fibrotic cells from surrounding healthy tissue (51, 52). In PVR, we observed a similar phenotype: MGCs exhibited hypertrophy and morphological rearrangements (i.e., reactive MGCs) typically confined to areas directly adjacent to the fibrotic regions. MGCs near the lesion changed their orientation to form an overlapping barrier (wall-like changes), in contrast to MGCs located farther from the lesion. Although we did not further investigate MGC function, it is plausible that reactive MGCs help demarcate fibrotic zones from neuron-rich retinal areas.

Multiple intercellular communication analysis identified Thbs1 as the key hub-gene involved in the RPE cell fate transition during PVR. Using samples from PVR animal models and patients, we confirmed the aberrant upregulation of the THBS1 post-PVR. TSP-1 is a prototypic matricellular protein with diverse and significant roles in tissue remodeling and fibrosis (53).

Here, we demonstrated that intraocular administration of antibodies targeting TSP-1 effectively attenuated PVR progression in both preventive and therapeutic settings using two experimental models. While surgery remains the standard treatment for PVR, integrating intraocular drug interventions—such as TSP-1 inhibitors—during surgical procedures offers a promising approach for prevention.

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive analysis of cell-type-specific contributions to PVR, highlighting the roles of RPE cells, macrophages, and MGCs. We identified Thbs1 as a key hub gene in RPE cell fate transition and demonstrated the therapeutic potential of targeting TSP-1 to mitigate disease progression. These findings enhance our understanding of PVR pathogenesis and suggest broad avenues for therapeutic intervention.
