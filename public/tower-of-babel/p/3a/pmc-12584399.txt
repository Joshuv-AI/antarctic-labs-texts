Post-spinal surgery infections: a two-year follow-up study on risks, resistance, and recovery
Authors: Aymery Stheme De Jubecourt, Piseth Seng, David Delarbre, Romain Ambrosino, Benjamin Blondel, Kaissar Farah, Andreas Stein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infections following spinal surgery (SSI-SS) are a major concern, leading to increased morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, and higher healthcare costs. This study aimed to analyze the epidemiology, microbiological characteristics, and treatment outcomes of SSI-SS at La Timone Hospital, Marseille, from 2016 to 2022.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with microbiologically confirmed SSI-SS. Demographic data, bacterial pathogens, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and treatment outcomes were analyzed over a two-year follow-up period.ResultsAmong 6,975 spinal surgery patients, 227 (3.3%) developed SSI-SS. The most commonly isolated pathogens were Staphylococcus aureus (40%)—8% of which were methicillin-resistant (MRSA)—and Enterobacteriaceae (35%), with 23% being multidrug-resistant. Surgical revision was required in 98% of cases, with debridement and osteosynthesis retention as the preferred approach. Immunosuppressive condition (HR 2.58 [1.3, 4.8], p = 0.005) and tumor resection (HR 2.4 [1.3–4.5], p = 0.007) were independently associated with poor outcomes. The use of rifampicin (HR 0.3 [0.1–0.7], p = 0.008) or clindamycin (HR 0.2 [0–0.9], p = 0.009) in antimicrobial targeted therapy was significantly linked to improved recovery rates. Despite cefazolin prophylaxis, 3.3% of patients still developed infections, raising concerns about its effectiveness.ConclusionOur findings highlight the high burden of antibiotic-resistant infections in SSI-SS and suggest that rifampicin or clindamycin-based regimens may improve patient outcomes. The efficacy of standard prophylaxis with cefazolin warrants further investigation, and alternative preventive strategies should be considered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11855-w.

Introduction

Surgical site infection is a significant complication following surgery, leading to increased healthcare costs and morbidity [1, 2]. Previous studies have primarily identified Staphylococcus aureus, S. epidermidis, and, to a lesser extent, Enterobacteriaceae as causative agents of SSI after Spinal Surgery (SSI-SS) [3, 4]. Additionally, Cutibacterium acnes has emerged as an increasingly recognized pathogen in SSI after spinal surgery [3].

The French Society of Anesthesia and Intensive Care recommends cefazolin as the first-line prophylactic antibiotic in spinal surgery [5], targeting methicillin-sensitive S. aureus and non-multi-resistant Enterobacteriaceae. Despite stringent infection control measures and prophylactic antibiotic administration, the prevalence of SSI-SS remains between 0.5% and 20% [3, 6–10].

Traditionally, SSI-SS are managed with revision surgery and antibiotic therapy [11]. The presence of osteosynthesis material has been associated with an increased infection risk [4], yet the necessity of its removal during revision surgery remains debated [12, 13], contingent on patient profiles and the time elapsed between the initial procedure and infection diagnosis.

This retrospective cohort study aims to characterize the patient population affected by SSI-SS in our center, the local bacterial flora, and evaluate patient outcomes based on demographics, bacterial etiology, antibiotic treatment, and surgical implant management.

Materials and methods

Study design

We performed a monocentric retrospective study with a two-year follow-up at inter-regional referral center for the treatment of bone and joint infection in the south of France. This study was approved by the AP-HM Ethics and Scientific Committee (PADS N° CSE24-4), registered on 30/05/2024.

From the register of patients who underwent initial spinal surgery without evidence of primary infection (excluding cases of vertebral osteomyelitis) at La Timone Hospital in Marseille between January 1, 2016, and January 31, 2022, we selected cases of Surgical Site Infection after Spinal Surgery (SSI-SS).

Definitions and inclusion criteria

SSI-SS was diagnosed based on medical history and clinical evidence of infection, supported by radiological findings and confirmed by biological and microbiological documentation, with a positive culture or molecular identification from surgical biopsies and/or vertebral punctures. In the cases that precise onset of symptoms was not mentioned, the first day of hospitalization for revision surgery was considered the onset date.

Patients diagnosed outside the study period, duplicates, minors, and those infected at index surgery and patients without positive bacterial cultures or PCR findings were excluded.

SSI-SS were classified based on their time of onset: (1) Acute infection was defined as an infection diagnosed within one month of the initial spinal surgery, and (2) Chronic infection was defined as an infection diagnosed more than one month after the initial spinal surgery.

Patients were considered immunocompromised if they had cancer, were receiving chemotherapy or immunosuppressive therapy, had long-term corticosteroid use, or had a diagnosed immune deficiency.

A complete 2-year follow-up was defined by any sign of activity on the medical record more than two years after diagnosis, without diagnosis of death or relapse. Patients who did not complete 2 years of follow-up were considered lost to follow-up.

Microbiological identification

Surgical samples obtained from all patients i.e. bone biopsies or tissue samples around the vertebral bone were grow in Eppendorf (Hamburg, Germany) tubes and inoculated on five percent (5%) sheep-blood, chocolate, Mueller–Hinton, trypticase soy and MacConkey agar plates (BioMérieux, France) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere and in an anaerobic atmosphere for 10 to 14 days. Bacteria were identified on the basis of pure bacterial cultures obtained from isolated colonies. Conventional phenotypic identification methods such as Gram staining (Aerospray Wiescor; Elitech), catalase and oxidase tests, automated phenotypic identification systems such as the Vitek 2 system (BioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France), MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and molecular methods were used for identification. The antimicrobial susceptibilities of pathogen isolates were determined and interpreted in accordance with the recommendations of the French Microbiology Society and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (CA-SFM/EUCAST).

Data collection

Data collected included patient demographics, medical and surgical history, details of the spinal surgery procedure, microbiological findings, and post-operative antibiotic therapy.

All our cases were treated according to a standard protocol which included surgical treatment, followed by post-operative empiric antimicrobial therapy combining a molecule from the beta-lactam family, such as tazocillin/tazobactam, with vancomycin or linezolid.

Surgical treatment modalities included: (1) revision surgery consisted a debridement, antibiotic, and osteosynthesis material retention; (2) osteosynthesis material removal or replacement. This surgery was followed by 6 weeks to 12 weeks of appropriate antibiotic therapy.

The primary outcome was assessed using a composite criterion two years post-treatment, incorporating radiological, biological, and clinical evidence of infection resolution. Outcomes were classified as follows:

Favorable outcome: Resolution of surgical site infection (SSI-SS) without relapse during the two-year follow-up.Unfavorable outcome: Recurrence of spinal infection within two years after the initial surgery or death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population. Continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. We analyzed clinical outcomes and prognostic factors such as comorbidities, location of infected prosthesis, clinical signs, C-reactive protein level, polymicrobial infection and antimicrobial resistance, medical and surgical treatment strategies using univariate Cox regression analysis. For this analysis, either documented persistent infection or death defined an event. Multivariate Cox regression analysis incorporated relevant variables with p < 0.02 before (Full model) and after (adjusted model) an AIC-based selection step. All statistical analyses were computed using R version 4.4.3 and R + + version 1.7.05 software’s. A p ≤ 0.05 significance level was retained for all the analyses.

All patients with all inclusion criteria and no exclusion criteria were considered in the demographic and bacteriologic descriptions. Only patients with a complete two-years follow-up were considered in the statistical analysis of the outcome.

To avoid introducing bias, the following variables were not taken into account for the multivariate analysis: Chemotherapy, immunosuppressive condition and tumor removal, as a strong association existed between them and cancer.

Results

Following the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1), we included 227 well-documented cases of post-spinal surgery infection in our study (Flowchart, Fig. 1). Of these, 119 (52%) were men, with a median age of 64 years. Demographic, clinical, biological and management data of our cohort

The most frequently performed procedures of initial surgery were osteosynthesis (n = 180, 79%), spinal laminectomy (n = 72, 32%), osteosynthesis removal (n = 38, 17%) and tumor resection (n = 36, 16%). Nearly half of the cohort (n = 111, 49%). had infections at multiple surgical sites. The most affected locations were lumbar spine (n = 153, 67%) and dorsal spine (n = 124, 55%). A posterior approach was used in 91% of cases (n = 206), anterior in 4% (n = 9) of cases and combined anterior–posterior in the remaining 5% (n = 12). (Table 1). Blood loss data was available for 20 patients, with a median loss of 900 ml (SD = 506). Surgery duration was available for 54 patients, with a median duration of 2.5 h (range: 1 to 8 h).

Infection onset and clinical presentation

The median interval between initial surgery and SSI-SS onset was 17 days (IQR: 10–30) In this study, 172 of SSI-SS (76%) occurred within 30 days of the initial surgery. Statistical analysis did not identify any significant association between the timing of SSI-SS (acute vs. chronic) and patient demographics, surgical site location, or the use of corticosteroids or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) before or after surgery. Similarly, neither the occurrence of bacteremia nor the type of microbial species were associated with the delay in SSI-SS onset. However, patients with acute SSI-SS were more likely to present with elevated inflammatory markers, with a CRP level > 40 mg/L significantly more frequent in this group (p = 0.001). Importantly, the time interval between initial surgery and SSI-SS onset did not significantly influence patient outcomes.

Microbiological findings and antimicrobial resistance

One hundred sixty-three cases (72%) had monomicrobial culture or PCR positive on per operative sample. 64 cases (29%) were polymicrobial, among which 13 patients had two different Enterobacteriaceae identified. In the same way, C. acnes was identified twelve times within a polymicrobial culture. These 64 cases of polymicrobial culture explain why the total of species identified is superior to the cases number in this study.

The most frequently identified pathogens were S. aureus, Enterobacteriaceae, S. epidermidis, and C. acnes. S. aureus (Fig. 2) was the most isolated pathogen, detected in 90 cultures (40%). Enterobacteriaceae were identified in 80 cases (35%). A total of 68 patients (30%) had at least one enterobacterium documented from their biopsy. S. epidermidis and C. acnes were found in 34 (15%) and 31 (14%) cases, respectively. Additionally, 70 samples isolated other pathogens, including Enterococcus spp. (n = 19, 8%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n = 7, 3%). Regarding resistance phenotypes, 8% of S. aureus isolates were methicillin-resistant (MRSA), 23% of Enterobacteriaceae were classified as multi-resistant (MRB) or highly resistant bacteria (HRB), and 48% of S. epidermidis isolates exhibited methicillin resistance.

Surgical management and antibiotic therapy

The median interval between SSI-SS diagnosis and revision surgery was two days (IQR 1–6). Among 227 patients included, 221 (97%) underwent revision surgery. 204 cases (90%) were treated with revision surgery consisting of debridement, osteosynthesis material retention, replacement of moving parts, and probabilistic anti-bacterial therapy. 17 cases (8%) of these patients had osteosynthesis material replacement, with a median delay of 15 days (IQR 9–30) between initial surgery and SSI-SS diagnosis.

Empirical anti-bacterial therapy was initiated immediately following revision surgery, always as a combination of two antibiotics, most commonly piperacillin-tazobactam along with either vancomycin or Linezolid, according to French anaesthesia guidelines [5].

This initial therapy was maintained for an average of six days post-revision surgery, followed by six weeks to six months course of targeted antibiotic therapy adapted to intraoperative cultures.

A total of 106 patients were treated with rifampicin for a median duration of three months. Thirty patients were treated with clindamycin for a median duration of three months. Seven patients had dual therapy with clindamycin plus rifampicin.

Outcomes and prognostic factors

One hundred sixty-three patients completed the two-years follow-up and were included in the outcome analysis. 114 (70%) patients had a favorable evolution over the study period. In contrast, 34 cases experienced a relapse within two years after revision surgery, with a median relapsing time of 44 days. Additionally, 15 cases died, with a median time to death of 40 days (2 to 270 days).

There were no significant difference between the favorable and unfavorable outcome groups regarding age, alcohol or tobacco consumption, diabetes, body mass index, hypertension, or the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids before the initial surgery (Table 1).

While the gender appeared more balanced in the unfavorable outcome group, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.32). Similarly, the American Society of Anesthesiologists Score (ASAs) at the time of initial surgery tended to be higher in the unfavorable outcome group, but this difference did not reach statistical significance either (p = 0.11). However, immunosuppressive condition was significantly associated with poor outcome (Fig. 3) in both uni- (HR 2.5 [1.4; 4.6]; p = 0,033) and multivariate analysis (HR 2.58 [1.4, 4.9], p = 0.005).Fig. 3Survival curve associated with immunocompromised status

The surgical site location did not appear to influence the outcome. Among surgery procedures, tumor removal was associated with an unfavorable outcome in univariate (p = 0,007) and multivariate analysis (p = 0.005), while less failure cases were observed among patients with osteosynthesis hardware (p = 0,03), though this last was confirmed in multivariate analysis (p = 0.002).

Regarding clinical presentation, no significant differences were found between the two groups (Table 1). Cases commonly presented with scare discharge (n = 123; 75%), fever (n = 52; 32%), scar desunion (n = 52; 32%), local inflammation (n = 42; 26%), sepsis or septic choc (n = 40; 25%), and pain (n = 29; 18%). The median time between initial surgery and the onset of clinical symptoms was 17 days in the favorable group (IQR 10–30) and 15 days in the unfavorable group (IQR 10–28).

Importantly, cases in the unfavorable group appeared more likely to have a CRP level > 40 mg/L, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09).

S. aureus was significantly associated with favorable outcome in univariate analysis (HR 0.47 [0.25–0.89], p = 0.021). This result was not confirmed in multivariate analysis (HR 1.39 [0.40, 4.85]; p = 0.61). Other species did not show a significant positive or negative association with favorable outcomes. The use of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole in antibiotic therapy was associated with a increased risk of failure in the univariate analylisis (HR 2.3 [1.1–4.8], p = 0.02) but this increased risk was not confirmed in multivariate analysis (HR 1.47 [0.57, 3.80], p = 0.43). In both univariate and multivariate analysis, the use of rifampicin (HR 0.3 [0.1–0.7]; p = 0.008) or clindamycin (HR 0.2 [0–0.9]; p = 0.003) were significantly associated with a favorable outcome.

No significant differences were observed with fluoroquinolone or glycopeptide use in both univariate and multivariate analysis (Fig. 4). In univariate analysis, chemotherapy was significantly associated with an unfavorable outcome (HR 5.2 [2.3–12], p = 0.00006). In multivariate analysis, this result was not confirmed (HR 2.3 [0.6–9.4], p = 0.247). The presence of osteosynthesis material was associated with an increased likelihood of a favorable outcome in univariate analysis (HR 0.5 [0.27–0.92]; p = 0.027) and multivariate analysis (HR 0.26 [0.11, 0.63]; p = 0.002). Discussion

Our retrospective cohort study provides valuable insights into infections following spinal surgery and is among the largest SSI-SS study, with a two-year follow-up.

In Marseille, the incidence of SSI after spinal surgery was 3.3%, aligning with previously published data [3, 6–10]. In contrast, the infection rate is significantly lower following interventional radiology procedures (0.12%) [14], However, given the fundamental differences in surgical techniques, this comparison is somewhat inappropriate.

Our study identified immunocompromised status as a significant risk factor for failure in SSI-SS management. In this cohort, immunosuppressive conditions included active cancer, chemotherapy, immunosuppressive treatment, long-term corticosteroid use, and acquired or congenital immune deficiencies. These conditions are well known for impairing the immune system’s ability to prevent and combat infections, which could explain this association.

However, neither the type nor the location of the surgical intervention significantly impacted the outcome. These results require further studies in dedicated subgroups, as confounding factors were not investigated in this study, such as indication according to spinal stages.

Regarding bacterial species, S. aureus was the most frequently encountered pathogen, consistent with previous studies [3, 4]. The relatively low prevalence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) in our cohort, compared to other reports [15] may reflect the effectiveness of strict hygiene protocols in operating rooms and France’s antibiotic resistance control policies.

Interestingly, we observed a notably higher incidence of Enterobacteriaceae and Cutibacterium acnes in SSI-SS compared to previous reports. It could possibly be linked to the improved detection rate of C. acnes over recent years, thanks to optimised blood culture media for anaerobic bacteria (enabling faster detection within four days). A meta-analysis had already identified C. acnes as an emerging pathogen in spinal surgery infections [3]. As a slow-growing microorganism, its detection rate in our study may be higher because intraoperative samples were systematically cultured for ten days. Surprisingly, despite cefazolin being effective against C. acnes [16, 17], its prevalence in SSI-SS has been increasing over the years. Future studies should explore the mechanisms responsible for this trend. Similarly, we observed a substantial proportion of Enterobacteriaceae in SSI-SS cases, with 35% of intraoperative positive cultures. This figure may be slightly overestimated due to polymicrobial infections. Among these isolates, 23% were classified as multi-resistant or highly resistant. Like MRSA, these resistant Enterobacteriaceae are not effectively targeted by cefazolin prophylaxis. A similar concern applies to Staphylococcus epidermidis, which exhibited methicillin resistance in 48% of cases in our study.

In France, cefazolin remains the first-line antibiotic for perioperative prophylaxis in spinal surgery, as it is effective against methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus species and susceptible enterobacteria. However, in our center, approximately 1.5% of the 6,975 patients who underwent surgery between 2016 and 2022 developed SSI-SS caused by cefazolin-resistant bacteria. This raises concerns about the relevance of standard prophylactic strategies in preventing SSI-SS. Further prospective, randomized studies are needed to evaluate whether alternative or adjunctive prophylactic regimens may offer better protection.

The use of intrawound vancomycin powder as an adjunct to cefazolin prophylaxis remains widely debated. A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant benefit of vancomycin powder in preventing deep wound infections when used alongside cefazolin [18, 19]. However, a 2023 meta-analysis, which included data from 34,301 patients, concluded that prophylactic vancomycin powder significantly reduced the incidence of deep SSI-SS [20]. In contrast, a 2023 randomized controlled trial found intrawound vancomycin powder to be inferior to standard postoperative intravenous antibiotic therapy. To date, no randomized study has directly compared cefazolin plus intrawound vancomycin powder versus cefazolin alone in preventing SSI-SS. This remains an area for future research that could potentially improve prophylactic strategies.

Regarding the removal of spinal osteosynthesis material, our protocols in Marseille recommend a single-stage replacement in chronic infections, combined with targeted antibiotic therapy. Though our study did not find a significant association between implant removal and patient outcomes, it was only performed in 8 of the 34 patients with chronic SSI following spinal osteosynthesis as the initial surgery, with a two-year follow-up. Due to the small sample size, no definitive conclusions can be drawn about the efficacy of implant removal. The complexity of removal, both technically and in terms of complications in frail patients, makes it difficult to generalize its indication. However, when patient characteristics and surgical feasibility allow, we recommend single-stage replacement of osteosynthesis material in cases of chronic infection.

The use of rifampicin in targeted antibiotic therapy was associated with significantly better outcomes, as was clindamycin intake. This statistical association has been previously demonstrated in meta-analyses for rifampicin [21, 22]. The higher likelihood of therapeutic success might be due to the high diffusion of both molecules in bone tissue and rifampicin’s ability to penetrate biofilm. These results are not conclusive given the small number of patients treated with clindamycin (n = 30). Future high-powered studies should explore the impact of these drugs on recovery, taking confounding factors into account.

However, our study has limitations. It is retrospective and single-centre, and 64 patients (28%) of patients did not complete two years of follow-up, increasing the risk of selection bias.

Conclusion

This study provides one of the most comprehensive analyses of SSI-SS over a two-year follow-up period. The high incidence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens, particularly MRSA and multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae poses significant challenges for infection control. Our results underscore the importance of optimizing prophylactic strategies, as 3.3% of patients developed SSI-SS despite cefazolin use. Furthermore, in targeted therapy, rifampicin- or clindamycin-based regimens appeared to improve outcomes when the identified pathogen was susceptible. Given the significant impact of immunosuppression, osteosynthesis, and tumor resection on infection risk, future research should focus on refining surgical and antimicrobial management to improve patient prognosis.
