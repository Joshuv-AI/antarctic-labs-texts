Views of European Union medicine regulators and healthcare professionals on the approved versus prescribed dose of protein kinase inhibitors: an interview study
Authors: Margot Brinkhof, Sieta T de Vries, Peter G M Mol, Dario Trapani, Irja Marije de Jong, K Esther Broekman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore perceptions regarding the approved and actual prescribed doses of protein kinase inhibitors (PKIs) in clinical practice in the European Union among medicine regulators and healthcare professionals (HCPs).DesignA qualitative descriptive study was conducted using semistructured interviews, continuing until thematic saturation was reached. Thematic analysis was undertaken using a combined deductive-inductive approach. Deductive main analytical themes were derived from the theoretical framework of questioning-based policy design, namely problem sensing, problem categorisation and problem decomposition. Subthemes were generated inductively and could coherently be situated within these main analytical themes.SettingInterviews were held online or in person at a location convenient for the interviewee, depending on the participant’s preference.ParticipantsSeven medicine regulators involved in the regulation of cancer medicines—including PKIs—and 10 HCPs prescribing PKIs in clinical practice, from various countries within Europe, were included.ResultsRegulators highlighted insufficient attention to optimal dose finding, yielding approved doses often based on outdated maximum tolerated dose concepts, leading to uncertainties in efficacy and safety. HCPs reported using alternative dosing strategies in clinical practice to improve tolerability and quality of life (QoL) but noted a lack of robust evidence to guide such adjustments and faced legal constraints to deviate from the approved dose. Participants emphasised the need for improved pre-approval and post-approval dose optimisation to improve safety, enhance QoL and bridge gaps between trial data and real-world patient diversity.ConclusionsCollaborative efforts involving multistakeholders including HCPs, regulators, pharmaceutical companies, insurers, governments and patient representatives are essential to advance dose optimisation and improve patient-centric outcomes, with further research needed to understand these stakeholders’ perspectives.

Introduction

Traditionally, dose determination of anticancer medicines is performed in dose-finding phase 1 clinical trials, informed by pharmacokinetic (PK) and some pharmacodynamic (PD) data, and dose recommendations defined by the occurrence of moderate-to-severe toxicity following the principle of maximum tolerated dose (MTD). The MTD principle aims to maximise treatment efficacy and minimise the loss of chance, that is, treatment failure.1 2 This approach fits the mechanism of action of conventional cancer treatments with a steep dose-response relationship, such as cytotoxic chemotherapy, where higher exposure generally leads to a larger treatment effect. New anticancer medicines, such as small molecule protein kinase inhibitors (PKIs), however, have a different dose-response mechanism, where a plateau in the dose-response curve might be reached while the dose-toxicity curve is still increasing. This means that a further increased dose does not per se improve efficacy, but only increases the risk of toxicity.3 Hence, clinical benefit might be achieved before reaching the MTD.4 Nevertheless, the marketing authorisation dossier submitted by the pharmaceutical company to a regulatory body is often limited to a single—possibly too high—investigated dose that was defined following this MTD principle.1 Within the European Union (EU), cancer medicines are centrally regulated. In this process, new applications are assessed by the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use of the European Medicines Agency (EMA), consisting of representatives of the national medicine agencies of EU countries.5 If they consider the benefit/risk balance of a medicine positive, the studied dose will then usually also become the approved registered dose as recommended in the label (Summary of Product Characteristics, SmPC).

Over the past two decades, PKIs have become a major therapeutic group in the development of new targeted therapies against various diseases in which protein kinases are involved, such as autoimmune disease, cardiovascular disease and cancer.6 The added value of PKIs is large because of the usual oral administration which increases patient autonomy, and the specific targeting which decreases off-target effects, resulting in better tolerability compared with chemotherapy.79 Nevertheless, PKIs are associated with many adverse effects such as cardiovascular complications, hepatotoxicity, haematotoxicity, dermatological and ocular toxicity, and gastrointestinal symptoms,10 which often require dose interruptions and reductions in clinical practice.1113 Additionally, many PKIs have a complex metabolic profile that may lead to variable drug exposure across different individuals. These unique PK/PD profiles of PKIs are not extensively captured in the usual limited dose-finding clinical trials. Unsurprisingly, healthcare professionals (HCPs) deviate from approved starting doses to prevent adverse drug reactions (ADRs) in clinical practice.14 15 Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), where the PKI dose is adjusted based on direct measurement of drug levels in the blood, is possible for some PKIs. Especially for more recently approved PKIs, however, data on potential exposure relationships are lacking, and for many PKIs, TDM thresholds still need to be prospectively validated to be able to incorporate TDM in clinical practice.16

To summarise, the dose-response curve and dose-toxicity curve are not necessarily the same for PKIs, there is large inter-patient variability in exposure at a fixed dose, and dose adjustments are frequently necessary in clinical practice. This suggests the existence of a policy problem concerning dose recommendations for PKIs. A policy problem can be understood as a deviation between the current situation and a desirable state, requiring collective action from governments, institutions and stakeholders.1719 Stakeholders may differ in how they perceive both the current situation and the desired state, the forms of knowledge they prioritise, and the norms and values they consider at stake. They also vary in the power and resources they can mobilise to shape which problems gain political attention and which elements of the status quo remain accepted.20 21 Consequently, policy problems are socially and politically constructed: they often begin as loosely defined collections of concerns, which gradually become structured into clearer problems that are more amenable to solutions. Problem structuring is therefore a crucial step, as it allows the integration of diverse perspectives and worldviews, while simultaneously shaping the set of feasible solutions. Against this backdrop, the aim of our study was to examine the potential policy problem of PKI dose recommendations by exploring how two key stakeholder groups—medicine regulators and HCPs—perceive the relationship between approved and prescribed doses for PKIs in EU clinical practice. Understanding these perceptions is essential for informing policy debates and supporting the development of dosing recommendations that are both evidence-based and responsive to stakeholder concerns.

Methods

Study design and procedure

A descriptive approach was adopted to systematically elicit and represent stakeholders’ perspectives within the context of (clinical) practice. Semistructured interviews enabled an in-depth exploration of regulators’ and HCPs’ views on PKI dose optimisation. Included were regulators involved in the regulation of cancer medicines—including PKIs—and HCPs prescribing PKIs in clinical practice, within the EU. Persons with experience in both professions were also included if they still worked within one of the professions (online supplemental table 1). Potential participants were recruited via purposeful sampling and snowballing: potential participants in the research teams’ networks and people identified via searches on Google were contacted by email, and participants were asked to propose any further potential participants. To enhance transparency and methodological rigour, the reporting of this study was guided by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist (online supplemental material 1).22

Data collection

Semistructured interview guides, with minimal differences between the version of regulators and HCPs (online supplemental material 2) were used consisting of a few demographic and background questions and detailed open-ended questions about the participants’ perceptions regarding dosing recommendations for PKIs in clinical practice. The interview guides were guided by Hoppe’s19 questioning-based policy design framework, which builds on Cowan’s23 problem-oriented view of policy analysis and the iterative problem structuring approach of Van de Graaf and Hoppe.24 The framework by Hoppe19 conceptualises policymaking as a structured process of successive questioning to make sense of complex and uncertain issues. The study focused on three guiding questions: what is problematic about the current situation? (problem sensing); what gaps exist between the current and desirable state regarding PKI dose recommendations? (problem categorisation) and which partial problems between the current and desired state need to be solved? (problem decomposition). The remaining concepts of problem diagnosis and problem definition fell outside the scope of this study. In addition, norms and values were incorporated as conceptual lenses to prompt deeper probing into the main analytical themes of problem sensing, categorisation and decomposition.

The interviews lasted between 30 and 60 min, were conducted in English or Dutch, took place between December 2023 and March 2024, and were held online via Microsoft Teams (V.24091.214.2846.1452) or in person at a location convenient for the interviewee, depending on participants’ preference. Two researchers (MB and STdV or KEB) were present at the interview, of whom the first was in the lead and the second made notes and asked additional questions when relevant. Interviews were conducted iteratively until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new relevant insights emerged. Saturation was achieved after 17 interviews (10 HCPs and 7 regulators). This is in line with previous observations that saturation often occurs within 6–12 interviews per homogenous group.25 A summary of each interview was made and sent to the participant to evaluate if the researchers’ interpretations were correct.26

Analysis

The interviews were video-recorded in Microsoft Teams (V.24091.214.2846.1452) and transcribed verbatim using Microsoft Word (V.2405). The transcriptions were checked for errors and, where relevant, adapted. The transcripts were coded using Atlas.ti 22 by one researcher (MB). A second researcher (STdV) verified the coded transcripts. Thematic analysis using both a deductive and inductive approach was performed. The main analytical themes consisted of ‘problem sensing’, ‘problem categorisation’ and ‘problem decomposition’ and are deductively derived from the model of questioning-based policy design.19 The codes for the main analytical themes and corresponding subthemes used for the analysis were systematically organised and depicted in a coding tree (online supplemental material 3). Inductive coding was conducted at the level of the subthemes, as all emergent codes could be meaningfully situated within the broader main analytical themes.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in any of the steps of this study.

Results

The interviewees consisted of 10 HCPs, that is, medical oncologists, haematologists and a pulmonologist, and seven regulators, including four who had worked as medical oncologists in the past (11 women; median age 41 (IQR 7.5)). The participants were from the following EU countries: Croatia, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, The Netherlands, Norway, Portugal and Sweden. Views on the current situation

Pre-approval

There was the general view that pharmaceutical companies rush too much in the development phase to bring a medicine to the market as soon as possible (Q1). In oncology, the bar for approval was seen as less strict compared with medicines for other diseases and the benefits might weigh heavier than the risks (Q2). Examples given were that PKIs are approved based on single arm trials, that ADRs are believed to be dealt with post-approval in pharmacovigilance, and that companies apply early for marketing authorisation (eg, after completing phase 1 or 2 studies only).

Regulators expressed that there is generally little attention to optimal dose finding within the applications for marketing authorisation of new oncological medicines by pharmaceutical companies. Also, the strategies used are unclear or incomplete, with reasonings being vaguely formulated. Therefore, there is uncertainty in whether the chosen dose is correct, and regulators consider a further refinement of the benefit/risk profile necessary after approval. (Q3)

Participants generally viewed the approved dose of PKIs too high (Q4). A lower dose was thought to be less toxic but efficacious. HCPs did not see the too high approved dose as a problem per se as they had their own ways of dealing with this in clinical practice (Q5). Some HCPs also mentioned having trust in the regulators and therefore in the evaluated and approved dose. Another topic raised was that the medicine might be approved early but not reach the patient in clinical practice as it might not be reimbursed in case evidence is weak or relevance is less.

Clinical practice

Participants mentioned that in clinical practice, PKI treatment within oncology is often life-prolonging instead of curative. Therefore, they considered quality of life (QoL) very important. Regulators and HCPs acknowledged that in principle the approved PKI dose should be used in clinical practice given the positive benefit/risk evaluation. Some HCPs mentioned prescribing the approved dose—in most of the cases—as they considered PKIs to have a large therapeutic window, regarded the guidelines as ‘binding’ (Q6), or faced national legal policies or restrictions by insurance companies in deviating from guidelines. Others saw the approved dose more as a suggestion (Q7).

Most HCPs expressed a preference to use—at least to some extent—alternative lower dosing strategies, such as starting at a lower dose and performing dose escalations if treatment is tolerated in all patients or in certain subpopulations depending on patient characteristics (eg, age, frailty, comorbidities such as liver or kidney impairment, comedication, a patient’s mental state) and the aggressiveness of the disease (whether there is time to wait for a response to treatment). Strategies used were based on HCPs’ own knowledge and experience, experiences of their colleagues, discussion with other oncological specialists (eg, geriatric oncologists), findings from literature, information on websites or documents of authorities/organisations (eg, European Society for Medical Oncology, US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), EMA) and hospital-specific guidelines. Reasons for alternative dosing strategies were mostly to make patients tolerate the medicine as long as possible, prevent increased frailty and to keep QoL as high as possible (Q8). Some HCPs felt confident with their treatment approach; others felt insecure as they feared that the lower dose might be less effective.

Participants expressed that in their desired situation, more information on dosing would be available, which would aid in better and safer treatments for patients, resulting in fewer ADRs, better QoL and less healthcare costs.

Gaps between the current and desired situation

Gaps identified between the current and desired situation are broadly divided into insufficient knowledge about the optimal dose of PKIs, and about their use in a wider patient population.

Insufficient knowledge about the optimal dose

Regulators mentioned that usually there is one dose reported in applications—based on the MTD studied in phase 1—with which all clinical trials have been conducted. Although the phase 3 studies were considered well-conducted, regulators and HCPs viewed MTD as an old concept, used for chemotherapy, and not applicable to the mechanism of action of PKIs (Q9). There were uncertainties about the mechanism of action of PKIs and the dose-response/dose-toxicity relationships (Q10). Nonetheless, participants had the opinion that there should be more attention on finding the optimal biologically active dose.

It was mentioned that pharmaceutical companies might choose the MTD principle for financial reasons and to avoid the risk of under-treatment/insufficient efficacy. Following the principle of MTD, prescribing in clinical practice would imply starting with the approved dose and reducing based on toxicity. Alternative dosing strategies used by HCPs focused on lower cumulative doses, such as more treatment interruptions (Q11), or starting with a lower daily dose, potentially followed by dose escalations. Strategies used would depend, for example, on the characteristics of the patient (elderly, frail), the type of PKI and available evidence for a PKI in a specific disease (Q12). There were also non-medical reasons for prescribing lower doses, for example, how long a PKI has been on the market (eg, older TKIs not being on a registry anymore), insurance/reimbursement/pricing-related aspects and availability of dosages (Q13). HCPs, however, indicated the lack of evidence on dose optimisation and effectiveness of dose reductions.

Insufficient knowledge about a wider patient population

Participants mentioned that it is difficult to translate pre-approval findings to post-approval prescribing due to trials’ eligibility criteria. Clinical trial participants were considered generally more fit and less heterogenous than the diverse patient population in clinical practice (Q14). Also mentioned was that the flat starting dose is often based on studies in the US, where patients vary greatly from patients in the EU with regard to, for example, body composition (Q15).

Therefore, it was acknowledged that there is uncertainty on how a medicine will perform in the real world. Distribution of the medicine (PK), effective dose (PD) and tolerability were considered of pivotal importance for deciding on which dose to initiate PKI treatment. Moreover, it was considered that there is too limited information on dosing strategies for subgroups of patients.

Problems to be solved between current and desired situation

Participants desired that dose optimisation was conducted at the pre-approval phase of medicine development. This would, in the long term, save money and resources and result in fewer ADRs and better QoL. Various possibilities for improving pre-approval dose optimisation were mentioned (figure 1). Concerns raised by participants, however, include increased short-term costs, increased time until approval, the much larger required trial population when investigating differences between dosages and short dose-escalation studies being not suitable for PKIs given their chronic toxicity, that is, toxic effects occurring only over time after exposure. It was also stated that not everything can be known pre-approval (Q16).

Therefore, suggestions for dose optimisation in the post-approval phase were also mentioned (figure 1). However, there was unclarity on what/when there is sufficient evidence to change dosing recommendations. Regulators expressed that post-approval studies are often providing insufficient evidence to change a medicines’ label. For alternative dosing, non-inferiority of efficacy compared with the approved dose was seen as required. Large trials were considered needed and even if performed, a lot of debate was foreseen in changing guidelines (Q17).

It was also considered hard to adapt the SmPC as this would need to be initiated by the marketing authorisation holder (MAH, ie, pharmaceutical company who developed the medicine). Participants, however, expected that the MAH would have low interest to do this as there might be little incentive for them. This also applied in general to the conduct of dose optimisation studies. The value of academia in studying different treatment patterns/schedules for PKIs including other dosing schemes was acknowledged, but the involvement of pharmaceutical companies was seen as important. Moreover, a multidisciplinary team, including HCPs, scientists with PK and PD knowledge, pharmacists and nutritionists, was considered valuable for the set-up and conduct of dose optimisation studies. An example mentioned was the FDA’s Project Optimus in which relevant stakeholders are involved to improve dose optimisation and selection in oncology drug development (Q18). However, a specific problem mentioned by HCPs was that there are too many information sources and scientific papers, and that the information is not easy to find (Q19).

Regulators expressed how they generally hold low legal power to impose companies to perform more thorough dose optimisation studies (Q20), both pre-approval and post-approval. They also experience it as hard to intervene in the pre-approval studies. They feel they cannot question the dose anymore in phase 3 clinical studies. Post-approval studies were considered to mostly focus on safety rather than efficacy and thereby generating limited knowledge about the dose/response relationship.

Discussion

This study assessed the perceptions of EU medicine regulators and HCPs on the approved and prescribed dose of PKIs in clinical practice. PKIs were chosen as the focus of this study, because for many of these targeted drugs the MTD might not be the optimal dose for both efficacy and safety. There are other new groups of oncology medicines for which this might also be considered. For immunotherapy agents, there is no clear exposure-toxicity relationship within a wide therapeutic margin around the currently approved doses.27 Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are medicines consisting of a monoclonal antibody targeting tumour cells linked to a cytotoxic drug. compared with immunotherapy monoclonal antibodies, ADCs have a relatively narrow therapeutic window, and further evaluation of dosing recommendations and dose optimisation is also warranted for this drug class.28

Participants generally viewed the approved dose of PKIs as too high. This has led to different dosing strategies being adopted by HCPs in clinical practice. HCPs draw attention to the need for additional data regarding appropriate and safe dosing for various patient subpopulations. Regulators also think that further dose optimisation studies would be necessary to guide PKI prescribing, but they lack the means of incentivising pharmaceutical companies to carry these studies out. Possible solutions were seen both in the pre-approval and the post-approval phase.

Improving dose optimisation pre-approval

Participants preferred to gather most information about dose optimisation pre-approval. Including treatment arms with different doses of a new medicine in phase 3 registration trials would provide the most information on their relative benefit/risk balance.4 This increases the study size and may increase the duration of clinical development, which is in conflict with the current dogma of quickly bringing medicines for life-threatening diseases to the market.28 29 Regulators and HCPs shared the opinion that dose development studies for small molecules such as PKIs should leave the principle of MTD to define the best dose for further development. Recently, guidance to inform new, more flexible and adaptive designs for phase 1 trials has been presented,30 resulting from the FDA’s Project Optimus.31 In these guidelines, the value of early and regular consultation with health authorities is highlighted, as was also highlighted by several participants in our study. Within the current EU regulations, however, these consultations—in the form of Scientific Advice on medicine development—can be requested by pharmaceutical companies but are not mandatory.32 Sufficient legal power for regulators could be ensured if the European Commission would make pre-approval Scientific Advice consultations mandatory and the advice on dose finding and optimisation during medicine development binding.

Improving dose optimisation post-approval

Post-approval studies on dose optimisation were also considered important to improve the safety profile of medicines that have a flat exposure-response relationship but with a positive exposure-toxicity relationship, as has been identified for many PKIs.16 33 34 Nonetheless, guidelines on what data or evidence would be sufficient to change the recommended dose in the SmPC are lacking. More clarity is needed whether a demonstration of non-inferiority for a lower dose is required or if equivalence is sufficient.35 But even if this were to be clear, regulators cannot initiate the process of adapting the label, unless there is a large safety concern. Within the EU, the MAH is the owner of the product label and possesses virtually exclusive authority to induce adaptations in the SmPC. Results of studies on alternative dosing regimens conducted without support of the MAH are often not incorporated into medicine labels.36 Within the EU, academia plays a large role in post-approval treatment optimisation studies. A recent example of such a study is the SONIA trial, which showed that adding a cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6-inhibitor to first line hormonal treatment in metastatic breast cancer was not superior to its use in second line treatment.37 38 Because treatment duration was shorter in the second line, initiating treatment only in the second line leads to less toxicity and costs, which therefore has benefits from a patient as well as a societal perspective. The findings of the SONIA trial have not been implemented in either the SmPC or treatment guidelines, and it is unclear if and when this will be the case. Providing regulators with the legal authority to adapt the label, as suggested by the participants, could indeed resolve this issue. However, this would require legislative changes by the European Commission, which is in turn subject to substantial lobbying by the pharmaceutical industry.39 National professional societies or collaborating hospitals could also use data from post-approval treatment optimisation studies to adjust treatment recommendations. A recent example is the introduction of weight-based dosing of pembrolizumab in patients with non-small cell lung cancer, which was implemented in 40 Dutch hospitals and shown to have non-inferior efficacy in a retrospective cohort study, while reducing treatment costs.40 An equally effective but lower dose of a new medicine would imply improved cost-effectiveness as less medicine is used; on the other hand, negatively influencing profits for the MAH. However, for several modern, expensive PKIs, this incentive is lost because there is no difference in price between tablets of a lower or higher strength (eg, cabozantinib41 and lenvatinib42). Overall, a critical yet often overlooked phase in optimising cancer drug dosing is the post-marketing setting, where real-world data on tolerability, QoL and adherence can meaningfully inform clinical use; in fact, few to no mechanisms exist to systematically revisit dosing once a drug enters routine care. Integrating structured stakeholder input, including HCPs, patients and payers, in the post-approval phase may offer a pragmatic and ethically grounded path towards dose optimisation. Regulatory initiatives such as the FDA’s Project Optimus exemplify this shift toward evidence-informed, real-world dosing strategies, despite their primary focus on the drug development approaches. Developing similar frameworks in Europe with a better definition of opportunities and actions that can be provided in the post-marketing phase, including as informed by real-world evidence, could align regulatory, clinical and reimbursement perspectives, ensuring that approved therapies are appropriately used.

For example, HCPs indicated a deviation from the label-recommended dose and used alternative lower dosing strategies in clinical practice (table 2, micro level). To empower HCPs, easy access to data from pre-approval and post-approval studies that investigate the effects of different dosages on different subpopulations of patients is needed, for example, in a central easy-to-use repository (table 2, meso level). Such a repository could function as a resource for the creation of guidelines at the level of individual healthcare institutions and professional oncology associations or at the national government level, including treatment advice that deviates from the label. It is important to note, though, that there are reports of HCPs participating in such collaborations having been the subject of intimidation strategies by the pharmaceutical industry.43 A significant problem of HCPs deviating from a label-recommended dose as mentioned by participants is that legal constraints prohibit medical autonomy of HCPs in several European countries (table 2, macro level). Prescribing a lower dose than included in the label, or different dosing schedule, is considered off-label treatment and not reimbursed by insurers, or not endorsed from a legal perspective. At first sight, allowing HCPs to prescribe lower dosages or for a shorter duration would be in the interest of payers (insurers and government) with respect to a reduction in healthcare expenditure. However, it could come at the expense of revenue of the pharmaceutical industry if the lower dosages and shorter treatment duration could limit their revenue.44

Therefore, interventions of such problems will require a certain degree of political power play. A policy entrepreneur would be useful for this: someone with the mandate to bring stakeholders together, the cognitive skills to integrate perspectives, as well as the political savviness to get issues on the policy agenda. Moreover, to ensure change, it is of key importance to engage all relevant stakeholders—regulators, pharmaceutical industry, clinicians and patients—in an iterative interactive process over an extended period of time. Examples of multistakeholder initiatives are the Cancer Medicines Forum organised by the EMA and the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer,45 as well as workshops like the one on optimising dose selection strategies in oncology by FDA and the American Society of Clinical Oncology.46 Especially when it comes to de-escalation of treatments, for example, by lowering doses or shortening treatment duration, it is essential to engage patients in this process and ensure that they are well informed about the available evidence for such strategies. Patient engagement activities with respect to pharmaceutical regulation are still relatively scarce and rarely reported in literature.47 Common Sense Oncology is an example of a movement started by HCPs and patients together, that aims for a more patient-centred cancer care, by empowering and educating patients and global oncologists.48 Initiatives like these could provide informal support for regulators to enforce studies aiming at not only efficacious but also optimal treatments.

Strengths and limitations

Several points of strength can be highlighted, including the coverage of EU countries, spanning across several countries with diverse healthcare settings; additionally, we could rely on a multidisciplinary stakeholder panel to inform our policy dissertation. Our focus on an innovative topic addressing a critical unmet need, rooted in a policy rigidity, and for which we aid at modernisation, is of note. Although we included participants until data saturation was reached, future studies are needed among other relevant stakeholders such as patients, pharmaceutical companies and clinical trial physicians as they might have different perspectives. Furthermore, it should be noted that the identified views, gaps and problems stem from a sample of EU regulators and HCPs and might not apply to professionals from non-EU, or low- or middle-income countries. Another limitation of the study is that it is based on stakeholders’ perception and report, but not a defined triangulation with real-life prescribing practices; also, we did not benefit from further exploration of how institutional, reimbursement or legal constraints shape clinician decision-making, which could limit the overall conclusions.

Finally, this study was initiated based on a growing number of literature reports on the necessity for dose optimisation for targeted agents such as PKIs,49 and several multistakeholder initiatives as were previously discussed.45 46 In addition, the suboptimal dosing was observed in the current regulatory and oncology practices of several of the authors. This could have led to a biased view on the subject of dosing recommendations for PKIs being a policy problem. To minimise this bias, the interviews were performed by a researcher not working as a regulator and two researchers were present at each interview, ensuring the interviews started as neutral and broad as possible by asking for the participants’ view on the current situation in an open-ended way.

Conclusion

HCPs and regulators shared the opinion that current MTD-based drug development leads to too high approved doses of PKIs, and that dose optimisation is necessary. Suggestions for improvement were mentioned in both the pre-approval and post-approval phase of medicine development. Due to the approved dose being too high, individual HCPs—at micro level—apply various strategies to reduce the total dose patients receive, such as initiating treatment at a lower dose. Potential interventions are also found at the meso and macro level, meaning that a collaborative and sustained effort is required by not only HCPs and regulators, but also other relevant stakeholders such as pharmaceutical companies, payers, that is, insurers and national governments, and patient representatives. However, viewpoints of these stakeholders on PKI dosing have not been studied yet, which is a pivotal step for further research. This study provides suggestions to incentivise further dose optimisation, which should contribute to improved patient outcomes of PKI therapy.
