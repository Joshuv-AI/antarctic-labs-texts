Association between thyroid dysfunction and cognitive impairment in type 2 diabetes mellitus: A cross‐sectional study
Authors: Mengmeng Zheng, Xing Zhong, Ping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the association between thyroid dysfunction and cognitive impairment in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, 486 adult T2DM patients between January 2022 and January 2024 were enrolled. General clinical data and laboratory parameters [glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH), free thyroxine (FT4)] were collected. Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA <26 defined impairment). Patients were categorized into euthyroid, hypothyroid, and hyperthyroid groups based on TSH and FT4 levels. Multivariable logistic regression was used to analyze independent associations, followed by dose–response and subgroup analyses.ResultsThe hypothyroid group had significantly lower MoCA scores and a higher prevalence of cognitive impairment compared to other groups (all P < 0.001). Hypothyroidism was an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment (P = 0.002), whereas hyperthyroidism showed no significant association. Other independent risk factors included age, BMI, diabetes duration, hypertension, and coronary heart disease (P < 0.05). A significant linear dose–response relationship was observed between increasing TSH levels and higher risk of cognitive impairment (P < 0.05), while FT4 showed a non‐significant decreasing trend (P = 0.08). The association of hypothyroidism with cognitive impairment was consistent across all subgroups (all P > 0.05).ConclusionHypothyroidism is an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment in T2DM patients, with a progressive risk increase associated with higher TSH levels. These findings underscore the importance of integrating thyroid function evaluation into the cognitive assessment and management strategy for this patient population.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance (IR) and/or impaired insulin secretion from pancreatic β‐cells. Its global prevalence has risen continuously in recent years. According to the International Diabetes Federation report, an estimated 590 million adults aged 20–79 years are living with diabetes, of which T2DM accounts for over 90% 1 . Chronic hyperglycemia can lead to multi‐system complications, including microvascular and macrovascular diseases, as well as neurological damage. Accumulating evidence indicates that T2DM not only affects peripheral metabolic functions but is also associated with structural and functional alterations in the central nervous system. As such, T2DM represents a significant risk factor for cognitive dysfunction and dementia 2 , 3 .

Cognitive impairment is a common neurological complication in T2DM patients, manifesting as memory decline, impaired attention, executive dysfunction, and can progress to diabetes‐related dementia in severe cases. Epidemiological evidence shows that T2DM patients have an approximately 1.5‐ to 2‐fold higher risk of developing cognitive impairment compared to non‐diabetic individuals 4 . The underlying mechanisms are complex and involve factors such as chronic hyperglycemia, IR, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, cerebrovascular injury, and metabolic dysregulation 5 . However, these mechanisms do not fully account for the individual variability in cognitive dysfunction among T2DM patients, suggesting that other metabolic or endocrine factors may be involved.

Thyroid hormones play a crucial role in maintaining central nervous system development, synaptic plasticity, and cerebral metabolic homeostasis. Thyroid dysfunction, particularly subclinical hypothyroidism and subclinical hyperthyroidism, is closely associated with cognitive decline 6 . Previous research has found that even subtle fluctuations in thyroid hormone levels can affect neuronal metabolism, cerebral blood flow perfusion, and neurotransmitter metabolism, leading to mild‐to‐moderate cognitive deficits 7 . Furthermore, T2DM and thyroid dysfunction frequently coexist in the population, potentially interacting through mechanisms such as IR, dyslipidemia, and chronic inflammation. Therefore, investigating the role of thyroid functional status in cognitive impairment among T2DM patients is crucial for early identification of high‐risk individuals and developing intervention strategies.

However, current research findings on the relationship between T2DM comorbid with thyroid dysfunction and cognitive function are inconsistent. Some studies suggest that low thyroid function increases the risk of cognitive impairment in T2DM patients 8 , while others report no significant association 9 . These discrepancies may be attributed to factors such as study population, sample size, criteria for thyroid function grouping, and control of covariates. Based on this, the present study aims to systematically evaluate the relationship between different thyroid functional states and cognitive impairment in adult T2DM patients using a cross‐sectional design. We will further analyze the dose–response relationship between continuous thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH) and free thyroxine (FT4) levels and the risk of cognitive impairment and explore interaction effects within key subgroups. This study will provide insights into the endocrinological basis of cognitive dysfunction in T2DM, which may guide improved clinical screening and management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

A total of 486 adult T2DM patients were recruited from the Outpatient and Inpatient Department of Endocrinology in our hospital between January 2022 and January 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital, and the research process strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study primarily utilized logistic regression analysis. According to the empirical rule of at least 10 events per independent variable (events per variable ≥10), this study planned to include approximately 10 independent variables. Based on previous research 10 , the incidence of cognitive impairment in T2DM patients is approximately 25%–30%. Estimating a cognitive impairment rate of 26%, the minimum required sample size was N = 10 × 10/0.26 ≈ 385. Considering approximately 10% potential sample loss or incomplete data, the target sample size was set at about 400. The actual enrollment of 486 patients exceeded the theoretical requirement, ensuring sufficient statistical power for subsequent multivariate analyses. Furthermore, with a primary outcome event rate of 26%, this sample size also ensured statistical power for common subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex, age, disease duration), capable of detecting relative risk ratios of approximately 1.5–1.7 between subgroups [α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80], indicating good capability for stratified and subgroup analyses.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) definitive diagnosis of T2DM according to the criteria in the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care in Diabetes 11 ; (3) No history of thyroid hormone replacement or anti‐thyroid medication within the preceding 3 months; (4) stable glycemic treatment regimen for ≥3 months prior to enrollment, with adjustments in medication type or dosage not exceeding 20% during this period; (5) possession of basic comprehension and communication abilities, enabling independent completion of cognitive assessments and laboratory tests; (6) availability of complete clinical and laboratory data.

Exclusion criteria

(1) History of thyroid surgery, radioactive iodine therapy, or thyroid malignancy; (2) Comorbid conditions potentially affecting thyroid function (e.g., pituitary or hypothalamic dysfunction); (3) Occurrence of severe acute complications within the preceding 3 months (e.g., ketoacidosis, severe infection, acute cardiocerebrovascular events); (4) Presence of severe hepatic or renal insufficiency or active malignancy; (5) Coexisting established neurological disorders (e.g., stroke, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease) or severe psychiatric disorders; (6) Hearing, visual, or language impairments affecting cognitive assessment completion; (7) Use of medications known to affect the thyroid axis (e.g., amiodarone, lithium, glucocorticoids); (8) Incomplete data or refusal to participate.

Data collection and measurement indicators

General data collection

Clinical and demographic data for all participants were collected using a standardized case report form (CRF), including sex, age, years of education, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol consumption history, diabetes duration, and comorbidities such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and coronary heart disease (CHD). BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Smoking was defined as consuming ≥1 cigarette per day for ≥6 months. Alcohol consumption was defined as drinking alcohol ≥1 time per week for ≥6 months. Diabetes duration was calculated from the time of diagnosis to the enrollment date.

Laboratory parameter measurements

Venous blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning after an overnight fast. Measured parameters included glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), TSH, and FT4.

HbA1c was measured using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). TSH and FT4 were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Cobas e601 automated analyzer). Participants were categorized into three groups based on serum TSH and FT4 levels, in accordance with the guidelines cosponsored by the American Thyroid Association and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists, as well as the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Management of Thyroid Diseases (2021 Edition) 12 , 13 . Specifically: (1) Euthyroid group: both TSH and FT4 within reference ranges (TSH: 0.27–4.20 mIU/L; FT4: 12.0–22.0 pmol/L); (2) hypothyroidism group: including overt hypothyroidism (FT4 < 12.0 pmol/L and/or significantly elevated TSH) and subclinical hypothyroidism (normal FT4 but TSH > 4.20 mIU/L); (3) hyperthyroidism group: including overt hyperthyroidism (FT4 > 22.0 pmol/L and/or significantly suppressed TSH) and subclinical hyperthyroidism (normal FT4 but TSH < 0.27 mIU/L).

TSH and FT4 were selected as the primary indicators of thyroid function because they are the most widely used and guideline‐recommended parameters for defining thyroid functional status in clinical practice.

Cognitive function assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) 14 . This instrument covers seven cognitive domains: attention and concentration, executive functions, memory, language, visuospatial abilities, abstract thinking, and orientation. The total score is 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. For participants with <12 years of education, 1 point was added to the raw MoCA score to correct for educational bias. Cognitive impairment was defined as a total MoCA score < 26. All assessments were conducted face‐to‐face by uniformly trained researchers in a quiet environment to ensure consistency and reliability.

Quality control

All data were independently double‐entered by two researchers and cross‐checked. Discrepancies were resolved by a third party verifying the original medical records. Laboratory tests were performed by the same group of technicians under identical conditions to minimize measurement error. MoCA assessors received uniform training and passed consistency assessments prior to the formal survey. Missing data were verified and, if irrecoverable, the extent of missingness was reported. Multiple imputation was applied where appropriate.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 or R 4.3.0 software. Continuous variables were tested for normality. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation x¯±s and compared using one‐way analysis of variance. Non‐normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)] and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages [n(%)] and compared using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test. Using cognitive status (MoCA <26 defining cognitive impairment) as the dependent variable and thyroid functional status as the primary independent variable, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to analyze their association. Covariates were adjusted stepwise: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, education years; Model 2 further adjusted for BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and CHD; Model 3 additionally adjusted for smoking and alcohol consumption on top of Model 2. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to explore the dose–response relationship between continuous TSH and FT4 levels and the risk of cognitive impairment. Subgroup analyses were performed by age, sex, education years, diabetes duration, and HbA1c levels, with interaction tests conducted. All statistical tests were two‐sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics by thyroid function group

A total of 486 T2DM patients were enrolled, comprising 366 euthyroid, 73 hypothyroid, and 47 hyperthyroid individuals. The three groups exhibited no significant differences in age, sex distribution, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, hyperlipidemia, or CHD (all P > 0.05). The prevalence of hypertension was slightly higher in the hypothyroid and hyperthyroid groups compared to the euthyroid group, with the difference approaching but not reaching significance (P = 0.071). Regarding thyroid function parameters, TSH and FT4 levels across the three groups aligned with grouping expectations: the hypothyroid group had significantly elevated TSH and decreased FT4; the hyperthyroid group had significantly suppressed TSH and elevated FT4 (all P < 0.001; Table 1).

Clinical characteristics by cognitive status group

Participants were divided into cognitively normal (n = 281) and cognitively impaired (n = 205) groups based on cognitive status. Compared to the cognitively normal group, the cognitively impaired group was older (P < 0.001), had higher BMI (P < 0.001), longer diabetes duration (P = 0.007), and higher prevalence of hypertension and CHD (both P ≤ 0.02). TSH was slightly higher in the cognitively impaired group (P = 0.003), while FT4 showed no statistically significant difference (P = 0.116). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, hyperlipidemia, or HbA1c distribution (all P > 0.05; Table 3).

Multivariable analysis of thyroid status and cognitive impairment

Using cognitive impairment (MoCA <26) as the dependent variable, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Using the euthyroid group as reference, hypothyroidism was independently associated with the risk of cognitive impairment: Model 1 (adjusted for age, sex, education): OR = 2.870 (95% CI 1.610–5.110), P < 0.001; Model 2 (further adjusted for BMI, duration, HbA1c, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, CHD): OR = 2.650 (1.410–4.960), P = 0.002; Model 3 (Model 2 + smoking, alcohol): OR = 2.710 (1.440–5.110), P = 0.002. Hyperthyroidism was not associated with cognitive impairment (Model 3: OR = 1.090, 95% CI 0.530–2.260, P = 0.813). Among covariates, age, BMI, diabetes duration, hypertension, and CHD were independently associated with cognitive impairment (P ≤ 0.01), while sex, education years, HbA1c, hyperlipidemia, smoking, and alcohol consumption showed no significant associations (P > 0.05; Table 4).

Dose–response relationship between TSH/FT4 levels and cognitive impairment risk

Based on the multivariable logistic regression model, RCS analysis was used to explore the dose–response relationship between continuous TSH/FT4 levels and cognitive impairment risk. The model adjusted for covariates including age, sex, education years, BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, CHD, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Results showed a significant positive linear relationship between TSH levels and the risk of cognitive impairment (P for trend <0.05, P for nonlinear >0.05), indicating a gradual increase in cognitive impairment risk with rising TSH, particularly more pronounced in the higher range. FT4 levels showed a negative linear trend with cognitive impairment risk (P for trend = 0.08, P for nonlinear >0.05), suggesting that lower FT4 might be associated with cognitive dysfunction, though not reaching statistical significance. Overall, the RCS smooth curves revealed a positive linear relationship between TSH and cognitive impairment risk, while FT4 exhibited an opposite negative trend (Figure 2).

Interaction analysis for hypothyroidism and cognitive impairment risk

To explore potential interactions between thyroid status and key clinical variables, interaction terms were added to the multivariable logistic regression model (same covariates as Model 3) for testing. Results showed that the association between hypothyroidism and cognitive impairment was consistent in direction across all subgroups, but the interactions of age, sex, education years, diabetes duration, and HbA1c with thyroid status did not reach statistical significance (all P for interaction >0.05; Table 5, Figure 3). These findings suggest that the magnitude of the effect of hypothyroidism on cognitive impairment risk was generally similar across different ages, sexes, education levels, diabetes durations, and glycemic control states, with no statistically significant effect modification observed.

DISCUSSION

Cognitive impairment is a common chronic complication in T2DM patients, with its pathological basis involving IR, chronic inflammation, vascular endothelial injury, and cerebral metabolic dysregulation. In recent years, thyroid dysfunction, particularly hypothyroidism, has been implicated in the development of diabetes‐related cognitive impairment 15 . Thyroid hormones exert broad effects on neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and energy metabolism. The relationship between TSH/FT4 abnormalities and cognitive decline has been established in the general population 16 , but systematic studies in diabetic populations are still lacking.

The findings of this study revealed significantly lower MoCA scores and a markedly higher prevalence of cognitive impairment in the hypothyroid group compared to the euthyroid and hyperthyroid groups. Previous research has reported that thyroid diseases, including hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, can affect the nervous system and play a role in dementia development 15 . A correlation exists between hypothyroidism and diabetes‐related neuronal dysfunction 17 . These results reflect central metabolic disturbances caused by thyroid hormone deficiency. Hypothyroidism can lead to reduced cerebral glucose metabolism and insufficient energy supply, particularly affecting functional activity in the frontal lobe and hippocampus, consequently impairing executive function and memory. Imaging studies have shown decreased cerebral metabolic rate, reduced cerebral blood flow perfusion, and formation of white matter hyperintensities in hypothyroid patients 18 . Furthermore, the hypometabolic state induced by hypothyroidism may further impair the synthesis of cholinergic neurotransmitters, affecting information processing speed and attention maintenance.

In the further dose–response analysis, the RCS model demonstrated a significant linear positive correlation between TSH and cognitive impairment risk, while FT4 levels showed a decreasing trend with risk. This suggests that the impact of thyroid functional changes on cognitive impairment may be gradual even within the physiological range. We assessed thyroid function using serum TSH and FT4, the guideline‐recommended primary indicators for clinical evaluation. FT4 can cross the blood–brain barrier and be locally converted to T3 within neural tissue, whereas serum T3 levels are more susceptible to short‐term fluctuations and non‐thyroidal illness, particularly in patients with chronic metabolic diseases such as T2DM. Thyroid autoantibodies, including thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPO‐Ab) and thyroglobulin antibodies (Tg‐Ab), are primarily used to determine autoimmune etiology rather than functional status. Therefore, these parameters were not included, as the primary aim of this study was to investigate the association between thyroid functional states and cognitive impairment. Elevated TSH levels may affect the nervous system through multiple pathways. Studies have found that TSH receptors (TSHRs) are expressed in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex, and their activation can influence neuronal signaling and synaptic plasticity, thereby impairing learning and memory functions 19 . Mansoori et al 20 . further confirmed in vitro that neuronal cells express TSHR, and TSH stimulation can promote neurite outgrowth and differentiation, suggesting the TSH/TSHR axis may be involved in neural structural remodeling. The linear incremental relationship between rising TSH and cognitive impairment risk observed in our study aligns with this mechanism. Conversely, decreased FT4 levels may reflect limited utilization of thyroid hormones in the brain. FT4 can cross the blood–brain barrier into neural tissue and be locally converted to T3 via deiodination to maintain neuronal energy metabolism and synaptic function. Its reduction can lead to impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, insufficient energy synthesis, and weakened neural network activity, thereby increasing the risk of cognitive impairment. Although the statistical association between FT4 and cognitive impairment risk did not reach significance in this study, its decreasing trend is consistent with previous findings. Prior research indicates that FT4 is converted to T3 within neurons, participating in the regulation of neural energy metabolism and synaptic plasticity 21 . Therefore, even mild decreases in FT4 might affect cognitive function through impaired energy metabolism and disrupted neural signaling.

Besides its direct cerebral effects, hypothyroidism may also impair cognition indirectly in T2DM. It often coexists with IR and chronic inflammation, potentially harming cognitive function through metabolic‐vascular pathways. Hypothyroidism can exacerbate IR, reduce cerebral glucose utilization efficiency, and promote advanced glycation end product deposition and oxidative stress, thereby inducing neuronal injury and cerebral small vessel dysfunction. Li et al. 22 reported that T2DM patients with comorbid hypothyroidism had elevated levels of interleukin‐6 and tumor necrosis factor‐α, which were negatively correlated with cognitive assessment scores, suggesting that systemic inflammation may play a mediating role in hypothyroidism‐induced neural damage. Furthermore, stratified analysis showed that the positive association between hypothyroidism and cognitive impairment risk was consistent in direction across all subgroups, and age, sex, education level, and diabetes duration did not significantly modify this effect. This aligns with findings from population studies by Pyun et al. 23 , indicating that the impact of thyroid dysfunction on cognitive function has population‐wide generality, suggesting that thyroid function assessment should be incorporated into routine clinical pathways for cognitive screening and management in T2DM patients.

Notably, this study did not observe a significant association between hyperthyroidism (whether overt or subclinical) and cognitive function, which is not entirely consistent with some previous studies. Some epidemiological studies have reported that high FT4 or low TSH states may also increase the risk of cognitive decline and dementia in elderly populations 24 . However, no similar trend was found in the present study, potentially due to the following factors: First, the proportion of hyperthyroid and subclinical hyperthyroid patients in our sample was relatively low, limiting statistical power and potentially leading to an underestimation of the association. Second, the study excluded individuals with acute metabolic disturbances and severe comorbidities; the hyperthyroid cases in our study population were mostly mild or of short duration, and their impact on central metabolism and vascular function might not yet be significant. Third, most previous studies often focused on elderly or non‐diabetic populations. In T2DM patients, the mechanisms of cognitive impairment are more complex. Factors such as IR, vascular pathology, and metabolic stress might mask or interfere with any independent effect of hyperthyroidism. Additionally, some studies suggest that hyperthyroidism‐related cognitive impairment might be reversible, with cognitive function improving after restoration of thyroid function 25 , which could also explain the lack of significant association detected in our study. Overall, the relationship between hyperthyroidism and cognitive function is likely influenced by disease duration, magnitude of hormone level fluctuation, and individual metabolic status, requiring further validation in larger samples and longitudinal follow‐up studies.

STUDY SIGNIFICANCE AND LIMITATIONS

This study systematically evaluated the relationship between thyroid status, TSH/FT4 levels, and cognitive impairment in a T2DM population, revealing the potential role of hypothyroidism in diabetes‐related cognitive damage. These findings suggest that routine thyroid function testing and cognitive screening should be integral components of the comprehensive management of diabetes. Early identification and intervention for hypothyroidism may help delay the progression of cognitive decline in T2DM patients. However, this study still has several limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design limits causal inference. Second, the lack of assessment of serum T3 levels, thyroid autoantibodies (TPO‐Ab and Tg‐Ab), and neuroimaging markers limited further exploration of the etiological and mechanistic links between thyroid dysfunction and cognitive impairment. Third, cognitive assessment relied solely on the MoCA scale without delineating specific cognitive domains. Future research should incorporate longitudinal follow‐up and neuroimaging techniques to further elucidate the dynamic mechanisms between thyroid dysfunction and cognitive impairment in diabetes.

CONCLUSION

Among patients with T2DM, those with hypothyroidism exhibit poorer cognitive performance and a higher prevalence of cognitive impairment. The risk of cognitive impairment increases with rising TSH and decreasing FT4 levels. This suggests that thyroid dysfunction may contribute to diabetes‐related cognitive decline. Therefore, thyroid function should be assessed in the clinical management of patients with T2DM.
