Comparison of Heated Humidified High-flow Nasal Cannula and Noninvasive Ventilation in Children with Acute Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure Using Clinical Respiratory Score: A Prospective Observational Study

Introduction

Respiratory failure remains one of the leading causes of pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admissions globally, accounting for about 17–20% of cases.1 Acute hypoxemic respiratory failure with diverse etiologies, including pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), bronchiolitis, and pulmonary edema, has historically been managed with invasive mechanical ventilation. However, complications such as ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI), ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), and sedation-related adverse effects have shifted focus toward noninvasive modalities.2

Over the past two decades, heated humidified high-flow nasal cannula (HHHFNC or HFNC) therapy and noninvasive ventilation (NIV) have gained prominence for improving oxygenation and reducing the work of breathing while minimizing risks of invasive ventilation.3 High-flow nasal cannula therapy provides heated and humidified oxygen at high-flow rates with laminar airflow, improving muco-ciliary clearance and splinting of airways with superior comfort but variable and modest positive pressure.4,5 Noninvasive ventilation delivers positive pressure through interfaces like nasal or oronasal interfaces, promoting alveolar recruitment and enhancing gas exchange.6–8

Despite widespread clinical use, comparative data on the efficacy of HFNC vs NIV in children are limited. This study aims to address this gap by comparing HFNC and NIV using the clinical respiratory score (CRS). It also provides insight into the possible factors that could affect the failure of the modality, leading to escalation of support.

Patients and Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted in the PICU of a tertiary care hospital in South India over a period of 20 months from May 2023 to December 2024. Our PICU is an 18-bed tertiary unit providing comprehensive critical care services with approximately 1,200–1,500 admissions annually, with respiratory illnesses accounting for around 25% of all PICU admissions. The study was initiated after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC no: MSRMC/EC/PG-59/04-2023).

All children aged between 2 and 18 years admitted to the PICU with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure were screened. Those who required escalation of therapy from a low-flow nasal cannula or simple face mask to higher respiratory support (NIV or HFNC) were eligible for the study. Children with multiorgan dysfunction, hemodynamically unstable patients, need for intubation in the emergency room (ER) or within 2 hours of admission, cyanotic congenital heart disease, craniofacial malformations, chronic lung diseases, neuromuscular diseases, and upper airway obstruction were exclusions. Written informed consent was taken from parents or legally authorized representatives (LARs), and assent was taken for children above 12 years of age.

Detailed demographic characteristics, relevant clinical history, and examination findings were documented in a predesigned proforma. All enrolled children were allocated to either HFNC or NIV therapy at the clinician's discretion. The severity of respiratory distress was assessed using the CRS (Supplementary Table 1).9 This score is based on respiratory rate, auscultation findings, use of accessory muscles, mental status, room air oxygen saturation (SpO2), and color. At the initiation of either HFNC or NIV therapy, the parameters recorded were heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, fraction of inhaled oxygen (FiO2), HFNC flow rate, and pressure settings for NIV and CRS at initiation (CRS 0H). These parameters were monitored at 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours.

High-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy was delivered using the Fisher and Paykel Airvo-2® system using an age-appropriate cannula. The flow rate was initiated at 1 L/kg/min and progressively increased to a maximum of 2 L/kg/min until clinical improvement was achieved. The protocol followed was 2 L/kg/min for the first 10 kg of body weight and an additional 0.5–1 L/kg for each kg above 10 kg. The flow rate was titrated based on clinical response and tolerance. The FiO2 was adjusted to achieve a SpO2 of 94% or higher. Good response on HFNC therapy was assessed clinically based on improvement in heart rate (20% reduction), respiratory rate (20% reduction), and CRS score getting better.10

Noninvasive ventilation was delivered through interfaces like an oronasal interfaces connected to an ICU ventilator (dual limb circuit) with pressure support (PS) applied in a NIV mode or a nasal mask (Pixi mask)® connected to a bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) machine (single limb circuit). The initial settings were positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) or expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) of 5 to 6 cm of H2O, and the PS or inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) level was adjusted as per patient requirements between 8 and 12 cm of H2O to achieve optimal tidal volume and to maintain a SpO2 > 94%. Further settings were titrated as per clinical condition, tolerance, and blood gas.

Children were continuously monitored, and treatment escalated to either NIV in the HFNC group or invasive mechanical ventilation in either group as per clinical status. Escalation of therapy was considered a treatment failure. General criteria used for treatment failure included heart rate and respiratory rate remaining unchanged or increased, oxygen requirement exceeding FiO2 ≥ 60% to maintain SpO2 ≥ 94%, no improvement in work of breathing, worsening sensorium, and hemodynamic instability. If there was improvement in CRS, the assigned therapy (HFNC or NIV) was continued, and CRS was calculated at 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours. Patients were considered clinically stable and eligible for weaning from respiratory support when the indication for using HFNC/NIV had resolved or the CRS score was 3 or lower.

A study by Surabhi et al. comparing HFNC in children with respiratory distress due to bronchiolitis vs pneumonia observed a mean CRS of 4.54 within 1 hour of initiating HFNC.10 Expecting to detect a minimum difference of 0.7 points in CRS between HFNC and NIV groups, with 80% power and 95% confidence level (CI), a minimum of 30 subjects were required in each group. The total sample size was therefore determined to be 60 patients (30 in the HFNC group and 30 in the NIV group).

Data was entered into Microsoft Excel data sheets and analyzed using SPSS version 22 software (IBM SPSS Statistics, Somers, NY, USA). Normality of data was checked using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical data were represented as frequencies and proportions. The proportions were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Continuous data was represented as mean and standard deviation for normal distribution or median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normal distribution. An independent t-test was used to identify the mean difference between two quantitative variables. For non-normally distributed continuous data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparison between two groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 631 children were admitted to the PICU with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure during the study period. Of these, 157 were eligible as per the inclusion criteria, of which 98 children were excluded based on the exclusion criteria. A total of 59 children were enrolled in the study and categorized into two groups: 38 children received HFNC therapy, while 21 were managed with NIV. The HFNC group had a significantly lower median age compared with the NIV group. Age stratification revealed that over half of HFNC patients were aged 2–5 years, whereas the majority of NIV patients were aged 5–12 years. Gender distribution and duration of illness were similar. Diagnosis distribution differed significantly, with pneumonia being more common in the HFNC group (63.1%), and a higher number of ARDS and capillary leak syndrome (33.3 and 28.6%, respectively) in the NIV group. Acute respiratory distress syndrome etiologies were predominantly infection-related, with severe pneumonia being the most common cause (n = 10); all pneumonia cases that met pediatric acute lung injury consensus conference (PALICC) criteria were categorized as ARDS. Sepsis-related ARDS occurred in two patients, and one case was associated with a metabolic disorder (lysinuric protein intolerance). Capillary leak syndrome was most commonly due to dengue (n = 4), followed by sepsis (n = 3), fulminant hepatic failure (n = 2), and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (n = 1). Baseline characteristics are as depicted in Table 1.

Treatment success rates were similar; however, the duration of respiratory support in successful cases was higher in the HFNC group, though this difference did not reach statistical significance [74 hours (66–96) vs 59 hours (36–120); p = 0.084]. Escalation to NIV occurred in 10.5% of the HFNC group and to invasive mechanical ventilation occurred in 13.2% of the HFNC and 28.6% of NIV patients. Outcomes are presented in Table 2.

Clinical respiratory scores improved progressively in both groups. At initiation, CRS was comparable [9 (8–10) vs 10 (9–10); p = 0.355]. At 2 hours, the difference remained non-significant [8 (7–9) vs 9 (7–10), p = 0.216]. At 6 hours, CRS showed significantly better improvement in NIV [7 (6–8) vs 5 (4–7); p = 0.023]. Differences at 12 hours (p = 0.064), 24 hours (p = 0.066), and 48 hours (p = 0.61) were not statistically significant. Combined CRS analysis showed progressive improvement across both groups, from a median of 9 (8–10) at baseline to 4 (3–5) at 48 hours, representing a 56% reduction. The most significant between-group difference occurred at 6 hours (p = 0.023), with NIV showing faster initial improvement. Diagnosis-specific outcomes varied between the groups and are shown in Supplementary Table 3. Pneumonia had higher success rates in both groups (HFNC: 95.8%, NIV: 100%). Acute respiratory distress syndrome showed high failure rates (HFNC: 83.3%, NIV: 57.1%). Capillary leak syndrome had better outcomes with NIV (83.3%) than HFNC (25%).

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, we analyzed 59 pediatric patients who were admitted with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure and compared HFNC and NIV using CRS. Noninvasive ventilation demonstrated early changes in CRS, which were observed within the first 6–24 hours, while CRS trends at later time points were comparable between the modalities. Noninvasive ventilation achieved faster clinical improvement, with significantly lower CRS at 6, 12, and 24 hours. These findings align with the previous study conducted by Essouri et al., who reported a rapid improvement in respiratory parameters with NIV in their pediatric cohort within the first 2 hours of therapy. They reported improvement in PaCO2 (−3.9 ± 13.5) and respiratory rate (−10.4 ± 20.8) within 2 hours of therapy.11 Yañez et al. showed that the heart rate and respiratory rate were significantly lower after 1 hour of treatment compared with baseline (p = 0.0009 and p = 0.0001, respectively), and the trend continued over time till 6 hours (p = 0.0176).12 We utilized CRS, which combines clinical parameters, to show overall improvement.

The clinical response analysis further substantiated NIV's early advantages, with percentage improvements from baseline, in comparison with HFNC, significantly favoring NIV at multiple time points. Osman et al., in their study in adult patients with cardiogenic pulmonary edema, demonstrated that helmet continuous positive airway pressure (hCPAP) led to a greater mean respiratory rate reduction at 1 hour as compared with the HFNC group [12 (95% CI: 11–13) vs 9 (95% CI: 8–10); p < 0.001], and heart rate difference after the 1 hour treatment was greater with hCPAP [−20 b.p.m. (95% CI: 17–23) vs −15 b.p.m. (95% CI: 12–18) p = 0.042]. These results are consistent with the present study, though their population differed significantly from our cohort in age, interface used, and underlying pathology.13 In this study, no difference was noted at 48 hours between NIV and HFNC, suggesting HFNC was non-inferior when comparing long-term outcomes, which were consistent with the study by Santos et al., where 29% of the NIV group and 23% in the HFNC group were intubated (p = 0.25) within the non-inferiority margin of 15% and the difference of 6.3% (95% CI: −4.5 to 17.1, p < 0.0001).14

Significant age difference was noted between the treatment groups, with HFNC patients being younger compared to NIV patients. This pattern reflects the clinical decision-making based on patient co-operation and anatomical considerations. This age distribution finds support in the literature, where Franklin et al. demonstrated HFNC's particular effectiveness in infants with bronchiolitis, with the percentage of escalation in the HFNC group being 12% compared to 23% in the low-flow oxygen group (p < 0.001).4 Yañez et al. found NIV more successful in older children, with younger children more likely to get intubated. The mean age of non-intubated patients was 44.2 ± 44.6 months in comparison with 7.3 ± 4.9 months in intubated patients.12 The physiological basis for this age-related effectiveness likely relates to upper airway anatomy, with younger children having proportionally larger heads and smaller airways that may benefit more from the anatomical dead space clearance provided by HFNC and the non-availability of an appropriate NIV interface and comfort in younger children.15

Diagnosis distribution differed significantly, with HFNC patients having more pneumonia cases, while NIV patients had more ARDS and capillary leak syndrome. These baseline differences suggest that treatment selection was influenced by underlying pathophysiology. A good understanding of the disease process, lung pathology, and severity may guide clinicians to choose the appropriate noninvasive respiratory support in children. The study by Grieco et al. supports that helmet NIV, when compared to HFNC, showed improved P/F ratio [255 mm Hg (140–299) vs 138 (101–172); p = 0.001] and lowered inspiratory effort [7 cm H2O (4–11) vs 15 (8–19); p = 0.001] in adult patients with severe hypoxemic respiratory failure.16 A study in adults by Angkoontassaneeyarat et al. showed that emergency physicians favor NIV for patients who had severe disease and comorbid conditions; however, there was no difference in intubation rates compared to HFNC.17 De Jong et al. opined that individualized patient management in the choice of respiratory support is critical, taking into account the complexity of the patient on a case-by-case basis.18 Milési et al. suggest restricting HFNC therapy to mild forms of respiratory distress and situations of interface intolerance.19 The improvement in S/F and P/F ratios after starting HFNC and NIV in our study was comparable.

Despite NIV's superior short-term clinical improvement, overall treatment success rates were remarkably similar between the groups. This finding challenges the assumption that faster clinical improvement necessarily translates to better overall outcomes. The success vs failure analysis revealed that higher CRS at initiation was a significant predictor of treatment failure in both NIV and HFNC groups. Also, improvement at 2 hours can identify children who are likely to have a successful outcome in both modalities, suggesting that a stringent bedside monitoring for the first few hours of noninvasive respiratory support is critical for determining treatment outcome. These findings were consistent with Muñoz-Bonet et al., who showed that children with improved heart rates and PaCO2 within 4 hours of initiation of noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV) had success.20 Mayordomo-Colunga et al. identified that a higher PRISM score (OR: 1.138; 95% CI: 1.022–1.267) and a lower RR decrease at 1 hour and at 6 hours (OR: 0.926; 95% CI: 0.860–0.997 and OR: 0.911; 95% CI: 0.837–0.991, respectively) were independent risk factors for NIV failure in critically ill children.21 Current PALICC-2 guidelines emphasize the importance of early recognition of treatment failure. They recommend that patients on NIV who do not show clinical improvement within the first 6 hours of treatment or have worsening disease be intubated early.22

In this study, overall escalation rates were similar in both groups, with NIV patients requiring mechanical ventilation more frequently, which may be explained by the fact that the NIV group had more severe cases like ARDS and capillary leak syndrome that typically require positive pressure support. The duration of HFNC was higher than NIV; however, the length of PICU stay was similar. Santos et al. also showed similar results, with a significantly longer duration of support in the HFNC group [77.5 (46.5–108) vs 48 (24–78) hours, p = 0.0003], but it did not influence the PICU or hospital length of stay.14 Munroe et al. showed that NIV required treatment for fewer hours (median: 7.5 vs 13.5, p < 0.001), but had no difference in ventilator-free days [28 (26–28) vs 28 (10.5–28), p = 0.199] compared to HFNC, likely explained by gradual weaning protocol for HFNC.23 These findings may vary based on the structured weaning protocols followed in different PICUs.

Our findings align with the recent high-quality randomized controlled trial (RCT), the first-line support for assistance in breathing in children (FIRST-ABC) trial by Ramnarayan et al., who compared HFNC with CPAP in critically ill children and found HFNC was non-inferior to CPAP, with a median time to liberation from respiratory support of 52.9 hours in the HFNC group compared with 47.9 hours in the CPAP group (absolute difference 5 hours). However, the advantages of HFNC included significantly lower sedation requirement (27.7 vs 37%), shorter critical care (5 vs 7.4 days), and shorter total hospital stays (13.8 vs 19.5 days).24 The TRAMONTANE study by Milési et al. demonstrated HFNC's non-inferiority to nCPAP in infants with viral bronchiolitis, with similar rates of treatment failure (12 vs 14%).25 However, their population was younger and had less severe disease compared to our PICU cohort, limiting direct comparison.

The strengths of this study include a pragmatic approach to noninvasive respiratory support use and objective tools like CRS to compare outcomes. The study supports an individualized approach by demonstrating that both HFNC and NIV can be effective when appropriately selected based on patient characteristics and clinical circumstances. The significant age-related treatment patterns observed suggest that current clinical practice appropriately considers patient factors in treatment selection. These findings support the implementation of structured assessment protocols to guide treatment selection and early identification of treatment failure. The complex relationship between rapid clinical improvement and overall patient outcomes highlighted in our study requires clinicians to consider not just the speed of improvement but also the sustainability and tolerability of respiratory support. We included only children with moderate to severe respiratory illness, ensuring that the study focused on those who truly needed advanced respiratory support. Children below 2 years were excluded because many in this age group have bronchiolitis and also face interface-related difficulties with NIV, which could affect the reliability of comparisons.

Limitations of this study include the inability to attain the initially calculated sample size in the NIV group. The relatively small numbers in the treatment failure groups limit the power of our success vs failure analyses. This study did not specifically measure comfort scores. Large-scale RCTs comparing HFNC and NIV in similar patient populations are needed to generalize; however, the choice of support is usually clinician-driven, taking into account the age, disease pathophysiology, patient comfort, and other comorbid conditions, making RCTs difficult to conduct and increases the likelihood of residual confounding in observational studies, suggesting use of propensity score analysis as a robust methodology for more meaningful comparisons. Economic implications were not addressed in our study; however, the similar duration of support observed suggests that economic considerations may not drive treatment selection.

Conclusion

There was no significant difference in treatment failures between the two groups. However, NIV demonstrated early respiratory improvement while HFNC showed similar long-term outcomes, showing trends toward better patient outcomes. These findings should be interpreted in the context of clinician-directed treatment allocation, where modality selection was influenced by age, disease severity, and interface tolerance. Clinical respiratory score at initiation was a significant predictor of treatment failure in both groups, and improvement within the first 2 hours can help identify the cohort of children likely to have a successful outcome. Due to the non-random allocation of participants, it is challenging to directly compare the efficacy of HFNC and NIV and draw definitive conclusions. Hence, the findings of this study require further validation before clinical adoption.

Clinical Significance

Lower CRS at initiation and improvement within the first 2 hours predict treatment success, suggesting that stringent bedside monitoring for the first few hours of noninvasive respiratory support is critical for determining treatment outcome. An individualized approach to choose the modality based on patient characteristics and clinical circumstances would improve the chances of success of HFNC and NIV.
