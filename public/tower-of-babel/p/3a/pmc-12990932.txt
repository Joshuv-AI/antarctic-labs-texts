Super-enhancer-associated miR-1260b coordinates adipogenesis and metabolic programming in human adipose stem cells
Authors: Sen Li, Shuhui Ji, Zihan Yu, Haiyang Liu, Jianxin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObesity and its metabolic complications represent a major and growing public health burden, for which effective therapeutic strategies remain limited. Although super-enhancers are recognized as key regulators of cell identity and adipogenesis, the roles of super-enhancer– microRNAs in obesity and adipocyte dysfunction remain insufficiently characterized. Here, we identify miR-1260b as a super-enhancer–associated miRNA that influences human adipogenesis. Analysis of adipose-related datasets from SEdb 2.0 revealed that MIR1260B is consistently linked to a clustered SE region across multiple human adipose tissues. Integrated ATAC-seq and Hi-C analyses demonstrated progressive chromatin opening and dynamic enhancer–promoter interactions between this SE and the MIR1260B promoter during adipogenic differentiation. Functionally, miR-1260b overexpression markedly inhibited adipocyte differentiation of human adipose-derived stem cells. Quantitative proteomic profiling revealed that miR-1260b suppresses adipogenic and lipogenic programmes while activating lipid catabolic pathways. Notably, the MIR1260B-associated SE overlaps with a previously reported diabetes-associated SNP, and external clinical datasets indicate reduced miR-1260b levels in umbilical cord serum from children at increased risk of obesity. Together, these findings suggest that miR-1260b may function as a super-enhancer–associated regulator that inhibits adipogenesis and indicate that SE-informed multi-omics approaches can aid in identifying miRNA regulators relevant to metabolic disorders of obesity.KEYWORDS: Adipogenesis, miR-1260b, super-enhancer, proteomics, Human adipose stem cells

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a critical global health challenge, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden worldwide [1]. Its prevalence is a major concern as it is a significant risk factor for a vast array of diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and neurodegenerative disorders [2]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as pivotal epigenetic regulators, governing a vast spectrum of cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, stem cell self-renewal, and differentiation [3]. In the context of metabolism, miRNAs function as important regulators of energy balance and metabolic-immune homeostasis, and their dysregulation is increasingly linked to obesity and its associated diseases [4]. They exert critical control over mature adipocyte functions, such as lipolysis, glucose uptake, and insulin sensitivity [5,6], and act as key modulators of adipogenesis, capable of acting as either pro- or anti-adipogenic factors [7–10]. The therapeutic potential of miRNAs is underscored by preclinical studies that have demonstrated the feasibility of miRNA-based therapies for treating obesity and related metabolic disorders [11].

In addition, microRNA expression is frequently governed by upstream cis-regulatory elements. Super-enhancers (SEs) are regulatory elements formed by clusters of enhancers with high transcriptional activity and marked cell-type specificity [12–14]. The critical role of SE in cell-fate has been extensively characterized [15–19]. Crucially, a landmark study by Suzuki et al. revealed that SEs orchestrate cell-type-specific miRNA networks, demonstrating that SE-driven miRNAs (SE-miRNAs) are primary drivers of cellular identity [20]. This concept provides a conceptual framework for identifying key regulators of cell fate decisions. By focusing on SE-miRNAs, it may be possible to identify candidate regulators involved in adipogenesis, thereby providing a conceptual basis for exploring potential therapeutic targets in obesity.

Guided by the concept that SE-miRNAs are important regulators of cell fate, we identified miR‑1260b as a candidate miRNA associated with adipogenesis. In this study, we employed a combination of epigenetic, functional, and proteomic approaches to comprehensively investigate the role of miR‑1260b in adipogenesis of human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs). We first characterized the epigenetic landscape of its associated SE, revealing progressive chromatin opening and increased promoter contact during adipogenesis. We then demonstrated that experimental overexpression of miR‑1260b significantly inhibits adipogenic differentiation. At the molecular level, our proteomic analysis revealed that miR‑1260b was associated with coordinated modulation of adipogenic and lipid metabolic pathways. These findings suggest that miR-1260b may act as a regulatory factor involved in coordinating adipocyte differentiation and lipid metabolism.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and adipogenic differentiation

The hASCs were cultured in OriCell™ complete medium for human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (Cyagen Biosciences, Guangzhou, China; Cat. No. HUXMD-90011). The complete medium was prepared by combining the OriCell™ basal medium for cell culture (Cat. No. BHDM-03011) with the OriCell™ fetal bovine serum + culture supplement (Cat. No. HUXMD-05001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the supplement was thawed at 4°C for at least 6 hours, added to the basal medium, and the mixture was gently homogenized. The prepared complete medium was stored at 4°C and used within one month. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

For adipogenic differentiation, hASCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 in culture vessels and grown to 100% confluence. The growth medium was then replaced with adipogenic differentiation medium, which consisted of two solutions used alternately. Solution A (induction medium) was prepared by combining OriCell™ Basal Medium (Cat. No. BLDM-03011), OriCell™ Premium Fetal Bovine Serum (Cat. No. FBSSR-01021), and OriCell™ Supplements for Human Adipose-derived MSC Adipogenic Differentiation A-I (Cat. No. HUXMD-04031-a1) and A-II (Cat. No. HUXMD-04031-a2). Solution B (maintenance medium) was prepared by combining OriCell™ Basal Medium (Cat. No. BLDM-03011), OriCell™ Premium Fetal Bovine Serum (Cat. No. FBSSR-01021), and OriCell™ Supplements for Human Adipose-derived MSC Adipogenic Differentiation B (Cat. No. HUXMD-04031-b). All reagents were from Cyagen Biosciences. The differentiation protocol involved culturing cells in Solution A for 3 days, followed by Solution B for 1 day. This cycle was repeated for 7 days, with the medium changed every 2–3 days, until sufficient lipid droplet formation was observed.

To evaluate the effect of JQ1 on miR-1260b expression, the BET bromodomain inhibitor JQ1 (0.3 μM) was added to the adipogenic differentiation medium throughout the entire differentiation process, while an equivalent volume of DMSO was added to the control group under otherwise identical differentiation conditions. Cells were harvested on day 7 of adipogenic induction for subsequent qRT-PCR analysis.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Capital Institute of Pediatrics (protocol code SHERLLM2023051).

miRNA mimic and inhibitor transfection

Subconfluent hASCs were washed with PBS and incubated with trypsin/EDTA. The cells were then resuspended, pelleted at 500 g for 5 min, and resuspended in OriCell™ Basal Medium (Cat. No. HUXMD-90011, Cyagen). For transfection, hASCs were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well and allowed to reach approximately 60% confluence after about 24 hours. miR-1260b mimic, miR-1260b inhibitor, and a miRNA mimic normal control (NC) (Genepharma Corporation, Suzhou, China) were transfected using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Catalogue Number: 13,778,150, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with a final miRNA concentration of 50 nM. Following transfection, cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. RNA was extracted 36 hours post-transfection to assess transfection efficiency. The sequences used were as follows: miR-1260b mimic (sense: 5’-AUCCCACCACUGCCACCAU-3’, antisense: 5’-GGUGGCAGGUGGGAUUU-3’), and miR-1260b inhibitor (sense: 5’-AUGGUGGCAGUGGUGGGAU-3’). After the first transfection, cells were allowed to reach confluence before undergoing adipogenic induction. A second transfection was performed after 3 days of differentiation. After adipogenic induction was completed, cells were harvested for subsequent analyses of adipogenic markers at the mRNA, protein, and proteome levels.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

For the analysis of miR-1260b, total RNA was extracted from hASCs using Trizol reagent (Catalogue Number: 15,596,018, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis for miR-1260b was performed using the Super Script II Reverse Transcription Kit (Catalogue Number: 18,064,014, Invitrogen, USA), with the reverse transcription reaction specifically carried out using Bulge-Loop™ miRNA qRT-PCR Primers (RiboBio, China). Relative expression levels of miR-1260b were quantified using U6 (RiboBio, China) as the internal control. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed in triplicate on an ABI-7900HT Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Catalogue Number: RR036A, Takara, Dalian, China).

For the analysis of mRNA expression, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript II Reverse Transcription Kit (Catalogue Number: 18,064,014, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate using a DNA-free kit (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) and SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara, Japan). The following primers were used to amplify target genes: PPARG (forward: 5’-AGCCCTTCACTACTGTTGACTTCT-3’, reverse: 5’-TCCACTTTGATTGCACTTTGG-3’); ADIPOQ (forward: 5′-CTGGCTGAGTTGTGTGCCTGT-3′; reverse: 5′-GTCCCAATTTTGCTTTAGGGAA-3′); FABP4 (forward: 5′-CTTCCACGAGAGTTTATGAGAGAGC-3′; reverse: 5′-CAGAATGTTGTAGAGTTCAATGCGA-3′); and ACTB1 (forward: 5′-TCAAGATCATTGCTCCTCCTGAG-3′; reverse: 5′-ACATCTGCTGGAAGGTGGACA-3′), which served as the internal reference gene. The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Oil Red O staining

Following the adipogenic induction period, lipid droplet formation was assessed by Oil Red O staining. Cells were gently washed with 1× PBS (OriCell™, Cat. No. PBS-10001) twice and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes at room temperature. The Oil Red O working solution was freshly prepared by mixing the OriCell™ Oil Red O Solution (Cat. No. OILR-10001) with distilled water at a 3:2 ratio, followed by centrifugation at 250 × g for 4 minutes to collect the supernatant. Fixed cells were washed twice with PBS, incubated with the Oil Red O working solution for 30 minutes at room temperature, and then washed gently with PBS 2–3 times to remove excess stain. Stained cells were observed under a microscope, and images were captured for documentation.

Protein isolation and western blot analyses

The cultured cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed with 0.5% NP-40 buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Nonidet p-40, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 15 min, the supernatant (lysate) was collected for western blot analysis. The protein concentration of the lysate was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Catalogue Number: P0012, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, proteins were separated by 10% SDS polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, US). The membranes were then blocked in 5% BSA in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were against PPARG (Cat: #2430S, Cell Signalling), ADIPOQ (Cat: #2789S, Cell Signalling), FABP4 (Cat: #50699S, Cell Signalling), and GAPDH (Cat: #10494, Proteintech). After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The immunoblots were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Catalogue Number: P0018S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and a ChemiDoc XRS system with Quantity One Software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

Protein extraction and digestion

Samples were lysed in a buffer that consisted of 1% sodium deoxycholate, 100 mM Tris Hydrochloride, pH 8.8, 10 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine, 40 mM 2-chloroacetamide (CAA) for 5 min, 95°C. The preliminary lysates were sonicated under a suitable condition. The lysates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were reserved as whole cell extract (WCE). The protein concentration was determined by NanoDrop One. Approximately 40 μg of WCE were digested with sequencing grade trypsin (V5280, Promega) that cleaves at the C-terminus of the Arg or Lys residues at 37°C.

Mass spectrometry analysis

MS samples were analysed on Q Exactive HF Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (MS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) interfaced with an Easy-nLC 1200 nanoflow LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, the samples were redissolved in 30 µl of Solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water), and one fifth of the reconstituted samples were loaded onto a homemade reversed-phase C18 column (1 cm × 150 μm; particle size, 3 μm; pore size, 300 Å) and then separated by a 150 μm × 30 cm silica microcolumn (homemade; particle size, 1.9 μm; pore size, 120 Å) with a linear gradient of 5–35% Mobile Phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 600 nl/min for 150 min. A data-dependent strategy was used by measuring MS1 in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 120,000 followed by tandem MS scans of the top 30 precursors using higher-energy collision dissociation with 27% of normalized collision energy and 30 s of dynamic exclusion time. Trypsin digests of 293T cells as quality control samples were routinely assayed to ensure good sensitivity and reproducibility.

Protein identification and quantification

The MS data files were processed using Proteome Discoverer version 2.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). MS/MS spectra were searched by MASCOT engine against a NCBI human reference proteome database (contains 32,015 protein entries, modified on 01/07/2013). The search parameters were as follows: Enzyme specificity was set to trypsin with two missed cleavages being allowed. Fixed modification was carbamidomethyl on cysteine; variable modifications were oxidation of methionine, and acetylation of the protein N-terminus. Precursor mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm; fragment mass tolerance was 50 mmu. Maximum false discovery rate (FDR) for protein and peptide identification was set to 1%. A label-free, intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) approach was used to calculate protein quantification based on the area under the curve (AUC) of precursor ions. The fraction of total (FOT) was used to represent the normalized abundance of a protein across experiments. The FOT was defined as a protein’s iBAQ divided by the total iBAQ of all identified proteins in one experiment. The FOT was further multiplied by 105 to obtain iFOT for the ease of representation. Missing values were substituted with zeros.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

SEs were obtained from SEdb 2.0 (http://www.licpathway.net/sedb), which contains 1.7 million SEs from 2670 human and mouse samples collected from GEO/SRA, ENCODE, Roadmap, GGR, and NGDC GSA. All SEs were identified using a unified computational pipeline and standardized parameters.

To identify enriched transcription factor (TF) binding motifs within the SE regions(chr11:96136449–96,343,241), we employed the HOMER software package (v4.11). The identified motifs were compared against the HOMER motif database to determine the most likely TFs associated with the SEs, using a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and annotated the results with the JASPAR database.

The ATAC-seq data used in this study were generated from hASCs during adipogenic differentiation [21]. Intermediate data processing and analysis followed our previously published workflows, using the same computational procedures except that sequencing reads were aligned to the hg38 human reference genome in the present study. ATAC-seq signal tracks were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV), focusing on the genomic interval chr11:96136449–96,343,241 to assess chromatin accessibility dynamics at the candidate regulatory region. We compared ATAC-seq profiles across multiple time points during adipogenic differentiation, with cells collected at 0 h, 2 h, 8 h, and 24 h following induction.

Hi-C data generated from hASCs at four stages of adipogenic differentiation (−2 d, 0 d, 1 d, and 3 d) were used to investigate long-range chromatin interactions involving the MIR1260B promoter. The Hi-C dataset used in this analysis was obtained from the GEO accession GSE109924. Interaction maps were visualized using the 3D-Genome Interaction Viewer and Database (3DIV; https://www.kobic.kr/3div/) to assess potential contacts between the MIR1260B promoter and distal genomic regions.

For quantitative proteomic analysis, three independent biological replicates were included for each experimental condition. Similarly, for quantitative real-time PCR analysis of both mRNA and miRNA expression, each experimental group consisted of three independent biological replicates. The statistical significance of this enrichment was determined by Fisher’s exact test, and the pathways with an FDR threshold of 0.05 were regarded as significantly regulated. Differentially expressed proteins were identified by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± SD. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All other analyses were performed in R (version 4.0.5). The spatial separation of samples was identified by performing the principal component analysis (PCA) using the R package factoextra. Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis was based on DAVID (the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery) Bioinformatics Resources [22].

Results

A putative SE is associated with the MIR1260B locus

To search for adipose-related miRNAs potentially regulated by SEs, we analysed adipose-related tissue datasets within the SEdb 2.0 database [23] and found that MIR1260B is associated with multiple SEs across independent adipose tissue samples. This search revealed 24 adipose-associated SEs that were markedly clustered within a single genomic region on chromosome 11. We defined the union of these clustered regions (chr11:96136449– 96343241) as a candidate SE for MIR1260B (Figure 1(A)). To explore its regulatory potential, we performed TF binding motif analysis using HOMER and annotated the results with the JASPAR database. Following statistical filtering (p < 10−8), based on the predicted TF binding motifs, the region was enriched for their binding motifs, and we constructed a regulatory network linking transcription factors to the candidate SE (Figure 1(B)). Notably, this network included several TFs known to be critical for adipogenesis and adipose tissue function, such as KLF6 [24], MAZ [25], PATZ1 [26], WT1 [27], and ZNF263 [28] further supporting its potential role as a regulatory sequence governing adipogenesis and adipocyte function.

A putative SE is associated with MIR1260B during adipogenesis. (A) Genomic landscape of a predicted SE located near the MIR1260B locus in adipose tissue. (B) A regulatory network illustrating the predicted transcription factors (TFs) that bind to the MIR1260B-associated SE region. (C) Genome browser view of ATAC-seq signal density at the MIR1260B associated SE region during human adipose-derived stem cell (hASC) adipogenesis (0 h, 2 h, 8 h, and 24 h).

In addition, the activity of an SE is dependent on an open chromatin state. We therefore assessed the chromatin accessibility of the candidate SE during adipogenesis by analysing our previously published ATAC-seq data [21] from adipogenesis of hASCs. A comparison of different time points following adipogenic induction (0 h, 2 h, 8 h, and 24 h) revealed a progressive increase in chromatin accessibility at the SE region from early to late stages of differentiation (Figure 1(C)). This dynamic increase suggests that the SE may provide an open cis-regulatory platform for MIR1260B during adipogenesis.

Dynamic chromatin looping connects the MIR1260B promoter to its distal SE during adipogenesis

To investigate whether the candidate SE near MIR1260B can functionally interact with its promoter, we visualized Hi-C data from hASCs [29] at several stages of adipogenesis (−2d, 0d, 1d, and 3d). Our analysis revealed a significant reorganization of the chromatin architecture at the MIR1260B locus. Specifically, the topology of the encompassing topologically associating domain (TAD) was dynamically remodelled during differentiation, suggesting an active restructuring of this genomic region (Figure 2(A–D)). Concurrently, we observed a marked increase in chromatin interactions anchored at the MIR1260B promoter. This progressive enhancement of promoter contacts implies a strengthening of regulatory connections as differentiation proceeds.

(A-D) Chromatin interaction maps centered on the MIR1260B promoter in hASCs at −2d (A), 0d (B), 1d (C), and 3d (D) of adipogenic induction. In the interaction heatmaps, blue triangles indicate topologically associating domains (TADs). The interaction frequency is represented by blue bars (bias-removed) and magenta dots (distance-normalized). Arc-diagrams, anchored by the MIR1260B promoter (indicated by the magenta bar), illustrate significant chromatin loops defined by a threshold of distance-normalized interaction frequency ≥2.5.

To validate this observation and pinpoint the specific interacting region, we performed a differential analysis comparing day 3 to day 0. A global view of the TAD confirmed a significant increase in overall internal interaction frequency by day 3 (Figure 3(A)). More importantly, a focused analysis revealed a striking and specific increase in interaction frequency between the MIR1260B promoter and the distal region harbouring the predicted SE (Chr11:96130000-96345000) (Figure 3(B)). This specific enhancement of the enhancer-promoter loop during adipogenesis provides strong evidence that the SE dynamically regulates MIR1260B by establishing direct physical contact with its promoter.

JQ1 is a BET bromodomain inhibitor frequently used as a pharmacological tool to modulate SE– associated regulatory activity. Through displacement of BRD4 from active enhancers and SEs and reduction of enhancer-associated transcription [30,31], JQ1 may serve as an indirect means of assessing SE– dependent gene expression. In this context, we treated hASCs undergoing adipogenic differentiation with JQ1 (0.3 μM) and observed a significant reduction in miR‑1260b expression at day 7 compared with the untreated control group, as determined by qRT-PCR (Figure 3(C)). This decrease in MIR1260B expression is consistent with a potential regulatory contribution of the candidate SE to MIR1260B transcription during adipogenesis, while remaining indirect evidence.

miR‑1260b acts as a negative regulator of adipogenic differentiation

Given the observed association between MIR1260B and the candidate SE, we next explored the possible functional role of miR‑1260b in adipogenesis. To do this, we performed loss-of-function and gain-of-function experiments in hASCs. qRT-PCR confirmed that miR‑1260b mimic transfection achieved high transfection efficiency, resulting in markedly elevated miR‑1260b levels compared with the NC (Figure 4(A)). Adipogenic differentiation was characterized on day 7 following induction. The impact of miR‑1260b on adipogenesis was then assessed by Oil Red O staining. Striking morphological differences were observed among the groups. Overexpression of miR‑1260b resulted in a visible reduction in both the number and size of lipid droplets. Conversely, inhibition of endogenous miR‑1260b, did not lead to a significant increase in lipid accumulation compared to the NC group (Figure 4(B)).

(B) Representative images of Oil Red O staining in hASCs transfected with NC, miR‑1260b mimic, or miR‑1260b inhibitor during adipogenesis. Scale bars represent 100 µm. (C, D) qRT-PCR (C) and Western Blot (D) analyses of key adipogenic markers (PPARG, ADIPOQ, and FABP4) in the three groups. Data represent the mean ± SD. To corroborate these findings at the molecular level, we examined the expression of key adipogenic markers. Both qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed largely concordant patterns: miR‑1260b overexpression markedly reduced the mRNA and protein levels of PPARG and ADIPOQ, whereas FABP4 exhibited a pronounced decrease at the mRNA level but only minimal change at the protein level. In line with the phenotypic data, inhibition of miR‑1260b did not result in a marked upregulation of these same markers compared to the NC group (Figure 4(C,D)). Taken together, these results demonstrate that miR‑1260b functions as a potent negative regulator of adipogenic differentiation, acting at both the phenotypic and molecular levels.

miR‑1260b orchestrates a global reprogramming of the cellular proteome

Given that proteins are the direct functional effectors of cellular processes, and that miRNA-mediated regulation ultimately manifests at the protein level, a systematic proteomic assessment is necessary to more comprehensively characterize downstream molecular effects. Quantitative mass spectrometry – based proteomics is considered to provide a broad and relatively unbiased evaluation of protein expression, owing to its dynamic range, quantitative precision, and independence from antibody specificity [32,33]. Accordingly, to delineate the molecular consequences of miR-1260b modulation, we performed an unbiased quantitative proteomic analysis on hASCs following adipogenic induction, analysing cells at day 7 after transfection with an miR-1260b mimic, an inhibitor, or a NC. Our analysis yielded high-confidence identifications for 7064 protein groups (GPs), each quantified with at least one unique peptide. The number of GPs identified in the NC, mimic, and inhibitor groups was 5088, 4747, and 4930, respectively (Figure 5(A)). The relative abundance of these proteins spanned approximately eight orders of magnitude, highlighting the substantial proteomic alterations in adipogenesis induced by miR-1260b (Figure 5(B)).

To assess the global impact of miR‑1260b modulation, we performed PCA. This analysis revealed that the NC, mimic, and inhibitor groups formed three distinct and well-separated clusters based on their protein expression profiles (Figure 5(C)), demonstrating that both overexpression and inhibition of miR‑1260b induce significant, global alterations in the adipocyte proteome.

We then identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) (p < 0.05; fold change >1.5) by comparing the mimic group to the NC group and the mimic group to the inhibitor group. Compared to the NC, miR‑1260b overexpression resulted in 944 upregulated and 980 downregulated proteins (Figure 5(D)). Compared to the inhibitor group, overexpression led to 271 upregulated and 413 downregulated proteins (Figure 5(E)). To pinpoint the most reliable core targets of miR‑1260b, we identified the intersection of these two DEP sets. A Venn diagram showed an overlap of 423 proteins that were consistently altered in both comparisons (Figure 5(F)). This core protein set, consisting of 181 proteins consistently upregulated and 226 consistently downregulated (Figure 5(G)), represents the central regulatory network governed by miR‑1260b.

To elucidate the biological functions of this core protein set, we performed KEGG and GO pathway enrichment analyses. The enrichment analysis revealed a clear dual regulatory role for miR‑1260b. It strongly activated pathways associated with lipid catabolism and metabolism, including Metabolism of lipids, Lysosome, and Cholesterol metabolism. Concurrently, miR‑1260b suppressed several cellular stress-response pathways, most notably the KEAP1-NFE2L2 pathway and Nuclear events mediated by NFE2L2 (Figure 5(H)).

To further explore the potential functional implications of the proteomic alterations, we examined differentially expressed proteins identified in our dataset with particular attention to those involved in lipid metabolism. Our results revealed a coordinated shift in proteins related to lipid handling. Specifically, miR‑1260b overexpression led to the significant downregulation of key proteins essential for adipogenesis and lipogenesis. This included FABP4 and FABP3, which are crucial for intracellular fatty acid transport and are hallmarks of mature adipocytes [34–36], as well as core lipogenic enzymes such as ACLY (ATP-citrate lyase) and ME1 (malic enzyme 1) that provide the necessary building blocks and reducing power for fatty acid synthesis [37–40]. Concurrently, there was a significant upregulation of proteins central to lipid catabolism. Notably, ABHD5 (α/β hydrolase domain-containing 5), a potent co-activator of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) that initiates triglyceride breakdown [41–43], was strongly induced. Furthermore, enzymes critical for mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, including HADHA and HADHB, were also upregulated [44–46].

In summary, our proteomic analysis not only confirms the profound reprogramming of the adipocyte proteome by miR‑1260b but also uncovers the molecular basis for its dual function: suppressing adipogenic differentiation by downregulating associated proteins while promoting lipid catabolism by upregulating metabolic pathways.

Discussion

Overexpression of miR‑1260b significantly suppresses adipogenesis, accompanied by reduced lipid accumulation and decreased expression of core adipogenic regulators, indicating that miR-1260b may participate in regulatory processes within the differentiation programme. Notably, inhibition of miR-1260b did not produce comparably robust effects under the present conditions. Such asymmetry between gain- and loss-of-function phenotypes is frequently observed in miRNA studies, where loss-of-function effects tend to be modest or context-dependent, potentially due to limited endogenous expression levels, network robustness and compensatory redundancy, as well as the threshold and nonlinear characteristics of miRNA-mediated regulation [47–49]. Therefore, the relatively mild loss-of-function phenotype may reflect context-dependent regulatory roles of miR-1260b.

Our quantitative proteomic analysis revealed that miR-1260b induces system-wide alterations at the proteome level, with metabolic-related functional modules showing relative enrichment and coordinated changes, suggesting a potential role in modulating the lipid metabolic network. Of note, both the KEAP1–NFE2L2 pathway and the gene set ‘Nuclear events mediated by NFE2L2’ were significantly downregulated among the differentially expressed proteins, suggesting attenuation of the NRF2 transcriptional axis. As NFE2L2 (NRF2) represents the central transcription factor of this pathway and the annotated nuclear events largely reflect ARE-dependent transcription following NRF2 nuclear translocation, their concurrent downregulation is consistent with reduced NRF2-mediated transcriptional activity under the present conditions. Previous studies indicate that NRF2 contributes to redox homeostasis and lipid metabolic regulation in adipose tissue and facilitates adipocyte differentiation, at least in part by promoting PPARG transcription. Its activity is tightly regulated by KEAP1-dependent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby modulating downstream antioxidant and metabolic gene programmes [50,51]. In this context, the observed suppression of KEAP1–NFE2L2 signalling may reflect reduced NRF2-dependent transcriptional output associated with miR-1260b – mediated modulation of adipogenic and metabolic programmes, potentially involving PPARγ-related mechanisms [52].

Further examination of selected metabolism-related proteins was consistent with the observed metabolic shift, with key components of the de novo lipogenesis (DNL) pathway, including ACLY and ME1, showing coordinated downregulation [37–40], alongside adipogenic markers FABP4 and FABP3 [34–36]. In striking contrast, miR‑1260b simultaneously activated the lipolysis and fatty acid β-oxidation pathways. This was driven by the upregulation of ABHD5, the co-activator for lipolysis-initiating ATGL [41–43], and the mitochondrial β-oxidation enzymes HADHA and HADHB [44–46]. Together, these observations suggest miR‑1260b as a modulator of metabolic programming that simultaneously suppresses lipid synthesis while promoting lipid catabolism.

Although miRNAs hold great promise as therapeutics due to their druggability, identifying functionally relevant candidates remains a major challenge [53]. Our findings indicate that integrating SE annotation with multi-omics data can aid in identifying functionally relevant miRNAs. Consistent SE association and promoter contact of MIR1260B, together with functional perturbation data, support a potential SE – MIR1260B regulatory link and its involvement in adipogenic and lipid metabolic programmes. Moreover, within the SE region identified in our analysis, we found a previously reported disease-associated SNP in the MIR1260B regulatory locus that has been linked to type 2 diabetes risk [54], and independent clinical data show significantly reduced miR‑1260b levels in umbilical cord serum from children at high risk of developing obesity later in life [55]. These genetic and human biomarker observations provide supportive and correlative context for the potential biological relevance of miR‑1260b. They indicate that SE-informed, multi-omics – integrated approaches may facilitate the identification of miRNA regulators with possible clinical context. With further validation in longitudinal and functionally characterized human studies, such findings may offer preliminary insights relevant to metabolic disease research and future exploratory applications.

Adipogenesis is essential for adipose tissue development and metabolic homeostasis, and its dysregulation is closely associated with obesity-related complications. In this study, we identify miR-1260b as a SE– associated regulator of human adipogenesis. Functional and proteomic analyses indicate that miR‑1260b overexpression suppresses adipogenic differentiation and is associated with coordinated changes in lipid metabolic pathways. Collectively, these findings suggest that miR-1260b participates in the regulation of adipocyte differentiation and metabolic programming, providing additional insight into adipose regulatory networks and a framework for further investigation of the SE– MIR1260B axis in metabolic disease contexts.
