Longitudinal ctDNA tracking by low-pass whole-genome sequencing predicts CAR-T outcomes in B-cell lymphomas
Authors: Sijun Zhang, Honghan Qiao, Feiyang Zong, Yukai Duan, Renjie Hua, Qingjiang Chen, Xudong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCurrent PET/CT monitoring for relapsed/refractory B-cell lymphomas treated with CAR-T therapy often lacks specificity. We evaluated longitudinal low-pass whole-genome sequencing (LP-WGS) of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in 11 patients with B-cell lymphoma receiving axicabtagene ciloleucel to assess treatment response and prognosis. We found that lower baseline tumor fraction correlated with complete remission (CR), and ctDNA positivity at baseline or 1-month post-infusion significantly predicted inferior survival outcomes. Combining ctDNA with PET/CT refined risk stratification, distinguishing high-risk patients more accurately than imaging alone. Furthermore, specific genomic features, such as 17p deletion and high copy number variation burden, were associated with poor prognosis. We conclude that LP-WGS of ctDNA shows promise as a complementary tool to PET/CT for response evaluation and risk stratification in CAR-T-treated B-cell lymphomas, highlighting its potential to guide personalized clinical management in lymphoma care.

Introduction

Refractory or relapsed (R/R) B-cell lymphoma is associated with poor therapeutic response and prognosis.1 Although chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy has transformed the treatment landscape for R/R B-cell lymphoma by yielding unprecedented response rates,2,3,4,5,6 many patients do not achieve complete remission (CR) and can eventually relapse. Furthermore, immune-mediated inflammatory responses can induce pseudoprogression, presenting as an apparent increase in lesion size on imaging.7 Accurate response assessment following CAR-T therapy is, therefore, critical to guide clinical decision-making.

Although fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) offers high sensitivity, its specificity is limited as FDG-avid lesions may reflect tumor, infection, or inflammation. While tissue biopsy remains the gold standard for confirming disease status, it suffers from significant clinical limitations, including procedural invasiveness, anatomical accessibility constraints for certain lesions, lack of repeatability, and potential sampling bias due to tumor heterogeneity. By contrast, the detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in the plasma provides a promising, minimally invasive, repeatable, accurate, and cost-effective approach for real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy.8,9,10,11 Indeed, quantitative analysis of ctDNA has been shown to correlate closely with tumor burden in B-cell lymphoma.10,12,13

Liquid biopsy approaches can be challenging in patients who achieve CR or partial response (PR) after multiple lines of therapy, as reduced tumor burden may result in undetectable ctDNA levels. To enable mutation detection in such low-burden settings, most studies have focused on increasing sequencing depth across selected high-yield target regions.10,12,14,15 In parallel, others have explored whole-genome sequencing (WGS) strategies, which prioritize breadth over depth to improve sensitivity for low-level ctDNA detection.16,17,18,19 As chromosomal aneuploidy is a hallmark of malignancy,20,21 such approaches enable the detection of copy number variations (CNVs) in patients with B-cell lymphoma.

Although these methodological strategies aim to overcome technical barriers, their clinical utility for treatment monitoring remains under investigation. Low-pass whole-genome sequencing (LP-WGS) has emerged as a relatively cost-efficient way to assess tumor burden and identify CNVs in plasma without knowledge of tumor mutations. Previous studies have shown that tumor burden in plasma determined by LP-WGS was highly consistent with that derived from targeted sequencing.22 While prior studies have established the prognostic value of ctDNA prior to CAR-T therapy,18 its utility for dynamic monitoring during treatment remains poorly defined. Here, we aimed to evaluate the use of LP-WGS to detect and monitor ctDNA in patients with B-cell lymphoma undergoing CD19 CAR-T therapy.

We next evaluated the circulating CAR-T cells' expansion dynamics for all 11 patients (Figure S2). Circulating CAR-T cells were monitored by flow cytometry at 1- to 3-day intervals during the first month after infusion. Dynamics of ctDNA during therapy correlate with disease response

We next investigated whether dynamic changes in ctDNA levels during treatment correlated with clinical outcomes. Tumor fraction (TF), derived from LP-WGS of cfDNA, was log10-transformed to calculate PRD, a surrogate for molecular minimal residual disease (MRD) that we use to infer residual tumor burden from plasma samples. Notably, quantifiable TF values were successfully obtained from all 33 samples (100%), with the median TF values of 0.154% (range, 0.038%–22%) at baseline, 0.091% (range, 0.035%–22%) at M1, and 0.114% (range, 0.046%–18.18%) at M3. The median TF value decreased following CAR-T infusion compared to pre-lymphodepletion. Patients who achieved CR at the last assessment had significantly lower baseline TF levels compared to those who did not (p = 0.045). They also tended to have lower peak TF levels during treatment (p = 0.036). Patient 7 was a particularly interesting case, who was diagnosed with low-grade follicular lymphoma and received four cycles of obinutuzumab plus bendamustine. Interim PET/CT after two cycles showed PR, but the scan after cycle four revealed a marked increase in the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) in a retroperitoneal soft tissue mass. Due to the lesion’s location, percutaneous biopsy was not feasible. Suspecting disease progression, autologous lymphocytes were collected for CAR-T manufacturing 1 month after stopping bendamustine. The patient exhibited robust in vivo CAR-T expansion, peaking at 53.16 cells/μL on day 15 (Figure 3J). PET/CT at M1 indicated SD, and the patient received local radiotherapy (24 Gy in 12 fractions). At M3, PET/CT showed stable SUVmax but increasing lesion size, leading to a PD classification. By month 7, both SUVmax and lesion size had further increased, prompting laparoscopic resection. Histopathological analysis of the resected mass revealed only inflammatory changes, with no evidence of lymphoma (Figure 3J). Throughout the course of treatment, serial TF measurements remained stable, and the patient was PRD− at baseline, M1, and M3.

Complex CNVs associated with poor outcome

We evaluated the association between ctDNA-detected CNVs and clinical outcomes. No abnormal CNVs were detected in 2 of the 11 patients at both baseline and M3. Moreover, CNV-positive and CNV-negative patients showed no statistically significant differences in OS, PFS, or EFS at either baseline (PFS, p = 0.29; OS, p = 0.49; EFS, p = 0.15; Figures S5A–S5C) or M3 (PFS, p = 0.29; OS, p = 0.49; EFS, p = 0.48; Figures S5D–S5F).

At baseline, 9 of 11 patients (82%) showed abnormal CNVs, and 5 of them (45%) had complex CNVs, defined as three or more chromosomal abnormalities. While median PFS was not reached for the overall cohort, patients with complex CNVs had a median PFS of only 2 months. Several recurrent CNVs were observed in the cohort (Figure 4A). Alterations involving 17p were detected in 7 of 11 patients (64%). Among these, two patients (29%) had a 17p deletion, which was significantly associated with inferior OS (p = 0.008; Figure 4D). Additional recurrent CNVs included gains on 1p, 10q, 15q, 18p, 19p, 19q, and 22q, and losses on 6p, 12q.

Among the four patients who experienced disease progression after CAR-T reinfusion, gains on 1p, 5p, and 18p and loss of 12q were the most frequently observed abnormalities. By contrast, among the seven patients who did not progress, the most common CNVs included 6p loss, 19p gain, 19q gain, and 22q gain. Patients carrying more than 11 CNVs had significantly worse OS compared to those carrying 11 or fewer CNVs (p = 0.044; Figure 4E).

Discussion

Despite the growing use of ctDNA monitoring in hematologic malignancies, standardized approaches for residual disease detection during CAR-T therapy remain lacking. This study aimed to evaluate LP-WGS as a minimally invasive, quantitative tool to dynamically assess treatment response, genomic complexity, and clinical outcomes following commercial CD19 CAR-T cell therapy for B-cell lymphoma. Most patients had received multiple prior lines of therapy and exhibited high-risk features, including an International Prognostic Index (IPI) score ≥3, Ann Arbor stage Ⅲ or IV disease, double-expressor phenotype, high-grade B-cell lymphoma, or TP53 mutation. The 1-year PFS, OS, and EFS rates were 64%, 82%, and 45%, respectively. Median EFS was 3 months, while median PFS and OS were not reached. These outcomes are consistent with previously reported efficacy of axicabtagene ciloleucel in similar populations.23,24,25,26 A prior study reported a median time to peak CAR-T cell expansion of 10 days and a median peak count of 35.55 cells/μL,27 which aligns closely with our findings. We did not, however, observe significant differences in survival outcomes between patients with high versus low CAR-T expansion, which may be due to the limited sample size.

Our study demonstrates that monitoring ctDNA offers a valuable tool for evaluating treatment efficacy and predicting outcomes during CAR-T therapy. In our cohort, patients who achieved CR at final assessment had lower baseline TF levels and lower TF peaks during treatment. This aligns with previous findings that baseline ctDNA reflects tumor burden, with lower levels predicting a more favorable prognosis.12,28 Moreover, patients with PRD+ status had significantly worse OS, PFS, and EFS than those with PRD− status. Among patients who remained PRD− at all three timepoints, 78% achieved sustained remission, and the remaining 22% attained CR after salvage therapy. By contrast, patients with persistently elevated TF values (and classified as PRD+) after infusion had notably poor outcomes.

In rare cases, severe infections may dilute ctDNA. In one case (patient 4), TF levels declined substantially at month 3 compared with month 1. This may be attributable to concurrent COVID-19 infection and acute bowel perforation due to tumor invasion, which led to septic shock. The severe infection likely caused a marked increase in cfDNA, diluting ctDNA levels and resulting in an outlier TF value.29

Our study is the first to integrate ctDNA detected by LP-WGS with PET/CT, revealing the potential of ctDNA to stratify risk in PET/CT-positive patients during CAR-T cell therapy. Although end-of-treatment PET/CT is prognostic for PFS and OS, its positive predictive value for disease progression or death at 2.5 years is <50%, and the negative predictive value is only 84%.13 Furthermore, 20% of patients in remission eventually relapse or died after therapy.13 PET/CT lacks sufficient specificity to reliably distinguish active lymphoma from inflammatory tumor flare (pseudoprogression), a phenomenon often observed with immune-activating therapies.30

In one case (patient 7), PET/CT after infusion indicated SD or PD, prompting local radiotherapy. Histopathological analysis performed 7 months later, however, confirmed only benign inflammatory changes. Throughout this period, TF levels remained stable, and PRD status was consistently negative. This case illustrates the value of incorporating ctDNA analysis to distinguish pseudoprogression from true disease progression. This case highlights the limitations of relying solely on PET/CT for response assessment when biopsy is not feasible. The discordance between radiographic, pathological, and ctDNA findings underscores the added value of multimodal monitoring, with ctDNA providing complementary specificity to PET/CT during CAR-T therapy.

Consistent with previous reports,28,31 we found that ctDNA can also further stratify PET/CT-positive patients: those who were both PET/CT+ and PRD+ exhibited significantly poorer OS compared to the PET/CT+/PRD− subgroup. However, the combination of PET/CT and ctDNA did not yield statistically significant stratification for PFS and EFS among PET/CT-positive patients in our cohort. Frank et al.31 utilized IgNGS to demonstrate that ctDNA positivity at M1 could successfully stratify risk in patients with PR or SD PET/CT results, predicting significantly worse PFS. Although our study utilized a different method and did not reach statistical significance in stratifying PFS and EFS results within PET/CT-positive subgroups, the observed trends align with their conclusions. We postulate several explanations for this discrepancy. First, in real-world clinical practice, patients with PET/CT-positive findings after CAR-T infusion typically receive aggressive consolidation therapies, which may confound EFS comparisons. In addition, the limited sample size may have restricted the ability to detect differences among PET/CT-positive patients using ctDNA. Therefore, future prospective and multi-center trials with larger cohorts are warranted to further validate the utility of ctDNA combined with PET/CT for prognostic stratification.

In our study, patients with complex CNVs exhibited poorer prognosis at both baseline and 1 month after CAR-T infusion. Patients carrying >11 CNVs also exhibited inferior survival. These findings suggest that CNV burden may hold prognostic significance, although validation in larger, prospective cohorts is needed. The deletion of 17p was associated with particularly poor outcomes, consistent with prior findings by Zhao et al.22 This association is likely attributable to alterations in the TP53 gene located on 17p, as TP53 disruption is a well-established adverse prognostic factor in hematologic malignancies.32 While CNV-positive patients generally tended to have poorer outcomes than CNV-negative patients,32 this trend did not reach statistical significance in our cohort, likely due to small sample size.

In NGS-based ctDNA studies of lymphoma, most research utilizes immunosequening or hybrid-capture NGS technologies. ctDNA levels are typically quantified as haploid genome equivalents per milliliter of plasma (hGE/mL). Ideally, thresholds are defined based on the assay’s limit of detection, with detectable ctDNA indicating MRD positivity and undetectable ctDNA indicating MRD negativity.31,33 Thresholds can also be derived from dynamic changes in ctDNA during treatment. For example, patients showing a 2-log reduction in ctDNA after one cycle are considered to have an early molecular response, while a 2.5-log reduction after two cycles defines a major molecular response.28 In contrast to these targeted approaches, LP-WGS-based ctDNA monitoring studies in lymphoma remain limited, and quantification methods are not standardized.18,34,35 As a result, we could not apply previously published thresholds to our data. Although we established a PRD threshold within this study, the small sample size precluded validation in an independent cohort. Larger studies are needed to confirm and standardize threshold values for clinical use.

In conclusion, our findings support the potential of LP-WGS as a feasible and clinically applicable method for ctDNA monitoring in lymphoma. Serial ctDNA assessment before and after CAR-T cell infusion enables treatment response evaluation and outcome prediction. ctDNA also provides additional risk stratification among PET/CT-positive patients. Furthermore, patients harboring 17p deletion or more than 11 CNVs had worse survival outcomes, highlighting the prognostic relevance of genomic complexity.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, there is currently no standardized clinical consensus on the optimal thresholds for defining PRD positivity or negativity across various ctDNA detection platforms. While we identified a potential threshold through statistical analysis, the relatively small size of our cohort may limit the generalizability of these findings. Therefore, larger independent cohorts are required to validate this threshold. Second, our study collected blood samples at a limited number of timepoints. Future longitudinal studies with more frequent and high-density ctDNA sampling are warranted to identify optimal monitoring windows and to further confirm the predictive utility of ctDNA for early disease relapse in CAR-T cell therapy.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, Henan Province (2023-KY-0869). All the processes conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki. This is an observational, single-center cohort study with prospective clinical follow-up. Between June 2023 and May 2024, a total of 36 patients with B-cell lymphoma, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, transformed follicular lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, and high-grade B-cell lymphoma, received CAR-T therapy at The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Of these, 16 patients received commercial CD19 CAR-T products and were initially screened for this study, while the remaining patients received investigational CAR-T products. To ensure cohort homogeneity and data completeness, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) receipt of CAR-T products other than axicabtagene ciloleucel (Axi-cel), and (2) early mortality resulting in incomplete follow-up (death before or within 3 months of infusion). The procedures of patient selection are displayed in Figure S6. Consequently, a final cohort of 11 patients was identified, consisting of 5 males and 6 females. At the time of CAR-T cells infusion, the median age of the cohort was 55 years (range, 36–74). All participants in our study were of Chinese ethnicity with East Asian ancestry. Ultimately, LP-WGS detection was performed exclusively on the 33 plasma samples collected from these 11 patients. Clinical and treatment-related data were collected throughout the course of therapy.

Method details

Sample collection and processing

Peripheral blood samples were collected at predefined time points after informed consents: (I) before initiation of the lymphodepleting conditioning regimen (baseline); (II) one month after CAR-T cell infusion (M1); and (III) three months after infusion (M3). Blood was drawn into EDTA tubes from the same venipuncture used for routine clinical sampling. Samples were centrifuged to isolate plasma and stored at −80 °C within 6 h of collection.

DNA extraction

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from 4 to 10 mL of plasma using the HiPure Circulating DNA Midi Kit B (Magen). A total of 10 ng of cfDNA was used to prepare sequencing libraries using the Rapid Plus DNA Lib Prep Kit for MGI V2 (ABclonal), with AFTMag NGS DNA Clean Beads (ABclonal) for purification, and Truncated DNA Adapter (UDI) Kit for MGI (ABclona) for ligation, followed by PCR amplification. Library concentration was quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientifi) or Qubit ssDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Purified libraries were sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000RS platform (MGI Tech) using massively parallel sequencing with MGISEQ-2000RS High-throughput Sequencing Reagent Kit (MGI Tech) and CPAS Barcode Primer 3 Kit (MGI Tech). Approximately 4 Gb of raw sequencing data per sample were filtered and aligned to the human reference genome hg19.

LP-WGS

CNVs were identified using a customized workflow called Ultrasensitive Chromosomal Aneuploidy Detection (UCAD). The cyclic binary segmentation (CBS) algorithm, implemented in the R package DNAcopy (v1.72.0), was utilized to identify significant genomic breakpoints and segments exhibiting copy number variations. For each sample, a minimum of 10 million paired-end reads were collected. These readings were compared to the human reference genome hg19 by BWA, and the average coverage per 200 kilobase pair dataset of the genome was determined utilizing the Samtools (v1.18) software. Raw data quality control was performed using FastQC (v0.12.1). The average coverage across all datasets was normalized using Equation 1, yielding the Z score, which serves as a metric for assessing the stability of individual chromosomes. A Z score exceeding 3 indicates amplification, whereas a Z score below 3 suggests deletion. The tumor fraction (TF), representing the proportion of tumor-derived chromosomal abnormalities, was used to estimate tumor burden. Response assessments

Response assessments were conducted based on imaging scans obtained at pre-lymphodepletion, and at 1 and 3 months post-infusion, according to the Lugano Criteria for Response Assessment.37 These time points were concurrent with peripheral blood samples collection. Further assessments were conducted throughout the follow-up period. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from lymphodepletion to the first occurrence of disease progression, relapse, or death from any cause. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the time from lymphodepletion to the earliest occurrence of disease progression, initiation of new anti-lymphoma therapy, or death. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from lymphodepletion to death from any cause.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented descriptively as medians or proportions. TF values were log10-transformed to normalize the data distribution. As TF increases, peripheral blood–based residual disease (PRD) increases, based on the relationship PRD = log10(TF). The optimal PRD cutoff for response prediction was determined using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, with 2000 bootstrap resamples for validation. PRD positivity (PRD+) was defined as a TF above the 0.63% threshold (i.e., PRD > −2.2). Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate PFS, EFS, and OS, and survival differences between patient groups were evaluated using the log rank test. Associations between categorical variables and clinical outcomes were assessed using Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 and RStudio 4.4.2. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
