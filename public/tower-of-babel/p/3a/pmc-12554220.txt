Three-year insights from a proton therapy centre: Evolving strategies in ependymoma treatment planning☆
Authors: Lieselotte Lauwens, Anneleen Goedgebeur, Tom Boterberg, Tom Depuydt, Dario Di Perri, Lorraine Donnay, Benjamin Ledoux, Kenneth Poels, Susana Santos Lopes, Karen Van Beek, Robin De Roover, Maarten Lambrecht
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•RT is crucial in the treatment of ependymoma patients.•Tailored RT approaches are essential based on tumour location.•RT for posterior fossa ependymoma is challenging due to brainstem proximity.•Hybrid PT-XT approach is feasible for selected posterior fossa ependymomas.•Hybrid approach achieves acceptable brainstem doses with no symptomatic toxicities.Keywords: Ependymoma, Brainstem injury, Asymptomatic radiographic changes, Treatment planning, Proton therapy, Hybrid plan

Introduction

Ependymomas are rare tumours of the central nervous system arising from the ependymal cells lining the ventricular system. These tumours represent the third most common brain tumour in paediatric patients, with 81 cases diagnosed in Belgium between 2011–2020. In adults, ependymoma account for only 2 % of all brain tumours, with 57 cases diagnosed between 2016–2020, typically diagnosed in young adults [1,2]. The standard treatment of ependymoma consists of maximal safe resection, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy (RT). The current dose prescription, based on international recommendations is 59.4 Gy in 1.8 Gy fractions, except for very young children (<18 months) or those with neurological impairments, for whom the dose can be lowered to 54 Gy in 1.8 Gy fractions [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

The use of proton therapy (PT) for the treatment of brain tumours, including ependymoma, has increased in the last decade. PT offers physical advantages compared to photon radiotherapy (XT), allowing for a reduction of low-to-medium doses to the healthy tissues and expected to mitigate the risk of toxicities, including neurocognitive impairments, endocrine dysfunction and reduced quality of life [7,8].

Recently, retrospective studies have focused on brainstem toxicity after PT in paediatric patients with posterior fossa tumours. Symptomatic brainstem toxicity was reported in 0–4.8 % of paediatric patients after PT [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Comparing incidence between PT and XT is complex, early reports suggested a potential increase of brainstem toxicity in patients treated with PT for posterior fossa tumours, however data remains contradictory [15]. As a result, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Workshop on Proton Therapy for Children, recommended more conservative brainstem dose constraints for PT [16].

Given these concerns, and due to the availability of an embedded XT in our PT centre, a hybrid PT-XT approach, was developed for posterior fossa tumours to address the risks of brainstem toxicity. In this study, we describe the various planning approaches employed for the treatment of intracranial ependymoma patients with PT at the Particle Therapy Interuniversity Centre Leuven (PARTICLE) and evaluate the outcomes associated with these treatments.

Materials and methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted on paediatric and adult intracranial tumour patients referred for PT at PARTICLE between July 1st, 2020, and December 31st, 2023, with at least 1 year of follow-up. This study was approved by the local ethical committee (S69348) and included patients treated for intracranial ependymoma with PT or hybrid-PT-XT approach with at least one follow-up MRI within the first year. Exclusion criteria were exclusive XT treatment or lack of follow-up MRI. All referred patients were evaluated by a multidisciplinary tumour board to evaluate the indication for RT and technical feasibility of PT.

A radiation planning CT scan and MRI scan were acquired and relevant MRI sequences were co-registered to the planning CT scan for delineation. The Orfit HPPRO immobilization system, including a Moldcare cushion and a three-point HPPRO Nanor head mask, was used to ensure patient immobilization for both PT and hybrid PT-XT treatments. For the XT portion of the hybrid treatment, the HPPRO plate was securely fixed on top of the Varian couch, with the head section extending over the table. In case of a hybrid planning approach, the same planning CT scan was utilized for both PT and XT treatment planning. The dose prescription was 59.4 Gy to the tumour bed (GTV1) with a 5 mm CTV margin in all directions, limited to 2 mm in direction of the brainstem (CTV1). If the tumour volume extended below the foramen magnum, a second GTV (GTV2) and CTV (CTV2) were defined, excluding this portion. In this case, CTV1 was treated to 54 Gy and CTV2 was treated up to 59.4 Gy. Robust optimization was used in PT planning using a set-up uncertainty of 3 mm and 3 % density uncertainty, resulting in 28 scenarios. In XT a geometric PTV margin of 3 mm was added. Organs at risk (OAR) were contoured according to the EPTN guidelines [17,18]. In all cases, EPTN dose constraints were used [19]. Additionally, brainstem constraints were retrospectively assessed using the University of Florida (UF) as outlined by the NCI workshop [16]. Deviations were categorized as moderate if the dose exceeded the constraint by up to 102 %, and severe if exceeding 102 %. Photon dose was calculated using the Acuros v16.1.0 algorithm and proton dose was calculated using the Monte Carlo algorithm (v5.4) at 0.5 % uncertainty, in relative biological effectiveness (RBE) dose (using a fixed factor of 1.1).

PT planning was performed in Raystation (Raystation 12.A, RaySearchLabs, Stockholm, Sweden). For PT planning, the primary goal was to ensure 98 % of the CTV was covered by 98 % of the prescribed nominal dose, adjusted on a case-by-case basis to limit dose to critical OARs. Robust evaluation was performed to ensure 98 % of the CTV was covered by 95 % of the dose in the voxel wise minimum [20]. Nominal brainstem D0.03cc was limited to 102 % of the prescribed PT dose. Robust evaluation was performed using 3 mm setup uncertainty and 3 % density uncertainty. Three to four PT beams were used, and the range shifter (RS) was omitted when possible. To minimize RS usage, single field objectives were removed, allowing more multi field optimization. Dose fall-off objectives on the CTV and the brainstem were included.

In some cases at physicians discretion, a hybrid-PT-XT planning approach (hybrid plan) was used. In these cases, XT planning was executed in Eclipse 16.1 (Varian, Palo Alto, USA) using a PTV approach, with the main goal to achieve the D95-95 ICRU. The PT part of the hybrid plan was planned based on our robust evaluation target goals, as described previously [20]. Both the PT and XT plans were similarly optimized for OARs. Dmax constraints were incorporated in the optimizer for the brainstem and spinal cord. The brainstem dose constraint was set to 98 % of the prescribed dose. For the PT plan high-weighted Dmax robust objectives were added for both brainstem and spinal cord, similarly set to 98 % of the prescribed dose. Additionally, a separate robust Dmax objective for brainstem was incorporated into the PT optimizer, set to 98 % of the prescribed dose. Maximum dose volume histogram objectives were included for the hippocampi, brain-CTV and temporal lobes, with an emphasis on minimizing the dose to these OARs in an ALARA setting. Low-weighted dose-fall off objectives were added for a 3–5 mm expansion of the CTV to optimize coverage while minimizing dose to the surrounding tissues.

A plan summation was created in Raystation by importing the XT plan from Eclipse and combining it with the PT plan. The summation was evaluated to ensure that dose constraints were met, and if any constraints were exceeded, the brainstem D0.03cc dose in the XT plan was adjusted and reduced accordingly. Plans of patients treated with this hybrid-PT-XT approach, were retrospectively replanned to a PT-only plan (Supplementary materials).

All patients were treated with PT at PARTICLE and XT at the Department of Radiotherapy at University Hospitals Leuven. PT treatments were delivered using the ProteusOne (IBA, Louvain-La-Neuve, Belgium), with an energy range of 70 MeV-226 MeV, S2C2 Synchrocyclotron and 4.1 cm RS. The XT treatments were delivered on the TruebeamStX (Varian, Palo Alto, USA).

Patient and tumour characteristics were extracted from the electronic health record. Extent of resection was evaluated based on surgical examination and postoperative MRI scan, with complete resection defined as no residual tumour observed by surgeon and on imaging. The number of surgeries and presence of neurological symptoms were recorded. Dosimetric data were extracted from the treatment planning systems.

We retrieved all MRI scans and reports from diagnosis until last follow-up. All patients had T1 with contrast and T2/FLAIR sequences during each MRI examination. A radiation oncologist reviewed all scans and reports and any MRI alterations were discussed at a multidisciplinary tumour board. Symptomatic brainstem injury (SBI) was defined according to Indelicato et al. [11] as new or progressive symptoms involving cranial nerves V-VII or IX-XII, motor weakness, or dysmetria with corresponding radiographic abnormality within the brainstem in absence of disease progression. We further defined radiographic abnormality as any new or increased contrast-enhancement lesions on T1-weighted sequences and/or T2/FLAIR hyperintensity, on follow-up MRI which was not present on postoperative MRI before start of PT. Additionally, we investigated asymptomatic radiographic change (ARC), defined as radiographic abnormalities without clinical symptoms.

The occurrence of SBI or ARC was reported as a binary outcome. The follow-up duration was calculated for each patient from start of PT to the date of last follow-up or date of death by any cause. Descriptive statistics for variables and endpoints were described as median and range for continuous variables, and absolute numbers for categorical variables. Dosimetric data were compared as an exploratory analysis between original hybrid plans and retrospectively replanned PT-only plans using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank non-parametric test with significance level of 5 %.

Results

Patient and treatment characteristics

A total of 19 patients were referred to PARTICLE for intracranial ependymoma treatment between 2020–2023, with 14 patients accepted for treatment and receiving PT as part of their RT (Supplementary Fig. A1). Baseline characteristics, including patient and treatment characteristics of the included patients are summarized in Table 1. Most patients presented with posterior fossa tumours (n = 11), with PFA being the most common molecular subtype (n = 6). Median age at diagnosis and start of PT were 4.8 years (1.9–22.2), and 5.2 years (2.0–28.7) respectively. All patients underwent surgery, with most (n = 8) requiring multiple procedures, resulting in a complete resection. Chemotherapy was administered in four patients, prior to (n = 2) or after RT (n = 2). The total prescribed dose of focal RT was 59.4 Gy(RBE) to the intracranial tumour bed for all patients. For metastatic disease (n = 4), 36 Gy(RBE) was delivered to the craniospinal axis, followed by a boost up to 50.4–59.4 Gy(RBE) to the tumour bed and/or metastatic sites, depending on location. These patients excluded from the detailed planning analysis.

Radiotherapy treatment planning strategies

Tumour location played a crucial role in determining RT approach in case of focal treatment. Among patients with intracranial ependymoma (supratentorial n = 2, posterior fossa n = 8), five patients were treated with PT-only, while the remaining five patients received a hybrid plan. For supratentorial ependymoma which were distant from the brainstem (n = 2), PT-only was used. For posterior fossa tumours, the approach depended on the relationship between the CTV and the brainstem, as illustrated in Fig. 1. When the CTV was “abutting” the brainstem—defined as being in contact with the posterior aspect of the brainstem while remaining fully posterior —PT-only was used (Panel A). In contrast, when the CTV was “encasing” the brainstem—characterized by extensive contact with the brainstem, where the CTV completely surrounds the posterior and lateral aspects of the brainstem—a hybrid-PT-XT approach was preferred (Panel C). When the CTV was “hugging” the brainstem—defined as being in contact with the brainstem, extending laterally without fully encasing it on the lateral sides—both treatment approaches were considered (Panel B). In cases where the CTV was “encompassing” the brainstem – completely surrounding or extending anteriorly to the brainstem – XT only was used, patients treated with this approach were not included in this analysis (Panel D). Five patients with posterior fossa ependymoma received a hybrid-PT-XT planning approach (four “encasing”, one “hugging”), the other three were treated with PT-only (two “abutting”, one “hugging”).

Categories of spatial contact between brainstem (=brown) and clinical target volume (=red). A: abutting: clinical target volume in contact with the posterior aspect of the brainstem. B: hugging: clinical target volume extending laterally without encasing both lateral sides of the brainstem. C: encasing: clinical target volume completely encasing both lateral aspects of the brainstem. D: encompassing: clinical target volume extending anteriorly to the brainstem. All patients were treated using pencil beam scanning (PBS) intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT) and most patients were treated using four coplanar beams (n = 7) (Supplementary Table A1). In case of hybrid plan, XT was delivered using dual or triple arc volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) with anterior avoidance sectors to avoid beam entry to the eye region as per protocol for fossa posterior treatments in our centre. The hybrid plans typically involved delivering 45 Gy(RBE) with PT, followed by 14.4 Gy(RBE) with XT. In case of two CTVs, two sequential VMAT plans were used(45–54 Gy(RBE) and 54–59.4 Gy(RBE)).

Brainstem doses are reported in Table 2 and Supplementary Table A2. Across all plans median brainstem D0.03cc was 58.8 Gy(RBE) (48.2–59.4 Gy(RBE)) and median brainstem core D0.03cc was 57.3 Gy(RBE) (36.8–58.6 Gy(RBE)). When evaluating the UF brainstem constraints, median D0.1cc, median D10% and median D50% were 58.7 Gy(RBE) (47.7–59.1 Gy(RBE)), 56.4 Gy(RBE) (7.8–53.3 Gy(RBE)) and 47.6 Gy(RBE) (0.2–53.3 Gy(RBE)) respectively. While all plans complied with the EPTN brainstem D0.03cc constraint, there was greater variability observed concerning adherence to the UF brainstem constraints (Fig. 2) [16,19]. The D50% was met in every case (n = 10), while the D10% was exceeded in most cases (n = 7, severe excess: n = 4). The D0.1cc was exceeded in four patients but always moderately.

Patient outcomes and toxicity

The median follow-up was 22.2 months (12.0–39.6 months). There was no local recurrence and distant recurrence occurred in three patients, two patients with localised disease treated with focal RT and one with upfront metastatic disease treated with CSI experiencing relapse at a new site. The median time to treatment failure was 5.0 months (3.0–23.0 months). None of the patients in our cohort developed SBI, regardless of the treatment planning approach. ARC were present in three patients (Supplementary Table A6). All patients had posterior fossa ependymomas; two were treated with PT-only, and one with a hybrid plan. ARC occurred at a median of 7.1 months (3.0–8.4 months) post-treatment and resolved in two patients.

Discussion

This study examined treatment planning strategies for ependymoma patients, focussing on the impact of tumour location on radiotherapy treatment approaches. Additionally, clinical outcomes and toxicities, including ARC and SBI, scored according to Indelicato et al., were assessed [11]. Ependymomas are often located in the posterior fossa, in proximity to the brainstem, presenting challenges in PT treatment planning. Additionally, concerns arise regarding the increase of RBE at the end of the spread-out Bragg peak due to high linear energy transfer (LET) [21]. This could lead to underestimation of the biological effect in OARs near the target volume, potentially resulting in unexpected toxicities [16].

In our centre, radiotherapy treatment approach was chosen based on the relationship between the target volume and the brainstem (Fig. 1). The proximity of these tumours to the brainstem requires careful consideration in treatment planning to ensure acceptable trade-off between dose coverage and brainstem constraints. An analysis of current practice in European PT centres in the treatment of posterior fossa tumours showed wide variations in planning approaches, with our centre being the only one out of sixteen centres using a hybrid-PT-XT planning approach in case of an encasing ependymoma [8]. Five patients with posterior fossa ependymoma where the CTV encased the brainstem, were selected for this hybrid-PT-XT approach, as the extensive contact made it challenging to achieve an optimal balance between coverage and brainstem constraints. This in-house strategy, allowed by an integrated XT department, offers potential to mitigate the risks associated with an increased RBE in the brainstem. The hybrid-PT-XT approach consists of part of the treatment (75 % of the prescribed dose to CTV) delivered using PT, while the remaining portion (25 % of the prescribed dose to CTV) is delivered using XT. However, it is important to acknowledge that the 75 %/25 % distribution, is somewhat arbitrary. While this approach was considered a balanced strategy by our multidisciplinary team to reduce RBE uncertainty and minimize the low-dose bath from a full XT treatment in patients at risk for brainstem toxicity, other centres may approach these challenges differently. Most centres tend to deliver proton-only in ependymoma patients, with reassuring results regarding toxicity and local control [9,12,22,23]. While some may opt for XT-only in more challenging cases, it remains unclear to what extent this is the case. These variations highlight the need for further exploration into optimizing treatment strategies for such complex cases.

In our study, all treatment plans met the EPTN brainstem dose constraint (D0.03cc), but UF constraints were not consistently achieved [16,19]. The D0.1cc constraint was exceeded in four posterior fossa ependymoma, due to prioritizing target coverage, a finding consistent with Toussaint et al., who reported that no ependymoma plans from PT centres across Europe met the D0.1cc goal [8]. In most cases, the D10% constraint was exceeded (n = 7), however the D50% constraint was always met, contrasting a recent analysis by Correia et al., which found this constraint challenging [24]. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, multidisciplinary review led to one patient with fully encompassing ependymoma being referred for XT due to brainstem toxicity concerns, in which case the D50% constraint would likely not have been met in PT-only plans. Additionally, our PT treatment was limited to posterior oblique fields and differences between PT systems, with all of our patients treated with PBS using the ProteusOne, could contribute to the observed differences.

The comparison of hybrid plans and PT-only replans revealed a trend towards lower brainstem doses in the hybrid plans for several brainstem dose parameters, including D0.1cc, D10%, D0.03cc, and brainstem core D0.03cc (with p-values of 0.058, 0.63, 0.63, and 0.63, respectively). However, no statistically significant differences were observed. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to better clarify these potential differences. We did observe that the degree of exceeding the dose constraints was more pronounced in the PT-only plans, especially concerning the D0.1cc. A significant advantage of the hybrid approach is that it reduces the radiobiological uncertainty accompanying a PT-only plan due to RBE variations. This hybrid-PT-XT strategy offers a solution in patients with challenging CTV locations. Key limitations of this hybrid approach include its applicability only in embedded PT-XT facilities and its substantially increased time requirements compared to PT-only or XT-only strategies. Furthermore, the combination of a CTV-based probability planning in PT with PTV-based planning for XT, leads to different uncertainty models being applied in each modality [20,25]. While encompassing target volumes were excluded from this analysis due to referral for XT following multidisciplinary review, future application of the hybrid-PT-XT approach may enable inclusion of these cases. However, further research and detailed case-by-case planning analysis are required for these complex cases.

Across Europe, different strategies are already being used to counteract the uncertainties and risk of toxicities resulting from an increased RBE [26]. In a retrospective study, directly biologically optimized plans for ependymoma patients, including LET and variable RBE-optimisation achieved lower variable RBE dose to OARs compared to a constant RBE of 1.1, demonstrating the feasibility of incorporating variable RBE models in PT optimization and a phase I trial is ongoing (NCT03750513). These strategies provide a valuable opportunity to directly address RBE uncertainties in the future, with increased availability of LET and variable RBE calculations allowing for easier implementation of such strategies.

In our small series, no cases of SBI were diagnosed. In literature the incidence of SBI following PT for ependymoma ranges from 0 to 3.6 % [9,10,14,27]. ARC was present in three patients, at the higher end of the 0 %–22 % incidence reported in other PT studies on ependymoma. However, most studies did not provide a detailed definition of ARC, making it unclear which radiographic changes were considered [9,28,29]. In our studies, most MRI alterations were transient, consistent with previous findings [9,13].

Limitations of this study include the small cohort size, limiting the generalizability of our findings and the short follow-up period preventing the ability to assess long-term toxicities and tumour control. While the study focuses on brainstem toxicity and dose parameters, it does not include data on spinal cord toxicity or doses. Furthermore, the lack of data on LET distributions and the use of variable RBE models is a limitation, as these factors could provide valuable insights to guide future treatment planning decisions.

Conclusion

Different planning approaches can be used in the treatment of ependymoma patients depending on location and relationship to the brainstem, with the intent to minimize treatment-related toxicities. A hybrid-PT-XT approach, is a feasible solution in well-selected patients, demonstrating adequate target coverage, acceptable brainstem doses and to date no instances of SBI.
