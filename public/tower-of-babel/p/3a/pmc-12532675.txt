Construction and validation of a novel prognostic model for postoperative patients with stage I intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: a population-based study
Authors: Xiaomeng Yao, Xinru Fan, Qing Wang, Huabang Zhou, Hui Wang, Heping Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIntrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is the second most common primary malignant tumor of the liver. Surgery is routinely recommended for stage I ICC patients. The independent prognostic factors affecting stage I ICC patients after surgery remain unclear.MethodsAltogether, 349 ICC stage I patients undergoing curative operation from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database were registered from 2010 to 2021 in this research. Random assignment of these patients was carried out in a 7:3 proportion, forming a training cohort consisting of 245 and an internal validation cohort consisting of 104. Independent prognostic factors were recognized by univariate and multivariate analysis in the training cohort. Subsequently, the prognostic nomogram was constructed using independent prognostic factors in the training cohort, and the nomogram was validated internally and externally using data from the internal validation cohort and a cohort from Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital, respectively.ResultsMultivariate analysis demonstrated that race, grade, tumor size, and lymph node removal had associations with overall survival (OS) in postoperative patients with stage I ICC in the training cohort (P < 0.05). Then the prognostic nomogram for these patients was constructed on the basis of the above variables. The C-indexes for internal and external validation of the nomogram were 0.65 and 0.63, respectively. In addition, the internally validated and externally validated 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS calibration curves exhibited a good fit, suggesting that the nomogram had good predictive power.ConclusionThe prognostic nomogram of postoperative patients diagnosed with stage I ICC performs well. This novel nomogram may help clinicians make individualized predictions of postsurgical outcomes for patients with stage I ICC and provide new perspectives for effectively assessing the value of surgery to the patient.Keywords: Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, Nomogram, Prognosis, Radical resection

Introduction

Liver cancer is ranked as the third leading cause of death resulting from cancer globally following lung and colorectal cancers [1]. Among primary hepatic malignancies , intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) comes in second place in terms of prevalence, accounting for roughly 10–15% in the entirety of primary liver tumors [2]. During the past few decades, the incidence of ICC has been increasing globally [3–5]. Despite the advent of advanced clinical managements such as surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the prognosis of these patients remains dismal, and the 5-year overall survival (OS) is within the range of 20–30% [6, 7].

While surgery remains the sole potentially healing therapy for ICC [8, 9], the high recurrence rate observed postoperatively, ranging from 49.6 to 83.6% within 24 months, is largely attributed to the advanced stage when initially diagnosed [10, 11]. Nevertheless, these studies encompassed patients spanning all stages of ICC and subgroup analyses were rarely performed. Notably, early-stage ICC, which is most likely to derive significant benefit from surgical intervention, has not been specifically examined in these studies.

Stage I ICC is characterized by a solitary tumor, with a maximum diameter either smaller than or greater than 5 cm, and in the absence of lymph node involvement, vascular invasion, or distant metastasis, as defined by the 8th edition TNM staging system of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) [12]. To our knowledge, limited research has been conducted on postoperative prognosis in those patients at stage I of ICC, who are apt to gain advantages from the operation. The early detection of high-risk populations is critical for the advancement of personalized monitoring strategies and the accurate assessment of prognosis following surgical intervention. Simultaneously, it also provides guidance for individualized postoperative adjuvant tumor therapy regimens. Currently, the nomogram predicting the prognosis in stage I ICC patients after radical resection is not available. Accordingly, the aim of this study is to identify independent factors affecting prognosis and construct a nomogram that would predict survival probabilities among stage I ICC patients following surgical treatment on the basis of clinical parameters, which is conducive to classifying patients with cancer, estimating survival probabilities, and guiding therapeutic decisions.

Materials and methods

Patients cohort

Patients who had stage I ICC and underwent radical surgery from the SEER database between 2010 and 2021 were retrieved into this survey. Besides, we retrospectively collected clinical data from patients with stage I ICC who had undergone radical resection at the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital between 2010 and 2017, which were used to establish the external validation cohort for this study. The inclusion and exclusion criteria applied to both cohorts are detailed as follows. The inclusion criteria included: (1) age >19 years; (2) pathologically verified as ICC; (3) staged as stage I ICC (T1N0M0) in accordance with the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging guidelines; (4) ICC is the only malignant primary tumor and there is no history of other malignant tumors; (5) underwent surgical treatment with the intention of radical cure. The exclusion criteria included: (1) mixed hepatocellular carcinoma and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma; (2) without undergoing radical surgical treatment; (3) incomplete clinical or follow-up data.

Data collection

This study collected these following clinicopathological variables of patients: the year of diagnosis, gender, age, marital status, race, grade, tumor size, the quantity of lymph nodes dissected, radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

Establishment and validation of nomogram

The 349 patients from SEER database were allocated to the training cohort and the internal validation cohort with the proportion being 7:3. The nomogram was created using the training cohort, with internal validation performed on the internal validation cohort. Furthermore, external validation was performed utilizing 114 patients at the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital. The concordance index (C-index), receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC), area under the curve (AUC), and calibration curves were generated during both internal and external validations to evaluate the discriminability, calibration, and clinical applicability of the nomogram.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics 30.0 analysis software was used for statistical analysis. Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted applying the independent samples T-test or Mann-Whitney U test, while categorical variables were evaluated through the chi-square test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Multivariate survival analysis was constructed to distinguish independent prognostic factors. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival curves were generated to contrast OS, with statistical significance determined by log-rank test. The nomogram was plotted using R 4.4.1. Internal and external validation of the nomogram’s accuracy was performed using data from the internal and external validation cohorts, encompassing calculation of the C-index, plotting of calibration curves, generation of ROC curves, and determination of the AUC. In addition, the forest plot was drawn with the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool. P < 0.05 was deemed suggestive of statistical significance.

Results

Basic characteristics of patients with stage I ICC

In this study, 396 patients who were diagnosed with stage I ICC and had excision between 2010 and 2021 were initially retrieved from SEER database and then screened. Subsequently, 47 patients were excluded, including 31 patients with incomplete clinical data and 16 patients with mixed hepatocellular carcinoma and Cholangiocarcinoma. Consequently, 349 patients who satisfied the inclusion criteria were incorporated in ultimate cohort and these patients were subsequently stratified into a training cohort of 245 ICC patients and an internal validation cohort of 104 ICC patients (Fig. 1).

Patient screening flowchart

The training cohort consisted of 116 males and 129 females. More than 70% of the patients were over 60 years old at the initial diagnosis. The majority of patients are of White ethnicity (190 [78%]). Most of the patients were at grade I and grade II, which corresponded to well-differentiated and moderately-differentiated, respectively (192 [78%]). In addition, the median tumor diameter was 42 (30, 64) millimeters. Approximately half of the patients underwent lymph node dissection during the surgery (129 [53%]). No more than 10% of the patients underwent radiotherapy (18 [7.3%]). Meanwhile, approximately 40% of the patients received chemotherapy (108 [44%]). Besides, approximately three-fifths of the patients were married when first diagnosed (154 [63%]). There were no statistically differences between the training cohort and the internal validation cohort (Table 1).

Survival outcomes and risk factors of patients in the training cohort

The median OS for the entire cohort was 27 months, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 93.2%, 72.8% and 60.8%, respectively. The median OS of the training cohort and the internal validation cohort was 25 and 28 months, respectively. In addition, the patients in the training cohort exhibited 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 92.2%, 70.8% and 60.9% respectively, while those of the patients in the internal validation cohort had survival rates of 95.6%, 77.4% and 60.8% at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference in OS between the two groups of patients (P >0.05).

The result of the univariate Cox survival analysis showed that race, grade, tumor size, and lymph node dissection were predictors of postoperative OS for patients with stage I ICC. Being of white ethnicity, having a large tumor diameter, being at a high grade, and without undergoing lymph node dissection during the surgery were associated with a poor prognosis (P < 0.05; Table 2; Fig. 2). On multivariable analysis, several factors independently affecting postoperative OS in patients with stage I ICC included race, grade, tumor size, and lymph node dissection (P value of the Entire model < 0.05; Table 2; Fig. 2).

Establishment and validation of the prognostic nomogram

On the basis of the result of the multivariable survival analysis, the prognostic nomogram was developed to forecast the survival probabilities of patients afflicted with stage I ICC following radical resection (Fig. 3a). In the internal validation, the C-index of the prognostic nomogram was 0.65. The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year AUC of ROC were 0.55, 0.65, and 0.63, respectively. In the external validation, the C-index of the prognostic nomogram was 0.63 with corresponding AUC values of 0.65, 0.88 and 0.99 for the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year ROC, respectively. Similarly, the calibration curves for the prognostic nomogram demonstrated a robust fit, signifying a remarkable consistency between the predicted and observed survival outcomes (Fig. 3b and d). The ROC curves, which were validated both internally and externally, further showed a high predictive accuracy over time, underscoring the nomogram’s discriminative ability (Fig. 3c and e). In brief, these results demonstrated that the prognostic nomogram’s utility as a valuable and promising instrument for the prognostic assessment of stage I ICC patients post-curative surgery.

Discussion

Patients with stage I ICC should undergo anatomical hepatectomy as early as possible, as recommended by guidelines [13–15]. Radical excision is the sole potentially healing remedy for ICC. Even though, a study from a multi-institutional database found that the 5-year survival rate of 1,095 patients with ICC after surgery was only 36.6% [16]. This may be attributed to the high risk of recurrence following the operation. Multiple studies have shown that the 5-year recurrence rate after ICC surgery can reach up to 60–70% [17–19]. However, all of the above studies involved postoperative patients with ICC at all stages, and limited research has been conducted on evaluating the prognosis of stage I ICC patients after surgery [20]. In this study, only postoperative stage I ICC patients were recruited. This study demonstrated that race, grade, tumor size, and lymph node dissection during surgery were closely associated with the OS of these patients. Ultimately, this study constructed a prognostic nomogram for postoperative stage I ICC patients using the results of multivariate survival analysis, aiming to predict the prognosis of postoperative stage I ICC patients. This nomogram may provide a reliable tool for the management of stage I ICC patients.

Race has been found to be connected with the incidence and outcome of ICC patients in previous studies [21–23]. An epidemiological study involving 11,296 patients with ICC revealed that among all ethnic groups, Asian individuals had the highest incidence of ICC [23]. In addition, a study on ICC patients of different ethnic groups showed that, although there was not a significant gap in the 1-year survival rate between White individuals and ICC patients of all ethnic groups, in terms of the 5-year survival rate, White individuals had a shorter 5-year survival period compared with ICC patients from other racial and ethnic groups [22]. In our study, we found that White individuals had a lower survival rate after surgery compared to those of other ethnic groups. Our findings, consistent with previous reports, further reveal that race is closely linked to the outcome of patients with stage I ICC after surgery. These disparities underscore that ICC patients of different races may have distinct potential progression pathways. And a thorough understanding of these potential pathways can guide the formulation of treatment strategies for patients of different races to improve patient outcomes.

Several studies have indicated that the tumor grade is highly relevant to the prognosis of tumor patients [24, 25]. Patients with grade III/IV (undifferentiated and poorly differentiated) have more aggressive tumor behaviors, which may be more prone to lymph node metastasis and postoperative recurrence. In our research, only about 20% of the patients are at grade III/IV, which may be ascribed to the fact that we involved patients only with stage I ICC. Besides, patients with grade III/IV have a worse prognosis than those with grade I/II (P < 0.05). Additionally, the tumor diameter of ICC is also relevant to the prognosis of ICC patients. However, the cutoff value of the tumor diameter of ICC, that is, the T stage, is controversial [26, 27]. The 8th TNM staging system uses 5 cm as the cutoff value for guiding the prognosis of patients [12]. While Japanese scholars such as Yamamoto believe that 2 cm can more distinctly group ICC patients with significant prognostic disparities [28]. In the current study, our further stratified analysis emphasizes indeed that 3 cm probably is a better predictive threshold value for patients with stage I ICC after surgery, which suggests that not only should we be wary of cases with tumors larger than 5 centimeters in diameter, but also that patients whose tumors are larger than 3 centimeters in diameter should be identified as high-risk postoperatively.

Currently, there remains considerable controversy regarding the necessity of routine lymph node dissection during surgical treatment of ICC. Furthermore, there is no universally accepted standard defining the optimal extent of lymphadenectomy in ICC. A recent retrospective study involving 308 ICC patients who received operations with curative intent reported that, although lymph node metastasis (LNM) was correlated with significantly worse OS and disease-free survival (DFS) in ICC, lymph node dissection did not confer a corresponding improvement in long-term survival or recurrence rates compared to lymph node biology. On the contrary, lymph node dissection was associated with impaired postoperative recovery and failed to provide a survival advantage for patients with ICC [29]. Similarly, Yoshino, J et al. have reported that, following propensity score matching, in cases of peripheral ICC no statistically differences were observed in OS or DFS between patients who underwent lymphadenectomy and those who did not [30]. In addition, another retrospective, multi-institutional study using propensity score matching demonstrated that patients with clinically node-negative ICC failed to derive a survival benefit from lymph node dissection. And there were no statistically significant differences in median OS or DFS between patients who underwent lymphadenectomy and those who did not [31]. However, our study demonstrates that among patients with stage I ICC, those who underwent lymph node dissection during surgery have a longer median OS compared to those without lymph node dissection during surgery (105 months vs. 79 months, Fig. 2e), a finding that is consistent with the findings of Sposito, C. and his colleagues, but contrasts with several other previous reports. The multicenter study by Sposito, C. and his colleagues, which included 706 clinically node-negative patients, revealed that adequate lymph node dissection (removal of ≥ 6 lymph nodes) significantly improved both median RFS and median OS in the subgroup of patients with less-advanced tumors. These less-advanced tumors were defined as those exhibiting solitary lesions, a maximum tumor diameter < 5 cm, serum CA19-9 levels < 200 U/mL, and absence of macrovascular invasion, which demonstrates substantial overlap with the stage I ICC patient cohort in our study [32]. Additionally, one potential reason for the discrepancies between our findings and those of other preceding studies may lie in differences in patient populations. The majority of patients in our cohort were White individuals, which may reflect distinct genetic backgrounds and biological behaviors of the disease. A further potential contributing factor is that patients included in our study were at an earlier disease stage, with better baseline physical condition and fewer comorbidities. Under these circumstances, lymph node dissection may have been more effective in removing microscopic metastatic lesions, thereby contributing to improved prognosis. In contrast, prior studies may have included more heterogeneous populations, with a higher proportion of patients in advanced phases. In such settings, the benefits of lymphadenectomy may be diminished, and the procedure could potentially increase the risk of surgery-related complications without improving long-term outcomes.

The effect that adjuvant chemotherapy has on the long-term outcome of ICC patients remains controversial, particularly in early-stage cases. Most studies suggested that patients with poor prognostic factors are more likely to benefit from postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy [33, 34]. However, other studies have indicated that even patients with early-stage tumors may derive significant benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. Ariizumi et al. performed a retrospective analysis of 224 ICC patients and proposed that individuals without metastasis within the liver or to lymph nodes represented the most suitable subset for hepatectomy combined with adjuvant therapy [35]. Additionally, the findings by Li et al. indicated that adjuvant chemotherapy significantly extended the OS for T1N0M0 ICC patients following radical resection [36]. Furthermore, a retrospective study involving patients with stage I-III ICC revealed that neoadjuvant chemotherapy was associated with longer OS compared to adjuvant chemotherapy administered after surgical resection (median OS: 40.3 months vs. 32.8 months; P = 0.01) [37]. Nevertheless, a recent study has shown that neoadjuvant therapy can reduce the positive rate of lymph nodes in ICC, but it does not confer additional survival benefits compared to upfront surgical resection [38]. Notably, our study finds that patients with stage I following surgical resection fail to gain a survival benefit from chemotherapy. These findings highlight the ongoing controversy regarding the efficacy of both neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy in ICC. Based on our results, routine administration of chemotherapy is not recommended for stage I ICC patients after surgery. Future research should focus on large-scale, multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trials to better identify subgroups at other stages who may truly benefit from chemotherapy, thereby achieving the goals of precision medicine.

In our study, we developed a postoperative prognostic nomogram for stage I ICC. Given the heterogeneity of data, reliance on the internal validation cohort alone may be inadequate for fully assessing its generalizability. To address this limitation, we incorporated an external validation cohort from a large clinical hospital in China, aiming to more comprehensively characterize the model’s performance. Notably, the nomogram demonstrated robust predictive efficacy in both the internal and external validation cohorts. Our findings indicated that patients without lymph node dissection, with the ethnicity of White, higher grade, and tumor diameters greater than 3 cm were at a significantly higher risk of poor outcomes. Based on the nomogram, high-risk patients can be effectively identified. For these patients, the implementation of lymph node dissection and more aggressive therapy may be necessary and beneficial, with the aim of improving outcomes. These findings provide valuable guidance for tailoring perioperative treatment strategies to optimize the management of stage I ICC.

There are several limitations of this study. First of all, since it is a retrospective analysis, it is inherently prone to potential selection bias. A second limitation is that the SEER data derive from multiple different institutions, and inter-institutional heterogeneity in pathological diagnosis, staging, and treatment may limit consistency and comparability, thereby affecting the generalizability of the research findings. Another potential drawback is that the SEER database lacks certain molecular information, which may lead to the omission of some molecular prognostic insights. Additionally, the use of data from a single institution for external validation may impose constraints on the extrapolation of model applicability to specific clinical scenarios. Furthermore, the comparatively small sample size further constrains the robustness of the conclusions. Prospective large-sample studies are needed in the future to expand the application of the model.

Conclusion

The nomogram incorporating clinicopathologic variables has been proven as a dependable tool for predicting prognosis. This model may effectively serve as a valuable resource in guiding individualized management strategies and informing clinical decision-making in stage I ICC patients.
