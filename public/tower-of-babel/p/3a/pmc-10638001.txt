The concept of reasons for eliminating corporate crime in criminal law in Indonesia
Authors: Randikha Prabu Raharja Sasmita, Sigid Suseno, Patris Yusrian Jaya
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing Indonesian criminal law, courts may not find defendants or offenders guilty for various grounds known as illegal abolition reasons. There are two justifications and grounds for forgiveness for abolishing crimes under the Indonesian Criminal Code. The justification for abolishing this punishment is solely applicable to human legal subjects. Contrarily, despite being acknowledged as criminal law subjects, legal entities, in this case companies, have not been subject to severe regulation. This study attempts to gain a general understanding of and examine the causes of corporate criminal offenses under Indonesian criminal law. A statutory approach and a conceptual approach are used in the research method, which is normative juridical. These approaches are then examined utilizing secondary data and qualitative data analysis techniques. The study's findings demonstrate that the justifications can cover corporate law topics for criminal write-offs. Due to the legal ramifications of classifying companies as criminal law subjects, the justifications for criminal write-offs also apply mutatis mutandis to concerns involving people. The reasons for outlawing the crime in Article 50 of the Draft Criminal Code have been considered. Still, related to the reasons for pardoning as outlined in the article, they do not correspond to the features of corporations. In this study, the authors develop the idea of justifications that adhere to the theory and characteristics of corporations, such as the corporation does not receive profits, acts committed by agents outside of the authority granted by the corporation, and the corporation has complied with its obligations to prevent corporate crime from occurring.Keywords: Corporation, Crime, Punishment, Responsibility, Criminal Law

Several ideas and doctrines that stray from the tenet of the error to ensnare corporations as criminal targets are beginning to promote the shift in the paradigm of punishment for businesses. The growth of the categories of crimes covered by special criminal laws outside the Criminal Code—laws that were formerly only applicable to people—marks the admission of corporations. However, more firms are now being targeted for illicit activity. Additionally, businesses are now considered criminal entities under Article 45 of the Draft Criminal Code, which will replace the Indonesian Criminal Code [3]. Last, corporations may be prosecuted under Indonesia's unique criminal law.

Following Indonesian criminal law, there are legitimate justifications for judges not to find guilty criminals or defendants accused of committing crimes. These justifications are referred to as criminal grounds. Crimes should be eliminated because regulations primarily target judges [4]. This clause governs various situations where the offender has met the legal conditions for the offense as defined by the statute but should not be penalized. In this instance, the judge asserts their authority to determine whether the offender's actions fall under exceptional circumstances, as stated in the criminal abolition justification [5].

There are two justifications for abolishing crimes in Indonesia's Criminal Code (hereafter referred to as the Criminal Code): justifications for justification and forgiveness. Forced defense, enforcing laws, and executing official commands are among the justifications listed in the Criminal Code [6]. The discrepancies must, however, be spelled out in the law. Izaak asserts that even though the defendant's actions have satisfied the legislative definition of the element of delict, the justification is the factor that makes up for the act's unlawfulness and renders it appropriate and suitable. If the action is not illegal, there is no penalty [7].

The justification for forgiveness, however, has to do with the offender in the sense that he should not be held accountable (legally) for his conduct. In other words, even though his activities are illegal, he is neither responsible nor guilty. Because of this, the maker's guilt is no longer there, and no punishment is necessary. The Criminal Code has forgiveness, provisions for lack of responsibility, excessive consumption of noodles, and good faith compliance with an unconstitutional position order [8].

As mentioned, reasonable grounds exist to eliminate crimes against human legal subjects, including justifying and forgiving. While companies have not explicitly been governed regarding the justifications for criminal justice reform, legal entities have. Corporations have the same status as other legal subjects, namely persons, even though sections of laws and regulations outside the Criminal Code have recognized corporations as subjects of criminal law.

In 1951, Indonesia made companies a part of its penal code. According to the law, this is stated in Emergency Law Number 17 of 1951 regarding the Hoarding of Goods' Article 11, paragraph 1 [9]. Some aspects of its execution, nevertheless, should yet be improved. Identifying the legal party who is criminally liable for corporate misbehavior is one of these challenges. The causes of the illegal abolition will also be attributed to criminal guilt. The corporation is a new entity for criminal culpability, as was previously stated. The authors of this study attempt to develop the idea of the reason for the write-off for corporations because the reason for the write-off in this situation also has to be further developed.

Development of corporate crime cases in Indonesia

Based on data from the Auriga Nusantara Institute, from 2015 to 2021, as many as 50 corporations have been named suspects in forest fire cases, and only 15 points have been sentenced by courts. Then in cases of criminal acts of corruption, based on the annual report from the Corruption Eradication Commission from 2017 to 2022, 5 Corporations have fallen. Meanwhile, accurate data has yet to be available for corporate crimes in other fields.

One example of a judge's verdict in a corporate case is the corruption case committed by the Giri Jaladhi Wana Company (from now on, referred to as the GJW case). Regarding cases of corporate corruption, GJW Company Decision Number 04/Pid.Sus/PT.BJM is a definitive and legally binding judgment. As the author hinted in the introduction, this is a crucial case for the Public Prosecutor, particularly in handling companies as defendants. It is the first instance of corporate corruption as a result. The GJW Company problem relates to the collaboration agreement between the City of Banjarmasin and the GJW Company. Antasari Central Market and its accompanying facilities, totaling 5145 square feet, were to be built by the firm in Banjarmasin, South Kalimantan, in 1998. Following the contract terms, the City of Banjarmasin was required to furnish the project with the necessary land and permissions.

GJW Company must also provide funding to expand the wholesale market. The contract gave the GJW Company the authority to control shop sales, use the market for a predetermined time, and split earnings with the Indonesian government. The government-owned land certificate for Pasar Induk Banjarmasin was used as collateral when GJW Company sought a loan at Bank Mandiri to fund the project.

Without the City of Banjarmasin's approval, GJW Company constructed an additional 900 units while constructing a wholesale market and sold them for business purposes. To avoid paying a portion to the Banjarmasin City government, the GJW company allegedly fabricated a report to the government indicating that the wholesale market construction needed to be finished. Additionally, delaying payment of obligations owed to Bank Mandiri was the goal of this case. Due to the GJW Company's efforts, the City of Banjarmasin lost 7.7 billion Rupiah in state funding or profit sharing. The non-performing loan from the GJW Company also represents a loss to the government because Bank Mandiri is a state-owned business. Because of this, the Public Prosecution Service filed a lawsuit against GJW Company for breaking the Corruption Eradication Act.

Interestingly, the Public Prosecutor is bringing charges against GJW Company employees. The Public Prosecutor files a new case to sue the company after a person has been found guilty in a final and irrevocable court ruling. The director of the GJW Company was found guilty of violating Article 2 paragraph (1) of the Law on the Eradication of Corporate Crime in the case's first trial. The GJW Company director was fined 150 million Rupiah and given a 5-year prison term for misdeeds. The Public Prosecution Service started charging the corporation based on the same Article 2 paragraph (1) after the court's final decision to impose a criminal sanction on the director of the GJW Company. The court also imposed an additional penalty on the director of the GJW Company to pay compensation of 24.8 billion Rupiah.

Because neither of the two parties appealed to the Supreme Court, the Banjarmasin High Court has made the final verdict in this case. The court found that the firms had committed conduct that resulted in financial losses or harm to the nation's economy while benefitting themselves or others. In the matter of the GJW Company, the court employed several standards based on Article 20 paragraph (2) to identify a corporate Payment Service Provider. Since no laws or regulations defined the grounds for corporate crime abolition before the passage of the new Criminal Code, no punishments for corporate crimes have been abolished thus far.

The urgency of reasons for eliminating corporate crime in corporate criminal liability

The discussion regarding the reasons for the abolition of criminal penalties for corporations has been discussed for a long time by Schaffmeister, around the 1990s. Schaffmeister argues that, as with individuals, legal entities can also refer to the basis for criminal abolition, which means that there are reasons for illegal repeal against corporations [11]. Regarding this opinion, Wibisana & Marbun [12] also said the same thing: the reason for criminal abolition generally applies to subjects of criminal law. Thus, companies may likewise be used for unlawful abolition purposes. The issue is whether the justification for eliminating punishment for violations of human law, as stated in Book One of the Criminal Code, may be extended to business law violations. The author attempts to investigate each justification for removing a crime from the Criminal Code to respond to this query. These justifications are then connected to the notion of corporate criminal responsibility.

There are two justifications for canceling crimes, notably those that excuse the crime and those that forgive the offender despite the Criminal Code's lack of classification [13]. Regarding the justifications mentioned therein, notably forcible defense. Forced defense is a defense that takes away an action's illegality. A forced defense called necessitas excusat aut exteneute absolves a person of criminal responsibility. Article 49, paragraph 1, of the Criminal Code, regulates “forced defense,” or "noodweer," which is defined as "whoever is forced to take action for defense because there is an attack or threat of an immediate attack which is against the law, against oneself or another person, against the honor of decency, one's property, or the property of another." Some people are not punished.

Second, following statutory instructions is exempt from punishment under Article 50 of the Criminal Code, which provides that no one shall be penalized for following statutory requirements. This article establishes that, in theory, a person who commits a criminal act that is carried out following a statutory order is not subject to punishment so long as the action is done for the benefit of the community and not the perpetrator's gain.

Third, a valid position order, following the rules outlined in Article 51, Paragraph 1 of the Criminal Code, a person who obeys or disobeys a command from a legitimate position or position of control, even though the order is a criminal offense may not be punished [14]. Another thing in the order of office is that the person receiving the order can receive the order directly from the person giving the order. That is, the charge is possible both in writing and verbally, including through various means of communication. According to Remmelink, in such a context, we come into contact with the theory of delegation of authority that the person receiving the order will likely issue rulings on behalf of the person giving the order. The leader in delegating authority does include not only the competency of providing orders but also the validity of all these orders [6].

The three arguments listed above, forced defense, obeying legislative instructions, and legal office directives, can all be used as explanations for abolishing corporate crime when connected to the notion of identity in corporate criminal culpability. According to identification theory, corporations can directly commit crimes through individuals close to the corporation, sometimes known as controlling officials [15]. They are regarded as the corporation itself so long as the acts conducted have something to do with the corporation. As a result, if the law permits the corporation's (Directing Mind's) acts, the corporate actions are not illegal. Because that person's acts (Directing Mind company) are perceived as corporate actions, Hasbullah contends that the justifications used to support individuals can be compared to those supporting businesses [8].

The reasons for forgiving someone are as follows: First, the inability to accept responsibility; if a person cannot be held accountable for his conduct, even though he has committed a crime, he cannot be punished. Every actor who cannot be held accountable for his conduct because he is mentally ill or had a flawed mind at the time of the act is subject to specific regulations made by legislators.

Second, Article 48 of the Criminal Code's rules on coercive force provides that anyone who violates the law while under the effect of coercive power shall not be prosecuted. It is not specified under Article 48 of the Criminal Code what is meant by "force." Coercion, on the other hand, is defined by MvT as "een kracht, een drang, een dwang waaraan meng geen weerstand kan bieden" (a force, an urge, a compulsion that cannot be resisted) [16].

Third, Article 49, paragraph (2) of the Criminal Code states that a forceful defense that goes over the allowed amount immediately brought on by severe mental shock due to an assault or threat of an attack is not considered criminal behavior. Overreaching forced defense can take one of two forms: either the attacker finds an intense shock while setting up their attack, turning it into an attack, or the defender is forced to encounter such a severe mental shock that he utilizes excessive self-protection right away [17].

An invalid position order is the fourth. A position order without power does not abolish the sentence, according to paragraph 2 of Article 51 of the Criminal Code; hence, it is unlawful. Unless the person being commanded honestly believes the order is valid and that carrying it out is within the scope of the job. According to this article, following an illegal position order is still criminal and cannot be used to justify not enforcing the law [9]. But let's say the individual who received the order did it in good faith because he believed it to be a directive from a legitimate official. The order's execution falls under the purview of his routine responsibilities. If such is the case, he won't be punished.

It concerns how the organ theory is applied to businesses. In that instance, the four justifications listed above can be used in theory as a foundation for criminal write-offs, including corporation law matters. This is because a corporation (a legal organization) is a physical substance that can commit crimes due to its flaws.

As a legal consequence, the justifications for the abolition of crimes that apply to human legal issues as outlined above also apply to corporate law subjects, as does the recognition of corporations as objects of criminal law. On the application level, though, much more effort will be required [18]. Consider the Dutch legal system as an example. Corporations may then present a defense based on eliminating crimes committed by human legal subjects, except psychological ones, as defined by Dutch criminal law.

As stated in Article 45 of the Draft Criminal Code, companies are recognized as subjects of criminal law under the Ius Constituendum, a draft for revising the Indonesian Criminal Code. Then, concerning the reasons for the elimination of crimes against corporations, they have also been taken into consideration in the Draft Criminal Code, as seen in Article 50 of the Draft Criminal Code, which states that justifications and excuses can be submitted by administrators who have open positions, issue orders, control holders, or the beneficial owner of the corporation, as long as the reason is directly related to the corporation.

Indonesia has controlled the grounds for criminal abolition of corporations in the ius constituendum. However, articulating these arguments continues to center on the idea of a real-world legal subject who collaborates with a company [5]. Lawmakers' policy direction is establishing a corporate responsibility model based on identification theory and vicarious liability theory in the justifications for criminal justice reform guidelines. Whether the criminal act is recognized as a corporate act (identification theory) or the person who committed the corporation is responsible (vicarious liability theory), both of these theories uphold the idea that corporations are held accountable for their criminal actions because of the actus reus of the people within the corporation, so it can be interpreted that the criminal acts of corporate personnel are also considered criminal acts by the corporation.

In the July 2022 revision of the Draft Criminal Code, the justification for forgiveness was eliminated. According to strict responsibility or vicarious liability, criminal culpability for corporations does not come from liability (liability without fault). Geen straf zonder schuld is not construed as "no criminal responsibility without guilt," according to the drafting team for the Draft Criminal Code, but rather as "no crime without fault." Corporate geen straf zonder schuld beginseel is therefore disregarded or altered. Although the new arrangements still seem hazy, the November 2022 revision of the Draft Criminal Code reorganizes the justifications for eliminating the offense in the form of excuses.

According to Putra & Linda [15], the application of excuses for corporations is based on the dualistic theory of corporate criminal responsibility, which is based on fault (liability based on the spot). In line with this opinion, Schaffmeister also believes that the reason for the abolition of punishment for legal entities (corporations) denies responsibility, not regarding criminals (daderschap). The defense utilizing a reason for the dissolution of the crime implicitly acknowledges being the maker of the crime. An exception to this can be a defense because of a criminal offense in a criminal act by negligence, which eliminates the element of negligence (culpa). In addition, the designation of a justification reason (rechtvaardigingsgrond) in a criminal act with features against the law is directed at the criminal, not the issue of responsibility. Based on Schaffmeister's opinion above, the writer concludes that excuses can be applied to corporations as the basis for criminal write-offs [20].

Regarding the reasons for forgiving as stipulated in the November 2022 version of the Draft Criminal Code, the author believes that the reasons for forgiving are not follow the characteristics of the corporation itself, therefore in this study, the authors offer several concepts of reasons for forgiving, including.

Corporations receive no profits

The vicarious culpability idea is the author's foundation for the concept of excuses. According to the vicarious responsibility doctrine, a corporation is liable for the decisions made by its administration as long as those decisions are made while the administrator is still acting in the course of their job or authority. Arlen concluded that, in several instances, the content of power did not always need the corporation to give its management explicit consent or authorization to commit the crime. Conversely, corporate criminal liability can be imposed if the regime that commits the crime does so in carrying out its duties and job obligations. Arlen added that if the management who commits the crime considers the act he has achieved as an obligation or a public work assignment that he usually does, the corporation can be held criminally liable [21].

Gobert stated that under the vicarious liability regime, the relationship between the ability to be responsible for a corporation and its management lies at the time of the occurrence of a crime. The perpetrator is still related to work relations with the corporation, and the action is carried out in pursuit of profit. In addition, in this doctrine, the things that must be fulfilled to transfer responsibility to the corporation are that it must fulfill the element that the criminal acts committed by its management must provide benefits to the corporation (for the use of the corporation).

Related to this element, Arlen (2012) argues that this condition must also be met, or even if it is not, at least the perpetrator who commits the crime aims to benefit the corporation. As long as the management commits a criminal act to provide direct benefits or benefits to the corporation, this element is still fulfilled, even though the management's actions are only driven by personal gain and the corporation that miracles do not realize the benefits it receives. Except if the corporation is the victim of a crime committed by its management.

In addition to the doctrines mentioned above, the author also bases the concept of excuses on the principle of identification theory. It is essential to understand in depth that, basically, in the doctrine of identification theory, corporations are not responsible for the actions of their management (corporate leaders). However, solely because the management's actions are identified as corporate actions (the corporate leadership is the corporation's identification), the corporation is responsible for its actions [19].

According to the identification doctrine, the relationship between a person's mental and physical states can be used to decide whose acts qualify as corporate actions. The mental state in question is typically classified as a "directing mind," "directing will," "ego center," or "control center" in this situation. In this situation, a company cannot be held accountable if the management engages in criminal activity not motivated by the corporation's objectives. Elements of a crime committed under the identity theory doctrine, as stated under the vicarious liability doctrine, must also be advantageous to the corporation [20]. Therefore, in the opinion of the author, if the corporation does not receive benefits or benefits from a crime committed by its management, then this should be an excuse for forgiveness as a reason for criminal write-offs for the corporation, especially considering the characteristics of the corporation as an artificial legal subject that has legal standing exceptional.

Actions carried out by agents outside of the authority granted by the corporation

According to this theory, corporate accountability is justifiable when it can better manage agent behavior than the government. This is especially true if the company has internal control, monitoring, or sanctions that allow it to watch how agents behave concerning corporate activities. However, it should also be emphasized that even while the principal's assets are sufficient to cover the relevant monetary sanctions, it may still be necessary to impose non-monetary sanctions when the principal (business) cannot control the agent's actions [22].

It should be emphasized that the relationship between the corporation and the high-ranking corporate executive (CEO) is related to the principal-agent relationship. However, the corporation could act as the principal and the high-ranking corporate officer (director) as the agent in the relationship.

This argument does not justify the imposition of individual responsibility on directors for the same activity, even though it is possible to hold the corporation criminally liable for an illegal act committed by an agent. Personal vicarious liability, which differs from corporate vicarious liability, is the imposition of criminal liability on high-ranking corporate leaders for an employee's criminal behavior.

The basis of corporate criminal accountability is incompatible with individual substitution criminal guilt, which is why. It would typically be presumed that the agent's offense was committed on behalf of the corporation or for the corporation's benefit by imposing an obligation on the corporation. However, it is inconceivable that the same crime was committed on behalf of or for the benefit of influential corporate figures like directors. In this situation, one can think of Sarre, who holds that imposing individual criminal liability on a senior corporate officer simply because the corporation has committed an offense is different from holding corporations criminally liable for violations of regulations committed by their senior officials [23].

According to this theory, a corporation differs from a collection of agents or individual agents based on complexity, formality, functioning, decision-making, and organizational structure. It also presupposes that agents who conduct crimes beyond the scope of their delegated authority must be exempt from corporate criminal culpability [24].

Agents who commit acts outside of the authority granted by the corporation are also known as the ultra vires act. The term "ultra vires" is derived from the Latin "ultra vires," which is typically rendered as "beyond authority" in Indonesian or, in English, "beyond (the agency's) legal powers," according to Timothy Endicott. Frank Mack (1930) has the following viewpoint concerning this: When appropriately used, the term "ultra vires" refers to a corporate act or transaction that would not be against the law or public policy if carried out by an individual but is instead outside the bounds of the corporation's legal authority as established by the applicable law, its charter, or its articles of incorporation [24].

The notion that a corporation is responsible for its actions if and only if the activity is carried out within the authority to carry out the matter as stipulated in the articles of association, or what is commonly known as intra vires, is discussed in the explanation provided by Frank Mack above [14]. Therefore, based on the theoretical basis above, if the actions committed by agents are outside the authority granted by the corporation, then this can be used as an excuse for the corporation itself.

Corporations have carried out their duties and obligations to prevent corporate crime from occurring

Corporations can take active actions to avoid criminal liability, namely: corporations can act not to allow criminal acts to occur; Corporations can take action to prevent their personnel from committing criminal acts or ensure that their personnel always comply with the law in carrying out their duties. These two things are a form of corporate error. However, if we look deeper, this form of error will eliminate the corporation's mistakes if actively carried out to stop criminal acts.

Corporate steps in stopping criminal acts can be in the form of the corporation immediately taking mandatory legal actions to prevent or report criminal acts or taking preventive measures in general or specifically. It can generally be in building legal compliance with its personnel. In particular, it can be in the form of preventing certain criminal acts within the corporation. Regarding compliance, in this circumstance, the corporation can still prove otherwise by proving that none of the criteria for error as stipulated in Article 1 paragraph (4) Supreme Court Regulation 13/2016 is owned by the corporation, one of these criteria is the existence of preventive measures that ensure compliance with the law.

So, implicitly, this reason can be used for forgiveness as a basis for the reason for criminal write-offs for corporations that have actively made efforts to prevent and prohibit criminal acts by corporate personnel. In theory, if there is a reason that can erase the maker's fault, then it can be said that this is an excuse. So the active action that is opposite can be an excuse for forgiveness which is a reason for criminal elimination for corporations.

Afwezigheid van alle schuld (avas)

In addition to the things mentioned above, excuses that may apply to corporate law subjects are afwezigheid van alle schuld (avas) or no fault at all, which is an excuse for criminal offenses in which corporate actors have made enough efforts not to commit crimes. This Avas is also called the pardonable heresy [25].

As previously explained, theoretically, there is no crime without guilt. This concept also applies to corporate crimes. Suppose in proof, the judge considers that the corporation has no fault, and the public prosecutor also fails to prove the men's rea of the defendant, who is a corporation. In that case, the corporation must also be freed from criminal liability. Thus Avas is a forgiving excuse that eliminates the corporate actor's reproachable element.

Conclusion

The Corporation has the same position as other legal subjects in Indonesia because the Corporation is recognized as a subject of criminal law in the provisions of rules and regulations outside the Criminal Code, namely, in this case, the issue of human law, including in this case regarding the reasons for erasing criminal. The justifications for eliminating crimes that pertain to human legal difficulties are mutatis mutandis, the legal ramifications of acknowledging corporations as objects of criminal law. The reasons for the abolition of crimes listed in Article 50 of the Draft Criminal Code have been considered in the draft, It specifies that officials who have an open post, have the authority to issue instructions, are in positions of control, or are the beneficiaries of business benefits may also present justifications for their actions. So long as the cause is directly connected to the offense for which the corporation has accused the corporation. The writers of this study develop the idea of justifications that adhere to the philosophy and features of the business itself since they consider that the grounds for forgiving mentioned in the article are not consistent with those of the corporation itself, namely: corporations do not receive profits, actions committed by an agent outside of the authority granted by the corporation, the corporation has carried out its duties and obligations to prevent corporate crime from occurring, and afwezigheid van alle schuld (avas).
