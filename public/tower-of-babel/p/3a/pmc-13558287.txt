Exploring the mechanism by which triphenyl phosphate promotes malignant phenotypes in bladder and kidney cancer through MMP9 based on bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation
Authors: Hao Wang, Hongquan Liu, Qian Li, Fengze Sun, Jian Ma, Jitao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Bladder and kidney cancer burden rises globally, with environmental toxicants driving their progression. Methods We integrated global epidemiological analysis, single-cell transcriptomics, cell–cell communication analysis, epithelial subclustering, pseudotime inference, toxicological target prediction, survival modeling, cross-cohort validation, single-cell virtual knockout, spatial transcriptomic deconvolution, molecular docking/dynamics, CETSA, and in vitro assays to define a shared molecular interface linking triphenyl phosphate (TPP) to bladder and kidney cancer. Results Both malignancies exhibited age- and SDI-associated burden patterns. Single-cell profiling identified conserved epithelial, stromal, and immune ecosystems, with tumor epithelial cells occupying central positions in intercellular communication networks. Epithelial subclustering revealed a reproducible EMT-high subcluster 4 in both cancers, which localized to a terminal-like pseudotime state and was associated with poor survival. Predicted TPP targets intersected with subcluster 4 signatures and converged on extracellular matrix organization, adhesion, and leukocyte transendothelial migration pathways. Integrative survival modeling and multi-cohort validation identified MMP9 as a robust prognostic candidate associated with tumor progression. Importantly, single-cell virtual knockout of MMP9 revealed convergent remodeling of proliferative, inflammatory, hypoxia-related, and stress-response programs across bladder and renal cancer epithelial cells, highlighting conserved regulatory circuitry. Spatial transcriptomics further localized MMP9 to macrophage- and fibroblast-enriched niches in both tumor types. Structural modeling and CETSA supported an interaction between TPP and MMP9. Experimentally, TPP upregulated MMP9 at both mRNA and protein levels in T24 and 786-O cells; higher concentrations reduced viability, whereas lower concentrations enhanced migration and clonogenic growth. Conclusions TPP promotes the malignant phenotypes of bladder and kidney cancer via MMP9, which is validated by virtual knockout and in vitro experiments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10238-026-02159-7.

Introduction

Bladder and renal cancers are two predominant urological tumors, collectively accounting for a substantial global cancer burden [1]. Despite distinct anatomical origins and histological characteristics, both tumors exhibit increasing incidence in many regions worldwide and share several epidemiological risk factors, including age, sex, and environmental exposure [2, 3]. Global Burden of Disease analyses have demonstrated marked socio-demographic gradients in the incidence of these cancers, particularly in high-development regions, suggesting that modernization-associated exposures may contribute to disease patterns beyond inherited susceptibility [4, 5]. As two major urogenital malignancies with rising global burden and partially shared exposure-related risk profiles, bladder and renal cancers provide a relevant comparative framework for exploring whether environmental toxicants converge on common malignant programs despite their distinct tissue origins.

Environmental pollutants act as key drivers of tumorigenesis and progression [6]. Among them, organophosphate flame retardants are widely used in consumer products, building materials, and electronic devices, leading to ubiquitous human exposure through indoor dust, air, and dietary intake [7]. TPP, a commonly detected organophosphate flame retardant, has been reported to exhibit endocrine-disrupting and cytotoxic properties in experimental systems [8, 9]. However, the molecular mechanisms through which TPP may influence tumor biology remain poorly characterized, particularly in urogenital malignancies. Most existing studies have focused on general toxicity endpoints rather than cancer-type–specific transcriptional programs or tumor microenvironment interactions [10, 11].

Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics have revealed that epithelial tumors are composed of heterogeneous cellular states with distinct functional properties [12–14]. In particular, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)–associated states have been implicated in invasion, immune modulation, and therapeutic resistance across diverse solid tumors [15–17]. Bladder and renal cancers, although arising from different epithelial lineages, both exhibit malignant progression closely linked to extracellular matrix remodeling, stromal interactions, and epithelial plasticity, making them a rational paired model for investigating whether toxicant-associated molecular perturbations converge on conserved epithelial programs across distinct urogenital tumor [18, 19].

In the present study, we integrated global epidemiological trends, single-cell transcriptomic profiling, toxicological target prediction, survival modeling, spatial transcriptomics, structural modeling, and functional assays to explore whether a shared molecular interface links TPP exposure to bladder and kidney cancer progression. Specifically, after identifying a shared epithelial state associated with malignant progression across both tumor types, we used TPP target prediction to examine whether this environmental toxicant may converge on the transcriptional programs enriched in that state. Rather than surveying all genitourinary malignancies, we focused on these two representative tumor types because they combine substantial disease burden, environmental exposure relevance, and tractable public multi-omics resources, while also providing a biologically meaningful contrast for identifying conserved epithelial plasticity-associated programs across distinct urogenital cancers. We identified a conserved epithelial subpopulation enriched for plasticity- and extracellular matrix–associated transcriptional programs across both malignancies and defined a subset of genes overlapping between predicted TPP targets and these epithelial signatures. Through regulatory network perturbation analysis, spatial localization mapping, molecular docking simulation, and in vitro phenotypic assays, we provide multi-layered evidence suggesting a convergent extracellular matrix–related regulatory axis that may represent a shared vulnerability in urogenital cancers within environmental exposure contexts.

Methods

Global burden of disease analysis

We extracted age-stratified incidence, mortality and ASR for bladder and renal cancers from the 2021 GBD database through the GHDx platform [20]. Data were extracted for both sexes across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. Socio-demographic index (SDI), a composite indicator of income per capita, educational attainment and total fertility rate, was used to stratify regions by development level. Age-specific incidence and mortality rates were calculated per 100,000 population. Age-standardized rates were computed using the GBD world standard population to enable cross-regional comparison independent of demographic structure. Temporal trends were visualized by plotting ASR against calendar year and SDI. To evaluate socioeconomic gradients, ASR was regressed against SDI at the country-year level, and trajectories over time were mapped to illustrate development-associated transitions in disease burden. Sex-stratified analyses were conducted globally and within SDI categories (Global, High SDI). For visualization of age patterns, we plotted age-specific rates across five-year age groups. Non-linear patterns between SDI and ASR were explored descriptively using smoothed scatter plots, allowing assessment of epidemiological transition dynamics. All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.5). Figures were generated using ggplot2 [21], with consistent scaling across cancer types to facilitate comparison.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Human scRNA-seq profiles of bladder cancer (GSE222315) [22] and renal cell carcinoma (GSE159115) [23] were downloaded from GEO. Raw count matrices were processed using the Seurat framework (v5.0) [24]. Cells with low transcript complexity or excessive mitochondrial gene expression were excluded to remove potential doublets and dying cells. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were discarded. Data were log-normalized and scaled. Highly variable genes were identified using variance-stabilizing transformation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction, and statistically significant principal components were selected based on elbow and JackStraw criteria. To reduce sample-specific batch effects within each cancer type, Harmony correction was applied after PCA prior to downstream clustering and visualization. Cell clustering was performed using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph and the Louvain algorithm at optimized resolution parameters. Non-linear dimensionality reduction was conducted using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) for visualization. Clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes curated from published renal and bladder tumor atlases. Cluster identities were assigned according to lineage-specific markers, including epithelial, fibroblast, endothelial, myeloid, T cell, B cell, mast cell, and NK cell markers. Dot plots were generated to display average expression and percentage of expressing cells across clusters. Clustering patterns were independently derived for each dataset. To assess reproducibility of cellular composition, marker expression distributions were compared across cohorts. Annotation was performed independently for each cancer type to avoid cross-projection bias. The bladder and renal single-cell datasets were analyzed separately rather than forcibly integrated across cancer types; therefore, cross-cancer comparisons in this study were based on convergence of lineage composition, pathway activity, pseudotime positioning, and clinically relevant epithelial programs, rather than on a shared cluster identity defined in a joint integrated space.

Cell–cell communication analysis in tumor tissues

Intercellular crosstalk in the tumor microenvironment was explored via ligand-receptor analysis on tumor-derived single-cell RNA sequencing datasets (GSE222315 and GSE159115). Cluster annotations established in the previous analysis were used as input cell identities. Only tumor samples were included. Genes expressed in fewer than 10% of cells within a given cluster were excluded to reduce noise from low-abundance transcripts. Cell–cell communication inference was conducted using a curated ligand–receptor interaction database integrated within the CellChat framework (v2.0) [25]. Normalized expression matrices were used to compute communication probability based on the law of mass action, incorporating both ligand expression in sender cells and receptor expression in receiver cells. Interaction probabilities were averaged at the cluster level to construct a weighted, directed communication network. Global interaction strength between cell types was quantified by summing inferred communication probabilities across all ligand–receptor pairs. Network centrality metrics were calculated to assess relative signaling roles of each cell type within the tumor microenvironment. For pathway-specific analysis, ligand–receptor pairs were filtered by signaling category (e.g., chemokine signaling, extracellular matrix interactions, antigen presentation). Differentially enriched interactions between epithelial cells and other compartments were visualized using bubble plots, where dot size represents statistical significance (− log10 adjusted P value) and color intensity represents inferred communication probability. All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.5). Network visualization was generated using built-in plotting functions and ggplot2.

Epithelial cell subclustering and pathway activity analysis

To characterize intratumoral heterogeneity within malignant epithelial compartments, epithelial cells were extracted from annotated single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of bladder cancer (GSE222315) and kidney cancer (GSE159115). Sample-specific batch effects within each cancer type were reduced by Harmony correction prior to downstream clustering (Supplementary Fig. 3). Cells previously assigned to the epithelial lineage based on canonical markers were subsetted for downstream analysis. The subsetted epithelial cells were re-normalized and scaled independently. Highly variable genes were re-identified within the epithelial compartment to avoid dominance by non-epithelial variance. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and significant principal components were selected based on variance explained and elbow criteria. Unsupervised clustering was conducted using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph with Louvain modularity optimization at optimized resolution parameters. Dimensionality reduction for visualization was performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). To assess functional heterogeneity among epithelial subclusters, pathway activity scores were calculated using gene set variation analysis (GSVA) [26]. Hallmark gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [27]. Pathway categories included EMT, hypoxia, MYC targets, G2M checkpoint, WNT/β-catenin signaling, TGF-β signaling, TNFα signaling via NFκB, IL6–JAK–STAT3 signaling, and Notch signaling. GSVA scores were computed for each cell and aggregated at the cluster level for heatmap visualization. Module scores were additionally calculated using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function to validate enrichment patterns at single-cell resolution. Differential pathway activity across subclusters was assessed using non-parametric tests with multiple testing correction. Key epithelial subclusters were defined based on consistent enrichment of EMT, hypoxia, and proliferation-associated signatures across analytical methods. All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.5). Therefore, the resulting clusters were defined by unsupervised re-clustering within the epithelial compartment after lineage-specific subsetting, rather than by clustering of all cells in the tumor microenvironment.2.4.

Epithelial trajectory analysis, bulk deconvolution, and differential expression profiling

To investigate differentiation relationships and transcriptional heterogeneity among epithelial subclusters, trajectory inference, bulk transcriptomic deconvolution, and differential expression analyses were performed on epithelial cells from bladder cancer (GSE222315) and kidney cancer (GSE159115). For trajectory inference, epithelial cells were converted into Monocle2 objects following re-clustering [28]. Monocle2 was used here as a graph-based pseudotime framework to model relative transcriptional continuity within epithelial cells, rather than to infer a true developmental lineage. Highly variable genes identified within the epithelial compartment were used to construct principal graphs. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis followed by UMAP embedding. A principal trajectory graph was learned using reversed graph embedding, and cells were ordered along pseudotime without imposing predefined lineage directionality. Accordingly, the inferred trajectory was used to describe relative positioning of epithelial transcriptional states within each dataset, rather than a definitive developmental hierarchy. Seurat-derived cluster identities were projected onto the inferred trajectory. For clinical association analysis, gene signatures were derived for each epithelial subcluster using differential expression analysis implemented in Seurat (Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction). Cluster-specific marker genes were defined using predefined adjusted P value and log2 fold change thresholds. Bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA-BLCA and TCGA-KIRC cohorts were normalized to TPM scale, and enrichment scores for epithelial subcluster 4 signatures were calculated using gene set–based scoring approaches. Patients were stratified into high and low groups according to median signature score. Overall survival was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier estimation with statistical significance assessed by the log-rank test. Comprehensive differential expression analysis was performed across all epithelial subclusters within each tumor type to characterize global transcriptional heterogeneity. Significantly upregulated genes for each subcluster were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with multiple testing correction.

In silico toxicity and target prediction of Triphenyl phosphate

The chemical structure of TPP was obtained from the PubChem database and converted into canonical SMILES for in silico analyses. Physiochemical properties and ADME profiles were evaluated using ADMETLab 3.0 [29], and acute and chronic toxicity endpoints were predicted using ProTox-III [30]. From ProTox-III, predicted median lethal dose (LD50), toxicity class, and probability scores for organ-specific toxicities (such as hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and mutagenicity) were recorded. Potential human protein targets of TPP were predicted using two independent resources: SwissTargetPrediction and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) [31, 32]. For SwissTargetPrediction, the TPP SMILES string was submitted with default parameters, and all human targets with predicted probability greater than 0 were retained for downstream analysis. This permissive threshold was used because SwissTargetPrediction served as an initial hypothesis-generating source, whereas target specificity was subsequently increased by intersection with CTD-supported targets and epithelial subcluster 4-associated genes from both cancers. From CTD, human genes annotated as interacting with or modulated by TPP were retrieved, and only genes supported by at least 2 were retained. This filtering step was applied to reduce dependence on sparse single-reference annotations and to improve the stability of the candidate toxicant-associated target set. All gene identifiers were standardized to official HGNC symbols. Intersecting genes were defined as the overlap among (i) high-confidence TPP targets, (ii) bladder cancer subcluster 4 DEGs, and (iii) kidney cancer subcluster 4 DEGs. This overlap strategy was used as a hypothesis-generating prioritization framework to enrich for candidates linking predicted TPP-associated perturbation with shared aggressive epithelial programs, rather than as proof of direct toxicological targeting. Functional enrichment analysis for the intersecting gene set was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment (biological process, cellular component, molecular function) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted with Benjamini–Hochberg corrected P values [33]; terms with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks for intersecting target genes were constructed using the STRING database (version 11.5) [34]. Only interactions with confidence scores ≥ 0.7 were included to ensure high-confidence connectivity.

Prognostic model construction and validation in bladder and kidney cancer

To evaluate the prognostic relevance of intersecting genes identified from the integrative network analysis, survival modeling was independently conducted in TCGA bladder cancer (TCGA-BLCA) and TCGA kidney cancer (TCGA-KIRC) cohorts. The input feature set for model construction was restricted to the intersecting genes derived from predicted TPP-associated targets and epithelial subcluster 4-associated transcriptional signatures. Corresponding clinical information and normalized transcriptomic data were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was first performed in each cancer type to identify genes associated with overall survival (P < 0.05) [35, 36]. Genes passing this initial screening step were then entered into downstream machine learning-based survival modeling pipelines. Candidate genes were subsequently subjected to integrative machine learning–based survival modeling. Multiple combinations of feature selection and modeling algorithms were evaluated, including Lasso-Cox, Ridge-Cox, Elastic Net, stepwise Cox regression (forward and backward), random survival forest (RSF), CoxBoost, generalized boosted models (GBM), and survival support vector machine (survival-SVM) [37–40]. This multi-model strategy was used to assess the stability of candidate prioritization across distinct survival-learning frameworks rather than to rely on a single modeling assumption. Model performance was assessed under framework-specific settings using tenfold cross-validation, and all candidate models were compared using a common concordance-based evaluation criterion. For each modeling framework, risk scores were calculated as weighted linear combinations of selected gene expression values based on model-derived coefficients or variable importance measures. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to the median risk score within each cohort. Model performance was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Mean C-index values were calculated across multiple cohorts to assess model stability. External validation was performed using independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) cohorts for bladder and kidney cancer [41]. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing was conducted to compare overall survival between risk groups. To further evaluate the independent prognostic value of individual candidate genes, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed where applicable. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.5), using packages including survival, glmnet, randomForestSRC, CoxBoost, timeROC, and survminer [42, 43]. Accordingly, the machine learning component in this study was intended to prioritize robust prognostic candidates from the biologically constrained intersecting gene set, rather than to establish mechanistic causality.

Expression, prognostic evaluation, and protein-level validation of MMP9

To further evaluate the clinical and biological relevance of MMP9, comprehensive transcriptomic and protein-level analyses were conducted across multiple independent cohorts. Normalized RNA sequencing data and clinical information for TCGA-BLCA and TCGA-KIRC cohorts were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons. Gene expression values were log2-transformed where appropriate. Differential expression analysis between tumor and normal tissues was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Associations between MMP9 expression and clinicopathological features, including tumor grade and stage, were assessed using Wilcoxon or Kruskal–Wallis tests as appropriate. External validation datasets were retrieved from the GEO, including GSE70691 [44], GSE167573 [45], GSE13507 [45], GSE19423 [46], GSE31684 [47], and GSE37815 [48]. Expression values were processed according to platform-specific normalization protocols. Survival analyses in external cohorts were conducted using Kaplan–Meier estimation with log-rank tests. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on median expression levels. Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed using the timeROC package to assess the prognostic performance of MMP9 at predefined time points. Area under the curve (AUC) values and confidence intervals were calculated. For immunotherapy-related validation, transcriptomic and clinical data from the IMvigor210 cohort were obtained from the IMvigor210CoreBiologies package [49]. Associations between MMP9 expression and clinical response or survival outcomes were analyzed using non-parametric tests and Kaplan–Meier methods. Protein-level localization and expression patterns of MMP9 were examined using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database [50]. Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence images were retrieved to assess subcellular distribution and protein expression across cell lines. Image interpretation was performed descriptively according to publicly available annotations. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.5).

Single-cell virtual knockout analysis of MMP9

To investigate the functional role of MMP9 within tumor epithelial subpopulations, we first examined its expression distribution across epithelial cell subclusters in bladder and kidney cancer single-cell RNA sequencing datasets. Expression levels and percentage of expressing cells were visualized using dimensional reduction plots and dot plots. To infer gene regulatory consequences of MMP9 perturbation at single-cell resolution, we performed virtual knockout analysis using the scTenifoldKnk framework [51]. Briefly, normalized count matrices of epithelial cells were extracted separately for bladder and kidney cancer datasets. Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) were constructed using principal component regression–based modeling. A virtual knockout of MMP9 was computationally simulated by removing its regulatory influence from the inferred network. Differential regulatory effects were quantified as signed Z-scores representing regulatory change magnitude. Genes were ranked according to absolute Z-score values, and significantly up- and down-regulated genes were identified based on permutation-derived thresholds. To characterize pathway-level perturbations, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on ranked Z-scores using Hallmark gene sets [52]. Ridge plots were generated to visualize enrichment distribution patterns. All analyses were performed independently for bladder cancer and kidney cancer datasets to ensure cancer-type–specific inference.

Spatial transcriptomic deconvolution analysis

To characterize the spatial distribution of tumor-associated cell populations, spatial transcriptomic datasets from GSE171351 [53] (bladder cancer) and GSE210041 [54] (kidney cancer) were analyzed. Raw spatial gene expression matrices were processed using the standard Space Ranger output format. Cell-type deconvolution was performed using the cell2location framework [55]. Briefly, reference single-cell RNA sequencing datasets were used to construct cell-type–specific expression signatures. Reference signatures were derived from the corresponding cancer-type single-cell datasets after quality control and lineage annotation, and only genes passing model-specific filtering criteria were retained for deconvolution training. A Bayesian hierarchical model was applied to estimate the abundance of each cell type in individual spatial spots. Posterior mean cell abundance values were extracted and visualized as spatial feature maps. The present spatial analysis was intended to provide supportive evidence for the microenvironmental context of MMP9 expression rather than a formal spatial co-localization model. Dominant cell types per spatial location were defined based on maximum posterior abundance. Spatial expression patterns of MMP9 were overlaid with inferred cell-type abundance maps to assess co-localization patterns. All analyses were performed using default model hyperparameters unless otherwise specified, following the recommended workflow of the cell2location pipeline.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

The binding interaction of TPP and MMP9 was verified by molecular docking and 100-ns MD simulation. The three-dimensional crystal structure of human MMP9 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [56]. Protein structures were prepared by removing water molecules and adding hydrogen atoms. The chemical structure of TPP was retrieved from the PubChem database and energy-minimized prior to docking. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina [57]. The docking grid was centered on the catalytic domain of MMP9. Binding affinity scores were calculated, and the top-ranked binding conformation was selected for visualization and subsequent simulation. For molecular dynamics simulations, the protein–ligand complex was parameterized using the AMBER force field. The system was solvated in an explicit water box with appropriate counterions added to neutralize the system. Energy minimization was performed, followed by equilibration under NVT and NPT ensembles. A 100-ns production simulation was conducted at 300 K. All structural visualizations were generated using PyMOL and trajectory analyses were performed using GROMACS utilities [58, 59].

Cell culture and drug treatment

Human bladder cancer T24 cells and human renal cancer 786-O cells were cultured in complete medium under standard conditions at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. To evaluate the biological effects of TPP, cells were exposed to gradient concentrations of the drug after attachment. For the qRT-PCR experiments, T24 cells were treated with 0, 2, 4, and 8 μM, whereas 786-O cells were treated with 0, 1, 2, and 4 μM. For western blot validation, cells were divided into control and treatment groups and harvested after the indicated exposure period. For cell viability assays, a broader concentration gradient was used and viability was measured at 24 h and 48 h after treatment.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from treated T24 and 786-O cells using a standard RNA extraction reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed prior to reverse transcription. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from equal amounts of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was then performed using a SYBR Green-based detection system on a real-time PCR platform. The mRNA expression level of MMP9 was normalized to an internal reference gene, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed with independent biological replicates.

Western blot analysis

After treatment, cells were lysed in protein extraction buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Total protein concentration was quantified using a standard protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with non-fat milk or bovine serum albumin, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MMP9 and β-actin. After washing, membranes were incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system. β-actin was used as the loading control.

Cellular thermal shift assay

To further assess whether TPP affects the thermal stability of MMP9 in bladder and renal cancer cells, a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was performed in T24 and 786-O cells. Cells were treated with DMSO or TPP under the indicated conditions and then harvested in PBS or an appropriate assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Aliquots from each group were heated at a temperature gradient (37, 42, 45, 48, 51, 54, 57, and 62 °C) for a short period, followed by cooling to room temperature. After freeze–thaw or lysis, samples were centrifuged to separate soluble fractions from precipitated proteins. The supernatants were collected and subjected to western blot analysis for MMP9, with β-actin used as a loading reference where appropriate. Band intensities were quantified by densitometry, normalized to the signal at 37 °C, and plotted against temperature to generate thermal stability curves.

Cell viability assay

We assessed cell viability with the CCK-8 kit. T24 and 786-O cells were seeded into 96-well plates at an appropriate density (approximately 1 × 103 cells per well) and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of TPP (T24: 0, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 μM; 786-O: 0, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 μM, adjusted according to preliminary experiments) for 24 h and 48 h.

After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the untreated control group. All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated independently at least three times.

Migration assay

Cell migration was measured via the scratch assay. T24 and 786-O cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 90% confluence. A sterile 200-μL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch across the cell monolayer. Detached cells were removed by gentle washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Cells were then cultured in serum-reduced medium containing indicated concentrations of TPP (T24: 0, 2, 4, 8 μM; 786-O: 0, 1, 2, 4 μM). Images were captured at 0 h, 12 h, and 24 h using a phase-contrast microscope. Wound area was quantified using ImageJ software, and migration was expressed as the percentage of wound closure relative to the initial wound area. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Colony formation assay

For colony formation assays, T24 and 786-O cells were seeded into 6-well plates at low density (approximately 1000 cells per well) and cultured in complete medium containing indicated concentrations of TPP (T24: 0, 2, 4, 8 μM; 786-O: 0, 1, 2, 4 μM).

Cells were maintained for 12 days with medium replacement every 2–3 days. At the end of incubation, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted under a microscope. Colony numbers were quantified and normalized to the control group. All experiments were performed independently at least three times.

Results

Socio-demographic gradients shape the global burden of bladder and kidney cancer

An overview of the study design and analytical workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.We first examined the global epidemiological landscape of bladder and kidney cancer using GBD 2021 data. Age-specific incidence and mortality rates demonstrated a pronounced age dependency in both malignancies (Fig. 2A). Incidence rose progressively after midlife and increased sharply beyond 60 years of age, with the highest burden observed in elderly populations. Across nearly all age strata, males exhibited higher incidence and mortality rates than females. When stratified by socio-demographic index (SDI), clear gradients emerged. Countries with higher SDI generally displayed elevated age-standardized incidence rates (ASR), whereas mortality rates showed a comparatively attenuated increase (Fig. 2B–C). This divergence suggests that improved diagnostic capacity and therapeutic access in developed regions may partially offset fatality despite higher disease detection. Temporal trajectories from 1990 to 2021 further revealed a dynamic shift along the SDI spectrum. Early years clustered predominantly in lower SDI settings with lower ASR, whereas more recent years showed dispersion toward higher SDI regions with greater incidence. Kidney cancer exhibited a steeper SDI-associated slope relative to bladder cancer, indicating stronger coupling between socioeconomic development and disease burden. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling delineates conserved tumor ecosystems

To characterize the cellular architecture underlying these malignancies, we analyzed independent single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from kidney cancer and bladder cancer. Unsupervised clustering resolved 14 transcriptionally distinct populations in kidney cancer and 13 populations in bladder cancer (Fig. 2D–E). Dimensionality reduction revealed discrete epithelial, stromal, and immune compartments in both tumor types, indicating preserved lineage segregation within the tumor microenvironment. Marker gene expression supported robust annotation of cellular identities (Fig. 2F–G). Epithelial clusters were enriched for canonical cytokeratins and epithelial adhesion markers. Fibroblast populations expressed extracellular matrix components and stromal activation markers. Endothelial cells exhibited vascular-associated transcripts, whereas myeloid populations expressed macrophage lineage markers. Adaptive immune subsets were defined by T- and B-cell–specific transcripts, and mast cell clusters were characterized by protease expression signatures. Despite tumor-specific heterogeneity, both cancers shared a conserved multicellular framework composed of malignant epithelial cells embedded within stromal and immune compartments. However, relative immune infiltration appeared more heterogeneous in kidney cancer, whereas bladder tumors displayed more prominent epithelial dominance.

Tumor epithelial cells exhibit extensive intercellular communication within the microenvironment

To further delineate tumor microenvironment organization, we analyzed cell–cell communication networks within tumor tissues from bladder cancer (GSE222315) and kidney cancer (GSE159115). t-SNE projections confirmed the presence of epithelial, stromal, endothelial, and multiple immune compartments in both tumor types (Fig. 3A, E). Sample-level composition analysis revealed inter-patient variability in immune and stromal fractions, while epithelial cells constituted a substantial proportion across most tumors (Fig. 3B, F). Network analysis of inferred ligand–receptor interactions demonstrated extensive intercellular signaling in both cancers (Fig. 3C, D). Epithelial cells displayed prominent outgoing and incoming interaction weights, indicating central positioning within the communication network. Stromal populations, particularly fibroblasts and endothelial cells, also contributed substantial interaction strength. Focused analysis of epithelial-derived signaling revealed multiple ligand–receptor axes connecting epithelial cells to immune and stromal compartments (Fig. 3G, H). Interactions involving chemokine signaling, extracellular matrix components, integrins, antigen presentation molecules, and growth factor pathways were detected across tumor types. Epithelial subclustering reveals functional heterogeneity and convergent EMT-high states across tumor types

To resolve intra-epithelial heterogeneity, we subsetted epithelial cells from bladder cancer and kidney cancer and performed re-clustering at higher resolution (Fig. 4A, C). Thus, all clusters shown in Fig. 4 represent epithelial-cell-derived subclusters rather than clusters from the full tumor cell population. This analysis delineated multiple epithelial subclusters with distinct transcriptional programs, indicating substantial functional diversity within the tumor epithelial compartment. We then quantified pathway activities across epithelial subclusters using Hallmark-based scoring. Cluster-level enrichment patterns (GSVA) revealed coherent modules spanning epithelial plasticity, inflammatory signaling, and cell-cycle–related programs, including epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), hypoxia, MYC targets, G2M checkpoint, TNFα signaling via NFκB, IL6–JAK–STAT3 signaling, WNT/β-catenin signaling, TGF-β signaling, and Notch signaling (Fig. 4B, D). Notably, EMT activity was not uniformly distributed across subclusters, but instead concentrated in a subset of epithelial states in each cancer type. Single-cell–level visualization of representative signatures further supported these patterns. Feature-style maps of module scores showed spatially restricted enrichment of EMT and hypoxia programs within specific epithelial regions, whereas proliferative signatures (MYC targets and G2M checkpoint) displayed distinct distributions (Fig. 4E, F). Consistently, violin plots confirmed marked inter-cluster differences in module scores, emphasizing that EMT-related activity represents a discriminating axis of epithelial heterogeneity rather than a global epithelial property (Fig. 4G, H). Across both bladder and kidney tumors, epithelial subcluster 4 emerged as a reproducible EMT-enriched state, showing relatively higher EMT module scores compared with most other epithelial subclusters (Fig. 4B, D, G, H). Here, “reproducible” or “conserved” refers to convergence of EMT-associated transcriptional enrichment, terminal-like pseudotime localization, and adverse clinical association across the two separately analyzed epithelial datasets, rather than to a formally shared cluster identity derived from cross-cancer joint integration. Epithelial subcluster 4 occupies a terminal pseudotime state and associates with adverse clinical outcome

To further define the biological positioning and clinical relevance of epithelial subclusters, trajectory inference was performed within the epithelial compartments of bladder and kidney tumors. Principal graph reconstruction revealed structured differentiation landscapes in both cancer types (Fig. 5A, B). Projection of epithelial subclusters onto the inferred trajectories demonstrated non-uniform spatial distributions along the pseudotime axis. Notably, subcluster 4 consistently localized to one extremity of the trajectory in both datasets (Fig. 5C, D), indicating that it represents a transcriptionally distinct terminal-like epithelial state within the inferred continuum rather than an intermediate population. This interpretation is based on relative pseudotime positioning within the inferred transcriptional continuum rather than on a confirmed developmental endpoint. To evaluate the clinical implications of this epithelial state, subcluster 4–specific gene signatures were derived and applied to TCGA-BLCA and TCGA-KIRC cohorts. Patients with higher subcluster 4 signature scores exhibited significantly reduced overall survival compared with those with lower scores in both tumor types (Fig. 5F, G), supporting an association between enrichment of this epithelial state and adverse clinical outcome. Comprehensive differential expression analysis across all epithelial subclusters further revealed marked transcriptional heterogeneity within the epithelial compartment (Fig. 5E, H). Distinct gene expression programs characterized individual subclusters, reflecting diverse functional states. Within this broader heterogeneity framework, subcluster 4 displayed a coherent transcriptional profile consistent with its terminal positioning along pseudotime and its previously observed enrichment of EMT-related programs. Collectively, these analyses delineate structured epithelial diversity and identify subcluster 4 as a reproducible terminal-like epithelial state associated with unfavorable prognosis in both bladder and kidney cancer. Organ-specific toxicity prediction indicated potential associations with hepatotoxicity and respiratory toxicity endpoints, whereas predicted probabilities for mutagenicity and carcinogenicity were comparatively low (Fig. 6B–C). To explore potential molecular targets, human protein targets of TPP were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). The intersection of these two resources was defined as a high-confidence TPP target set. Differential expression analyses identified epithelial subcluster 4–specific genes separately in bladder and kidney tumors. Integrative survival modeling identifies MMP9 as a reproducible prognostic marker

To prioritize biologically and clinically relevant candidates within the intersecting gene set derived from predicted TPP targets and epithelial subcluster 4 signatures, integrative survival modeling was performed independently in TCGA bladder cancer and kidney cancer cohorts. The use of multiple modeling frameworks was intended to evaluate the consistency of candidate prioritization across different survival-learning assumptions, rather than to selectively report a single favorable model. Multiple combinations of feature selection and survival modeling algorithms were evaluated, and model performance was compared using cross-validated C-index values across training and validation cohorts (Fig. 7A; Fig. 8A). Consistent survival discrimination between high- and low-risk groups was observed in both cancer types (Fig. 7C–E; Fig. 8C–E), and time-dependent ROC analysis further supported the predictive performance of the constructed models (Fig. 7F–I; Fig. 8F–H). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified several intersecting genes significantly associated with overall survival in both cancers (Fig. 7B; Fig. 8B). Among these, MMP9 consistently demonstrated a significant hazard ratio in both tumor types and was retained across multiple modeling frameworks. Importantly, MMP9 was not prioritized solely because of its prognostic performance in bulk cohorts, but because it simultaneously belonged to the overlap between predicted TPP targets and epithelial subcluster 4 differentially expressed genes in both cancers, while also showing the strongest enrichment in epithelial subcluster 4 in the single-cell datasets. Although MAP1A was also ranked among the potentially relevant candidates, it did not show the same degree of cross-layer consistency as MMP9, particularly with respect to preferential enrichment in epithelial subcluster 4 across both tumor types and cross-cohort bulk expression support. Correlation analysis further indicated that MMP9 expression positively associated with risk score in both cohorts (Fig. 7J; Fig. 8I). Matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) is a member of the matrix metalloproteinase family involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and tumor microenvironment regulation [60]. Previous studies have linked elevated MMP9 expression to tumor invasion, metastasis, and poor clinical outcome in various solid malignancies, including bladder and kidney cancer [61–63]. The recurrent identification of MMP9 within independent modeling strategies, together with its established role in extracellular matrix dynamics, suggests that it may represent a biologically relevant component of the TPP–epithelial subcluster 4 interaction axis. Structural modeling and CETSA support an interaction between MMP9 and Triphenyl phosphate

To explore potential molecular interactions between MMP9 and TPP, molecular docking was performed targeting the catalytic region of MMP9. Docking analysis identified a plausible binding configuration within the active-site pocket, with a predicted binding energy of − 8.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 11K–M). The ligand was positioned in proximity to residues including HIS-226, HIS-236, and GLN-227, forming potential hydrogen-bond and hydrophobic contacts. A 100-ns molecular dynamics simulation was conducted to assess stability of the protein–ligand complex. RMSD analysis indicated structural stabilization following initial equilibration (Fig. 11N). Radius of gyration (Rg) values remained relatively constant throughout the trajectory (Fig. 11O), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) fluctuations were modest (Fig. 11P). Hydrogen bond analysis demonstrated intermittent but persistent interactions over time (Fig. 11Q). Residue-level flexibility (RMSF) showed localized fluctuations without global destabilization (Fig. 11R), and free energy landscape mapping revealed energetically favorable conformational states (Fig. 11S). Together, these structural analyses support a stable modeled interaction between TPP and the catalytic domain of MMP9.

Triphenyl Phosphate induces MMP9 expression in T24 and 786-O cells

To verify the candidate target identified by network toxicology, we next assessed MMP9 expression in TP-treated T24 and 786-O cells. These experiments were designed to determine whether TPP exposure could directly induce the key candidate emerging from the TPP–epithelial subcluster 4 analytical framework. qRT-PCR results demonstrated that TP exposure significantly induced MMP9 mRNA expression in both cell models. In T24 cells, MMP9 expression remained largely unchanged at lower concentration but increased significantly at higher concentrations, whereas in 786-O cells, TP elicited a more evident inductive effect across the tested doses. Western blotting further showed that MMP9 protein levels were elevated after TP treatment in both T24 and 786-O cells relative to controls (Fig. 12A-C). Taken together, these data provide experimental support for the computational prediction and suggest that MMP9 is a responsive downstream effector of TP exposure in bladder and renal cancer cells.

TPP exhibits dose-dependent effects on proliferation and enhances migratory and clonogenic potential at low concentrations

Cell viability was first assessed using the CCK-8 assay in T24 and 786-O cells to define the dose range at which TPP exerts cytotoxic effects versus sub-cytotoxic biological responses relevant to malignant phenotype modulation. In T24 cells, higher concentrations of TPP reduced cell viability at both 24 h and 48 h, with a more pronounced decrease observed at 48 h (Fig. 12H). A similar trend was observed in 786-O cells, where elevated concentrations led to reduced viability, particularly at 48 h (Fig. 12I). These results indicate that TPP exerts inhibitory effects on cell viability at higher doses.

Based on the viability results, lower concentration ranges that did not produce overt cytotoxicity were subsequently used to evaluate whether TPP could promote malignant phenotypes associated with epithelial plasticity. Wound healing assays were subsequently performed to evaluate migratory capacity. In T24 cells, treatment with 4 μM and 8 μM resulted in increased wound closure compared with the control group at 24 h (Fig. 13A–B), indicating enhanced migratory ability at these concentrations. Colony formation assays further revealed that TPP promoted long-term proliferative potential at low to moderate concentrations. In T24 cells, colony numbers increased progressively at 4 μM and 8 μM (Fig. 13E–F). Similarly, in 786-O cells, 2 μM and 4 μM treatment significantly elevated colony formation compared with the untreated group (Fig. 13G–H).

Taken together, these findings indicate that TPP exerts dose-dependent effects in bladder and renal cancer cells: higher concentrations impair short-term viability, whereas lower concentrations enhance migratory and clonogenic capacities. This biphasic pattern is consistent with the notion that sub-cytotoxic TPP exposure may potentiate malignant phenotypes associated with epithelial plasticity, thereby functionally supporting the TPP–MMP9–subcluster 4 axis proposed from the integrative analyses.

Discussion

Herein, we established a multi-omics integrated strategy to explore the shared molecular basis linking environmental toxicant exposure with bladder and kidney cancer progression. By combining global epidemiological analysis, single-cell transcriptomics, in silico toxicology, integrative survival modeling, spatial transcriptomics, virtual knockout simulation, structural modeling, and in vitro functional assays, we identified a convergent ECM–epithelial plasticity axis centered on MMP9 that is consistently implicated across both malignancies. The combined analysis of bladder and renal cancers in this study was not intended to represent the full spectrum of genitourinary malignancies, but rather to test whether two high-burden urogenital tumors with distinct tissue origins nonetheless converge on shared toxicant-responsive epithelial programs.

The Global Burden of Disease analysis revealed pronounced age dependency and clear socio-demographic gradients for both bladder and kidney cancer. Higher SDI regions exhibited increased incidence but relatively moderated mortality trends, suggesting that modernization-related exposures may contribute to disease burden while healthcare access influences outcomes. Notably, kidney cancer demonstrated a steeper SDI-associated slope compared with bladder cancer, implying stronger coupling with development-linked environmental factors. Given the widespread use of organophosphate flame retardants such as TPP in industrialized environments, these epidemiological patterns provide contextual support for investigating toxicant-associated mechanisms in urogenital malignancies [64, 65].

At single-cell resolution, both tumor types displayed conserved multicellular ecosystems composed of epithelial, stromal, endothelial, and immune compartments. Within the epithelial lineage, higher-resolution subclustering revealed substantial functional heterogeneity. Importantly, a reproducible epithelial subpopulation (subcluster 4) emerged in both cancers, characterized by enrichment of EMT, hypoxia, and inflammatory signaling programs. This subcluster localized to a terminal position along the inferred pseudotime trajectory and was associated with inferior overall survival in TCGA cohorts. We acknowledge that pseudotime inference in tumors does not recapitulate a true developmental lineage and should instead be interpreted as a relative ordering of transcriptional states. Therefore, the terminal-like designation used here refers to the distal positioning of subcluster 4 within the inferred epithelial continuum, rather than to a definitive terminal differentiation fate. These findings indicate that EMT-enriched epithelial states represent clinically relevant phenotypes shared across anatomically distinct tumors rather than stochastic transcriptional variation. The CellChat analysis in this study was primarily used to show that epithelial cells occupied a central position in the intercellular communication landscape of both tumors, thereby providing contextual support for focusing on epithelial-state heterogeneity; however, we did not intend this analysis to define or validate a specific ligand–receptor axis.

More broadly, renal cancer biology is shaped by marked subtype heterogeneity and metabolic reprogramming, and emerging evidence suggests that these programs may intersect with stromal remodeling and invasive phenotypes; although these aspects were not directly interrogated in the present study, they provide a broader contextual framework for interpreting epithelial plasticity-associated progression in renal malignancies [66].

Integration of predicted TPP targets with differentially expressed genes from the EMT-high epithelial subcluster in both cancers identified a convergent set of genes enriched in ECM organization, collagen-containing matrix, integrin signaling, and focal adhesion pathways. These enrichment patterns align closely with EMT-associated structural remodeling and cell–matrix interaction programs. The ECM functions not only as structural support but also as a dynamic signaling interface regulating migration, immune infiltration, and mechanotransduction [18, 67–69]. The convergence between toxicant-predicted targets and EMT-enriched ECM programs suggests that environmental exposure may intersect with pre-existing epithelial plasticity circuits, potentially amplifying invasive phenotypes. We acknowledge, however, that this intersection-based strategy is hypothesis-generating and may be biased toward genes that are already highly expressed in aggressive epithelial states, particularly extracellular matrix-related genes; therefore, it should be interpreted as a prioritization framework rather than definitive evidence of direct toxicological convergence.

Among the intersecting genes, MMP9 consistently emerged as a reproducible prognostic factor across bladder and kidney cancer. Elevated MMP9 expression correlated with adverse survival across TCGA and external cohorts and associated with higher tumor grade and stage. Spatial transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that MMP9 expression was preferentially enriched in macrophage- and fibroblast-dominant regions in both cancers, rather than uniformly distributed across epithelial compartments. This conserved spatial localization indicates that MMP9 operates within stromal–immune interaction niches at the tumor–microenvironment interface. Given the established role of MMP9 in ECM degradation, invasion, and inflammatory signaling, its recurrent identification across independent analytical layers supports its biological centrality within the shared regulatory framework [70–72].

Virtual knockout analysis further reinforced this regulatory role. Computational deletion of MMP9 in epithelial cells from both bladder and kidney cancer datasets resulted in convergent perturbation of proliferative and inflammatory transcriptional programs, including G2M checkpoint, E2F targets, TNFα signaling via NF-κB, and hypoxia pathways. The recurrence of these pathway-level shifts across independent tumor types suggests that MMP9 is embedded within conserved regulatory circuitry governing epithelial plasticity and stress adaptation. These results extend beyond correlative expression analysis and provide network-level evidence for MMP9 as a functional regulator within EMT-associated states.

Structural modeling supported the plausibility of an interaction between TPP and the catalytic domain of MMP9. In addition, CETSA showed that TPP treatment increased the apparent thermal stability of MMP9 in both T24 and 786-O cells, providing orthogonal cellular evidence consistent with target engagement. Although CETSA does not by itself establish the precise binding mode, the concordance between docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and thermal stabilization strengthens the rationale for considering MMP9 as a biologically relevant node linking TPP exposure to malignant phenotypes.

In vitro functional assays revealed a concentration-dependent dual response. Higher concentrations impaired short-term cell viability, whereas lower concentrations enhanced migration and clonogenic capacity in both T24 and 786-O cells. This biphasic pattern is consistent with hormetic responses observed for certain environmental toxicants, in which sub-cytotoxic exposure activates adaptive, migratory, or proliferative signaling pathways. Within the context of EMT-enriched epithelial states, low-dose exposure may potentiate cell–matrix interaction programs without inducing overt cytotoxic stress, aligning with the ECM–plasticity axis identified in our integrative analyses. Collectively, these findings support a unified model in which bladder and kidney cancers share a conserved EMT-high epithelial state that is transcriptionally and clinically significant. Predicted TPP targets converge on ECM and adhesion-related programs enriched within this state, with MMP9 serving as a central regulatory node linking matrix remodeling, inflammatory signaling, and proliferative control. Spatial, network, structural, and functional evidence converge to suggest that environmental toxicants may preferentially perturb epithelial plasticity circuits common to both malignancies. In this context, the low-dose enhancement of migration and clonogenicity provides phenotypic support for the transcriptomic observations that TPP-associated perturbation converges on an EMT-high epithelial state and its MMP9-centered regulatory program.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Toxicity predictions, docking analyses, and virtual knockout simulations are computational and require biochemical and genetic validation. Spatial transcriptomic datasets were limited in sample size, and in vitro assays were performed in two cell lines, which cannot fully recapitulate tumor ecosystem complexity. Future studies incorporating genetic perturbation of MMP9, organoid or co-culture systems, and in vivo exposure models will be necessary to validate the proposed toxicant–ECM–EMT axis and clarify causal relationships. In particular, we did not perform siRNA-mediated knockdown, CRISPR-based editing, or inhibitor-based rescue experiments targeting MMP9 in the present study. Therefore, although our multi-layered analyses support an MMP9-centered regulatory axis associated with TPP exposure and malignant phenotypes, they do not yet establish that the observed effects are definitively mediated through MMP9. In addition, because the bladder and renal single-cell datasets were analyzed separately rather than through formal cross-cancer integration, the conserved epithelial state described here should be interpreted as biological convergence across independently processed datasets rather than as a strictly shared cluster identity. In addition, because the primary renal single-cell discovery dataset used here was derived from clear cell renal cell carcinoma, the broader generalizability of renal epithelial subcluster 4-associated genes across additional kidney cancer subtypes remains uncertain. Our supplementary comparison across ccRCC, pRCC, and chRCC suggested subtype heterogeneity rather than complete consistency, and therefore broader subtype-level validation will be necessary in future studies. The cell–cell communication analysis was descriptive and was not followed by functional validation of specific ligand–receptor interactions; therefore, its contribution in the present study should be interpreted as contextual rather than definitive mechanistic evidence.

Overall, this study integrates epidemiological, single-cell, computational, spatial, and experimental evidence to propose a conserved molecular interface linking environmental toxicant exposure to epithelial plasticity in bladder and kidney cancer, highlighting MMP9 as a shared mechanistic node worthy of further investigation.
