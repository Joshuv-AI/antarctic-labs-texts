Mitochondrial dysfunction, a new marker warning of neuropsychiatric disorder risk: evidence from genetics and epidemiology
Authors: Qingan Fu, Jizhen Li, Huangxin Zhu, Qingyun Yu, Tianzhou Shen, Zhekang Liu, Yue Liu, Wei Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in the pathogenesis of a variety of neuropsychiatric disorders, but its causal role remains unclear. Mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNA-CN) and methylmalonic acid (MMA) are well-recognized biomarkers of mitochondrial function, but their association with psychiatric disorders has not yet been fully assessed.MethodsWe performed two-step two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses using genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to assess causal associations between mtDNA-CN and 13 major neuropsychiatric disorders. In addition, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data 2011–2014 to examine the association between serum MMA levels and cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms to further validate the correctness and robustness of the results of the MR analysis.ResultsMR analysis showed a significant negative causal effect of mtDNA-CN on bipolar disorder, Alzheimer’s disease, dementia, depressive symptoms, and autism spectrum disorders (OR ranged from 0.15 to 0.84, all p < 0.05). Reverse MR analysis showed that only depressive symptoms had a significant causal effect on reducing mtDNA-CN. NHANES analysis further showed that higher MMA levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment (OR = 1.56, p = 0.036) and depression (OR = 1.53, p = 0.020), suggesting that mitochondrial dysfunction and neuropsychiatric disorders have a close association.ConclusionThe mitochondrial function biomarkers mtDNA-CN and MMA are expected to be potential therapeutic targets for depression and cognitive dysfunction, emphasizing the need for mitochondrial function monitoring and interventions in future therapies targeting neuropsychiatric disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-025-00604-7.

Introduction

Mitochondria (Mt) are important organelles within cells that are primarily involved in a variety of key physiological processes, including oxidative phosphorylation, which synthesizes adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through the oxidation of electron carriers such as NADH and FADH, as well as cell differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, reprogramming, and immune regulation [1]. Mitochondrial dysfunction refers to the impairment of mitochondrial function in multiple physiological pathways such as energy metabolism, oxidative stress regulation and cell homeostasis [2]. Due to its important role, mitochondrial dysfunction is often considered a warning bell for impaired human health, revealing the potential increased risk of multiple diseases [3, 4]. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is the mitochondrial double-stranded circular genome, with a length of 16.6 kb in humans [5, 6]. MtDNA copy number (mtDNA-CN, mitochondrial abundance) is the ratio of mitochondrial to nuclear DNA copy number, which is correlated with the number of mitochondria, cellular energy production, and metabolic status and can be used as a surrogate biomarker of mitochondrial function because it indirectly reflects the degree of mitochondrial dysfunction [7, 8]. Although there are currently some limitations in terms of the cost and technical accessibility of mtDNA-CN measurement, its close association with various chronic disease phenotypes highlights its enormous potential for clinical application and it is still expected to become a biomarker for the early onset and development of diseases [9]. Large-scale cross-sectional and Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have demonstrated significant associations and provided evidence supporting a causal relationship between elevated whole-blood mtDNA-CN and increased risks of metabolic disorders, including diabetes mellitus, obesity, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension, suggesting that mtDNA-CN is a robust predictor for these diseases [10, 11]. Methylmalonic acid (MMA) is a mitochondrial intermediate metabolite, and when mitochondria are dysfunctional, inactivation of the mitochondrial methylmalonyl-coenzyme A translocase (MUT) hinders the metabolism of MMA, which can lead to aberrant MMA accumulation [12]. Increasingly, MMA has been found to play a key role in mitochondrial dysfunction, possibly due to its ability to interfere with the mitochondrial respiratory chain and induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [13, 14]. This further suggests that MMA is a good marker of mitochondrial dysfunction and may share similar abilities with mtDNA-CN to represent mitochondrial function.

Neuropsychiatric disorders constitute a significant health challenge, severely compromising the physical and mental well-being of affected individuals and resulting in a reduction of life expectancy by approximately 10–15 years compared to the general population, thereby imposing an escalating burden on healthcare systems annually [15, 16]. Although genetic factors have long been recognized as promising avenues for neuropsychiatric disorder research, effective biomarkers for early diagnosis remain elusive [17]. Accumulating evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction may underlie a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations observed in neuropsychiatric conditions, highlighting mtDNA-CN as a promising therapeutic target and biomarker candidate for these disorders [18]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the importance of mitochondrial dysfunction impairs cellular resilience and synaptic plasticity, leading to a range of neuropsychiatric disorders [19]. The inevitable physiological process of aging is also closely related to mitochondrial dysfunction, and aging is also one of the most important co-pathological mechanisms for a variety of neuropsychiatric disorders [20, 21]. Existing studies have shown a strong association between lower mtDNA-CN and an increased incidence and severity of schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s disease [8, 22], whereas patients with autism and ADHD (ADHD) have higher mtDNA-CN levels in their serum [23]. Different trends in mtDNA-CN levels have been observed in depressed patients are significantly elevated, decreased, or not significantly different relative to those in controls [24–26]. To date, the correlation between mtDNA-CN, which represents mitochondrial function, and various neuropsychiatric disorders remains controversial, and no study has yet comprehensively explored the association between mtDNA-CN and neuropsychiatric disorders from a causal perspective. Similarly, MMA, a representative biomarker of mitochondrial dysfunction, remains poorly characterized regarding its role in neuropsychiatric conditions, although previous research has identified its associations with chronic diseases and mortality [27, 28].

MR is an emerging epidemiological approach based on the principle of randomized pairwise matching in meiotic prophase, using exposure- and outcome-related single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to substitute for exposure and outcome for analysis [29, 30]. MR has been mainly used in studies to explore the causal relationship between exposure and outcome phenotypes from a genetic perspective. It is able to minimize the influence of confounders and reverse causality effects, and it is viewed as a natural randomized controlled study [31, 32]. The aim of the present study was to comprehensively investigate the causal associations between mtDNA-CN and 13 neuropsychiatric disorders using a two-sample MR approach, encompassing both forward and reverse causal analyses. This approach provides robust genetic evidence beyond that offered by traditional retrospective and prospective observational studies. To further validate the reliability of the MR findings, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were utilized, employing MMA levels as an alternative biomarker of mitochondrial function, thus reinforcing the association between mitochondrial dysfunction and the risk of neuropsychiatric disorders.

Where the MR study design was guided by three basic assumptions of MR: (1) IVs are only associated with exposure; (2) IVs are independent of exposure and outcome co-risk factors; and (3) IVs can only act on outcomes through exposure. SNPs associated with mtDNA-CN and mental disorders were used as IVs in forward and reverse MR, respectively, to minimize the influence of confounders and reverse causal effects. A total of 13 neuropsychiatric disorders were included in this study, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), dementia, bipolar affective disorder (BD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), depression, major depressive disorder (MDD), depressive symptoms, schizophrenia, anxiety disorder, ADHD, anorexia nervosa, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and cross disorder. The original studies with GWAS data for all phenotypes included in this study were open and ethically approved, with all participants signing an informed consent form, and detailed information on the pooled data is displayed in Supplementary Table 1. In addition, the Mendelian Randomization of Observational Studies with Enhanced Epidemiology Reporting (STROBE-MR) was strictly used when designing the MR study. The details are shown in the supplementary materials.

To further validate the results of MR and to explore the relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and neuropsychiatric illness, we also collected data from two survey cycles from NHANES from 2011 to 2014 for conducting the present study [33]. These cycles recruited a total of 19,931 participants. To investigate the relationship between MMA and depressive symptoms, we selected adults aged 18 and above as subjects and conducted the following screening. Data sources

MtDNA copy number

The GWAS data for mtDNA-CN used in this study were obtained from the UK Biobank for 395,718 participants of mostly European ancestry (containing a total of 22,679,963 SNPs). This is the most comprehensive genetic data on mtDNA-CN to date [34, 35], where mtDNA-CN levels were determined by the AutoMitoC method developed by Chong et al. [36]. The AutoMitoC method has unique advantages in mtDNA-CN research. In this method, researchers use rare variants rather than common variants on autosomes for signal normalization, leverage extensive experience to assess the correlation between corrected probe signal intensity and off-target genomic intensity, and use principal component analysis to determine mtDNA-CN signals. These innovations enable the AutoMitoC method to be unaffected by linkage disequilibrium and independent of probe sequence homology, ultimately improving the accuracy of mtDNA-CN estimates.

Neuropsychiatric disorders

The GWAS data for the 13 neuropsychiatric disorders we analyzed were all publicly published and are available at the IEU OpenGWAS (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The information related to all included GWAS data is displayed in Supplementary Table 2. MDD (9,240 cases and 9,519 controls), schizophrenia (52,017 cases and 75,889 controls), ASD (18,382 cases and 27,969 controls), ADHD (20,183 cases and 35,191 controls), anorexia nervosa (3,495 cases and 10,982 controls), obsessive–compulsive disorder (26,888 cases and 7,037 controls) and cross disorder (33,332 cases and 27,888 controls) data were obtained from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC, https://pgc.unc.edu/), an organization that includes more than 400,000 participants in the field of psychiatry from 36 countries. Second, we also extracted data from the FinnGen consortium for AD (159 cases and 111,471 controls), dementia (1,589 cases and 111,471 controls), BD (4,501 cases and 192,220 controls), depression (23,424 cases and 192,220 controls), and anxiety disorder (2,163 cases and 198,110 controls) in the FinnGen study (https://www.finngen.fi/), a large-scale genomics program analyzing more than 500,000 Finnish Biobank participants [37]. For depressive symptoms (from the Within Family Consortium, 16,782 cases overall), we used the Within Family Consortium (https://www.withinfamilyconsortium.com/), a database containing a total of 159,701 sibling family GWAS data from 17 cohorts. All mental disorder phenotypes included in the MR studies were diagnosed in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10). Detailed descriptions can be found in the original GWAS data studies and on the original data acquisition websites of the PGC, UKB, Finnish Biobank, Within Family Consortium, etc.

NHANES project

The NHANES project represents a significant health assessment survey undertaken by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) within the United States. This cross-sectional survey is designed to evaluate the health and nutritional conditions of the U.S. population, encompassing both adults and children. The data of extensive health and nutrition are collected through multi-stage probability sampling design and continuous multi-cycle surveys [33]. Participants will be invited to the Mobile Examination Center (EMC) for physical examination and biological sample collection. In addition, participants will also undergo family interviews to be collected information about their dietary habits, physical activity, and use of health services. The National Center for Health Statistics’ Ethics Review Committee has granted approval for the survey. Informed consent was obtained from all individuals participating in the study. Consequently, further approval by the Institutional Review Board is not necessary for this research. The NHANES data involved in this study is publicly available and open, and can be obtained for free on the website: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm.

Measurement of methylmalonic acid

MMA is a recognized biomarker for mitochondrial dysfunction. The concentration of serum methylmalonic acid can be measured by Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method. The lower limit of detection (LLOD) for serum MMA concentration detected based on this method is 22.1 nmol/L. All subjects had serum MMA concentrations above the LLOD.

Assessment of cognitive performance

In NHANES 2011–2014, the cognitive function of elderly people aged 60 and above was evaluated through conducting the following four questionnaire testings, including the immediate recall test (IRT), the delayed recall test (DRT), the animal fluency test (AFT) and the digit symbol substitution test (DSST). The higher the scores of test participants in various cognitive assessments, the better their cognitive function. To facilitate the comparison of outcomes across various cognitive tests, we transformed the raw scores into Z-score, which provide a standardized measure for each assessment. The Z-score is calculated using the formula Z=(x-µ)/σ, where x is the participant’s raw score, µ is the mean score and σ is the standard deviation of the scores within the particular test. The comprehensive cognitive Z-score, derived from summing the Z-scores of the four individual tests, serves as an index of overall cognitive performance. We chose to use the lowest quartile of the comprehensive Z-score as the threshold for cognitive impairment, which participants with Z-scores below the threshold were considered to have cognitive impairment.

Assessment of depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed through Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a widely used depression screening tool. The scoring range of the PHQ-9 scale is from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. If the score is greater than or equal to 10 points, the participant is considered to have depression.

Potential covariates

Covariates considered included some of the following demographic characteristics, dietary data, body measurements, laboratory tests and questionnaires: age (years), gender (male/female), race (Mexican American/non-Hispanic White/Non-Hispanic Black/Other race), education level (high school or below/above high school), marital status (married/living with partner and widowed/divorced/separated/never married), body mass index (BMI, Kg/m2), triglyceride (TG, mmol/L), total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L), white blood cell (WBC, 109/L), hemoglobin (HB, g/L) and platelet (PLT, 109/L). Smoking status was determined based on the response to the query, “Have you smoked at least a hundred cigarettes in your lifetime?” Participants were classified as either having smoked (Yes) or not smoked (No). The drinking status of participants is determined by whether their annual alcohol consumption exceeds 12 drinks, which is considered as Yes or No. Hypertension is diagnosed based on both blood pressure readings and patient medical history. Criteria for diagnosis include a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mmHg or higher and/or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 90 mmHg or higher, or “informed by doctors that you have hypertension” and “currently taking antihypertensive medication”. In accordance with the American Diabetes Association (ADA) diagnostic standards, diabetes was identified as having a fasting blood glucose (FBG) level of 126 mg/dL or higher, or a glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of 6.5% or higher, or by affirming any of the following inquiries: “Have you been diagnosed with diabetes by a healthcare professional?”, “Are you currently on insulin therapy?”, and “Are you currently using oral hypoglycemic medications?” [38, 39].

Selection of instruments

We enforced strict criteria for selecting IVs by including SNPs strongly associated with the exposure phenotype (p < 5 × 10−8), excluding SNPs associated with the outcome (p < 5 × 10−6), and further screened the IVs by chain disequilibrium (window size of 10,000 kb and threshold of r2 = 0.001). Moreover, we removed duplicates, SNPs with missing information, and palindromic sequences to ensure the accuracy of the results and applied PhenoScanner to assess whether the IVs were associated with other phenotypes to avoid confounding effects. In addition, all IVs with weak effects (F < 10, F = R2*(N-2)/1-R) were excluded, where N is the number of samples and R2 represents the variance of exposure-related SNPs, which resulted in a study free of weak IV bias [40].

Statistical analyses

Four methods for causal inference are applied in MR studies, including inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR‒Egger, median weighting, and weighted mode, with IVW as the primary method [41]. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted considering that the horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity of the results may affect the causal inference of MR [42]. MR pleiotropy residuals and outliers (MR-PRESSO) was used to identify and remove outliers from the SNPs to avoid horizontal pleiotropy, and the Q value of Cochran’s test was used to quantify the degree of heterogeneity of the IVs [42]. A funnel plot can help researchers quickly determine whether horizontal pleiotropy is present, and the stronger the symmetry of the scatter in the plot is, the less likely pleiotropy is present. Finally, the “leave-one-out” method was used to identify potentially heterogeneous SNPs by deleting each SNP in turn and observing the effect on the overall results, while reverse MR analysis was performed using the same approach to make the results more comprehensive and unaffected from the interference of reverse causality.

In the cross-sectional study, considering the skewed distribution characteristics of MMA, it is necessary to perform log-10 transform. The baseline characteristics of study participants were delineated based on cognitive performance and depressive symptoms. The continuous variables are examined using Mann-Whitney U tests and expressed as median and quartiles. The categorical variables are compared using Chi-square test, and described by number (n) and percentage (%). A p-value of less than 0.05 from a two-tailed test was considered to indicate statistical significance. Multiple logistic regression was applied to investigate the relationship between LgMMA and cognitive impairment and depression. Multiple linear regression was employed to explore the relationship between LgMMA and cognitive and depression scores. For cognitive performance, Model 1 adjusts for no variables. Model 2 adjusts for gender, age, race, marital status, BMI, TC, TG and WBC. Model 3 adjusts for gender, age, race, marital status, BMI, TC, TG, WBC, drinking status, smoking status, hypertension and diabetes. For depressive symptoms, Model 1 adjusts for no variables. Model 2 adjusts for gender, age, race, BMI, TC, TG, WBC, HB and PLT. Model 3 adjusts for gender, age, race, BMI, TC, TG, WBC, HB, PLT, drinking status, smoking status, hypertension and diabetes. Both forward and reverse MR were performed using the “TwoSampleMR” package and the “MR-PRESSO” package, all analyses were completed in the R environment (version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The results of the MR and cross-sectional analyses are presented as ratios and 95% confidence intervals, and two-sided p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Causal effects of mtDNA-CN on neuropsychiatric disorders

After screening the IVs, we included 58 SNPs for autism; 53 SNPs for AD, BD, dementia, anxiety, depression, and obsessive–compulsive disorder; 52 SNPs for schizophrenia and anorexia nervosa; 50 SNPs for ADHD; 44 SNPs for depressive symptoms; and 15 SNPs for MDD and cross disorder to perform MR analysis of mtDNA-CN for various types of neuropsychiatric disorders; and the F of all included SNPs ranged from 29.75 to 467; see Supplementary Table 2 for specific details about the SNPs.

The results of MR analysis demonstrated significant causal effects of mtDNA-CN on AD, BD, ASD, dementia and depressive symptoms (Fig. 3), and the direction of causality was negative for all of them, i.e., a lower mtDNA-CN strongly genetically predicted a greater occurrence of the above five neuropsychiatric disorders. The results of the positive MR analysis are displayed in Fig. 4, and the results of the main IVW methods were BD (OR = 0.65; 95% CI 0.46–0.92; p = 0.015), AD (OR = 0.15; 95% CI 0.03–0.76; p = 0.022), dementia (OR = 0.52; 95% CI 0.29–0.96; p = 0.036), ASD (OR = 0.80; 95% CI 0.67–0.96; p = 0.018), and depressive symptoms (OR = 0.84; 95% CI 0.72–0.98; p = 0.029). A scatterplot of the results is shown in Fig. 5. Subsequently, we also performed sensitivity analyses, and the findings of the MR-PRESSO for the five disorders were in agreement with the results of the four MR methods. In the subsequent heterogeneity test and horizontal multiplicity test, no significant heterogeneity or horizontal multiplicity was found, and the p values of all of the results were much greater than 0.05 (Supplementary Table 3). The results of the leave-one-out method and the funnel plots are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 2–3.

Causal effects of neuropsychiatric disorders on mtDNA-CN

Reverse MR analysis was also performed in this study, aiming to explore the causal effect of various types of neuropsychiatric disorders on mtDNA-CN using the same SNP screening methodology, and the specific information is shown in Supplementary Table 2. The results of the reverse MR analysis revealed that there was a significant causal effect of AD and depressive symptoms on mtDNA-CN, with significant causal effects on mtDNA-CN in the cases of AD (OR = 1.00; 95% CI 1.00-1.01; p = 0.035) and depressive symptoms (OR = 0.96; 95% CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.018); however, we believe that the range of the 95% CI for the AD result is too small, and the value is approximately 1, which may be a no-effect statistic. Therefore, we believe that only depressive symptoms demonstrated a significant negative causal effect on mtDNA-CN in the reverse MR analysis, i.e., patients with depressive symptoms showed a decrease in mtDNA-CN (Fig. 6). According to the sensitivity analysis of the significance results, no significant heterogeneity was detected in the results of the Cochran Q test for depressive symptoms on the mtDNA-CN (Q = 9.14; p = 0.11), and the results of the horizontal pleiotropy analysis (p = 0.78) demonstrated that the same pleiotropy did not exist (Supplementary Table 3). Association between MMA and cognitive function, depression

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristic of participants based on cognitive performance. A total of 2,665 participants were ultimately included in the study, with a median age of 68 years. Among them, 666 (24.99%) participants were defined as having cognitive impairment. Compared with the control group, participants in the cognitive impairment group had significantly higher proportions of older age, male gender, black race, low educational attainment, living alone, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes. Additionally, the cognitive impairment group had higher serum MMA concentrations and significantly lower TC, HB, and PLT levels. Table 2 shows the baseline characteristics of participants based on depressive symptoms. A total of 9,044 participants were ultimately included in the study, with a median age of 49 years old. Among them, 838 participants (9.27%) suffered from depression. Compared with participants without depression, they were more likely to be older, female, less educated, living alone, smokers, suffering from hypertension and diabetes, and have higher BMI, serum TC, TG, WBC and MMA concentrations.

Association between MMA and cognitive performance in older adults

Table 3 presents a summary of the findings from the multiple logistic and linear regression analysis investigating the relationship between MMA and cognitive performance among the elderly population. The result of multiple logistic regression revealed, in the unadjusted Model 1, for every 1 nmol/L increase in LgMMA, the incidence of cognitive impairment increased by 147% (OR = 2.47, 95%CI: [1.69, 3.61], p < 0.001). After adjusting for confounding factors, the positive correlation between Model 2 (OR = 1.57, 95%CI: [1.04, 2.39], p = 0.033) and Model 3 (OR = 1.56, 95%CI [1.03, 2.38], p = 0.036) continued to be significant. The result of multiple linear regression revealed, in the unadjusted Model 1, for every 1 nmol/L increase in LgMMA, the cognitive comprehensive Z-score decreased by 1.97 units (β=−1.97, 95%CI: [−2.49, −1.44], p < 0.001). After adjusting for confounding factors, the negative correlation between Model 2 (β=−0.98, 95%CI: [−1.49, −0.47], p < 0.001) and Model 3 (β=−0.96, 95%CI: [−1.47, −0.46], p < 0.001) continued to be significant.

Association between MMA and depressive symptoms

Table 4 summarizes the results of multiple logistic and linear regression analyses on the relationship between MMA and depressive symptoms. The multiple logistic regression results showed that in unadjusted Model 1, for every 1 nmol/L increase in LgMMA, the incidence of cognitive impairment increased by 63% (OR = 1.63, 95%CI: [1.19,2.24], p = 0.003). After adjusting for confounding factors, the positive correlation between Model 2 (OR = 1.56, 95%CI: [1.10,2.22], p = 0.012) and Model 3 (OR = 1.53, 95%CI: [1.07,2.18], p = 0.020) continued to be significant. The results of multiple linear regression showed that in unadjusted Model 1, for every 1 nmol/L increase in LgMMA, the PHQ-9 score increased by 0.69 units (β = 0.69, 95%CI: [0.26,1.12], p = 0.002). After adjusting for confounding factors, the positive correlation between Model 2 (β = 0.71, 95%CI: [0.26,1.16], p = 0.002) and Model 3 (β = 0.71, 95%CI: [0.26,1.15], p = 0.002) remained significant. In addition, considering that a small number of patients included in the study may have comorbid cognitive impairment and depression, this may have skewed our results. Therefore, we excluded 96 patients with both depression and cognitive impairment and performed logistic regression on the depression and cognitive impairment groups separately as a sensitivity analysis. The results were basically consistent with the previous ones, as shown in Supplementary Table 4, further confirming the robustness of our results.

Discussion

The present study is the first to explore the relationship between mitochondrial function and multiple neuropsychiatric disorders using MR and NHANES cross-sectional methods, utilizing mtDNA-CN and MMA, respectively, to represent mitochondria-specific functions. Our findings indicate a significant negative causal relationship between mtDNA-CN and BD, dementia, AD, depressive symptoms, and ASD. In the NHANES study, a significant association between MMA and depression and cognitive impairment was also observed. This also suggests that lower mtDNA-CN levels are associated with an increased prevalence of these five neuropsychiatric disorders, consistent with previous findings. Meanwhile, higher MMA was significantly and positively associated with higher risk of depression, risk of cognitive dysfunction, and lower cognitive scores. That is, mitochondrial dysfunction may be considered an important etiologic agent and potential therapeutic target for a variety of neuropsychiatric disorders.

From the physiological significance of mtDNA-CN in healthy people, it has important value from birth to adulthood and then to aging. Researchers found that mtDNA-CN in healthy people reaches its peak at around 50 years old, and then decreases with age. In general, the mtDNA-CN of men is lower than the average level of women [43, 44]. In addition, changes in mtDNA-CN can indicate the risk of multiple chronic diseases and death in healthy people, because changes in mtDNA-CN often precede the disease process, which has been proven in our study [7]. However, mtDNA-CN in the blood of healthy people can also change under the influence of certain factors. In a GWAS study, two loci were associated with mtDNA-CN levels. Two loci within mitochondrial transcription factor A and cyclin-dependent kinase 6 were considered to be associated with mtDNA-CN levels, but the specific mechanism of action remains unclear [45]. Current studies generally agree that there is a strong association between mtDNA-CN and BD, i.e., a lower mtDNA-CN can lead to an increased incidence of BD, which is consistent with our findings [46]. However, in some studies, this association was not significant, or the mtDNA-CN was greater in BD patients than in healthy individuals, which may be influenced by the small sample size, patients’ emotional state, antipsychotics, and different subtypes of BD [46–48]. In observational studies of patients with mitochondrial disease, a 7–21% probability of developing BD was found, and abnormal mutations in mtDNA loci and mitochondrial dysfunction were also detected via brain magnetic resonance spectroscopy and gene sequencing in patients with BD [48, 49], yet direct studies on the association between MMA and BD are still relatively scarce at this point in time.

BD1 and BD2 are the two main subtypes of BD, and the main difference in the diagnostic criteria between the two is that patients with BD1 have predominantly manic symptoms, whereas the number of depressive episodes and the degree of depression are much greater in patients with BD2 than in patients with BD1 [50]. Interestingly, the two BD subtypes, in addition to differing in clinical manifestations, also differed sharply in mitochondrial function with opposite changes, with mtDNA-CN being significantly lower in BD1 patients than in healthy controls and significantly greater in BD2 patients [48]. This strong association between mtDNA-CN and BD may be influenced by a variety of factors, such as emotional, age, environmental and genetic factors, of which the patient’s emotional state is believed to have the most significant influence [51]. Researchers have shown that the severe or acute phase of BD directly leads to low levels of mtDNA-CN, especially in BD1 patients with predominantly manic episodes and high-stress states, which are characterized by a high level of oxidative stress and a proinflammatory state throughout the body, thus impairing mitochondrial function [46]. Mitochondria are sensitive to oxidative stress and inflammation, and the levels of oxidative stress products such as ROS/NOS and inflammatory mediators such as intercellular adhesion molecules and vascular cell adhesion molecules are significantly correlated with low mtDNA-CN and high MMA levels [52]. The number of episodes is similarly negatively correlated with mtDNA-CN in BD1 patients, whereas mitochondrial abundance is able to recover gradually with time and mood stabilization [51]. In contrast, no correlation between the mtDNA-CN and mood episodes was detected in BD2 patients or in those with major depressive disorder (MDD), possibly because BD2 patients experience longer periods of depression, although this finding is not consistent with our findings [47]. In another meta-analysis of Asian BD populations, we did not observe a difference between type 1 and type 2 BDs in terms of mtDNA-CN, and the conflicting findings may have been influenced by antipsychotic drugs [47], such as clozapine/valproic acid, have been shown to affect mtDNA-CN [53]. These findings suggest that changes in mtDNA-CN in BD patients may differ from those in MDD patients, and mtDNA-CN could serve as a diagnostic marker to distinguish BD from MDD [54]. In conclusion, our study results support a negative causal relationship between mtDNA-CN and BD/MDD, but the exact cause remains unclear and requires further investigation. In the second part of the analysis, it was found that higher MMA led to a higher risk of depression, suggesting that the degree of mitochondrial dysfunction is positively associated with depression. In two U.S. population studies, MMA demonstrated a positive association with the risk of depression and MMA accounted for 4.1% of the causes of premature death from depression, which is consistent with our findings [28, 55]. MMA is an important marker of mitochondrial dysfunction, and elevated MMA levels indicate worsening mitochondrial dysfunction. In the brains of deceased patients with depression, 21% of mitochondrial protein levels were found to be altered and oxidative phosphorylation processes were abnormal [56], which further explains our findings.

In this study, we also found that a lower mtDNA-CN leads to a greater incidence of AD and associated dementia, consistent with the findings of previous clinical studies. Several studies have shown that mtDNA abundance is associated with cognitive decline in AD patients, and the relationship between mtDNA-CN and cognitive deficits was again demonstrated in a study of cerebrospinal fluid and postmortem brain tissue from AD patients [8, 57]. In another study based on brain samples from older adults, mtDANA-CN was found to be decreased by 7–14% in the brain tissue of AD patients compared to that of normal subjects, and mtDNA-CN was thought to allow early recognition of the intermediate stage between normal and dementia (the mildly cognitively impaired state) [58, 59]. AD often occurs in elderly patients, and aging is an important risk factor for AD, which can be explained by the mechanism of the mitochondrial cascade, i.e., genetically determining the decline in mitochondrial function, and AD occurs after exceeding the age-corresponding threshold for mitochondrial decline, which further lends theoretical support to our findings [60]. In studies of common brain pathologic features in AD patients, tau protein was significantly associated with reduced mtDNA-CN, particularly in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex regions of the brain, which could also explain the negative causal effect of lower mtDNA-CN with AD [58]. Pathologically, tau proteins are closely associated with cognitive function in AD patients [61]. Reduced mtDNA-CN promotes the aggregation of tau proteins in brain tissue, and phosphorylated tau proteins can in turn impair mitochondrial function [62, 63]. There is a complex link between the two proteins, which together accelerate brain aging and cognitive impairment in AD patients [64]. Cholinesterase inhibitors have been the first line of treatment for AD in recent years, effectively slowing AD progression and improving cognitive function [65]. Recently, researchers have also found that these drugs, such as donepezil, may also be able to treat CNS degenerative diseases by elevating mtDNA-CN, which promotes mitochondrial production through upregulation of PGC-1α expression and activation of protein kinases [66]. In animal experiments, researchers also found that AD mice had increased levels of D-loop methylation, which is known to be negatively correlated with mitochondrial DNA copy number [67, 68]. In conclusion, our findings further confirm the close association between mitochondrial dysfunction, represented by decreased mtDNA-CN and increased MMA, and cognitive decline, and novel therapeutic options such as mitochondrial production and function agonists can be tried in the future to mitigate the progression of AD and dementia.

MtDNA-CN levels may also be closely related to the onset of neuropsychiatric diseases such as autism, schizophrenia, and ADHD [19, 23, 69]. Mitochondria are the main site of ROS production, mediating oxidative stress, and neuronal cells are extremely sensitive to oxidative stress; excessive oxidative stress can lead to the development of a variety of neuropsychiatric disorders and aggravate mitochondrial dysfunction [70]. The upregulation of oxidative stress and subsequent neuronal damage and mitochondrial dysfunction has been observed in both ASD and ADHD patients [71]. Further measurements of mtDNA-CN levels in patients revealed no significant change in ASD patients relative to normal subjects but a significant increase in ADHD patients [72, 73]. In contrast, mtDNA-CN levels were significantly decreased in patients with schizophrenia who had never received medication [19]. Significant telomere erosion, a manifestation of accelerated aging, has also been observed in schizophrenic patients, and telomere length is strongly correlated with mtDNA-CN [74, 75]. Researchers have concluded that shortened telomere length and decreased mtDNA-CN together contribute to the onset and progression of schizophrenia [76]. Although the results of these studies are not consistent with the MR results of the present study, which may be because clinical studies are affected by a variety of confounding factors, large-scale randomized controlled trials with a larger number of patients and a more rigorous study design are needed to further explore the relationship between mtDNA-CN, MMA and the onset of disease in patients with these neuropsychiatric disorders [25]. In addition, we also found some significant differences in the depression group compared with the healthy control group at the baseline of the NHANES data analysis. For example, the depressed population is generally older, more likely to be elderly, female, lack education, live alone, and have multiple chronic diseases, which is consistent with the current study [77–79]. In addition to the differences in MMA and these demographic variables discussed above, we also found that depressed patients had significantly higher BMI, serum TC, TG and WBC concentrations. Several studies have found that BMI is a direct risk factor for depression, and a higher BMI may lead to a higher number of depressive episodes and aggravate depression [80, 81]. This may be because recurrent depressive symptoms lead to weight gain through mechanisms such as emotional eating, reduced physical activity, and circadian rhythm disruption. On the other hand, elevated BMI itself may worsen the course of the disease through disturbances in metabolic homeostasis, reduced self-esteem, and chronic somatic inflammation [82, 83]. In addition, BMI was found to be one of the factors mediating lipid metabolism abnormalities such as hypertriglyceridemia in patients with severe depression, which could explain why depressed patients in our baseline analysis had higher TC and TG [84]. Finally, the increase in WBC in depressed patients reveals another phenomenon, that is, the activation of the inflammatory environment in the patient’s body may lead to the occurrence or aggravation of depression. According to previous studies, inflammatory factors are believed to play a role in the development of stress response and depression, both of which can lead to suicide. Conversely, depressive symptoms will also aggravate the response of cellular inflammatory factors, further increasing the levels of serum inflammatory markers such as WBC [85, 86].

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first study to comprehensively explore the association between mitochondrial function and multiple neuropsychiatric disorders. The findings are generally consistent with those of previous studies and provide strong supporting evidence. Second, this study utilized two of the more recognized biomarkers of mitochondrial function, mtDNA-CN and MMA, to explore the relationship between mitochondrial function and neuropsychiatric disorders as represented by them using bidirectional MR and traditional cross-sectional studies, respectively, to minimize reverse causation and bias due to a single study method. Third, the GWAS dataset we used contained a large number of participants, a larger sample size than previous clinical studies, and more reliable findings. Finally, the analysis also included sensitivity analyses such as leave-one-out and funnel plots, which made the findings more robust. However, the limitations of this study should not be overlooked. First, the GWAS and NHANES datasets used in this study primarily come from participants of European and American descent, lacking generalizability to other ethnic groups. Future studies should analyze data from additional populations to validate our findings. Second, while MR analysis revealed a significant causal relationship between mtDNA-CN and five neuropsychiatric disorders, the limitations of the NHANES dataset restricted our subsequent cross-sectional studies to validating only two disease phenotypes-depression and cognitive impairment-among adult participants. Additionally, the use of a single PHQ-9 score may result in less precise depression diagnoses. Future studies require more comprehensive psychiatric cohorts with broader age coverage and more comprehensive diagnostic criteria to validate our findings. Finally, while mtDNA-CN and MMA were significantly associated with multiple neuropsychiatric disorders in this study, the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

Conclusion

This study provides robust genetic and epidemiological evidence supporting the involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of multiple neuropsychiatric disorders. We demonstrate that decreased mtDNA-CN is causally associated with an increased risk of bipolar disorder BD, AD, dementia, depression, and ASD. Furthermore, our findings indicate that depression itself may exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction. Cross-sectional validation using the NHANES dataset further confirmed that elevated MMA levels were significantly associated with cognitive impairment and depression, reinforcing the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction in neuropsychiatric conditions. These findings highlight the potential of mitochondrial function as a mental health biomarker and therapeutic target. Future research should focus on interventions that target mitochondria to improve clinical outcomes and provide new strategies for precision medicine in neuropsychiatric disorders.
