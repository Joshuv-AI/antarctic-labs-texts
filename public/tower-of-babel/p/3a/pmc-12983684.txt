Use of artificial intelligence for health science in low- and middle-income countries: NIH portfolio landscape, gaps and opportunities
Authors: Andrew D Forsyth, Laura K Povlich, Peter H Kilmarx
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo analyse the landscape of active US National Institutes of Health (NIH) artificial intelligence (AI) health research grants, with emphasis on studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), to characterise use cases, health challenges addressed and gaps relevant to the ethical and responsible application of AI-enabled health science.DesignDescriptive portfolio analysis of NIH-funded AI health research grants.SettingNIH research portfolio analysis, with a focus on global health studies in LMICs.ParticipantsNone. Data are derived from active NIH-funded grants involving AI applications in health research, as of 31 January 2025.InterventionsNot applicable (portfolio analysis).Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary measures included the proportion and funding of AI health research grants focused on LMICs and their thematic use cases. Secondary measures compared LMIC-focused and high-income country (HIC)-focused grants by research focus and health area and identified gaps relevant to ethical and responsible AI use in global health.ResultsOf 1850 active NIH AI health research grants, 97 (5.2%) focused on LMICs, representing US$40.2 million (2.4%) of the total US$1.66 billion portfolio. compared with HICs, LMIC-based studies emphasised diagnostics and treatment (72.2% vs 66.8%), health system optimisation (18.6% vs 15.6%), disease surveillance and outbreak response (14.4% vs 8.8%), and telemedicine and remote care (7.2% vs 4.4%). HIC-based grants more frequently addressed public health education (10.4% vs 8.2%) and ethics and data governance (12.8% vs 7.2%). All settings emphasised data science training and capacity strengthening, as well as basic research and early-stage AI-augmented tools. LMIC-based studies most often targeted non-communicable diseases (39%), communicable diseases (30%) and health system strengthening (24%). 31 awards were made directly to LMIC-based principal investigators (1.7% of the portfolio), most commonly in South Africa, Kenya and Uganda.ConclusionsNIH investment in peer-reviewed AI-enabled health research is expanding globally. LMIC-focused studies prioritise areas aligned with pressing global health needs, including outbreak detection, disease surveillance, diagnostics and treatment, health system optimisation and remote care. Greater attention to ethics, data governance and public health communication, alongside support for digital infrastructure and meaningful collaboration, may help strengthen the relevance and sustainability of AI-enabled research for population health.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Capacity Building, Health, ETHICS (see Medical Ethics), Implementation Science

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to the capability of algorithms used in computer systems and tools to learn from data to perform tasks without explicit human input.1 It has reached a pivotal moment in its evolution, with recent breakthroughs demonstrating its potential to deliver innovative, cost-efficient solutions in resource-constrained settings while addressing local health priorities.2 AI offers unparalleled opportunities to strengthen health systems, improve disease surveillance (DS), develop new prevention and treatment options, and scale evidence-based solutions that address critical health challenges, regardless of geographical or economic setting.3 Recent National Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded studies illustrate this potential, including studies of machine learning to predict tuberculosis treatment resistance, explainable AI methods to improve clinical decision support and deep-learning enabled imaging tools for real-time detection of anal precancer in people living with HIV.4,6 However, AI is a revolutionary technology with considerable risks, requiring adherence to foundational principles if it is to protect human autonomy, advance public interests, ensure transparency, foster responsible and ethical use, provide equitable access and ensure its sustainability.7 Indeed, the inherent risks must be anticipated and mitigated if it is to achieve its full potential for advancing public health, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).8,11

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the potential of AI-augmented tools and computing infrastructure for addressing complex, emergent threats to public health. Machine learning models predicted patterns from outbreak data without explicit programming, while deep learning—a specialised subset of machine learning—excelled at solving highly complex tasks through multilayered neural networks that learn step by step to make sense of raw data.12 For instance, BlueDot, an international database company, used AI in a layered process to analyse multiple sources of infectious disease and travel records to warn its customers to avoid Wuhan province 10 days before the WHO and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issued alerts.13 AI-enabled tools proved invaluable in the pandemic response, improving diagnosis, prognosis, drug repurposing and discovery, genomic sequencing and vaccine development.14,16 These and other demonstrations of AI’s potential have ignited a race to identify ways the technology can advance public health and clinical health practice across sectors, including the private healthcare companies; international, federal, state, local, tribal and territorial government; donors and multilateral institutions; implementing agencies; and researchers.17 We focus on describing NIH investment patterns, research themes and gaps within the AI health science landscape most relevant to LMICs; evaluation of the quality or outcomes of these ongoing projects is outside the scope of this analysis, in part because the findings are not yet publicly available.

Methods

We used the World Bank’s FY2025 income classification (released July 2024) to define LMICs.18 Projects were categorised as LMIC-relevant if they: (1) explicitly referenced LMIC settings, populations or implementation in the project descriptions (eg, abstract, project terms, title, public health relevance); or (2) included capacity strengthening or partnership activities targeting LMIC institutions or stakeholders, even if led by US-based investigators. Projects focused solely on high-income countries (HICs) were excluded, except where justified by clear LMIC relevance (eg, global conferences with LMIC participation).

Data were collected from the NIH Research Portfolio Online Reporting Tools Expenditures and Results (RePORTER) system.19 A structured database query was executed on 24 February 2025 to identify all active NIH-funded health science awards with funding status active as of 31 January 2025, irrespective of original project start year. Awards were identified using a Boolean keyword search applied to the project title, project abstract, project terms and public health relevance statement fields in RePORTER. The search strategy targeted AI-related research and used the following exact search string: (“artificial intelligence” OR “machine learning” OR “deep learning” OR “neural network” OR “natural language processing” OR “predictive modeling”). All NIH activity codes were included (eg, R-series, U-series, P-series, K-series and T-series awards), and no restrictions were applied by institute or centre. Projects and associated subprojects sharing a common project number were deduplicated and counted once, with funding amounts aggregated across subprojects. Data elements exported for analysis included project descriptions (eg, title, abstract, project terms, public health relevance), award characteristics (eg, activity code, award year, project number), organisational information (eg, recipient institution and country), personnel (contact principal investigator) and funding data (eg, total costs and cofunding institutes and centres). Standard data management procedures were applied to assign unique identifiers, resolve duplicate records, address missing values and construct a project-level analytical dataset.

To describe NIH investment patterns, research themes and gaps within the AI health science landscape, we used a large language model (LLM) (ChatGPT4o) to assist with study coding, leveraging its strength in semantic pattern recognition to improve consistency and reduce human coding drift, similar to approaches evaluated in prior work using ChatGPT for systematic review data extraction.20 To do so, we launched a pilot phase in which the LLM was trained on a predefined classification scheme empirically generated from a sample of nearly 80 peer-reviewed, scientific articles published between 2021 and 2024 on the use of AI in global health. The scheme yielded eight use-case categories: (1) DS and epidemic response (ER) focused on monitoring diseases, detecting outbreaks and preparing for epidemics; (2) diagnostics and treatment (D&T) improved disease screening, diagnosis and treatment; (3) health systems optimisation addressed efficiency, sustainability and resource management within health systems; (4) telemedicine and remote care (T&RC) expanded healthcare access and delivery through telemedicine, mobile health and wearable technologies; (5) public health communication (PHC) disseminated health information and study findings to the public and responded to misinformation and disinformation; (6) ethics and data governance (E&DG) focused on ethics, privacy, algorithmic bias and data sharing and stewardship; (7) data science training and capacity strengthening (DST&CS) addressed skills and capacity strengthening through training, workshops and educational initiatives; and (8) other, which encompassed research and development in basic or preclinical science, foundational models and platform and application development.

We also categorised NIH portfolio awards by the non-exclusive health challenges they addressed, assigning them to one or more of the following eight categories: (1) communicable diseases (CDs), such as tuberculosis, malaria, HIV, COVID-19; (2) non-communicable diseases (NCD), including cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes and other chronic conditions; (3) maternal and child health (M&CH), relating to pregnancy, neonatal and infant health and vaccine-preventable diseases; (4) public health and systems, capturing health disparities, using health data and digital infrastructure, and implementing evidence-based interventions; (5) climate and environmental health, assessing health effects associated with pollution and severe weather; (6) neurological disorders (ND), including stroke, neurodegeneration and brain imaging; (7) health equity (HE), describing access to care and social determinants of health; and (8) behavioural health (BH), encompassing depression, suicide prevention and substance use disorders. Once ChatGPT-4o was trained using the study codebooks and category definitions, its outputs were used to support initial classification (see online supplemental appendix B). Agreement between AI-assisted and human-reviewed classifications was assessed using a random subsample of approximately 2.5% of projects, yielding approximately 90% concordance following iterative calibration. No other automated tools were used; data analyses were manually conducted to ensure transparency, reproducibility and methodological rigour. Simple descriptive statistics (eg, counts, means, modes and ranges) were generated using standard formulas in Google Sheets. Of the 1964 active awards, 114 (6%) were excluded from the analyses because they used the same unique project number and were funded by multiple institutes or centres or had subawards. Each project was counted only once, but total funding was summed for each project.

Results

A cross-sectional analysis of 1850 active, unique NIH awards on AI and health science revealed that 5.2% (k=97) were conducted in LMICs, which were defined by World Bank FY2025 classification of economies by gross national income per capita.18 We deemed awards LMIC relevant if the abstract indicated research, implementation or capacity strengthening activities for those settings. The amount invested in these international grants was small, accounting for only US$40.2 million (2.4%) of the total US$1.66 billion NIH AI and health science portfolio. All included studies used AI, with many applying machine learning (46.9%), deep learning (24.0%), neural networks (13.6%) or other AI technologies (13.7%). Included studies were funded through a mix of opportunity types. Most awards stemmed from programme announcements (PAs, 37.5%) or program announcements with special receipt, referral, or review considerations (PARs, 30.2%), which typically support investigator-initiated research. About a quarter (24.6%) came from requests for applications (RFAs), which target specific NIH priorities and include set-aside funds—not all of which were AI-related (eg, RFA-HL-19-008 alone accounted for 3.0% of awards). Smaller shares were linked to PAs with set-aside funds (0.7%), other mechanisms (2.3%) or had no listed opportunity type (4.7%). This distribution reflects both open-ended exploration and targeted investment in NIH’s AI funding landscape.

As table 1 shows, the NIH’s AI and health science portfolio consisted of grants addressing D&T (67.1%), health system optimisation (15.7%), DST&CS (12.8%) and E&DG (12.5%), with an additional allocation focused on research and development (35.2%). DS and ER (9.1%) and T&RC (4.5%) were the least common areas of focus. LMIC-focused studies mirrored these trends, with some notable differences. These grants placed a greater emphasis on D&T (72.2% vs 66.8%), health system optimisation (18.6% vs 15.6%), DS and outbreak response (14.4% vs 8.8%), and T&RC (7.2% vs 4.4%) than did HIC-based grants. In contrast, HIC-based studies concentrated more on public health education (10.4% vs 8.2%), and E&DG (12.8% vs 7.2%). All settings emphasised DST&CS, basic science and early-stage AI-augmented tools and platforms.

Discussion

This analysis of NIH-funded AI-enabled health science grants found that 5.2% of projects (k=97) were conducted in LMICs, representing approximately US$40.2 million or just 2.4% of the total US$1.66 billion AI portfolio. Although there were notable similarities in AI health science research between LMIC and HIC settings, differences were apparent: LMIC-based AI health science grants prioritised D&T, health system optimisation, DS and ER, and T&RC. In contrast, HIC-based studies focused more on public health education and E&DG. DST&CS, basic research and early-stage AI-augmented tools and platforms were universally emphasised across all settings. The most common health issues addressed in LMIC-based AI health science were NCDs, CDs and health system strengthening, respectively.

These findings highlight several critical gaps and opportunities for the field. First, the relative proportion of LMIC-based grants (5.2%) compared with their overall share of funding (2.4%) likely reflects their lower direct (eg, investigator salary) and indirect costs (eg, lab space, institutional review boards) compared with HIC-based grants. It also suggests that LMIC-led, peer-reviewed, NIH-supported research has the potential to deliver critical scientific output and public health impact, thereby optimising finite resources to bring far-reaching benefits. At the same time, these investments address local health priorities, strengthen regional capacity, foster equitable access to technological solutions, reduce reliance on development aid and accelerate global trade.21 By sampling a more representative subset of the human population and health data, this research may also produce more generalisable findings and better trained AI systems.

Second, a greater emphasis in LMIC-based AI-enabled health science on DS and ER may reflect the higher prevalence of infectious diseases such as HIV, tuberculosis and malaria than in HICs. Along with maternal health conditions, infectious diseases account for a large share of the preventable premature mortality gap between LMICs and HICs.22 Similarly, a greater focus on D&T, health system optimisation and remote care may align with local challenges related to the availability of a skilled healthcare workforce, access to quality care and sustainable infrastructure.23 24 Conversely, a lower focus on ethics, data governance and PHC in LMICs suggests an opportunity to address concerns arising from technology regulation, user trust and privacy, data sharing, human rights and algorithmic bias. Building on prior agreements, the recent C4IR Global AI Summit in Kigali yielded a commitment to closing these gaps through continent-wide AI education and capacity strengthening; a robust data governance framework; and the promotion of ethical, equitable and locally-tailored solutions that benefit multiple sectors, including public health.25

Third, it is essential to build or maintain mechanisms for knowledge sharing to ensure valuable lessons developed in LMICs are accessible and adaptable across geographical and economic contexts, including under-resourced areas within the US and other HICs. Such reciprocal innovation may be vital and cost-effective, allowing countries facing similar constellations of health challenges—including low-resourced rural and urban settings in the US—to gain from advances originating in other settings even when local public health priorities shift. Ongoing investment in data science training and research capacity strengthening will be key to empowering meaningful participation in the future of AI-enabled health science, ensuring that all populations derive maximum benefit and protection from its use.

This analysis has several limitations. First, the study sample consisted of active NIH awards as of January 2025 and does not include AI-related health science funded by other US government agencies or other entities (eg, foundations, industry or other national governments). In addition, all projects were ongoing at the time of data collection, precluding an evaluation of their individual-level study outcomes. In addition, LMIC classifications may change year to year, and project information may not always specify all target populations or partners. One award based in Romania (5UE5TW012539-02), an HIC as of FY2025, was misclassified as LMIC by the authors and retained due to their mention of its applicability to tobacco control in other populations. A second award (1R13AI186468-01), proposed a side-meeting at a global vaccine conference in South Korea, an HIC, was retained because it funded LMIC researcher participation, aligning with LMIC capacity-strengthening goals.

In addition, while we analysed funding opportunity types and found that most AI-related awards were funded through investigator-initiated mechanisms (PAs and PARs), structural factors such as eligibility criteria, institutional capacity and the availability of targeted initiatives may have influenced LMIC participation. Targeted initiatives such as those supporting DS-I Africa (eg, RM-22-023) across a range of scientific activities resulted in LMIC-led, AI-enabled health science awards that might not otherwise have been possible. That said, interpreting the share of LMIC-focused awards based on targeted initiatives alone can be misleading; a more appropriate benchmark would be whether the proportion of LMIC-relevant projects differs meaningfully from other broad NIH research domains. Regardless, the share will inevitably shift under an emerging ‘America First’ research prioritisation, which will reduce global engagement in future NIH funded science—whether AI-related or otherwise.

Second, although an impressive number of rigorous studies from LMICs was included, the small sample size and clustering of research within countries and institutions suggested a potential lack of independence among findings. Such clustering, where focal areas and results from one study may be dependent on others at the same institution, argued against using inferential statistics to ascertain the statistical significance in observed differences (eg, by country income status or health condition). Third, although standard approaches to prompt engineering were tested and deployed to ensure reliable coding of grants, different categorisations of study focus, health condition or other features may have been possible. We took extra care to use a coding scheme derived from the relevant published literature and standard review procedures, and to limit the application of ChatGPT to data categorisation. Finally, and importantly, this analysis was conducted prior to significant shifts in research priorities within NIH, which has drastically affected funded research with foreign investigators and associated subawards.26 As a result, it is unclear how well these data reflect the NIH portfolio today.

Despite these limitations, this analysis suggests that a new, rapidly evolving and robust area of scientific inquiry has emerged in recent years in the global deployment of AI in health science. Moreover, by focusing on highly competitive, NIH-funded science underway, this analysis identifies and describes more rigorously designed evaluations of the use of AI-augmented health science that have the potential to yield robust findings that are superior to those commonly reported use cases. As more AI-enabled health science enters the literature, strategic frameworks such as Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials-AI and Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials-AI will be critical to help readers evaluate the quality of clinical trials and assess the risk of bias in reported findings.27 Given the tremendous value of international collaboration on AI-enabled health science, we identify a need for ongoing research support for digital science capacity strengthening and infrastructure that ensures the ethical and responsible use of the technology, thereby improving health and well-being for all populations.
