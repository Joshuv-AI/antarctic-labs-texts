Understanding challenges and opportunities to implement preconception iron and folic acid supplementation to prevent neural tube defects and iron deficiency anaemia in Ethiopian health care system: a qualitative study
Authors: Anteneh Berhane, Tefera Belachew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreconception iron and folic acid supplementation (IFAS) is a key intervention to prevent neural tube defects (NTDs) and maternal and child iron deficiency anaemia (IDA). Despite its potential benefits, many healthcare systems rarely provide preconception IFAS to women who plan to become pregnant and mothers with a history of spontaneous abortion who declined contraceptive use. The aim of this study was to explore stakeholder perspectives on the challenges and opportunities for implementing preconception IFAS for these high-risk groups in the Ethiopian healthcare system, a country with a high burden of NTDs and IDA.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study was conducted among 45 participants, including women attending antenatal care, women planning pregnancy including mothers with a history of spontaneous abortion who declined contraceptive use, healthcare providers and maternal and child care experts. Participants were selected using purposive sampling. Data were first analysed separately for each participant group and then triangulated to synthesise a comprehensive understanding of practices, challenges and opportunities.ResultsMajor barriers operate at policy, health system and individual levels, including the absence of a structured national preconception care programme, inadequate health system capacity, limited knowledge among women and providers, behavioural constraints to preconception care utilisation and a high prevalence of unplanned pregnancies. However, existing health extension programme, family planning clinic and gynaecology department with supportive policy frameworks offer viable entry points for integrating preconception IFAS.ConclusionThis study identified major gaps in the implementation of preconception IFAS within the Ethiopian healthcare system. Women with a history of spontaneous abortion who decline contraceptive use represent an overlooked group with unmet preconception nutritional needs. Strengthening policy, training and community awareness is essential. Future implementation research is needed to support integration of preconception IFAS and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes, contributing to Sustainable Development Goals for maternal and child health.

Introduction

Neural tube defects (NTDs) are serious birth defects that occur early in gestation and can be largely prevented by provision of adequate maternal folate intake around conception.1 2 Globally, NTDs occur in about 1 in 1000 pregnancies, making them the second most common congenital anomaly. Annually, they result in 300 000 affected births, 88 000 deaths and 8.6 million disability-adjusted life years.3 4 In Ethiopia, the NTD burden remains a significant public health concern, with substantial economic, social and health implications. A recent meta-analysis estimated roughly 71 cases per 10 000 births with NTDs in Ethiopia, about seven times the rate in high-income countries with folate interventions.5 Global evidence shows that adequate intake of preconception iron and folic acid supplementation (IFAS) is a critical intervention to prevent maternal IDA and congenital anomalies, including NTDs and spontaneous or miscarriage abortion that occur very early in pregnancy, and significantly improves maternal and neonatal outcomes.6 7 8 1 9

Despite strong global evidence and recognition of preconception IFAS as an effective strategy for improving maternal and child health outcomes, its uptake remains extremely low, and it is largely neglected, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa where NTDs and IDA remain highly prevalent.10 The consequences of this missed preventive opportunity are substantial, contributing to persistent burdens of adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes.

In Ethiopia, although national strategies such as wheat flour fortification with folic acid and iron and salt iodisation, including emerging double-fortified salt initiatives, have been implemented,11 12 no structured IFAS programme or national guideline has been established for women planning pregnancy or those with a history of spontaneous abortion. This is particularly important because NTD prevention is strongly dependent on adequate periconceptional folate status; however, folate stores may remain suboptimal following a pregnancy outcome, especially with short inter-pregnancy intervals, if dietary intake or supplementation is insufficient. Despite this biological rationale, the timing and practices of folate restoration after spontaneous abortion remain poorly understood, and little is known about supplementation behaviours or the barriers influencing them.

These gaps reflect a broader implementation failure in translating micronutrient strategies into preconception care. Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore stakeholder perspectives on the implementation gap in preconception IFAS among women planning pregnancy, women with a history of spontaneous abortion, healthcare providers and maternal and child health (MCH) experts in Ethiopia, a setting with a high burden of NTDs and IDA, to inform future intervention design and policy development and improve integration of preconception nutrition into reproductive health services.

Methods and materials

Study design

This study employed an exploratory qualitative design to explore perspectives of women attending antenatal care (ANC), women planning pregnancy, mothers with a history of spontaneous abortion, healthcare providers and experts regarding preconception IFAS.

Participants and recruitment procedure

Participants were recruited from three groups including women those who were currently pregnant and attending ANC services, women attending family planning clinics who declined modern contraceptive use to conceive within the next 12 months and mothers with a history of spontaneous abortion. Key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with purposively selected health professionals, including midwives, senior nurses and MCH experts working in obstetrics and gynaecology departments, family planning units and ANC services at participating facilities. Additionally, MCH experts from regional health bureaus were included. Eligible women were identified during routine clinic visits by trained data collectors in collaboration with healthcare providers. Each potential KII was contacted and provided with detailed information about the study and invited to participate after giving written informed consent. Interviews were conducted iteratively, continuing until thematic saturation was reached to ensure comprehensive coverage of practice on preconception IFAS, including programme implementation, service delivery, challenges and opportunities.

Sample size

The study utilised purposive sampling method, and the number of participants was determined by data saturation. Based on this, a total of 45 participants were recruited, including 20 women, 15 frontline healthcare providers and 10 MCH experts.

Data collection tool and measurement

For each group of participants, three separate semi-structured in-depth interview guides were developed based on an extensive review of literature on preconception care IFAS. The guides were designed to elicit detailed insights into the practice, key barriers and potential opportunities of preconception IFAS for the prevention of IDA and NTDs and MCH outcomes, challenges hindering the implementation of such services and potential opportunities and enablers for integrating IFAS into existing MCH programmes of the Ethiopian healthcare system (online supplemental file 1).

Women were asked about their knowledge and practices regarding preconception IFAS and perceived barriers. Healthcare providers were asked about the practice of routine maternal health service delivery, challenges in service delivery and opportunities within existing health services to support preconception supplementation. MCH experts were asked about programmatic and health system factors influencing preconception IFAS, as well as opportunities for strengthening preconception care within current MCH programmes and in the Ethiopian healthcare system.

The interview guides were reviewed for content validity by two independent qualitative research experts and pre-tested. The pre-test was conducted prior to the actual data collection among participants with characteristics similar to the study population in a health facility outside the selected study sites to assess the clarity, relevance, sequencing and comprehensibility of the interview questions. Participants involved in the pre-test were not included in the final study. Based on the findings of the pre-test, minor modifications were made to improve the wording, flow and consistency of the interview guides. Five trained data collectors, including midwives and MCH experts, conducted all interviews in the local language of participants. Data collection was conducted from May to June 2021.

Prior to data collection, informed consent and demographic information were obtained. The interviews were conducted face-to-face in private and quiet rooms within the participating health facilities or other participant-preferred locations that ensured privacy, confidentiality and minimal interruption. Only the interviewer and the participant were present during the interviews to maintain confidentiality and encourage open discussion. Each interview lasted between 45 and 90 min and was audio-recorded with participants’ permission. To protect confidentiality, no personal identifiers were included in the transcripts, and all audio recordings and transcripts were stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. Verbatim transcription was performed, followed by translation into English for analysis.

Trustworthiness of the study

Several strategies were employed to ensure the trustworthiness of the study. Prior to data collection, interviewers received 2 days of training on the interview procedures, qualitative interviewing techniques and the conceptual foundations of preconception IFAS. Credibility was enhanced through triangulation of data sources, peer debriefing among the research team and continuous discussion of emerging findings during the data collection and analysis processes. Transferability was supported by the use of purposive sampling and providing detailed descriptions of the study context and participants’ characteristics. Dependability and confirmability were ensured through consistent documentation of the research process, including interview procedures, coding frameworks, analytic memos and audit trails throughout the analysis.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted using Atlas.ti V.7.1. The research team first engaged in iterative readings of the transcripts to immerse themselves in the data and develop an initial coding framework. Significant statements were identified, and meaningful units were extracted and condensed. These units were then grouped into categories, from which broader themes and subthemes emerged. Coding and analysis were performed collaboratively by different researchers to ensure consistency, enhance credibility and minimise bias. Interpretive summaries were generated for each theme and validated through group discussions and cross-checking with field notes and audio recordings. Representative verbatim quotes were included to preserve participants’ voices and maintain contextual nuance.

Patient and public involvement

In this study there was no involvement of patient and public.

Practice of preconception iron and folic acid supplementation

Provision of iron and folic acid supplements

Almost all healthcare providers and experts reported that preconception IFAS for the prevention of NTDs and IDA is not provided to women planning pregnancy, including women discontinuing modern contraception and women with a history of spontaneous abortion who decline contraceptive use. Participants explained that most women present to health facilities only after pregnancy is confirmed or when fetal complications arise.

One public health expert stated:

“…Generally, we do not provide preconception IFAS for women planning pregnancy. We only provide IFAS during ANC” (an MCH expert from the Health Bureau).

Similarly, healthcare providers highlighted the absence of preconception services within routine maternal healthcare. A senior midwife explained:

“…In Ethiopia, preconception care is not part of routine maternal health services in health facilities. Therefore, folic acid supplementation is not provided for women planning pregnancy, including women discontinuing modern contraception or women with spontaneous abortion who refuse contraceptive use” (a senior midwife from the family planning service).

Women also reported the absence of preconception services before pregnancy:

“…The reason is the unavailability of the service in health facilities. Women usually go to health facilities only for antenatal care because that is where the service is available” (a woman attending ANC).

Overall, perspectives from MCH experts, healthcare providers and women consistently indicated that preconception IFAS is not currently integrated into routine maternal health services.

Challenges for implementation

Absence of clear policy

Public health experts indicated that in Ethiopia, there is no clear policy on preconception care services, including the provision of IFAS for the prevention of NTDs and other MCH outcomes such as IDA. They further emphasised that health programmes are implemented in accordance with established guidelines and policies, which currently prioritise focused ANC and skilled delivery services.

One public health expert explained:

“…Health care providers, as part of their clinical practice, may provide preconception care services to prevent neural tube defects (NTDs) in an informal and inconsistent manner. For example, a gynecologist may prescribe folic acid supplementation for 3 months as part of management for women with a history of pregnancies affected by NTDs. However, other health care providers do not routinely provide preconception care services in family planning clinics or gynecology wards” (an MCH expert from the Health Bureau).

Healthcare providers reported similar experiences. A midwife working in a family planning clinic explained:

“…For example, a university instructor once asked me to provide IFAS (iron and folic acid supplementation). However, I do not have any guideline to provide IFAS or folic acid supplementation to non-pregnant women, whether they are planning pregnancy or not” (a midwife nurse from the family planning clinic).

Women also reported that they did not receive information or guidance about such services:

“…Another reason is that healthcare providers do not have sufficient knowledge or information about preconception care services, including the provision of IFAS (iron and folic acid supplementation), for the prevention of neural tube defects. As a result, they do not provide this service to us” (a woman who follows ANC).

Perspectives from MCH experts, healthcare providers and women consistently indicate that the absence of national guidelines and clear policy direction is a major barrier to the implementation of preconception IFAS.

Health system barriers to implementation

Absence of programme in the healthcare system

Most healthcare providers explained that the provision of preconception IFAS for women planning pregnancy is a neglected component within health facilities and the broader Ethiopian health system. They further indicated that women are not routinely assessed for conditions such as IDA, nutritional status or other health problems prior to pregnancy.

One healthcare provider from the gynaecology ward explained:

“…Our focus is primarily on providing contraceptive services to women who have experienced spontaneous abortion. If a woman declines contraceptive use, we do not proceed further to provide preconception care services, including folic acid supplementation (FAS) for the prevention of NTDs affected pregnancies” (a midwifery nurse from a hospital gynecology ward).

Another healthcare worker added:

“…For women requesting removal of long-acting reversible contraceptives, I take considerable time to explore the reasons for discontinuation. However, health system officials often express concern and question the observed increase in the number of women seeking removal of long-acting contraceptives” (a diploma nurse from the family planning clinic/section).

Another midwife nurse working in the gynaecology ward added:

“…I only give counseling to take the prescribed folic acid tablets properly for three months for women who delivered a baby with NTDs and any type of congenital anomaly, but not linked with health extension” (a midwife nurse from the hospital gynecology ward).

Similarly, MCH experts emphasised that

“…The absence of structured programs contributes to missed opportunities for improving maternal and child health outcomes” (MCH expert from the Health Bureau).

Women also reported relying entirely on services provided by healthcare providers: “…We usually rely on healthcare providers. We assume that all necessary services are delivered to us” (women planning pregnancy and women with a history of spontaneous abortion).

High staff workload

Healthcare providers reported that the high workload in family planning and gynaecology clinics limits the integration of preconception care services, including preconception IFAS. Almost all providers indicated that routine responsibilities already demand substantial time and effort, leaving limited opportunity to introduce additional activities related to the prevention of NTDs.

One diploma nurse working in the gynaecology and family planning unit further explained that service delivery is constrained by limited staffing and a high client load:

“…Even if additional activities are introduced, there is already a high workload in this unit; for example, only one healthcare provider is responsible for contraceptive counseling and subsequent insertion. Frankly speaking, our workload is heavy, and the work is challenging and exhausting” (a diploma nurse from the gynecology ward and family planning section, after briefing about pre-conception care).

Findings from experts, healthcare providers and women consistently indicate that preconception IFAS is not integrated into routine maternal health services, leading to missed opportunities for prevention.

Lack of knowledge among caregivers and women

All participants acknowledged a substantial knowledge gap regarding preconception IFAS for the prevention of NTDs and other adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes, including IDA. They reported limited understanding of the scope of preconception care and insufficient knowledge of its practical implementation.

MCH experts further emphasised that preconception care is not adequately addressed in professional education or training. They noted that it is generally not included in the formal curriculum for many health professionals and that there is no specific service focus on preconception folic acid supplementation for women planning pregnancy to prevent NTDs. According to the experts, healthcare providers often lack both theoretical preparation and practical opportunities to deliver preconception care services.

One MCH expert explained:

“…Currently, preconception care is not included in the routine maternal health service package, and most health professionals are not trained to provide it. Consequently, services such as preconception folic acid supplementation are rarely offered prior to pregnancy” (MCH expert).

Healthcare providers corroborated this perspective, highlighting their first exposure to the concept of preconception IFAS. A diploma midwife nurse from the gynaecology ward explained:

“…This is the first time I have heard about preconception IFAS for the prevention of neural tube defects and other adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes among women who have experienced spontaneous abortion and who may decline contraceptive use while planning for pregnancy” (a midwife nurse from the gynecology ward).

A few healthcare providers acknowledged limited knowledge:

“…They had limited knowledge about preconception care and the provision of FAS or IFAS for women planning pregnancy for the prevention of NTDs and (IDA” (a midwife nurse from the family planning clinic).

A senior midwife added,

“…If you ask any healthcare provider, you are unlikely to get an answer on what preconception care means in relation to the prevention of NTDs. We have not heard about preconception care services for women planning pregnancy for the prevention of NTDs and other adverse pregnancy outcomes from any media source” (a midwife nurse from the family planning service).

Women reflected similar gaps in knowledge and experience. Those attending ANC reported limited awareness of preconception care:

“…We have not done anything before pregnancy because we do not have knowledge or information about preconception care with IFAS” (a woman attending ANC).

Women who discontinued contraceptive use in preparation for pregnancy reported relying on healthcare providers for guidance: “…We usually rely on healthcare providers. We assume that all necessary services are delivered to us” (a woman planning pregnancy and woman with a history of spontaneous abortion).

Another woman emphasised the consequence of this knowledge gap:

“…Why do healthcare providers not provide IFAS or FAS before pregnancy for the prevention of neural tube defects, and why have I not heard about it in any media? However, she [the nurse] knows why I discontinued contraceptive, so she should have provided preconception care services, including IFAS or FAS” (a woman who discontinued long-acting contraceptive).

The perspectives of MCH experts, healthcare providers and women converged in highlighting a systemic knowledge gap regarding preconception care and IFAS. These findings indicate that both healthcare providers and service users are under-informed, representing a significant barrier to the provision and utilisation of preconception care services.

Behavioural and contextual barriers to preconception preparation

Participants described several behavioural and contextual factors limiting women’s ability to prepare for pregnancy and benefit from preconception IFAS. Key barriers included low adherence to supplementation and the high prevalence of unplanned pregnancy.

Healthcare providers reported that adherence to IFAS remains challenging even among pregnant women attending ANC services, suggesting that similar difficulties could affect supplementation during the preconception period.

One public health expert explained:

“…You can see that pregnant women attending ANC programs are not adherent to IFAS. There is low adherence even among healthcare providers unless another strategy is employed” (an MCH expert).

Participants also emphasised that unplanned pregnancy substantially limits opportunities for preconception care. Both healthcare providers and women reported that many pregnancies occur without prior preparation, reducing the likelihood of receiving preventive interventions before conception. One MCH expert and senior midwife explained:

“…It is due to unplanned pregnancy. For some women, this may be related to menstrual disturbance. For example, even women who know about pregnancy preparation may conceive before receiving this care because of irregular menstrual cycles” (an MCH expert and a senior midwife).

Women participants described similar experiences within the community:

“…The majority of women do not do anything before conception. Some do not even realise they are pregnant until fetal movement begins. Others only learn about their pregnancy when they visit a health facility for another illness. Some women also give birth without knowing their gestational age” (woman attending ANC).

Overall, perspectives from MCH experts, healthcare providers and women consistently indicated that low adherence, unplanned pregnancy and delayed pregnancy recognition reduce opportunities for preconception preparation and limit the uptake of preconception IFAS.

Existing health system platforms for integration of preconception iron and folic acid supplementation

Participants identified several existing platforms within the Ethiopian health system that could facilitate the integration of preconception IFAS. In particular, the health extension programme, family planning services and gynaecology ward were repeatedly described as potential entry points for delivering preconception care interventions aimed at preventing NTDs and other adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Health extension programme

Most MCH experts and healthcare providers agreed that the Ethiopian health extension programme represents an important opportunity for delivering preconception care services at the community level. Participants explained that health extension workers already provide door-to-door services and could play a key role in identifying women planning pregnancy and linking them to health facilities for preconception IFAS. They further suggested that preconception care could be initiated at health facilities for women planning pregnancy or women with a history of spontaneous abortion who decline contraceptive use and subsequently reinforced through community-based follow-up by health extension workers.

One public health expert emphasised the broader public health significance of this approach:

“…Preconception care services with provision of IFAS or folic acid supplementation are crucial for achieving sustainable development goals by reducing neonatal morbidity, mortality, and disability” (an MCH expert).

Family planning service and gynaecology ward

Participants also identified family planning clinics and gynaecology wards as important platforms for integrating preconception care services. Almost all MCH experts and healthcare providers explained that women attending family planning services, as well as women receiving care following spontaneous abortion, represent valuable opportunities for assessment and counselling prior to pregnancy.

According to participants, women planning pregnancy should be assessed for nutritional status, including folate and iron status, lifestyle and environmental risk factors and pre-existing medical conditions such as diabetes mellitus, asthma, hypertension and anticonvulsant medication use. Participants further emphasised that women planning pregnancy, including those with a history of spontaneous abortion who decline contraceptive use, should ideally receive IFAS for at least 3 months before conception.

Similarly, a midwife and an MCH expert from the gynaecology ward reflected on missed opportunities within current services:

“…We miss a great opportunity for mothers who refuse contraceptive use after spontaneous abortion [indirectly indicating intention for pregnancy], although this could be an important strategy for improving maternal and child health, including prevention of NTD-affected pregnancies” (an MCH expert and a diploma midwife nurse from the gynecology ward).

Overall, perspectives from women, healthcare providers and MCH experts consistently highlighted the absence of structured preconception IFAS services while identifying feasible opportunities for integration within existing maternal health programmes, particularly through the health extension programme, family planning services and gynaecology care.

Discussion

This study explored the challenges and opportunities related to IFAS within the Ethiopian healthcare system. The findings revealed that preconception IFAS is largely absent from routine maternal health services, with supplementation typically initiated during antenatal care rather than before conception. Several barriers were identified, including the absence of national policies, programmes and guidelines; broader health system constraints; limited knowledge and awareness; behavioural barriers; high workload among healthcare providers; and the high prevalence of unplanned pregnancy. Participants also identified existing opportunities for integrating preconception IFAS into family planning services and the health extension programme. Collectively, these findings highlight a substantial gap between global evidence supporting preconception nutrition interventions and current practice in Ethiopia.

Triangulated evidence from participants indicates that preconception IFAS is not routinely practised within the Ethiopian healthcare system, potentially limiting opportunities to optimise maternal micronutrient status before pregnancy. This finding is consistent with evidence from several sub-Saharan African countries, where uptake of preconception folic acid supplementation remains below 15%.101316 The consistency of these findings across diverse settings suggests that preconception supplementation continues to be largely overlooked within maternal health programmes, which tend to prioritise antenatal and delivery care rather than preventive interventions before conception. This neglect persists despite substantial global evidence demonstrating that adequate folic acid and iron intake before conception and during early pregnancy significantly reduces the risk of NTDs and IDA.17 18 Consequently, the absence of structured preconception supplementation within the Ethiopian healthcare system represents an important implementation gap requiring strategic policy attention and integration into routine maternal health services, particularly for women planning pregnancy and women with a history of spontaneous abortion.

The study also identified several health system barriers that hinder implementation of preconception supplementation services. These included the absence of clear policies and operational guidelines, lack of structured preconception care programmes, high workload among healthcare providers and competing clinical responsibilities. Providers frequently described limited institutional support for preconception services and explained that existing maternal healthcare activities already require substantial time and resources, leaving little capacity to introduce additional preventive interventions such as preconception counselling or supplementation. Similar barriers have been documented in settings where preconception care remains poorly integrated into routine services. Evidence from systematic reviews further indicates that unclear policy direction, inadequate resources and fragmented care systems are common obstacles to implementing preventive interventions before pregnancy.19 Together, these findings suggest that effective integration of preconception IFAS into routine healthcare services will require not only improved provider training and awareness but also stronger institutional support through clear policy frameworks, operational guidance and adequate resource allocation.

Another important finding of this study was the substantial knowledge gap regarding preconception care and IFAS among both healthcare providers and women. Healthcare providers reported that preconception supplementation is rarely addressed in professional training programmes, and many providers were unfamiliar with the concept of delivering IFAS before pregnancy. Women similarly described limited awareness regarding the importance of pregnancy preparation and the use of folic acid prior to conception. Comparable findings have been reported in studies conducted in Ethiopia and other low-income and middle-income countries, where awareness of preconception folic acid supplementation remains low.1415 2023 For example, a community-based study in Ethiopia found that only approximately 35% of women were aware of the role of preconception folic acid supplementation in preventing NTDs.23 Limited pre-service training and inadequate continuing professional education on preconception health may contribute to the low prioritisation of these services within routine clinical practice. Similarly, insufficient public health communication regarding the benefits of preconception nutrition may reduce women’s awareness and demand for preventive services. Strengthening knowledge and awareness among healthcare providers is particularly important, as providers play a central role in counselling women and promoting preventive maternal health behaviours.

In addition to health system and knowledge-related barriers, participants identified several behavioural and contextual factors limiting the uptake of preconception IFAS. In particular, the high prevalence of unplanned pregnancy and low adherence to IFAS during ANC were described as major challenges. Many women reported becoming aware of their pregnancy only after conception or later in gestation, thereby missing the critical window for effective preconception intervention. These findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating that unintended pregnancy is associated with lower utilisation of preconception care services, including folic acid supplementation.24 25 In settings where pregnancies are unplanned or recognised late, opportunities to optimise maternal nutritional status before conception are substantially reduced. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of integrated reproductive health strategies that promote pregnancy planning, improve awareness of preconception care and support proactive maternal health behaviours.

Despite these challenges, participants identified several promising opportunities for integrating preconception IFAS into existing health services. In particular, the Ethiopian health extension programme was described as a valuable platform for delivering community-based preconception interventions.14 Health extension workers already provide a broad range of preventive health services and maintain close contact with households, placing them in a strategic position to identify women planning pregnancy and provide counselling, follow-up or referral for supplementation services.

In addition, family planning services and gynaecology wards were identified as strategic entry points for providing preconception counselling and IFAS to women discontinuing contraception or planning pregnancy or open to pregnancy. Evidence from similar low-resource settings suggests that family planning clinics can improve the uptake of maternal and newborn health interventions, including nutrition counselling and supplementation.26 27 Integrating preconception IFAS into these established service platforms may enhance early access to preventive care without requiring substantial structural reforms.

The findings of this study have important implications for MCH policy and practice. First, the absence of clear national guidelines for preconception IFAS highlights the need for stronger policy commitment to integrating preconception care into maternal health programmes. Developing and implementing national guidelines could provide healthcare providers with a structured framework for delivering preconception interventions. Particular attention should be given to women with a history of spontaneous abortion who decline contraceptive use, as they may become pregnant before restoration of adequate folate status. Integrating preconception IFAS counselling and supplementation into post-abortion care, family planning services and gynaecology care may help reduce missed opportunities for the prevention of NTDs and other adverse pregnancy outcomes. Second, strengthening training for healthcare providers is essential to improve awareness, knowledge and competency in delivering preconception care services. Integrating preconception health into professional education curricula and continuing professional development programmes may help address existing knowledge gaps. Third, public health communication strategies are needed to increase awareness among women regarding the importance of pregnancy preparation and the use of IFAS before conception. Community-based health promotion through the Ethiopian health extension programme may be particularly effective in reaching women of reproductive age.

This study has several strengths. The qualitative design enabled an in-depth exploration of perceptions and experiences related to preconception IFAS from multiple perspectives within the health system. Including women, healthcare providers and MCH experts allowed triangulation of findings and provided a comprehensive understanding of the barriers and opportunities related to implementation of preconception care services. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. The study involved a relatively small number of participants, particularly among MCH experts, which may have limited the diversity of perspectives captured. In addition, participants were recruited from selected health facilities and may not fully represent experiences from other regions or more rural settings. Despite these limitations, the study provides important contextual insights into factors influencing the implementation of preconception IFAS within the Ethiopian healthcare system.

Conclusions

This study identified major gaps in the implementation of preconception IFAS within the Ethiopian healthcare system. Preconception IFAS is not routinely integrated into maternal health services, and its implementation is hindered by the absence of national guidelines and structured programmes, limited knowledge among healthcare providers and women, high workload, behavioural barriers and the high prevalence of unplanned pregnancy. Women with a history of spontaneous abortion who decline contraceptive use were identified as an important but overlooked group with potential unmet preconception nutritional needs. Despite these challenges, existing platforms such as the health extension programme, family planning services and gynaecology wards provide feasible opportunities for integrating preconception IFAS into routine reproductive health services. Strengthening national policy frameworks, developing operational guidelines, improving provider training and increasing community awareness are essential to improve implementation. Integrating preconception IFAS into post-abortion care and family planning services may help reduce missed opportunities for preventing NTDs, maternal IDA and other adverse pregnancy outcomes. Further implementation and intervention research is needed to evaluate effective strategies for integrating preconception IFAS into the Ethiopian health system with potential to reduce NTDs and maternal IDA and contribute to the achievement of Sustainable Development Goals for maternal and neonatal health.
