INTRODUCTION

Hemorrhagic shock is a life‐threatening form of circulatory failure resulting from acute blood loss that causes inadequate tissue perfusion, cellular hypoxia, and metabolic derangement. 1 , 2 It remains a major global cause of morbidity and mortality across both traumatic and non‐traumatic etiologies. Traumatic hemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable death in adults under 45 years of age and accounts for approximately 1.5 million deaths annually worldwide. 1 , 3 , 4 Non‐traumatic causes, including gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, obstetric hemorrhage, and vascular rupture, also contribute substantially to the global burden of shock, particularly in settings where access to blood products and rapid resuscitation is limited. 4 , 5 , 6 Despite advances in trauma systems and critical care, the temporal distribution of hemorrhage‐related deaths has changed little over time, with most fatalities occurring within hours of injury or onset. 7 , 8 , 9 Early mortality is frequently the result of uncontrolled bleeding before definitive hemostasis can be achieved, emphasizing the critical importance of timely recognition and early initiation of hemostatic resuscitation. 3 , 10

Efforts to improve survival from hemorrhagic shock have increasingly focused on early, balanced transfusion strategies that restore circulating volume and coagulation function simultaneously. 11 Historically, whole blood (WB) was the standard resuscitative fluid during much of the twentieth century but was replaced by component therapy (CT) to facilitate inventory management and targeted transfusion. 12 Modern balanced resuscitation approaches, such as the 1:1:1 ratio of red blood cells, plasma, and platelets, were developed to approximate WB and have demonstrated improved hemostatic control and reduced exsanguination in trauma. 13 WB has since re‐emerged as a practical and physiologic alternative, offering red cells, plasma, and platelets in a single product with preserved hemostatic properties. 14 , 15 , 16 In the prehospital environment, where transport time, storage capacity, and logistics limit access to multiple components, a single balanced product may simplify resuscitation and expedite delivery of hemostatic therapy. 17 , 18 , 19

Several foundational trials have evaluated prehospital transfusion of blood products for patients with hemorrhagic shock. 20 The Prehospital Air Medical Plasma (PAMPer) and Control of Major Bleeding After Trauma (COMBAT) trials evaluated prehospital plasma compared with crystalloid resuscitation and reported mixed results. 21 , 22 These studies were conducted between 2009 and 2013 and primarily involved helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS). The RePHILL trial reported no survival difference between prehospital red blood cell and plasma transfusion versus crystalloid. 23 Differences in patient acuity, injury mechanism, transport timelines, and prehospital team composition limit direct comparison with contemporary civilian paramedic‐based emergency medical services (EMS). 21 , 22 , 23 Observational studies suggest that earlier transfusion, rather than product type alone, is associated with improved short‐term survival. 24 , 25 , 26 Civilian and military systems have increasingly adopted WB for early resuscitation, reporting favorable feasibility and safety profiles in both trauma and non‐traumatic hemorrhage. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 However, the existing evidence remains limited, and uncertainty persists regarding the specific effectiveness and safety of WB when administered in the prehospital setting.

The literature evaluating prehospital transfusion demonstrates substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity. 30 , 31 Studies differ in patient populations, including traumatic and non‐traumatic hemorrhage, transfusion strategies, EMS system structure, transport times, and outcome definitions. Most available data are derived from observational designs with moderate to serious risk of bias, which limits causal inference and generalizability.

The objective of this systematic review was to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of prehospital WB transfusion compared with CT or crystalloid‐based resuscitation in adults with hemorrhagic shock from traumatic and non‐traumatic causes.

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines 33 , 34 and was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024604327).

Eligibility criteria

Study eligibility criteria were defined using the Population/Intervention/Comparison/Outcomes/Study Design/Timeframe framework. 35 The population included adults aged 16 years or older with hemorrhagic shock from either traumatic or non‐traumatic causes. Both civilian and military populations were eligible. The intervention was prehospital WB transfusion administered before hospital arrival as part of early resuscitation. Studies describing in‐hospital transfusion were included only when WB administration was initiated during the prehospital phase as part of early resuscitation. Out‐of‐hospital surgical teams, such as forward surgical units, were excluded because their capacity for definitive surgical hemostasis precludes direct comparison with conventional prehospital care.

Comparators included CT or crystalloid‐based resuscitation. The primary outcome was 24‐h all‐cause mortality. 36 Secondary outcomes included mortality at 1 h, 3–6 h, and 28–30 days; 24‐h transfusion requirements (volume and units of blood products); and transfusion‐related adverse events, including febrile non‐hemolytic transfusion reactions (FNHTR), allergic or anaphylactic reactions, transfusion‐associated circulatory overload (TACO), transfusion‐related acute lung injury (TRALI), and transfusion‐transmitted infection. 37 Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials, non‐randomized controlled trials, and observational studies (cohort or case–control). While included as safety outcomes, complications such as TACO, TRALI, and transfusion‐transmitted infection typically manifest beyond the prehospital timeframe. Accordingly, these complications are unlikely to be detected during prehospital care and are primarily relevant to in‐hospital monitoring.

Information sources

The electronic search strategy was developed with an experienced information specialist (R.S.) and peer‐reviewed by a second specialist following Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guidelines. 38 The following databases were searched: EMBASE (Ovid), MEDLINE (Ovid), Scopus, Web of Science, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Searches were conducted independently by two information specialists using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free‐text terms combined with Boolean operators. Searches were performed from database inception to August 27, 2025. All publication years were considered, but only studies published in English or French were included. Non‐English or French language papers, conference abstracts, and unpublished data were excluded to ensure all included studies had undergone peer review. Reference lists were screened for additional studies, and subject matter experts reviewed the final selection for completeness and relevance.

Search strategy

Complete search strategies for each database, including all search terms, filters, and limits, are provided in Table S1: Electronic Search Strategies. The strategy incorporated both controlled vocabulary and free‐text keywords related to “whole blood,” “prehospital transfusion,” and “hemorrhagic shock,” adapted to each database's indexing system.

Selection process

All records were imported into Covidence systematic review software (version 2.0; Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (P.‐M.D. and A.G.) independently screened titles and abstracts according to the eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion, and unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer (B.N.). No automation tools were used.

Data collection process

Two reviewers (P.‐M.D. and A.G.) independently extracted data in duplicate using a standardized, piloted form in Covidence. Extracted data were compared for accuracy, with discrepancies resolved by consensus or third‐party adjudication (B.N.). No automation or machine‐assisted tools were used. When information was incomplete or unclear, study authors were contacted, and data were extracted as reported in the published article or updated upon author response.

Data items

Data were extracted for all reported measures of mortality (1‐h, 3‐6‐h, 24‐h, and 28‐ to 30‐day), transfusion requirements (volume and number of blood units), and transfusion‐related adverse events, including febrile non‐hemolytic, allergic, or anaphylactic reactions, as well as TACO, TRALI, and transfusion‐transmitted infection. Additional data included publication details (author, year, and country), study characteristics (design, setting, and inclusion criteria), population characteristics (age, Injury Severity Score [ISS]), and intervention details (type, volume, and timing of transfusion). Assumptions regarding missing or unclear data were documented, and no imputation was performed.

Study risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers (P.‐M.D. and A.G.). Randomized controlled trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (Risk of Bias 2 tool [RoB 2]) 39 and, when applicable, the version with additional considerations for cluster‐randomized trials. 40 Non‐randomized studies were assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non‐Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool. 41 Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (B.N.).

Effect measures

For mortality outcomes, risk ratios or odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted as reported. For continuous variables, mean differences or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) were collected.

Synthesis methods

Data were tabulated and analyzed descriptively using Microsoft Excel 365 (Version 16.101.3; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). A quantitative meta‐analysis was planned using a random‐effects model when studies were sufficiently homogeneous in design, population, intervention, and outcome reporting. Pooled effect estimates were to include risk ratios, ORs, or mean differences with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐tailed p‐value <.05.

When meta‐analysis was not feasible, a structured narrative synthesis was conducted following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions 42 and Synthesis Without Meta‐Analysis (SWiM) reporting guidelines. 43 Findings were summarized by outcome domain (mortality, transfusion requirements, and safety) and time interval (≤6 h, 24 h, and 28–30 days). The direction and magnitude of effects were described, and consistency and certainty of evidence were evaluated.

Reporting bias assessment

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot asymmetry and Egger's test when at least 10 studies with comparable outcomes were available. 44 , 45

Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. 46 Each outcome was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low certainty based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

RESULTS

Database searches identified 5482 records. After removing 2910 duplicates, 2572 unique records were screened, and 98 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. A total of seven studies met inclusion criteria, comprising 6954 patients. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 The study selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Characteristics of included studies are summarized in Table 1. All included studies were conducted in the United States and evaluated low‐titer group O whole blood (LTOWB) use in civilian prehospital programs or EMS‐linked trauma systems (2020–2025). The included designs consisted of a pragmatic cluster‐randomized pilot trial 50 and six observational cohort studies. 47 , 48 , 49 , 51 , 52 , 53 Comparator strategies included CT 47 , 49 , 50 , 52 , 53 or crystalloid‐based resuscitation approaches. 48 , 51 Across included studies, transfusion strategies varied. Some programs administered WB only during the prehospital phase, while others initiated WB prehospital with continuation after emergency department (ED) arrival, or evaluated early WB administered as part of a combined prehospital and ED pathway. In one multicenter study, WB was administered prehospital at a single site and initiated early in‐hospital at other participating centers. 52 Given heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcome measures, a meta‐analysis was not performed; instead, a structured narrative synthesis was conducted in accordance with SWiM reporting guidelines. 42 , 43

Mortality and survival outcomes are summarized in Table 4. The study by Williams et al. analyzed 350 patients (198 LTOWB vs. 152 CT); 30‐day survival was 73% versus 74% (p = .81), with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 2.19 (95% CI 1.01–4.76; p = .047); the authors reported this adjusted association favoring LTOWB despite similar unadjusted survival. 47 Braverman et al. included 538 patients, 214 of whom were propensity‐matched (58 prehospital WB vs. 156 non‐WB); unadjusted mortality was 0% versus 7.1% in the ED (p = .04), 5.3% versus 14.1% at 6 h (p = .08), 17.2% versus 23.1% at 24 h (p = .36), and 29.0% versus 34.8% at discharge (p = .25); none reached statistical significance after adjustment. 48 Brill et al. evaluated 1377 patients (840 LTOWB vs. 537 CT) and reported 30‐day survival of 75% versus 76%; unweighted OR 4.10 (95% CI 2.22–7.45; p < .001) and weighted OR 1.59 (95% CI 1.28–1.98; p < .001) both indicated improved adjusted survival with LTOWB. 49 Guyette et al. studied 86 patients (40 LTOWB vs. 46 CT); mortality was 10.0% versus 10.9% at 3 h (p = .90), 12.5% versus 13.0% at 6 h (p = .94), 15.0% versus 17.4% at 24 h (p = .76), and 25.0% versus 26.1% at 28 days (p = .91), with no significant differences. 50 Braverman et al. analyzed 1562 patients (171 prehospital WB vs. 1391 non‐WB) and found mortality of 2.3% versus 4.5% in the ED (p = .18), 12.9% versus 16.1% at 24 h (p = .27), and 18.7% versus 23.7% in hospital (p = .14), with no adjusted survival difference. 51

Sperry et al. conducted a multicenter prospective cohort of 1051 patients (624 LTOWB vs. 427 CT). After propensity adjustment, relative risks (RR) for mortality were 0.90 (95% CI 0.59–1.39) at 4 h, 1.08 (95% CI 0.77–1.52) at 24 h, and 1.10 (95% CI 0.83–1.47) at 28 days. In prespecified high‐risk subgroups, LTOWB was associated with lower mortality: ≥5% predicted mortality, 4 h RR 0.52 (0.32–0.87); ≥10% predicted mortality, 24 h RR 0.67 (0.47–0.97); ≥20% predicted mortality, 28 days RR 0.70 (0.51–0.96); and ≥50% predicted mortality, 28 days RR 0.64 (0.45–0.93). 52 Kostolni et al. analyzed 1990 prehospital transfusions (1515 LTOWB vs. 475 CT); adjusted aOR 1.68 (95% CI 0.58–4.89) for mortality showed no significant difference. Unadjusted mortality was 18.9% versus 7.1% (p = .003); in stratified analyses as reported by the study authors, trauma mortality was 16.0% versus 7.3% (p = .358) and medical mortality 22.2% versus 9.0% (p = .615). 53

Transfusion and physiologic response outcomes are summarized in Table 4. Findings by Guyette et al. indicated lower RBC utilization with LTOWB (0 vs. 2 units at 6 h and 0.5 vs. 2 units at 24 h; p < .01). 50 Brill et al. observed reduced 24‐h component transfusion (aOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.21–0.70; p < .001) and lower total transfusion volume (p < .001). 49 Braverman et al. reported fewer massive transfusion activations (22.6% vs. 32.4%; p = .01) and greater shock index (SI) reduction (−0.04 vs. +0.05; p = .002). 51 Braverman et al. similarly noted lower massive transfusion protocol (MTP) activation (30.8% vs. 42.2%; p = .03). 48 Williams et al. found fewer post‐ED products (0 vs. 3 units; p = .001) with aOR 0.47 (95% CI 0.23–0.94; p = .033). 47 Sperry et al. observed comparable total units at 24 h (median 12 [IQR 4–27] vs. 10 [4–22]; p = .07) but higher total transfusion volume (4105 [1765–9290] mL vs. 2945 [1265–6935] mL; p < .001). 52 Kostolni et al. reported modest differences in SI change (−0.20 vs. −0.30; p = .04) and heart rate (HR) reduction (−4 vs. −7 bpm; p = .007). 53

Safety outcomes are summarized in Table 4. No study reported an increased incidence of transfusion reactions with LTOWB. Williams et al. recorded two suspected reactions in the component group and none among LTOWB recipients (p = .061). 47 Brill et al. observed no transfusion reactions and a lower composite of death plus sepsis in the LTOWB group, with sepsis in 25% versus 30% (p = .041). 49 Sperry et al. found no transfusion reactions and similar rates of nosocomial infection (24.8% vs. 22.2%; p = .33), deep‐vein thrombosis (7.9% vs. 5.2%; p = .09), pulmonary embolism (PE) (6.1% vs. 6.1%; p > .99), and multiple organ failure (23.7% vs. 27.4%; p = .16). 52 Kostolni et al. reported no transfusion reactions in the ED and three thromboembolic events among LTOWB recipients, all deemed unrelated. 53 Across all studies, no evidence of hemolysis, TRALI, TACO, or transfusion‐transmitted infection associated with LTOWB was observed. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53

DISCUSSION

Interpretation

This systematic review found that prehospital administration of WB in adults with hemorrhagic shock is feasible and demonstrates a consistent safety profile across included studies. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Across studies, WB transfusion was consistently associated with lower subsequent transfusion requirements compared with CT or crystalloid‐based resuscitation. These findings suggest that early administration of WB supports more efficient and physiologically balanced resuscitation during the initial phase of shock management. Adverse reactions most likely to occur during short prehospital transport intervals include allergic, anaphylactic, and febrile non‐hemolytic reactions. These events were rare across included studies. No included study demonstrated an increased risk of transfusion‐related complications such as hemolysis, TACO, or acute lung injury, indicating that prehospital WB can be delivered safely under structured protocols. The risk of hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn associated with RhD‐positive LTOWB exposure is low and must be interpreted in the context of the immediate mortality risk associated with uncontrolled hemorrhage.

The observed reduction in blood‐product use likely reflects the physiologic advantages of WB, which restores oxygen delivery, clotting factors, and platelets simultaneously. Early correction of coagulopathy and prevention of dilutional effects are key factors in the management of hemorrhagic shock. 1 , 2 WB simplifies transfusion logistics by replacing multiple components with a single balanced product, reducing handling time and potential errors during transport. 15 , 16

Mortality outcomes varied across studies, with some reporting improved early survival following prehospital WB transfusion 48 , 49 and others showing no significant difference compared with CT. 52 , 53 Differences in comparator strategies may have contributed to variability in reported outcomes. Studies comparing WB with crystalloid‐based resuscitation or no prehospital transfusion more frequently reported physiologic or early outcome differences, while comparisons with balanced CT demonstrated less consistent effects. This variability may reflect differences in study design, inclusion criteria, and system‐level factors such as transport duration. Six of the seven included studies evaluated traumatic hemorrhage exclusively. One study included both traumatic and non‐traumatic hemorrhage and contributed the largest proportion of patients. 53 Mortality following hemorrhagic shock is influenced by multiple system‐level factors. These include use of adjunctive therapies such as tranexamic acid and calcium supplementation, effectiveness of hemorrhage control, receiving trauma center capabilities, time to definitive surgical intervention, and ED to operating room workflows. These variables were inconsistently reported across included studies. Evidence supporting the use of WB in non‐traumatic hemorrhagic shock remains very limited, and its applicability requires further evaluation. In this registry‐based cohort, non‐traumatic hemorrhage included GI, obstetric, and other medical etiologies. 53 Outcomes were not reported with sufficient granularity to allow etiology‐specific comparisons, and analyses were not powered to detect differences between traumatic and non‐traumatic subgroups. Importantly, non‐traumatic hemorrhage encompassed heterogeneous medical etiologies with distinct pathophysiology and resuscitation trajectories, often managed with different definitive interventions, further limiting direct comparison with traumatic hemorrhage and precluding etiology‐specific inference.

Timing may influence the potential benefit of prehospital transfusion. Prior studies have demonstrated associations between delayed transfusion and increased mortality, while earlier initiation of hemostatic resuscitation has been associated with improved physiologic parameters and early survival. 24 , 25 , 26 In the present review, transport time was inconsistently reported across included studies and could not be assessed directly. Evidence suggesting differential benefit based on transport duration is derived primarily from studies evaluating prehospital plasma transfusion and post hoc analyses rather than from WB‐specific data. 3 , 54 Consequently, the relationship between transport time and the effectiveness of prehospital WB transfusion cannot be determined from the available evidence. 3 , 54 Overall, current data support that prehospital WB transfusion is safe and logistically feasible when implemented within systems capable of maintaining appropriate supply, storage, and quality control; however, effectiveness remains uncertain and is likely influenced by broader system‐level factors that were inconsistently measured across studies.

Previous studies

Prior studies evaluating prehospital plasma or red blood cell transfusion reported inconsistent survival effects. 21 , 22 , 23 The RePHILL trial enrolled patients with relatively preserved blood pressure, predominantly blunt mechanisms of injury, delayed initiation of transfusion, and physician‐led prehospital teams. 23 The primary outcome included lactate clearance, which may be influenced by factors such as sedation, ventilation strategies, and vasopressor use. These characteristics limit comparability with paramedic‐based EMS systems managing patients with profound hemorrhagic shock. 23 These findings align with the results of this review, which emphasize that geography, system structure, and transport time may influence the applicability of findings across systems.

Strengths and limitations

This review adhered to established methodological standards and was prospectively registered. It employed a comprehensive, peer‐reviewed search strategy across multiple databases and included independent dual screening and data extraction. Risk of bias and certainty of evidence were evaluated using validated tools, ensuring methodological transparency and consistency.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Most included studies were observational, introducing potential residual confounding related to injury severity, physiology, and treatment selection. The limited number of eligible studies and variation in design, population, and outcome reporting precluded meta‐analysis. With the exception of one study, all examined trauma‐related hemorrhage, restricting applicability to non‐traumatic causes of hemorrhage. The predominance of data from the United States may further limit generalizability to other prehospital systems. Adverse events were infrequently reported, but none of the included studies identified significant safety concerns. Large prospective civilian cohorts evaluating LTOWB as the primary prehospital resuscitative fluid remain limited. Most published evidence consists of single‐system observational studies, pilot trials, or analyses combining prehospital and ED transfusion. Standardized data elements for prehospital transfusion are not currently established in the United States. 55 Variability in documentation practices, electronic health record capabilities, definitions of shock and transfusion triggers, linkage to trauma registries, and completeness of follow‐up contributes to inconsistent reporting across systems.

Clinical implications

The available evidence supports the safe implementation of prehospital WB programs within systems equipped to maintain rigorous donor screening, cold storage, and traceability. 18 , 56 WB simplifies resuscitation by providing a balanced transfusion product and may facilitate earlier initiation of hemostatic resuscitation when compared with component‐based or crystalloid strategies. 56 The timing and feasibility of early administration remain dependent on local protocols, logistics, and coordination between prehospital and hospital‐based teams. While timing of administration appears relevant to potential benefit, transport duration was inconsistently reported in existing studies, and its independent effect cannot be determined. 57 Clinical outcomes following prehospital WB transfusion are therefore best interpreted within the context of broader system‐level factors, including early recognition of hemorrhagic shock, coordination between EMS and receiving trauma centers, and timely access to definitive hemorrhage control. 18 , 58

Research implications

Further investigation should aim to clarify which patient populations derive the greatest benefit from prehospital WB transfusion, as well as the optimal timing of administration and system‐level factors that influence outcomes. 19 Ongoing randomized controlled trials, including the Study of Whole Blood in Frontline Trauma (SWiFT), SWiFT‐Canada, and the Type O Whole Blood and Assessment of Age During Prehospital Resuscitation (TOWAR) trial, are expected to provide important evidence on clinical effectiveness, resource utilization, and operational feasibility. 59 , 60 , 61 Expanding research to include non‐traumatic causes of hemorrhage, such as obstetric and GI bleeding, will help determine the broader applicability of WB transfusion in prehospital care.

CONCLUSION

Prehospital WB transfusion is feasible and demonstrates a consistent safety profile across the included studies. Mortality outcomes were variable and generally similar to CT or crystalloid‐based resuscitation, while transfusion requirements were consistently lower and no increase in adverse events was reported. Although the certainty of evidence remains limited, the available data indicate that WB may be a practical option for early, balanced resuscitation in the prehospital setting when implemented within systems capable of appropriate supply, storage, and quality assurance.
