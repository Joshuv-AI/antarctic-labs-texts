Arterial thrombosis and cancer: insights from observations in myeloproliferative neoplasms and CHIP
Authors: Tiziano Barbui, Alessandro Maria Vannucchi, Ayalew Tefferi
Published: 2026

Cardiovascular diseases and cancer are the leading global causes of morbidity and mortality and are increasingly recognized as biologically interconnected rather than independent conditions. Growing epidemiological and mechanistic evidence supports a bidirectional relationship between thrombosis-related cardiovascular events and cancer development [1]. In particular, major arterial thrombosis (AT) has been associated with an increased risk of subsequent solid tumors, suggesting a shared pathogenic substrate rather than simple comorbidity [2]. Within this framework, atherosclerosis emerges as a central pathogenic hub. As a chronic inflammatory disease, it not only precipitates acute arterial events but also sustains a pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative systemic milieu that may favor carcinogenesis and tumor progression [2].

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) - including polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET) and myelofibrosis (MF) - and clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) constitute a unique clinical and biological model to explore this relationship [3]. In MPN, clonal hematopoiesis, chronic inflammation, and a high propensity for arterial and venous thrombosis coexist, thereby MPNs provide a valuable model to explore the interplay between thrombosis, inflammation, and cancer, suggesting that AT may represent not only a vascular complication but also a systemic marker of malignancy risk [4].

Thrombosis is the first manifestation of PV or ET in about 20% of patients, reflecting a prolonged preclinical phase. Thereafter, thrombotic incidence remains elevated at approximately 3.5% per patient-year, three- to four-fold higher than in the general population [5, 6]. The international nested case–control MPN-K study [7–9] carried out by European LeukemiaNet (ELN) centers, evaluated the association between thrombosis and second cancers (SC) in 647 MPN patients with malignancy and 1234 matched controls. Most SC were carcinomas (65.8%). Thrombosis before or at MPN diagnosis did not differ between groups, arguing against reverse causation. In contrast, thrombosis after diagnosis was more frequent in patients who developed cancer (11.6% vs 8.1%), driven by AT (6.2% vs 3.7%), while venous thrombosis showed no significant difference. Nearly one-third of thrombotic events preceding cancer occurred within a median of 48 months of diagnosis, suggesting AT may serve as a clinical signal of future malignancy. In multivariable analysis adjusting for confounders, AT remained independently associated with carcinoma (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.14–3.41), supporting a biologically plausible link through MPN-driven inflammation and clonal hematopoiesis (Fig. 1). In this issue of Blood Cancer Journal, the Spanish national MPN registry provides further evidence that thrombosis after diagnosis has major prognostic implications in ET and PV. Angona et al. [10] analyzed 5376 WHO-defined MPN patients (3074 ET; 2302 PV) diagnosed after 2000, assessing the impact of post-diagnosis thrombosis on survival, progression, and SC risk.

AT was significantly linked to shorter survival in both ET and PV patients. In multivariable analysis, AT was associated with an increased risk of death: in ET, HR 1.33 (95% CI: 1.05–1.68, p = 0.015) and in PV, HR 1.47 (95% CI: 1.12–1.92, p = 0.005). In contrast, venous thrombosis did not significantly affect survival in either group. The progression to MF was not associated with thrombosis in either ET or PV. AT, particularly in older patients with additional comorbidities, appeared to reflect more aggressive disease biology, with AT frequently affecting critical vascular areas such as the cerebral or coronary circulations. Regarding second cancer, AT was not linked to an increased risk of SC in ET. However, in PV, AT was associated with a significantly higher 10-year probability of SC (22.6% vs. 12.2%, p = 0.02). In multivariable analysis, AT showed a trend toward an increased risk for SC (HR 1.47, 95% CI: 0.99–2.2, p = 0.056). These findings underscore the importance of AT as a prognostic marker for survival and potentially for SC, highlighting the need for targeted prevention and management strategies in MPN.

Overall, these Spanish results confirm our previous data in PV. In 1508 ECLAP patients we examined inflammation and vascular events as predictors of mortality [11]. Using a generalized additive model, neutrophils-to-lymphocytes ratio (NLR) showed a progressive, nearly linear association with death, indicating a biologically graded effect. In multivariable Cox analysis NLR ≥ 5 independently predicted mortality beyond age, leukocytosis, and prior thrombosis. When modeled as time-dependent variables, AT during follow-up emerged as a major determinant of death, with an effect comparable to evolution to MF (Table 1). Taken together, these findings strengthen the concept that AT in PV represent an intermediate disease event that signals increased biological vulnerability, including mortality and a higher risk of SC, which may ultimately contribute to reduced survival.

Importantly, this association is not limited to MPN. In the general population, major arterial events, (myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, heart failure, and peripheral arterial disease), are consistently followed by an increased incidence of solid tumors. Although modest, this excess risk is reproducible and often emerges years after the vascular event, arguing against detection bias and suggesting persistent biological vulnerability, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and metabolic alterations [12]. CHIP (defined by age-related somatic mutations in hematopoietic genes, most commonly TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, EZH2, SRSF2, and JAK2, in individuals with normal blood counts and no overt hematologic malignancy) has emerged as a plausible biological link between vascular disease and cancer in the general population [13]. Its prevalence rises with age and is associated with increased mortality.

Beyond its association with future myeloid neoplasms, CHIP independently increases the risk of athero-thrombotic events, even after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Notably, even low-level mutations (<2% variant allele frequency) detected in individuals with AT have been linked to a higher incidence of SC. Although progression to overt hematologic malignancy remains uncommon, it appears more frequent among those with prior AT.

Mechanistically, CHIP provides a coherent explanation for persistent vascular inflammation. Clonally expanded myeloid cells exhibit dysregulated inflammatory signaling, enhanced cytokine production, and exaggerated responses to vascular injury. Experimental models show that CHIP-associated mutations amplify inflammatory pathways within atherosclerotic plaques, accelerating progression and destabilization. Clinically, CHIP-related inflammation may persist despite control of conventional risk factors, suggesting that clonal hematopoiesis represents an intrinsic and durable driver of vascular risk. According to Larsen et al., an increase in cancer, vascular risk and mortality can be observed even with a very low JAK2V617F mutation burden (1–2%), and NLR amplifies the mortality risk. Prospective studies should therefore assess NLR for monitoring clonal expansion [14].

CHIP also appears to influence cancer outcomes [15]. Large population studies indicate that the presence of CHIP before a cancer diagnosis is associated with worse overall and cancer-specific survival, implying that hematopoietic clonality may affect tumor progression, immune surveillance, or host–tumor interactions [13]. The observation that even small clonal expansions exert systemic effects supports the concept that CHIP contributes not only to thrombosis but also to a biological environment permissive for malignant transformation and progression, particularly in the setting of chronic inflammation.

Integrating CHIP into a cardio-oncology framework helps explain key epidemiologic findings: (i) excess cancer risk after AT in individuals without high cardio-metabolic burden; (ii) the temporal gap between vascular events and cancer diagnosis; and (iii) the central role of sustained inflammation rather than isolated exposures. Within this continuum, CHIP and myeloproliferative neoplasms may represent quantitative variations of a shared substrate. In this integrated model, AT emerges as a sentinel event marking convergence of inflammation, vascular injury, and clonal hematopoiesis, and identifying individuals at increased long-term cancer risk.

Therapeutic targeting of the permissive inflammatory state might be considered as a universal preventive measure and might also enhance the efficacy of other treatment modalities for both thrombosis and cancer.
