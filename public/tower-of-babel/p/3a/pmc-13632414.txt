Lifespan variation during mortality transition in Bangladesh: evidence from a local surveillance and global estimates
Authors: Ahbab Mohammad Fazle Rabbi, Mohammad Bellal Hossain, Lucia Zanotto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVariation in life expectancy has become a critical dimension in understanding inequality in human populations, complementing average longevity by capturing how unequally years of life are distributed across individuals. Reducing such variation, especially by preventing premature mortality, has emerged as a key priority in global health and demographic research. For low- and middle-income countries, monitoring lifespan variation alongside life expectancy enables the detection of persistent mortality inequalities and uneven survival gains that average indicators alone may fail to reveal. Given its ongoing demographic and epidemiological changes, Bangladesh offers a relevant setting for studying these patterns. This paper examines lifespan variation in Bangladesh during its mortality transition.MethodsWe used two different sources of mortality data from 1974 to 2019: the Matlab Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS), representing a long-standing rural demographic site, and the World Population Prospects 2024 (WPP), representing national-level trends. Comparing HDSS and WPP estimates allows us to assess how data resolution and national-level modeling affect the measurement of lifespan variation, which is critical for population health surveillance. We used multiple indicators of lifespan variation, including lifespan disparity, life table entropy, the Gini coefficient, standard deviation and interquartile range (IQR) of age-at-death distribution, to examine trends over time. We calculated 95% bootstrap confidence intervals to account for sampling variability and smoothing effects.ResultsThe study finds a consistent long-term decline in lifespan variation for both sexes across both datasets. Lifespan disparity declined from 19.6 to 13.8 years for Matlab males and from 20.9 to 12.4 years for Matlab females; in WPP, it dropped from 24.4 to 13.1 years for males and from 24.5 to 12.1 years for females. Similar downward trends are observed for life table entropy and Gini coefficient, with identifiable peaks during mortality crises, such as the 1974-75 famine and the 1991 cyclone. Since the early 2000s, the highest demographic variation has been observed among Matlab males, while WPP data show higher values for statistical measures in recent years- partly due to smoothing and national-level heterogeneity. Sex-specific differences and regional disparities persist, while bootstrapped confidence intervals indicate that these trends are robust despite smoothing and national-level aggregation in WPP data.ConclusionThis study provides empirical evidence on lifespan variation trends during Bangladesh’s mortality transition and illustrates the utility of high-resolution mortality data for monitoring inequality in LMIC populations.Keywords: Life expectancy, Lifespan variation, Mortality transition, Population health metrics, Health equity, Measurement bias, Bangladesh

Introduction

Human mortality has decreased globally over the past centuries [1, 2]. Between 1800 and 2001, the global average life expectancy rose by about 1.9 years per decade [3]. Despite some regional exceptions, modern healthcare systems and increased public awareness of health behaviors have been key factors driving this transition [4]. Therefore, studying improvements in mortality over the lifespan has become a crucial element in decision-making for social welfare and healthcare facilities [5]. The most commonly used metric to indicate a population’s mortality conditions is period life expectancy at birth. Denoted as e(0), this straightforward measure reflects the average number of years a newborn is projected to live based on the mortality conditions at a specific time. Changes in mortality are often summarized through trends in e(0), and the effectiveness of mortality models is assessed by their ability to align with historical trends in life expectancy [6, 7].

However, life expectancy does not reflect the variations in individual lifespans [8]. In fact, these differences can be significant both between individuals and across populations. To gain a comprehensive understanding of mortality changes in relation to higher lifespan, life expectancy can be supplemented with various measures of lifespan variation [4, 9, 10]. These different measures are valuable in this context as they capture the variations in mortality patterns throughout the lifespan rather than merely indicating expected lifetime at birth [7]. Additionally, the level and trends of these lifespan variation measures can shed light on how equitably health improvements are distributed within a society [11]. From a population health perspective, lifespan variation provides critical insight into how survival gains are distributed within a population, offering information that life expectancy alone cannot capture. Monitoring such variation is increasingly relevant for equity-focused health policy evaluation. This is particularly important in the context of the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 3 on ensuring healthy lives and well-being for all. Lifespan variation indicators offer a useful tool for tracking whether mortality improvements are equitably shared across age and sex groups [12]. Furthermore, the application of lifespan variation measures is also significant in mortality forecasting, as they can enhance forecast accuracy [12, 13].

A number of different indices exist to calculate lifespan variation (also documented as lifespan inequality), each with different underlying properties. Widely used measures of lifespan variation includes lifespan disparity [9], life table entropy [14], Gini coefficient [15], various statistical measures of dispersion on length of lifespan [6]. Vast literature exists on new invention or analysis of lifespan variation for the low-mortality populations [6, 9, 11, 15–19]. However, most of these methods are defined and applied mainly for the low-mortality countries. Many of these populations are noted for an almost steady decline in age-specific mortality rates over a long period [20]. Fewer applications of these methods exist for populations with higher mortality regimes or for those undergoing a mortality transition, as well as for those that have faced short-term mortality crises at different points in their lifespan or as a whole, alongside the ongoing mortality transition [21, 22]. The availability of quality mortality data remains a barrier to such research, and this problem is common in many developing and least-developed countries [20, 23]. Far less is known about how these measures behave in settings undergoing rapid mortality transition from relatively high to moderate mortality regimes. Theoretical expectations derived from low-mortality contexts, particularly regarding mortality compression and the relationship between life expectancy and lifespan variation, may not fully apply where early-age mortality decline, adult mortality shifts, and data limitations coexist [23]. Examining Bangladesh therefore provides an opportunity to assess whether established patterns hold in a transitioning South Asian context and to evaluate the empirical performance of different lifespan variation measures under such conditions.

This paper examines the levels and long-term trends of lifespan variation in Bangladesh within the context of its rapid and policy-driven mortality decline. Over the past five decades, Bangladesh has experienced substantial improvements in survival, particularly through dramatic reductions in early-age mortality and sustained gains in female life expectancy [24, 25]. This combination of pronounced early-age mortality compression and ongoing shifts toward adult and older-age mortality makes Bangladesh a particularly informative setting for evaluating how lifespan variation evolves during advanced but incomplete mortality transition. While these changes have led to marked increases in life expectancy at birth, far less is known about how the distribution of ages at death has evolved during this transition. In particular, it remains unclear whether mortality improvements have resulted in a compression of deaths around older ages or whether emerging heterogeneity at adult and older ages has altered patterns of lifespan variation. To address this gap, we apply five different measures of lifespan variation to evaluate changes in the dispersion of age at death over time. We explicitly examine differences between females and males to assess how sex-specific mortality dynamics have shaped lifespan variation. In addition, to assess robustness and potential data-related discrepancies, we compare estimates derived from two distinct sources: national-level mortality data from World Population Prospects and sub-national data from the Matlab Health and Demographic Surveillance System. Finally, we evaluate the relationship between lifespan variation and life expectancy at birth as a sensitivity and consistency assessment of these measures. By integrating sex-specific and data-source comparisons, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of how mortality transition has reshaped lifespan variation in Bangladesh.

Methods

The following subsection provides details on data used in this study and different measurement of lifespan variation computed.

Data

Detailed mortality data for Bangladesh from the vital registration system is not fully available or adequate for this research due to partial data. Although Bangladesh’s vital registration system provides fertility and mortality statistics, as well as abridged life tables with mortality rates, these are only available for the most recent years and not consistently over time. We considered two data set for this analysis as alternative sources: (i) The 2024 revision of the World Population Prospects (WPP), representing national level data, and (ii) Matlab Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) as regional level. For Bangladesh, WPP-2024 provides estimated life tables in single years which are constructed up to age 100 years [25]. It is important to note that the WPP life tables are model-based estimates derived from multiple data sources and demographic techniques designed to ensure internal consistency over time [25]. As part of this modelling framework, short-term mortality shocks (e.g., famine, epidemic outbreaks, or other abrupt crises) are smoothed in the estimation process. While this approach enhances cross-national and temporal comparability, it may attenuate temporary fluctuations in mortality that could influence measures sensitive to the age distribution of deaths. The other source of data came from the published life tables of Matlab Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) [24]. Matlab HDSS is recognized worldwide as one of the long-term demographic surveillance sites for a developing country. The Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) has maintained the registration of births, deaths, and migrations since 1966 in Matlab. Details about the HDSS may be found elsewhere [24]. Bangladesh became independent in 1971, so the life tables from 1974 to 2019 are considered for the current study (as this time period is available for both WPP and HDSS).

Measures of lifespan variation

The following subsections describe the measures of lifespan variation used in the current study, which are broadly classified into two groups: demographic measures and statistical measures. The demographic measures include lifespan disparity, life table entropy, and the Gini coefficient, all of which are derived from period life tables and are widely used in demographic research to capture different aspects of inequality in age at death [8, 9, 16, 31]. The statistical measures include the standard deviation of age at death and the interquartile range (IQR), which summarize dispersion in the empirical distribution of ages at death [6, 7]. We focus on these five selected measures because they jointly capture absolute and relative variation in age at death, reflect both early- and late-life mortality shifts characteristic of populations undergoing mortality transition, and remain robust when applied to low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts where vital registration is incomplete or age-specific mortality rates are irregular. Together, these measures are interpreted in a complementary manner, with differences across indicators reflecting distinct sensitivities to changes in different parts of the age-at-death distribution rather than a hierarchy of importance.

Demographic measures of lifespan variation

Lifespan disparity

The basic measurement we emphasize in this study is the lifespan disparity. Lifespan disparity illustrates the variation in the lifespan which is the differences in the length of life across the members of a population. To measure lifespan disparity, we utilize the definition of [9] and [10] where it is defined as the average number of life years lost at birth (as a result of death). Symbolically,

where ω is the maximum attainable age, e(x) is the remaining life expectancy at age x, d(x) is the distribution of death and l(x) is the number of people alive at age x (l(0) is the life table radix). Several other formulation is available for measuring lifespan disparity [9], however, the sensitivity analysis suggests that the difference is negligible between the outcomes of the methods [7].

Lifespan disparity measures the average life years lost due to death, which can also be interpreted as the average remaining life expectancy at the age of death. Because deaths occurring at younger ages contribute larger remaining life expectancies, this measure is highly sensitive to premature mortality. When deaths are concentrated at older ages, the difference between the expected and actual age at death decreases, leading to lower lifespan disparity [9, 10]. This measure is particularly useful in populations undergoing mortality transition, because declines in infant, child, and young-adult mortality substantially reduce life years lost and therefore strongly influence lifespan disparity. Its clear demographic interpretation in units of years also facilitates public-health interpretation and decomposition analysis for LMICs.

Entropy therefore reflects how age-specific mortality changes influence overall lifespan variability [8]. Because entropy is dimensionless, it allows meaningful comparisons across populations even with different mortality regimes and levels of life expectancy. The Gini coefficient measures inequality in the distribution of ages at death among individuals by summarizing the average absolute differences between ages at death relative to the mean age at death [15, 16]. Unlike measures that emphasize specific parts of the mortality distribution, the Gini coefficient captures inequality across the entire age-at-death distribution, allowing it to reflect both premature mortality and variability at older ages. The measure also satisfies several desirable properties of inequality indices, including scale invariance and population independence, which makes it particularly suitable for comparing mortality inequality across populations and over time.

Statistical measures of lifespan variation

There are several measures of lifespan variation that consider statistical measure of the length of lifespan, such as the Variance, Standard Deviation, Inter Quartile Range (IQR) and others [7]. We considered standard deviation and IQR in our study as both measures provide comparable values with other measures of lifespan variation. The standard deviation measures the overall statistical dispersion of ages at death around the mean age at death. Unlike demographic indices that emphasize premature mortality or relative variability, the standard deviation captures absolute variability across the full distribution of deaths. As a result, it reflects changes occurring both at younger ages and at older ages. Because it is derived directly from the distribution of deaths, the standard deviation provides a straightforward statistical interpretation of lifespan variability.

In this context, the IQR measures the spread of the central portion of the age-at-death distribution. By focusing on the middle 50% of deaths, the IQR reflects dispersion among typical ages at death rather than extreme values. Because the IQR is less influenced by extreme ages at death or irregular mortality patterns, it is considered a robust measure of lifespan variation, particularly when mortality data contain fluctuations or measurement noise. This property makes it useful when analyzing mortality data derived from surveillance systems or model-based estimates which is common for LMICs.

Bootstrap confidence intervals

To quantify the sampling variability in each lifespan variation measure, we implemented a non-parametric bootstrap procedure applied to the age-specific mortality schedules used for life-table construction. For each year, sex, and data source, bootstrap death counts were generated under a Poisson assumption using the observed mortality rates and corresponding population exposures. Bootstrap mortality rates were then obtained by dividing the resampled death counts by the fixed exposures. For each bootstrap replicate b = 1, … , B, a complete period life table was reconstructed and all lifespan variation measures were recalculated. These intervals reflect stochastic variability in the age-specific mortality rates and the resulting life-table quantities. We used bootstrap replicates for each year, sex, and data source. This choice ensures stable percentile-based confidence intervals, as the Monte Carlo error of bootstrap estimates decreases approximately in proportion to as the number of replicates increases [32].

Demographic measures of lifespan variation in Bangladesh

In this subsection, we summarize the demographic measures of lifespan variation for Bangladesh. All subsequent figures include 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for both HDSS and WPP, allowing for a formal comparison across sex and data source. Figure 3 presents the estimated lifespan disparity () by sex and data source for the period 1974–2019 with bootstrapped confidence intervals. In 1974, lifespan disparity in Matlab HDSS was 19.62 years for males and 20.97 years for females, declining to 13.78 and 12.41 years, respectively, by 2019. In WPP, the corresponding values were higher at the beginning of the period (24.37 years for males and 24.53 years for females) but decreased to 13.10 and 12.14 years, respectively, by 2019. From the 1990s onward, males consistently exibited higher lifespan disparity than females in both datasets. Early-period volatility is more pronounced in HDSS, with peaks observed in 1982 for males and 1983 for females. In contrast, WPP exhibits a smoother trajectory, with only a moderate elevation around 1990. Despite differences in early levels and short-term fluctuations, both data sources demonstrated a substantial long-term decline in lifespan disparity, consistent with mortality compression during the transition. Although the WPP series displayed smoother trajectories than HDSS due to model-based estimation, the associated 95% bootstrap confidence intervals are generally wider. This reflects differences in data sources and the scale of variability captured by the bootstrapping procedure: HDSS intervals represent sampling variability within the surveillance population, whereas WPP intervals capture variability in the model-derived life tables across time and age. Consequently, smoother temporal patterns in WPP do not imply narrower statistical uncertainty. This pattern is observed across all measures of lifespan variation.

Association between measures of lifespan variations with life expectancy

Tables 1 and 2 report Pearson correlations based on annual observations. A strong negative association between life expectancy at birth and all lifespan variation measures is confirmed in both datasets; however, the strength and consistency differ across indicators. Gini coefficient and life table entropy displayed the strongest and most stable correlations across sex and data sources. and standard deviation show slightly lower but still strong associations. In contrast, IQR exhibits weaker and less consistent correlations, particularly in HDSS (r = −0.88 for males). Inter-measure correlations further demonstrate structural similarity among demographic indicators, while IQR correlates less strongly with standard deviation and other measures. This suggests that demographic inequality measures capture a common underlying compression dynamic more consistently than purely statistical dispersion metrics. Bootstrapped mean differences in measures of lifespan variation by sex and data source, 1974-2019

To assess whether observed differences in lifespan disparity measures were statistically significant, we employed a nonparametric bootstrap procedure. For each year between 1974 and 2019, we computed differences in disparity measures for (i) females versus males within each data source and (ii) HDSS versus WPP within each sex. We then generated bootstrap samples by resampling years with replacement and recalculated the mean difference across the study period. The 95% confidence intervals were obtained using the percentile bootstrap method. Statistical significance was determined by whether the confidence interval excluded zero.

Figure 9 summarizes the period-averaged mean differences in lifespan variation measures; calculated as female minus male and HDSS minus WPP over 1974-2019 along with their bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals based on resampling years. These estimates summarize the average magnitude and bootstrap confidence intervals across the study period. Point estimates sometimes suggest marginally lower lifespan variation among females and in HDSS relative to WPP; however, no statistically significant differences were detected as all confidence intervals included zero. Although none of the intervals exclude zero at the 95% confidence level, the magnitude and variability of the descriptive differences vary considerably across measures. For the demographic measures of lifespan variation, point estimates descriptively indicate slightly lower lifespan disparity for females compared to males within HDSS, and modestly lower levels in HDSS compared with WPP for both sexes; however, these differences were not statistically significant. These differences were also not symmetric over time; in some years the gaps widened, while in others they narrowed or reversed, yielding period-averaged differences close to zero across 1974–2019.

The width of the confidence intervals differs markedly across measures, providing insight into their temporal stability. Life table Entropy and the Gini coefficient exhibit the narrowest intervals and the smallest mean differences in both sex and data-source comparisons, suggesting that these measures are relatively stable and less sensitive to short-term fluctuations in the age-at-death distribution, despite the absence of statistically significant group differences. In contrast, the standard deviation shows substantially wider intervals, particularly in the HDSS-WPP contrasts, indicating greater sensitivity to variation at older ages and to changes in the upper tail of the mortality distribution, rather than statistically supported differences between groups. Differences in lifespan disparity are also more variable than those for entropy and Gini, reflecting its responsiveness to shifts in premature mortality across a broad age range. The widest confidence intervals are observed for the interquartile range (IQR), both for female-male comparisons and for data-source contrasts. This pattern points to considerable year-to-year variability in the central spread of ages at death, implying that mid-distribution mortality fluctuations, rather than persistent statistically supported disparities, drive much of the observed variation in IQR.

Discussion

This study examined long-term trends in five measures of lifespan variation in Bangladesh during its mortality transition, comparing national-level estimates (WPP 2024) with sub-national surveillance data (Matlab HDSS) and assessing differences by sex. Across all measures, lifespan variation declined substantially over 1974–2019, consistent with sustained mortality improvement. While point estimates often suggested slightly lower inequality among females and marginally lower levels in HDSS relative to WPP, bootstrapped period-averaged mean differences revealed no statistically sustained differences by sex or data source, noting that the two datasets represent structurally different data-generating processes rather than directly comparable observational systems. The magnitude and temporal stability of differences varied across indices. Entropy and the Gini coefficient exhibited the narrowest confidence intervals and the smallest average contrasts, indicating greater stability and lower sensitivity to short-term mortality fluctuations. In contrast, lifespan disparity and particularly the standard deviation showed wider intervals, reflecting responsiveness to shifts in premature and older-age mortality. The interquartile range (IQR) demonstrated the greatest year-to-year variability, suggesting that mid-distribution mortality changes, rather than persistent structural inequality, account for much of its fluctuation. These findings suggest that the observed sex and data-source variations in the utilized measures of lifespan disparity primarily reflect temporal mortality dynamics rather than statistically supported systematic divergence in lifespan variation patterns. Moreover, these confidence intervals explicitly account for both random fluctuations and smoothing/model-based estimation, demonstrating that observed trends remain robust despite methodological differences between datasets.

Difference of lifespan disparity stayed within 0.02 to 3.14 years between males and females of Matlab HDSS. The highest peak in the trend of lifespan disparity is observed at 1982 for males and 1983 for females in Matlab HDSS. A drop in life expectancy was observed at that period in Matlab HDSS due to outbreak of diarrhea which increased infant and child mortality at that time [26]. Similarly, an increase in mortality is observed in WPP data in 1991 which caused a raise in lifespan disparity (and all other measures as well). The increase in 1991, which is also observed in all measures from the WPP, was caused by excess mortality due to the cyclone [34]. Research into the high death toll from Cyclone Gorky in 1991, which resulted in over 138,000 fatalities in coastal Bangladesh, highlighted the factors contributing to these deaths [35]. However, this effect is less pronounced in the Matlab HDSS area, as it is located far from the coastal region [24]. Lifespan disparity estimates from the WPP were often higher than those from Matlab HDSS for both sexes, although bootstrap analyses did not detect statistically significant period-averaged differences between data sources. The HDSS series also appeared visually more variable over time, whereas the WPP trends were comparatively smoother (Fig. 5). Matlab HDSS is known for involving local communities in health initiatives; this fosters trust and cooperation, enhancing the effectiveness of health programs which may cause less variation in lifespan [36]. However, data quality remains an issue, as these lower values of lifespan disparity still come from smoothed data of Matlab HDSS. On the other hand, the almost linear trend obtained from WPP may also come as a result of the estimation techniques used in WPP [25]. In addition, differences between the two sources should be interpreted in light of their distinct data-generating frameworks, rather than as direct measurement discrepancies alone.

Like many low-mortality countries [11, 12], the correlation coefficient also gives a strong negative correlation between life expectancy at birth and lifespan disparity for Bangladesh (for both sexes in both datasets). The values of correlation coefficients for Bangladesh are comparable to historical values observed for many low mortality countries, specially for females [11, 12, 17]. As previously noted, measures of lifespan variation are known to have a strong inverse relationship with life expectancy at birth, and this characteristic acts as a sensitivity analysis for these measures [7, 11]. Following the findings from low mortality countries, these findings for Bangladesh also suggest two things: (i) most of the raise in life expectancy is due to the reduction of disparities in lifespan, achieved by preventing premature mortality (for example, reducing infant and maternal mortality in Bangladesh), and (ii) efforts to reduce death rates among individuals who live longer than average have had minimal impact on levels of life disparity and have only modestly contributed to the raise in life expectancy [11].

The findings for life table entropy are complimentary measures to lifespan disparity [8]. Higher entropy reflects more variability in mortality (more variation in ages at death). Life table entropy provides additional insights for understanding changes in overall survival in a population; in particular, the changes in the entropy depend on the relative changes in both life expectancy lost due to death () and in life expectancy at birth. Findings from Bangladesh suggests almost similar pattern obtained for lifespan disparity (a decreasing trend for both sexes in both datasets) except for an earlier raise in 1975. The excess mortality during 1974-75 resulted in a significant increase in life table entropy, which later affected the Gini coefficient as well. This rise in mortality stemmed from the famine, caused by severe monsoon flooding and its subsequent effects [37]. Mortality rates were 62% higher during the famine (1974-75) and 31% higher during the post-famine period (1975-76) compared to the non-famine period (1978-79) [38]. Besides that, higher entropy is observed for males in both datasets than females since 1990 s and entropy was higher for males and females of WPP than Matlab HDSS until 2000; however, bootstrap analyses did not identify statistically significant sustained differences over the full study period. Such rapid fall till 1990 s indicate that the female population tends to experience death at more similar ages compared to the male population in the later part of mortality transition. Such transition may be explained by drop in child mortality in Bangladesh [36], however, age and cause-specific analysis may give us more insight about it. Similar finding were also obtained for multiple Latin American countries [33].

Application of Gini coefficient is very common in inequality research for different social and economical perspective [7, 16]. Besides the same seasonal jump observed in 1982 and 1983, another peak is observed in trend of Gini coefficient for both sexes during 1975 in Matlab HDSS. As mentioned before this raise is attributable to the excess mortality due to famine. It should be noted that this peak is observed for all demographic measures and IQR in Matlab HDSS but completely absent in measures using the WPP data. Such a trend also indicates the superiority of the Gini coefficient over the other index of lifespan variation in capturing temporal inequality in mortality trends. Beside this, the observed trend of Gini Coefficient indicated higher inequality at the national level compared to Matlab HDSS throughout the observation period; although these differences were not statistically significant in the bootstrap analysis. A better healthcare system in Matlab HDSS may be responsible for this trend which ensure such lower level of inequality in age-at-death distribution [23, 36]. However, data quality remains an issue for the Gini coefficient as these higher values of Gini coefficient still came from smoothed data for Matlab HDSS.

Previous studies mentioned that historical increases in life expectancy have been accompanied by equally striking decreases in the variance (or standard deviation) of adult age-at-death for industrialized countries [39]. Like the previous measures of lifespan variation, the observed trend in the standard deviation of the age-at-death distribution for Bangladesh also reflects the decline in mortality over time, as evidenced by the standard deviation between individuals at the same time, alongside the increase in period life expectancy (Figs. 2 and 7). Unlike the demographic measures of lifespan disparity, the WPP data show a higher trend in the standard deviation of the age-at-death distribution than the Matlab HDSS throughout the entire observation period, except for peaks for females in Matlab in 1983 and 1984. However, the bootstrap confidence intervals did not indicate statistically significant period-averaged differences between datasets. Previous study observed a nearly similar inverse relationship between subgroup variance and average from the cross-sectional pattern of the variance of the age-at-death distribution [6]. While overall variance has declined over time, very large differences in variance between subgroups may still persist [6]. Such a trend is observed in populations with higher heterogeneity in the distribution of death [40]. Less advantaged groups in the population experience both lower average length of life and higher variance [20, 40]. A further analysis comparing the variance/standard deviation of age-at-death within different strata of the population could be a topic for future research in Bangladesh as well. [11], which is also plausible during a mortality crisis or mortality transition [22]. It should be noted that there are different approaches to estimating the standard deviation of the age-at-death distribution. Previous study proposed measuring variance in adult ages at death by choosing a cut-off age , at which the probabilities of death are near their minimum and remain relatively stable over time beyond that age [6]. Another study also considered age 10 as the starting point for computing the standard deviation of death [7]. However, such approach is not suitable for Bangladesh, as infant mortality has been significantly reduced for both sexes during this mortality transition, along with maternal mortality [36].

Although a declining trend is observed in the IQR for both datasets in Bangladesh, the level and trajectory of the IQR contrast with those observed in low-mortality countries (Fig. 8). Previous studies have reported a relatively narrow range of IQR values in low-mortality populations, reflecting the typically left-skewed age distribution of deaths in such settings [40]. In such cases, the IQR provides insight into mortality variability in older adulthood. It should be noted that the first quartile gives insight into the presence of mortality in the earlier part of the lifespan, whereas the third quartile reflects senescence mortality. We observed a sharp rise in the third quartile due to a shift in mortality toward older ages, with a faster pace for females (Fig. 2) in both datasets for Bangladesh. The rise in the third quartile for women doesn’t suggest that older women are dying earlier than men. It suggests that, within the female population, a larger proportion is living to older ages, contributing to a shift in the distribution of lifespans. Women still tend to have higher life expectancy, but they might now be experiencing even more favorable survival rates as they age. In addition, the gap between males and females was high in Matlab HDSS initially for IQR, but this situation changed after female life expectancies became higher than those of the male population in Matlab HDSS. Such a transition was also observed in Mexico [21]. Unlike low-mortality populations, a weaker correlation between the IQR and life expectancy at birth (for both sexes) is observed in both datasets. As mentioned earlier, a within-group comparison considering different socio-economic strata may explain such a relationship. The IQR showed a stronger correlation compared to the standard deviation. To explain this pattern, previous research has suggested that the IQR is a relatively robust measure of lifespan variation and is less influenced by reductions in mortality at early ages, such as infant or child mortality [7].

The observed long-term decline in lifespan variation alongside rising life expectancy is broadly consistent with the theory of mortality compression, whereby reductions in premature mortality shift deaths toward older ages and narrow the dispersion of age at death [6, 19]. However, the Bangladeshi experience also illustrates that mortality transition in a lower- to middle-income context may involve periods of fluctuation driven by environmental shocks, infectious disease outbreaks, and uneven improvements across age groups. The stronger and more stable inverse association between life expectancy and demographic inequality measures (e.g., entropy and Gini) suggests that classical patterns documented in low-mortality Western populations are increasingly observable in Bangladesh, particularly among females. At the same time, persistent sex differences and variability in adult and older-age mortality indicate that mortality shifting; rather than pure compression, continues to characterize the later stages of transition. As Bangladesh undergoes rapid population aging, understanding whether survival gains are concentrated at older ages or remain driven by early-life improvements becomes critical for forecasting health care demand, pension systems, and long-term care planning [23]. These findings therefore contribute to mortality transition theory by demonstrating how established relationships between longevity and lifespan variation operate in a context of accelerated but incomplete epidemiological transition.

From a population health perspective, our obtained results underscore the importance of complementing life expectancy with measures of lifespan variation when monitoring progress toward equitable survival gains. While rising life expectancy in Bangladesh reflects substantial achievement, particularly aligned with SDG 3.2 through sustained reductions in child mortality, the decline in lifespan variation indicates that these gains have been broadly distributed rather than confined to specific age groups. However, the sensitivity of certain measures (notably the standard deviation and IQR) to smoothing and short-term mortality fluctuations highlights the need for careful metric selection in routine surveillance. Entropy and the Gini coefficient appear more stable under data constraints, suggesting their suitability for monitoring inequality in settings with evolving vital registration systems. In relation to SDG 3.8 and universal health coverage (UHC), sustained reductions in lifespan disparity imply expanding access to essential health services across the life course, although sex-specific mortality patterns observed in several measures may still warrant attention to gender-responsive health strategies, particularly in adult and older-age care. Incorporating lifespan variation metrics into national health monitoring frameworks could therefore strengthen equity-oriented evaluation, improve targeting of premature mortality, and support strategic planning in the context of demographic aging.

Strength and limitations

This study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report variation in lifespan across subgroups using population-level measures in Bangladesh, providing novel insight into mortality patterns. Second, it leverages two distinct datasets: Matlab HDSS and WPP; which allows assessment of lifespan variation at both regional and national levels. Bootstrapped comparisons indicate that source of data does not materially distort overall patterns, ensuring that the datasets provide complementary and reliable perspectives. Third, the comparison between sexes across data sources highlights structural and temporal differences in mortality. Finally, the consistency of entropy and Gini estimates across datasets, with relatively narrow confidence intervals and limited temporal instability, reinforces the robustness of the findings and supports the reliability of conclusions regarding sex differences and long-term trends in lifespan variation.

There are also several limitations. To address data limitations of Matlab HDSS, we applied a smoothing technique to facilitate consistent estimation of lifespan variation measures over the same age range. While this reduces random fluctuations, it also diminishes the natural variability of the data, lowering observed variance [12]. The HDSS mortality schedules were smoothed using a validated procedure, and sensitivity analyses presented in the Appendix indicate that the resulting life expectancy and age-at-death distributions are virtually unchanged within conventional precision thresholds. In contrast, WPP data are inherently smoothed due to the estimation methods used to construct the life tables. This smoothing reduces variance and produces highly regular, almost linear trends for all measures. Additionally, WPP data fail to capture abrupt historical events, such as the 1974-75 famine, which is evident in HDSS but not reflected in WPP trends. Secondly, each measure of lifespan variation captures a distinct aspect of inequality and has specific sensitivities. For example, standard deviation and IQR are more sensitive to fluctuations in the tails or central spread of the age-at-death distribution, while entropy and Gini coefficient provide more stable assessments of structural inequality. Users should be cautious when interpreting these measures in isolation, as point estimates may differ depending on the chosen metric and data source. Thirdly, the smoother trajectories in WPP should not be interpreted as reflecting higher statistical precision. Observed sex differences and temporal changes should be interpreted alongside bootstrapped confidence intervals and historical context. While the measures provide insight into population-level inequality trends, they do not identify causal drivers of mortality changes or the precise contribution of specific age groups to observed variation. Policymakers should use these metrics as complementary indicators rather than definitive predictors of health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study examined long-term trends in multiple measures of lifespan variation in Bangladesh during a period of sustained mortality transition. All measures indicate a substantial decline in lifespan variation alongside rising life expectancy, suggesting that survival gains have increasingly been shared across the population. Although short-term fluctuations reflect mortality shocks and data-related differences, no statistically significant differences by sex or data source were detected in period-averaged comparisons. From a population health measurement perspective, this study demonstrates that monitoring lifespan variation alongside life expectancy provides a more complete assessment of equity in survival gains during demographic transition. Among the measures examined, entropy and the Gini coefficient appear particularly stable under data constraints, suggesting their suitability for routine inequality monitoring in low- and middle-income countries. Future methodological development should focus on refining lifespan variation metrics for transitional mortality regimes, incorporating cause-specific and age-decomposed analyses, and improving measurement robustness in settings with evolving vital registration systems.
