Enzymatic activity screening in marine bacteria exposed to oxo-polyethylene in artificial marine microcosms
Authors: Watumesa Agustina Tan, Felicia Felicia, Lois Bunga Lestari, Adi Yulandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOxo-polyethylene (oxo-PE) is marketed as a biodegradable plastic, yet its environmental degradation remains poorly understood, particularly in marine contexts. This study aimed to screen for bacterial isolates with enzymatic potential relevant to oxo-PE breakdown using a simulated marine environment. Microbial communities were enriched in Winogradsky columns containing oxo-PE sheets, and isolates were screened for enzymes known to contribute to polymer breakdown.ResultsAfter 60 days of incubation, no significant weight loss was detected in oxo-PE sheets. Surface-level alterations, including cracks and roughness, were observed using field emission scanning electron microscopy. Biofilms formed on the polymer surface over time yielded numerous bacterial isolates, of which 25 exhibited one or more enzymatic activities associated with polymer degradation, including alkane monooxygenase, laccase, lipase, and manganese peroxidase. Among them, isolate D30065 (Priestia megaterium) demonstrated the most diverse enzyme profile, suggesting potential for oxidative enzyme activity relevant to polyethylene breakdown. However, further studies are needed to directly evaluate the ability of this isolate to degrade oxo-PE. These findings highlight early microbial responses to oxo-PE exposure and provide a foundation for future studies on biodegradable plastic-microbe interactions in marine systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07803-9.Keywords: Artificial marine environment, Biodegradable plastic, Oxo-polyethylene, Oxo-polyethylene-degrading enzymes

Introduction

Plastic pollution, particularly from single-use petroleum-based plastics, poses a growing threat to marine ecosystems. In 2022, global plastic production exceeded 360 million tons, with a substantial portion entering the environment due to limited recycling and landfill capacity [1, 2]. Oxo-biodegradable-polyethylene (oxo-PE), particularly in packaging applications, are forecast to expand over the coming decade as demand for sustainable materials rises globally [3]. Oxo-PE is made of conventional petroleum-based polyethylene supplemented with pro-oxidant additives, typically metal salts such as manganese, iron, or cobalt stearates. These additives catalyze oxidative chain scission when exposed to heat, light, and oxygen, thereby initiating fragmentation and promoting subsequent microbial degradation [4].

However, the actual environmental performance of oxo-PE remains controversial. Some studies have reported structural degradation and weight loss of oxo-PE in soil and aquatic environments [5, 6], while others have observed minimal changes even after prolonged exposure [7–10]. These inconsistencies are likely due to variations in environmental conditions, microbial communities, and the physicochemical properties of the plastic itself.

Oxo-PE decomposition is a complex process involving both abiotic oxidation and microbial activity. Pro-oxidant additives, such as metal salts, initiate free radical reactions that break down the polymer backbone, forming oxygen-containing functional groups [11]. These oxidized fragments become susceptible to microbial attack, which is facilitated by enzymes such as laccases, peroxidases, lipases, and alkane monooxygenases [12–15]. The efficiency of this process depends on microbial community composition and environmental conditions, which vary significantly across ecosystems [16, 17].

Most studies to date have focused on terrestrial microbes. For example, Pleurotus ostreatus has been shown to mineralize oxo-PE using laccase activity [18], while Rhodococcus rhodochrous can assimilate oxidized polyethylene fragments following artificial weathering [19]. In contrast, there is limited information about how marine microbial communities participate in oxo-PE degradation. Aside from a recent study showing growth of Dunaliella salina on oxo-degradable plastic [20], marine-specific microbial pathways remain poorly understood. This gap highlights the importance of characterizing potential degraders from marine systems and examining their enzymatic profiles under environmentally relevant conditions.

This study aimed to explore the enzymatic potential of marine bacteria for oxo-PE breakdown by using Winogradsky columns as artificial marine microcosms. These columns create physicochemical gradients that support diverse microbial populations under semi-controlled conditions [21, 22]. By screening for specific enzymes associated with polymer degradation, we identified candidate bacterial isolates that may contribute to understanding microbial responses to oxo-PE in marine environments and may inform future biodegradation strategies.

Methods

Sediment and seawater sampling

Seawater and sediment samples were collected on September 1st, 2023, from Ancol, North Jakarta, Indonesia (6°07′12.8″S, 106°50′51.2″E), approximately 20 m offshore. A 3 × 3 grid was established using aluminum poles and nylon ropes, and samples were taken from the four corner grids and one central grid. Seawater from all five points was collected using a vacuum hose and transferred into the same sterilized jerrycans, while sediment from each point was combined directly into a single plastic box. This on-site mixing created composite seawater and sediment samples representing the overall sampling area. Seawater temperature (34.9 °C) and pH (7.5) were measured at the time of collection to characterize the ambient conditions of the microbial community and to guide subsequent incubation conditions during culturing and enzymatic assays. Samples were transported in a cool box and used the same day to prepare Winogradsky columns.

Winogradsky column preparation

All equipment and materials were pre-sterilized in an autoclave. Eight Winogradsky columns were prepared in 2 L glass measuring cylinders. For each column, 500 g sediment was mixed thoroughly with 4 g CaSO4, 0.5 g CaCO3, 4 g K2HPO4, and 23 g hard-boiled egg yolk in a basin. Newspaper pieces (approximately 1.5 × 1.5 cm) were added incrementally until the sediment maintained its structure when clenched by hand. The mixture was compacted into measuring cylinders up to the 400 mL mark, ensuring no visible air cavities, and seawater was added to the 2 L mark.

Oxo-PE carrier bags marketed under the brand name Oxium were purchased from an e-commerce platform in Indonesia. These bags are labelled as containing pro-oxidant additives; however, the exact composition of the additives is proprietary and not disclosed by the manufacturer. These carrier bags were cut into 5 × 5 cm sheets, and then pre-sterilized under UV light for 15 min on each side. Five sheets were then placed using sterile tweezers on the surface of the water phase of each column. Columns were sealed and incubated on a shaded outdoor balcony under natural light and ambient temperatures (30–35 °C) for 15, 30, 45, or 60 days. Two replicate columns were prepared per time point.

Biofilm and remaining oxo-PE sampling

At the end of each incubation period, oxo-PE sheets were retrieved from two replicate columns using sterile tweezers and transferred to sterile petri dishes. The biofilm formed on the oxo-PE surface was sampled in a Class II Biosafety Cabinet. Oxo-PE sheets were gently rinsed with sterile 0.85% physiological saline thrice to remove loosely associated cells. While this step reduces the influence of free-living microbes, complete elimination cannot be guaranteed. The subsequent swabbing was therefore intended to recover primarily surface-attached microbial communities. Each oxo-PE sheet was soaked in 3 mL sterile 0.85% physiological saline in a sterile petri dish. Biofilm material was gently scraped from the surface of oxo-PE sheets using sterile collection swabs modified to have flat-head polypropylene tips (Bio-Seen, China), with nylon tips removed using sterile scissors prior to use. These non-absorbent, rigid tips allow for effective mechanical collection of surface-associated microbial material without introducing fibrous contaminants. Collected biofilm was then resuspended in 7 mL sterile 0.85% physiological saline in a screw-capped bottle and homogenized by vortexing. Biofilm material from both replicates was pooled to generate one composite microbial sample per time point, which was used for downstream enrichment and isolation.

The oxo-PE sheets, now relatively free of biofilm, were sterilized with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and reweighed. Representative oxo-PE sheets from D0, D30, and D60 were cut into 2 × 2 cm squares and subjected to field emission-scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) analysis at 5,000 magnifications at the Advanced Physics Imaging Laboratory, BRIN, Indonesia.

Isolation of bacteria from oxo-PE biofilms

Biofilm suspensions (1 mL) were enriched in 50 mL seawater complete (SWC) broth containing 0.1% (w/v) pulverized oxo-PE. Cultures were incubated for 7 days at 30–35 °C with agitation. Serial dilutions were plated on SWC agar, and distinct colonies were isolated and purified. To assess isolate ability to utilize oxo-PE under nutrient-limited conditions, purified isolates were subsequently streaked onto minimal salt medium (MSM) agar supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) pulverized oxo-PE as the sole carbon source and 3% (w/v) NaCl. The MSM agar composition per liter was as follows: 4.69 g Na₂HPO₄, 4.49 g KH₂PO₄, 1 g NH₄Cl, 1 g MgSO₄·7 H₂O, 0.05 g FeCl₃, 0.007 g CaCl₂·2 H₂O, and 20 g agar. Isolates showing growth on MSM–oxo-PE agar were retained for enzymatic screening.

Enzymatic screening

Isolates were screened for four enzymes implicated in polyethylene degradation. All assays were incubated at 30–35 °C, depending on each isolate’s original cultivation temperature. The relative proportion of enzymatic activity at each time point was calculated as a percentage by dividing the number of isolates testing positive for a specific enzyme by the total number of isolates recovered at that time point.

Alkane monooxygenase assay

Alkane monooxygenase activity was tested using MSM agar supplemented with n-hexadecane vapor as the carbon source [23]. To supply n-hexadecane vapor, 30 µL of n-hexadecane was pipetted into a Durham tube stuffed with sterile cotton and attached to the petri dish lid using paper tape. Plates were incubated for 2–3 days. Positive activity was indicated by colony growth on the supplemented media.

Laccase assay

Laccase activity was screened on Nutrient Agar supplemented with 0.01% guaiacol and 0.35 mM CuSO4 [24]. Plates were incubated for 2–3 days. A reddish-brown color change in the colonies indicated positive activity.

Lipase assay

Lipase activity was assessed using Rhodamine B Agar (0.5% peptone, 0.3% yeast extract, 1.5% agar, 0.01% rhodamine B, and 3% olive oil) [25]. Plates were incubated at 30 °C and 35 °C for 2 days. Positive isolates displayed orange fluorescence around colonies under UV light.

Manganese peroxidase assay

Manganese peroxidase activity was tested in glucose-peptone-magnesium (GPM; 1% glucose, 0.1% peptone, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, 0.1% K2HPO4) broth supplemented with 0.01% phenol red [26]. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C and 35 °C with 120 rpm agitation for 2 days. A yellow color change in the media indicated positive activity.

Molecular identification

Ten isolates with diverse enzyme profiles were selected for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. DNA was extracted and amplified using universal primers 63f and 1387r [27]. Sequences were identified via BLASTN and aligned in MEGA11 to construct a phylogenetic tree [28].

Results

Visual and structural changes in winogradsky columns and oxo-PE

During the 60-day incubation, the Winogradsky columns exhibited noticeable changes in both the seawater and sediment phases (Fig. 1A). At Day 0 (D0), the seawater appeared clear, and the sediment was dark brown. By Day 15 (D15), the seawater had turned reddish-purple, and the sediment surface began changing to reddish-brown. At this time, the oxo-PE sheets, which had initially floated on the water surface, were observed to have sunk and rested at the sediment–water interface. Between Day 30 and Day 60, the seawater transitioned to greenish and yellow-green tones, while the sediment developed reddish-purple and greenish surface patches. Similar color changes were observed in the biofilms accumulating on the oxo-PE sheets (Fig. 1B).

A Color changes in the Winogradsky columns; B Biofilm formation on oxo-PE surface; C Physical appearance of oxo-PE sheets after biofilm removal and washing; D FE-SEM images showing surface alterations on oxo-PE, including cracks and holes (indicated by arrows). Number following each sample code signify period of oxo-PE exposure in days

After washing, the oxo-PE sheets remained visually intact for all time points, with no fragmentation, discoloration, or shrinkage. Gravimetric measurements indicated minimal weight changes in oxo-PE sheets over time. Weight loss was measured at 0.30%, 1.01%, 0.61%, and 0.82% for D15, D30, D45, and D60, respectively. These differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Surface morphology analysis using FE-SEM revealed physical alterations on oxo-PE sheets collected at later time points (Fig. 1C). The Day 0 sample displayed a smooth surface, while Day 30 samples showed cracks. At Day 60, surface roughness and holes were observed.

Enzymatic activity in oxo-PE–associated bacterial isolates

A total of 152 bacterial isolates that could grow on minimal agar supplemented with pulverized oxo-PE as their sole carbon source were recovered from the columns. Among these, 25 isolates exhibited one or more tested enzyme activities: alkane monooxygenase, laccase, lipase, or manganese peroxidase (Table 1). Sixteen isolates showed activity for only one enzyme, eight exhibited activity for two enzymes, while one isolate (D30065) tested positive for three enzymes, including alkane monooxygenase, laccase, and manganese peroxidase.

Taxonomic identification and morphology of selected isolates

Ten isolates with diverse enzymatic profiles and colony morphologies were further observed microscopically and selected for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Colony morphologies and Gram staining results for these isolates are detailed in Supplementary Material 2. Most isolates were Gram-negative, while Bacillota isolates were Gram-positive and formed irregular or mucoid colonies.

Based on sequence similarity and phylogenetic analysis, the isolates were classified into three bacterial classes. Six belonged to Gammaproteobacteria (Vibrio alginolyticus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Gallaecimonas pentaromativorans, and Marinobacter nauticus), one to Alphaproteobacteria (Roseibium aggregatum), and three to Bacillota (Priestia flexa and Priestia megaterium) (Table 2; Supplementary Material 3).

Discussion

This study was designed as an exploratory screen of microbial colonization and enzymatic activity associated with oxo-PE in artificial marine microcosms. Winogradsky columns were used to simulate microbial succession in a controlled yet complex environment. The study focused on early surface-level interactions between environmental microbes and oxo-PE sheets over a 60-day incubation period. Rather than a quantitative analysis of degradation, the aim was to recover cultivable biofilm and oxo-PE surface-associated bacterial isolates for enzymatic screening.

The visual and structural changes observed in the Winogradsky columns reflected the establishment of microbial activity throughout the incubation period. The progressive shifts in color in both the seawater and sediment layers, along with the development of biofilm on oxo-PE surfaces, indicate that microbial communities responded to the environmental gradients present within the columns. Similar patterns of stratification have been reported in Winogradsky systems and are known to create redox and nutrient gradients that favor diverse microbial assemblages [29–31]. The biofilm formation observed on oxo-PE is consistent with previous reports showing that biofilms enhance microbial adhesion and enzymatic activity on plastic surfaces [32, 33].

Despite that, remaining oxo-PE sheets did not exhibit significant weight loss. This is consistent with reports showing that polyethylene degradation is a slow process that may require months to years of combined abiotic and biotic activity [11]. Abiotic factors such as temperature, oxygen availability, and light exposure initiate oxidation by introducing functional groups into the polymer backbone. These oxidized fragments then become more accessible to microbial enzymes [12, 32]. The surface cracks and rough textures detected by FE-SEM at Days 30 and 60, may have resulted from abiotic oxidation, early microbial colonization, or even sample handling artifacts. Similar microstructural alterations have been reported as early indicators of polymer oxidation and microbial colonization [9, 34]. While these were not accompanied by measurable degradation of polymer mass, they are consistent with the gradual onset of surface-level modification. FE-SEM provides useful visual evidence of surface alteration, but future studies should complement these observations with analytical methods such as Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), gel permeation chromatography (GPC), or metabolite profiling to detect chemical modifications or polymer breakdown.

Prior to weighing, sheets were cleaned from biofilm using a stepwise procedure involving soaking in sterile saline, mechanical swabbing, ethanol sterilization, and air drying, a protocol consistent with those used in previous plastic degradation studies [35, 36]. While this approach minimizes biomass interference, the complete removal of biofilm cannot be guaranteed. This challenge is widely acknowledged in the field, particularly in studies involving environmental microcosm.

Growth of isolates on minimal agar supplemented with oxo-PE confirmed their ability to persist under nutrient-limited conditions with oxo-PE as the sole carbon source. The recovery of isolates capable of producing enzymes associated with polymer degradation provides evidence of enzymatic potential within the microbial community. Alkane monooxygenase activity was the most frequently detected, aligning with its role in the initial oxidation of long-chain hydrocarbons [15]. The detection of manganese peroxidase and laccase activities, particularly at later time points, suggests that oxidative enzymes may participate in the modification of polymer fragments [12, 37, 38]. Lipase activity was present throughout the incubation period, consistent with its role in early biofilm maturation and hydrolysis of surface-bound compounds [39]. The temporal variation of enzyme activity profiles likely reflects microbial succession within the developing biofilms, as reported in previous studies of polyethylene degradation [40, 41]. The simultaneous presence of alkane monooxygenase and oxidative enzymes indicates potential for synergistic mechanisms, although no measurable polymer degradation was detected in this study. However, it remains unclear whether these enzymes were actively produced during colonization on the oxo-PE surfaces within the Winogradsky columns. Enzyme production observed during in vitro screening may not directly reflect microbial activity under environmental microcosm conditions, where gene expression can be tightly regulated by nutrient availability, stress responses, or substrate accessibility [42].

Molecular identification of selected isolates revealed representatives of the classes Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, and Bacillota, all of which include species known for hydrocarbon or plastic degradation. Several isolates identified here, including V. alginolyticus, K. pneumoniae, and M. nauticus, have been previously linked to polyethylene degradation under laboratory conditions [43–47]. P. megaterium and P. flexa, isolated at later time points, also align with earlier findings of Gram-positive species contributing to polymer degradation and biofilm formation [48–50]. The detection of these genera supports the suitability of the Winogradsky column system for enriching environmentally relevant bacteria from marine sediment and seawater [30].

Biofilm formation is considered a prerequisite for efficient polymer degradation because it enhances microbial attachment and enzyme localization [32, 51]. Several isolates identified in this study, such as V. alginolyticus, K. pneumoniae, and M. nauticus, have been reported to form robust biofilms on plastic surfaces [33, 51]. P. megaterium and P. flexa have also been documented as biofilm formers under various conditions [48, 49], indicating their potential contribution to biofilm-mediated degradation processes. Although G. pentaromativorans and R. aggregatum lack experimental evidence of biofilm formation, their presence alongside established biofilm producers suggests potential complementary or cooperative roles, as described in previous consortia-based degradation studies [52].

Among the isolates, P. megaterium (D30065) exhibited the most diverse enzymatic profile, suggesting potential versatility in targeting oxidation intermediates. The non-pathogenic nature of P. megaterium, coupled with its enzymatic capacity, supports its potential for biotechnological applications in plastic waste management. However, direct assessment of oxo-PE degradation by this isolate remains to be conducted. Further studies using this isolate under controlled conditions may help elucidate enzyme-specific contributions and enhance oxo-PE degradation efficiency.

Conclusion

This study screened marine bacterial isolates from Winogradsky columns for enzymatic activities associated with oxo-polyethylene degradation. Although no significant weight loss or chemical degradation products were detected during the 60-day incubation, biofilm formation, surface alterations on oxo-PE, and the detection of relevant enzymes may suggest microbial interaction with the plastic surface. Among the 25 enzyme-producing isolates, P. megaterium demonstrated the highest enzymatic potential, expressing alkane monooxygenase, laccase, and manganese peroxidase. These findings suggest that bacteria with enzymatic capabilities relevant to polymer breakdown are present and may contribute to degradation under appropriate conditions. However, it remains unclear whether such enzymes are actively expressed during colonization in marine microcosms. Longer incubation periods and complementary chemical analyses are needed to evaluate degradation efficacy and in situ microbial activity. This work supports continued investigation into microbial contributions to oxo-PE breakdown and highlights the utility of Winogradsky columns as a tool for enriching candidate degraders in marine systems.

Limitations

This study focused on early microbial responses to oxo-polyethylene exposure in Winogradsky columns over a 60-day incubation period. While surface-level changes and enzymatic activities were observed, longer incubation times may be needed to detect measurable degradation outcomes in this system. Abiotic controls were not included, as sterilizing complex environmental samples such as marine sediment poses substantial logistical and technical challenges and would likely disrupt the physicochemical gradients essential to the Winogradsky system. These factors limit the ability to isolate microbial contributions from natural oxidative processes. Alternatively, abiotic controls may be more feasible in evaluating the degradative potential of single isolates or defined microbial consortia under controlled laboratory conditions. Finally, gravimetric analysis and FE-SEM alone may be insufficient to detect degradation processes. Future studies should incorporate complementary chemical and molecular analyses, such as FTIR, GPC, or metabolite profiling, to better assess polymer modification and breakdown.
