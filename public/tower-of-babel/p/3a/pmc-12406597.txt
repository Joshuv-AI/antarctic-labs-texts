Optineurin deficiency disrupts phosphorylated tau proteostasis and clusterin expression in human neurons
Authors: Zachary M Augur, Garrett M Fogo, Mason R Arbery, Andrew M Stern, Courtney R Benoit, Yi-Chen Hsieh, Tracy L Young-Pearse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptineurin (OPTN) is an autophagy adaptor protein involved in selective autophagy, including aggrephagy and mitophagy. Pathogenic mutations in OPTN have also been linked to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, frontotemporal dementia, and glaucoma, supporting its role in the etiology of neurodegenerative diseases. Despite its established biological roles, knowledge about its potential contribution to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology and neuronal functioning is lacking. AD is characterized by the accumulation of extracellular amyloid-β plaques and intracellular phosphorylated tau (pTau) tangles, with dysfunction in the autophagy-lysosomal pathway exacerbating tau pathology and impairing proteostasis. To investigate the role of OPTN in neuronal proteostasis and AD, we utilized induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neuron (iN) and astrocyte (iA) models. Analyses revealed a significant negative correlation between OPTN and specific pTau epitopes in neurons, as well as a decrease in OPTN protein abundance in brain tissues of individuals with AD. Given these findings, we generated OPTN knockout (KO), heterozygous, and wildtype iNs and iAs using CRISPR/Cas9 editing of iPSCs in two genetic backgrounds. Loss of OPTN in iNs increased specific pTau proteoforms without substantially affecting autophagy processes or mitochondrial respiration. Despite no clear effect on mitochondrial function, several mitochondrial proteins, including OXCT1, were enriched in an unbiased analysis of the OPTN interactome in iNs, as well as proteins involved in intracellular trafficking. Proteomic analyses further identified intracellular clusterin, an AD risk gene, as significantly upregulated in OPTN KO iNs, suggesting OPTN may influence its intracellular processing. Our model system demonstrates modest roles for OPTN in certain neuronal biological processes and potential implications for AD pathogenesis. These findings also suggest that OPTN may exhibit functional redundancy with other autophagy adaptor proteins in human neurons, leading to relatively mild phenotypic changes with complete loss of OPTN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-025-02103-y.Keywords: Optineurin, Autophagy, Tau, Alzheimer’s disease, Clusterin

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive neurodegeneration, driven by the accumulation of phosphorylated tau (pTau) and amyloid-β (Aβ), as well as disruptions in the proteostasis network. Specifically, the autophagy-lysosomal pathway (ALP) is critical in regulating these pathological features of AD, where a dysfunctional ALP has been found to contribute to tau aggregation, impaired protein clearance, and neurodegeneration [4, 46, 49, 53]. Genetic studies have further highlighted the role of autophagy in AD, with several risk alleles associated with AD being linked to key regulators of protein clearance. Variants in genes such as BIN1, SORL1, PICALM, TMEM106B, and CLU, among others, have been implicated in endolysosomal trafficking and autophagy, suggesting that disruptions in these pathways may contribute to the accumulation of protein aggregates in AD [4].

While autophagy was initially defined as a bulk, nonselective process, it is now widely recognized that it can also be a selective degradative process directed by adaptor proteins that facilitate the localization of specific substrates to budding autophagosomes [69]. One important autophagy adaptor protein in neurodegeneration is optineurin (OPTN), which has been implicated in intracellular trafficking, mitophagy, aggrephagy, and xenophagy [8, 33, 64]. OPTN contains key domains, including an N-terminal coiled-coil region, a ubiquitin-binding domain for recognizing ubiquitinated substrate, and a C-terminal zinc finger domain involved in various cell processes. OPTN also interacts with ATG8 proteins (such as LC3 and GABARAPs) through its LC3-interacting region, facilitating the recruitment of ubiquitinated substrates to autophagic membranes for efficient degradation [56, 58]. The role of OPTN in mitophagy, the autophagic degradation of mitochondria, has been widely studied. The PINK1-PRKN pathway poly-ubiquitylates damaged mitochondria, which then recruits autophagy adaptor proteins including OPTN, CALCOCO2/NDP52, and SQSTM1/p62. This ubiquitylation recruits TBK1 kinase, which requires OPTN for complete activation and functioning within mitophagy. HeLa cells lacking both OPTN and CALCOCO2 mimic TBK1-deficient cells and are deficient in mitophagy [18].

Previous analyses of the OPTN interactome have further elucidated its association with additional key regulators of autophagy, NF-κB signaling, and intracellular trafficking, underscoring its multifaceted role in cellular homeostasis. In mouse primary cells, co-immunoprecipitation experiments identified Optn interactions with key modulators of NF-κB signaling and autophagy, including TNIP1, RNF31, and VCP [5]. Additionally, several motor proteins, such as DYNL1, were detected, further implicating OPTN in intracellular transport [5]. A separate experiment in HeLa cells demonstrated that OPTN is enriched at a 'mitochondria-autophagosome synapse’, where it interacts with mitophagy adaptors such as CALCOCO2 and autophagy regulators including ATG3, WIPI2, and members of the GABARAP family [17]. Furthermore, key mitochondrial outer membrane components—TOMM20, TOMM40, TOMM70A, and VDAC2—were identified as candidate interactors with OPTN [17]. Separate research in C2C12 mouse muscle cells revealed that Optn physically interacts with and facilitates the autophagic degradation of GSK3β, further broadening its functional scope in proteostasis [63]. Despite the rich interactome of OPTN, the interaction of OPTN with proteins in human neurons remains yet to be fully explored.

Both loss and gain of function mutations within OPTN have been linked to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), frontotemporal dementia (primarily FTD-ALS), and primary open-angle glaucoma [36, 39, 57]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests OPTN and its associated proteins may have a broader role in neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and Parkinson’s disease, via its regulation of autophagy and protein aggregate accumulation [30, 41]. However, a potential contribution of OPTN to AD-related pathology and neuronal functioning remains incompletely understood, particularly in human experimental systems. Recent studies in mouse and bacteria cells have shown that the depletion of Optn increases protein aggregate formation via suppression of selective autophagy, while Optn upregulation can induce an upregulation of total autophagic flux [55, 59, 70]. Another study suggested that Optn plays a protective role in maintaining tau homeostasis through autophagy-dependent pathways in mice [70]. The authors showed that overexpression of Optn enhanced the functionality of Tau-P301L-expressing HT22 cells and increased memory in Tau-P301L mice, while reducing overall p-Tau levels. Clinically, in ALS cases with OPTN mutations, tau-positive neuronal inclusions have been reported, further highlighting a potential mechanistic link between OPTN dysfunction and tau aggregation [3]. In similar studies, some individuals with OPTN mutations exhibited tau-positive astrocytic inclusions, raising the possibility that OPTN may play a functional role in other brain cell types, beyond neurons [50]. It has also been shown in vitro that OPTN selectively degrades soluble MAPT while SQSTM1 (p62) appeared to degrade insoluble mutant MAPT, emphasizing the specificity of autophagy adaptor proteins in tau proteostasis [71]. These findings suggest that neuronal OPTN is a critical regulator of tau turnover via aggrephagy and that its dysfunction may exacerbate tauopathies by impairing proteostasis and autophagic clearance.

In the present study, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to generate isogenic sets of wildtype (WT), heterozygous (HET), and knock-out (KO) OPTN cell lines in two genetic backgrounds. These iPSCs were differentiated into induced neurons (iNs) and astrocytes (iAs), allowing us to investigate the effects of OPTN loss on tau phosphorylation, autophagy-related processes, mitochondrial health and morphology, and proteome-wide alterations. We identify increased levels of specific forms of aggregated pTau as well as an increase in intracellular CLU following OPTN ablation in theseneurons. Our findings provide new insight into the role of OPTN in human neurons on tau homeostasis and its biological functionality in human neurons and astrocytes. However, even complete loss of OPTN protein did not induce dramatic alterations in the proteome or mitochondrial health of these neurons, supporting a role for functional redundancy of other factors. By elucidating the interplay between OPTN, its interactome, and other relevant biological processes, we can advance the overall understanding of this important autophagy adaptor protein.

Reagent and resource table

Sequential human brain extract preparation

Frozen angular gyrus tissue was thawed and meninges were peeled off and discarded. Aqueous tris-buffered saline (TBS) extracts were prepared according to the “soaking” method as previously described [20, 65], with some modifications. The tissue was chopped on a McIlwain tissue chopper set to 0.05 mm width, then soaked for 30 min in a 5 mL Eppendorf Protein LoBind tube with nutation at 4 °C in five volumes TBS (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) supplemented with 5 μg/mL leupeptin, 5 μg/mL aprotinin, 2 μg/mL pepstatin, 120 μg/mL 4-benzenesulfonyl fluoride hydrochloride, and 5 mM NaF. The suspension was centrifuged at 2000 g in a swinging-bucket rotor for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were then centrifuged in 1.5 mL LoBind tubes at 20,000 g for 110 min at 4 °C in a tabletop centrifuge. The supernatants were pooled and re-aliquoted and frozen at −80 °C as the TBS soluble fraction for further analysis. The pellets were frozen at −80 °C for subsequent extraction.

To create the sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and urea extracts, a pellet from the TBS extract was thawed on ice and reweighed. The pellet was resuspended in five volumes TBS with 2% SDS. The samples were sonicated at 35% power three times for 10 s. They were then centrifuged again at 20,000 g for 110 min at 4 °C in a tabletop centrifuge as above. The supernatants were re-aliquoted and frozen at −80 °C as the SDS extract. The pellets were resuspended in another 5 volumes of 8 M urea followed by sonication, centrifugation, and aliquoting as was performed for the SDS extracts [32].

Cell culture

Induced pluripotent stem cell lines

Human brain analyses and human iPSC work were performed following IRB review and approval through MGB/BWH IRB (2016P000867 and 2015P001676). iPSCs were generated from cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from autopsied participants from the ROS and MAP cohorts. iPSCs were generated using Sendai reprogramming method [24]. iPSCs undergo a rigorous quality procedure that includes a sterility check, mycoplasma testing, karyotyping, and pluripotency assays performed by the New York Stem Cell Foundation (NYSCF) [24]. iPSCs were maintained using StemFlex Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma using a PCR kit (MP0035-1KT) and STR profiling to prevent potential contamination or alteration to the cell lines [24, 32].

CRISPR/Cas9 editing to generate OPTN WT, HET, and KO iPSCs

The following lines were chosen for CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis of OPTN: BR33 and BR24, two iPSC lines derived from non-cognitively impaired individuals (male and female, respectively) from the ROSMAP cohorts [24]. gRNA ‘AGATTGAAACCCAGACAGAG’ was used to target exon 8 within the OPTN sequence, which is conserved within relevant OPTN transcript isoforms. The Zhang Lab CRISPR Design website (crispr.mit.edu) was used to generate guide RNAs (gRNAs) with minimal off-target effects. gRNAs were cloned into the pXPR-003 vector. iPSCs were electroporated with the sgRNA-encoding plasmid and Cas9, cells were isolated for monoclonal selection and Sanger sequenced to determine OPTN mutations. For each targeted iPSC line, one unedited/wildtype clone and two mutant clones (HET and KO) were chosen for downstream analyses. IDT CRISPR Cas9 guide RNA design checker was used to check for any potential off-target effects [32].

Differentiation of iPSCs to induced neurons (iNs)

iPSC-derived neurons (iNs) were differentiated following a previously published paper with minor modifications [24, 44, 76]. iPSCs were plated at a density of 95k cells/cm2 on plates coated with growth factor reduced Matrigel one day prior to virus transduction (Corning #354230). Then, iPSCs were transduced with two lentiviruses—pTet-O-NGN2-puro (Addgene plasmid #52047, a gift from Marius Wernig) and FUdeltaGW-rtTA (Addgene plasmid #19780, a gift from Konrad Hochedlinger). The cells were then replated at 200k cells/cm2 using StemFlex Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and ROCK inhibitor (10 mM at day 0). The media was changed to KSR media (day 1), 1:1 of KSR and N2B media (day 2) and N2B media (day 3). On day 4, cells were dissociated using accutase and plated at 50k cells/cm2 using iN D4 media (NBM media + 1:50 B27 + BDNF, GDNF, CNTF (10 ng/mL, Peprotech)). Doxycycline (2 μg/mL, Sigma) was added from day 1 to the end of the differentiation, and puromycin (5 mg/mL, Gibco) was added from day 2 to the end of the differentiation. On day 3, B27 supplement (1:100, Life Technologies) was added. From day 4 to the end of the differentiation, cells were cultured in iN day 4 media and fed every 2–3 days.

Induced neuron protocol media:

KSR media: Knockout DMEM, 15% KOSR, 1× MEM-NEAA, 55 mM beta-mercaptoethanol, 1× GlutaMAX (Life Technologies).

KSR media: Knockout DMEM, 15% KOSR, 1× MEM-NEAA, 55 mM beta-mercaptoethanol, 1× GlutaMAX (Life Technologies).

N2B media: DMEM/F12, 1× GlutaMAX (Life Technologies), 1× N2 supplement B (Stemcell Technologies), 0.3% dextrose (D-( +)-glucose, Sigma).

N2B media: DMEM/F12, 1× GlutaMAX (Life Technologies), 1× N2 supplement B (Stemcell Technologies), 0.3% dextrose (D-( +)-glucose, Sigma).

NBM media: Neurobasal medium, 0.5× MEM-NEAA, 1× GlutaMAX (Life Technologies), 0.3% dextrose (D-( +)-glucose, Sigma).

NBM media: Neurobasal medium, 0.5× MEM-NEAA, 1× GlutaMAX (Life Technologies), 0.3% dextrose (D-( +)-glucose, Sigma).

Differentiation of iPSCs to induced astrocytes (iAs)

iPSC-derived astrocytes (iAs) were differentiated following previously published papers with minor modifications [6, 24, 26, 27]. iPSCs were plated at 95k cells/cm2 on growth factor reduced Matrigel (Corning #354230) coated plates prior to virus transduction. Then, iPSCs were transduced with three lentiviruses—Tet-O-SOX9-puro (Addgene plasmid #117269), Tet-O-NFIB-hygro (Addgene plasmid #117271), and FUdeltaGW-rtTA (Addgene plasmid #19780). The cells were then replated at 200k cells/cm2 using StemFlex Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and ROCK inhibitor (10 mM at D0). The media was changed daily with Expansion Media (EM) from days 1 to 3, and gradually switched from EM to FGF media from day 4 to 7. On day 8, cells were dissociated using Accutase, and plated at 84k cells/cm2 using FGF media. Doxycycline (2.5 μg/mL, Sigma) was added from day 1 to the end of the differentiation, puromycin (1.25 mg/mL, Gibco) was added on days 3 and 4 of the differentiation, and hygromycin (100 mg/mL, InvivoGen) was added from days 4 to 6 of the differentiation. From day 8 to the end of differentiation day 21, cells were cultured with maturation media and fed every 2–3 days.

Western blotting (WB)

Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific #89900) with the protease inhibitor (Complete TM mini protease inhibitor, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor (phosphoSTOP, Roche) added freshly before the lysis. Cells were lysed for 30 min on ice before transferring lysates to microcentrifuge tubes. Cell debris was pelleted by centrifugation (15,000 g) for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatant (cell lysate) was collected and stored at −20 °C until use. Protein concentration in cell lysate samples was determined with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (ThermoFisher, #23225). Cell lysates were prepared with 4X LI-COR loading buffer (Fisher Scientific, #NC9779096) and 2.5% β-mercaptoethanol, centrifuged, and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were resolved using Novex NuPAGE™ 4–12% Bis–Tris gels (ThermoFisher, #NP0336BOX) and NuPAGE™ 1X MOPS-SDS or MES-SDS running buffer (ThermoFisher, #NP0001). Gel electrophoresis was run at 200 V for 50 min. SeeBlue Plus2 (ThermoFisher, #LC5925) pre-stained protein standard was used for evaluation of molecular weight. The gel was extracted and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane by incubation with 20% methanol tris–glycine transfer buffer at 400 mA for two hours. The transferred blot was blocked with Odyssey blocking buffer (LI-COR, #927-50100) for 1 h at room temperature with agitation and incubated with primary antibody (diluted in blocking buffer) overnight at 4 °C with agitation. Blots were washed twice (10 min per wash) with TBST. Blots were then incubated with LI-COR secondary antibody diluted 1:10,000 in TBST for 1 h at room temperature with agitation. Blots were washed twice (10 min per wash) with TBST and stored in 1X TBS until imaging. Blots were imaged on a LI-COR Odyssey machine and quantified using ImageStudio software.

OPA1 WB

The WB procedure for OPA1 was largely the same as described above, with minor changes. Samples were run on 7% tris–acetate gels in 1X Tris Acetate SDS Running Buffer (Novex) for 1 h at 150 V. Five predominant bands of OPA1 are visible, the top two bands consist of splice isoform long OPA1 (L-OPA1), while the bottom three bands consist of proteolytically processed short OPA1 (S-OPA1). The abundance of L-OPA1 and S-OPA1 was quantified by densitometry in ImageJ. The abundance of S-OPA1 was compared to total Opa1 (T-OPA1) abundance to determine the S-OPA1/T-OPA1 percentage.

Chloroquine LC3B-II accumulation autophagy measurements

On Day 20 of iN or iA differentiation, neurons were fed with media containing 40 µM chloroquine (iNs) or 80 µM chloroquine (iAs) for 24 h along with vehicle control (water). After 24 h, samples were harvested, and WBs were run as described above. LC3B has two bands, LC3B-I (top band) and LC3B-II (bottom band). Autophagic activity was calculated by subtracting the amount of LC3B-II in the vehicle treated controls from the quantified amount of LC3B-II in the chloroquine treated cells, after respective normalization to GAPDH protein levels. In separate experiments, chloroquine-treated cells were incubated with 0.3% saponin (Millipore Sigma, SAE0073) in DPBS for 10 min at 37⁰C prior to lysis [11]. Cells were then gently washed twice with DPBS and lysed as described above in RIPA buffer. WBs were performed as above for these samples and LC3B-II (bottom band only) was quantified and normalized to Beta-III-Tubulin levels.

Immunocytochemistry

On Day 21, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma) for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were blocked in either 2% donkey serum or 4% goat serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) and 0.3% Triton-X-100 (Sigma) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature with agitation. Primary antibodies were diluted in either fresh donkey or goat serum blocking buffer and cells were incubated with primary antibody solution overnight at 4 °C. Then, cells were washed with PBS three times, incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature with agitation, then washed with PBS three times. Cells were treated with DAPI stain (1:1000 dilution) for 10 min at room temperature with agitation, followed by a final wash to prepare for imaging. Images were taken on a Zeiss LSM710 Confocal, Andor Dragonfly 600 Spinning Disk Confocal, or GE INCELL Analyzer 2000.

Mitochondrial morphology analysis

Z-stack images of mitochondria were processed in FIJI. Local contrast was enhanced using CLAHE algorithm. Background signal was subtracted using a rolling ball radius of 5 pixels. A median filter with radius of 1 pixel was applied. Mitochondrial signal was segmented using trainable Weka segmentation [2]. Segmented images were converted to binary and 2D objects smaller than 0.15 µm were filtered out. 3D mitochondrial objects were connected using the connected components labeling plug-in [29]. Measurements of individual 3D mitochondrial objects were taken using 3D Object Manager [48]. 6 z-stacks were evaluated for each technical replicate of each differentiation. 3D meshes were exported and visualized in MeshLab.

Live cell imaging

iAs were grown on glass 24 well plates at a density of 100k cells/well. iAs were differentiated normally and on day 21, cells were washed once with DPBS and incubated in warm HBSS with 40 nM MitoTracker Green FM (ThermoFisher Scientific M7514) and 20 nM of TMRM (Image-iT, ThermoFisher Scientific, I34361) for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were washed once with DPBS and incubated in warm HBSS during imaging. Micrographs of MitoTracker Green and TMRM were acquired under a 20× objective on a GE INCELL Analyzer 2000. 12 micrographs were captured per well and were analyzed using FIJI. Mean intensity fluorescent values were calculated for TMRM and MitoTracker Green channels, respectively. The ratio of TMRM/MitoTracker Green intensity was used as a relative measure of mitochondrial membrane potential.

DQ Red BSA lysosomal degradation assay

A 1 mg/mL stock solution of DQ Red BSA (Invitrogen, D12051) was prepared in PBS. iNs were grown on 96 well plates at a density of 15k cells/well. iNs were differentiated normally and on day 21, were washed once with DPBS and exposed to 1 μg/mL of DQ-BSA diluted from the stock solution. DQ-BSA was added and incubated at 37 °C while protected from light. After 5 h of incubation, DQ-BSA treated cells were washed two times with fresh PBS and fresh iN media was added. Immediately after fresh media was added, cells were monitored every 1 h using an Incucyte live cell imager (Sartorius). Images were filtered in a blinded manner for oversaturation of DQ-BSA signal using the Incucyte. The amount of DQ-BSA signal after 2 h of chase was normalized to cell area using the light microscope on the Incucyte.

MitoTracker Deep Red

MitoTracker Deep Red was used as described in the user guide. Briefly, iNs were plated at 10k cells per 96-well. On day 21, the iNs were incubated with 300 nM MitoTracker Deep Red and 1:1000 Hoescht diluted in iN cell culture media for 30 min. Cells were then carefully washed and fixed in 100% methanol for 15 min at −20 °C. Fixed cells were imaged using a GE INCELL Analyzer 2000. 9 images per well were taken and averaged to get a per-well value of MitoTracker intensity per nuclei count.

Seahorse

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

ECAR was measured in iAs by Agilent Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer using the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (#103020-100) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, iAs were grown on Agilent Seahorse 96 well plates at a seeding density of 20k cells per well. iAs were differentiated normally and on day 21, iAs were incubated in XF DMEM Media + 2 mM glutamine for 1 h at 37 °C in a CO2-free incubator. The Seahorse cartridge was loaded with 20 µL of glucose to a final concentration of 10 mM, along with final spike concentrations of 5 µM oligomycin and 20 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG). At the conclusion of the assay, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors and protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay. Baseline ECAR values derived from the Glycolysis Stress Test were normalized to protein content determined by BCA.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

OCR was measured in iNs by Agilent Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (#103015-100) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, iNs were grown on Agilent Seahorse 96 well plates at a seeding density of 60k cells per well. iNs were differentiated normally and on day 21, iNs were incubated in XF DMEM Media + 1 mM pyruvate + 2 mM glutamine + 10 mM glucose for 1 h at 37 °C in a CO2-free incubator. The Seahorse cartridge was loaded with 20 µL of oligomycin in port A for a final spike concentration of 2 µM. Port B was loaded with 22 µL of FCCP for a final spike concentration of 0.75 µM. Port C was loaded with 25 µL of rotenone and antimycin for a final concentration of 0.5 µM. At the conclusion of the assay, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors and protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. OCR values derived from the Mito Stress Test were normalized to protein content determined by BCA.

Intracellular and extracellular lactate quantification

iAs were plated on 24-well plates at a density of 150k cells per well and differentiated normally. iA media was collected on day 21, 48 h after the last full media change. Media was centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet any floating cells or debris. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation. iAs were washed with DPBS once and lysed in RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors and protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Lysates were diluted 1:50 in DPBS and media samples were diluted 1:25 in DPBS. Lactate content in samples was determined with the Lactate-Glo Assay (Promega Cat# J5021) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase signal was quantified by Infinite M Plex plate reader (Tecan) after a 1 h incubation of samples with luciferase reagents. Extracellular (media) and intracellular (lysate) lactate concentrations were normalized to protein content determined by BCA assay.

ELISAs

48 h conditioned media was collected immediately prior to harvest. Extracellular proteins were measured following manufacturer’s instructions for the Aβ Peptide Panel 1 (6E10) Kit, V-PLEX Human IL-6 Assay, and the U-PLEX Human CLU Assay. Samples were normalized to total protein concentration as determined by the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (ThermoFisher, #23225).

TMT proteomic and data analysis

Sample processing

iNs were plated in 6 well plates (1 million cells per well) and differentiated to day 21. Media was removed from the wells and iNs were washed two times with ice-cold DPBS. Samples were processed as previously described [7, 26]. Cells were lysed in 250 μL of urea lysis buffer (8 M urea, 100 mM NaHPO4, pH 8.5) with the protease inhibitor (Complete TM mini protease inhibitor, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor (phosphoSTOP, Roche) added freshly before the lysis. All homogenization was performed using a Bullet Blender (Next Advance) according to manufacturer protocols. Briefly, each tissue piece was added to urea lysis buffer in a 1.5 mL Rino tube (Next Advance) harboring 750 mg stainless steel beads (0.9–2 mm in diameter) and blended twice for 5 min intervals at 4 °C. Protein supernatants were transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and sonicated (Sonic Dismembrator, Fisher Scientific) 3 times for 5 s with 15 s intervals of rest at 30% amplitude to disrupt nucleic acids and subsequently vortexed. Protein concentration was determined with BCA assay, and samples were frozen in aliquots at −80 °C. Protein homogenates (50 μg) were treated with 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by 5 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) at 25 °C for 30 min in the dark. The protein mixture was digested overnight with 1:100 (w/w) lysyl endopeptidase (Wako) at room temperature. The samples were then diluted with 50 mM NH4HCO3 to a final concentration of less than 2 M urea and then further digested overnight with 1:50 (w/w) trypsin (Promega) at 25 °C. Resulting peptides were desalted with a Sep-Pak C18 column (Waters) and dried under vacuum.

Tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling

Peptides were reconstituted in 100 μL of 100 mM triethyl ammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) and labeling was performed using TMTPro isobaric tags (Thermofisher Scientific, A44520) as previously described [19, 51]. Briefly, the TMT labeling reagents were equilibrated to room temperature, and anhydrous ACN (200 mL) was added to each reagent channel. Each channel was gently vortexed for 5 min, and then 20 mL from each TMT channel was transferred to the peptide solutions and allowed to incubate for 1 h at room temperature. The reaction was quenched with 5% (v/v) hydroxylamine (5 mL) (Pierce). All 16 channels were then combined and dried by SpeedVac (LabConco) to approximately 100 mL and diluted with 1 mL of 0.1% (v/v) TFA, then acidified to a final concentration of 1% (v/v) FA and 0.1% (v/v) TFA. Peptides were desalted with a 60 mg HLB plate (Waters). The eluates were then dried to completeness. High pH fractionation was performed essentially as described with slight modification [52]. Dried samples were re-suspended in high pH loading buffer (0.07% v/v NH4OH, 0.045% v/v FA, 2% v/v ACN) and loaded onto a Water’s BEH (2.1 mm 3 150 mm with 1.7 mm beads). A Thermo Vanquish UPLC system was used to carry out the fractionation. Solvent A consisted of 0.0175% (v/v) NH4OH, 0.01125% (v/v) FA, and 2% (v/v) ACN; solvent B consisted of 0.0175% (v/v) NH4OH, 0.01125% (v/v) FA, and 90% (v/v) ACN. The sample elution was performed over a 25 min gradient with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min with a gradient from 0 to 50% of B. A total of 96 individual equal volume fractions were collected across the gradient and dried to completeness using a vacuum centrifugation.

Data processing protocol

All raw files were searched using Thermo’s Proteome Discoverer suite (version 2.4.1.15) with Sequest HT. The spectra were searched against a human Uniprot database downloaded August 2020 (86,395 target sequences). Search parameters included 10 ppm precursor mass window, 0.05 Da product mass window, dynamic modifications methionine (+15.995 Da), deamidated asparagine and glutamine (+0.984 Da), phosphorylated serine, threonine, and tyrosine (+79.966 Da), and static modifications for carbamidomethyl cysteines (+57.021 Da) and N-terminal and Lysine-tagged TMT (+304.207 Da). Percolator was used to filter PSMs to 0.1%. Peptides were grouped using strict parsimony and only razor and unique peptides were used for protein level quantitation. Reporter ions were quantified from MS2 scans using an integration tolerance of 20 ppm with the most confident centroid setting. Abundance data were log2 transformed and batch effects were regressed using ComBat [28]. Differential expression was calculated using the DEP package in R [75].

Gene set variation analyses (GSVA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

BR107 iNs were differentiated to day 20 and treated for 24 h with 40 µM chloroquine. iNs were then harvested and processed for TMT-MS proteomics, as described above. The top upregulated (q < 0.05) and top 100 downregulated (q < 0.05) proteins with autophagy inhibition were used as the “autophagy repressed” and “autophagy activated” target gene sets, respectively. These gene sets were applied by GSEA (UCSD and Broad Institute) across the proteomic profiles of 53 ROSMAP iNs and correlated to the OPTN protein abundance in each line [43, 66].

Immunoprecipitation followed by DIA mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

iN were lysed in 0.1% CHAPSO buffer and assessed for protein concentration using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (ThermoFisher, #23225). Lysates were pre-cleared with Protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen, #10003D). Primary antibody was cross-linked to Protein G Dynabeads with BS3 (ThermoFisher, #21580). 5 µg of OPTN primary antibody was used for immunoprecipitation. Immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating pre-cleared lysates with the cross-linked antibody-Dynabead complex overnight at 4 °C. For immunoprecipitation followed by WB (to validate specificity of antibody), beads were washed 3 times with CHAPSO lysis buffer before eluting protein with 5% β-mercaptoethanol and loading sample onto a Novex NuPAGE 4–12% Bis–Tris gel (ThermoFisher, #NP0336BOX). For immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry, beads were washed with CHAPSO lysis buffer and frozen at -80 °C. Samples were sent to the Emory Integrated Proteomics Core (Emory University) for peptide sequencing and identification. Enrichment analysis was performed with Perseus according to guidance from the Emory Integrated Proteomics Core.

IP-MS analysis

Significantly enriched proteins identified by IP in OPTN WT versus KO iNs were analyzed by gene ontology (GO) analysis. PANTHER overrepresentation tests (Fisher’s exact) were performed for GO biological process, GO cellular compartment, and GO molecular function [13, 68]. GO terms that passed FDR (q-value) <0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Data visualization

Schematics were generated using Biorender. Graphs and heat maps were generated using R Studio or GraphPad Prism 10.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Information regarding statistical analyses can be found in the figure legends. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 or R Studio. All data is shown as mean ± SEM. Generally, comparisons between two groups used paired student’s t test, and comparisons between more than two groups were analyzed using paired one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s post-hoc test. Pearson and Spearman correlation methods (linear relationship) were used for correlation analyses.

Results

OPTN protein levels are reduced in AD brain and significantly correlate with AD relevant phosphorylated tau abundance in neurons

To probe OPTN protein levels in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), angular gyrus brain tissue from a cohort of 30 individuals from the Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Projects (ROS and MAP) were homogenized using sequential extraction in tris-buffered saline (TBS), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and lastly, urea. OPTN protein was assessed using western blot (WB). We compared OPTN abundance across two categories of individuals: non-cognitively impaired (NCI) and AD. NCI individuals had neither a clinical nor neuropathological diagnosis of AD while AD individuals had both a neuropathological and clinical diagnosis (Supplemental Table S1). Analysis of sequential TBS, SDS, and urea-extracted OPTN protein levels showed significant reduction in AD compared to NCI within the SDS and urea-soluble lysates (Fig. 1A–C, Supplemental Figure S1). No statistically significant changes were observed within the TBS extracted samples (Supplemental Figure S1). The majority of OPTN was extracted in SDS and urea, with very little being extracted in TBS, suggesting the majority isn’t freely soluble within the cytosol. A limitation of bulk analyses of brain tissue is that the relative composition and ratios of brain cell types are altered in AD brains (fewer neurons, more glia) and thus these results must be interpreted in this context. Nonetheless, assessing overall trends in OPTN protein abundance across individuals remains valuable, particularly because OPTN is expressed at comparable levels across neuronal and glial populations [22, 47].

Given the observed reduction of OPTN in human AD brain tissue, we next investigated whether changes in OPTN levels were associated with alterations in phosphorylated tau within our human iN model system. To accomplish this, tau antibodies specific for six different phosphorylated sites were quantified using WB across 53 ROSMAP iN cell lines, as previously described and reported [21]. When separating the pTau WB measurements by AD diagnosis, three of the selected six pTau epitopes were significantly higher in AD compared to NCI iNs. These tau proteoforms included aggregated high molecular weight (HMW; ~250 kDa) pTau202/205, HMW pTau214, and HMW pTau404 (Fig. 1D–F) [9, 21]. We next compared the abundance of OPTN protein in the same 53 NCI and AD iNs and found OPTN was significantly decreased in AD iNs (Fig. 1G). The OPTN protein measurements were extractedfrom a TMT-MS proteomic dataset published previously [21]. With this information, and from the same previously published TMT-MS proteomic dataset [21], we compared relevant autophagy proteins across these ROSMAP iN lines to the measured pTau levels. Spearman correlations between the selected autophagy proteins and pTau major as well as aggregated HMW pTau were computed separately, showing a strongly negative correlation between OPTN and several HMW pTau epitopes (Fig. 1H–J, Supplemental Figure S2A–D). Notably, the three pTau epitopes most upregulated in AD iNs showed the strongest correlations with OPTN abundance.

Loss of OPTN modestly increases specific phosphorylated tau proteoforms, independent of an overall change in autophagy-related processes

OPTN iPSCs were differentiated into neurons (iNs) using NGN2 overexpression, as previously described [24, 76]. Neurons were assessed for differentiation efficiency using immunocytochemistry by assessing the expression of Beta-III-Tubulin and DAPI, along with the observation of characteristic neurites (Fig. 2A). Reduced OPTN protein levels in HET and KO iNs were validated by WB (Fig. 2B, C).

Considering the strong correlation observed between OPTN abundance and certain HMW pTau epitope abundance (Fig. 1H–J), we next measured some of these pTau proteoforms of interest in theCRISPR modified OPTN iNs. Across both genetic backgrounds we observed an increase in HMW pTau202/205 (normalized to total pTau202/205) with loss of OPTN (Fig. 2D). While a similar trend was observed in KO iNs with HMW pTau214 levels (normalized to total pTau214), the difference did not reach significance (Fig. 2E, F, Supplemental Figure S4A–E). No change to the major band of pTau214 and pTau202/205 (normalized to total tau) was observed (Supplemental Figure S4F, G). Loss of OPTN resulted in modest to no changes in lysosomal degradation (as measured by DQ Red BSA) or LC3B-II accumulation during autophagy inhibition (Fig. 2G–K). The lack of measurable impact of OPTN reduction on LCB-II and lysosomal activity occurred despite a strong correlation between OPTN protein abundance and an autophagy gene set variation analysis (GSVA) signature applied to proteomic profiling data from 53 ROSMAP iNs (Supplemental Figure S5A, B). The GSVA “autophagy” signature was derived from the up (positive) and downregulated (negative) proteins (q > 0.05) within neurons following chloroquine treatment. When this same chloroquine treated “autophagy” signature was applied to the proteomic profiles of our OPTN loss-of-function iN model using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), a similar association was observed in the positive direction (proteins increased with chloroquine treatment; Supplemental Figure S5C, D). The concordance between protein-level “autophagy” signatures applied to data sets exploring OPTN protein levels within CRISPR KO iNs and across ROSMAP iNs with natural variation in OPTN abundance suggests an underlying alteration in autophagy-related pathways with OPTN loss, despite no overall functional change observed.

Loss of neuronal OPTN causes modest changes to the proteome, but significantly alters intracellular clusterin

To determine if and how OPTN levels affect the neuronal proteome, we analyzed OPTN WT, HET, and KO iNs by TMT-MS (Supplemental Table S2). Limited TMT-MS changes were consistently observed across both BR24 and BR33 genetic backgrounds, likely due to weak effect sizes of loss of OPTN in iNs. Specifically, combined analyses identified only 25 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) following multiple comparisons testing between HET and WT, and 111 DEPs were identified when comparing KO and WT (out of > 10k unique proteins quantified). Of these, only 11 DEPs overlapped between HET and KO (Fig. 3A, B).

To further probe the individual genes that may be associated with OPTN levels, we calculated the correlation coefficients between protein and RNA levels of OPTN, and all genes detected at both the protein and RNA levels across the data sets of 53 ROSMAP iNs (Supplemental Table S3, Fig. 3C). This analysis identified genes consistently associated with OPTN at both the RNA and protein levels, including several genes relevant to neurodegenerative disease including BIN1, CLU, and PICALM. To narrow our search criteria for candidate proteins relevant to complete loss of OPTN, we integrated ROSMAP iN analyses with the data from isogenic OPTN WT and KO lines (Fig. 3D). Of the 11,302 proteins identified across both BR24 and BR33 in our TMT-MS, 345 had a nominal p-value < 0.001. Of these, 31 proteins also were associated with OPTN protein and RNA levels across ROSMAP iNs using a threshold of r > 0.4 for both RNA and protein (from Fig. 3C). For a gene to be considered, the RNA and protein level correlations were required to have the same directionality and correspond to the OPTN proteomics accordingly (Fig. 3E).

Of these 31 proteins, we selected clusterin (CLU) for further investigation given its relevance to late onset AD, its established roles in tau proteostasis and autophagy pathways, and its status as one of only two proteins identified in Fig. 3C that was both an AD GWAS hit and passed the cutoffs used in Fig. 3D [4, 60, 72]. Within the ROSMAP iN cohort, OPTN and CLU protein levels have one of the strongest inverse correlations (r = −0.87) (Fig. 3F). Using WB analyses, we found intracellular mature CLU is significantly upregulated in OPTN KO iNs, validating the proteomic findings (Fig. 3G, H). However, extracellular secreted CLU was not changed (Fig. 3I). Given that CLU is most highly expressed in astrocytes within the human brain [14], we also examined its levels in astrocytes lacking OPTN. Interestingly, the upregulation of CLU in OPTN KO iNs appears to have some cell type specificity as intracellular mature CLU levels were not elevated in OPTN KO iAs (Supplemental Figure S6A–F). Like iNs, OPTN KO iA did not show a measurable change in LC3B-II accumulation (Supplemental Figure S6G, H). However, extracellular CLU levels were markedly reduced in OPTN KO iAs, as well as other secreted proteins, including APOE and IL-6 (Supplemental Figure S6I–K). For the remainder of this study, we focused on neuronal phenotypes, but additional studies are warranted to understand the role of OPTN in astrocytes.

The OPTN interactome in human neurons includes mitochondrial and microtubule proteins, but its ablation alone is insufficient to alter mitochondrial function or morphology

We next sought to elucidate the OPTN interactome using this isogenic OPTN iPSC resource. To this end, we immunoprecipitated OPTN in OPTN WT and OPTN KO iNs and performed data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA-MS; IP-MS). Differential protein abundance was calculated between WT and KO samples to identify the highest-confidence OPTN-interacting proteins (Fig. 4A, Supplemental Table S4). WB of the input vs. immunoprecipitated samples demonstrated pull-down of OPTN in WT neurons, with the KO neurons included as a negative control for proteins immunoprecipitated in a non-specific manner (Fig. 4B). As predicted from the reported role of OPTN in mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics [42], we observed several proteins relevant to mitochondrial function and dynamics in the top 20 highest confidence interactors that are highlighted in red (Fig. 4C). Some of these proteins included OXCT1, an inner mitochondrial matrix protein, as well as ACSL1 and SLC27A4, which are thought to direct fatty acids into membranes including those within the mitochondria. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was also performed on all significantly enriched proteins (WT vs. KO) identified by IP-MS to assess the OPTN interactome more broadly (Fig. 4D). Several actin, cytoskeletal, and microtubule terms were also enriched, highlighting the potential importance of OPTN in trafficking and actin assembly within neurons.

Considering the relevance of mitochondrial interacting proteins to OPTN biology and the existing literature around this, we wanted to determine if the loss of OPTN was sufficient to alter mitochondrial content, morphology, or function within human neurons. Mitochondrial morphology was investigated by immunostaining for ATPB (ATP5F1B), the beta subunit of mitochondrial ATP synthase. No mitochondrial size or morphological abnormalities were observed due to OPTN ablation (Fig. 5A–C). There also was no difference in mitochondrial content, assayed by MitoTracker, between OPTN iN genotypes (Fig. 5D). These findings are supported by the lack of mitochondrial DEPs identified by proteomic profiling (Supplemental Table S2). The mitochondrial protein OPA1 is proteolytically processed in response to stress [12, 23]. Therefore, we assayed OPA1 isoforms to determine if OPTN ablation induces mitochondrial stress in both OPTN WT and KO neurons. No change in OPA1 cleavage was observed in OPTN KO neurons (Fig. 5E, F). These assays do not measure the actual functionality of the mitochondria. Therefore, oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using the Seahorse Mitochondrial Stress Test, but no respiratory differences were detected between OPTN genotypes (Fig. 5G–I). Broad mitochondrial dysfunction also was not observed in differentiated astrocytes (Supplemental Figure S7). Together, our data suggest that OPTN ablation does not cause broad mitochondrial dysfunction in the human iN or iA experimental systems analyzed herein.

Discussion

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the accumulation of phosphorylated tau tangles and amyloid-β plaques, as well as disruptions in cellular proteostasis. Our findings highlight OPTN as a potential contributor to neuronal homeostasis and a modulator of AD relevant pathology. Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated disruption of OPTN in iNs and iAs, we uncovered a modest yet significant increase in select pTau species, accompanied by alterations in CLU, without global changes in LC3B-II accumulation, lysosome function, or mitochondrial function. These data position OPTN as a potential regulator of specific pathways relevant to AD pathophysiology.

Selective autophagy is essential for post-mitotic neuronal health, facilitating the degradation of protein aggregates and damaged organelles. OPTN is well-characterized as an adaptor protein in mitophagy and aggrephagy, but it also participates in other types of selective autophagy. Alongside OPTN, other adaptors—including SQSTM1/p62, CALCOCO2/NDP52, and TAX1BP1—have been implicated in mitophagy, raising the possibility of functional redundancy in neurons and astrocytes. While some studies suggest OPTN acts as a largely non-redundant adaptor [37, 42], the modest phenotypes observed in our OPTN KO iNs and iAs suggest that other autophagy adaptors are able to compensate for its loss [73]. This hypothesis is supported by recent quantitative proteomic analyses demonstrating that multiple autophagy adaptors can compensate for each other in the clearance of damaged lysosomes and other cargo [10]. More specifically, that study found that while OPTN can drive damage-dependent lysophagy when overexpressed, cells lacking either OPTN or CALCOCO2 can still maintain significant lysophagic flux in HeLa cells, arguing for a certain degree of functional redundancy [10]. Another group systematically demonstrated that OPTN and CALCOCO2/NDP52 are redundant in mitophagy within a HeLa cell model, but it was later found that the two adaptors use slightly different autophagy machinery upon recruitment [25, 45]. Our findings, in conjunction with previous literature, suggest cell-type specificity for OPTN functional redundancy may provide an explanation for the lack of broad mitochondrial or autophagy defects observed in the OPTN KO iNs and iAs studied herein. While CALCOCO2, p62, and other relevant adaptor proteins in mitophagy and aggrephagy are identified at the protein level in our iN system, they were found to be unchanged in our OPTN WT vs KO iN proteomics. Further validation of compensatory autophagy pathways in neurons and astrocytes will be critical in determining whether alternative mechanisms are engaged following OPTN loss.

Such potential compensation by autophagy adaptor proteins is particularly relevant to tau proteostasis, as distinct adaptors have been shown to differentially direct specific forms of tau for degradation [71]. The regulation of other aggregation-prone proteins could also be relevant to this hypothesis. A recent study reported that compared to TDP-43 transgenic mice, those with concurrent Optn knockdown exhibited reduced TDP-43 levels, decreased spinal microgliosis, and no observable behavioral deficits [35]. While the authors proposed that Optn downregulation may suppress TDP-43 toxicity by regulating its abundance through aggregate formation, it remains possible that compensatory activity by other autophagy adaptor proteins contributes to some of the dampened phenotypes [35]. Another important consideration is that OPTN mutations, rather than complete knockout, may have distinct effects on neuronal phenotypes. For instance, a recent study found that specific OPTN coding mutations impair axonal transport and lead to neurodegeneration [33]. These effects were rescued by overexpression of OPTN, TRAK1, and KIF5B [33]. Thus, further studies using human neurons are warranted to determine the impact of disease relevant OPTN mutations on the phenotypes observed.

Another striking finding is the upregulation of intracellular CLU in neurons following OPTN loss. This inverse relationship between OPTN and CLU was evident across our ROSMAP iN lines and was further supported by our analysis of human AD brain tissue, where we found OPTN levels were reduced in AD brains while CLU levels are elevated in AD brain [32, 62]. CLU is a molecular chaperone involved in protein quality control and has been identified as a genetic risk factor for AD in multiple genome-wide association studies [4, 16]. Although CLU is primarily secreted, studies have revealed cytosolic localization under conditions of cellular stress. One study found that cytosolic CLU facilitates the trafficking of misfolded proteins to autophagosomes for degradation [60], while another demonstrated that CLU expression protects against intracellular proteotoxicity in neurodegenerative disease models [15]. Additionally, CLU has been shown to act as a stress-induced autophagy adaptor protein, enhancing autophagosome biogenesis and autophagy activation in cancer cells [74]. Our findings align with these reports and suggest that CLU upregulation in neurons may serve as another potential compensatory response to OPTN loss, perhaps mitigating some of its deleterious effects on not only proteostasis and neuronal biological processes, but also mitochondrial stability and functioning [54].

While intracellular CLU was significantly upregulated in neurons, its levels remained unchanged in OPTN KO astrocytes. However, we observed a notable reduction in secreted CLU, APOE, and IL-6 following OPTN knockdown in iAs. These findings suggest that astrocyte secretory pathways may be modulated by OPTN activity, potentially influencing extracellular proteostasis and neuroinflammation. A previous study demonstrated that OPTN functions as an adaptor protein linking myosin VI to secretory vesicles and autophagosomes, with OPTN loss impairing secretory protein trafficking [67]. Given the crucial role of astrocytes in extracellular proteostasis and neuroinflammation, the regulation of CLU and other secretory proteins by OPTN represents an important future direction for this research in astrocytes.

Despite the limited mitochondrial phenotypes observed following OPTN ablation, our IP-MS interactome data indicate that OPTN likely exists in complexes with multiple mitochondrial proteins within human neurons. Notable interactors include OXCT1, a key enzyme in ketone metabolism essential for mitochondrial energy homeostasis and oxidative stress regulation [34]. Other interactors include ACSL1 and SLC27A4, both involved in fatty acid metabolism and mitochondrial lipid handling, which might suggest a potential crosstalk between OPTN and mitochondria/lipid homeostasis [31, 38, 40]. HAGH, an enzyme responsible for detoxifying reactive byproducts of glycolysis, may implicate OPTN in metabolic resilience and oxidative stress protection [1]. Beyond interacting partners involved in mitochondrial processes, DYNLL1 and DYNLL2, components of the dynein motor complex, were also identified as strong interactors of neuronal OPTN. The validation of this previously described interaction in human neurons suggests OPTN may play a similar role in intracellular transport and cytoskeletal organization, which could have implications for tau pathology and axonal integrity [5]. While the modest phenotypic changes following OPTN loss may be attributed to functional redundancy with other autophagy adaptors, the composition of the OPTN interactome in human neurons reveals compelling molecular associations that could shed light on additional facets of OPTN biology. Future studies leveraging these interactions may deepen our understanding of the role of OPTN in mitochondrial dynamics, metabolism, and cytoskeletal regulation, ultimately refining its implications in neurodegenerative processes.

Conclusions

Our study identifies OPTN as a regulator of pTau homeostasis in human neurons and reveals its effects on proteostasis in neurons and astrocytes. While OPTN loss modestly increases select HMW pTau species, it does not broadly disrupt autophagy or mitochondrial function, likely due to redundancy among autophagy adaptors. The upregulation of intracellular CLU in neurons and the decrease in astrocyte secretion following OPTN loss suggests a complex interplay between intracellular and extracellular proteostasis, with implications for AD progression across brain cell types.

By further dissecting the role of OPTN in selective autophagy, tau homeostasis, and secretory pathways, we can gain deeper insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying AD and potentially identify new therapeutic targets to restore proteostasis in neurodegenerative diseases.
