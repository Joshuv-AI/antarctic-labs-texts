Association of Coronary Microvascular Rarefaction and Myocardial Fibrosis With Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Duncan J. Campbell, Victoria C. M. Francis, Gregory R. Young, Noel W. F. Woodford
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate, in a cohort study, whether coronary microvasculature and myocardial structure differ between people with and without coronary artery disease (CAD).Methods and ResultsWe performed histological analysis of left ventricle free wall obtained at autopsy from 25 men and 23 women with ≥1 coronary artery with ≥75% area stenosis, and 25 men and 25 women without (no or minimal) CAD, matched for sex and age, who died suddenly from noncardiac causes. Decedents with myocardial infarction or other cardiac abnormality were excluded. Decedents with and without CAD had similar height and weight. Heart weight of decedents with CAD was higher than that of decedents without CAD (mean, 391 versus 364 g; mean difference, 27 g [95% CI, 0.3–54.0], P=0.048). Decedents with CAD had lower arteriole density (mean, 1.4 per mm2 versus 1.8 per mm2; mean difference, −0.4 per mm2 [95% CI, −0.6 to −0.2], P=0.0001), lower capillary length density (mean, 3164 versus 3701 mm/mm3; mean difference, −537 [95% CI, −787 to −286], P<0.0001), and higher total myocardial fibrosis (mean, 7.5% versus 5.7%; mean difference, 1.7% [95% CI, 1.0–2.5], P<0.0001), than decedents without CAD.ConclusionsCAD was associated with coronary microvascular rarefaction and increased myocardial fibrosis. The association of CAD with coronary microvascular rarefaction and increased myocardial fibrosis may contribute to the increased risks of death, myocardial infarction and heart failure that accompany CAD, and may attenuate the impact of percutaneous coronary intervention on cardiovascular risk in people with stable angina.

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of death, 1 , 2 myocardial infarction (MI), 2 and heart failure 2 , 3 ; however, the role of coronary stenoses in these outcomes is uncertain. 4 Correction of coronary stenoses with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) added to optimal medical therapy, in comparison with optimal medical therapy alone, in patients with stable angina did not reduce all‐cause mortality 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 or hospitalization for heart failure. 9 , 10 The failure of PCI added to optimal medical therapy to reduce all‐cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure, in comparison with optimal medical therapy alone, puts into question whether mechanisms other than coronary stenoses contribute to cardiovascular risk in patients with stable angina. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

Individuals with CAD have reduced coronary flow reserve, 13 which was not corrected by PCI. 14 The prediction of cardiovascular outcomes by impaired coronary flow reserve not only in patients with obstructive CAD but also in patients without evidence of obstructive CAD, 15 , 16 , 17 throws into focus the potential role of coronary microvascular dysfunction in cardiovascular outcomes. 18 Moreover, the similar reduction of coronary flow reserve for myocardium supplied by stenotic and nonstenotic coronary vessels of patients with CAD, 19 , 20 , 21 indicated that coronary microvascular dysfunction in CAD was global rather than restricted to myocardium supplied by stenotic vessels. Proposed pathogenic mechanisms for coronary microvascular dysfunction include abnormal dilatory responses of coronary microvessels, coronary microvascular spasm, autonomic dysfunction, extravascular compressive forces, luminal obstruction, vascular remodeling, vascular wall infiltration, vascular rarefaction, and perivascular fibrosis, 18 , 22 , 23 , 24 and the number of proposed mechanisms illustrates uncertainty about which mechanisms contribute to the pathogenesis of microvascular dysfunction. Reduced coronary microvascular density was described in myocardial hypertrophy, 25 , 26 , 27 and in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), 28 and idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy. 29 Moreover, Campbell et al. 27 reported lower capillary length density in epicardial biopsies from patients with than without CAD who had cardiac surgery. To gain insight into possible mechanisms of coronary microvascular dysfunction in CAD, the present cohort study investigated the association of CAD with microvascular density and myocardial structure in decedents with and without CAD who died suddenly of non‐cardiac causes and underwent autopsy at which a transmural block of the left ventricle (LV) wall was formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded.

METHODS

Data Sharing Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

This study was conducted with formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded blocks of LV free wall, collected as part of normal autopsy procedure, including toxicologic testing, by the Victorian Institute of Forensic Medicine. The National Coronial Information System for Victoria, Australia, conducted 18 276 autopsies (of a total of 32 502 reported cases) for the years 2005 to 2012. Autopsy reports from the years 2005 to 2012 were searched with the following search terms: coronary artery disease, stroke, motor vehicle accident, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, ruptured aortic aneurysm, and suicide (Figure 1). This search yielded 731 autopsy reports, including reports of myocardial histology, that were examined for eligible decedents. Inclusion criteria: sudden death, either witnessed death or found dead within 24 hours of last seen alive, confirmed by police report, due to a noncardiac cause evident on autopsy, in association with CAD (≥1 coronary artery with ≥75% area stenosis), or no CAD (NoCAD, coronary arteries described as having no or mild/minimal CAD).

CONSORT diagram of strategy for search of National Coronial Information System to identify decedents with sudden noncardiac death and with or without coronary artery disease.

None of the decedents with CAD had complete occlusion of a coronary artery or intravascular thrombosis; one decedent with CAD had a small intra‐plaque hemorrhage. CAD indicates coronary artery disease; CONSORT, Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials; MI, myocardial infarction; and PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention.

Exclusion criteria: decedents where cause of death could not be ascertained; decomposition or autolysis; evidence of previous MI, including contraction bands or myocardial scar; greater than mild patchy myocardial fibrosis or other myocardial pathology, including >mild myocardial fibrosis noted on review of myocardial histology by pathologist at autopsy; previous coronary artery surgery or PCI; significant valve disease or valve prosthesis; heart weight >500 g; multiorgan failure. Decedents were excluded if there was a possibility that death attributed to an accident was precipitated by a cardiac event, and decedents for whom death was associated with the possibility of a hypoxia‐precipitated cardiac event, including deaths due to respiratory depressants, food aspiration, or attributed to asthma or epilepsy, were also excluded. In addition, decedents for whom death was attributed to diabetic ketoacidosis or gastrointestinal hemorrhage were excluded because of the possibility that ketoacidosis or gastrointestinal hemorrhage precipitated a cardiac event.

Forty‐eight decedents (25 men and 23 women) with sudden death due to a noncardiac cause, with CAD, were matched for sex and age (within 5 years) with 50 decedents (25 men and 25 women) with sudden death due to a noncardiac cause, without CAD (Figure 1). None had complete occlusion of a coronary artery or intravascular thrombus; 1 decedent had a small intraplaque hemorrhage. Details of each decedent's medical history and medications were obtained from the autopsy report, which included information from the police report, the decedent's health care providers, and the decedent's family. The autopsy report also provided details of all organs examined, including organ histology, which provided information about comorbidities. Once decedents were identified, paraffin blocks of the LV free wall were retrieved from storage and processed for histological analysis. This study was approved by local ethics committees, with waiver of requirement for consent from next of kin.

Autopsy Procedure

Autopsies were conducted by the Victorian Institute of Forensic Medicine using standard procedures that included careful examination of the heart for the presence of hypertrophy, fibrosis or infarction. 30 After gross examination, hearts were serially sectioned along the short axis at apical and midventricle levels. From the midventricle, representative transmural blocks of tissue from the LV free wall were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin for histological analysis. Hematoxylin–eosin‐stained sections of the LV wall were examined by the pathologist, and the presence or absence of myocardial fibrosis or other pathology was noted in the autopsy report. The epicardial coronary arteries were serially sectioned along the length of each vessel and CAD was quantified as percent area stenosis. Kidney glomerulosclerosis and kidney arteriosclerosis (≥mild) were described in the autopsy report.

Histologic Analysis of LV

Although autopsies where the pathologist reported the presence of autolysis were excluded, cardiac histology showed postmortem changes due to the delay between death and formalin‐fixation of tissue, including some shrinkage of cardiomyocytes in tissue sections. No sections showed evidence of myocarditis. Histological analysis was performed as previously described. 27 , 31

The present study of myocardial structure was limited to 1 LV block per decedent; however, hematoxylin–eosin‐stained sections from an average of 2 LV blocks per decedent were available to the pathologist for assessment of myocardial fibrosis and other myocardial pathology.

Four micrometer sections were cut from each paraffin block and stained with picrosirius red for quantification of interstitial and perivascular fibrosis, counting of arterioles, and measurement of minimal arteriole diameter; reticulin‐stained for measurement of cardiomyocyte width 32 ; and immunostained for CD31 (mouse antihuman CD31 monoclonal antibody, Dako Denmark A/S, Glostrup, Denmark) for quantification of capillaries.

Cardiomyocytes, arterioles, and capillaries were of every orientation from cross‐sectional to longitudinal. The area of each section was determined by quantitative morphometry of digitized images (Aperio Image Analysis System, Aperio Technologies, Inc., Vista, CA). Median total section area was 224 (interquartile range: 169–271, n=98) mm2. The width of the LV wall was determined by dividing the area of the tissue section by the longitudinal length of the section (Figure 2A). Trabeculae and papillary muscle, when present, were excluded from estimation of section area and LV wall thickness.

Picrosirius red‐stained section of left ventricle.

Digitized images of picrosirius red‐stained left ventricle section illustrating section area and length of section (A), and inner, middle, and outer regions of section (B). Left ventricle wall thickness was calculated by dividing the section area (262 mm2) by the section length (21.02 mm) to give left ventricle wall thickness (12.47 mm).

Digitized images of transmural picrosirius red‐stained and reticulin‐stained sections, and sections immunostained for CD31, were divided into nonoverlapping inner, middle, and outer regions of approximate equal area (Figure 2B), and the regions were analyzed separately by quantitative morphometry. All histological analyses of digitized images were performed by the same individual without knowledge of the presence or absence of CAD, sex, age, or cause of death.

Cardiomyocyte Width

Cardiomyocyte width, determined on sections stained for reticulin, 32 was the shortest diameter of cardiomyocyte profiles with a nucleus (Figure 3). Medians were calculated for >100 measurements of cardiomyocyte width for each of the inner, middle, and outer regions of each section, and group data presented as mean values of individual medians for each region, with SD.

Digitized image of reticulin‐stained left ventricle section illustrating the outlines of cardiomyocytes, and measurement of the shortest diameter of cardiomyocyte profiles with a nucleus.

Quantification of Total Fibrosis

Total fibrosis of the inner, middle, and outer regions of each LV section was quantified by digital analysis of picrosirius red‐stained sections using Aperio Imagescope software's Positive Pixel Count algorithm calibrated for hue and color saturation thresholds. Total fibrosis was calculated as the sum of the total pixel count for fibrosis, expressed as a percentage of the total pixel count for the tissue area for each region of each section (Figure 4).

Quantification of Arterioles

The tissue was immersion fixed and the arterioles were usually oval in shape because of deformation or because they were cut at an oblique angle. Campbell et al. 31 previously reported immunohistochemical staining for elastin showed blood vessels were relaxed in immersion‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissue. Arterioles were identified by the presence of a layer of media and nuclei in the smooth muscle layer in picrosirius red‐stained sections (Figure 5). Arterioles, diameter <200 μm, in all orientations, were counted manually and minimal arteriole diameters for all arterioles measured in the digitized images of the inner, middle, and outer regions of each section, with care to avoid counting the same arteriole more than once, and expressed as arterioles/mm2. Tissue area was calculated with the positive pixel count algorithm of the Aperio ImageScope Analysis software. Arteriole wall area/circumference ratio and perivascular fibrosis were measured by planimetry of the digitized image for ≥5 arterioles per region in approximate cross‐section and of a size that facilitated planimetry (minimal arteriole diameter >20 μm). The inner and outer vessel borders and the outer border of the perivascular fibrosis were carefully traced manually, and the Aperio ImageScope Analysis software automatically calculated the circumference and the area of inner and outer vessel borders, and the outer border of the perivascular fibrosis (Figure 5). Perivascular fibrosis was calculated as the ratio of the area of perivascular fibrosis (total area of vessel and fibrosis minus total vessel area) and the total vessel area (area of vessel wall plus lumen). 33 Arteriole wall area/circumference ratio was calculated as the ratio of wall area (total vessel area minus lumen area) and vessel circumference. Medians were calculated for the minimal diameters of all arterioles, and for ≥5 measurements of perivascular fibrosis and arteriole wall area/circumference ratio for each of the inner, middle, and outer regions of each section, and group data presented as means (±SD) of individual medians.

Quantification of Capillary Length Density and Diffusion Radius

Capillary length density and diffusion radius were calculated as previously described. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Capillary length density, which is the length of capillaries per unit volume of tissue, was determined by analysis of sections immuno‐stained for CD31 (mouse anti‐human CD31 monoclonal antibody, Dako Denmark A/S, Glostrup, Denmark) after antigen retrieval using standard stereological techniques. 34 , 36 , 37 , 38 Capillaries were counted in 60 separate counting frames of 120 × 120 μm in size for the inner, middle, and outer regions of each section (180 counting frames per section). Capillaries were present in all orientations and counting frames were superimposed over areas with minimally distorted tissue architecture where capillaries were predominantly in approximate cross‐section and CD31 staining of endothelial cells and capillaries was strongly positive (Figure 6). Capillary length density was corrected for tissue coverage quantified using the positive pixel count algorithm of the Aperio ImageScope Analysis software. Six decedents with CAD and 2 decedents without CAD were excluded from capillary quantification because capillaries were predominantly in longitudinal orientation or CD31 immunostaining was not strongly positive.

Statistical Analysis

Based on previous estimates of capillary length density in epicardial myocardium obtained at cardiac surgery from patients with and without CAD, 27 , 31 47 decedents per group provided 90% power to detect a 15% lower mean capillary length density in decedents with CAD, from 1200 mm/mm3 (SD 300) in decedents without CAD, at a 2‐sided type I error of 5%.

Categorical data were summarized as numbers and percentages. Continuous data were summarized as means±SD. Histological parameters for which multiple measurements were made for each region of each section (cardiomyocyte width, minimal arteriole diameter, arteriole perivascular fibrosis, arteriole wall area/circumference ratio) were expressed as the median value for each region of each section for each decedent, and group data for these histological parameters were summarized as means±SD of these medians.

Unpaired t test was used to test for differences between decedents with and without CAD, and between men and women, and expressed as mean difference and 95% CI. Differences in histological parameters between decedents with and without CAD, and between men and women, were calculated for all regions of each section, and for each section region (inner, middle, outer). In addition, for histological parameters, multivariable analysis of variance was performed with regions (inner, middle, outer) as the dependent variable, and CAD, sex, and age category (<35, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, and ≥75 years) as independent variables. Correlations between variables were evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Comparison of proportions was performed using the normal approximation of the binomial calculation, with P values from the chi‐square test. Height or weight were missing for 1 male decedent and 1 female decedent without CAD because these data were not collected at autopsy. Moreover, the kidneys were not examined for 1 female decedent with CAD. Analyses using height or weight were restricted to complete cases. Heart weight data were complete. Statistical analyses were performed using StatView statistical software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Height, weight, body mass index and body surface area were similar for decedents with and without CAD. Heart weight included all 4 cardiac chambers and epicardial fat. Mean±SD heart weight was 18% (95% CI, 11%–25%; P<0.0001) higher in men (407±53 g) than women (346±68 g), and also 7% (95% CI, 0%–15%; P=0.048) higher in decedents with (391±68 g) than without CAD (364±66 g); however, there were no differences in LV wall thickness between men and women or between decedents with and without CAD (Table). There were no differences in proportions of decedents with mild myocardial fibrosis, as assessed at autopsy, between men and women or between decedents with and without CAD Table).

With respect to comorbidities, there were no differences in the proportions with cancer or chronic obstructive airways disease, between men and women or between decedents with and without CAD (Table). There was no difference between men and women in the proportions with hypertension; however, a lower proportion of decedents with CAD had hypertension than decedents without CAD (10% versus 28%; mean difference −18% [95% CI, −2% to −33%], P=0.024). Moreover, there were no differences in the proportions with diabetes between men and women or between decedents with and without CAD.

Mean±SD total kidney weight was 32% (95% CI, 24%–43%; P<0.0001) higher for men (315±64 g) than women (239±64 g), and was 13% (95% CI, 2%–24%; P=0.025) higher in decedents with (295±80 g) than without CAD (262±65 g). Similarly, total kidney weight/body weight ratio was 14% (95% CI, 2%–25%; P=0.023) higher for men (4.0±1.1 g/kg) than women (3.5±0.9 g/kg), and was 14% (95% CI, 2%–25%; P=0.022) higher in decedents with (4.0±1.0 g/kg) than without CAD (3.5±1.0 g/kg). There was no difference between men and women in the proportions with kidney glomerulosclerosis; however, a lower proportion of decedents with CAD had kidney glomerulosclerosis than decedents without CAD (6% versus 26%; mean difference −20% [95% CI, −34% to −6%] P=0.008). There was no difference in the proportions with kidney arteriosclerosis between men and women or between decedents with and without CAD (Table).

DISCUSSION

The prediction of cardiovascular outcomes by impaired coronary flow reserve not only in patients with obstructive CAD but also in patients without evidence of obstructive CAD 15 , 16 , 17 indicated a potential role of coronary microvascular dysfunction in cardiovascular outcomes. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 By demonstrating an association between CAD and lower coronary microvascular density, this study provided a potential anatomical basis for microvascular dysfunction and impaired coronary flow reserve in CAD. Moreover, the higher diffusion radius and diffusion radius/cardiomyocyte width ratio in decedents with than without CAD illustrated the potential impact of lower capillary length density on delivery of oxygen and nutrients to cardiomyocytes in CAD. In addition, this study demonstrated an association between CAD and increased total myocardial fibrosis, and the correlation between myocardial fibrosis and arteriole density provided insight into the potential role of microemboli in the lower arteriole density and increased myocardial fibrosis in decedents with CAD. Increased myocardial fibrosis predicts sudden death in individuals with CAD. 39 The association of coronary microvascular rarefaction and increased myocardial fibrosis with CAD had the potential to increase myocardial vulnerability to ischemia, to reduce myocardial compliance, and to promote arrhythmogenesis in CAD, thereby contributing to the increased risk of death, MI, and heart failure that accompany CAD, 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 40 and also to attenuate the impact of PCI on cardiovascular risk in individuals with stable angina. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 40 This hypothesis‐generating study contributes to the evolving management paradigm for patients with stable ischemic heart disease 41 and needs to be further verified in large studies.

The reduced myocardial capillary length density in decedents with CAD was in agreement with the previous report of lower capillary length density in epicardial biopsies from patients with than without CAD who had cardiac surgery. 27 Among the large number of proposed pathogenic mechanisms for microvascular dysfunction in CAD, 18 , 22 , 23 , 24 this study found no evidence that arteriole hypertrophy or perivascular fibrosis played a role.

There are few previous autopsy studies of myocardial structure of the “normal” human heart, 25 , 26 , 34 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 and this is the first comparison of coronary microvasculature and myocardial structure of decedents with and without CAD who died of noncardiac causes, without evidence of myocardial injury. Reduced coronary microvascular density may be a consequence of increased cardiomyocyte width 26 , 27 ; however, cardiomyocyte width did not differ between decedents with and without CAD. The 7% higher heart weight of decedents with CAD was unexplained. It is of note that decedents with CAD also had higher kidney weight. Although cardiomyocyte number was not measured, the similar LV wall thickness, cardiomyocyte width, arteriole wall area/circumference ratio, and similar proportions of decedents with kidney arteriosclerosis suggested that blood pressures were similar in decedents with and without CAD and did not contribute to the higher heart weight in decedents with CAD. Another potential contributor to the higher heart weight of decedents with CAD was epicardial adipose tissue; however, although the weights of individual cardiac chambers and epicardial fat were not measured in this study, decedents with and without CAD had similar body mass index. Whereas myocardial fibrosis is a well characterized consequence of myocardial injury such as MI, and is associated with many different cardiac pathologies including heart failure and valve disease, 28 , 46 its association with CAD in the absence of MI or other myocardial pathologies has not been previously described. Moreover, although perivascular fibrosis is associated with cardiac dysfunction, 47 the significance of reduced perivascular fibrosis in the myocardium of decedents with CAD in this study is unknown.

Our finding of a lower coronary microvascular density in decedents with CAD was in agreement with the association of decreased myocardial capillary density with impaired coronary flow reserve in patients with heart failure due to idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy, reported by Tsagalou et al. 29 Furthermore, Mohammed et al. 28 reported coronary microvascular rarefaction and increased myocardial fibrosis in an autopsy study of decedents with HFpEF, with an inverse association between coronary microvascular density and myocardial fibrosis. In the study by Mohammed et al., microvessels were defined as the combination of capillaries and small precapillary arterioles. 28 In this study by Mohammed et al., 65% of decedents with HFpEF and 13% of decedents without HFpEF had ≥1 coronary artery with >50% diameter stenosis. Moreover, heart weight was 60% higher for decedents with than without HFpEF; however, cardiomyocyte width was not measured by Mohammed et al. and the contribution of cardiomyocyte hypertrophy to reduced coronary microvascular density in decedents with HFpEF is unknown. 48 The associations between CAD and reduced coronary microvascular density and increased myocardial fibrosis, identified in the present study, may explain in part the association of CAD with HFpEF risk. 49 , 50

Whereas coronary embolization is well described in autopsy studies of sudden ischemic cardiac death, 51 this study examined decedents with noncardiac death, without evidence of coronary thrombosis or myocardial injury. In an autopsy study of normal human myocardium obtained within 3 hours of death from decedents without CAD or hypertension, Kaneko et al. described areas of intramyocardial micronecrosis and fibrosis that corresponded in size to that of the intramyocardial microcirculatory unit, 45 consistent with microembolic obstruction of arterioles causing vessel dropout and fibrosis. 51 The finding of an inverse association between arteriole density and total myocardial fibrosis in the present study was consistent with microemboli causing vessel dropout and fibrosis in people with and without CAD. CAD may accelerate vessel dropout and fibrosis because of a higher number of microemboli released from coronary artery atherosclerotic plaque. Moreover, CAD‐associated reduced coronary microvascular density may increase myocardial susceptibility to infarction caused by emboli released from coronary artery atherosclerotic plaque. 27 The finding that the lower arteriole density of decedents with CAD was more evident for arterioles with smaller minimal diameter in the present study was consistent with a higher susceptibility of smaller arterioles to obstruction by microemboli.

Additional evidence in support of a role for microemboli in cardiac events in individuals with CAD is the lower mortality and MI after coronary artery bypass surgery in comparison with PCI in multivessel CAD, 52 which may be explained by the bypass graft providing “surgical collateralization” and diverting flow from coronary atherosclerosis, 53 thereby reducing embolization of plaque material and microthromboses. By contrast, PCI may fail to prevent embolization from nonflow‐limiting coronary atherosclerosis and, in addition, the PCI procedure may cause embolization of plaque material and microthromboses. 54 The benefits of optimal medical therapy may be due in part to reduction in embolization of plaque material and microthromboses from the atherosclerotic plaque, consequent to stabilization of the plaque surface and reduced inflammation and thrombosis. 55

Clinical Implications

Our finding of an association between coronary microvascular rarefaction and CAD provides a potential explanation for the impaired coronary flow reserve in myocardium supplied by nonstenotic vessels in patients with CAD. Moreover, coronary microvascular rarefaction, together with increased myocardial fibrosis, provides a potential explanation for the increased risk of death, myocardial infarction, and heart failure in CAD 1 , 2 , 3 and also provides a potential explanation for the failure of PCI added to optimal medical therapy to reduce all‐cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure, in comparison with optimal medical therapy alone, in patients with stable angina. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 This study thereby contributes to the evolving management paradigm for patients with CAD. 41

Strengths and Limitations

The notable strengths of this study included the inclusion and exclusion criteria, which were designed to reduce potential confounders to the interpretation of these data, and our separate quantification of arteriole density and capillary length density. Another strength, with respect to comorbidities, was the data for kidney weight and kidney histology for all except 1 decedent.

This study, however, also had important limitations. The relevance of the decedents in this study to the general population is unknown, given the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Decedents with and without CAD were matched for age and sex; however, because this was a population‐based cohort study, women were older than men. All decedents experienced noncardiac death. Approximately half of deaths were natural, due to cerebrovascular or other vascular causes; however, the causes of death were similar for decedents with and without CAD. Although we did not have echocardiographic data from these decedents before death, the autopsy did not reveal evidence of abnormal cardiac function in any decedent. Decedents with >mild myocardial fibrosis or other myocardial abnormality on histology at autopsy were excluded.

This study specifically addressed the associations between CAD and coronary microvascular density and myocardial structure, and the differences in coronary microvascular density and myocardial fibrosis between decedents with and without CAD remained statistically significant in multivariable models with CAD, sex, and age as independent variables. Although decedents with CAD had higher heart weight, they also had higher kidney weight, and lower proportions of decedents with CAD had hypertension and kidney glomerulosclerosis. The higher kidney weight, and lower proportions with hypertension and kidney glomerulosclerosis for decedents with CAD would be expected to bias these results toward the null hypothesis. Medication compliance could not be verified; however, if a greater proportion of decedents with CAD than without CAD had been taking cardioprotective medications such as statins, this would also be expected to bias these results toward the null hypothesis. These findings were restricted to CAD with ≥1 coronary artery with ≥75% area stenosis, without MI, and further studies are required to determine the association of less extensive CAD with coronary microvascular density and myocardial fibrosis. Moreover, this study did not exclude the potential contribution of mechanisms other than CAD to the differences in coronary microvascular density and myocardial fibrosis between decedents with and without CAD nor exclude additional mechanisms that have been proposed to contribute to coronary microvascular dysfunction and to cardiovascular events in patients with and without obstructive CAD and in those who receive PCI added to optimal medical therapy. 18 , 22 , 23 , 24 Given that this was an autopsy study, it provided no information about coronary vascular physiology. However, our measurement of arteriole wall area/circumference ratio provided no evidence for arteriole hypertrophy.

This study of myocardial structure was limited to 1 transmural block of tissue from the LV free wall from each decedent, although hematoxylin–eosin‐stained sections from a median of 2 LV blocks per decedent were available to the pathologist for assessment of myocardial fibrosis and other myocardial pathology. The median total area of LV sections in this study was 224 mm2. The purpose of this study was not to compare myocardial structure in different regions of the LV, but to compare myocardial structure of 48 decedents (25 male, 23 female) with CAD with myocardial structure of 50 decedents (25 male, 25 female) without CAD, matched for sex and age, thereby providing adequate statistical power to detect differences in myocardial structure between decedents with and without CAD. The statistical power of this study was increased by the separate measurement of histological parameters in the inner, middle, and outer regions of each LV section.

This study did not attempt to compare regions with and without ischemia, and decedents with macroscopic or histological evidence of ischemic injury were excluded. Of note, coronary flow reserve was similarly impaired for myocardium supplied by stenotic and nonstenotic coronary vessels in patients with CAD and reduced global myocardial coronary flow reserve, indicating that coronary microvascular dysfunction was global rather than regional. 15 , 16 , 19 , 20 , 21 Moreover, impaired coronary flow reserve predicted cardiovascular outcomes in patients both with and without evidence of obstructive CAD. 15 , 16 , 17

Although decedents with histological evidence of autolysis were excluded, the time delay between death and fixation of tissue was associated with some deterioration in histology, and shrinkage of cardiomyocytes may have led to underestimation of cardiomyocyte width; however, this artifact was likely to have been similar for decedents with and without CAD. Measurement of capillary length density was not random because counting frames were superimposed over areas where capillary profiles were predominantly in approximate cross‐section with strongly positive staining for CD31. However, the median of 3905 capillaries counted for 180 counting frames per section compensated in part for the nonrandom distribution of counting frames. Estimates of cardiomyocyte width, arteriole perivascular fibrosis, and arteriole wall area/circumference ratio were also not random, because of the need to estimate cardiomyocyte width from cardiomyocyte profiles containing a nucleus, and to estimate perivascular fibrosis and wall area/circumference ratio of arterioles in approximate cross‐section with minimal diameter >20 μm. By contrast, arterioles were counted, their minimal diameter measured, and total myocardial fibrosis measured for the whole of each inner, middle, and outer region of each LV section, thereby providing unbiased estimates of arteriole density, minimal arteriole diameter, and total myocardial fibrosis.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this cohort study demonstrated an association between CAD and coronary microvascular rarefaction and increased myocardial fibrosis in decedents without evidence of myocardial injury. Coronary microvascular rarefaction and increased myocardial fibrosis may, by increasing myocardial vulnerability to ischemia, promoting arrhythmogenesis, and reducing myocardial compliance, contribute to the increased risk of death, MI, and heart failure that accompany CAD 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 40 and may attenuate the impact of PCI on cardiovascular risk in individuals with stable angina. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 40 This hypothesis‐generating study contributes to the evolving management paradigm for patients with stable ischemic heart disease, 41 and needs to be further verified in large studies.
