Analyzing the Chemical Space of Opioid Receptor Agonists and Antagonists: Insights from Computational Models
Authors: Tianshi Yu, Nuttapat Anuwongcharoen, Zi-Jun Wang, Theeraphon Piacham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The opioid crisis has imposed a significant financial burden on the United States, costing billions of dollars annually. The recent surge in opioid overdoses has further exacerbated this crisis, placing immense strain on public health resources and the criminal justice system. Currently, all four FDA-approved medications for medication-assisted treatment (MAT) of opioid use disorder (OUD) target opioid receptors (ORs), highlighting the importance of understanding their pharmacology. This computational study investigates the chemical space of agonists and antagonists of the three primary opioid receptorsmu-opioid receptor (MOR), kappa-opioid receptor (KOR), and delta-opioid receptor (DOR)by analyzing their physicochemical properties, Murcko scaffolds, and structure–activity relationships (SARs). Using data sets sourced from the ChEMBL database, the study focuses on IC50 and EC50 values, compiling a total of six data sets. To visualize the distribution of selective and nonselective ligands, the compounds were mapped within chemical space using t-SNE dimensionality reduction and embedding, employing the Klekota-Roth Count fingerprint. Within this space, k-means clustering was applied to group compounds from each data set, supporting the development of QSAR models, which demonstrated satisfactory predictive performance across most clusters. Additionally, matched molecular pair (MMP) analysis was conducted, identifying MMP cliffs, which highlight key structural modifications influencing bioactivity. These findings provide valuable insights for opioid receptor-targeted drug discovery, particularly in the optimization of opioid receptor agonists and antagonists, which could contribute to the development of safer and more effective therapeutic alternatives for pain management and opioid addiction treatment.

Introduction

Opioid use disorder (OUD), commonly referred to as opioid addiction, is a chronic relapsing condition characterized by compulsive opioid use despite adverse consequences. Individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD) commonly exhibit symptoms such as physiological dependence, intense opioid cravings, euphoria, respiratory depression, delusions, paranoia, and mood instability. OUD continues to be a significant public health crisis in both the United States and globally. It is estimated that over three million individuals in the United States and approximately 16 million worldwide have either previously experienced or are currently affected by OUD. The opioid crisis imposes a substantial economic burden on the United States, costing billions of dollars annually, largely due to the recent prescription opioid epidemic. This crisis has significantly impacted both public health and the criminal justice system. Until now, there are four medications as the first-line prescriptions for the use of medication-assisted treatment (MAT) against OUD: methadone, buprenorphine, naloxone and naltrexone, which all target opioid receptors. Methadone is a full opioid receptor agonist, and patients undergoing methadone maintenance treatment (MMT) experience reduced withdrawal symptoms, such as muscle aches and bone pain. Consequently, they are less likely to relapse in an attempt to alleviate these discomforts. Buprenorphine, a partial opioid receptor agonist, exhibits a lower activation of opioid receptors compared to methadone, thereby reducing the risk of respiratory depression and euphoria in individuals receiving buprenorphine treatment. Naloxone, a nonselective and competitive opioid receptor antagonist, is primarily used to reverse opioid overdoses. Naltrexone, an opioid receptor antagonist targeting all opioid receptor subtypes, prevents the binding of opioid receptor agonists, thereby mitigating opioid-induced effects such as respiratory depression and euphoria. Additionally, various opioid receptor modulators exist that can function as either agonists or antagonists, offering diverse pharmacological effects on opioid receptor activity. These modulators are currently undergoing experimental and clinical investigations to evaluate their potential for clinical application. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of their structure–activity relationships and chemical space is crucial for optimizing their therapeutic efficacy and safety. Structurally, opioid receptors are members of the G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) family. They are integral membrane proteins that interact with heterotrimeric Gi/o proteins to mediate signal transduction. The three classical subtypes of opioid receptors are the mu-opioid receptor (MOR), kappa-opioid receptor (KOR), and delta-opioid receptor (DOR). Additionally, nociceptin receptors (NORs), also known as opioid receptor-like receptor 1 (ORL1), play a crucial role in the development of tolerance to opioid receptor agonists used for analgesia. Opioids produce analgesic effects by targeting both presynaptic and postsynaptic sites. The three primary opioid receptors are extensively distributed throughout the brain, spinal cord, heart, and gastrointestinal system, enabling analgesia at both spinal and supraspinal levels. Among the three major types of opioid receptors, mu-opioid receptors (MORs) are primarily associated with analgesia, euphoria, physical dependence, and sedation within the central nervous system. In the peripheral system, MOR activation is linked to bradycardia, constipation, miosis, and respiratory depression. Within the brain, MORs are predominantly located in the brainstem and medial thalamus. The kappa-opioid receptor (KOR) plays a key role in modulating pain, mood, and stress response. It is particularly known for its ability to produce analgesia at the spinal level. Activation of KOR can effectively alleviate visceral and neuropathic pain while avoiding the respiratory depression commonly associated with other opioid receptors. However, activation of the kappa-opioid receptor (KOR) induces dysphoria rather than euphoria, which aids in the body’s adaptation to stress but may contribute to negative mood states with prolonged activation. Additionally, KOR activation diminishes the rewarding effects of addictive substances, thereby reducing drug-seeking behaviors and presenting potential therapeutic applications in addiction treatment. The DOR, in contrast, is associated with mood enhancement and resilience to stress, showing promise as an antidepressant target. While delta-opioid receptor (DOR) activation provides analgesia with a slower onset compared to kappa-opioid receptor (KOR) activation, it is particularly effective in managing chronic and inflammatory pain. Additionally, DOR activation contributes to emotional stability, potentially alleviating anxiety and supporting stress recovery. This effect may indirectly aid in addiction recovery by reducing stress-related cravings. Opioid receptors are targeted by various endogenous opioidsnaturally occurring ligands that play a crucial role in neurotransmission, pain modulation, and the maintenance of homeostatic functions. Exogenous opioids, which primarily bind to mu-opioid receptors (MORs), include both mild and potent analgesics used for pain management. These range from milder opioids, such as codeine and tramadol, to more potent analgesics, including oxycodone, morphine, hydromorphone, meperidine, tapentadol, methadone, fentanyl, sufentanil, and remifentanil. Mu-opioid receptor (MOR) agonists are among the most effective treatments for moderate to severe acute and chronic pain. However, their clinical use is restricted by significant adverse effects, including constipation, respiratory depression, and both physical and psychological dependence. These side effects are classified as on-target effects, as they are mediated by MOR activation and result from the widespread distribution of these receptors throughout the body. Despite these limitations, the high efficacy of MOR agonists in pain relief ensures that MOR remains a primary target in the development of novel analgesics. Cheminformatics is a multidisciplinary field that combines chemistry, information technology, and artificial intelligence. , This study utilizes cheminformatic techniques to investigate the chemical space of opioid receptor agonists and antagonists, with a particular focus on their distribution patterns, overlaps, and interrelationships. In cheminformatics, chemical space refers to the theoretical domain encompassing all possible organic molecules that conform to specific structural and physicochemical constraints. This space is mathematically defined by molecular descriptors and their embeddings, allowing each compound to be represented as a point with distinct coordinates within a multidimensional framework. This concept is fundamental to drug discovery and optimization, contributing to various processes such as chemical database compilation, molecular diversity and similarity assessments, virtual screening, structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis, chemical modification, and lead optimization. Chemical space is typically explored by projecting high-dimensional molecular data into lower-dimensional representations to facilitate interpretation.

In this study, the t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) method, an unsupervised machine learning technique for dimensionality reduction, was employed. This analysis utilized the Klekota-Roth Count fingerprint, which encompasses a total of 4860 features, to characterize and visualize the distribution of opioid receptor ligands within chemical space. Through exploratory data analysis, dimensionality reduction, Murcko scaffold analysis, and structure–activity relationship (SAR) investigation, this study examined the distribution patterns, matched molecular pairs (MMPs), and MMP cliffs of opioid receptor agonists and antagonists. The findings and models developed in this research contribute to advancements in computer-aided drug discovery (CADD), particularly in the optimization of mu-opioid receptor (MOR) agonists and antagonists for improved therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Data Collection

In this study, all the bioactivity data for both MOR, KOR and DOR were collected from the ChEMBL database (ChEMBL target IDs: 233 for MOR, 237 for KOR and 236 for DOR). The bioactivity data includes values labeled with EC50, and IC50, resulting in 3009, 2423, 2371, 2712, 1797, and 2509 data sets, respectively.

Data Cleansing

Data cleansing involves several essential steps to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data set. First, unqualified datasuch as entries lacking standard equations or containing symbols like “>” or “<”are removed, along with duplicate records and data sets with missing information.

Additionally, chemical data standardization is a crucial step for computing molecular descriptors or fingerprints and for subsequent modeling. This process includes detecting aromaticity, standardizing nitrogen representations, normalizing tautomers, and removing salts when present.

Data transformation further enhances the clarity and interpretability of bioactivity values, such as converting EC50 values to pEC50. For data labeling, bioactivity values are classified as follows: pChEMBL values greater than 8 are categorized as highly active (bio_code: 3), values between 7 and 8 as active (bio_code: 2), values between 6 and 7 as intermediate (bio_code: 1), and values below 6 as inactive (bio_code: 0). Furthermore, assay descriptions from the ChEMBL database are categorized based on their terminology: entries containing the term “agonist” are labeled as agonists, while those with “antagonist” are classified as antagonists. To ensure accuracy, all agonists and antagonists were cross-verified with their corresponding literature and experimental designs. Assays that solely measure binding affinities without specifying agonistic or antagonistic activity were excluded from further analysis. Data Cleansing Inputs and Outputs

There is one issue for the origin of the data. The original data sets are from ChEMBL database, which is a compilation and curation of comprehensive assay data. The data are from different sources, research methodologies and settings. They may be skewed due to the diversity of the data origins. Regarding this issue, we have the following countermeasures: during the data cleansing process, we have removed data sets with outlier values, data sets with “<” or “>” notations, and then transformed end point values to negative logarithmic values (pEC50, pIC50), which can significantly reduce the skewness. In QSAR modeling designs, we have exclusively implemented classification modeling, instead of regression modeling. Because in classification models, the end point values are further labeled as classes, using discrete, categorical values, unlike continuous, numerous values in regression models, which makes them more robust to data noise than regression models. Therefore, they are more reliable in the case of large data sets with diverse backgrounds.

Exploratory Data Analysis

A total of 6 physicochemical property descriptors were calculated for the exploratory data analysis. These molecular descriptors include: molecular weight (MW); octanol–water partition coefficient (LogP); number of hydrogen-bond acceptors (nHA); number of hydrogen-bond donors (nHD); number of rotatable bonds (nRot) and topological polar surface area (TPSA).

Chemical Space by Dimensionality Reduction and Clustering

T-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) is a nonlinear, stochastic technique used for dimensionality reduction, enabling high-dimensional data to be embedded into a low-dimensional space, typically two or three dimensions. In this study, the KlekotaRothCount fingerprint set was computed for compounds, consisting of 4860 columns (dimensions). These molecular fingerprints were then embedded into a 2D chemical space using t-SNE for visualization purposes. Following the t-SNE embedding, the compounds were further grouped into clusters using the k-means algorithm, which created subsets for subsequent QSAR modeling. The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined using the elbow plot method. To ensure reproducibility of the results, the t-SNE hyperparameters were set with a perplexity of 30 and a random state of 42.

Chemical Scaffold Analysis

In this study, Murcko scaffolds were extracted and analyzed. Additionally, the frequency of occurrence for both skeletons was ranked. RDKit was used to generate and visualize Murcko scaffolds, providing a clearer understanding of the structural patterns and trends in the compound data set.

Structure–Activity Relationship

Quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling is a computational approach used to establish empirical, linear, or nonlinear relationships between molecular descriptors or fingerprints derived from the molecular structures of compounds and their experimentally measured properties or bioactivities. QSAR models play a crucial role in predicting the biological activity of untested compounds, aiding in drug discovery, lead optimization, and toxicity assessment. These models are then applied to predict or design new chemical entities with desired properties or bioactivities.

In this study, QSAR models were developed for mu-opioid receptor (MOR), kappa-opioid receptor (KOR), and delta-opioid receptor (DOR) agonists and antagonists to facilitate the prediction and optimization of compounds with favorable bioactivity profiles. All QSAR models in this study are 2D fingerprint-based, ligand-centric classifiers. Specifically, we represent each compound using Klekota–Roth (KR) fingerprints, which encode the presence/absence of predefined 2D substructures derived from the molecular graph. No 3D conformers, alignment procedures, pharmacophore features, or receptor-structure information were used.

Prior to QSAR modeling, t-SNE clustering was applied to data sets containing a large number of compounds. Clustering the input data sets in QSAR modeling enhances predictive accuracy by partitioning heterogeneous ligand data sets into more homogeneous subsets. For opioid receptors such as MOR, KOR, and DOR, agonists and antagonists exhibit distinct structural and binding characteristics. By grouping structurally similar compounds, each QSAR model can focus on specific structural features relevant to its subset, improving pattern recognition and predictive performance. This tailored approach enhances model precision and interpretability by capturing receptor-specific binding trends more effectively than a generalized model. For smaller data sets, such as DOR antagonists (which contain only 132 compounds), clustering was not applied, as fewer data points make single-model training more stable and reliable. Overall, clustering ensures that QSAR models are better suited to predicting interactions within specific ligand subsets, thereby maximizing relevance and accuracy. To ensure the reproducibility of the results, a random state of 42 was set for each step of the modeling process.

Feature Selection

Feature selection is a critical step in machine learning, aimed at reducing the dimensionality of a training data set by preserving high-quality features closely related to the target values and eliminating irrelevant or redundant ones. This process is vital for building models that generalize well to unseen data. Effective feature selection can improve a model’s learning efficiency by reducing its complexity. There are several methods for performing feature selection.

In this study, the correlation-based filter method was employed: features with a variance lower than 0.1 and those demonstrating high correlation (greater than 0.95) were removed. To further refine the feature set and remove features of less importance, recursive feature elimination (RFE) technique was applied by ranking features based on their importance or correlation with the target variable, removing the least important ones, and repeating the process until optimal model performance is achieved. As a result of using RFE, the models retained the same level of performance, with some even showing improved outcomes compared to models without RFE. Potential biological bias from feature selection: The feature selection steps used here (variance filtering, correlation filtering, and recursive feature elimination) are designed to reduce dimensionality and redundancy for improved statistical generalization. However, these procedures can influence which chemical motifs are emphasized by the final models. Variance filtering may remove infrequent substructures that occur in only a small subset of compounds; such motifs can still be biologically important (e.g., scaffolds or substituents driving receptor engagement or affecting physicochemical properties relevant to CNS exposure). Correlation filtering may discard features that are highly correlated with others (common in substructure fingerprints), which can reduce redundancy but may also remove interpretable fragments that co-occur with key pharmacophores. Finally, RFE selects features that maximize performance on the modeled end point (agonist/antagonist class) and therefore does not guarantee retention of descriptors relevant to other biological properties such as CNS penetration or safety liabilities. Because the present study does not include explicit BBB/CNS penetration end points or receptor-structure interaction features, the selected fingerprint features should be interpreted primarily as predictive markers for the modeled activity labels rather than mechanistic determinants of CNS exposure or receptor binding. Future work could incorporate CNS-relevant end points (e.g., experimental BBB permeability or established physicochemical proxies) and evaluate feature stability under structure-aware splits to assess robustness across chemotypes.

Data Balancing and Splitting

In classification modeling, data imbalance can lead to poor predictions for the minority classes. To mitigate potential overfitting caused by data imbalance, data sets were balanced using the SMOTE (synthetic minority oversampling technique) oversampling method. This technique ensures that all bioactivity classes have equal counts in the model by generating synthetic data sets for the minority class. Following data balancing, data splitting was then applied to divide the data sets into training and test sets, with the default ratio set at 80:20. For each receptor/activity data set, compounds were split into training and test sets using random stratified splitting to preserve the proportion of class labels (agonist vs antagonist) across splits. The held-out test set was reserved exclusively for final evaluation and was not used for model selection, feature selection, or hyperparameter tuning. Model development and algorithm comparison were performed on the training set using stratified k-fold cross-validation (k = 10) and a fixed random seed for reproducibility. All reported CV metrics represent the mean (and standard deviation) across folds (and repeats where applicable). After selecting the preferred model configuration (see Model selection criteria), the final model was refitted on the full training set and evaluated once on the held-out test set. To reduce potential information leakage between training and test sets, we applied standard chemical data hygiene procedures prior to splitting. Specifically, we standardized molecular representations by salt/solvent removal, charge normalization, and canonicalization, removed exact duplicate structures (identical standardized SMILES) across the full data set, and ensured that duplicate entries were not split across training and test sets. Feature selection steps (variance filtering, correlation filtering, and recursive feature elimination) were carried out within the cross-validation procedure using training folds only, and then applied to the corresponding validation fold, to prevent information leakage from validation data into model fitting.

Applicability Domain Determination

The applicability domain (AD) defines the chemical structure and response space within which a QSAR model can generate reliable predictions. Establishing the AD is essential for evaluating the uncertainty associated with a given compound’s prediction by assessing its similarity to the training compounds used in model development. By defining these boundaries, the AD ensures that predictions are only made for compounds that fall within the scope of the model, thereby improving the reliability and interpretability of the results. , AD provides information about the boundaries, likelihood, applicability, and reliability of the model’s predictions. Evaluating the AD is crucial for determining whether a model can generalize effectively to a query compound.

In this study, bounding box method was applied to define the AD for the entire data set. This method assesses whether the test set compounds fall within the boundary of the training set. Particularly, the t-SNE embedding technique was used to visualize and determine the AD boundary.

Model Validation

Model validation consisted of both internal and external processes. To ensure robustness, reliability, and assess generalization, internal validation was performed within the training set using 10-fold cross-validation. This technique involves partitioning the training set into 10 folds, iteratively training the model on nine folds and validating it on the remaining fold. Subsequently, external validation was conducted by testing the model on an independent test set to evaluate its predictive performance on entirely new data. The model’s performance during these validation steps was quantified using three key metrics: accuracy (ac), the proportion of correctly classified instances; recall (re), also known as sensitivity, which measures the ability to identify true positives; and Matthew’s correlation coefficient (MCC), a balanced metric that accounts for true and false positives and negatives to yield a correlation coefficient between actual and predicted classifications. These metrics help evaluate both the correctness and balance of the model’s predictions across the different classes. Model acceptance and selection criteria. “To enable transparent comparison across algorithms and clusters, we defined explicit performance thresholds and a selection rule prior to downstream interpretation. Models were evaluated using training performance, cross-validation (CV) performance on the training set, and performance on an external held-out test set. A model was considered acceptable if it met all of the following criteria: (i) accuracy ≥0.90 (train), ≥0.80 (CV), and ≥0.80 (test); (ii) recall ≥0.90 (train), ≥0.80 (CV), and ≥0.80 (test); (iii) MCC ≥0.90 (train), ≥0.70 (CV), and ≥0.70 (test). These criteria were chosen to ensure high sensitivity (recall) while maintaining balanced performance under potential class imbalance (MCC). Among models that satisfied these criteria for a given receptor/dataset/cluster, the preferred model was selected by ranking primarily on mean CV MCC (higher is better). If multiple models had similar CV MCC, tie-breakers were applied in the following order: higher test MCC, smaller train–CV MCC gap (to reduce overfitting risk), and preference for the simpler model when performance was comparable. Only the preferred models were used for subsequent interpretation and discussion.”

MMP and MMP Cliff

In this study, a matched molecular pair (MMP) analysis was conducted to identify pairs of compounds that differ by a single structural modification and to assess the resulting changes in their biological activities. MMPs are defined as pairs of compounds that vary by a single, small chemical transformation, allowing for a systematic evaluation of the impact of specific structural changes on bioactivity. This analysis provides valuable insights into structure–activity relationships (SAR) and aids in the rational optimization of opioid receptor ligands by identifying key molecular modifications that enhance or diminish activity. MMP cliffs were identified within these MMPs as pairs where a small structural change resulted in a significant difference in bioactivity, defined in this study by a pEC50 or pIC50 change of at least 2.0. These ACs highlight critical structural features that significantly impact MOR agonist potency. The MMP and AC analysis was conducted using SMILES strings to generate molecular pairs, followed by the calculation of Tanimoto similarity and pEC50 (or pIC50) differences. This analysis provided inspiring insights into structural modifications that enhance activity.

Results and Discussion

In this study, chemical space of opioid receptor agonists and antagonists was explored through visualization of physicochemical property space, dimensionality reduction, drug-likeness profiling, Murcko scaffold analysis, structure–activity relationship (SAR) modelings, and MMP cliff identification through SALI plots.

Physicochemical Property Space

To assess whether fundamental molecular descriptors influence bioactivity, we compared the physicochemical parameters across the four activity groups (Figure ). Statistical analysis using the Kruskal–Wallis H-test revealed no significant differences among the groups for any of the parameters tested, including molecular weight (MW), AlogP, nHA, nHD, nRot, and TPSA (all). These results indicate that the physicochemical property space is relatively homogeneous across all bioactivity levels. Consequently, the differences in potency observed in this data set are likely driven by specific structural motifs and binding interactions rather than global property-based biases. Given that global physicochemical properties do not differentiate the bioactivity classes, we applied t-SNE dimensionality reduction to KlekotaRothCount fingerprints (4860 features) to capture more nuanced structural relationships. This high-dimensional embedding provides a visualization of the chemical space that reflects the underlying structural complexity of the opioid receptor ligands, moving beyond simple molecular descriptors. Figure represents the joint chemical space of agonists targeting the three opioid receptors, visualized by t-SNE embedding. This representation provides an overview of how compounds are distributed in reduced-dimensional space according to receptor class (MOR, KOR, and DOR) and bioactivity level. The plot shows that compounds are not randomly dispersed but instead form local groupings and transitions that reflect underlying structural and bioactivity-related similarities. In particular, highly active compounds tend to occupy more localized regions, whereas compounds with low activity or inactivity are more broadly distributed, suggesting that the molecular descriptors retain meaningful information related to agonist potency. This pattern indicates that the embedding captures broad structure–activity relationships across the combined data set. Although some regions may appear locally enriched for compounds associated with a given receptor, substantial overlap remains among MOR, KOR, and DOR compounds, including among highly active agonists. This overlap suggests that structurally related compounds may share chemical features relevant to agonist activity across more than one opioid receptor. Therefore, the t-SNE plot is more appropriately interpreted as illustrating broad activity-related organization in chemical space rather than providing direct evidence of receptor selectivity. Compounds with intermediate activity often appear in transitional regions between highly active and inactive areas, further supporting the presence of gradual structure–activity trends. Overall, Figure highlights general relationships between molecular structure and agonist activity, while receptor selectivity cannot be concluded reliably from this visualization alone.

Chemical Scaffold Analysis

The chemical scaffold analysis in this study aims to visualize and assess the structural diversity of Murcko scaffolds through RDKit. In Figure , the top five frequent Murcko scaffolds within the MOR agonist data set are illustrated. Scaffold 1, with a high count of 40, contains a piperazine ring, which is often linked to CNS pharmacology, which suggests that it may contribute positively to bioactivity, making it a promising scaffold for further optimization. Scaffold 2 consists of a pyrrolidine ring and amide bond, likely enhancing hydrogen bond formation and membrane permeability. Its moderate presence indicates that it could provide specific activity or selectivity within certain compound series. Scaffold 3 includes an indoline ring, which is a known privileged structure for its bioactivity, suggesting it may also contribute positively. Scaffold 4 has a piperidine ring and ester group, which are commonly present in bioactive compounds. While versatile, its stability might vary due to the ester linkage. Scaffold 5 is a complicated polycyclic structure with stereochemistry that might offer selective bioactivity due to its structural rigidity, though it appears less frequently. In summary, scaffolds 1 and 2 are widely represented and may positively contribute to broad bioactivities. Scaffolds 3 and 4 could provide moderate, versatile activity, while scaffold 5 might be valuable for specific, high-affinity interactions. Further evaluation of bioactivity data associated with each scaffold would clarify their roles in bioactivity optimization.

MMP Cliff Analysis

Figure demonstrates how specific structural modifications in MMP affect their potency as MOR agonists. In SAR analysis, certain structural featuressuch as functional groups, linkers, and electronic propertiesare known to directly influence biological activity. Here, we demonstrate that in the first pair, CHEMBL4748910 versus CHEMBL4754352, a modification near the ring is likely to enhance hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions within the MOR binding pocket. This adjustment results in a significant increase in potency, with CHEMBL4754352 exhibiting a higher pEC50 value. Similarly, in the second pair, CHEMBL5081955 and CHEMBL5087973, a slight alteration in the linker structure between the pharmacophoric groups appears to optimize the molecule’s spatial arrangement within the receptor site, further increasing potency. According to SAR principles, such modifications enhance binding stability by improving molecular fit and interaction strength, thereby transforming each compound into a more effective agonist. This SAR-driven interpretation illustrates how targeted changes to structural componentssuch as substituent placement and linker flexibilityplay a critical role in optimizing MOR agonist activity, guiding the design of more potent therapeutic agents. However, it is important to note that this is only one part of a broader analysis. Following the analysis of MMPs and MMP cliffs, which highlighted key structural features influencing MOR agonist potency, we proceed with quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling to further analyze structure–activity relationships. While the MMP and AC analysis provided inspirational SAR insights, identifying structural modifications associated with bioactivities, QSAR modeling enabled us to systematically analyze and predict the activity of compounds based on their structure information. Since structure dictates activities, this integrated approach enabled a deeper understanding of the structural determinants of bioactivities.

Having developed and validated a series of QSAR models to predict the bioactivities of compounds targeting MOR, KOR, and DOR receptors, we now turn to discuss these findings within the broader landscape of opioid receptor drug discovery. By discussing recent developments, we can better understand how our study fits into and potentially advances current strategies, as well as comparing our study with previous related studies, offering tools for rapid screening and structural optimization to facilitate opioid receptor-targeted therapies.

Recent Advances and Challenges in Cheminformatics of Opioids

Overall, the 2016–2026 literature reflects a clear expansion of opioid QSAR and cheminformatics from relatively small, receptor-focused studies to broader and more integrated modeling frameworks. Recent work has increasingly combined public bioactivity repositories, qHTS data sets, machine learning, and structure-informed descriptors to support prediction of opioid receptor binding, receptor subtype selectivity, functional class, and, in some cases, pharmacokinetic properties of synthetic opioids and related metabolites. − Across these studies, common modeling end points include Ki, IC50, EC50, binding, and signaling readouts, while methodological strategies range from classical QSAR and pharmacophore modeling to graph-based learning, transfer learning, and hybrid ligand–structure approaches. , Applicability-domain analysis, confidence estimation, and external validation have also become increasingly visible components of opioid modeling workflows, particularly in studies addressing diverse chemical space or emerging fentanyl-like analogs. In parallel, interpretability has been supported through descriptor analysis, docking-derived interaction patterns, ligand–receptor contact fingerprints, and saliency-based approaches, which together provide additional context for understanding predicted receptor activity and selectivity. , Collectively, these studies indicate that opioid cheminformatics is evolving toward more comprehensive, data-rich, and translationally oriented frameworks, with growing attention to receptor pharmacology, structural context, and prospective utility in ligand discovery and profiling. In summary, the body of work published during 2016–2026 shows that opioid-focused QSAR and cheminformatics have become increasingly diverse in both data scale and methodological scope. The field now encompasses ligand-based ML, 3D-QSAR, docking-informed modeling, transfer learning, graph-based neural networks, generative design, and QSAR-supported PBPK applications, reflecting a broader effort to connect molecular structure with receptor activity, selectivity, efficacy, and drug-like behavior across multiple opioid-related contexts. Together, these studies illustrate a transition from isolated predictive models toward more integrated workflows that combine curation, representation learning, structural interpretation, and experimental or translational follow-up, thereby strengthening the role of cheminformatics in opioid ligand discovery, profiling, and risk-oriented evaluation.

Compared to previous studies, there has been limited research on the chemical space and SAR of opioid receptor agonists and antagonists. For instance, Giuseppe Floresta et al. developed regression models based on fentanyl scaffolds, while Jia et al. used PubChem bioassay data to build regression models. Additionally, several studies have focused on QSAR models for sigma receptor ligands. − In contrast, this study incorporated opioid receptor modulators from more diverse origins and employed classification modeling rather than regression modeling, providing a broader scope of analysis. The main limitation of this study is due to the inherent nature of computational research. All the data sets utilized were retrospective, meaning they were collected from pre-existing studies rather than generated through ongoing experiments. This approach excludes any newly emerging experimental data that might be relevant to the study’s focus, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the findings. Moreover, the study relies entirely on computational analyses and modeling. While these methods are robust for generating hypotheses and identifying patterns, they lack experimental confirmation. These experimental applications are critical for verifying the accuracy and biological relevance of computational predictions. Future research can address these limitations by incorporating experimental validation to strengthen the study’s conclusions. This could involve designing experiments to test the computational findings in real-world biological systems, thereby providing a more robust and translational foundation for the results. Such efforts would ensure that the study’s insights are not only theoretically sound but also applicable in practical scenarios. Another limitation is that adverse effects (e.g., respiratory depression, constipation, dependence liability) are multifactorial and not captured by agonist/antagonist classification alone; dedicated safety end points and off-target profiling would be required to model these risks.

Conclusions

This computational study explores the chemical landscape of agonists and antagonists targeting the three major opioid receptorsmu-opioid receptor (MOR), kappa-opioid receptor (KOR), and delta-opioid receptor (DOR)by analyzing their physicochemical properties, Murcko scaffolds, and structure–activity relationships (SARs). Using data sets retrieved from the ChEMBL database, the study focuses on IC50 and EC50 values, compiling six data sets and mapping them within chemical space through t-SNE dimensionality reduction and embedding, utilizing the Klekota-Roth Count fingerprint. Within this chemical space, patterns in the distribution of selective and nonselective ligands are identified. To enhance QSAR modeling, compounds within each data set are clustered using k-means based on t-SNE outputs, improving predictive accuracy across multiple clusters. Additionally, representative matched molecular pairs (MMPs), and MMP cliffs are analyzed to highlight key structural modifications influencing bioactivity. The insights derived from this study contribute to the rational design of opioid receptor modulators, providing a foundation for future drug discovery efforts aimed at optimizing opioid receptor agonists and antagonists for therapeutic use.
