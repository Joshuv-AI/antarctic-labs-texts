Factors influencing the provision of human milk feeding support for preterm infants in Neonatal Intensive Care Units in China: a qualitative study of neonatal nurses’ perspectives
Authors: Wenjie Guo, Haiyan Zhang, Li Wang, Fang Huang, Dongmei Ma, Maggie Zgambo, Sanmei Chen, Yan Wu, Yanmei Bu, Yoko Shimpuku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman milk feeding is crucial for the survival and development of preterm infants, yet their human milk feeding rates at Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICUs) discharge remain lower than those of healthy newborns. Effective support is essential for improving breast milk feeding outcomes, but remains challenging for NICU nurses. Therefore, this study aimed to examine factors that influence NICU nurses in providing human milk feeding support, including the challenges they face and their needs.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative descriptive design. Using maximum variation and purposive sampling, 19 nurses in the NICU were recruited. Between August and September 2024, semi-structured interviews were conducted in the NICUs of two tertiary public hospitals in Jiaozuo, China. The interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using a thematic analysis approach.ResultsThis study highlighted the broader context of human milk feeding support in NICUs, influenced by institutional policies that limit parental involvement and the cultural practice of postpartum confinement, which keeps mothers at home after delivery. It underscored the key challenges NICU nurses faced in providing human milk feeding support in China. Against this backdrop, three main themes were identified, representing factors that influence human milk feeding support at the levels of preterm infants and their families, healthcare professionals, and institutions.ConclusionsThis qualitative study identified multiple modifiable factors influencing human milk feeding support for mothers of preterm infants in NICUs, especially offering new insights into the significant role of NICU management policy and postpartum culture. Future interventions should prioritize creating a family-friendly NICU environment and adopting culturally sensitive education and support strategies to address the unique challenges posed by institutional and cultural barriers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-025-00778-6.

Background

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, is the leading cause of neonatal mortality in children under five globally [1]. It also increases the risk of growth impairments, neurodevelopmental disabilities, and chronic diseases in survivors [2, 3]. Preterm birth remains a significant challenge to healthcare systems worldwide, with prevalence rates either stagnant or rising in some countries, such as China, the United States, and Pakistan, among others [4, 5].

Preterm infants are classified by gestational age as extremely preterm (< 28 weeks), very preterm (28–<32 weeks), and moderate-to-late preterm (32–<37 weeks), with risks of complications increasing at lower gestational ages [1]. Infants born earlier are highly susceptible to complications such as late-onset sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), and ventilator-associated pneumonia [6, 7], and are usually managed in Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICUs) [8]. However, NICU environments often do not support family-centered care [9], leading to separation of mothers and infants. This separation negatively affects mother-infant bonding and disrupts human milk feeding [10, 11], resulting in insufficient milk supply, breast engorgement, delayed lactogenesis, and even feeding failure, which can hinder infant growth and development [11, 12]. Human milk is a key intervention to enhance immune and nutritional status and prevent infections [6, 7]. Nevertheless, preterm infants in NICUs are less likely to receive human milk, and for shorter durations, compared with full-term infants both during hospitalization and after discharge [13, 14].

To mitigate the adverse effects of NICU separation on human milk feeding, countries such as Canada and Australia have implemented Family Integrated Care, which enables parents to spend several hours daily with their infants, thereby enhancing bonding and feeding outcomes [15, 16]. In contrast, Chinese NICUs often restrict parental involvement due to limited resources and high patient volumes. A survey of 44 hospitals reported that only 65.9% permitted parental participation during the 1–to 2–week period before discharge [17]. Cultural practices also play a role. “Doing the Month” (DTM), a traditional postpartum practice, requires mothers to stay at home for about one month after childbirth [18], and about 60% of Chinese women still adhere strictly to these customs [19, 20]. This tradition often results in mother-infant separation, making direct breastfeeding difficult and shifting caregiving responsibilities, such as milk delivery and NICU visits, to fathers [11, 17, 18]. Consequently, many mothers rely on pumping or manual expression, which is less effective than direct breastfeeding [21]. These factors contribute to the low exclusive human milk feeding rate among preterm infants in China, reported as 18.8% at NICU discharge, declining to 17.2% at 3 months and 10.4% at 6 months, compared with 36.2% among full-term infants before 6 months [22–24]. Greater efforts are needed to improve human milk feeding outcomes for preterm infants in China, starting during NICU hospitalization.

Healthcare professionals, particularly NICU nurses, play a crucial role in supporting human milk feeding by counseling mothers, facilitating skin-to-skin contact and kangaroo care, and promoting on-demand feeding [25, 26]. Without such support, mothers of preterm infants are more likely to encounter feeding difficulties and early cessation [27, 28]. However, surveys in China have shown that healthcare professionals often lack sufficient knowledge and confidence in supporting human milk feeding for preterm infants, especially in smaller NICUs [29, 30]. Similar challenges have been reported internationally, with studies in Italy, Sweden, and Jordan highlighting knowledge gaps, limited confidence, and concerns about potential harm [31–33]. Collectively, these findings suggest that NICU nurses in China often face substantial challenges in providing effective human milk feeding support; however, evidence on the specific barriers and needs in this context remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to explore NICU nurses’ perceptions of factors influencing human milk feeding support for mothers of preterm infants in China, focusing on the barriers they face and their support needs.

Methods

Design

A qualitative descriptive design was employed in this study as it facilitates the exploration and description of participants’ perspectives, processes, or relevant phenomena [34, 35]. This approach aligns with the study’s purpose, which is to understand nurses’ perspectives on the factors influencing human milk feeding support for preterm infants in China, as it yields detailed information about specific events [36]. To ensure comprehensive reporting, the study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist (Additional file 1) [37].

Setting

This study was conducted in the NICUs of two tertiary public hospitals in Jiaozuo, a medium-sized city in central China with a population of approximately 3.5 million. The general hospital (12 beds, 12 nurses, not designated as Baby-Friendly) accepted milk deliveries twice weekly (Tuesdays and Fridays, 15:00–16:00), while the maternal and child specialty hospital (48 beds, 39 nurses, Baby-Friendly) accepted daily deliveries at the same time. Both had milk preparation rooms, but neither had lactation consultants or milk banks; nurses provided feeding guidance at admission and discharge. In the 1–2 weeks before discharge, mothers were allowed 1–2 h per session in the NICU for Kangaroo Care and direct breastfeeding.

Participants

Purposive sampling [38] was used to recruit nurses who (a) were able to speak fluent Chinese, (b) held a nurse professional qualification certificate, (c) were actively registered with the practice regulatory body, (d) were currently working in a NICU, (e) had at least six months of NICU experience, and (f) had direct responsibility for providing patient care, which allowed for meaningful reflections and ensured that rich data were collected for analysis. The maximum variation sampling technique [39] was also employed to recruit a diverse group of nurses based on age, education, nurses’ personal breastfeeding experience, years working in the NICU, position, and professional title.

The researchers contacted potential participants, who were identified as eligible by the nursing department heads based on the inclusion criteria, either by phone or in person, to explain the study’s purpose, procedures, and participation requirements, and to answer any initial questions. To minimize any perceived pressure, the researchers emphasized that participation was entirely voluntary, refusal or withdrawal would have no negative consequences, and individual responses would remain confidential. Those who expressed interest in participating were then provided with detailed written information about the study and asked to sign an informed consent form. The sample size was determined by data saturation [40], which is reached when additional interviews no longer provide new insights.

Data collection

Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted by a single researcher (WG) between August and September 2024 to ensure consistency. The researcher reviewed relevant literature, received guidance from experienced qualitative experts, and practiced interview skills before data collection. An interview guide (Additional file 2), developed from the study aims and literature, contained six open-ended questions on demographic characteristics, experiences, and perceived challenges and needs in supporting human milk feeding. The guide was piloted with three participants and, as no revisions were required, their data were included in the analysis.

Interviews were scheduled after obtaining consent, and the interview time was negotiated between the interviewer and the participant. The interviews were conducted in Chinese, in a quiet and private office within the NICU. Each session lasted between 22 and 103 min, with a mean duration of 54 min. During the interviews, the researcher audio-recorded the entire conversation.

Data analysis

The recordings were transcribed verbatim in Chinese within 24 h of the interviews by the first researcher (WG), who was fully engaged in the data throughout the process, taking notes, rereading the transcripts, and uploading them into NVivo 12 for analysis. The data were translated from Chinese into English after collection and initial organization by the interviewer (WG), a PhD candidate and native Chinese speaker proficient in English, to develop the codebook for analysis. To ensure translation accuracy, the interviewer conducted multiple rounds of back-translation and consistency checks. Additionally, the translations were also reviewed and verified by the co-author SC, who is fluent in both Chinese and English.

An inductive thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s six phases: (1) familiarization with the data through repeated reading, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report [41]. This flexible method was chosen for its effectiveness in identifying and interpreting patterns, aligning well with the interpretive descriptive approach.

Two female researchers (YS and WG), both registered nurses with extensive maternal and infant research experience and not involved in patient care at the study sites, independently coded the transcripts. They then discussed discrepancies and reached consensus, grouping codes into sub-themes and refining these into overarching themes. Interpretations were continuously checked against the transcripts to ensure alignment with participants’ perspectives, and adjustments were made as needed to enhance coherence and clarity.

Rigour

We followed Lincoln and Guba’s (1989) strategies for strengthening rigour in qualitative research, which include criteria for credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [42]. To enhance credibility, the first author spent one week before data collection observing and familiarizing herself with the NICU environments in both hospitals, gaining contextual insight to inform data collection and analysis. Additionally, a peer debriefing session was conducted with research team members familiar with the study context. During this session, findings, study design, and emerging themes were presented. Feedback received helped identify potential biases and refine the analysis, thereby strengthening the study’s credibility. To strengthen the dependability and confirmability, we maintained a comprehensive audit trail across all research stages. We practiced reflexivity by explicitly acknowledging and addressing preconceived notions and personal biases during data collection and analysis. The transferability of our findings was supported by rich, detailed descriptions obtained through in-depth interviews.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Boards of the Second People’s Hospital of Jiaozuo City (Ref: KY2024-03-130) and the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Jiaozuo City (Ref: 2024-K-Y-0408). All participants provided verbal and written consent, and data were accessible only to the research team.

Themes

The factors contributing to the provision of human milk feeding support for preterm infants in NICUs were multidimensional. The following themes were identified during data analysis: preterm infant and family-level factors, health care professional-level factors, and institution-level factors. These themes and sub-themes are listed in Fig. 1 and explained as follows.

Theme 1: Preterm infant and family-level factors

Nurses emphasized that family involvement is crucial for providing human milk feeding support in NICUs. Families ensure the timely delivery of safe and sufficient milk, while nurses manage storage, processing, and feeding. Key factors include the infant’s feeding readiness, the mother’s physical condition, and caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, practices, and resources, all of which affect the safety and effectiveness of human milk feeding support provided by NICU nurses.

Subtheme 1.1: Physical factors in mothers and infants

Physical factors like the mother’s milk production and the preterm infant’s feeding readiness were crucial for human milk feeding support in NICUs. Nurses must adjust their care based on the mother’s milk supply and the infant’s digestibility.

Most NICU nurses reported that many mothers of preterm infants experienced delayed lactation and insufficient milk production due to their bodies not being fully prepared for lactation and the prolonged separation from their infants. These challenges significantly hindered their ability to provide timely and continuous human milk feeding support to preterm infants.Some mothers of preterm infants have suboptimal physical conditions, leading to preterm birth and delayed lactation, sometimes until 3–4 days postpartum. Many also struggle with insufficient milk supply, making it hard to meet their infants’ nutritional needs and affecting human milk feeding support in the NICU. (N15)

Some mothers of preterm infants have suboptimal physical conditions, leading to preterm birth and delayed lactation, sometimes until 3–4 days postpartum. Many also struggle with insufficient milk supply, making it hard to meet their infants’ nutritional needs and affecting human milk feeding support in the NICU. (N15)

Preterm infants, particularly those born very or extremely preterm, often have limited coordination of sucking, swallowing, and breathing, and immature gastrointestinal function, which may hinder the initiation and continuation of human milk feeding and pose challenges to support strategies.For extremely preterm and very preterm infants, we encourage families to prioritize feeding expressed human milk to reduce the risk of complications such as abdominal distension, diminished bowel sounds, and NEC. However, such complications may still occur, sometimes requiring a temporary pause in enteral feeding and making full enteral nutrition difficult. (N11)

For extremely preterm and very preterm infants, we encourage families to prioritize feeding expressed human milk to reduce the risk of complications such as abdominal distension, diminished bowel sounds, and NEC. However, such complications may still occur, sometimes requiring a temporary pause in enteral feeding and making full enteral nutrition difficult. (N11)

Subtheme 1.2: Limitations in caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors

NICU nurses noted that limitations in family caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices—such as mothers’ conflicting attitudes with medical advice on human milk feeding decisions, inadequate knowledge and skills in providing breast milk, low adherence, and postpartum cultural beliefs—could reduce family involvement and cooperation with nurses, thereby hindering effective support for human milk feeding.

NICU nurses noted that some mothers were deeply concerned about the safety of their breast milk, especially if they had infections, took medication, or their infant developed NEC. Fearing harm to their baby despite medical reassurance, many chose to stop human milk feeding against advice. These concerns made it harder for nurses to provide support, as they had to first dispel misconceptions and reduce anxiety. When anxiety was severe, nurses often felt powerless to change mothers’ decisions, limiting the education, emotional support, and guidance they could provide.Some mothers with hepatitis B or syphilis can breast milk feed according to guidelines, but concerns may lead them to avoid it. As a result, mothers with infectious diseases may choose not to provide breast milk…In such cases, there are no effective solutions to change their decision. (N7)

Some mothers with hepatitis B or syphilis can breast milk feed according to guidelines, but concerns may lead them to avoid it. As a result, mothers with infectious diseases may choose not to provide breast milk…In such cases, there are no effective solutions to change their decision. (N7)

The nurses emphasized that some mothers of preterm infants, particularly those around 20 years old, often lacked knowledge and skills in providing breast milk. This frequently led to difficulties in maintaining an adequate milk supply and added complexity to supporting human milk feeding for preterm infants. Nurses emphasized the importance of strengthening both prenatal and postnatal education.Very young mothers, under 20, often lack human milk feeding knowledge, which can lead to a reduced milk supply…They need more education on human milk feeding. (N2)Strengthening training for mothers can make the entire process of providing human milk during hospitalization smoother — for example, by offering prenatal training through antenatal human milk feeding classes. (N6)

Family members of preterm infants often exhibited low adherence to nursing guidelines on human milk expression, storage, and transportation. Almost all nurses noted that strong postpartum confinement practices (DTM) often led to maternal absence during preterm infants’ hospitalization, reducing mother-infant contact and communication with healthcare providers. This absence limited maternal participation in educational programs. It also restricted various forms of human milk feeding support that nurses could provide to mothers of preterm infants, such as face-to-face counseling, hands-on guidance, and emotional support.We rarely meet the mother, as she stays at home for postpartum confinement, and most interactions are with other family members. However, direct breastfeeding education for the mother is more effective than educating family members. (N4)

Subtheme 1.3: Family logistical factors

Nurses reported that families faced logistical challenges, including geographic distance from the hospital and competing family responsibilities. These factors limited caregivers’ availability and timely human milk delivery, thereby disrupting the continuity of NICU nurses’ human milk feeding support and complicating the implementation of the human milk feeding plan.Some families live far from the hospital and cannot deliver human milk daily. In such cases, they are only able to deliver it every two or three days. (N7)Sometimes the fathers need to work and don’t have time, so the human milk is not delivered. (N5)

Theme 2: Healthcare professional-level factors

Healthcare professional-level factors encompassed the care philosophy and professional competence of healthcare providers in delivering human milk feeding support. These factors significantly influenced the quality of care provided to mothers and infants.

Subtheme 2.1: Disease-centered care philosophy

Nurses noted that the disease-centered care philosophy within the neonatal care system, which prioritizes immediate medical treatment over integrated nutritional and developmental interventions, shaped the overall NICU environment and resulted in human milk feeding support not being fully integrated into routine care.We don’t usually get involved in guiding mothers; this should mainly be the responsibility of the obstetrics and gynecology department. We rarely have the chance to meet mothers, so we focus on the care of neonatal illnesses in our unit. (N14)

Theme 3: Institution-level factors

Institution-level factors, including NICU management policies, human milk feeding support processes, and hospital resources, directly impacted human milk feeding support. Nurses highlighted that the restricted NICU parental accompaniment policy, fragmented procedures, and limited resources hindered effective support for human milk feeding.

Subtheme 3.1: Restricted NICU parental accompaniment policy

Both NICUs enforced a restricted parental accompaniment policy, limiting preterm infant access and allowing mothers only brief interactions, such as kangaroo care and direct breastfeeding, for about an hour during the 1–2 weeks before discharge. This led to prolonged mother-infant separation and hindered nurse-mother communication, significantly complicating NICU nurses’ human milk feeding support.A challenge in human milk feeding support is the limited interaction with family members, as they are not allowed to accompany newborns most of the time. Communication over the phone is insufficient. (N1)

A challenge in human milk feeding support is the limited interaction with family members, as they are not allowed to accompany newborns most of the time. Communication over the phone is insufficient. (N1)

To address these challenges, NICU nurses recommended creating a supportive environment for human milk feeding through family-friendly NICUs, which would promote parental involvement and enhance nurse-family communication.Establishing family rooms could improve early mother-infant contact, allowing human milk feeding to begin sooner and strengthening the mother’s confidence in human milk feeding. (N11)

Subtheme 3.2: Fragmented human milk feeding support process

The human milk feeding support process in the NICU was fragmented due to the lack of clear protocols for colostrum administration, restricted milk reception times that disrupted feeding continuity, inadequate follow-up with families during hospitalization, and poor interdisciplinary collaboration. These challenges fragmented the support system, reducing its overall effectiveness.

Although nurses recognized the importance of colostrum for preterm infants, the absence of standardized procedures and requirements for oropharyngeal colostrum administration hindered timely human milk feeding support. It might have resulted in missed opportunities to improve infant health outcomes.Oropharyngeal administration of colostrum has not become a routine practice in our department; this is not part of our standard medical orders. (N11)

However, both NICUs adopted fixed reception times, which were associated with delays in feeding preterm infants, reduced flexibility for nurses in coordinating feedings, and missed opportunities for breast milk intake. Subtheme 3.3: Insufficient staff and facility resources

Nurses emphasized that insufficient staffing and inadequate facilities pose significant barriers to providing effective human milk feeding support, critically undermining both the accessibility and quality of care for families in the NICU.

Insufficient human resources posed significant challenges to human milk feeding support, as heavy workloads and staffing shortages limited human milk reception times, family health education, human milk feeding training, communication, and continuous follow-up. Discussion

This study explored factors influencing human milk feeding support for preterm infants in NICUs from the perspectives of neonatal nurses in China. Data analysis identified three key themes, reflecting factors at the levels of the preterm infant and family, healthcare professionals, and institutions. The findings highlight barriers nurses faced and their support needs within the context of restrictive parental accompaniment policies and cultural beliefs about maternal recovery.

Preterm infant and family-level factors

Preterm infant and family-related factors directly influence how NICU nurses deliver human milk feeding support. Preterm infants often have immature digestion, requiring medications, fortifiers, or supplements, which can cause feeding intolerance, delayed digestion, and higher NEC risk [43]. These challenges limit direct breastfeeding and shift nurses’ focus to alternative feeding and close monitoring [43], necessitating cautious, individualized strategies [32]. Mothers may also experience delayed lactation and low milk supply, particularly older mothers, those with smaller infants, or pregnancy complications [44], which delays feeding interventions and increases the need for tailored education, emotional support, and ongoing reassessment.

Caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices affect human milk feeding support, as nurses view the family as a unit with each member contributing [32]; gaps in caregivers’ understanding can hinder feeding and complicate nurses’ support [28, 45]. Cultural practices such as DTM may limit early maternal involvement, reducing mother–infant contact and delaying feeding [17]. Its impact remains controversial, and further evidence is needed to clarify effects on maternal and infant outcomes during NICU hospitalization [46].

In this study, restricted milk delivery schedules and competing family responsibilities often disrupted the timely provision of human milk, leading to greater formula use and frustration among nurses. This aligns with research showing that restricted visitation and frequent travel reduce NICU involvement, especially among families with limited resources, long commutes, jobs, or other children [47, 48].

Health care professional-level factors

In both NICUs, clinical priorities focused more on treating illness than supporting human milk feeding, which was not viewed as standard care. A qualitative study in Jordan also found that, despite recognizing its benefits, staff often prioritized medical procedures over milk support due to time and workload pressures [33]. Additionally, nurses’ professional competencies—including knowledge, skills, and experience—are critical to effective human milk feeding support in the NICU. Studies have shown that higher human milk feeding knowledge among healthcare professionals is associated with better feeding outcomes [25, 31, 49]. However, current evidence indicates that NICU nurses often have limited knowledge and receive insufficient training [29–31]. Limited expertise can hinder support and lead to adverse outcomes [50]. This issue is particularly pronounced among younger and less experienced nurses, who often lack confidence [49, 50]. Importantly, supporting human milk feeding in the NICU requires specialized skills that differ from those needed for healthy, full-term infants, due to distinct risk factors and management needs [51]. Therefore, education programs for NICU nurses should be specifically designed following a standardized framework to better equip nurses in supporting families and promoting human milk feeding in the NICU [51, 52].

Institution-level factors

Restricted parental access in NICUs, driven by infection control, patient volume, and resource limits, reduces face-to-face communication with families, especially mothers, hindering human milk feeding support. Previous studies have shown that in China, parental visitation in most NICUs is strictly limited, with parents generally prohibited from entering the unit and having little direct contact with their infants [17, 44]. To address this, a family-friendly environment, supported by hospital policies, is needed to enable mothers’ early and unrestricted involvement in NICU care.

Nurses in the two Chinese NICUs reported a lack of multidisciplinary teams for human milk feeding support and highlighted the need for stronger collaboration with obstetrics and hospital administration. Active involvement of IBCLCs is essential, as multidisciplinary teams have been shown to improve feeding rates among preterm infants [53] and are widely implemented in countries such as Canada and the United States [33, 54]. However, such teams remain limited in Chinese NICUs, with only 56.8% present, mostly neonatologists and nurses, with a shortage of IBCLCs [17], a problem also reported globally due to training and certification costs [55].

Nurses reported that staff shortages and heavy workloads limit their ability to support human milk feeding and monitor mothers’ lactation. In busy settings, nurses tend to prioritize technical tasks over feeding support, leading to missed care, more stress for novice nurses, and increased formula use for infants needing human milk [25, 33, 56]. These issues reduce the quality of feeding support in NICUs. Additionally, a lack of equipment like refrigerators and sterilizers in maternity wards hinders colostrum collection and oropharyngeal administration, weakening feeding support. Heavy workloads also increase occupational stress, further affecting care quality [25, 56].

The non-baby-friendly hospital limited milk deliveries to twice a week, whereas the baby-friendly hospital allowed daily deliveries, providing greater flexibility. This reflects differences in human milk feeding support policies and management practices. Previous studies have reported that non-baby-friendly hospitals face more barriers, such as higher costs, staff resistance, competing priorities, and inadequate infrastructure, compared with baby-friendly hospitals [57]. In line with this, the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative designation is known to increase human milk feeding rates among mothers, suggesting that more supportive policies in baby-friendly hospitals may enhance human milk feeding [57, 58]. To further improve support, hospitals should strengthen staff training and secure sufficient resources, particularly in non-baby-friendly settings.

Although three independent factors were identified, these findings indicate that the challenges reported by nurses are possibly primarily driven by structural barriers in institutional approaches to human milk feeding, rather than by limitations of mothers or families. These barriers, including restrictive visitation policies, insufficient staffing and resources, and limited training, undermine both nurses’ ability to provide support and individuals’ capacity to initiate and maintain breastfeeding. While nurses recognized some of these obstacles, they may not fully appreciate the extent to which systemic factors contribute to poor human milk feeding outcomes, including limited opportunities for human milk feeding education and support. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of systemic changes in institutional policies and practices to prioritize human milk feeding in NICU care [7, 59].

Implications for policy and practice

Human milk feeding support from NICU nurses is critical for preterm infants’ outcomes. Factors influencing this support include infant and family needs, healthcare professionals, and institutional policies. Restricted parental access and traditional postpartum practices limit nurse-family interactions, highlighting the need for family-friendly NICUs, open visitation, family-integrated care, and culturally sensitive education [60]. Nurses’ professional competence is also essential; targeted training in human milk feeding, combining theory, practice, and communication skills, can improve guidance and avoid mixed messages [51, 53]. Strengthening collaboration, expanding the IBCLC workforce, and supporting multidisciplinary NICU teams are key strategies. Heavy workloads, staff shortages, and limited facilities hinder support, so hospitals should increase resources and provide practical assistance, such as rest areas, breast pumps, and childcare, while equally promoting a culture that prioritizes human milk through education, standardized policies, and unit-wide lactation support, especially in non–Baby-Friendly NICUs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. All participants were female NICU nurses from tertiary hospitals in a single Chinese city, which limited the transferability of the findings. Future research should include male nurses, hospitals from diverse regions and levels, and other stakeholders such as doctors, midwives, IBCLCs, and families to provide a broader perspective. Member checking was not conducted, which may limit confirmation of participants’ intended meanings; however, credibility was enhanced through NICU observation and peer debriefing.

Conclusions

This study offers valuable insights into the factors that affect NICU nurses’ ability to support human milk feeding for preterm infants in China, revealing multiple complex influences that can be addressed through targeted interventions. The nurses identified restricted parental accompaniment policies and postpartum confinement practices as major barriers in China. They called for creating family-friendly NICU environments and adopting culturally sensitive strategies to overcome these challenges. Strengthening human milk feeding training, especially for younger nurses, and increasing investment in staffing and infrastructure were also emphasized. Achieving these goals requires collaboration among families, healthcare professionals, and policymakers to improve maternal and infant health support.
