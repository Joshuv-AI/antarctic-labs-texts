Clinical accuracy of cephalometric analysis using deep learning–based automated landmark identification on CBCT in class I and class II malocclusions
Authors: Yan Jiang, Rana A. A. M. AL-Mohana, Canyang Jiang, Xiaojing Zhang, Bin Shi, You Wu, Xinghao Wang, Jianping Huang, Xiaohong Huang, Lisong Lin, Li Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to perform three-dimensional (3D) cephalometric analysis based on automatically identified landmarks, to evaluate their clinical accuracy, and to investigate the relationship between algorithmic precision, measured by mean radial error (MRE), and clinical validity. Retrospective cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans representing diverse dentition stages and malocclusion types were used to develop an automated landmark identification model incorporating an optimized U-Net architecture with an Efficient Global Attention module. Seventy-one 3D cephalometric measurements derived from manually annotated landmarks and AI-generated landmarks were compared across 75 randomly selected CBCT scans of Class I/II malocclusion patients. Statistical analyses included paired t-tests/wilcoxon signed-rank test, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), and Bland–Altman analysis. Analysis revealed that 9 out of the 71 measurements (12.68%) showed statistically significant differences; however, all mean differences between AI-derived and ground truth measurements were clinically negligible (≤ 1 mm/°). ICC analysis demonstrated excellent agreement overall, with only two parameters (PP–HF–MSP and Me–MSP; 2.82%) showing ICC values below 0.90. Bland–Altman analysis indicated that 59.15% of AI-based cephalometric measurements achieved clinical interchangeability with ground truth, defined by limits of agreement within ± 2.0 mm/°. Among 36 linear measurements, all 26 parameters associated with landmarks exhibiting an MRE below 2 mm fell within clinically acceptable limits, whereas angle-based measurements did not demonstrate a clear correlation with MRE. The precision of automated 3D cephalometry is contingent upon the magnitude and directional vector of landmarking error. Angular measurements are particularly susceptible to unconstrained directional errors. Consequently, the MRE metric alone is insufficient to comprehensively evaluate the accuracy of automated cephalometric analysis, particularly in regions lacking definitive anatomical contours. Clinically applicable automated 3D cephalometry may therefore benefit from minor manual refinement at specific landmarks, such as the gonion and incisor root apex, particularly in patients with mixed dentition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41408-3.

Introduction

Cephalometric analysis is a cornerstone of diagnosing and planning treatment for maxillofacial deformities, requiring clinicians to identify anatomical landmarks for clinically relevant linear and angular measurements1. Although artificial intelligence (AI) has advanced automated two-dimensional (2D) cephalometric systems with reliable clinical integration2–4, limitations such as structural superimposition, magnification artifacts, and positioning inconsistencies constrain their diagnostic accuracy. Three-dimensional (3D) cephalometry overcomes many of these issues by providing precise spatial localization and superior anatomical representation, making it particularly valuable for complex cases5–7. However, manual landmark identification in 3D imaging remains labor-intensive and highly dependent on medical expertise. To address this, deep learning-based algorithms have been developed, enabling automated landmark detection in 3D-CT images with submillimeter accuracy through refined feature extraction and spatial mapping techniques8,9.

The European Union AI Act (2023) emphasizes that AI technology must remain transparent and trustworthy10. However, end-to-end deep learning approaches are often criticized as “black-box” methods, raising concerns about the transparency and accuracy of automated 3D CT landmark localization. Model performance has typically been evaluated using Mean Radial Error (MRE) and Success Detection Rate (SDR)11, with many studies adopting MRE < 2 mm as the threshold for clinical accuracy12. Yet, in three-dimensional space, Euclidean distance errors are compounded with dimensionality, and no standardized criteria currently exist for defining clinically acceptable accuracy in 3D landmark localization8,9,13. Although the MRE quantifies discrepancies between predicted and ground truth (GT) values, it does not fully reflect clinical relevance in three-dimensional applications. For instance, certain landmarks such as the bilateral porion (Po) points lack distinct anatomical turning points, making localization along the X- and Y-axes challenging and often resulting in higher MRE values. Nevertheless, when the Z-axis is accurately identified, the Frankfort plane constructed from these points remains clinically reliable14,15. Therefore, relying solely on MRE and SDR is insufficient for evaluating the accuracy of all anatomical landmarks.

Due to the computational challenges associated with three-dimensional spatial geometry, and given that no statistically significant differences have been observed between cephalometric measurements obtained from the mid-sagittal plane (MSP) of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images and traditional 2D measurements in patients with facial symmetry16, Dot G et al. validated algorithmic accuracy by comparing AI-derived results with the GT based on 12 orthogonal projections from the mid-sagittal plane of 3D images, along with three additional coronal plane measurements, including linear distances and angles17; Gupta A et al. reduced the computational complexity in three-dimensional space by predefining the horizontal reference plane, thus enabling comparison of knowledge-based automated landmarking with manual landmarking through 3D measurements in skeletal Class I patients with facial symmetry18. Using a similar simplified 3D approach, Park J compared cephalometric indices measured by GT and AI across different patient groups (with or without implants) to evaluate the performance of the algorithm19. In another study, Young-Eun Jung et al. evaluated an automated landmark identification (ALI) system based on 25 landmarks and 18 cephalometric measurements20. Despite these advances, existing methods have not fully realized the potential of 3D-CT measurements7. In particular, for patients with mandibular deviation, MSP projection angles may substantially deviate from actual anatomical positions due to asymmetry. Consequently, the clinical implementation of automated 3D-CT landmarking and diagnostic systems remains challenged by significant technical and practical obstacles. To enhance the transparency and clinical applicability of AI-assisted automated 3D-CT landmarking, this study introduces a complementary evaluation method that integrates geometric features with clinical anatomical relevance. A comprehensive three-dimensional cephalometric analysis was performed using AI-identified coordinates of 40 commonly used anatomical landmarks, which were compared against expert-annotated ground truth values. This approach aims to assess the accuracy of clinically meaningful measurements and to define precision boundaries for automated landmarking algorithms in 3D space, thereby informing their potential clinical deployment.

Materials and methods

Design and ethical approval

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (approval number: MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2024]537). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and, where applicable, their relatives, permitting the use of their imaging data for clinical research.

Data collection and acquisition

All data were collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University under clearly defined eligibility standards. The inclusion criteria specified patients aged 8 years or older, of either sex, with an ANB angle greater than 0°. Exclusion criteria applied to CBCT images with substantial scatter artifacts from restorative procedures or missing key anatomical landmarks, as well as to patients with incomplete clinical records. All scans were anonymized and reassigned new serial numbers.

CBCT scans were acquired using an i-CAT Next Generation machine (Imaging Sciences International, Hatfield, PA, USA) with a field of view of at least 16 × 13 cm and a scan time of 26 s. Datasets were stored in DICOM format with isotropic voxel sizes ranging from 0.25 to 0.40 mm.

3D-CT data manual and AI landmarking

Our research team previously collected and annotated 498 CBCT scans encompassing various dentition stages and malocclusion types. Using this dataset, we optimized the traditional U-Net architecture by incorporating an Effective Global Attention module to establish a deep learning-based automatic landmark identification model that met clinical accuracy standards11. For the present study, 75 eligible CBCT scans which were entirely independent of the model’s training and development phases were selected for clinical validation. All CBCT scans were imported into ITK-SNAP software (ITK, Davis, CA, USA), and 40 anatomical landmarks were manually annotated in the triplanar view of Multiplanar Reconstruction (MPR). Initial annotation was performed by an attending orthodontist and subsequently verified by an associate chief physician. These verified annotations were saved in nii.gz format and served as the manual GT21. The anatomical definitions of all landmarks used in 2D and 3D cephalometric analyses are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and their 3D localization is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The same 75 CBCT scans were then processed using the automated landmark identification system. The coordinates of all landmarks were exported in Excel format. Automated localization accuracy was evaluated using two widely adopted metrics: (1) Mean Radial Error (MRE), defined as the mean Euclidean distance between the predicted and reference landmarks; and (2) Success Detection Rate (SDR), defined as the proportion of landmarks with radial errors within thresholds of 2 mm, 3 mm, and 4 mm22.

Clinical accuracy verification of 3D-CT automatic positioning

The Zhilong Craniomaxillofacial Dimensional Analysis System was previously developed by our research team and comprises a React-based front end, a Python 3 back-end framework, and an integrated SQLite database. The software is designed to identify the three-dimensional coordinates of 40 anatomical landmarks and to perform cephalometric measurements with clinical-level precision.

To establish reference planes, the system first determined the Frankfort horizontal plane (FH) using bilateral porion (Po) and orbitale (Or) points via least-squares fitting, deriving its normal vector23,24. The midsagittal plane (MSP) was then defined as the plane passing through Sella (S) and Nasion (Na), oriented perpendicular to FH25. Finally, the coronal plane (CP) was constructed as the plane passing through S and oriented perpendicular to both MSP and FH25. Projection coordinates and normal vectors of landmarks, line segments, and planes were calculated using affine transformation matrices26. Linear distances were computed with the Euclidean formula, angles between line segments with vector dot products18,19, and plane-to-line angles were defined between the projected line segment on the plane and the original segment, restricted to 0–90°. The software generated both direct 3D measurements and projections onto the MSP (3D-MSP). Detailed definitions of coordinate systems, reference planes, and cephalometric parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Table S3.

For validation, 10 CBCT scans were randomly selected prior to formal measurements. Landmarks were identified and 3D cephalometric analyses were performed using InVivo Dental (version 5.2; Anatomage, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Mimics (version 20.0; Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Landmark coordinate data (XLS format from InVivo Dental and XML format from Mimics) were then imported into the Zhilong system. Comparative registration analyses across 20 trials confirmed 100% accuracy in cephalometric measurements.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For each cephalometric measurement, the mean and standard deviation were calculated, and continuous data are reported as Mean ± SD. Pairwise comparisons between AI and GT measurements were conducted using either a paired t-test or a Wilcoxon test27, following an assessment of normality (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test) on their differences. Bland-Altman analysis28 was used to visualize measurement biases, with the mean difference (systematic bias) and 95% limits of agreement (LoA = mean difference ± 1.96 SD) calculated to evaluate clinical acceptability (linear bias ≤ 2 mm; angular bias ≤ 2°)17,29,30. The reliability of AI-based measurements was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from a two-way mixed-effects model, with values interpreted as follows: > 0.99, excellent; 0.90–0.99, good; < 0.90, poor31–33.

Sample size determination was performed using power analysis with PASS 15.0 software. Assuming an anticipated correlation coefficient of 0.95 and testing whether ICC exceeded 0.9 (two-tailed α = 0.05, β = 0.10), the required minimum sample size was 68. Considering the retrospective design and a < 5% missing data rate, 75 CBCT scans were ultimately included to ensure sufficient analytical power.

Results

Dataset composition

A total of 75 CBCT scans meeting the inclusion criteria were retrospectively analyzed from patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The cohort comprised 38 males and 37 females, aged 8–34 years (mean ± SD: 19.73 ± 4.67 years). Of these, 24 participants were in the mixed dentition stage and 51 in the permanent dentition stage. Cephalometric analysis classified 53 cases as skeletal Class I and 22 as skeletal Class II malocclusion. All scans were performed following the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle to minimize radiation exposure.

Results of reference landmark detection

The three-dimensional coordinates of landmarks identified by AI were compared with the GT from manual annotations. The overall MRE was 1.70 ± 1.18 mm, while the SDRs within 2 mm, 3 mm, and 4 mm were 60.57%, 89.08%, and 96.77%, respectively. Subgroup analysis showed MREs of 1.73 ± 1.23 mm for skeletal landmarks, 1.53 ± 1.07 mm for dental landmarks, and 1.86 ± 1.16 mm for soft tissue landmarks. Clinical consistency analysis of AI and ground truth cephalometric measurements

A total of 71 cephalometric variables—30 angular, 36 linear, and 5 linear ratios—were derived from landmarks identified by both AI and GT. Among these, boxplot inspection identified outliers in 13 of the 66 angular and linear parameters (see Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 2). Bland–Altman analysis17 demonstrated that the LoA of 42 variables fell entirely within ± 2.0 mm/°. For 9 variables, one boundary slightly exceeded this threshold. Discussion

Despite substantial advances in automated 3D landmarking, the routine clinical adoption of fully automated 3D cephalometric analysis remains constrained by challenges related to landmark definitional ambiguity, coordinate system selection, and reference plane construction7,8. Based on an automatic landmarking algorithm with an overall recognition accuracy of MRE 1.70 ± 1.18 mm, our study automatically constructed the coordinate system and calculated 71 3D cephalometric indices commonly used in clinical practice. The results showed that the mean differences between AI and GT for all measurements were less than 1 mm/°, with most differences approaching 0 mm/°. No statistically significant differences were found between AI and GT for 62 indicators (P > 0.05), while 69 metrics demonstrated excellent agreement (ICC > 0.90). Bland–Altman analysis indicated that 42 indicators had LoA within ± 2.0 mm/°, suggesting that AI-based automated cephalometric measurements can clinically replace the gold standard (GT). Linear cephalometric measurements from landmarks (MRE < 2 mm) showed clinically acceptable LoA, whereas the accuracy of angular measurements, affected by error direction, showed a discrepancy from MRE-based precision (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5).

At present, there is no unified standard for evaluating the accuracy of automated three-dimensional landmark identification. Although most studies report MRE, the primary clinical objective is to support diagnosis and treatment planning. Therefore, predicted landmark coordinates must be translated into clinically interpretable cephalometric parameters, such as angles, distances, and areas, to enable meaningful clinical use. Yet the “black box” effect and the immaturity of technologies capable of directly transforming AI-identified landmarks into diagnostic data limit the clinical adoption of 3D automatic landmarking10. In multidimensional space, distances can be easily calculated using the Euclidean formula, whereas angular measurements are more complex and more sensitive to geometric dependencies.

From a geometric perspective, angles can be classified as those formed by three points, those between two lines, or those between a line and a plane. The angle between two non-coplanar lines in 3D can be computed using methods such as the direction vector angle method, the translation method, or the triple cosine theorem. The direction vector angle method calculates the angle between two lines from their direction vectors. Gupta et al18. and Jungeun Park et al19. applied this approach to measure angles between skew lines in 3D cephalometric analysis, validating its clinical accuracy. However, this method is not directly applicable to line–plane angles. For example, when calculating the 3D FMA (angle between the mandibular and FH planes), Gupta et al18. preprocessed DICOM data in Dolphin software by reorienting the volume to align the Frankfort plane with the horizontal plane, then computed the angle between the mandibular line segment and the horizontal plane (equivalent to a 2D calculation). By contrast, Jungeun Park et al19. defined the mandibular plane angle as the angle between the vectors of PoR–OrR and GoR–Me. Because the four landmarks defining the FH plane (PoR, OrR, PoL, OrL) are rarely perfectly coplanar in 3D, these methods may yield different FMA results. In this study, the translation method was used to calculate angles between line segments and line–plane intersections. First, PoR, OrR, PoL, and OrL were automatically identified, and the FH plane was defined through least-squares fitting23,24, yielding its normal vector, and then MSP25. Other reference planes were similarly constructed. Using an affine transformation matrix, projected coordinates and normal vectors of points, segments, and planes were computed for subsequent angular calculations. Although the direction vector method18,19and the translation method give identical results for line–line angles, our approach leverages automatically identified landmarks to construct a 3D coordinate system and reference planes. This framework enables comprehensive cephalometric analysis, including direct and projected linear distances, three-point angles, skew-line angles, line–plane angles, and plane–plane projection angles, thereby enhancing both extensiveness and comparability in validating AI-based automated landmark placement. Sophie Alt et al34. also used automatically identified landmarks to define the MSP and evaluate facial asymmetry, finding high accuracy. In our study, although the MRE of the porion and orbitale landmarks exceeded 2 mm, the FH plane itself remained highly accurate, as it depends primarily on the Z-axis positions of these four landmarks, where alignment was preserved14,15,35. For six indicators (FH-NA, FH-NA-MSP, FH-NPo, FH-NPo-MSP, FMA (R), MA (L)), paired T-tests showed no significant differences between AI and GT, ICC values exceeded 0.95, and Bland–Altman LoA were within 2 mm. These findings indicate that the AI-generated FH plane and related measurements are highly accurate and can reliably substitute GT. Furthermore, Table 3 shows that patients exhibited minimal asymmetry (Me–MSP distance: 1.08 mm). Directly measured 3D angles and linear distances also agreed closely with their MSP-projected counterparts. Together, these results confirm that automatically identified landmarks and the constructed 3D coordinate system are stable and accurate.

Building on this framework, we compared 71 cephalometric measurements commonly used in clinical practice36. Boxplot inspection identified outliers in 13 parameters. With the exception of three measurements related to flared central incisors, where tooth shape interfered with landmarking37, substantial errors were found in only 1–2 of the 75 CBCT scans (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 2). By reviewing the original CBCT images and the corresponding coordinate data for each flagged case, we found that outliers primarily occurred at landmarks with ambiguous anatomical boundaries, a lack of a well-defined turning point, or high inter-individual morphological variability (see Fig. 3). Since cephalometric analysis is not the sole basis for clinical diagnosis, and the landmark positions in such cases are not only prone to unavoidable localization errors even among human experts but may also yield cephalometric measurements with limited direct clinical relevance. We therefore present box plots to transparently report the occurrence and frequency of these outliers across metrics and to provide a reference for future human–AI collaborative annotation workflows. In order to align with clinical practice and to comprehensively evaluate algorithm performance, we conducted a statistical comparative analysis of all cephalometric measurements, both with and without outliers included. The results show that, whether or not outliers are excluded, paired T-tests confirmed that mean differences between AI and GT were < 1 mm/° for all indicators, with most values close to 0 mm/°, indicating negligible systematic bias. Accuracy and reliability are essential for clinical use, as they reduce the time and effort required by orthodontists38. The low incidence of outliers and minimal bias in this study suggest that AI-based automated landmarking meets clinical accuracy criteria (per MRE), performs consistently, and can reduce variability caused by observer perception and experience.

Unlike the evaluation of automated landmarking error, which primarily focuses on the magnitude of the MRE, the reliability of cephalometric measurements is influenced by both the magnitude and the directional pattern of localization error. In this study, the LoA for nearly all linear measurements derived from landmarks with an MRE < 2 mm, as well as for all linear ratio measurements, fell within the clinically acceptable range. However, the correlation between MRE and measurement accuracy was weaker for angular measurements than for linear measurements. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that, in angular measurements, even small coordinate errors can significantly compromise precision when they follow a specific directional pattern, such as systematic bias or inconsistency. In this study, the directional error of AI-based landmark localization was random. Consequently, even though the majority of landmarks exhibited an MRE of less than 2 mm, except SNB and ANB, only 8 of the 30 angular indicators [FH-NA, FH-NA-MSP, FH-NPo, FH-NPo-MSP, PP (ANS-PNS)-HF, Y-axis, FMA (R), FMA (L)] exhibited LoA within clinically acceptable ranges, and all involved line–plane angles. These results indicate that imposing directional constraints on key landmark coordinates in the development of automatic landmarking algorithms may effectively improve the utility and accuracy of automated cephalometric systems.

Moreover, low systematic bias does not necessarily guarantee clinical applicability. Bland–Altman plots revealed that, while mean differences between AI and GT were close to zero for all indicators (except upper incisor inclination, ~ 1°), LoA exceeded clinical thresholds (2 mm/2°) for all incisor angular measures and for a substantial proportion measurements involving the gonion, at least on one side of the range. These findings highlight that even when overall MRE meets accuracy standards, manual review remains necessary for certain landmarks at the current stage. Khanehmasjedi et al39. also reported that errors in the nasolabial angle during CBCT were influenced by soft tissue quality and software parameter definition, while in our study, chin soft tissue measurements were limited by the chin pocket, reducing the accuracy of Pogs–Ns vert measurements.

As this work was conceived as a preliminary investigation focusing on the relationship between algorithm evaluation metrics and clinical validation, several factors may limit the generalizability of our findings, including the single-center design, the relatively limited datasets, the assessment of only one automated landmarking algorithm, and the restricted scope of cephalometric parameters analyzed. Future studies will incorporate external multicenter datasets, evaluate additional algorithms, and expand the range of 3D cephalometric measurements to enable more comprehensive benchmarking and to support the development of more widely applicable standards for automated landmarking accuracy in both the AI and clinical communities.

Conclusions

In AI assisted 3D CT automated landmark identification, the algorithm demonstrates favorable accuracy and robustness. However, the precision of automated 3D cephalometry depends on both the magnitude and directional components of landmarking error. Angular measurements are particularly sensitive to unconstrained directional errors, especially for landmarks lacking distinct anatomical inflection points. Therefore, MRE < 2 mm should not be considered the sole criterion for assessing clinical accuracy. Given the localization uncertainties associated with specific anatomical landmarks such as open apex incisor apices and the gonion point and the inherent limitations of CBCT soft tissue imaging, manual verification of these landmarks after AI based identification is recommended prior to cephalometric analysis. Future research should investigate the clinical significance of individual landmarks and their axis specific accuracy and direction to further advance automated 3D landmarking technology toward broader clinical applicability.
