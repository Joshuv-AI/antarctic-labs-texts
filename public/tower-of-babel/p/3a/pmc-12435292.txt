Tumor-expressed GPNMB orchestrates Siglec-9+ TAM polarization and EMT to promote metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Thuy Linh Dang Cao, Kunio Kawanishi, Sachie Hashimoto, Kowit Hengphasatporn, Chiaki Nagai-Okatani, Takaharu Kimura, Mohammed Abdelaziz, Rie Shiratani, Thanasis Poullikkas, Nuriza Ulul Azmi, Masaki Baba, Yukari Okita, Yukihide Watanabe, Hiroko Bando, Satoshi Yamazaki, Yasuteru Shigeta, Atsushi Kuno, Mitsuyasu Kato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMetastasis is the primary cause of cancer-related deaths, yet the glyco-immunological mechanisms by which the tumor microenvironment (TME) regulates this process remain incompletely understood. Glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), overexpressed in subsets of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), drives tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) polarization by inducing GPNMB expression in monocytes, establishing a feed-forward amplification loop. Distinct sialylation patterns on tumor- vs. macrophage-derived GPNMB enable selective recognition by sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin (Siglec)-9, an inhibitory myeloid checkpoint. Coexpression of GPNMB and Siglec-9 correlates with poor prognosis in TNBC. Dual blockade of Siglec-E (murine Siglec-9 ortholog) and PD-1 reduces tumor stemness, suppresses IL-6-mediated epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and limits metastasis in vivo. The GPNMB–Siglec-9 axis represents a promising therapeutic target for metastatic TNBC.

Metastasis remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, driven by complex interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME). The TME is composed of tumor cells, stromal components, and immune cells, influencing critical processes such as immune evasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis (1, 2). One of the hallmark phenomena of metastasis is epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), where epithelial cells lose cell–cell adhesion and acquire mesenchymal traits, enhancing their motility and invasiveness (3). EMT is closely associated with changes in the TME, where signaling from stromal and immune cells promotes tumor cell dissemination (2). Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key components of the TME and play a pivotal role in modulating EMT and metastasis through the secretion of cytokines and growth factors (4).

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is recognized as one of the most aggressive and metastatic forms of breast cancer, characterized by high recurrence rates and resistance to treatment (5). Currently, therapies targeting PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathways (6, 7), cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors, microtubule inhibitors, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs)—such as sacituzumab govitecan, which targets trophoblast cell surface antigen 2—are under clinical evaluation (8, 9). Moreover, advanced omics technologies have led to the identification of novel therapeutic targets, with a particular focus on posttranslational modifications (PTMs) (10).

Among PTM targets, abnormal sialylation and sialoglycoconjugates are critical factors that influence cancer cell structure, stability, and function. Elevated sialylation in cancer cells, driven by sialyltransferases (STs) and dysregulated sialidases, can promote tumor growth, metastasis, immune evasion, and drug resistance (11). Accumulating evidence suggests that sialylation is strongly correlated with malignancy in breast cancer, particularly in TNBC (12), however, the key protein targets of sialylation remain largely unexplored.

One such key molecule is glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), a highly glycosylated protein with an extracellular domain (ECD), a single transmembrane domain, and a short cytoplasmic domain, which plays a critical role in various cancers (13). Studies utilizing tumor microarrays and transcriptional profiling in primary melanoma and breast cancer have identified GPNMB as a potent prometastatic factor (14). GPNMB is recognized for its critical role in multiple cancers, including TNBC, glioblastoma, uveal and cutaneous melanoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, and osteosarcoma (15, 16). Its function in cancer progression is particularly notable, as it exhibits both protumorigenic and antitumorigenic properties (13) and contributes to stemness induction (17, 18). Additionally, GPNMB interacts with Syndecan-4 in antigen-presenting cells, leading to impaired T cell function (19). TAM-derived GPNMB has been demonstrated to promote secondary tumor growth and metastasis in mouse models (20). Given its significant role in promoting metastasis in TNBC, GPNMB has emerged as a promising therapeutic target (21).

Recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses have identified GPNMB as a signature marker of immunosuppressive TAM subsets associated with poor prognosis in colorectal liver metastases (22). While these studies highlight the prognostic relevance of TAM-derived GPNMB, the upstream mechanisms by which tumor-intrinsic GPNMB regulates TAM programming remain poorly defined in TNBC.

The present study demonstrates that tumor-expressed GPNMB directly induces monocyte-to-TAM polarization by promoting GPNMB expression in monocytes, establishing a feed-forward amplification loop that reinforces immunoregulatory macrophage networks within the TME. Distinct sialylation patterns further modulate this process: Tumor-derived GPNMB carries α2,3-sialylation, whereas macrophage-derived GPNMB exhibits α2,6-sialylation, allowing differential recognition by Siglec-9, an inhibitory sialic acid-binding receptor expressed on myeloid cells. Integrative analyses incorporating clinical cohorts, tissue microarrays, in silico docking, and 3D coculture systems elucidate the role of GPNMB–Siglec-9 interactions in driving TAM-mediated EMT plasticity and metastatic dissemination. Furthermore, dual blockade of Siglec-E (the murine ortholog of Siglec-9) and PD-1 significantly suppresses IL-6-dependent EMT and lung metastasis in vivo, highlighting the translational potential of targeting the GPNMB–Siglec-9 axis to reverse immune suppression and limit metastatic progression in TNBC.

Results

Open Data Analysis and In Silico Docking Have Highlighted the Significant Impact of Siglec-9 in High-GPNMB-Expression-Level TNBC.

GPNMB is highly overexpressed and hypersialylated in TNBC. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of 1,231 breast cancer samples confirmed that GPNMB expression is significantly higher in TNBC compared to non-TNBC (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Furthermore, TNBC exhibited upregulation of sialyltransferases, particularly ST3GAL2 and ST6GAL1, contributing to aberrant sialylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Tissue microarray analysis further supported these findings, demonstrating increased GPNMB expression alongside elevated α2,3- and α2,6-sialic acid levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D−J).

To further characterize the TNBC immune microenvironment, deconvolution based on the CIBERSORTx LM22 reference signature matrix (23) was applied to bulk RNA-seq data. Compared to non-TNBC tumors, TNBC demonstrated significantly elevated infiltration of memory B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ memory activated T cells, follicular helper T cells, resting NK cells, activated dendritic cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Both M0 and M1-like macrophages were increased, whereas M2-like macrophages were decreased, suggesting a skewed immune contexture toward proinflammatory components. This immunological remodeling, together with heightened Siglec expression, supports the hypothesis that TNBC tumors may exploit Siglec–sialoglycan interactions to attenuate antitumor immunity while preserving immune infiltration. In GPNMB-high TNBC tumors, bulk RNA-seq deconvolution revealed a significant decrease in CD8+ T cells, resting memory CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), monocytes, activated dendritic cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils, whereas M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and resting mast cells were increased compared with GPNMB-low tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). To identify the Siglec family members primarily involved in TNBC, a combination of public transcriptomic data analysis and in silico protein docking was applied. The analysis showed that Siglec-1, -7, -8, -9, -10, and -15 were significantly upregulated in the high-GPNMB-expression TNBC group compared to the low-GPNMB group, based on TCGAbiolinks data (Fig. 1A). Protein–protein docking analysis was then performed to compare the binding affinities of Siglec-1 to -16 with GPNMB (Fig. 1B). HDOCK binding scores (ranging from −310.45 to −243.73) revealed that Siglec-15, -16, and -9 exhibited the highest binding potential to GPNMB, while Siglec-1, -7, and -8 demonstrated weaker interactions.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis using TCGAbiolinks revealed no significant prognostic impact for GPNMB-high or individual Siglec-high expression alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–D). However, coexpression of Siglec-9-high and GPNMB-high was significantly associated with poorer survival, while similar associations were not observed for Siglec-7-high or Siglec-15-high (Fig. 1 C–E).

These findings were independently validated using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter, further confirming the poor prognosis of GPNMB-high/Siglec-9-high coexpression (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E–L and Table S1). No similar correlation was found for other Siglecs, except Siglec-16-high (SI Appendix, Fig. S4L), which has been linked to M1-like TAMs and better prognosis in polysialic acid-positive glioblastoma (24).

To further dissect macrophage heterogeneity in TNBC, we reanalyzed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data from GSE169246, which included both tumor-derived TAMs and peripheral blood-derived tumor-infiltrating monocytes (TIMs) (Fig. 1F). Consistent with prior studies, GPNMB expression was predominantly enriched in TAMs, while Siglec-9 expression was broadly distributed across both TAMs and TIMs (Fig. 1G), suggesting distinct but overlapping roles in macrophage differentiation and function (Fig. 1G).

Given the limited number of TAMs captured in the scRNA-seq dataset (approximately 1,400 cells), we sought to improve the resolution of macrophage subtyping by constructing a customized TAM signature matrix. While previous frameworks (25) and existing functional gene modules were referenced, these did not adequately resolve TAM and TIM heterogeneity in our dataset (SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6).

Therefore, we reestablished a refined signature matrix encompassing eleven TAM subtypes, capturing distinct immunosuppressive, inflammatory, metabolic, and antigen-presenting phenotypes. This matrix was constructed from the gene sets derived from TAM clusters 1 to 7 identified in tumor tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and Dataset S1). Cluster 6 was excluded due to the high expression of canonical T cell–associated genes such as CD3D, CD3E, CD2, NKG7, CD7, and PTPRCAP, suggesting potential T cell contamination. The finalized signature matrix used for deconvolution is provided in Dataset S2.

We subsequently applied this scRNA-seq–derived signature matrix to TCGA TNBC bulk RNA-seq data (n = 164) using CIBERSORTx deconvolution (Fig. 1 H–K). The analysis revealed that tumors with high GPNMB expression exhibited significantly elevated proportions of immunosuppressive TAM subtypes, including Siglec-9+ TAMs and EMT-associated TAMs (Fig. 1 H, J, and K). These findings support the notion that the GPNMB–Siglec-9 axis is closely linked to the emergence of immunoregulatory macrophage networks within the TNBC microenvironment.

Meanwhile, the expression patterns of GPNMB, Siglec-7, Siglec-9, and Siglec-15 were further evaluated using TMA analysis in TNBC (n = 39) and non-TNBC (n = 20) cases. Consistent with transcriptomic data, GPNMB and Siglec-9 displayed substantial intertumoral heterogeneity, with Siglec-9 primarily localized to infiltrating immune cells, particularly CD68+ macrophages. Siglec-7 and Siglec-15 were observed in both tumor epithelial and immune compartments with variable expression levels. In node-positive patients (N ≥ 1), GPNMB expression correlated significantly with Siglec-7 and Siglec-9, but not with Siglec-15 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

GPNMB-Expressing TNBC Spheroids Drive Macrophage Polarization via Coordinated Transcriptional and Functional Reprogramming across In Vitro, Ex Vivo, and In Silico Platforms.

Our initial CIBERSORTx analysis revealed enrichment of immunoregulatory TAM subsets in GPNMB-high TNBC tumors. However, bulk RNA-seq limits resolution of GPNMB expression across cell types. To address this, we combined 3D macrophage–tumor spheroid coculture, primary monocyte validation, and in silico analysis to dissect GPNMB–Siglec-9–mediated TAM programming. We first established a PMA-free 3D coculture system using Hs578T spheroids and THP-1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Bulk RNA-seq revealed that siRNA-mediated GPNMB knockdown in spheroids induced substantial transcriptional shifts in THP-1 cells, with 1,472 genes upregulated and 2,265 downregulated relative to controls. Of these, 112 were consistently suppressed by GPNMB knockdown (Fig. 2 A–C, SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A–C, and Dataset S3). Enrichment analysis highlighted EMT, extracellular matrix remodeling, M2-like polarization (IL-4/IL-13, IL-10), and posttranslational modification pathways (Fig. 2D). Motif analysis revealed enrichment of PSMB5, CEBPB, NF-κB, and LXR targets in GPNMB-regulated genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). GSEA further confirmed activation of NF-κB, CEBPB, ETS1, SP1, STAT3, MAPK, and BHLHE40 regulons (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 E–K). Notably, GPNMB promoted expression of IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, MMP9, PLAUR, SPP1, COL1A2, and FN1, accompanied by increased TNF-α secretion suppressed by GPNMB knockdown (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 H and L). To assess clinical relevance, GSEA of TCGA TNBC tumors (n = 39 GPNMB-high vs. 39 GPNMB-low) showed enrichment of IL-4/IL-13 and IL-10 signaling (Fig. 2E), consistent with in vitro findings (Fig. 2D). We then applied our TAM signature matrix to THP-1 bulk RNA-seq data post coculture (Fig. 2F). Tumor-expressed GPNMB induced THP-1–intrinsic GPNMB expression, which strongly correlated with EMT_Associated_TAM and Siglec-9_TAM signatures (Fig. 2 F and G), indicating that tumor GPNMB both initiates monocyte reprogramming and amplifies subtype-specific TAM phenotypes. To validate this in a physiologically relevant context, we performed coculture experiments with primary human monocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Monocyte GPNMB and CD163 expression was upregulated by GPNMB-high spheroids, while Siglec-9 expression remained constitutive across all conditions—consistent with scRNA-seq data (Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6H). These findings demonstrate that tumor-expressed GPNMB consistently promotes monocyte polarization toward immunoregulatory TAMs across in vitro, ex vivo, and in silico platforms. By triggering monocyte-intrinsic GPNMB expression and activating EMT, inflammatory, and Siglec-9+ TAM programs, GPNMB functions as both an upstream initiator and internal amplifier of macrophage reprogramming. These results position GPNMB as a central regulator of the immunosuppressive, prometastatic microenvironment in TNBC.

Cancer-Specific Sialylation-Dependent Recognition of GPNMB by Siglec-9 in the TME.

To further elucidate the interaction between GPNMB and Siglec family members in the TME, we employed a 3D coculture system using spheroid-forming Hs578T TNBC cells and THP-1 monocytes. Compared to THP-1 cells cultured alone, THP-1 cells cocultured with Hs578T spheroids exhibited significantly increased expression of Siglec-7 and Siglec-9, suggesting a tumor-derived modulation of macrophage Siglec expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). Genetic knockdown of GPNMB in Hs578T cells attenuated the induction of Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 expression in cocultured THP-1 cells, although the degree of suppression varied depending on the siRNA construct employed (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B). GPNMB depletion also reduced the expression of macrophage markers, including both proinflammatory (TNF-α, CD80) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10, CD163) subsets to varying degrees (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C), indicating that tumor-expressed GPNMB contributes to multiple polarization programs within monocytes. Notably, Siglec-15 expression remained largely unaffected by GPNMB knockdown, suggesting a differential regulatory mechanism (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B). Induction of Siglec-9, TNF-α, CD80, IL-10, and CD163 was similarly observed in THP-1 and U937 cells cocultured with MDA-MB-157 spheroids, a distinct TNBC model with high endogenous GPNMB expression, further supporting the role of tumor-expressed GPNMB in promoting TAM-like transcriptional programs across multiple monocyte-like systems (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D).

To assess direct interactions between GPNMB and Siglec family members, we performed ELISA using purified GPNMB protein ectopically expressed in HEK293T cells, which allows endogenous human glycosylation to be retained (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). Unlike conventional prokaryotic (or insect) expression systems, this method preserves complex sialylation patterns relevant for Siglec binding. Under these conditions, GPNMB exhibited strong binding to Siglec-9, moderate interaction with Siglec-15, and minimal reactivity with Siglec-7 (Fig. 3A). Sialidase pretreatment, which removes terminal sialic acids, markedly reduced GPNMB’s binding to Siglec-9 while having no effect on Siglec-15, suggesting distinct glycan dependencies (Fig. 3A). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) analysis quantitatively assessed binding affinities, revealing minimal interaction between Siglec-7 and GPNMB (Fig. 3 B, a). In contrast, both Siglec-9 and Siglec-15 exhibited dose-dependent binding curves toward GPNMB (Fig. 3 B, b and c). Notably, sialidase treatment did not affect Siglec-7 binding (Fig. 3 B, d), but led to 5.42-fold and 3.12-fold reductions in the binding affinities of Siglec-9 and Siglec-15, respectively, indicating a greater dependency of Siglec-9 on sialylation (Fig. 3 B, e and f and SI Appendix, Table S2).

To dissect the glycosylation profile underlying these interactions, we conducted lectin microarray profiling of recombinant and endogenous GPNMB combined with glycosidase treatments. Western blot analysis confirmed that HEK293T-derived GPNMB exhibited N-linked glycosylation, as evidenced by molecular weight shifts after PNGase F digestion (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B). Immunoprecipitated GPNMB from HEK293T cells, Hs578T spheroids, and phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)-stimulated THP-1 cells exhibited distinct glycan binding profiles across a panel of 45 lectins (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Notably, GPNMB derived from Hs578T cells was predominantly α2,3-sialylated, while GPNMB from THP-1 cells was enriched in α2,6-linked sialic acids (Fig. 3D). These differential sialylation patterns were further supported by sialidase-sensitive lectin binding, in which MAL-I (α2,3-specific) and SNA (α2,6-specific) signals were selectively diminished upon enzymatic cleavage (Fig. 3D). Imaging analysis of 3D spheroids revealed that Siglec-9+ THP-1 cells preferentially adhered to regions of the spheroid surface enriched in tumor-derived GPNMB bearing α2,3-linked sialic acids (Fig. 3E). In contrast, GPNMB expressed by macrophage-lineage cells, including THP-1, predominantly exhibited α2,6-sialylation (Fig. 3E), indicating cell type–specific sialylation patterns that may govern differential Siglec-9 recognition.

To mechanistically validate this glycoform-specific recognition, in silico molecular docking incorporating glycan structures demonstrated stronger binding affinity of Siglec-9 to α2,3-sialylated GPNMB (−6.052 kcal/mol) compared to α2,6-sialylated GPNMB (−5.570 kcal/mol) (Fig. 3 I–K). This finding aligns with data from the Consortium for Functional Glycomics, which showed Siglec-9 preferentially recognizes α2,3-sialylated glycans, while Siglec-7 interacts with both α2,3- and α2,8-sialylated structures (Datasets S4 and S5).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TAMs in TNBC, via Siglec-9, selectively recognize tumor-derived α2,3-sialylated GPNMB, potentially facilitating TAM-mediated signaling within the TME through macrophage-intrinsic Siglec-9 engagement.

Combination Immunotherapy Targeting Siglec-E and PD-1 Enhances Antitumor Immunity and Reduces Stemness in TNBC.

Building on the findings of this study, we hypothesized that targeting Siglec-9 with an anti-Siglec-9 antibody could be particularly effective in TNBC cases with high GPNMB expression. Previous research has shown that Siglec-E, the murine ortholog of human Siglec-9, plays an immunosuppressive role in the TME, and Siglec-E knockout mice exhibit enhanced antitumor responses across various tumor models, particularly when combined with anti-PD-1 therapy (26, 27). Given that an anti-Siglec-E antibody has demonstrated blocking effects in vitro (28), we sought to evaluate its therapeutic efficacy in vivo and explore its potential synergy with anti-PD-1 therapy in a BALB/c mouse model of TNBC. To assess treatment effects, we performed tumor-limiting dilution analysis to evaluate tumor stemness and conducted scRNA-seq to investigate the underlying mechanisms (Fig. 4A).

Although no statistically significant differences in tumor growth or volume were observed across treatment groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 B and C), Extreme Limiting Dilution Analysis (ELDA) revealed significant variations in tumor stem cell frequencies (Fig. 4B). In the combination therapy group, the frequency of sphere-forming stem cells was approximately 1 in 153 cells, compared to 1 in 70.5 cells in the anti-Siglec-E monotherapy group. In contrast, the frequencies were notably higher in the anti-PD-1 and isotype control groups, with 1 in 31.5 and 1 in 39.7 cells, respectively (Fig. 4B). To further investigate the immunological effects of anti-Siglec-E treatment, we performed scRNA-seq analysis on EpCAM+ and EpCAM− cells isolated from primary tumors in BALB/c mice, using the BD Rhapsody Mouse Immune Response Panel. This panel encompasses 397 default immune-related genes and 74 custom-selected genes, yielding a total of 471 targets optimized for profiling macrophage plasticity, EMT signaling, and tumor immunity (SI Appendix, Table S3). Unsupervised clustering identified 19 transcriptionally distinct cell populations based on canonical immune and stromal lineage markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S13D). Siglec-E transcripts were spatially restricted to the monocyte/macrophage clusters on the left margin of the UMAP projection (Fig. 4 C and D), consistent with their known expression profile.

To evaluate treatment-induced changes at the cellular level, we analyzed both average expression and percentage expression of selected lineage and polarization markers across treatment conditions. Monocyte/macrophage-related clusters showed that the anti-Siglec-E antibody significantly suppressed the expression of genes associated with TAM polarization, including Mrc1, Il6ra, Arg1, Fpr1, Cd3, and Cxcl3, with a more pronounced effect observed in combination with the anti-PD-1 antibody (Fig. 4E). Notably, Mrc1, Arg1, and Il6 are well-established markers of the immunosuppressive TAM phenotype (29, 30). In parallel, combination therapy significantly upregulated markers associated with T cells, B cells, and dendritic cells, including Cd4, Cd8, Cd19, and Cd79, respectively, suggesting a robust enhancement of tumor-killing immunity (Fig. 4E). Given the fixed gene content of the BD Rhapsody panel, surrogate module scores were defined to infer specific immune and stromal states at single-cell resolution. Suppressive TAM (Suppressive_TAM) were assessed using a module comprising Hmox1, Fcgr3, Arg1, and Il10, reflecting M2-like polarization (Fig. 4F). Regulatory CD4+ T cell (CD4_Treg) activity was inferred from Il2ra, Ctla4, Ikzf2, and Tnfrsf18, compensating for minimal Foxp3 expression in CD4+ clusters (Fig. 4G). CD8+ T cell exhaustion (CD8_Exhaustion) and effector function (CD8_Effector) were assessed using Eomes, Gzma, Tox (Fig. 4H) and Cd8a, Gzmb, Nkg7 (Fig. 4I), respectively.

Module scores were calculated using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function and compared across treatment groups using Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. The Suppressive_TAM score was significantly reduced only by combination therapy (adjusted P < 0.0001), with no effect from αPD-1 or αSiglec-E monotherapy (P > 0.19). Both monotherapies showed higher scores than the combination group (P = 0.0015 and 0.0002) (Fig. 4J).

The CD4_Treg score was significantly decreased by αPD-1 (P < 0.0001) and combination therapy (P = 0.0001), but not by αSiglec-E (P = 0.93), indicating that PD-1 blockade primarily drives Treg suppression (Fig. 4K). The combination offered no added benefit over αPD-1 alone (P = 0.83).

For CD8_Exhaustion, αPD-1 reduced exhaustion scores (P < 0.0001), whereas αSiglec-E increased them (P = 0.0017 vs. isotype). Notably, the combination failed to reduce exhaustion compared to isotype (P > 0.99) and was less effective than αPD-1 alone (P < 0.0001), suggesting antagonism between Siglec-E and PD-1 blockade (Fig. 4L).

CD8_Effector scores were elevated in αPD-1 and combination groups (P < 0.0001), with no additive effect from combination therapy. αSiglec-E alone had no impact (P > 0.99) and was significantly less effective than αPD-1 or combination (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4M).

Together, these results demonstrate that while combination therapy reprograms immunosuppressive macrophages and Tregs, it does not enhance CD8+ T cell activity beyond PD-1 blockade alone. Furthermore, Siglec-E inhibition may impede CD8+ T cell reinvigoration, highlighting potential checkpoint interference in the TNBC microenvironment.

Combination Immunotherapy Targeting Siglec-E and PD-1 Suppresses IL-6-driven EMT and Significantly Reduces Lung Metastasis.

To evaluate how Siglec-E/PD-1 coinhibition modulates EMT within the TME, we analyzed scRNA-seq data focusing on Cluster 5 (EpCAM+ cancer cells), Cluster 16 (inflammatory CAFs; iCAFs), and Cluster 17 (Cpa3+ mast cells) (SI Appendix, Fig. S13G). Il6 and Il6ra were highly expressed in these EMT-relevant clusters (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

To comprehensively assess EMT-associated molecular alterations, we constructed a curated EMT gene panel incorporating 33 genes, guided by published pancancer EMT resources (31) and single-cell EMT regulatory network analyses (32). This panel included: i) canonical EMT markers (e.g., Cdh1, Zeb1, Fn1); ii) upstream signaling regulators (TGF-β, MAPK, NF-κB, JAK/STAT); and iii) downstream effectors (Mmp9, Vegfa, Cxcr4, Myc). This allowed comprehensive assessment of EMT plasticity (Fig. 5A). Il6 was enriched in iCAFs and mast cells, while Il6ra was more broadly distributed, including in cancer cells (Cluster 5) (Fig. 5A). Combination therapy reduced Il6- and Il6ra-expressing cells (Fig. 5 B–D), suggesting inhibition of IL-6–mediated EMT support (Fig. 5E). qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence confirmed that combination treatment upregulated Cdh1 and downregulated Cdh2 expression compared to isotype and monotherapies (Fig. 5 F and G). These molecular changes coincided with reduced lung metastases (Fig. 5 H–J), highlighting the therapeutic potential of Siglec-E/PD-1 blockade in mitigating EMT-driven progression.

Discussion

Current cancer immunotherapies primarily focus on T cell–based strategies, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy, T cell receptor (TCR)-T cell therapy, and checkpoint inhibitors targeting CTLA-4 and PD-1. While these approaches have shown clinical efficacy, they are often limited by cytokine release syndrome (CRS), neurotoxicity, and resistance due to tumor heterogeneity, including altered receptor expression that enables immune evasion. These limitations underscore the need for alternative strategies targeting the TME. TAM-directed therapies, such as CD47/SIRPα blockade (33) and CAR-M therapies (34), have shown promise, and recent advances in scRNA-seq have further refined our understanding of immune cell composition in TNBC.

In this study, we demonstrated that GPNMB knockdown in TNBC cells and anti-Siglec-E antibody treatment in vivo significantly reduce M2-like TAM polarization and lung metastasis. This effect is mediated by immune cell recruitment and suppression of interleukin-6 (IL-6)-associated EMT, with a reduction in inflammatory CAFs (Cluster 16) and Cpa3+ connective tissue mast cells (Cluster 17), both of which exhibit elevated IL-6 expression (Fig. 5 C and D). Given that IL-6 signaling via the IL-6/IL-6R axis activates protumorigenic JAK/STAT pathways (35–37), the present data suggest that IL-6 represents one contributing mediator downstream of the GPNMB–Siglec-9 axis. However, we did not perform in vivo IL-6 neutralization or recombinant IL-6 rescue experiments in this study, due to the pleiotropic nature of IL-6 and the complexity of the cytokine milieu in our 3D coculture and in vivo systems. IL-6 alone is unlikely to fully account for the complex macrophage–TME crosstalk observed in TNBC, as multiple cytokine, chemokine, and metabolic pathways are concurrently engaged. In particular, factors such as IL-1β, TNF-α, CCL2, CXCL8, and matrix remodeling molecules also showed modulation in response to GPNMB perturbation (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S9), supporting the concept that GPNMB regulates broad transcriptional programs beyond IL-6 alone. Thus, while IL-6 blockade may hold therapeutic promise, combinatorial targeting of upstream regulators such as GPNMB or Siglec-9 may offer a more comprehensive approach. GPNMB-targeted therapies, such as glembatumumab vedotin (GV), have shown limited clinical success in melanoma (38), lung squamous cell carcinoma (39), osteosarcoma (40), and metastatic breast cancer (41), likely due to GPNMB’s broad tissue expression. Refining its targeting within the TME, particularly in modulating TAM polarization, could improve therapeutic efficacy in TNBC. The role of Siglec-9 in cancer remains controversial, with context-dependent effects across malignancies. While its loss induces M2-like TAM polarization in lung cancer (42), its interaction with MUC1 in acute myeloid leukemia promotes tumor progression (43). The present study demonstrates that tumor-intrinsic GPNMB, predominantly carrying N-glycosylation, exhibits strong Siglec-9 binding affinity, with glycoform specificity (α2,3- vs. α2,6-sialylation) likely contributing to selective receptor engagement. This suggests that both sialoglycan lineage and protein–protein interactions govern Siglec-9’s binding specificity. The observed heterogeneity in GPNMB and Siglec-9 expression within TNBC tissues suggests the existence of distinct TAM subpopulations. Specifically, the coexpression of GPNMB and Siglec-9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7L) may define a unique immunosuppressive TAM subset with prognostic and functional relevance, while other TAMs may lack this dual phenotype. Further, Siglec family members such as Siglec-12 and Siglec-15 are implicated in cancer progression (44, 45), with Siglec-8 expression observed in breast cancer, though relatively low in TNBC (46).

Our findings align with emerging evidence supporting Siglec-targeted therapies as complementary to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Ongoing clinical trials of anti-Siglec-15 antibodies (NCT03665285, NCT04699123) emphasize the therapeutic relevance of targeting Siglecs beyond sialoglycan recognition. Moving forward, clinical evaluation of the GPNMB–Siglec-9 axis in TNBC patients, particularly in relation to neoadjuvant therapy response and metastatic progression, will be critical to establish its translational applicability.

In conclusion, integrating Siglec-E/PD-1 coinhibition with TAM-targeted therapies offers a promising strategy for remodeling the TME in TNBC. By targeting key upstream regulators such as GPNMB and Siglec-9, which orchestrate multiple downstream cytokines and EMT programs including IL-6. Immune checkpoint efficacy may be enhanced while overcoming tumor heterogeneity-driven resistance. Further studies are needed to translate these findings into clinical applications, improving outcomes for patients with aggressive metastatic cancers like TNBC.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement.

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Tsukuba (approval No. 22-221). All procedures complied with institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki (1995). For human primary monocyte experiments, informed consent was obtained from all donors, and protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB), University of Tsukuba Hospital (No. R04-093). Tumor tissue microarrays were commercially obtained, fully anonymized, and did not require IRB approval.

Tumor Transplantation and Single-Cell Isolation.

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were injected with 5 × 104 4T1 cells in 50 µL PBS into the left fourth mammary fat pad. Tumors formed within 7 d and reached approximately 70 mm3 in volume. Three days postinjection, mice received treatment antibodies (200 µg/mouse) peripherally every 3 d for five doses: IgG2a Isotype Control (Leinco Tech), anti-mouse Siglec-E (BioLegend), anti-PD-1 (Leinco Tech), or a combination of anti-PD-1 and Siglec-E.

Tumors were measured weekly using a digital caliper, and volumes were calculated as Volume (mm3) = (L × W2)/2, where L is the major axis and W is the minor axis. After 3 wk, tumors were harvested, weighed, and embedded in paraffin for analysis. Lung metastases were assessed by injecting India ink into the trachea postmortem, followed by lung extraction, formalin fixation, and microscopic quantification of white nodules on the dark-stained lung surface (47). For single-cell isolation, primary tumors were minced and dissociated using BD Horizon™ Dri Tumor & Tissue Dissociation Reagent (BD Biosciences). Red blood cells were lysed (Invitrogen), and dead cells were removed using Dead Cell Removal MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec). Epithelial and immune cells were separated via the CD326 EPCAM microbead protocol (Miltenyi Biotec).

3D Cell Sphere Formation.

Hs578T, 4T1, and MDA-MB-157 cell lines, which exhibit stable GPNMB expression (48), were purchased from ATCC. THP-1 and U937 cells were kindly provided by Professor Kazuko Shibuya (University of Tsukuba, Japan). At 80% confluence, cancer cells were detached and seeded in sphere medium (DMEM: F12 = 1:1) containing 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor, 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (both Wako), 2% B27 (Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin G, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin sulfate (Wako) in ultralow attachment plates (Corning). Spheres formed within 3, 5, or 7 d. For coculture experiments, PMA stimulation was omitted to preserve monocyte phenotypes. The detailed protocol is provided in SI Appendix, Methods.

RNA Extraction and Real-Time PCR.

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Life Technologies) and 2 µg of the product was converted into cDNA using the High Capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were run using a QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR instrument. Each experiment was performed in at least three biological replications with two practical replications for each PCR run (49). The results were normalized using the housekeeping gene gapdh/beta-actin. The list of primers used is shown in SI Appendix, Table S4.

ELDA.

ELDA was performed as previously described (50). Briefly, the mouse-derived tumor cells were seeded at various concentrations from 200 cells/well to 1 cell/well in a 100 μL sphere culture medium and cultured for 7 d. The number of wells containing spheres for each seeding cell number was counted and then analyzed using an online ELDA software (http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda).

Lectin Microarray.

To analyze the GPNMB glycan profile, an antibody-overlay lectin microarray was performed with modifications (51). Immunopurified, heat-denatured GPNMB (2 ng) was diluted in 60 μL phosphate-buffered saline with 1% Triton X-100 (PBSTx) and applied to a lectin microarray slide (LecChip 45-uni; Precision System Science, Japan) containing 45 lectins (SI Appendix, Table S5). Incubation occurred overnight at 20 °C. After incubation, human IgG (20 μg/well; Sigma-Aldrich) was added for 30 min at 20 °C to block unintended lectin–antibody interactions. The slide was then washed with PBSTx and incubated with a biotinylated anti-GPNMB antibody (100 ng/well) and human IgG (20 μg/well). Following further washes, Cy3-conjugated streptavidin (200 ng/well; Cytiva, USA) was added for 30 min at 20 °C. After a final wash, the slide was scanned using a GlycoStation Reader 2300 (emukk, Japan).

BLI.

Binding affinities between GPNMB and Siglec-Fc fusion proteins were measured using BLI on a BLItz system (ForteBio). HIS-tagged GPNMB was immobilized, and serial dilutions of Siglec-7-Fc, Siglec-9-Fc, and Siglec-15-Fc were used as analytes. Kinetic parameters (KD, kon, koff) were calculated using a 1:1 binding model. (Additional experimental parameters are available in SI Appendix, Methods).

RNA-seq.

Four RNA samples were collected from THP-1 under specified conditions: THP- 1, THP-1+Hs578T sphere (THP-1ctl), THP-1+GPsi1 Hs578T sphere (THP- 1si1), and THP-1+GPsi2 Hs578T sphere (THP-1si2). RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer with the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano kit. Library preparation used 1,250 ng RNA with the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module and NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (12 final PCR cycles). Library quality was verified with the Agilent DNA 7500 kit. Sequencing was performed on a NextSeq 500 using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit v2.5 (75 cycles, 36+36 bp paired-end). The FASTA file was analyzed using CLC Genomics Workbench 23.02. Single-cell library preparation and RNA-seq and open data analysis details are in SI Appendix, Method.

In Silico Molecular Docking.

Structural models of human GPNMB were generated using AlphaFold2, and docking simulations with Siglecs were performed using HDOCK. Glycan docking to Siglec-9 was carried out using AutoDock Vina and Gaussian-optimized ligands. Docking was constrained to biologically relevant domains (GPNMB NTD/PKD; Siglec V-set), and binding poses were visualized with UCSF Chimera. Expanded docking protocols are described in SI Appendix, Methods and Table S6.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Parametric tests, including Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc analyses, were used where applicable. Nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons) were applied when parametric assumptions were not met. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated for correlation analysis.
