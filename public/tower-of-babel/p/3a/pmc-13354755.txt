Watch and Wait Strategy for Locally Advanced Rectal Cancer: Update From Results of Recent Clinical Trials
Authors: Ruocheng Yin, Zhigang Chen, Xinliang Liu, Dan Zong, Xia He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLocally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) is a common malignant tumor in the digestive system. Currently, despite growing recognition of personalized medicine, systematic risk stratification and full incorporation of patient preferences for anal preservation and treatment tolerance remain challenging in some clinical settings. This review summarizes the latest clinical research progress on the watch‐and‐wait (W&W) strategy for LARC, including the applicable population of W&W, the factors predicting tumor regression, and the methods to increase the W&W rate. By synthesizing the current evidence, this review aims to inform clinical decision‐making and advance the practice of evidence‐based precision medicine.Keywords: immunotherapy, locally advanced rectal cancer, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, total neoadjuvant therapy, watch and wait

Introduction

Locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), which clinically encompasses stages T3–T4 and/or node‐positive (N+) disease without distant metastasis, accounts for approximately 70% of all rectal cancer cases. Traditional treatments mostly adopt a comprehensive treatment strategy that includes neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT), followed by total mesorectal excision (TME), and then adjuvant chemotherapy (aCT) [1, 2, 3]. In modern practice, LARC management is coordinated by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) to deliver tailored, multimodal care. However, despite the oncological efficacy of standard radical surgery, it is frequently associated with significant short‐term and long‐term surgical morbidities. These include anastomotic leakage, pelvic infections, permanent colostomy, and severe urogenital or sexual dysfunction. Moreover, a profound proportion of patients experience low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), characterized by fecal incontinence, frequency, and urgency, which severely impairs postoperative quality of life (QoL) [4]. In clinical practice, the lack of systematic risk stratification frameworks and the oversight of critical decision‐making factors, such as patient preferences for anal preservation and treatment tolerance, lead to the indiscriminate application of the standard of care (SoC) [4]. Therefore, balancing oncological safety against surgical morbidity within an MDT framework remains a critical challenge, and achieving individualized, precise treatment for LARC patients is largely unresolved.

Of note, a major therapeutic breakthrough has emerged in a distinct molecular subgroup of LARC: patients with mismatch repair–deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability–high (MSI‐H) tumors can achieve remarkable clinical complete response (cCR) rates of nearly 100% and favorable long‐term outcomes with neoadjuvant immunotherapy alone [5]. The cCR status, characterized by the complete absence of detectable tumor cells across digital rectal, endoscopic, and radiological examinations, yields highly favorable long‐term outcomes. Consequently, this exceptional cohort is systematically excluded from the current analysis. This review instead focuses on the predominant clinical challenge—the management of LARC patients with proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) or microsatellite stable (MSS) status.

In this context, the W&W strategy has gained substantial momentum as a viable organ‐preserving alternative for selected patients who achieve a cCR following neoadjuvant therapy (NAT). This paradigm challenges the historical dogma of mandatory surgery for all, aiming to spare patients the morbidity of radical resection without compromising oncological safety. Accordingly, this review systematically synthesizes the latest clinical evidence supporting the W&W strategy specifically in pMMR/MSS LARC. We will critically evaluate its evolving patient selection criteria, reliable clinical and molecular predictors of tumor regression, patterns and management of local regrowth, and the efficacy of novel treatment intensification strategies, including total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) and radiotherapy boost techniques, designed to maximize cCR rates and expand the pool of candidates eligible for non‐operative management. This article seeks to establish a modern, evidence‐based framework for clinical decision‐making by synthesizing results from recent high‐impact trials and consensus guidelines, thereby promoting the advancement of precision oncology within this intricate and prevalent patient demographic.

W&W Strategy

Origin

The management of LARC has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past two decades, with the W&W strategy emerging as a paradigm‐shifting approach that challenges traditional surgical doctrines. This evolution has been characterized by sequential solutions to critical clinical problems, supported by an expanding body of high‐quality evidence. The development timeline of the W&W strategy is shown in Figure 1.

The development timeline of the W&W strategy.

Phase 1: Proof of Concept and Exploration

The initial clinical problem centered on the significant morbidity associated with radical surgery following nCRT; particularly, many patients achieving the pathological complete response (pCR) rate were potentially overtreated with surgery [6]. This dilemma prompted Habr‐Gama et al. to pioneer the W&W approach, demonstrating in their landmark 2004 study that patients with clinical cCR could achieve excellent outcomes without immediate surgery, with a 5‐year overall survival of 100% and disease‐free survival of 92% [7]. However, this innovative approach revealed new clinical challenges: the lack of standardized cCR criteria and concerns about local regrowth rates. The solution emerged through multicenter validation studies and the establishment of the International Watch & Wait Database (IWWD) in 2014, which consolidated data from multiple institutions [8]. The IWWD analysis demonstrated manageable local regrowth rates with successful salvage surgery in most cases, addressing safety concerns and validating the strategy's oncological soundness [8].

Phase 2: Evidence Accumulation and Dissemination

The subsequent clinical problem centered on optimizing patient selection and increasing cCR rates. This was addressed through two key developments: the standardization of cCR assessment using multimodal criteria (digital rectal exam, endoscopy, and MRI) and the introduction of TNT [8, 9]. The shift from an “opportunistic” to an “intentional watch and wait” strategy represented a significant evolution in treatment philosophy [9]. Recent evidence has further strengthened this approach. The OPRA trial demonstrated that TNT could significantly increase organ preservation (OP) rates, effectively transforming W&W from an opportunistic approach to a planned therapeutic goal [10, 11]. Furthermore, the previously published RAPIDO trial and PRODIGE 23 trial demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemotherapy before chemoradiotherapy significantly increased pCR rates, facilitating more W&W approaches and bringing new thoughts to the treatment of LARC [12, 13, 14, 15].

Phase 3: Precision and New Frontiers

Most recently, the clinical challenge of managing rectal cancer led to another innovative solution. Research into immunotherapy for rectal cancer patients shows promise, with studies (NCT06304597) exploring novel methods such as fluorescent molecular endoscopy to visualize PD‐1/PD‐L1 expression, potentially identifying patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Current research continues to address remaining challenges through advanced technologies. The circulating free DNA fragmentomics approach demonstrates potential in predicting pathological response to nCRT, which could improve patient selection for W&W [16]. Similarly, artificial intelligence (AI) models are being developed to integrate multi‐modal data (MRI, CT, endoscopy images, and genomics) to more accurately predict pCR, cCR, metastasis, and disease‐free survival. A 2025 pooled analysis of the CAO/ARO/AIO‐12 and OPRA randomized phase II trials, involving 628 patients, provided further high‐level evidence [10]. It concluded that for LARC patients with a good response to NAT, the W&W strategy was a safe and feasible treatment option, with no significant differences in disease‐free survival, distant recurrence‐free survival, local recurrence‐free survival, or overall survival compared to those undergoing mandatory TME [10]. The evolution of the W&W strategy demonstrates how sequential solutions to clinical problems can fundamentally transform treatment paradigms. From initial skepticism to current acceptance, the strategy has matured through rigorous scientific investigation, with recent high‐quality evidence confirming its role in modern rectal cancer management. Ongoing research focusing on molecular profiling, advanced imaging, and biomarker development promises to further refine this OP approach. Crucially, the successful implementation and surveillance of the W&W strategy cannot be achieved by a single specialty; it requires an integrated, high‐functioning MDT framework. From the initial multi‐modal restaging assessment to the intensive long‐term follow‐up and timely execution of salvage TME upon local regrowth, a collaborative and shared decision‐making model is paramount. This emphasizes patient‐centered treatment plans, enabling patients to avoid surgical morbidity and achieve clinical benefits while maximizing their QoL [17].

Applicable Population

In clinical practice, not all patients derive the maximum benefit from surgical intervention. Studies have shown that patients who achieve pCR, defined as the absence of detectable viable tumor cells in the resected primary tumor and regional lymph nodes (ypT0N0), following NAT have a favorable prognosis [18]. Following nCRT and radical surgery, surgical specimens from select patients revealed no residual tumor cells. Patients achieving pCR exhibited an excellent prognosis, with reported rates of 0.7% for local recurrence, 8.7% for distant metastasis, 90.2% for 5‐year OS, and 87% for 5‐year DFS [19]. Theoretically, preoperative efficacy evaluations for these patients should indicate cCR, defined as the absence of residual tumor via clinical and imaging assessments (cT0N0). Therefore, patients achieving cCR following NAT may represent a candidate population for non‐surgical management.

The non‐surgical management option, known as the watch‐and‐wait (W&W) strategy. After NAT, restaging assessment is typically performed at 8–12 weeks to allow for maximal tumor regression and to accurately identify candidates [20]. Clinically, digital rectal examination, endoscopy, rectal MRI (T2WI/DWI) combined with pathological biopsy, CEA, transrectal ultrasound, thoracoabdominal MR and CT, and PET‐CT are generally used to determine whether the patient has achieved cCR or near‐cCR [21]. The determination of cCR relies on a combination of digital rectal examination (no palpable mass), high‐definition endoscopy (flat white scar or normal mucosa with negative biopsies), and rectal MRI (absence of intermediate/high T2 signal and no diffusion restriction on DWI) [2, 22]. If these stringent criteria are met, patients may enter a W&W program, which involves intensive follow‐up: digital rectal examination and endoscopy are performed every 3–4 months in the first 2 years and semiannually from year 3 to year 5. Pelvic high‐resolution MRI is conducted semiannually within the first 3 years and annually thereafter [2, 22]. Serum CEA is tested synchronously with endoscopic assessment, and chest‐abdomen‐pelvis CT is acquired yearly [2, 22]. It is useful to acknowledge that W&W protocols are not standardized across institutions; considerable heterogeneity exists in the choice of modalities, follow‐up intervals, and total surveillance duration, and no strong evidence base supports any single approach [23]. For instance, Finlay et al. revised their protocol by eliminating PET‐CT from surveillance and shortening the overall duration after finding no added value [24].

For near‐cCR patients with higher local regrowth risk, the follow‐up intervals should be appropriately shortened [21]. Near‐cCR denotes an intermediate response with subtle mucosal lesions and faint focal tumor signal on MRI, negative biopsies, and regressed benign lymph nodes [25, 26]. Consensus remains elusive regarding the inclusion of near‐cCR and <ycT2N0 patients in W&W protocols. While the Chinese Consensus (2024) allows local excision for near‐cCR patients who need to keep their sphincter, the NCCN 2025 guidelines do not give clear advice on this [21]. The 2021 International Consensus Recommendations caution that local excision in near‐cCR patients, while enabling pathological confirmation, may complicate subsequent salvage TME [27]. Clinical research data further reveal the potential risks of this strategy: the PKUCH‐R01 trial conducted by Peking University Cancer Hospital showed that the 2‐year local regrowth rate in the near‐cCR group reached 42.9%, significantly higher than the 12.9% in the cCR group [28]. The OPRA study included cCR/near‐cCR patients in the W&W cohort, and its 33% local regrowth rate was higher than that reported in similar studies, but the study did not stratify the analysis to see whether the regrowth cases were mainly from the near‐cCR subgroup [20]. However, there is still a lack of high‐level evidence‐based evidence on the optimal treatment decision for near‐cCR patients, and further research is urgently needed [29]. In conclusion, there is still a need for further standardization and clarification on how to precisely identify the target population for the W&W strategy.

Predictors

cCR refers to the state in which no evidence of tumor residue (yield clinical T0N0, ycT0N0) is found in the primary lesion and regional lymph nodes after NAT [21]. The implementation of W&W strategy is related to whether the patient achieves CCR, many cCR patients are confirmed as pCR after surgery. So it is important to predict the influencing factors of cCR and pCR. The achievement of complete tumor regression is influenced by multiple factors. Al‐Sukhni et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from 23,747 patients in the U.S. National Cancer Database (NCDB) between 2006 and 2011, identifying pathological characteristics (e.g., tumor grade, clinical T/N stage), treatment parameters (e.g., RT dose, interval between nCRT and surgery), and healthcare system factors (e.g., insurance coverage) as variables associated with pCR [30].

Similarly, cCR rates have been shown to exhibit a negative correlation with tumor invasion depth, length, tumor burden, and CRM status. Specifically, cCR rates for T4 tumors, lesions ≥ 7 cm, and volumes > 120 cm3 were 5.4%, 2.7%, and 0%, respectively [31]. Research presented at the 2021 ASTRO Annual Meeting demonstrated that patients with gross tumor volumes (GTV) < 18.7 cc exhibited a pCR rate 3.1 times higher than those with GTV ≥ 18.7 cc, with 90% predictive sensitivity [32]. A separate retrospective analysis revealed tumor mobility status as a determinant of regression and recurrence, with CR rates of 49%, 22%, and 9% observed for mobile, partially fixed, and fixed tumors, respectively; all fixed tumors experienced local recurrence [33]. Additionally, patients harboring KRAS mutations (particularly at G12V or G13D loci) exhibited poorer responses to nCRT compared to KRAS wild‐type counterparts, with pCR rates of 15% versus 34% (p = 0.001) [34]. No significant association was observed between TP53 mutations and pCR (p = 0.874) [34].

A prospective phase II trial (NCT02008656) investigated associations between baseline clinical characteristics (e.g., age, ethnicity) and MRI‐derived parameters (e.g., T/N stage, MRF/EMVI status) with OP outcomes [11]. EMVI positivity and CRM involvement, particularly nodal positivity, were identified as predictors of incomplete tumor regression post‐NAT [35]. Notably, lateral lymph node metastasis occurs in 10%–30% of mid‐to‐low rectal cancer cases at initial diagnosis, presenting three clinical challenges: First, significant heterogeneity exists in diagnostic criteria due to reliance on imaging findings in the absence of preoperative pathological confirmation, resulting in inconsistencies across guidelines. Second, complete resolution following nCRT occurs infrequently, with only 5% of lateral lymph nodes ≥ 7 mm achieving full regression; the majority demonstrate poor regression (77%) or no response (18%), correlating with elevated local recurrence rates. The ESMO guideline recommends that high‐risk criteria include cT4a or cT4b, MRF+, cN2 (4 suspicious nodes), EMVI+, and lateral lymph node enlargement of 7 mm [3]. Third, controversy persists regarding the necessity and extent of surgical resection. Therefore, predicting tumor regression following NAT accurately—through the development of predictive technologies or identification of reliable indicators—and achieving an optimal balance between treatment efficacy and functional preservation remain significant clinical challenges that warrant further investigation.

While numerous clinical, pathological, and molecular factors have been associated with tumor regression following NAT, their interplay remains incompletely characterized. For example, a larger tumor volume (GTV ≥ 18.7 cc) is frequently associated with an advanced T‐stage and lateral lymph node involvement, which collectively reduce the probability of achieving a cCR [31, 32]. Similarly, KRAS mutations may synergize with EMVI positivity to further reduce pCR [34, 35]. To translate these predictors into clinical practice, several institutions have begun developing multivariate prediction models. These models integrate imaging features (e.g., MRI‐based tumor volume, diffusion‐weighted imaging), molecular profiling (e.g., KRAS status), and clinical parameters (e.g., tumor mobility, CRM status) to generate individualized probability scores for cCR [30, 35]. Such tools can assist clinicians in identifying ideal candidates for a W&W strategy and personalizing the intensity of neoadjuvant regimens. Future efforts should focus on external validation of these models and their integration into prospective clinical trials.

Failure Modes

While W&W can preserve the normal function of the organ, there is also a risk of tumor recurrence. In clinical practice, local regrowth is the main reason for treatment failure, happening in 20%–30% of cases, which is much higher than the 7.7%–15.6% recurrence rate seen in patients who achieve pCR, and most of this regrowth happens within 2–3 years after treatment and usually appears as intracavitary recurrence in about 80% of cases [36, 37, 38, 39]. Regrowth risk correlates significantly with tumor location and baseline invasion depth, with lower tumor positions or advanced T‐stage associated with higher recurrence rates [38]. A 2021 ASTRO Annual Meeting study further identified tumor size (maximum diameter > 5 cm) as a predictor of increased regrowth frequency and inferior DFS [40]. Luckly, salvage surgery for regrowth demonstrates high success rates, with comparable DFS to immediate radical resection [41, 42]. The low incidence of locoregional lymph node metastasis may reflect hematogenous dissemination predominance or technological limitations in detecting micrometastases. Distant metastasis remains the primary determinant of patient prognosis, underscoring the need for improved systemic micrometastasis monitoring in W&W protocols.

The high incidence of local recurrence may be attributed to occult residual lesions. After NAT, residual cancer cells exhibit an uneven distribution across intestinal wall layers, with the mucosal, submucosal, and muscularis propria layers demonstrating residual rates of 13%, 56%, and 98%, respectively, primarily localized to the leading edge of the tumor invasion site [43]. This phenomenon may be associated with tumor invasiveness heterogeneity and microenvironmental variations, including the submucosal layer's abundant vascular supply and the mucosal layer's proximity to the intestinal lumen. The positive detection rate of conventional endoscopic biopsy has been reported to be only 12.9%. To address this limitation, Wang et al. initiated a multicenter study evaluating transanal multipoint full‐layer puncture biopsy (TMFP; NCT04743102). TMFP demonstrated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and accuracy rates of 100%, 87.5%, 90.9%, and 94.4%, respectively, for pCR assessment [44]. This research result indicates that TMFP is a safe and feasible technique for assessing treatment response. Moreover, it shows high sensitivity and accuracy in predicting pCR, suggesting its potential as a valuable supplement to the current cCR criteria [45]. However, the technique demands a high level of operator proficiency and is associated with a prolonged learning curve. Therefore, how to promote and apply this technology in clinical practice requires further exploration.

Furthermore, circumferential rectal tumors treated with TNT are at risk for developing stenosis [46]. This condition, which complicates the assessment of clinical response, is clinically significant: a prior study found that 75% of patients with stenosis still had residual disease upon surgical pathology [46]. Consequently, these factors underscore the inherent difficulty of implementing nonoperative management in this patient population [46].

Feasibility

For patients achieving cCR post‐NAT, the W&W strategy minimizes surgical morbidity, optimizes intestinal function preservation, and enhances survival and QoL. These benefits have been validated across multiple studies (Table 1). The relevant guidelines state that for patients with local tumor recurrence during the W&W process, the TME is the standard treatment plan. We have summarized the relevant research data and found that the success rate of postoperative remediation is also very high. This gives doctors and patients more confidence when choosing W&W treatment in clinical practice.

Intestinal Function and QoL

Preservation of bowel function and QoL constitutes a main consideration for LARC patients electing the W&W strategy. Hupkens and Maas et al. demonstrated superior overall health scores and reduced incidence of LARS in the W&W cohort (33% vs. 66.7%), alongside better‐preserved bowel and urogenital function [70]. The OnCoRe trial reported significantly higher stoma‐free survival in W&W patients (74% vs. 47%, p < 0.0001) [55]. Longitudinal Danish cohort data (NCT00952926) revealed a 69% 2‐year complete bowel control rate, while Custers et al. observed stable severe bowel dysfunction rates (24%–25.3%) over 24 months [51, 71]. Notably, QoL assessments exhibit multidimensional heterogeneity: female patients reported lower overall QoL and higher LARS scores than males, though RT modality (long‐ vs. short‐course) showed no functional impact [72], Retrospective analyses may underestimate functional risks due to baseline disparities (e.g., older age, comorbidities in W&W cohorts), necessitating prospective studies to mitigate confounding [73]. A recently published meta‐analysis (total of 923 patients) demonstrated that a pooled analysis of fecal incontinence scores showed a significant advantage for W&W, and a qualitative synthesis of low anterior resection syndrome scores favored W&W [74]. Therefore, W&W may offer functional advantages without compromising QoL [74]. The decision to perform W&W should be individualized, balancing potential functional benefits against tumor risks [74]. Despite the referenced data, there is still a lack of understanding of the full benefits of W&W in the treatment of LARC. Furthermore, aspects such as baseline differences in QoL may affect the true effect of W&W on long‐term QoL. Further research is needed to explore the clinical benefits regarding intestinal function and QoL.

Cost

The W&W strategy requires regular follow‐up evaluation, which puts a certain burden on the patient's economy, and common follow‐up evaluation items include digital rectal examination, colonoscopy, pathological biopsy, MRI, rectal ultrasound, serum CEA level, chest and abdominal CT, and PET‐CT. In clinical practice, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the patient's economic situation for further diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, the cost of the W&W strategy is also an important factor to consider. A previous review comparing the economics of surgery with the W&W strategy suggests that the economic cost of W&W after NAT with LARC may be cost‐effective and cost‐saving [75]. Furthermore, Gani et al. conducted a cost simulation analysis on four German hospitals participating in the CAO/ARO/AIO‐16 trial. Based on the “Gebührenordnung für Ärzte” and “diagnosis‐related group G‐16B system,” the study found that the estimated cost for optimal disease control (with no regrowth within 5 years) was €6344, and for the worst‐case scenario (25% regrowth requiring salvage surgery) it was €8299, compared to €14,511 for immediate surgery [76]. In addition, a recent Australian real‐world cost analysis by Cooper et al. compared the W&W surveillance protocol (including frequent sigmoidoscopy, MRI, CT, PET, and CEA) with standard surgical resection. They found that the 5‐year surveillance cost for W&W was AUD 45,246, significantly lower than AUD 87,473 for ultra‐low anterior resection/loop ileostomy/closure of loop and routine postoperative surveillance or AUD 82,290 for abdominoperineal resection followed by routine 5‐year surveillance [77]. These findings from different healthcare systems consistently indicate that W&W does not increase financial burden compared to standard care and may even offer substantial cost savings.

Attempts to Increase W&W Rate

A series of studies have investigated strategies to enhance complete tumor regression and expand non‐surgical treatment options for LARC, with promising results reported.

Local Boost RT

Local boost RT can enhance the radiobiological effect in the tumor area, increase the degree of tumor regression, and thereby increase the possibility of achieving cCR. The evolution of rectal cancer boost RT traces to 1973, when Papillon pioneered contact X‐ray brachytherapy (CXB) [78]. The Lyon R96‐02 phase II trial validated this approach, comparing EBRT (39 Gy/13 f) with EBRT plus CXB boost (85 Gy/3 f) [79]. The experimental group demonstrated significantly higher cCR (24% vs. 2%) and anal sphincter preservation rates (76% vs. 44%) compared to the control group [79]. The OPERA phase III study optimized this strategy: Group B received EBRT (45 Gy/25 f) combined with CXB boost (90 Gy/3 f), achieving superior cCR rates (92% vs. 64%, p < 0.001) and TME‐free survival (79% vs. 57%, p = 0.0026) versus Group A (EBRT boost alone: 9 Gy/5 f), without increased toxicity (ostomy rates: 10 vs. 15 cases) [80].

Another study analyzed the radiation treatment doses received by 880 patients with cCR included in the IWWD database. The results showed that 79% of the patients received doses ranging from 45 to 50 grays, while 21% of the patients received doses ranging from 54 to 60 grays [22]. In fact, the radiation dose–response curve shows that as the radiation dose increases, the tumor shrinks more, and the dose required for a 50% CR is 92 Gy [81]. Delivering this dose via external beam radiation (EBRT) would result in severe RT‐related toxicity, rendering this approach impractical. Intracavitary brachytherapy modalities, such as high‐dose‐rate brachytherapy (iridium‐192) and contact X‐ray brachytherapy (50 kV X‐ray), may safely and effectively reduce local residual or recurrent lesions [79] (For a summary of local boost RT studies, see Table 3).

The Danish WW [51], WW2 [82], and WW3 (NCT04095299) phase II clinical trials utilized a dose‐escalation RT regimen. The 2019 ASCO‐published 5‐year follow‐up data from the WW study demonstrated a cCR rate of 78.4%, a local recurrence rate of 31%, and an OS rate of 85% [87]. Updated WW2 results in 2022 showed further efficacy improvements [82]. Notably, while dose escalation enhanced tumor control, LARS incidence demonstrated a bimodal distribution, with no, mild, and severe cases observed in 37%, 28%, and 35% of patients, respectively, underscoring the need to balance oncological outcomes with functional preservation [82].

Investigators have attempted to identify residual lesions via imaging modalities, thereby enabling precise escalation of local RT doses. The THUNDER‐2 study was conducted in patients with cT2‐3N0‐2M0 or cT4N0‐2M0 rectal cancer involving the sphincter, utilizing the Early Tumor Regression Index (ERI) to assess tumor regression and implementing MRI‐guided RT (MRIgRT) to escalate the radiation dose to 60.1 Gy in non‐responders [88]. It was demonstrated that 52.4% of patients exhibited good tolerance to the radiation dose escalation [88]. In addition, the AIDA study administered a 10 Gy/2 f dose escalation to active lesions identified via PET‐CT 2 weeks post‐RT initiation in LARC patients [84]. Through comparative analysis of baseline and interim PET/CT, 18F‐FDG‐PET/CT achieved 77.8% accuracy in predicting pCR when interim PET/CT was employed [84]. Results demonstrated that dose escalation to the reduced target volume during the concluding phase of chemoradiotherapy (CRT) was well tolerated and associated with an increased pCR rate [84].

In summary, local boost RT may offer clinical benefits to a majority of patients, with long‐term survival rates comparable to radical surgery and a favorable toxicity profile. However, there are several considerations warrant attention: First, the majority of these studies included early‐stage rectal cancer patients, where RT is not guideline‐recommended. Second, further research is needed to explore and validate the impact of local boost RT on local recurrence. Finally, no established consensus exists regarding optimal RT techniques or dose parameters for escalation. Thus, for low‐lying rectal cancer patients with strong functional preservation preferences, local boost RT represents a viable therapeutic alternative. Future validation through large‐scale phase III clinical trials is required to evaluate long‐term functional preservation efficacy and survival outcomes.

Prolonging the Interval Between NAT and Reassessment

Radiobiological principles indicate that RT‐induced tumor cell death exhibits time‐dependent characteristics. As the waiting interval is prolonged, the incidence of CR will be higher, enabling more patients to meet the entry criteria for W&W. Ionizing radiation predominantly induces DNA double‐strand breaks, terminating mitosis through prolonged cell cycle arrest and repair mechanisms [89, 90]. Extending the neoadjuvant‐to‐evaluation interval may thus enhance tumor regression. The Lyon R90‐01 trial (1991–1995) demonstrated significantly higher cCR rates with 6–8 weeks versus 2‐week intervals, without increased toxicity [91]. A meta‐analysis of 7 studies (n = 3085) identified 10 weeks as the optimal interval for maximizing pCR rates in LARC [92]. Beyond 16–20 weeks, tumor regression plateaus, with no OS improvement observed [92]. Concerns persist regarding prolonged intervals potentially exacerbating pelvic fibrosis, surgical complexity, and recurrence risks [92]. These findings suggest that extending the interval between NAT and re‐evaluation within a reasonable time window may better balance tumor regression and surgical safety; however, the optimal timing requires comprehensive evaluation based on the biological characteristics of individual tumors. To optimize NAT for LARC, a phase III randomized controlled trial (GRECCAR‐6) was conducted by the French GRECCAR study group to systematically investigate this issue [93, 94]. The trial enrolled 265 patients with cT3‐4 or N+ low‐ to mid‐rectal cancer, who were randomly assigned to undergo TME either 7 weeks (W7 group) or 11 weeks (W11 group) after NAT [93]. No significant difference in pCR (ypT0N0) was observed between the W7 and W11 groups (15.0% [20/133] vs. 17.4% [23/132], respectively; p = 0.598) [93]. The 11‐week group exhibited a significantly higher complication rate (32.8% vs. 19.2%; p = 0.014) and morbidity rate (44.5% vs. 32.0%; p = 0.040), along with inferior mesorectal excision quality (complete excision: 78.7% vs. 90.0%; p = 0.016) [93]. This trial demonstrated that extending the waiting period to 11 weeks following nCRT did not improve the pCR rate after surgical resection. Prolonged waiting intervals were associated with increased morbidity and greater technical complexity during surgical resection. These findings provide critical evidence for optimizing surgical timing after NAT in rectal cancer, underscoring the need to avoid indiscriminate extension of waiting periods and to tailor treatment plans according to individualized factors, including tumor response and complication risk [95].

TNT and Immunotherapy‐Based Total Neoadjuvant Therapy

TNT involves moving part of the postoperative aCT to the preoperative stage, allowing rectal cancer patients to complete all chemotherapy and radiotherapy while their physical condition is still favorable before surgery. TNT not only maximizes tumor volume reduction and eliminates potential systemic micrometastases to enhance preoperative treatment efficacy but also improves patients' long‐term prognosis. Furthermore, compared to NAT, TNT may offer superior survival benefits and help increase patient treatment compliance (see Table 4). The ESMO guideline recommends that for patients with LARC and additional risk factors (cN2, cMRF+, EMVI+), the SoC is to use the TNT approach [3]. The French UNICANCER‐PRODIGE 23 phase III trial provided the first confirmation through long‐term follow‐up data that an mFOLFIRINOX‐based TNT regimen (modified oxaliplatin, irinotecan, and fluorouracil) significantly enhances survival outcomes [12, 13, 14]. Notably, no significant difference was observed in post‐metastasis median survival between groups (36.5 vs. 35.7 months, p = 0.71), demonstrating that the mFOLFIRINOX regimen neither triggered the adjuvant therapy‐related shortened survival (ATRESS) phenomenon nor diminished its sustained antimetastatic efficacy [12, 13, 14]. This study's milestone significance lies in demonstrating the first phase III trial‐confirmed OS improvement from NAT since the 1997 Swedish rectal cancer trial [14, 110]. Its benefits may stem from early micrometastasis clearance and improved treatment adherence (higher aCT completion rates in the experimental group) [14, 110]. The long‐term follow‐up data in PRODIGE 23 trial establish mFOLFIRINOX as the evidence‐based standard for LARC treatment, offering both survival advantages and safety profiles without secondary leukemia risk, thereby advancing TNT strategy [14]. The Dutch multicenter Phase III RAPIDO study demonstrated that for high‐risk LARC, the TNT—comprising preoperative short‐course radiotherapy (SCRT) followed by 18 weeks of chemotherapy—compared to conventional chemoradiotherapy plus aCT, significantly reduced the 3‐year disease‐related treatment failure rate (23.7% vs. 30.4%) and distant metastasis rate (20.0% vs. 26.8%), while achieving a higher pCR rate (28% vs. 14%) [15]. The findings of this study indicate that TNT can increase the pCR rate and may contribute to OP. Furthermore, early assessment of treatment response and determination of systemic therapy requirements requires further optimization to continuously reduce the risk of distant metastasis.

While TNT alone has demonstrated substantial improvements in pCR rates and survival outcomes, its efficacy remains inherently limited in patients with pMMR/MSS tumors, which are generally immunologically “cold” and resistant to conventional immunotherapy [111, 112, 113]. To overcome this limitation and further amplify tumor‐specific immune activation, recent investigative efforts have shifted toward combining TNT with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). This integrated approach—termed immunotherapy‐based total neoadjuvant therapy (iTNT)—aims to harness the synergistic potential of radiotherapy‐induced immunogenic cell death and chemotherapy‐mediated myeloid suppression with enhanced T‐cell cytotoxicity facilitated by PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade.

Data from multiple prospective clinical studies have shown that the pCR rate of TNT is 10%–35%, and the pCR rate of TNT combined with immunization is 30%–60%. A 2022 ASCO‐reported phase II trial (NCT04911517) achieved a 58.3% pCR rate and 100% sphincter preservation rate using concurrent tislelizumab administration during nCRT [96]. Similarly, a 2022 trial (NCT05215379) evaluating sintilimab consolidation chemotherapy post‐NAT in ultra‐low T1‐3aN0‐1M0 rectal cancer patients demonstrated promising outcomes [97]. Multiple ongoing investigations continue to refine this paradigm. The phase II TORCH trial (NCT04518280) conducted by Zhang et al. assessed whether the integration of PD‐1 inhibitor with TNT could enhance CR rates and enable implementation of W&W strategy in pMMR/MSS LARC patients [98]. CR rates of 56.5% in Group A and 54.2% in Group B were observed, significantly exceeding historical benchmarks (p < 0.001), with pCR rates reaching 50% in both cohorts [98]. This investigation represents the first validation of iTNT combined with a W&W strategy in pMMR/MSS rectal cancer. Of the 32 patients achieving cCR, 30 (93.8%) maintained tumor‐free status, demonstrating the feasibility of OP strategies [98]. Postoperative analysis revealed pCR confirmation in 34.2% of patients without imaging‐defined cCR, indicating potential immunotherapy‐induced “pseudo‐residual” phenomena and highlighting the need for optimized response assessment criteria [98]. The temporal relationship between RT and immunotherapy was further investigated, revealing that SCRT‐first sequencing may amplify synergistic effects through extended post‐radiation immune activation windows [98]. The iTNT regimen demonstrated significant CR rate improvements in pMMR/MSS LARC compared to historical controls, with manageable toxicity profiles. Future definitive investigations will prioritize SCRT followed by immunochemotherapy regimens [99].

To optimize LARC neoadjuvant strategies, investigators are systematically evaluating novel RT‐immunotherapy integration models. The Chinese UNION Phase III trial first demonstrated that SCRT followed by camrelizumab (PD‐1 inhibitor) plus CAPOX chemotherapy significantly increased pCR rates [100]. The experimental group achieved a pCR rate of 39.8% (95% CI: 30.7–49.5) versus 15.3% in controls (OR = 3.7, p < 0.001), with combined pCR and cCR rates reaching 41.6% [100]. The experimental group maintained a 38.6% pCR rate in high‐risk patients (cT4/cN2/positive vascular/circumferential margin involvement), with 94.2% anal preservation rates supporting OP strategies [100]. Notably, this study represents the first phase III trial to validate the synergistic efficacy of SCRT combined with immunochemotherapy, offering a novel therapeutic option for patients with pMMR/MSS tumors (pCR rate: 40%) [100]. The underlying mechanism may involve synergy between RT‐induced immunogenic cell death and PD‐1 inhibition [100]. Furthermore, SCRT minimizes lymphocyte depletion while preserving immune function [100]. The UNION trial establishes a novel LARC neoadjuvant paradigm through triple‐modality synergy, marking significant progress in combined local‐systemic treatment approaches. A randomized Phase II trial (NCT04304209) evaluated sintilimab (PD‐1 antibody) combined with nCRT for pMMR LARC treatment optimization [101]. The experimental group demonstrated superior CR rates (44.8% vs. 26.9%, p = 0.031) encompassing both cCR and pCR, with acceptable safety profiles [101]. Subgroup analyses identified PD‐L1 combined positive score (CPS ≥ 2) as a potential predictive biomarker [101]. CPS ≥ 2 patients in the experimental group achieved a 53.8% CR rate versus 23.7% in controls (p = 0.007) [101]. The research finding significantly enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment was observed in the experimental group, suggesting immune activation as a potential synergistic mechanism [101]. Conventional nCRT yields low pCR rates (< 20%) in MSS/pMMR tumors, while extensive radiation fields may compromise tumor‐draining lymph node (TDLN) immune cells, potentially limiting immunotherapy efficacy [102]. The mRCAT trial (NCT05972655) introduced a node‐sparing SCRT approach (25 Gy/5 f targeting the primary tumor bed) combined with CAPOX and tislelizumab [102]. This approach hypothesizes that preserving T progenitor cells (Tpex) in TDLNs while reversing T‐cell exhaustion via PD‐1 inhibition may simultaneously enhance antitumor immunity and mitigate radiation‐induced fibrosis [102]. Preliminary analyses suggest delayed surgery (14–15 weeks post‐SCRT) might improve pCR rates and reduce anastomotic leakage risk, though confirmatory data remain pending [102]. Recently, the STELLAR II study led by Professor Jin Jing's team from the Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences has achieved a significant breakthrough:By adopting an innovative combination of “SCRT + chemotherapy” followed by “immunotherapy,” the CR of these patients has been significantly increased from 25% to 45.5%. This means that more patients are expected to achieve a “clinical cure” and retain their anus, avoiding permanent colostomy surgery [108]. However, treatment intensification often comes at a price. It is worth noting that the incidence of grade 3–4 toxicity during immunochemotherapy in the iTNT group (34.5%) was higher than that observed in the TNT group (19.4%), though the toxicity was predominantly hematologic and manageable [108]. This requires further investigation and underscores the need for clinical vigilance regarding toxicity.

A recent SPRING‐01 trial further confirmed that in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, SCRT combined with sintilimab and capecitabine‐oxaliplatin as a TNT significantly increased the pCR rate while maintaining a manageable safety profile [109]. Collectively, these trials demonstrate that TNT prioritizes systemic treatment preoperatively, potentially enhancing therapeutic outcomes through immune activation while reducing postoperative locoregional recurrence and distant metastasis, thereby improving overall prognosis. The first reported phase II single‐arm trial (NCT04340401) conducted by Peking University Cancer Hospital demonstrated that incorporating PD‐1 blockade into a TNT regimen comprising induction chemoimmunotherapy and extended‐course nCRT yielded a pCR rate of 33.3% and a cCR rate of 48% [103, 104]. Subsequently, the multicenter NECTAR trial further supported this strategy, reporting a pCR rate of 40% with tislelizumab combined with chemoradiotherapy, along with a manageable safety profile in MSS/pMMR LARC patients [105]. Notably, the PRECAM study utilizing SCRT combined with CAPEOX and envafolimab achieved a remarkable pCR rate of 62.5% [106]. Furthermore, a multicenter randomized controlled trial led by Zhang Zhongtao's team provided the first randomized evidence that sequential PD‐1 inhibition following chemoradiotherapy significantly improved pCR rates, establishing a novel therapeutic paradigm for pMMR/MSS rectal cancer and informing the design of subsequent phase III trials [107]. In summary, these studies collectively indicate that strategically incorporating immune checkpoint inhibitors into TNT frameworks can substantially improve therapeutic outcomes in traditionally immune‐resistant pMMR/MSS LARC. However, special attention must also be paid to toxicity during immunotherapy. Further optimization of treatment sequencing and patient selection through translational research is warranted to validate and refine this promising approach.

Challenge and Outlook

The adoption of the W&W strategy in LARC, while promising, is accompanied by several clinical challenges that require careful consideration. Firstly, the accurate identification of a cCR remains a central issue. There is currently no internationally standardized and validated set of diagnostic criteria for cCR. Conventional assessment tools—including digital rectal examination, endoscopy, biopsy, and conventional MRI—lack the sensitivity to reliably detect minimal residual disease or consistently predict pCR. This diagnostic uncertainty can lead to two critical scenarios: undertreatment of patients with residual tumors misclassified as cCR, or overtreatment of patients who achieve cCR but undergo unnecessary surgery due to a false‐negative assessment. Recent studies, such as the CAO/ARO/AIO‐16 trial, have demonstrated that through a structured assessment protocol including delayed evaluations, a significant proportion of patients (37%) can achieve cCR, with 36% successfully avoiding immediate TME [67]. This underscores the potential of structured protocols but also highlights the variability in assessment methods across institutions. Secondly, the optimal timing, frequency, and components of response assessment and subsequent surveillance during the W&W period are not yet standardized. The interval between the completion of NAT and the first restaging, the schedule of follow‐up visits, and the selection of diagnostic tools directly impact the early detection of local regrowth and the patient's QoL. The combined analysis of the CAO/ARO/AIO‐12 and OPRA trials validated that a selective W&W strategy following TNT produced survival outcomes comparable to those of mandatory TME, thereby affirming the strategy's efficacy [10]. Nonetheless, the absence of a standardized surveillance protocol necessitates evidence‐based guidelines to reduce variability in management. Thirdly, managing the clinical aspects of tumor regrowth, especially the timing and effectiveness of salvage therapy, is a major problem. Although most local recurrences are amenable to salvage TME with high success rates (e.g., 100% in the CAO/ARO/AIO‐16 trial), successful outcomes are inextricably linked to early detection through rigorous follow‐up [67]. Furthermore, the choice of subsequent treatment upon recurrence—whether to proceed directly to TME, consider re‐irradiation, or intensify systemic therapy—requires an individualized risk–benefit analysis.

To address these challenges and enhance the precision and safety of the W&W strategy, future efforts should focus on the following frontier directions: Firstly, refining response assessment with advanced technologies. The integration of AI with medical imaging shows great promise. MRI‐based radiomics and deep learning can extract high‐dimensional data from images that are imperceptible to the human eye, potentially improving the prediction of treatment response and tumor regression grade. Beyond anatomical imaging, novel functional and molecular imaging techniques are emerging. For instance, research into PET‐derived metabolic parameters aims to non‐invasively predict tumor characteristics like MSI status [114]. Additionally, the development of novel dual‐target PET tracers, such as 68Ga‐pentixafor‐c(RGDfK), has demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing primary and metastatic lesions compared to conventional 18F‐FDG PET/CT [114]. These technologies could significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy in the W&W context. Secondly, leveraging liquid biopsies and biomarkers. The development and validation of predictive biomarkers, particularly circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), represent a paradigm shift toward dynamic risk stratification. Monitoring ctDNA levels before, during, and after NAT could objectively identify minimal residual disease, enabling tailored treatment intensification for high‐risk patients and supporting confidence in OP for those with undetectable ctDNA. Simultaneously, research into the tumor immune microenvironment is uncovering new biomarkers. For example, the DLX4‐ZC3HC1 regulatory axis has been identified as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in colorectal cancer, influencing both tumor proliferation and the immunosuppressive microenvironment [115]. Thirdly, personalizing strategies based on molecular subtypes. The remarkable success of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in dMMR/MSI‐H rectal cancer, as evidenced by the PICC trial showing a 5‐year overall survival rate of 100% in the combination therapy group, establishes a new benchmark [116]. For the majority of pMMR/MSS patients, iTNT is an area of intense investigation. Future strategies may involve leveraging novel biomarkers to select optimal candidates for iTNT or using immunomodulators to convert “cold” MSS tumors into “hot,” immunoresponsive ones [115]. Fourthly, implementing prospective registries and shared decision‐making. There remains an urgent need for more robust, long‐term data from randomized controlled trials. In the interim, the establishment of prospective, multinational registries can collect real‐world evidence on functional outcomes, QoL, and the long‐term oncological safety of W&W. Finally, the success of W&W is deeply personal. Therefore, integrating patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) and fostering shared decision‐making are crucial. Ensuring that treatment choices align with patient values and preferences, particularly regarding the trade‐offs between survival, OP, and potential risks, is paramount for the successful implementation of the W&W strategy in clinical practice.

The shift toward precision medicine in LARC extends beyond molecular and radiographic profiling to encompass patient values, psychological readiness, and treatment preferences. Shared decision‐making is paramount when considering W&W, as patients must weigh the benefits of OP against the uncertainties of tumor regrowth and the need for rigorous surveillance [117]. Psychological factors—including anxiety about recurrence, tolerance for uncertainty, and personal priorities regarding bowel function and QoL—significantly influence treatment adherence and satisfaction [71]. Effective physician‐patient communication, supported by decision aids and multidisciplinary consultation, can align therapeutic choices with individual patient contexts. This approach not only respects patient autonomy but also enhances treatment compliance and long‐term psychosocial outcomes [118].

The feasibility of W&W and organ‐preserving strategies varies considerably across healthcare systems. A cross‐sectional online survey of 34 questions involving multiple institutions regarding W&W for LARC: There was wide variation in how surgeons defined eligibility for W&W based on tumor location and NAT [119]. In high‐income countries with robust infrastructure for MRI, frequent endoscopic surveillance, and specialized brachytherapy, W&W is increasingly integrated into standard care [22]. In contrast, regions with limited access to advanced imaging, pathology services, or multidisciplinary tumor boards face significant barriers. Moreover, the financial burden of intensive follow‐up—including serial MRI, colonoscopy, and biomarker testing—may be prohibitive in low‐resource settings. These disparities underscore the need for adaptable, cost‐effective surveillance protocols and the development of telemedicine and centralized referral systems to broaden access to OP strategies worldwide.

Emerging evidence highlights the importance of nutritional status in modulating treatment tolerance, immune function, and overall outcomes in LARC. Pre‐treatment sarcopenia and low albumin levels have been associated with reduced completion rates of NAT and poorer response to chemoradiation [111]. Integrative nutritional support—including protein supplementation, omega‐3 fatty acids, and immunonutrition—may mitigate treatment‐related toxicity, preserve lean body mass, and improve therapeutic efficacy [111]. Ongoing trials, such as the “Tools To Be Fit” study (NCT05056077) for colorectal cancer survivors, are evaluating whether tailored nutritional interventions can enhance regression rates and qualify more patients for W&W, representing a promising adjunct to conventional oncologic care. A pilot study (NCT03063918) on personalized diet interventions for rectosigmoid cancer survivors further explores the feasibility of improving bowel function and QoL.

Summary

This article reviews the latest progress of the W&W strategy in pMMR/MSS LARC, focusing on its applicable population, predictive factors for complete tumor response, failure patterns, and strategies to improve the success rate of W&W. The W&W strategy is mainly applicable to patients who achieve cCR after NAT. The long‐term survival rate of such patients is comparable to that of those who achieve a pCR and undergo surgery while significantly improving QoL and intestinal function. Studies have shown that local recurrence is the main failure mode of W&W, but the overall success rate of salvage surgery is relatively high. To increase the cCR rate, various strategies have been attempted, including intensified local radiotherapy, extended intervals between NAT and reassessment, and the combination of TNT with immunotherapy.
