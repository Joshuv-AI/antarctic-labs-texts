A unified FLC-blockchain framework for optimized carbon credit trading in multi-microgrid systems
Authors: Nimrah Saeed, Fushuan Wen, Muhammad Zeshan Afzal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the growing demand for sustainable energy solutions, microgrids face the dual challenge of optimizing energy production and carbon credit trading to facilitate sustainable and efficient operations. This paper proposes a novel hybrid framework for carbon credit trading among microgrids, integrating Interval Type-2 Fuzzy Logic Controllers (IT2-FLC) and blockchain technology to enhance operational efficiency, security, and sustainability. The framework consists of three islanded microgrids, each characterized by a unique energy consumption profile and production capacity, with decision-making governed by IT2-FLCs. A continuous double auction mechanism facilitates decentralized energy and carbon trading, while smart contracts securely settle transactions, ensuring tamper-proof execution and transparent record-keeping. The IT2-FLCs provide robust, real-time control in the presence of uncertainty, enhancing system adaptability. Simulation results confirm the framework’s ability to maintain operational balance, reduce carbon emissions, and enable trustless, scalable coordination across microgrids. This study presents a notable advancement in integrating fuzzy logic control with blockchain-based market mechanisms for real-time, resilient carbon credit trading, offering a robust and scalable pathway for future smart grid implementations under uncertainty.Keywords: Microgrids, Blockchain, Smart contracts, Fuzzy logic control, Carbon tradingSubject terms: Engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Climate change is a major challenge for humanity’s survival and development in the twenty-first century. It has adversely affected the ecological environment and quality of life, leading to an international consensus that green and low-carbon development must be aggressively promoted to ensure the survival of humanity and sustainable development of the global economy. The energy sector remains heavily reliant on centralized, fossil-fuel-based generation, leading to substantial carbon emissions from the commercial domain. To address environmental concerns, policymakers encourage the integration of distributed renewable energy sources (DRESs) into distribution systems.

Microgrids have emerged as a transformative solution, reshaping the way energy is produced, distributed, and consumed. Distributed energy exchange enables microgrids to generate revenue by trading excess renewable energy, which provides economic and ecological benefits, including reduced carbon emissions and lower energy procurement costs1,2. In parallel, carbon credits have gained prominence as a market-driven mechanism to curb greenhouse gas emissions. Carbon credits are permits that authorize the holder to release a specific quantity of carbon dioxide or other greenhouse gases. One credit allows for the release of one metric ton of CO2. Carbon trading is a specific financial market where carbon credits are traded. It involves one party paying another for permits to emit greenhouse gases. The purchaser can utilize bought emission credits instead of reducing greenhouse gas emissions to meet its emission reduction goals3,4.

To address the complexities of energy systems and carbon trading, advanced technologies such as blockchain and artificial intelligence (AI) offer promising solutions. Blockchain and artificial intelligence are highly promising technologies that could significantly influence the future of energy and address various challenges within the energy sector. Blockchain offers decentralization solutions, while AI facilitates intelligent autonomous optimization and serves as a decision-making entity for power system operations. Blockchain technology removes intermediaries from digital transactions. It is an open-source, decentralized, public digital ledger technology (DLT) disseminated among network participants5–7. Blockchain has acquired popularity for its security, privacy and data integrity qualities. Each block contains encrypted data, forming a sequence of cryptographic hashes that guarantees the information remains unaltered by any third party. Transactions are first broadcast to the network of peers. Each node then accumulates and verifies the transaction to ensure it meets the criteria set by both parties. The finalized list of transactions is proposed by a node since there is no involvement of a central authority. The chosen node transmits the completed transaction to the network. All peers verify the transactions suggested by the node and approve the entry into the ledger8–10. The integration of FLC and blockchain in energy markets is a novel approach that addresses two critical challenges: real-time decision-making for optimized energy management and transparent, secure carbon credit trading. FLC offers adaptability and precision in handling the inherent uncertainties of energy production and consumption in microgrids, enabling dynamic decision-making based on factors such as load demand, renewable energy availability, and battery status. Blockchain, on the other hand, ensures the integrity, transparency, and immutability of transactions, creating trust among participating microgrids without relying on centralized authorities11.

The integration of AI and blockchain technologies has emerged as a powerful avenue for optimizing energy systems and carbon credit markets. Recent blockchain-enabled designs are placing growing emphasis on prosumer empowerment, low-carbon trading, and the integration of green certificates. A blockchain-driven peer-to-peer carbon allowance platform on a modified IEEE 37-bus feeder is introduced in12. By recording every transaction on a tamper-proof ledger, prosumers are enabled to set their own prices, reduce settlement costs, and lower feeder-level carbon intensity. A green-electricity P2P mechanism for interconnected microgrids is proposed in13, in which a non-cooperative Nash game allocates trading volumes and an ADMM-based decentralized coordinator balances power flows, yielding lower energy costs and CO₂ emissions. A dual-market blockchain platform that simultaneously clears surplus consumption vouchers and government-mandated green certificates through smart contract “intelligent recommendations” is designed in14. Large-scale emulation (~ 215,000 vouchers and 144,000 certificates) is conducted to confirm the platform’s scalability for multi-attribute energy products. Collectively, studies12–14 highlight three gaps that our work addresses: (i) sub-second physical control of power flows, delivered via an IT2-FLC; (ii) continuous double-auction price discovery rather than single-shot games; and (iii) a tightly coupled on-chain loop linking energy and carbon trading for immutable feedback.

In15, Hyperledger Fabric and three smart contract types are utilized to create an incentive-compatible carbon credit market. A continuous double auction embedded in a decentralized electricity trading scheme is proposed in16, which equips participants with adaptive and aggressive bidding strategies. On the analytics side, an enhanced delegated proof-of-stake consensus combined with AI is developed in17 to accelerate industrial IoT block creation and data transmission. In18, machine-learning models are built to predict carbon emissions with high precision and are deployed on-chain to support a decentralized carbon market, highlighting the synergy between advanced analytics and secure data recording. A blockchain-based ownership and authentication system for emission rights aligned with UN SDG 13 is presented in19.

Cross-sector optimization studies reinforce how advanced scheduling and hybrid models enhance flexibility and reduce emissions across energy systems. In maritime applications, a piecewise-linear mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model is developed in20 to select the optimal mix of diesel generators, batteries, and heat-recovery units for a Baltic cruise ship, thereby reducing operating costs and CO₂, NOₓ, and SOₓ emissions. In the built-environment domain, a Wasserstein distributional robust day-ahead scheduler for smart-building energy systems aggregated with electric vehicles (EVs) is presented in21. The mixed-integer Stackelberg formulation reduces operating costs by 26% and flattens the net load, demonstrating that robust optimization can monetize demand and price uncertainty.In the industrial sector, a distributed robust optimization model for a Steel Plant Integrated Energy System (SPIES) is developed in22. The model accounts for steam, electricity, and coal gas flow optimization under uncertainty. By constructing a fuzzy set from multisource coal gas information using an imprecise Dirichlet model (IDM), and reformulating the distributed robust optimization into a solvable MILP model using duality theory and the big-M method, the study enhances operational efficiency. Case studies demonstrate that this approach reduces total energy costs by 1.91% and lowers computational time by 1326.94 s compared to conventional stochastic optimization, effectively balancing decision conservatism and computational performance.

Studies12–22 reinforce two key themes central to our work: first, that hybrid MILP and distributed robust optimization (DRO) formulations effectively handle discrete operational decisions and stochastic energy contexts across domains ranging from microgrids to maritime, built-environment, and industrial systems; and second, that deep integration of storage, flexible demand, and market interaction is essential for cost-effective decarbonization. Our IT2-FLC + blockchain framework extends these principles to peer-to-peer microgrids, enabling real-time fuzzy control and trust-free settlement to address sub-second variability and transactional risk. Nevertheless, prior research continues to rely on isolated solutions, centralized coordination, static auctions, or offline prediction models, leaving real-time uncertainty management, transparent multi-microgrid coordination, and trust assurance largely unresolved. Our approach bridges these gaps by combining adaptive fuzzy control with a continuous double auction and an immutable on-chain feedback loop.

To address these gaps, our proposed model establishes a novel hybrid framework that integrates an Interval Type-2 Fuzzy Logic Controller (IT2-FLC), a blockchain-enabled auction mechanism, and smart contract execution to achieve secure, adaptive, and decentralized energy-carbon optimization. Unlike existing models, our architecture enables cross-microgrid coordination while simultaneously accounting for sub-second decision-making and immutable verification. The IT2-FLC enables robust handling of imprecise inputs, such as load power, battery state-of-charge, and market volatility, by utilizing dual-bound membership functions, allowing for fine-grained control decisions under uncertainty. This control intelligence is coupled with a decentralized auction engine that dynamically determines clearing prices for energy and carbon credits, allowing microgrids to participate in buy-sell decisions based on real-time market feedback autonomously. These decisions are executed through smart contracts on the Ethereum blockchain, ensuring tamper-proof record-keeping and eliminating the need for intermediaries. Fuzzy logic

Fuzzy logic emerged as a response to the need for integrating logical reasoning and the intuitive decision-making abilities of an expert operator into an automated system. Fuzzy logic’s tools enable the construction of models for phenomena that, to some extent, resemble human reasoning. It is a computational approach that aims to solve problems by considering all available information to determine the most optimal decision based on the input, originating from the mathematical examination of multivalued logic. The purpose of fuzzy logic is to imitate the way humans examine problems and make decisions, by relying on vague or imprecise values instead of absolute truth or false23–26.

Fuzzification is the process of converting precise numbers into fuzzy sets with varying degrees of membership. This process involves interpreting and converting the input values into terms that correspond to the defined fuzzy sets for each input variable. The value varies from 0 to 1, representing the degree of similarity between the input data and a specific fuzzy set. Then the inference system utilizes a set of fuzzy rules (IF–THEN rules) to conclude the appropriate output based on the input fuzzified sets. The inference system aggregates the fuzzy sets of all the inputs based on these rules to identify relevant rules and their degree of relevance. Following the processing by the inference system, the output persists in a fuzzy state. The final stage in the FLC is defuzzification, where fuzzy output is transformed into precise values, denoted as u. Defuzzification techniques employ methods such as the centroid method, which calculates the center of mass for a given set, or the mean of maxima, which computes the average value from the highest points in the set27–29.

Fuzzy logic-based microgrid control

Microgrids are being established globally to integrate decentralized and renewable energy generation. The grid configuration consists of interconnected smaller systems for generation, storage, and load management. These systems can either be connected to the main electricity grid or operate independently from it. Microgrids are being deployed more frequently due to recent technological advancements, software improvements, reduced energy costs, and growing demand from motivated customers30. Nevertheless, the unpredictable nature of renewable energy sources and the variations in load demand can lead to intermittent energy deficits or surpluses in microgrids. Direct energy trading between microgrids can serve as a practical solution to achieve energy balance and reduce operational expenses, as it offers advantages to both sellers and buyers by eliminating the need for intermediaries, such as utility companies, thereby streamlining the trading process. However, there are numerous obstacles in formulating a direct energy trading mechanism. Reaching a consensus on the trading price is challenging, as it should be impartial and not favor either sellers or buyers31. Furthermore, it is imperative to ascertain the power transmission from the sellers. In our proposed framework for carbon trading, we have considered three islanded microgrids, which can be configured into an intelligent system that enables energy production, storage, and usage to fluctuate based on the availability of renewable sources and batteries. Blockchain as a catalyst for transactional integrity

Blockchain technology and smart contracts have the potential to completely transform the energy sector by offering a secure, efficient, and transparent platform for energy transactions. Blockchain is a decentralized technology that enables direct transactions between peers, eliminating the need for a central authority or supervising entity40. It has the potential to facilitate peer-to-peer (P2P) energy trading, where users can directly transact in the purchase and sale of energy with one another. Consequently, reduced energy costs and enhanced energy market efficiency are achieved41.

Smart contracts’ self-enforcing nature automates a standardized auction procedure. A peer-to-peer trading framework underpinned by blockchain technology streamlines the exchange of energy and carbon allowances42. The bidding and selling prices set by prosumers can act as economic incentives, encouraging behavioral adjustments to achieve regional energy equilibrium and enhance carbon credit efficiency through reduced emissions. Blockchain technology can facilitate energy trading for both consumers and prosumers43. Therefore, a diverse range of elements, including residential consumers, renewable energy resources, energy storage, electric vehicles, and others, can participate in the market. In addition, each component has the potential to suggest the energy cost associated with its capabilities and the cost of energy production, thereby contributing to a competitive energy market. System architecture

This study adopts a code-driven simulation methodology to develop, test, and evaluate a decentralized framework for energy and carbon credit trading in multi-microgrid environments. The architecture integrates IT2-FLC for adaptive energy decision-making and blockchain-based smart contracts for secure, tamper-proof transaction execution. All data used in this framework is synthetically generated via a Python-based simulation environment that models realistic energy production and consumption using dynamic sine wave profiles.

Compared to traditional Type-1 Fuzzy Logic Controllers (T1-FLC), the Interval Type-2 FLC (IT2-FLC) adopted in our framework offers significantly greater robustness in the presence of uncertainty. This enhancement arises from the additional degrees of freedom provided by the footprint of uncertainty (FOU), enabling the controller to handle dynamic variations, sensor noise, and modeling imprecision more effectively. Prior studies have consistently validated this advantage. For example, a comparative investigation by44 demonstrates that IT2-FLC outperforms T1-FLC across multiple benchmark nonlinear systems under various noise levels, exhibiting improved control stability and performance. Additionally,45 emphasizes that higher-order fuzzy systems, including interval and general Type-2 FLSs, are especially well suited for real-world applications where uncertainty is a dominant factor. These findings align with our application context, where the IT2-FLC manages fluctuations in load demand, intermittent generation, and volatile market conditions, challenges that would otherwise impair control fidelity with a Type-1 approach. Therefore, the adoption of IT2-FLC in our system directly contributes to more adaptive and resilient decision-making in peer-to-peer microgrid environments.

The system comprises three islanded microgrids, each with unique energy capacities and demand patterns. A dedicated IT2-FLC governs each microgrid, analyzing real-time values for energy generation, load, battery status, and carbon credit balances to determine optimal operational actions. Based on these evaluations, the FLC autonomously generates buy/sell/hold decisions for energy and carbon credits. These decisions are passed to a decentralized auction mechanism, which iteratively matches bids and asks across the microgrids to identify a market-clearing price. If a valid match is found, the auction executes the transaction at the clearing price; otherwise, it is deferred. The outcome of each trade is enforced using Ethereum smart contracts, ensuring that transactions are executed only under predefined, tamper-proof conditions.

Smart contracts also autonomously update participant balances and record the transaction on the blockchain ledger, providing immutable auditability and full transparency. The blockchain further serves as a feedback mechanism, feeding verified data back into the IT2-FLCs for future decisions. This closed-loop process enables dynamic, trustless coordination across distributed energy nodes. Figure 3 illustrates the end-to-end system architecture. (4)Inference mechanism

Inference mechanism

The FLC evaluates all applicable rules based on the current input values. For each rule, it calculates the degree of truth. for example, if two rules recommend “Sell”, and their degrees of truth are 0.6 and 0.8, FLC collects these values to regulate the overall confidence in Sell action.

The rule activation strength is determined by taking the minimum degree of membership for each input. This determines how strongly a particular rule should be applied in the decision-making process. In other words, the activation of a rule is constrained by the weakest (lowest) degree of membership among the input variables. (5) Defuzzification

Defuzzification

After the fuzzy logic system has evaluated the rules and generated fuzzy output, defuzzification is performed to convert the fuzzy results into a concrete decision. This step translates the fuzzy outputs, which are expressed in terms of membership degrees, into specific, actionable values. In the centroid method of defuzzification, the output is determined by calculating the center of the area under the membership function curve. For example, suppose the FLC decides to sell carbon credits. In that case, the defuzzification process determines not only the action (Sell) but also how many credits to sell or the appropriate price at which to sell, based on the calculated centroid of the output membership function.

(6)Implementation

Implementation

The defuzzification and decision-making processes are implemented using a series of conditional statements that evaluate the degree of membership for each fuzzy rule and calculate the corresponding output. These conditional statements assess the fuzzified input values (such as load power, battery charge, and market conditions) and apply the predefined fuzzy rules to determine the output action. The system then uses the calculated fuzzy output (e.g., battery power, price for carbon credits, or energy transaction decisions) to make real-time adjustments to the microgrid. These decisions are based on the underlying membership functions, which represent the degree to which different actions should be taken in response to specific conditions.

Auction mechanism design

Market participation by each microgrid is governed by its respective fuzzy decision outputs. Specifically, microgrids evaluate their internal states, such as carbon credit balance, energy surplus or deficit, and market conditions, using IT2-FLC. A microgrid becomes eligible to participate in the auction if the fuzzy decision confidence exceeds a predefined threshold (e.g., 0.6). This threshold ensures that only microgrids with strong incentive signals engage in bidding or asking, thereby reducing unnecessary market noise and improving trading efficiency.

The auction process follows a Continuous Double Auction (CDA) model, wherein microgrids dynamically submit bids (buy offers) or asks (sell offers) based on their real-time surplus or deficit conditions. The iterative nature of the auction allows microgrids to revise their offers across successive rounds, adapting their strategies in response to previous market outcomes and changes in their own energy metrics. Conclusion

This research presents a novel AI-integrated blockchain framework for decentralized energy management and carbon credit trading in microgrids. By coupling an Interval Type-2 Fuzzy Logic Controller (IT2-FLC) with a blockchain-based continuous double auction mechanism, the proposed system enables adaptive, secure, and transparent trading of electricity and carbon credits among interconnected microgrids. The integration of fuzzy logic empowers each microgrid to make autonomous, uncertainty-resilient decisions regarding energy dispatch and bidding, while smart contracts ensure the automatic and tamper-proof execution of trading rules.

The results demonstrate that this architecture significantly improves both economic and environmental performance. Bidding strategies based on fuzzy inference enable iterative adjustments, thereby optimizing price discovery and resource allocation. Although the auction continues until market equilibrium is reached, the comparison between the first two bidding rounds effectively illustrates the gains in renewable energy utilization, carbon credit reallocation, and energy cost savings. For instance, in round two, MG3 achieved a renewable energy utilization of 354.13 units and energy savings of 35,471.66 USD, which are substantially higher than in round one.

These improvements validate the framework’s capability to enhance operational efficiency, promote sustainable practices, and decentralize control across microgrids. By ensuring real-time adaptive decision-making and immutable transaction logging, the proposed system addresses critical challenges in energy-carbon optimization. In summary, this work contributes a scalable and transferable model for future smart energy ecosystems, enabling microgrids to reduce carbon emissions, maximize renewable energy usage, and establish localized, transparent carbon trading environments.

Practical application

This research presents an innovative approach to energy management and carbon credit trading within microgrids, leveraging blockchain technology to ensure secure and transparent transactions. By integrating fuzzy logic controllers (FLCs) with a blockchain-based auction mechanism, the proposed system enables direct carbon credit trading between microgrids, facilitating decentralized optimization of energy consumption, storage, and trade decisions. While such peer-to-peer carbon trading is not yet widely adopted in practice, this study envisions a scalable, autonomous infrastructure that could underpin the next generation of sustainable energy systems.

A particularly relevant scenario for deployment is in rural electrification, where isolated microgrids, often powered by solar PV and batteries, could trade excess generation and carbon credits with neighboring villages. This would reduce reliance on diesel generators, enhance energy resilience, and generate carbon revenue streams, especially when integrated with government-backed clean energy incentives. Similarly, in industrial parks with multiple manufacturing units, this system can coordinate power usage and emissions, allowing energy-intensive units to purchase surplus clean energy credits from less active peers in real-time.

In both cases, economic feasibility hinges on the low cost of blockchain deployment (especially using private or consortium chains) and the potential revenue from avoided penalties and improved energy efficiency. However, policy challenges remain, particularly in standardizing carbon credit valuation at the microgrid level and ensuring legal recognition of peer-traded offsets.

As regulatory frameworks for decentralized energy evolve, this model offers a practical and technically robust foundation for secure, accountable, and scalable energy and carbon credit management.

Practical limitations and corresponding strategies

While the proposed framework offers substantial advancements in decentralized carbon credit trading for microgrids, several practical limitations must be addressed for its real-world deployment:

1. Blockchain Infrastructure Limitations: In regions with limited access to reliable blockchain infrastructure, such as rural areas or underdeveloped regions, the lack of consistent power supply and computing resources could hinder the deployment of the system.

Strategy: To mitigate this, the use of private or consortium blockchains can reduce the infrastructure burden. Additionally, incorporating lightweight blockchain nodes or edge computing techniques can allow microgrids to participate with minimal infrastructure requirements.

2. Scalability Challenges: The system has been tested in a simulated environment with three microgrids. Scaling it to hundreds of microgrids may introduce computational complexity and increased transaction latency due to the higher volume of data and bids processed on the blockchain.

Strategy: To overcome this, second-layer blockchain solutions like sidechains or sharding can be explored. Additionally, optimizing consensus mechanisms and integrating off-chain transaction layers can enhance the scalability and reduce transaction latency.

3. Policy and Regulatory Constraints: The adoption of blockchain-based energy trading and carbon credit mechanisms may be subject to local regulations that are not yet well-defined or supportive. In many regions, policies may not recognize peer-to-peer carbon trading or blockchain transactions for carbon credits.

Strategy: To tackle this limitation, collaborative efforts with policymakers and regulators are essential to establish standardized frameworks for blockchain-enabled carbon trading. Additionally, the system can be designed to be flexible, allowing it to adapt to future policy updates and regulatory changes. Smart contract programming could also integrate compliance rules directly into transaction execution, ensuring automatic adherence to local regulations.

4. Integration with Legacy Systems: Existing microgrids may rely on conventional energy management systems that are not readily compatible with blockchain-based solutions, requiring significant infrastructure upgrades.

Strategy: To ease the transition, the system can support hybrid setups where blockchain solutions are gradually integrated with traditional systems. Modular adapters can be developed to connect legacy systems with the blockchain and IT2-FLC controllers, ensuring that both old and new systems can function in parallel during the integration phase.

By addressing these practical limitations through strategic approaches, the proposed system can be scaled and deployed in diverse real-world environments, fostering greater sustainability and efficiency in microgrid energy and carbon credit trading.

Future work

The future research and improvement of this system can investigate numerous research domains. To use this system on a larger scale, future efforts should concentrate on enhancing blockchain scalability by investigating second-layer solutions or incorporating more efficient consensus processes to accommodate increased transaction volumes. Fuzzy logic is employed in the proposed system, yet it is effective in managing uncertain data; more advanced AI techniques like reinforcement learning can be integrated into the system. As energy trading rules and carbon markets evolve, further research could explore the integration of regulatory frameworks directly into smart contract logic, guaranteeing that transaction automatically adhere to compliance norms. This innovative methodology guides policymakers and investors in deploying similar systems on a larger scale.

The system offers an innovative method for the future by illustrating the concept of direct carbon credit trading. Microgrids could significantly contribute to emissions management and sustainability objectives by combining carbon credit trading with energy transactions.
