Timing of treatment-switch to telitacicept in high-risk IgA nephropathy: an observational case series of two clinical scenarios
Authors: Jiangyuan Hao, Chendan Wang, Miao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide. A significant proportion of patients, approximately 20–40%, progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) within 10 to 20 years. Telitacicept, a dual inhibitor of BAFF and APRIL, is now recommended in clinical guidelines for the treatment of high-risk IgAN. However, the optimal timing for initiating this therapy remains undefined.MethodsThis retrospective study included 19 patients with high-risk IgAN (persistent proteinuria of at least 1 g/d despite at least 3 months of optimised therapy, including adequate supportive therapy and conventional anti-inflammatory treatments and/or other immunosuppressants) who received telitacicept. Patients were stratified by the interval from diagnostic kidney biopsy to telitacicept initiation into two groups: <3 years (n = 11) and ≥ 3 years (n = 8). This analysis focused on describing clinical outcomes within each group, aiming to characterise distinct clinical scenarios rather than to perform direct intergroup comparisons. The primary outcome was the change in 24-hour urinary protein levels at 12 months.ResultsAt the 12‑month follow‑up, reductions in 24-hour urinary protein were observed in both groups. In the < 3 years group (n = 11), median proteinuria decreased from 1.21 g/d (IQR 1.10–1.29) at baseline to 0.17 g/d (IQR 0.10–0.34). The overall efficacy rate (complete or partial remission) was 54.5% (6/11). In the ≥ 3 years group (n = 8), median proteinuria decreased from 2.36 g/d (IQR 1.85–2.75) to 1.01 g/d (IQR 0.64–1.89). The overall efficacy rate was 37.5% (3/8). No serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionsIn this descriptive case series of patients with high-risk IgAN, telitacicept was associated with proteinuria reduction and stable renal function over 12 months. Median proteinuria reduction was 84% in patients initiating treatment earlier (< 3 years from biopsy) and 50% in those starting later (≥ 3 years). These observations are descriptive and, given the small sample and baseline imbalances, should be interpreted with caution. These preliminary observations may inform clinical decision-making and highlight treatment timing as a question warranting further investigation in larger prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04943-9.

Introduction

Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is the predominant form of primary glomerulonephritisworldwide. It is especially prevalent among East Asian populations [1, 2]. Epidemiological evidence from China reveals that IgAN accounts for between 39.7% and 54.3% of primary glomerular diseases and is also a primary contributor to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in young adults [3, 4], with 20% to 40% of individuals progressing to ESRD within 10 to 20 years post-diagnosis [1, 5, 6].

Current management strategies for IgA nephropathy (IgAN) primarily focus on supportive care, including renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i). The 2021 KDIGO guidelines recommend a 6-month course of glucocorticoids for patients at persistently high risk of progression, defined as sustained proteinuria (> 0.75–1 g/day) despite at least 90 days of optimised supportive care [7]. Nevertheless, the significant adverse effects associated with corticosteroid therapy restrict its clinical application. Moreover, a subset of patients exhibits refractoriness to conventional immunosuppressive treatment, underscoring the inadequacy of existing therapies and warranting alternative strategies [8]. The updated 2025 guidelines propose a “cause + symptom” dual-layer treatment framework. The emphasize actively evaluating patients at high risk of progression to timely start novel targeted agents that address core disease pathways, provided that adequate supportive care is in place [9].

The “multiple-hit” hypothesis elucidates the progression from immune abnormalities to kidney injury in IgA nephropathy (IgAN) [10, 11]. A pivotal component of this hypothesis is the synthesis of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), which acts as a self-antigen and triggers an immune response. This response ultimately results in the deposition of IgA-containing immune complexes within the glomerular mesangium. It also activates the complement system, leading to local inflammation, cell proliferation, and tissue fibrosis. Additionally, B-lymphocyte stimulator (BAFF, also known as BLyS) and its homolog, a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL), are critical cytokines that regulate B-cell survival, proliferation, differentiation, and antibody class switching [12, 13]. Serum levels of the relevant biomarkers are significantly elevated in patients withIgAN and correlate strongly with Gd-IgA1 levels and disease activity [14, 15].

Telitacicept, a novel humanised, recombinant TACI-Fc fusion protein [16], binds both BAFF and APRIL strongly. This dual inhibits their pro-survival and pro-antibody secretion effects on B cells and plasma cells [17]. Its efficacy and safety have been established in a randomised, double-blind, phase II trial. After 24 weeks of treatment, telitacicept reduced proteinuria by approximately 49% versus placebo, while maintaining a stable estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and demonstrating a favourable safety profile [18]. The latest Chinese clinical practice guidelines (2025) have suggested telitacicept as a new treatment option for high-risk IgAN or refractory IgAN [19].

While telitacicept is increasingly used as either initial therapy or an alternative to conventional immunosuppressants for IgAN, the optimal timing for its initiation remains unclear. For instance, a retrospective study with a substantial proportion (65%) of patients switching to telitacicept due to inadequate prior response reported significant proteinuria reduction, indicating its potential as an effective alternative [20]. This critical question of optional timing for telitacicept initiation may benefit from insights gained through research on other autoimmune diseases. For example, early intervention with belimumab (BEL)—starting treatment within five years of a systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) diagnosis—shows better therapeutic outcomes than starting treatment later [21]. This finding suggests that the duration between diagnosis and the transition to telitacicept in IgA nephropathy may also affect treatment response. Still, this aspect has not yet been specifically studied. Consequently, this research aimed to provide real-world descriptive data on treatment timing and outcomes, offering preliminary insights that may inform clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective single-centre cohort study included patients diagnosed with IgA nephropathy (IgAN) who were treated with telitacicept (160 mg/week) at our institution from March 2023 to March 2025. The baseline for all efficacy analyses was established as the final clinical assessment conducted before the initial dose of telitacicept.

Inclusion criteria: (1) a diagnosis of primary IgA nephropathy confirmed by renal biopsy; (2) age of 18 years or older; (3) persistent proteinuria of at least 1 g/d despite at least 3 months of optimized therapy, including adequate supportive therapy, which included RASi, SGLT2i, and conventional anti-inflammatory treatments such as corticosteroids and/or other immunosuppressants; (4) a treatment duration with telitacicept and follow-up of at least 1 year.

Exclusion criteria: (1) acute kidney injury; (2) concomitant autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, or myasthenia gravis; (3) a history of malignant tumours; (4) active infection; (5) eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m² at screening; (6) missing data for the primary outcome (24-hour urinary protein at baseline or 12 months) or the key grouping variable (time from biopsy to telitacicept initiation).

Grouping strategy

Evidence-based guidance on the optimal timing of treatment switching in high-risk IgA nephropathy remains limited. Based on clinical experience, patients were categorised according to the time interval from diagnostic kidney biopsy to telitacicept initiation: <3 years (n = 11) and ≥ 3 years (n = 8). This stratification aimed to reflect distinct clinical scenarios encountered in practice—representing earlier versus later transition to targeted therapy—rather than to establish a biological threshold. Owing to differences in baseline characteristics (particularly higher proteinuria in the ≥ 3 years group) and the limited sample size, this study refrained from conducting intergroup statistical comparisons. Instead, the analysis focuses on describing the trajectory of clinical outcomes after telitacicept initiation within each group, consistent with a descriptive case series design.

Data collection

The collected data encompassed the following categories: (1) General information, including age, sex and body mass index (BMI); (2) Clinical data, which comprised blood pressure measurements (systolic and diastolic), the 24-hour urinary protein quantification, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculated using the CKD-EPI equation [22], serum albumin (ALB) levels, urinary red blood cells (RBC), comorbidities, medical history, medication history, renal pathologic data, including Oxford classification MEST-C scores (mesangial [M] and endocapillary [E] proliferation (hypercellularity), segmental glomerulosclerosis [S], tubular atrophy / interstitial fibrosis [T], and crescents [C]) were collected; (3) Telitacicept usage, detailing the initiation time and dosage; (4) Safety data, which included documentation of all adverse events (AEs).

Outcomes and efficacy assessment

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in the 24-hour urinary protein quantification at 12 months of treatment. Treatment response: CR was defined as patients with 24-hour proteinuria ≤ 0.3 g, ALB > 35 g/L, and stable renal function (a decrease in eGFR ≤ 20%). Patients who experienced a > 50% reduction in 24-hour proteinuria while maintaining stable renal function but did not achieve CR were classified as PR. Patients who failed to achieve CR or PR after 12 months of treatment were categorised as no remission. The overall response rate was calculated as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR, and is presented for descriptive purposes only; no intergroup statistical comparisons were performed.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included changes from baseline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and serum albumin levels. Urine RBC data were also collected.

Safety assessment

All adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) occurring during the follow-up period were documented. AEs were defined as any unfavourable medical occurrence following drug administration, encompassing non-infectious reactions (e.g., injection site itching, pain), infectious reactions (e.g. skin, respiratory, digestive, and urinary tract infections), and hypersensitivity reactions. SAEs were characterised as AEs that resulted in death, necessitated or extended hospitalisation, or led to persistent or significant disability or incapacitation.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.2. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], while categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage). For baseline characteristics, differences between the two groups were examined using Mann-Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact tests; the resulting P values are reported for descriptive purposes only and should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample. For efficacy outcomes, no statistical comparisons were made between groups—the data are presented descriptively to show within-group changes over time. Exploratory correlations were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study involved 19 patients, categorised into two groups according to the treatment switch interval: less than 3 years (n = 11) and more than 3 years (n = 8). Medication histories of all patients before enrollment were presented in the supplemental materials. The follow‑up period was 12 months for all patients. No significant differences in age, sex, or BMI were observed between the two groups. Baseline 24-hour urinary protein was 1.21 g/d (IQR 1.10–1.29) in the < 3 years group and 2.36 g/d (IQR 1.85–2.75) in the ≥ 3 years group. Baseline eGFR was 79.2 mL/min/1.73 m² (IQR 57.3–125.7) in the < 3 years group and 81.1 mL/min/1.73 m² (IQR 47.0–115.9) in the ≥ 3 years group. Baseline serum albumin was 41.7 g/L (IQR 36.6–45.1) and 38.5 g/L (IQR 34.9–40.8), respectively(Table 1).

Primary outcomes

After 12 months of telitacicept treatment, the 24-hour urinary protein levels decreased from baseline in both groups. The < 3 years group (n = 11) showed a reduction from 1.21 g/d (IQR 1.10–1.29) at baseline to 0.17 g/d (IQR 0.10–0.34) at 12 months; in the ≥ 3 years group (n = 8), from 2.36 g/d (IQR 1.85–2.75) to 1.01 g/d (IQR 0.64–1.89) (Fig. 1a). The assessment of urinary protein target achievement and the percentage reduction also demonstrated this trend (Supplementary Table 1 and Table 2). Individual patient trajectories are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. An exploratory correlation between baseline proteinuria and percent reduction at 6 and 12 months is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 (see Table 2).

Secondary Outcomes

eGFR remained stable or showed numerical increases in both groups over the 12-month follow-up. In the < 3 years group, median eGFR changed from 79.25 mL/min/1.73 m² (IQR 59.16–122.45) at baseline to 105.34 mL/min/1.73 m² (IQR 61.72–112.50) at 12 months. In the ≥ 3 years group, median eGFR changed from 81.09 mL/min/1.73 m² (IQR 49.45–113.39) to 102.77 mL/min/1.73 m² (IQR 73.14–134.99) (Fig. 1b). No consistent decline in eGFR was observed during the 12-month follow-up, even among patients with lower baseline values.

Serum albumin levels also increased in both groups. In the < 3 years group, median albumin rose from 41.70 g/L (IQR 38.61–44.22) at baseline to 44.54 g/L (IQR 43.27–44.99) at 12 months. In the ≥ 3 years group, median albumin increased from 38.48 g/L (IQR 34.90–40.85) to 41.94 g/L (IQR 40.58–44.14) (Fig. 1c).

Safety outcomes

No serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred among the 19 patients with high-risk IgA nephropathy (IgAN) who received telitacicept during the study period. Adverse reactions were reported by three patients, with two cases exhibiting injection site reactions characterised by local swelling and pruritus, and one case presenting with an upper respiratory tract infection. None of the patients discontinued medication due to adverse reactions.

Discussion

Recent years have witnessed significant advancements in targeted therapy for IgA nephropathy (IgAN). Notable developments include budesonide enteric-coated capsules aimed at intestinal mucosal immunity (Nefecon), an anti-APRIL monoclonal antibody (sbelimumab/VIS649), and a BAFF/APRIL dual inhibitor (Atacicept), all demonstrating potential to reduce proteinuria and Gd-IgA1 levels in clinical trials [23–27]. Among these, the domestically developed telitacicept, a TACI-Fc fusion protein that simultaneously targets BLyS and APRIL, is a promising option for patients with high-risk IgAN.

This retrospective analysis of 19 patients with high-risk IgAN is the first to investigate the “time from diagnosis to treatment switch” variable to assess telitacicept’s efficacy. Patients were categorised based on the interval from diagnostic kidney biopsy to telitacicept initiation: <3 years (n = 11) and ≥ 3 years (n = 8). This categorisation is not aimed at strict intergroup comparisons but rather at representing two typical clinical scenarios encountered in practice: earlier versus later transition to targeted therapy based on disease course and treatment response. Despite heterogeneity in baseline characteristics (particularly higher proteinuria and smoking prevalence in the ≥ 3 years group), telitacicept treatment was associated with reductions in proteinuria and stable renal function in both groups. These observations are consistent with findings from the pivotal clinical trial of telitacicept in IgAN [18] and subsequent real‑world studies. In terms of safety, our study, in line with most reports, noted no serious adverse events, indicating telitacicept’s good tolerability in this high-risk population. A study involving seven children with high-risk IgA vasculitis nephritis also demonstrated significant improvement in proteinuria after switching to telitacicept, further reinforcing our conclusions [28].

The “multiple-hit” hypothesis of IgAN suggests that the deposition of immune complexes formed by aberrantly glycosylated IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) and its autoantibodies plays a crucial role in disease initiation [29]. We speculate that in the early stages of the disease, renal pathology is marked by active immune-inflammatory responses. Administering telitacicept during this phase may more effectively inhibit Gd-IgA1 production and disrupt the pathogenic cycle, potentially leading to improved remission of proteinuria. This hypothesis is supported by a study involving 106 patients, which demonstrated that pre-treatment urinary protein levels of < 3.5 g/d and an eGFR > 60 ml/min/1.73 m² were significantly associated with achieving post-treatment urinary protein levels of < 1.0 g/d. This study found that the effect of reducing proteinuria was more pronounced in patients with high eGFR, suggesting that the application of telitacicept in the early stage of renal dysfunction may be more effective [30]. In the present cohort, patients with a shorter interval to treatment (< 3 years) showed a median proteinuria reduction of 84% at 12 months, with 8 of 11 patients (73%) achieving the ideal proteinuria target (< 0.3 g/d). Patients in the longer-interval group (≥ 3 years) exhibited a median reduction of 50%, and none achieved complete remission. While these numerical differences align with the notion that earlier treatment may be associated with greater response, the substantial baseline imbalances—particularly higher baseline proteinuria in the ≥ 3 years group—make it difficult to draw firm conclusions about causality. Given that persistent proteinuria is a well‑established risk factor for progression to end‑stage kidney disease(ESKD) [31], the observed trend toward greater proteinuria reduction in patients who initiated telitacicept earlier, though inconclusive, may still offer clinically useful signals. These observations provide preliminary real‑world insights that could inform clinical decision‑making, highlighting treatment timing as a factor worth considering when managing patients with persistent proteinuria despite prior therapy.

When interpreting the observed trend toward greater response in the < 3 years group, two hypotheses may be considered. The first, termed the “timing hypothesis,” posits that early intervention directly improves therapeutic outcomes. The second, known as the “selection hypothesis,” suggests that patients with longer switch intervals may represent a more aggressive disease subtype, characterised by reduced sensitivity to conventional treatments and greater baseline proteinuria. The elevated baseline proteinuria and higher prevalence of smoking in the ≥ 3 years group are consistent with this latter interpretation, though whether these factors reflect disease severity or confound the observed association cannot be determined from the present data. These mechanisms are not necessarily mutually exclusive and may operate concurrently. In clinical practice, the “treatment switch interval” serves as a complex marker that integrates both temporal factors and a history of prior treatment failures, thereby accounting for observed differences in therapeutic effects.

Oxford pathological classification provided a histopathological context for this cohort. Renal biopsy data were available for 14 of 19 patients (74%), with Oxford MEST‑C scores obtainable in 12 cases (63%): 8 in the < 3 years group (8/12, 67%) and 4 in the ≥ 3 years group (4/7, 57%). Among patients with available scores, endocapillary hypercellularity (E1) was present in 1 patient (13%) in the < 3 years group, and 2 patients (50%) in the ≥ 3 years group; cellular/fibrocellular crescents (C1) were present in 3 patients (38%) and 4 patients (100%), respectively. In the ≥ 3 years group, all four patients with available scores had active lesions (E1 and/or C1), and all showed proteinuria reduction at 12 months. Given the limited sample size, the relationship between these histological findings and treatment response could not be clearly established, and larger prospective studies are needed to validate this observation. Notably, eGFR remained stable or improved during follow-up, particularly in the ≥ 3 years group. The mechanisms underlying this observation are likely multifactorial. One possibility is that the marked reduction in proteinuria lessens tubular injury and intraglomerular pressure, thereby contributing to hemodynamic stabilisation [32]. Additionally, by targeting the BAFF/APRIL pathway, telitacicept may inhibit the production of Gd‑IgA1 and its autoantibodies, potentially contributing to renal functional stability even in patients with longer disease duration [33].

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Firstly, the retrospective design and small sample size (n = 19) limit statistical power and generalizability, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Secondly, Oxford MEST‑C scores were obtainable for only 12 patients (63%), with more missing data in the ≥ 3 years group due to outside referrals, limiting assessment of pathology‑response relationships. Thirdly, the lack of pre‑treatment eGFR data necessitated defining the study population by proteinuria alone, consistent with KDIGO 2021 high‑risk criteria. Fourthly, detailed prior treatment regimens were not uniformly available. Fifthly, urinary RBC data were recorded in non-standardised formats, precluding quantitative analysis. Given these limitations, the findings should be regarded as exploratory hypotheses rather than definitive conclusions. Nonetheless, they provide preliminary real-world observations that may inform larger prospective studies in the future.

Looking forward, there is an urgent need for larger prospective studies to determine the optimal timing of telitacicept initiation in high-risk IgAN. Future studies should enrol patients prospectively upon identification of inadequate response to conventional therapy and incorporate comprehensive biomarker assessments, including serial measurements of Gd-IgA1, serum IgA, autoantibodies, and eGFR, as well as complete histopathological data, to further clarify the association of treatment timing with treatment response.

Conclusion

This descriptive case series of 19 patients with high-risk IgA nephropathy found that telitacicept treatment was associated with proteinuria reduction and stable renal function over 12 months, with a favourable safety profile. In patients who initiated treatment earlier (< 3 years from biopsy), median proteinuria reduction was 84%; in those starting later (≥ 3 years), the reduction was 50%. These numerical differences are descriptive only and, given the small sample and baseline imbalances, should be interpreted with caution. As the first study to explore the timing of telitacicept initiation in this population, these preliminary observations may inform clinical decision-making and provide a reference for designing larger prospective studies.
