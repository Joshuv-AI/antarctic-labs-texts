A scalable workflow using digital cognitive assessment and pTau217 for MCI detection
Authors: Srinivasan Vairavan, Nicholas Griffin, David Wilson, Gallen Triana‐Baltzer, Victoria Brugada‐Ramentol, Elena Vera‐Campuzano, Daniel Alcolea, Silvia Fallone, Lucy Mackintosh, Lindsey Mette, James D Doecke, Joanne Robertson, Larry Ward, Gonzalo Sánchez Benavides, Carolina Minguillon, Clàudia Porta Mas, Adrià Tort‐Merino, Mircea Balasa, Oriol Grau Rivera, Marc Suárez Calvet, Lewis Taylor, Íñigo Rodríguez‐Baz, Javier Arranz, Juan Fortea, Ioannis Tarnanas, Marc Jones, Gayle Wittenberg, Emmanuel Streel, Maria Florencia Iulita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBlood biomarkers like phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)217 offer high diagnostic accuracy for Alzheimer's disease (AD) but face implementation challenges in low‐prevalence settings. We evaluated the Altoida NeuroMarker Platform as a digital cognitive triage tool before plasma p‐tau217 testing.METHODSXGBoost models were trained to predict mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and p‐tau217 status in 688 individuals across Australia, Spain, and the United States using the Altoida NeuroMarker. p‐tau217 status was dichotomized using a threshold of 0.04 pg/mL (LucentAD). We modeled a two‐step workflow (Altoida, plasma p‐tau217) to enrich downstream biomarker testing, assuming 30% prevalence of AD pathology.RESULTSThe Altoida NeuroMarker accurately identified MCI (receiver operating characteristic area under the curve [ROC AUC] = 0.89 ± 0.01) and p‐tau217 elevation (ROC AUC = 0.77 ± 0.08). Predicted MCI was significantly associated with elevated pTau217 (p = 0.004). When used upstream, the Altoida NeuroMarker ruled out 64.4% of participants (negative predictive value 90.2%), shifting the pre‐test probability of AD pathology to 66.6%, and improving the modeled positive predictive value of plasma p‐tau217 from 81.7% to 95.3%.DISCUSSIONThis workflow outlines a scalable path to enrich blood‐based biomarker testing after digital cognitive screening.Keywords: augmented reality, diagnosis, digital biomarkers, machine learning, mild cognitive impairment, phosphorylated tau 217

BACKGROUND

The 2024 revision of criteria for the diagnosis and staging of Alzheimer's disease (AD) builds on decades of biomarker advances to place biology at the center of AD diagnosis. 1 , 2 While biological markers provide objective measures of AD pathology, they do not fully capture the clinical expression of cognitive impairment across different stages. 3 , 4 Therefore, integrating biological markers with sensitive methods for detecting cognitive decline is essential for achieving a more precise and clinically meaningful characterization of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD. 5

Digital biomarkers offer a promising approach to capture subtle changes that precede advanced symptoms. 6 Particularly, digital cognitive assessments (DCAs) that leverage machine learning (ML) can enable complex analytical predictions without requiring lengthy and often subjective neuropsychological batteries. 7 Altoida has developed a tablet‐based digital biomarker platform that assesses cognitive and functional abilities through a 10‐minute self‐administered test. Previous studies demonstrated strong performance in identifying preclinical and prodromal AD. 8 In those studies, the Altoida Platform showed numerically higher discrimination than conventional neuropsychological batteries in preclinical stages and comparable discrimination in prodromal disease.

The integration of the Altoida Platform with blood biomarkers like plasma phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) 217 represents an important step toward a more comprehensive characterization of cognitive and functional impairment. While blood biomarkers provide objective measures of AD pathology, they should not be interpreted in isolation. 9 Integrating these complementary modalities may therefore provide insight into both neuropathology and clinical expression, offering a scalable path to early detection.

Accordingly, we evaluated whether the Altoida NeuroMarker could support plasma p‐tau217 testing within a two‐step digital–biological workflow by: (1) identifying MCI using a digital cognitive assessment, (2) exploring the relationship between digitally derived cognitive–functional signals and plasma p‐tau217 status through an exploratory ML model and concordance analyses, and (3) modeling whether upstream digital screening enriches downstream biomarker testing.

METHODS

Machine learning (ML) training dataset

ML training data were obtained from a total of 688 participants of both sexes aged ≥ 50 years, recruited between May 2022 and October 2024 at 10 clinical sites across the United States, Spain, and Australia. Participants were recruited from community settings, research cohorts, and memory clinics. Details on recruitment are available in the Supplement in supporting information.

The study enrolled individuals with MCI and cognitively unimpaired (CU) participants. Cognitive status was determined through clinical workup or established cohort classification. Two datasets were defined for ML modeling: one for MCI classification (CU vs. MCI) and a second for plasma p‐tau217 status prediction (low vs. elevated). Inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the Supplementary Methods in supporting information.

Clinical workup

Across all sites, participants underwent a standardized clinical evaluation including demographics, medical and medication history, cognitive screening with the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), followed by site‐specific neuropsychological assessment batteries aligned with established diagnostic standards.

Diagnostic procedures for MCI were based on the 2011 National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association guidelines for the clinical syndrome. 10 Accordingly, diagnostic classification for all participants was based on clinical evaluation and neuropsychological assessment and was determined independently and prior to evaluation with the Altoida NeuroMarker.

Altoida NeuroMarker Platform

Participants completed the Altoida NeuroMarker at the study centers in a self‐administered manner using iOS tablets. The assessment consists of motoric, augmented reality (AR), and speech tasks (Figure 1). Participants consented to complete either one or two assessments. Additional details are in the Supplementary Methods.

Examples of representative screenshots from the task battery of the Altoida NeuroMarker Platform illustrating motoric (top panels), augmented reality (AR, bottom left), and speech tasks (bottom right). Motoric tasks include shape tracing and tapping that assess fine motor control, processing speed, coordination, and executive function. AR tasks require participants to place and retrieve virtual objects within their real environment, engaging learning and memory, navigation, planning, and task sequencing. Speech tasks involve verbal description and recall of visual scenes. The assessment is self‐administered on iOS tablets. Together, these tasks generate multimodal digital features reflecting cognitive and functional performance across several cognitive domains.

p‐tau217 analysis

Plasma p‐tau217 was measured using the LucentAD p217tau assay (Quanterix). Plasma p‐tau217 status was dichotomized using a threshold of 0.04 pg/mL (≤0.04 vs. > 0.04 pg/mL), corresponding to the lower bound of a previously published dual‐threshold framework for the assay. 11 This threshold was selected a priori to enable a binary classification for ML modeling purposes and to support evaluation of a sensitivity‐oriented triage workflow, such as patient screening. Within this framework, prioritizing sensitivity was intended to minimize the risk of excluding individuals with underlying pathology from downstream assessment. Details on plasma processing are provided in the Supplementary Methods. The final sample for the p‐tau217 classifier consisted of 155 participants who completed 242 Altoida assessments (Figure S1 in supporting information).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by a central institutional review board in the United States (Advarra) and by the research ethics committees of the sites in Australia and Spain. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed in Python (v3.12). Data exclusion decisions are summarized in the Supplement. For demographic characteristics, continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were assessed using chi‐squared tests. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Model training

Model predictions were made using XGBoost binary classification models. All model development steps, including threshold optimization, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning, were performed with a 5‐fold cross‐validation framework. Additional details on model development are described in the Supplementary Methods.

The MCI classifier was trained on 807 Altoida assessments from 533 participants without p‐tau217 data (Table 1), using 104 features. Of these features, 100 captured performance on motoric, AR, and speech tasks, and four represented demographic variables.

The p‐tau217 classifier used the remaining 242 Altoida assessments collected from 155 participants for whom p‐tau217 data were collected (Table S1 in supporting information). 11 Details on the p‐tau217 classifier are described in the Supplementary Methods. Within this cohort, 92 participants (59.4%) were classified as having elevated plasma p‐tau217 and 63 (40.6%) as having low plasma p‐tau217 based on the selected threshold. At the assessment level, 135 of 242 assessments (55.8%) were classified as elevated and 107 (44.2%) as low plasma p‐tau217.

Participants without plasma p‐tau217 measurements were included in the MCI training and cross‐validation framework, while participants with p‐tau217 data were reserved for independent biological concordance analyses. Each fold in the cross‐validation framework served as an internal holdout set, providing an unbiased estimate of model performance on previously unseen individuals. A phi coefficient (ɸ) was calculated to test the alignment between predicted MCI class and the true p‐tau217 groups, followed by a chi‐squared test (χ2).

As a comparator analysis, a logistic regression model was trained using MMSE scores to predict the clinical MCI classification. The clinical classification was established through the diagnostic procedures described above and the model was evaluated using the same cross‐validation framework as the Altoida NeuroMarker model. This analysis was intended to provide a benchmark comparison against a widely used cognitive screening instrument and to contextualize the performance of the Altoida NeuroMarker relative to MMSE alone.

To compare the discriminative performance of the Altoida NeuroMarker and MMSE, a paired comparison of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves was performed using the DeLong test on the subset of participants with both Altoida NeuroMarker and MMSE data available (n = 445 participants; 703 assessments).

Two‐step workflow combining Altoida NeuroMarker and p‐tau217

To evaluate the Altoida NeuroMarker's potential as an upstream triage tool preceding biomarker testing, we assessed a modeled two‐step workflow combining it with plasma p‐tau217. This analysis did not involve the development of a combined predictive model. Rather, it was designed to reflect a clinically relevant scenario in which the rapid digital cognitive assessment is applied first to enrich the population, followed by p‐tau217 only in those testing positive for MCI, using Bayesian modeling of sequential test performance. We assumed 30% baseline prevalence of underlying AD neuropathology in older adults, consistent with reported meta‐analyses, 12 and aligned with recent population‐based estimates. 13

Post‐assessment probability of AD neuropathology after the Altoida assessment was calculated using the assumed baseline prevalence and the sensitivity (79%) and specificity (83%) from cross‐validation results of the MCI model in the present study. Predictive values for p‐tau217 within the enriched subgroup were then calculated using standard Bayesian formulations based on plasma p‐tau217 test performance, modeled with sensitivity = 90.3% and specificity = 91.3% based on previously published data, 14 with the intent to illustrate the potential operational impact of a sequential testing strategy under realistic population‐level prevalence assumptions, using observed test performance characteristics.

RESULTS

Study population comprising the MCI classifier

The ML training cohort is presented in Table 1, consisting of 533 participants with a clinical diagnosis of CU (n = 381) or MCI (n = 152). Among participants who completed two assessments (274), only three (1.1%) observed change in their clinically assigned diagnostic category from CU to MCI at follow‐up. Females were more prevalent in the CU group compared to the MCI group (64% vs. 49%; P < 0.01). Participants with MCI were older, had lower MMSE scores, and differed in education and ethnicity relative to CU participants (Table 1). Characteristics of the p‐tau217 cohort are described in the Supplementary Results in supporting information.

Prediction of MCI with the Altoida NeuroMarker

Applying 5‐fold cross‐validation, the Altoida NeuroMarker demonstrated strong performance to detect MCI (Figure 2A) with a ROC area under the curve (AUC) = 0.89 ± 0.01, sensitivity = 0.79 ± 0.07, and specificity = 0.83 ± 0.05 at the optimized threshold. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.82 ± 0.02 and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.90 ± 0.04 (Table S2 in supporting information).

Application of the MCI classifier in participants with plasma p‐tau217 data

After training the MCI classification model using participants without plasma p‐tau217 measurements, we applied the trained model to predict MCI in a separate subset of participants with available p‐tau217 data. The model correctly identified 73% of MCI cases and 81% of CU participants (Figure 2B). The MCI classifier showed strong discrimination overall between CU and MCI in this subset (ROC AUC = 0.87), an accuracy of 0.78, and balanced predictive values (positive predictive value [PPV] = 0.75; NPV = 0.80; Table S3 in supporting information).

Prediction of MCI with MMSE

Using cross‐validation, MMSE detected MCI with a ROC AUC of 0.84 ± 0.02 (Figure S2 in supporting information), sensitivity of 0.67 ± 0.09, and specificity of 0.82 ± 0.06 at the optimized threshold. This model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.77 ± 0.04 and an NPV of 0.85 ± 0.05 (Table S4 in supporting information).

To directly compare the discriminative performance of the Altoida NeuroMarker and MMSE, a paired comparison was performed on the subset of participants with both Altoida NeuroMarker and MMSE data available. The Altoida NeuroMarker achieved a ROC AUC of 0.87 compared to 0.84 for MMSE. A DeLong test demonstrated that this difference was statistically significant (difference in ROC AUC = 0.03, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.001–0.077; Z = 2.03; p = 0.04).

Classification of plasma p‐tau217 categories using digitally derived cognitive–functional signals

As an exploratory analysis, we next evaluated whether multimodal cognitive–functional signals derived from the Altoida NeuroMarker were associated with plasma p‐tau217 status by training a separate classifier to distinguish low from elevated p‐tau217 groups.

Applying 5‐fold cross‐validation, the model showed a ROC AUC of 0.77 ± 0.08, with sensitivity 0.68 ± 0.18 and specificity 0.70 ± 0.16, at the mean optimized threshold (Figure 3A). The overall accuracy was 0.67 ± 0.08 (Table S5 in supporting information).

Association between MCI predictions and plasma p‐tau217 categories

We next evaluated the association between MCI predictions and plasma p‐tau217 status. Predicted MCI was significantly associated with p‐tau217 (p = 0.004; Figure 3B). Participants with elevated plasma p‐tau217 were more likely to be classified as MCI by the Altoida NeuroMarker (67%), indicating an association between digitally derived cognitive impairment and biomarker evidence consistent with AD pathology. Participants with low p‐tau217 showed a more balanced split between CU and MCI.

Performance of a two‐step workflow with the Altoida NeuroMarker and p‐tau217

To evaluate the operational impact of a sequential testing approach, we modeled a cascading workflow starting with the Altoida NeuroMarker, followed by plasma p‐tau217. Because 35.6% of the initial cohort tested positive for MCI on the digital assessment, this strategy resulted in a 64.4% reduction in the number of individuals requiring biomarker testing (Table 2). Applying the Altoida NeuroMarker as an upstream screening tool increased the conditional probability of AD pathology from the assumed baseline prevalence of 30% to 66.6% (Table 2).

When applied alone, p‐tau217's PPV was 81.7%. 15 With the reported sensitivity (90.3%) and specificity (91.3%) of plasma p‐tau217, the expected PPV for the enriched subgroup was calculated using the Bayes theorem. Altoida NeuroMarker pre‐screening increased the diagnostic certainty of the subsequent blood test, resulting in a modeled PPV of 95.3% for the two‐step workflow (Table 2). This was accompanied by a modest reduction in NPV (from 95.7% to 90.2%), which remains highly reliable for excluding pathology in those testing screening negative.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrate that the Altoida NeuroMarker can accurately identify individuals with MCI using a self‐administered, multimodal digital assessment based on AR and ML. We further show that digitally derived cognitive–functional signals are associated with plasma p‐tau217 status and can be used to classify biomarker‐defined categories with meaningful performance. To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of the first demonstrations that an unsupervised, tablet‐based multimodal assessment may support a scalable sequential workflow combining cognitive and biological markers of disease.

Importantly, the p‐tau217 classification model should be interpreted as an exploratory analysis of the relationship between multimodal, digitally derived cognitive–functional signals and biomarker‐defined categories, rather than as evidence that the Altoida NeuroMarker directly identifies AD pathology. Cognitive impairment and AD pathology are related but distinct constructs, and cognitive impairment may arise from multiple etiologies. In the proposed workflow, the digital assessment is intended to support upstream cognitive triage and enrichment of the population undergoing biomarker testing, while plasma p‐tau217 provides biological information relevant to AD pathology.

The significant association between MCI predictions and elevated plasma p‐tau217 supports the clinical relevance of the Altoida NeuroMarker and suggests that this digital assessment captures cognitive changes associated with an increased likelihood of underlying AD pathology. Specifically, participants with elevated plasma p‐tau217 were more likely to be predicted as MCI, consistent with the relationship between this biomarker and cognitive impairment. Conversely, the balanced distribution of predicted MCI and CU among those with low p‐tau217 likely reflects heterogeneity in both clinical status and underlying pathology, including non–AD‐related causes of cognitive impairment and early, subtle cognitive changes that may precede detectable plasma p‐tau217 elevations. 16

To contextualize this performance relative to traditional tests, we evaluated MMSE's ability to discriminate MCI within the same dataset. The Altoida NeuroMarker demonstrated higher sensitivity with strong overall discrimination. We also observed high NPVs across datasets. Thus, the Altoida NeuroMarker may serve as an early, rule‐out step prior to further biomarker testing, particularly in settings in which access to specialty care is limited. In recent survey data, clinicians and payors indicated preference for tools with high NPV (> 90%) to enable more efficient allocation of health‐care resources. 17 A digital “rule‐out” at the frontline ensures biological testing is reserved for those with the highest clinical probability of disease.

The sequential testing approach demonstrated that the upstream use of the Altoida NeuroMarker may enrich the population undergoing biomarker testing, increasing the pre‐test probability of AD pathology and thereby improving the modeled PPV of subsequent plasma p‐tau217 testing. Implementation of plasma p‐tau217 in community‐based or primary care settings is hampered by the prevalence challenge. In low‐prevalence settings (e.g., 30%), even a highly specific test can yield high rates of false positives, limiting biomarker performance in unselected populations. Per our model, the Altoida NeuroMarker upstream usage allows subsequent p‐tau217 testing to achieve higher PPV, minimizing the diagnostic ambiguity seen in frontline clinical settings.

A potential concern in sequential testing is the trade‐off between predictive values. In the present workflow, the gain in PPV (81.7% to 95.3%) was accompanied by modest reduction in NPV (95.7% to 90.2%), reflecting a net clinical benefit rather than a loss of diagnostic certainty. Increased PPV is critical in ensuring that high‐cost biological interventions target the correct patients. The reduction in NPV does not compromise reliability for frontline triage; individuals who screen negative can be safely monitored and re‐evaluated over time. Further, these performance measures go beyond recommended standards for triaging tests by the Global CEO Initiative. 18 This workflow may also help address physician‐reported concerns regarding the efficiency and placement of biomarker testing, which have been identified as barriers to broader adoption in both primary care and clinical research settings. 15

Beyond primary care, this workflow may also support clinical trial enrichment by reducing the number of confirmatory biomarker tests required to identify biomarker‐positive individuals, accelerating recruitment timelines, and lowering the per‐patient cost to randomize, making large‐scale preventive trials more economically viable.

Recent advances in remote blood collection technologies may further expand the feasibility of such workflows beyond traditional clinical settings. Emerging evidence suggests that capillary blood sampling and home‐based collection approaches can support the measurement of AD‐related blood biomarkers with analytical performance comparable to conventional venipuncture‐based methods. 19 , 20 When combined with self‐administered digital cognitive assessments, these approaches may enable scalable and geographically accessible models for early cognitive and biological screening, risk stratification, and longitudinal monitoring across diverse populations.

Growing evidence supports the complementary use of digital cognitive assessments and blood‐based biomarkers, with improved diagnostic performance when applied in conjunction. For example, the BioCog digital test battery showed higher accuracy for identifying AD‐related cognitive impairment when combined with p‐tau217 than standard physician evaluation or the blood test alone in primary care. 21 Other digital paradigms have reported similar benefits, including improved prediction of longitudinal cognitive decline with the Computerized Cognitive Composite (C3) 22 and enhanced detection of cognitive impairment using the Linus Digital Clock and Recall. 23 Prior studies have not examined whether a digital cognitive platform can support functional‐to‐biological assessment through complementary models for detecting cognitive impairment and predicting plasma p‐tau217 status, linking digitally derived cognitive classification with underlying neuropathology in alignment with emerging clinical workflows.

Some study limitations warrant consideration. First, the p‐tau217 prediction results were based on a relatively small sample size, potentially curbing the model's classification performance. These results should be interpreted with caution until replicated in larger datasets and independent cohorts. Second, recruiting participants with MCI posed challenges for balancing the proportion of CU and MCI groups within the training dataset. 24 We mitigated this challenge by recruiting a large, multi‐site cohort, though further evaluation across diverse populations will be important. Third, all participants without available plasma p‐tau217 measurements were intentionally included in the training set. Future studies should evaluate generalizability across fully independent datasets and diverse hardware configurations. In addition, because the workflow was developed using the LucentAD p‐tau217 assay and its published classification framework, further work will be required to evaluate generalizability across alternative p‐tau217 assays and thresholding approaches. Finally, while the two‐step workflow results suggest that the Altoida NeuroMarker provides value for clinical triage and trial enrichment, evaluation in real‐world settings will be an important next step to validate implementation at scale. Future studies will need larger sample sizes to evaluate the performance of this sequential workflow in preclinical and symptomatic populations separately.

Taken together, these findings offer a proof‐of‐concept demonstration that the Altoida NeuroMarker can support functional‐to‐biological inference in individuals with MCI. Looking ahead, the Altoida NeuroMarker could support models that integrate digital cognitive performance with biological, genetic (i.e., apolipoprotein E ɛ4 carriership), and demographic risk factors to improve stratification of clinically relevant subpopulations. 25 Such approaches may enable more personalized triage and could be evaluated alongside established biomarkers, including plasma p‐tau217 and cerebrospinal fluid or positron emission tomography–based measures.
