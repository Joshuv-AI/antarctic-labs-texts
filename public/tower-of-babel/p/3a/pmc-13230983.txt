Associations between smartphone addiction, anxiety, depression, and academic performance among university students
Authors: Shazli Ezzat Ghazali, Ponnusamy Subramaniam, Hend Faye Al-Shahrani, Roslee Rajikan, Noor Alaudin Abdul Wahab, Qodriyah Hj Mohd Saad, Suzaily Wahab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn today’s globalized world, technology significantly influences daily life. While it offers convenience, it also brings challenges. The growing prevalence of smartphone use has raised concerns regarding smartphone addiction. This study examined the relationship between smartphone addiction, anxiety, depression, and academic performance among university students. A total of 1,846 students (1,362 females and 484 males; mean age = 19.62 ± 1.11) participated by completing an online questionnaire that included the Smartphone Addiction Scale-Malay version (SAS-M), Beck Anxiety Inventory-Malay version (BAI-M), and Beck Depression Inventory-Malay version (BDI-M). Findings revealed that higher levels of smartphone addiction were significantly associated with greater symptoms of anxiety and depression. In contrast, no direct association was observed between smartphone addiction and academic performance. These results indicate that while smartphone addiction may not directly impair academic performance, it poses substantial risks to students’ psychological well-being. Intervention programs are necessary to promote digital well-being and reduce the psychological impact of excessive smartphone use among students. Given the cross-sectional design and the absence of several potentially important confounding and mediating variables, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and warrant confirmation in future longitudinal and multivariable studies.Keywords: Smartphone addictions, Depression, Anxiety, Academic achievement, University studentsSubject terms: Human behaviour, Psychology, Health care

Introduction

In today’s globalized world, the use of technology, especially smartphones, has expanded rapidly and significantly influenced daily life. This surge, fueled by advanced smartphone models and versatile applications, has enhanced communication, entertainment, education, and social connectivity. In 2016, there were approximately 2.5 billion smartphone users worldwide, projected to grow to 3.8 billion by 20211. In Malaysia, users increased from 18.46 million in 2016 to over 33 million by 20241.

According to the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission in 2018, smartphone ownership rose from 74.0% to 76.4%, with usage highest among individuals aged below 34. Notably, 95% of tertiary education students in Malaysia use smartphones2. These devices serve practical and social purposes, with Malaysian users spending an average of 187 min daily on their smartphones3.

Despite these benefits, excessive smartphone use has led to growing concerns about smartphone addiction—a form of behavioral addiction marked by compulsive usage patterns. Lin, Chiang et al.4 identified four core features: compulsive checking behaviors, increased tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and resulting functional impairment. This aligns with the broader concept of technostress, described by5 as psychological strain resulting from an inability to adapt healthily to new technologies. Smartphone addiction, therefore, can be understood as a manifestation of technostress, particularly when overuse disrupts users’ mental health and functional capacity.

Research indicates that compulsive smartphone use—for texting, social media, gaming, or entertainment—can contribute to psychological distress, including anxiety and depression6–9. These effects are amplified when technostress arises from constant connectivity, performance pressure, or fear of missing out. Students experiencing high levels of technostress often report frustration, impaired concentration, and disrupted interpersonal relationships10,11.

Surveys among Malaysian university students have linked prolonged smartphone use to headaches, sleep disturbances, and cognitive difficulties12. Zulkefly and Baharudin13 also reported that students with high smartphone usage had lower self-esteem and increased psychological symptoms.

In terms of academic impact, the literature presents mixed findings. Several studies have reported that excessive smartphone use negatively impacts academic performance due to distractions, procrastination, and reduced cognitive focus14–16. However, other studies have found no significant relationship, suggesting that academic performance may be influenced by other moderating factors such as motivation, time management, or type of smartphone use17,18 This inconsistency underscores the need for further research. Clarifying whether the addiction–achievement link is direct or contingent has clear practical implications for student support services. In the Malaysian setting, few projects have studied mental health and academic performance at the same time using validated Malay-language instruments and large, diverse cohorts. Addressing this gap will help design targeted and culturally sensitive interventions while meeting rigorous standards.This study is grounded in two complementary theoretical perspectives: (i) behavioral addiction theory and (ii) technostress theory. Behavioral addiction theory conceptualizes excessive smartphone use as a non-substance addiction characterized by salience, tolerance, withdrawal-like symptoms, and functional impairment4,19. In parallel, technostress theory, originally proposed by Brod (1984) and later extended in organizational and psychological research20, explains how maladaptive responses to information and communication technologies may produce psychological strain, including anxiety and depressive symptoms, particularly under conditions of constant connectivity and reduced perceived control. Within these frameworks, excessive smartphone use is expected to function both as a maladaptive coping strategy and a source of chronic psychological stress. Accordingly, we hypothesized that higher levels of smartphone addiction would be positively associated with anxiety (H1) and depression (H2), and negatively associated with academic performance (H3), reflecting functional impairment in academic domains.”

Against this backdrop, the present study examines associations between smartphone addiction, anxiety, depression, and academic performance among first-year undergraduates drawn from multiple faculties. Using validated Malay-language instruments (SAS-M, BAI-M, BDI-M) in a large Malaysian cohort, we tested the following hypotheses: H1 smartphone addiction is positively associated with anxiety; H2 smartphone addiction is positively associated with depression; and H3 smartphone addiction is negatively associated with academic performance. We also conducted an exploratory subgroup analysis by gaming status to examine whether specific usage patterns relate to addiction severity.

Methods

Design and sample

This cross-sectional study was conducted in September 2019 among newly enrolled undergraduate students at a local university in Malaysia. A multi-stage stratified random sampling method was employed. First, students were stratified by faculty and income category to ensure representation across academic disciplines and socio-economic background. Within each stratum, participants were randomly selected using a list of registered students provided by the university.

Full-time Malaysian undergraduates were included in this study. Exclusion criteria comprised non-Malaysian students, students from intakes other than 2019/2020, duplicate or incomplete responses, and those with extreme low academic performance. Academic performance was measured using “PGNK” (Purata Gred Nilai Keseluruhan), the Malaysian equivalent of a Cumulative Grade Point Average (CGPA). A PGNK cutoff of < 2.0 was used to exclude outliers with exceptionally low academic standing that may bias the analysis, as this is commonly regarded as below the academic probation threshold.

Data collection procedure and ethics

Prior to the actual research commencement, a pilot study was administered on 69 undergraduates who were not part of the study. The pilot study respondents showed no difficulty in understanding and answering the self-administered online questionnaire. Hence, the actual research was carried out. A concise introduction to the study purpose was given to the students. Data were collected through a self-administered online questionnaire consisting of demographic information, patterns of smartphone use, the Smartphone Addiction Scale-Malay (SAS-M), the Beck Anxiety Inventory-Malay (BAI-M), and the Beck Depression Inventory-Malay (BDI-M). Reliability analyses confirmed satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.74–0.96). Students who consented to participate in the study completed the self-administered questionnaire via Google Form. Consent was implied through voluntary participation and submission via Google Form. Ethical approval for this examines acquired from the Research Ethics Committee, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (RECUKM) (Approval Code: JEP-2021-747). All techniques observed the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent changed into implied via voluntary completion of the web questionnaire. Instrument

A self-administered Google Form questionnaire was distributed to 2,181 participants. The questionnaire consisted of six sections: (a) demographic characteristics, including information on age, race, gender, and family income; (b) patterns of smartphone usage, covering daily usage duration (in hours), monthly expenses on smartphones, and primary smartphone functions; (c) factors influencing smartphone use; (d) the Malay version of the Smartphone Addiction Scale (SAS-M); (e) the Malay version of the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI); and (f) the Malay version of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). The pilot study demonstrated that the selected instruments were reliable for the actual study, with Cronbach’s Alpha values ranging from 0.74 to 0.96.

Smartphone Addiction Scale

The Malay version of the ‘Smartphone Addiction Scale’ was adapted from21. The SAS-M consists of 33 items divided into six domains: daily-life disturbance, positive anticipation, withdrawal, cyberspace-oriented relationship, overuse, and tolerance. Symptoms are assessed using a response scale from 1 to 6, where 1 indicates strong disagreement and 6 indicates strong agreement. The SAS-M has a minimum score of 33 and a maximum score of 198; higher scores indicate a greater risk of smartphone addiction. The Cronbach alpha value for the SAS-M is 0.92.

Beck’s Anxiety Inventory

The Becks Anxiety Inventory-Malay Version, developed by Firdaus and Nor Sheeren22 was used as an instrument to measure anxiety in this study. The inventory which comprises of 21 items with a Cronbach Alpha of 0.94 was scored using a four-point scale and measured in categories that are minimal anxiety (score 0–7), mild anxiety (score 8–15), moderate anxiety (score16-25) and severe anxiety (score 26–63).

Beck’s Depression Inventory

Beck’s Depression Inventory-Malay version developed by Mukhtar and Oei23 was used to measure depression. Though the original version consists of 21 item, one item was omitted from the Malay version due to the cultural factors resulting in a total of 20 items. The inventory was further subdivided into four categories which are no mild depression (score 0–9), mild-moderate depression (score 10–18), moderate-severe depression (score 19–29) and severe depression (score 30–63)24.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS software version 26. Descriptive statistical analysis was conducted to calculate the mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. Pearson’s correlation assessed the strength of the relationship between the variables, while Simple Linear Regression was performed to evaluate the effect of smartphone addiction on anxiety and depression.

Discussion

Smartphone usage is on the rise despite its unfavourable implications, particularly among university students. Smartphones have become an essential part of daily life, with nearly all university students now owning one. Although smartphones have their beneficial purpose of use, over-usage could be detrimental.

The results of this study uncovered that 40.9% of students use smartphones for 2–5 h per day. This finding is supported by9, who reported that approximately 45.0% of students spend 4–6 h a day on their smartphones. Similarly, Sut, Kurt et al.25 found that about 40.1% of students also use their smartphones for 4 to 6 h daily. Even more concerning, 58.3% of students reported using smartphones for over six hours daily A study by Ko, Song et al.26 indicated that while long-term smartphone usage can enhance concentration, it may also lead to increased muscle stiffness and decreased visual acuity if users do not incorporate stretching and eye movement during prolonged use. Additionally, Jamal, Sedie et al.27 found that heavy smartphone usage could contribute to long-term memory impairment, prolonged sleep, insomnia, chronic headaches, and concentration issues among students. Similarly, Hammad, Alyami et al.28 reported that internet addiction significantly affected sleep quality among medical students in Saudi Arabia.

The findings of this study found no significant difference between gender and smartphone addiction. This is in line with the study by14, who also found no significant difference between gender and addiction score. Demirci, Akgönül et al.29 found through their study that the addiction is prevalent irrespective of the students’ gender. However, a Korean study revealed that female students were more addicted to their smartphones compared to male students30.

It was found that there is association between smartphone addiction with anxiety through this study. Those who are found to have high scores of smartphone addiction reported high scores of anxiety and depression as well. These findings are in line with similar earlier studies carried out9,31,32, including31, who found that social media addiction was associated with symptoms of depression and generalized anxiety among deaf and hard-of-hearing students. Hawi and Samaha33 in their study found that those who are addicted to smartphones have greater odds to experience anxiety compared to those who are not. A study carried out among the Turkish university students identified that smartphone overuse could result in depression, anxiety, low sleep quality, and daytime dysfunction29. While most studies indicate a positive relationship between smartphone addiction and anxiety, a study by Lepp, Barkley et al.34 found a negative correlation between the two variables. In contrast, Aker et al.35 suggested that smartphone addiction is influenced by depression, anxiety, insomnia, and familial social support.

The study’s findings indicate a relationship between smartphone addiction and depression. A systematic review by Elhai, Dvorak et al.36 identified a common link between depression and problematic smartphone usage. In a study involving 353 Korean college students, depression was found to be a significant predictor of smartphone addiction. Similar relationships have been reported in other research17,37,38. Additionally, a study by Bian and Leung39 highlighted that loneliness, which correlates positively with depression, emerged as a key predictor of smartphone addiction scores. While most studies identified a positive association between smartphone addiction and depression30, reported a negative association between the two variables.

The study’s results revealed no significant correlation between smartphone addiction and students’ academic performance. This aligns with the findings of Matar Boumosleh and Jaalouk17, who also concluded that academic performance was not associated with smartphone addiction scores. Other studies, however, found that smartphone addiction negatively affects students’ academic performance14,40,41. Nayak18 Nayak (2018) found that besides behavioural changes, smartphone addiction hardly affects female students. However, male students who are addicted to smartphones tend to neglect work, feel anxious and lose control of themselves. Kibona and Mgaya42 through their study on a Ruaha Catholic University, found that smartphones adversely affect students’ academic performance as they reported to be distracted from the lectures when someone texted or called them during a lecture.

The non-significant correlation between smartphone addiction and academic performance (r = 0.005, p = 0.822) in this study suggests that smartphone use does not directly impact academic outcomes. This finding aligns with some previous studies17, which also reported no direct relationship between smartphone addiction and academic performance. One possible explanation is that the impact of smartphone addiction on academics may be mediated by other factors such as academic stress, time management, and the primary purpose of smartphone usage.

Academic stress could act as a mediator, where students experiencing high stress may use smartphones as a coping mechanism, leading to addiction without directly affecting their academic results. Similarly, the purpose of smartphone use (e.g., academic research versus social media) may also determine its impact. For instance, using smartphones primarily for educational purposes may have neutral or even positive effects on academic performance, while excessive use for entertainment or social media may hinder academic focus.

Further studies should investigate these mediators using structural equation modeling to explore the complex relationships between smartphone addiction, academic stress, usage patterns, and academic performance. Such insights can guide targeted interventions for students at risk of smartphone addiction without negatively impacting their academic success.

Nevertheless, Karuniawan and Cahyanti43 brought a new meaning on the association between smartphone addiction and academic stress. They found that the higher the academic stress, the greater the smartphone dependence on students and vice versa. Students tend to use smartphones as a stress reliever when feeling stressed. This situation could eventually lead to smartphone addiction if used uncontrollably44.

Overall, the findings of this study align with other research that indicates a relationship between smartphone addiction, anxiety, depression, and academic performance among university students. While it is concerning that smartphone addiction is increasing, future studies should be conducted across universities in Malaysia to gain a more comprehensive understanding of this issue.

The findings of this study should be interpreted as preliminary. Although a large and diverse undergraduate sample was examined using validated Malay-language instruments, several important variables that may influence the relationship between smartphone addiction, mental health, and academic performance were not measured. These include attentional difficulties (e.g., inattention), academic stress, motivation, time management skills, sleep quality, and prior mental health problem. The absence of these factors limits causal inference and may partly explain the lack of a direct association between smartphone addiction and academic performance. Future research should adopt longitudinal or structural equation modeling approaches that incorporate these variables to clarify causal pathways and potential mediation mechanisms.

Limitations

This study has some clear limitations. First, several critical confounding factors were not included in data collection. These include symptoms like hyperactivity and inattention, parental involvement levels, and any previous psychiatric treatments. Excluding these factors may have introduced bias affecting the results. Second, important moderating factors such as motivation, stress levels, and time management skills were not measured, which could partly explain the absence of a direct link between problematic smartphone use and academic outcomes. Third, the study depended solely on self-reported questionnaires. This method may reduce accuracy because participants might have provided incorrect estimates of their smartphone use or mental health symptoms. Such inaccuracies threaten the reliability and validity of the findings. To improve future studies, it is advisable to use longitudinal designs that monitor participants over time, incorporate objective tools for tracking smartphone behavior, and apply advanced statistical methods like structural equation modeling. These approaches can better illuminate causal relationships and mediation mechanisms underlying the observed associations.

Conclusion and recommendations

Smartphone addiction can lead to significant functional impairments, interfering with students’ academic, personal, and social lives. This study highlights a concerning association between smartphone addiction and elevated levels of anxiety and depression among university students, although no significant link was found with academic performance. These findings underscore the need to treat excessive smartphone use as a public mental health concern within higher education.

To address this issue, universities should implement evidence-based interventions such as digital well-being workshops, digital detox programs, and tech-free campus zones. Time-management and mindfulness training can be integrated into student orientation and counseling services to foster healthier digital habits. In addition, mental health services should provide targeted support for students experiencing technology-related distress, and peer-support groups can offer a safe platform for shared coping strategies.

From a broader public health perspective, these findings point to the necessity of embedding digital health literacy and psychological support into institutional mental health policies. Educational settings must be proactive in promoting mental well-being by aligning campus-based interventions with national mental health strategies. Incorporating smartphone addiction awareness into university health promotion frameworks can help prevent long-term psychological consequences and foster more resilient student populations. In doing so, higher education institutions can contribute meaningfully to broader efforts in mental health prevention and digital wellness among young adults.

Finally, the study’s novel contributions are found in its large representative sample, its use of validated Malay-language instruments, and the subtle evidence that, despite its negative effects on mental health, smartphone addiction may not directly affect academic achievement. Public health policy and institutional practice are informed by these insights.
