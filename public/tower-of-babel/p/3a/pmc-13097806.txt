Network analysis of childhood trauma and meaning in life in adolescents with and without depression
Authors: Wanwan Xie, Hui Lei, Chumei Ning, Daifeng Dong, Xiaocui Zhang, Hengyi Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent depression represents a global mental health crisis with dramatically increasing rates worldwide. Childhood trauma (CT) is an established risk factor that disrupts meaning in life (MIL), a process that may exact in a complex interaction with depression. Yet, the trauma-meaning interactions in depressed adolescents remain poorly understood, which impedes development of targeted interventions for vulnerable youth.MethodsIn this study, we applied network analysis in a large sample of 2,523 Chinese adolescents (Mage = 14.3 years, 46% male) to examine how CT and MIL interrelate in adolescents, and whether these relationships differ by depression status. Based on Children’s Depression Inventory scores (cutoff = 20), 859 adolescents were classified as depressed and 1,664 as non-depressed. Partial-correlation networks were estimated for four CT subtypes (physical abuse, physical neglect, emotional abuse, and emotional neglect) and two MIL dimensions (presence of meaning and search for meaning).ResultsDepressed adolescents exhibited significantly stronger global connectivity between trauma and meaning constructs, indicating maladaptive coupling when depression is present. Six network edges differed between groups, with “Physical Abuse-Search for Meaning” and “Emotional Neglect-Search for Meaning” bridging CT and MIL and stronger in depressed adolescents.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that CT shows a broader association with meaning-making processes in depression. Physical abuse and emotional neglect are particularly associated with adolescents’ capacity to search for and construct meaning. Results underscore the critical need for comprehensive trauma screening in adolescent depression assessments and highlight meaning-centered interventions as promising therapeutic approaches for prevention and treatment of depression in trauma-exposed youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04218-w.Keywords: Adolescents, Childhood trauma, Meaning in life, Depression, Network comparison

Background

Depression is one of the most common and debilitating mental health disorders in adolescents, marked by persistent low mood, anhedonia, and reduced energy. Its global prevalence has increased substantially in recent decades [1]. Beyond emotional suffering, adolescent depression impairs cognitive, emotional, and social functioning and substantially elevates suicide risk [2, 3], making it a pressing public health concern. Understanding the mechanisms that shape vulnerability to depression during this critical developmental period is therefore of urgent importance. Adolescence is characterized by a unique psychological sensitivity [4], during which emotions are often intense, labile, and internalized. This developmental period is marked by an elevated susceptibility to internalizing problems, notably anxiety and depression [5]. The chronicity of negative affect poses a significant threat to holistic development [6], compromising not only cognitive capacities but also core psychological resources such as meaning in life and happiness [7–9], and may even lead to self-injury or suicide [10]. According to Erikson’s theory of psychosocial development, the developmental task of an individual during adolescence is to establish “self-identity”, that is, to form an internally coherent cognition about “who I am” and “the direction of my life” [11]. This process inevitably involves an individual’s active exploration of the meaning of life and the purpose of existence. Childhood trauma (CT) can seriously disrupt this normative development, precipitating a state of role confusion characterized by a fragmented self-concept, eroded self-worth, and profound disorientation about the future [12, 13]. The interruption of this development pathway is closely related to depression [14]. Therefore, in the specific developmental window of adolescence, it is of unique theoretical necessity and clinical urgency to explore how trauma affects an individual’s meaning construction and the connection between this process and depression.

CT is one of the most robust risk factors for adolescent depression. Typically, CT is defined as abuse or neglect occurring before age 18, including emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical abuse, physical neglect, and sexual abuse [15]. According to the diathesis-stress model, CT acts as a potent stressor that heightens vulnerability to later psychopathology [16]. Adolescents with trauma histories show disproportionately high rates of depression and other psychiatric disorders [17, 18]. Moreover, CT subtypes appear to exert differential effects: emotional abuse is strongly linked to depressive symptoms and non-suicidal self-injury [19]; emotional abuse, physical abuse, and emotional neglect predict early-onset chronic depression [20]; and sexual abuse further increases risk for anxiety and depression [21]. Recent network analyses of symptom structures suggest that trauma exposure not only intensifies symptom severity but also alters interconnections among depressive and anxious symptoms, pointing to distinct pathways of vulnerability in trauma-exposed youth [22]. However, beyond directly influencing depression, CT fundamentally disrupts the individual’s meaning in life (MIL) [23].

MIL was defined as the sense of significance, coherence, and purpose in one’s existence. MIL comprises two dimensions: presence of meaning (the degree to which life feels valuable and purposeful) and search for meaning (the motivation to find or strengthen meaning) [24]. Previous studies have shown that the severity of CT exposure is negatively correlated with MIL, impairing the individual’s capacity to feel a sense of coherence, significance, purpose [25],and MIL [26]. Further research has extended these findings by examining specific trauma subtypes, indicating, for example, that psychological maltreatment negatively predicted MIL [27], emotional abuse significantly predicted lower purpose in life, physical abuse significantly predicted greater purpose in life [28]. A longitudinal study found that childhood abuse negatively predicted the presence of meaning, positively predicted meaning confusion, but only meaning confusion positively predicted depression [29]. However, most prior work has either focused on single trauma types or treated MIL as a unitary construct, without systematically examining how different CT subtypes relate to different MIL dimensions. Therefore, adopting a dimensional perspective is essential to clarify the distinct pathways through which CT influences MIL.

According to the dual-system model, all adaptive behaviors can be attributed to two fundamental biopsychological systems—the approach system and the avoidance system [30]. However, the ability to create meaning from adverse experiences is influenced by various factors, such as an individual’s depressive state [31]. Existing research indicates that there is a bidirectional relationship between depression and MIL. A study indicated that strong MIL has been identified as a critical protective factor in adolescent psychological development [32]. Empirical studies consistently demonstrate that adolescents with higher MIL report fewer depressive symptoms [33, 34], in part because MIL buffers the negative psychological effects of CT [25]. Interventions such as logotherapy, which help individuals cultivate meaning and purpose, have proven effective in reducing depressive symptoms [35–39]. At the same time, depression also may serve as a risk factor for a diminished sense of MIL. For instance, some researchers have argued that severe depressive symptoms can deplete an individual’s psychological resources and vitality, thereby contributing to feelings of meaninglessness, and the research showed that the MIL was a significant factor in identifying an individual’s level of depression, where more depressed groups consistently report lower average MIL scores [40]. A meta-analysis found that the correlation between presence of meaning and depression was significantly stronger than that between search for meaning and depression. Moreover, different dimensions of psychological distress (depression vs. anxiety) were differentially associated with the presence of meaning and the search for meaning [41]. Collectively, these findings imply that the relationship between CT and MIL is not static but may be dynamically moderated by an individual’s depressive state. Specifically, we propose that CT impairs the development of MIL, which in turn serves as a key mediating mechanism leading to depression. Furthermore, this mediating pathway is moderated by the depressive state: individuals with depression tend to process CT through a more negative cognitive‑affective lens. This intensifies the negative association between CT and MIL, resulting in a dysregulated psychological network that sustains depression. To empirically examine this integrative model, we test its core conditional hypothesis: that the association between CT and MIL is moderated by depressive state. While existing research has established a correlation between CT and MIL, no study has investigated this moderating role. The present study addresses this gap. Specifically, leveraging a network analytical approach, we aim to identify which CT subtypes have their negative associations with which MIL dimensions most strongly amplified in the presence of depression. Elucidating these conditional pathways is expected to yield targeted insights for prevention and intervention.

Traditional research on CT, MIL, and depression has often employed structural equation modeling (SEM), which has provided valuable insights. For example, previous SEM studies have demonstrated that CT reduces MIL and that social support mediates this effect [42]. Yet, SEM and related approaches typically assume unidirectional effects between latent constructs and may obscure complex, reciprocal relationships among specific CT subtypes and MIL dimensions. More importantly, the causal directions in SEM are specified a priori, while cross-sectional data cannot test such directional hypotheses. For example, although a model specifying “CT→ depression → MIL” might yield statistically significant path coefficients in our data, the cross-sectional design precludes valid causal inference regarding this proposed directional model. Network analysis provides a powerful complementary approach, conceptualizing observed variables as nodes and their partial correlations as edges [43, 44]. This framework does not require specifying the direction of relationships, thereby avoiding premature causal claims and enabling the identification of “bridge” variables that link trauma and meaning processes, offering a finer-grained and less assumption-laden understanding of how CT and MIL interact in shaping depression risk [45, 46].

The present study leverages network analysis to examine how CT subtypes and MIL dimensions interrelate in adolescents, and whether these relationships differ between depressed and non-depressed groups. We hypothesized that (a) the CT-MIL network in the depressed group would exhibit stronger connectivity than in the non-depressed group, and (b) within the depressed network, specific connections between CT subtypes and MIL dimensions would be significantly stronger, and the central nodes would differ from those in the non-depressed group. These intensified and maladaptive network features are expected to maintain negative interpretations of traumatic experiences and are thereby associated with increased vulnerability to depression. By clarifying the interplay between CT and MIL, this study addresses a critical gap in understanding the mechanisms of adolescent depression and may inform the development of targeted prevention and intervention strategies aimed at fostering resilience in trauma-exposed youth.

Methods

Participants and procedure

A total of 2,763 questionnaires were distributed in local middle schools. After excluding invalid responses (e.g., patterned answers, incomplete data), 2,523 valid responses remained. Participants were aged 10–18 years (Mage = 14.30, SD = 1.32) and 46% were male (n = 1,162). All adolescents and their guardians provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (KYZ20240039).

Measures

Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI)

Depression was assessed with the CDI [47], a self-report scale containing 27 items and four subscales for ages 7–17, including interpersonal problems, negative mood, negative self-esteem, ineffectiveness, and anhedonia [47]. Each item is scored 0–2; total scores range from 0 to 54. Based on prior work with Chinese adolescents [48], a cutoff of 20 was used to define significant depressive symptoms. Participants with CDI ≥ 20 were placed in the depressed group, and those scoring below 20 in the non-depressed group. Using this criterion, 859 adolescents (Mage = 14.30, SD = 1.52; 35.9% male) were classified as depressed, and 1,664 (Mage = 14.31, SD = 1.73; 51.3% male) as non-depressed. In the current sample, the CDI showed excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ)

The CTQ [49, 50] is a 28-item inventory measuring five CT subtypes (physical abuse, emotional abuse, physical neglect, emotional neglect, sexual abuse) on 5-point Likert scales. Due to privacy and ethical reasons, the sexual abuse items were not administered in this study. Thus, four subscale scores (range 5–25 each) were obtained. Higher scores indicate more severe trauma. In our sample, the CTQ had acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.74).

Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ)

The 10-item MLQ [24] was used to measure MIL, with two subscales of five items each: Presence of Meaning and Search for Meaning. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Total MLQ scores (10-70) reflect overall life meaning, with higher values indicating greater MIL. In this study, the MLQ had good reliability (α = 0.86 for total; α = 0.82 for Presence of Meaning, and α = 0.87 for Search for Meaning).

Network analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0) using the qgraph, mgm, Bootnet, networktools, and NetworkComparisonTest packages [51]. We constructed Gaussian graphical models (partial-correlation networks) for the four CTQ subscales and two MLQ dimensions in each group. Edge weights were estimated using the graphical LASSO with Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) regularization (tuning parameter = 0.5) [52]. In these networks, nodes represent the four CT subtypes and two MIL dimensions, and edges represent regularized partial correlations. A thicker edge indicates a stronger association.

For each network, we used the mgm package to compute node predictability, the proportion of each node’s variance explained by its neighbors [53, 54]. Centrality was assessed using expected influence (EI) to identify the most central nodes in the network, with higher EI value indicating a more significant influence [55]. We also calculated bridge EI [56] to determine which nodes serve as bridges between the CT cluster and MIL dimensions. The R-package qgraph was used to visualize the network [57].

Network accuracy and stability were assessed via non-parametric bootstrap methods in the Bootnet [44]. We used case-dropping bootstraps to estimate the centrality stability (CS) coefficients to evaluate the accuracy of the network. The CS coefficient identifies the maximum proportion of participants that can be dropped from the analysis while after-dropped results that highly correlate (r > 0.70) with the original ones, with 95% probability. A CS coefficient above 0.25 indicates adequate stability and above 0.50 is preferred. We obtained 95% confidence intervals for each edge weight based on 1,000 bootstrap samples. We also used a non-parametric bootstrap test to assess the statistical significance of differences in EI between nodes.

Finally, the R-package Network Comparison Test (NCT) [58] was applied to compare the networks of the depressed vs. non-depressed adolescents. The NCT evaluates whether the overall network structure and global connectivity (sum of absolute edge weights) differ between groups, and it tests individual edge-weight differences using permutation tests (10,000 permutations).

Network Structures

In the depressed group (Fig. 1a), 12 of 15 possible edges (80%) were non-zero. Most edges within the CT and MIL clusters were positive, whereas most between-cluster edges were negative. Node predictability ranged from 76.8% to 89.0%. “Search for Meaning”, “Presence of Meaning”, and “Physical Abuse” had the highest predictability, showing that 89.0%, 88.6%, and 83.8% of their variance can be explained by their neighboring nodes. The edges “Physical Abuse-Emotional Abuse” and “Emotional Neglect-Emotional Abuse” were the strongest. The bootstrapped difference test showed that these two edges were significantly larger than 81% of other edge weights (Supplementary Fig. S1a). The bootstrapped difference test for EI revealed that all nodes differed significantly from “Presence of Meaning”, while “Emotional Abuse”, “Physical Abuse”, and “Physical Neglect” also differed significantly from both “Search for Meaning” and “Emotional Neglect” (Supplementary Fig. S2a).

The overall pattern was similar in the non-depressed group (Fig. 1b), with 12 of 15 edges (80%) non-zero, most within-cluster edges positive and most between-cluster edges negative. Node predictability ranged from 84.0% to 92.0%. “Physical Abuse”, “Search for Meaning”, and “Emotional Abuse” had the highest predictability, showing that 92.0%, 88.6%, and 87.7% of their variance can be explained by their neighboring nodes. The “Physical Abuse-Emotional Abuse” edge was strongest and significantly higher than all other edges (Supplementary Fig. S1b). The bootstrapped difference test for EI showed that all nodes differed significantly from “Presence of Meaning”, while “Emotional Abuse”, and “Physical Neglect” also differed significantly from both “Emotional Neglect” and “Search for Meaning” (Supplementary Fig. S2b).

Core and Bridge Nodes

In the depressed group and (Supplementary Figs. S3a, S4a), the node EI of “Emotional Neglect” are the highest, followed was “Emotional Abuse” (z-score > 1), both of which are significantly greater than those of most other nodes. “Physical Abuse” showed the strongest bridge EI value (z-score > 1).

In the non-depressed group (Supplementary Figs. S3b, S4b), “Emotional Neglect” also had the highest EI (z-score > 1), whereas “Presence of Meaning” showed the second highest. “Physical Abuse” also had the strongest bridge EI (z-score > 1).

As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, 95% bootstrapped CIs for edges were narrow, supporting edge reliability. Case-dropping analyses (Fig. 2a-b) indicated stable centrality indices. Network comparison

The Network Comparison Test revealed significant differences between the depressed and non-depressed networks. Specifically: (a) The network structure invariance test indicated a significant difference in the overall network structure (M = 0.14, p = 0.007); (b) The global strength invariance test showed that global connectivity (the sum of absolute edge weights) was significantly higher in the depressed group (S = 0.49, p = 0.002). (c) The edge invariance test identified that six individual edges differed in strength between the groups. Four of these were within the CT cluster: “Physical Neglect-Physical Abuse” (p = 0.002), “Physical Abuse-Emotional Neglect” (p = 0.004), “Physical Abuse-Emotional Abuse” (p = 0.022), and “Emotional Neglect-Emotional Abuse” (p < 0.001) were all stronger in the depressed network (see the complete list of edge weights for both networks in Supplementary Table S1). The other two differed edges connected CT with MIL: the edges “Physical Abuse-Search for Meaning” (p = 0.008) and “Emotional Neglect-Search for Meaning” (p = 0.015) were significantly stronger in the depressed group. No edges were stronger in the non-depressed group than in the depressed group. (d) The centrality invariance test demonstrated that the EI values of depressed group for “Physical Abuse” (p = 0.006) and “Emotional Abuse” (p = 0.012) were significantly higher than non-depressed group.

Discussion

This study used network analysis to examine how childhood trauma (CT) and meaning in life (MIL) interrelate in adolescents, and how these relationships differ by depression status. Our primary finding was that the CT-MIL network of depressed adolescents was more densely connected than that of non-depressed peers, indicating stronger global connectivity. From a network perspective [59], denser connections suggest that the activation of one symptom or factor more readily propagates to others. Thus, trauma experiences are more broadly and strongly associated with life meaning among depressed adolescents compared to their non-depressed counterparts.

We interpret this stronger coupling as reflecting cognitive-emotional vulnerability in depressed youth. Feelings of worthlessness [60], hopelessness [61], low self-esteem [62], and rumination [63] may magnify the adverse effects of trauma on MIL. In contrast, non-depressed adolescents may rely on protective resources, such as resilience or social support, which buffer trauma’s impact [64]. For instance, adolescents with strong social support demonstrate greater resilience and well-being, helping preserve their sense of meaning in the face of negative experiences [65]. These findings underscore the importance of timely identification and intervention for trauma-exposed depressed adolescents to prevent symptom escalation and strengthen their life meaning.

Another key finding was the stronger interconnections among CT subtypes in the depressed group. Edges linking physical abuse, emotional abuse, and neglect were significantly stronger, consistent with prior evidence that exposure to multiple traumas substantially increases risk for psychopathology [66, 67]. Clinically, this highlights the necessity of assessing and addressing all trauma types, as cumulative exposures may compound vulnerability to depression.

Of particular note, two edges bridging CT to MIL were significantly stronger in the depressed group, including “Physical Abuse-Search for Meaning” and “Emotional Neglect-Search for Meaning”. This suggests that, in depressed adolescents, experiences of physical abuse or emotional neglect are more strongly linked to a reduced drive or capacity to find meaning in life. In other words, these two trauma subtypes appear particularly detrimental to an adolescent’s search for meaning when depression is present. These findings are consistent with prior work showing that trauma (especially physical abuse and emotional neglect) is associated with lower current meaning in life [68] and that meaninglessness can emerge as a central problem in depression [69].

These results have clear practical implications. Physical abuse undermines bodily integrity and safety, leading to chronic stress responses [70], somatic symptoms [71], and impaired self-concept, which may hinder meaning-seeking. Depressed adolescents with abuse histories also show heightened emotion dysregulation and negative self-beliefs [72], further diminishing engagement in meaning-making. Conversely, searching for meaning has been linked to cognitive flexibility [73] and post-traumatic growth [74], suggesting that interventions enhancing MIL could promote resilience.

The centrality invariance test demonstrated that the EI values of depressed group for “Physical Abuse” and “Emotional Abuse” were significantly higher than non-depressed group. This suggests that, in depressed adolescents, these two forms of trauma exhibit stronger connections to other trauma and MIL nodes and are more likely to function as core nodes connecting multiple psychological issues. Depressive features such as negative cognitive bias, worthlessness, and rumination may heighten sensitivity to abusive, threatening experiences [75–77]. According to the Dimensional Model of Adversity, both abuse types belong to the “Threat” dimension [78], which may profoundly affect neurodevelopmental processes related to threat detection, salience processing, and emotion regulation [79, 80], thereby altering children’s emotional perception, attention to emotional stimuli, and regulatory capacity [81–83]. Consequently, these nodes more prone to activating other pathological nodes and sustaining depressive states. This finding underscores the clinical importance of targeted assessment and intervention for abuse-related trauma in the treatment of depression.

Emotional neglect, which emerged as the node with the highest EI value in both groups, also deserves special attention. Its high prevalence [84] and strong links to emotional dysregulation and reduced positive affect [85–88] may explain its pervasive impact, independent of depression status. Neglected adolescents often struggle with emotional awareness and regulation [85, 86], which are associated with a diminished capacity to derive meaning. Targeted interventions such as fostering emotional support and teaching adaptive coping may therefore be especially beneficial for this group. Taken together, these findings highlight that the potential relevance of meaning-centered therapies may be particularly effective for depressed adolescents with histories of physical abuse or emotional neglect. By focusing on meaning-making processes, such approaches represent a potential strategy that is associated with lower depressive symptoms and greater resilience.

Furthermore, the node of “Presence of Meaning” exhibited a higher EI in the non‑depressed group (ranking second), while both meaning related nodes showed lower centrality in the depressed group. This pattern suggests a functional reorganization: a well‑established sense of meaning may provide a stable psychological foundation, enabling adaptive interpretation and buffering against trauma‑related experiences [85, 89]. In contrast, the meaning system in depression appears more passively eroded than proactively protective. This finding underscores the clinical importance of enhancing an individual’s presence of meaning to prevent depression and alleviate depressive symptoms.

“Physical Abuse” emerged as a bridge node in both networks, suggesting it may be a key pathway through which trauma affects MIL. While its association with depression severity is relatively weak or non-specific [90, 91], physical abuse may primarily undermine core psychological foundations—such as security and self-worth—thereby fostering negative worldviews, heightened stress sensitivity, avoidance, and negative cognitive biases [92–94]. Therefore, even when not a direct predictor of depression severity, physical abuse remains a critical therapeutic target due to its foundational role in disrupting meaning and adaptive coping.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, all data were self-reported, which may introduce biases such as social desirability. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships among CT, MIL, and depression. Third, the sexual abuse subscale of the CTQ was not included, limiting the scope of trauma assessment. Future studies should incorporate all CT subtypes and multimodal measures to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Last, this study adopted a binary classification (depressed/non-depressed) design to compare the differences in network structure, which may lead to information loss and reduced statistical power. Future research can adopt longitudinal dynamic network models or continuous conditioning analysis to examine how the severity of depression continuously affects the network structure over time. Despite these limitations, our large sample and innovative analytic approach provide robust insights into the interplay of trauma and meaning in adolescent depression. In sum, the present study extends our understanding of adolescent depression through both theoretical and methodological contributions. Theoretically, the findings not only confirm a more tightly coupled trauma-meaning-depression pathway but also visually illustrate how a depressive state acts as a “negative cognitive filter”, systematically reshaping the psychological architecture. Methodologically, the application of network analysis offers a novel perspective for elucidating the maintenance mechanisms of depression that moves beyond traditional variable‑centered approaches.

Conclusion and implication

This study employed network analysis to investigate the relationships between childhood trauma (CT) and meaning in life (MIL) in a large sample of depressed and non-depressed adolescents. Results indicated that depressed adolescents exhibit a more densely interconnected network of CT and MIL compared to their non-depressed peers. Notably, physical abuse and emotional neglect emerged as pivotal trauma subtypes, significantly disrupting the search for meaning among depressed adolescents. These findings highlight the importance of screening for trauma and the integration of meaning-making in therapeutic interventions for adolescent depression. Specifically, meaning-centered therapies and resilience-focused programs may offer substantial benefits for depressed adolescents with trauma histories. For those with a history of physical abuse, whose search for meaning is often deeply entangled with traumatic memory, interventions could focus on guiding the integration of the trauma narrative into the reconstruction of life meaning, rather than merely encouraging the pursuit of positive meaning. This represents a potentially more nuanced and effective clinical pathway within a meaning-centered framework. Early identification and interventions that foster a sense of purpose could attenuate the long-term mental health consequences of CT in this vulnerable population.
