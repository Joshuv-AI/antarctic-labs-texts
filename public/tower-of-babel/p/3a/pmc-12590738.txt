Prognostic value of controlling nutritional status score (CONUT) in patients with colorectal cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Weiwei Gu, Hong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study assesses the prognostic value of the controlling nutritional status score (CONUT) in colorectal cancer patients.MethodsA systematic search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases until December 2024. The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and relapse-free survival (RFS). Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used for data synthesis. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were used to assess the results stability and heterogeneity sources. All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 and STATA 15.1.ResultsFifteen studies involving 9,258 colorectal cancer patients were included. Meta-analysis showed higher CONUT scores were linked to shorter OS (HR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.70–2.65; P < 0.00001), DFS (HR: 1.71; 95% CI: 1.11–2.64; P = 0.02), and RFS (HR: 1.75; 95% CI: 1.39–2.20; P < 0.00001). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of CONUT’s prognostic value for OS, DFS, and RFS. Subgroup analysis identified the CONUT cut-off value as a major factor contributing to OS heterogeneity.ConclusionsCONUT appears to be associated with worse prognosis in colorectal cancer patients. However, due to potential publication bias, heterogeneity, and the retrospective study design, further large-scale, multicenter, prospective cohort studies are required to confirm its prognostic utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15097-6.Keywords: CONUT, Colorectal cancer, OS, DFS, RFS

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is among the most prevalent malignant tumors globally. According to the American Cancer Society, its incidence and mortality rates rank third worldwide [1]. According to the data of Global burden of colorectal cancer in 2020 and 2040, over 1.9 million new colorectal cancer cases and 930 000 deaths were estimated in 2020. The burden of colorectal cancer is projected to increase to 3.2 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths by 2040 with most cases predicted to occur in high or very high Human Development Index countries [2]. Surgical resection with regional lymph node dissection remains the primary treatment for colorectal cancer [3]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, adjuvant radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and the introduction of new molecular targeted therapies, recurrence rates and long-term survival in postoperative patients remain poor [4–6]. The 5- and 10-year survival rates for colorectal cancer patients after surgical resection are 65%, whereas survival drops to 10% in those with postoperative recurrence [7]. Research shows that colorectal cancer progression is influenced by factors including inflammation, immune function, and nutritional status. Both short-term and long-term nutritional status, alongside tumor cells, contribute to cancer progression [8]. Malnutrition significantly affects the prognosis of malignant tumors [9–13], highlighting the need to assess the patient’s nutritional status.

The controlling nutritional status score (CONUT), developed by Ignacio et al. [11] is a reliable tool for evaluating nutritional status, based on serum albumin, peripheral lymphocyte count, and total cholesterol, all of which are readily accessible in clinical practice [14–16]. The CONUT score, derived from serum albumin, peripheral lymphocyte count, and total cholesterol, is easily accessible in clinical practice [17]. Numerous studies have emphasized its widespread use in assessing nutritional status across various diseases, including cancer, with several exploring its correlation with cancer prognosis [18–22]. The CONUT score is an established prognostic factor for both short- and long-term survival in several cancers, including gastric, esophageal, and breast cancer [11, 23–25]. A recent cohort study by Liu et al. [26] involved 217 newly diagnosed colorectal cancer patients. After a median follow-up of 49.6 months, CONUT significantly predicted overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS) in colorectal cancer patients post-surgery. However, not all studies support this finding; some report no significant association between CONUT and long-term prognosis in these patients [27–29]. Currently, no definitive evidence supports CONUT’s ability to predict colorectal cancer prognosis.

The 2020 meta-analysis by Takagi et al. [30] including six studies, identified a significant association between high CONUT scores and poor OS and RFS in postoperative colorectal cancer patients. However, the small sample size and lack of sensitivity and subgroup analyses compromised the evidence quality. Since Takagi et al.‘s meta-analysis, numerous large cohort studies have reported inconsistent findings on the relationship between CONUT and colorectal cancer prognosis [26, 28, 29, 31–37]. This article evaluates CONUT’s prognostic value for colorectal cancer prognosis through a systematic literature meta-analysis, incorporating the latest clinical data to provide updated evidence for developing a prognostic model related to malnutrition in these patients.

Methods

Literature search

This study adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [38] and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251012639). PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane was conducted to search literature up to December 2024. Search terms included “Controlling Nutritional Status Score,” “CONUT,” and “Colorectal Neoplasms.” The PubMed search strategy was: ((“Colorectal Neoplasms“[Mesh]) OR (Colorectal Neoplasm OR Colorectal Tumors OR Colorectal Tumor OR Colorectal Cancer OR Colorectal Cancers OR Colorectal Carcinoma OR Colorectal Carcinomas)) AND (controlling nutritional status score OR CONUT). Reference lists of included studies were manually reviewed. The literature screening process was conducted independently by two authors, who resolved any discrepancies through discussion. Searching strategy details are shown in Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials (although the inclusion criteria allowed for randomized controlled trials, no eligible randomized controlled trials were found for inclusion; all included studies were observational cohort studies), cohort, or case-control designs; (2) conducted in colorectal cancer patients; (3) assessed the prognostic role of CONUT in colorectal cancer; (4) reported at least one survival outcome, such as OS, RFS, or DFS; and (5) provided sufficient data for hazard ratio (HR) analysis. Exclusion criteria: study protocols, unpublished or non-original studies (e.g., letters, comments, abstracts, corrections, and replies), studies lacking sufficient data, and reviews. To minimize bias, low-quality studies (quality assessment score < 7) were also excluded.

Data abstraction

Data abstraction was conducted independently by two authors, with discrepancies resolved by another. First author, publication year, study country, design, population, sample size, age, cut-off values, OS, DFS, and RFS were extracted (OS, DFS, and RFS follow the definitions of each original study). If data were incomplete, we would contact the corresponding authors for further information. HRs and 95% CIs were preferentially extracted directly from the results of multivariable Cox proportional hazards models within each study. Kaplan-Meier curves were not estimated when these were not directly provided by the study to ensure data quality. For studies with missing data, we contacted the corresponding author by email to attempt to obtain the original data.

Quality evaluation

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to evaluate cohort studies’ quality [39]. High quality studies scored 7–9 [40], while those with a score < 7 were excluded. Two authors independently assessed all studies, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager 5.4.1 was conducted to synthesize survival data, and meta-analysis results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cochran’s Q test (χ²) and the inconsistency index (I²) were used to assess heterogeneity [41], with high heterogeneity defined as χ² P < 0.05 or I² >50%. A random-effects model was applied to estimate the pooled HR. Sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the impact of individual studies on the overall results. Funnel plots in Review Manager 5.4.1 and Egger’s regression tests in Stata 15.1 (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA) were performed to evaluate publication bias [42] for outcomes with more than 10 studies, with P < 0.05 indicating significant bias. For outcomes with significant publication bias, we used the trimming and filling method to assess the impact of publication bias on the results. All planned subgroup and sensitivity analyses were pre-planned during the design phase.

Results

Literature retrieval, study characteristics, and baseline

The literature retrieval and selection process is depicted in Fig. 1. A comprehensive systematic search identified 293 studies across four databases: PubMed (n = 104), Embase (n = 68), Web of Science (n = 119), and Cochrane Library (n = 2). After removing duplicates, 165 titles and abstracts were screened. Because some studies reported independent patient cohorts and corresponding HRs according to different CONUT score results (low, intermediate, and high), these independent comparisons (intermediate vs. low and high vs. low) were each considered as a “comparison group” and included in the meta-analysis. Ultimately, 15 cohort studies (19 comparative groups) involving 9,258 patients were included [26–29, 32–34, 36, 37, 43–45]. The characteristics and quality assessments of the cohort studies were provided in Table 1. Patients were predominantly male (approximately 60.2%), with a median age of 60 to 70 years. Regarding tumor location, in studies providing detailed data, left-sided colon cancer and rectal cancer were roughly equally prevalent. All patients received treatment centered around radical surgical resection. CONUT score cutoffs varied across studies, ranging from 2 to 7, with 2, 3, 4, and 5 being the most common. This reflects the lack of standardized assessment criteria across different research centers.

Characteristics of included studies

OS

A meta-analysis of 14 cohort studies revealed that patients in the high CONUT group had significantly shorter OS (HR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.70–2.65; P < 0.00001). This suggests that the mortality risk of patients with high CONUT scores is approximately 2.12 times that of patients with low scores. Notable heterogeneity was detected (I² = 70%, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis based on CONUT cut-off values showed that CONUT predicted OS in both subgroups (≥ 3 and < 3). Heterogeneity reduced from 70% to 49% in the < 3 cut-off subgroup, and from 70% to 61% in the ≥ 3 cut-off subgroup (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis of OS based on CONUT cut-off

DFS

A meta-analysis of six cohort studies demonstrated significantly shorter DFS in the high CONUT group (HR: 1.71; 95% CI: 1.11–2.64; P = 0.02). This suggests that the risk of disease progression in patients with high CONUT scores is approximately 1.71 times that of patients with low scores. Heterogeneity was moderate and not statistically significant (I² = 50%, P = 0.08) (Fig. 4A).

Forest plots of (A) DFS and (B) RFS

RFS

A meta-analysis of four cohort studies revealed significantly shorter RFS in the high CONUT group (HR: 1.75; 95% CI: 1.39–2.20; P < 0.00001). This suggests that the risk of recurrence in patients with high CONUT scores is approximately 1.75 times that of patients with low scores. No significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0%, P = 0.48) (Fig. 4B).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Publication bias for the association between CONUT and OS was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests. Both the funnel plot (Fig. 5A) and Egger’s test (P = 0.0001, Fig. 5B) indicated significant bias. The impact of publication bias on OS was assessed by trimming and filling. The results showed that the significant association between CONUT and OS did not change after trimming and filling (HR: 1.77; 95% CI: 1.42–2.20), suggesting that the results were not significantly affected by publication bias (Fig. 6). Sensitivity analysis was conducted for OS, DFS, and RFS to evaluate the influence of individual cohort studies on the pooled HR. The overall HR remained stable after sequentially excluding each study for OS (Fig. 7A), DFS (Fig. 7B), and RFS (Fig. 7C). Eliminating any study did not change the direction of the combined HR or cause it to lose statistical significance, indicating that the main conclusions of this study are robust.

Discussion

Malnutrition is prevalent in patients with gastrointestinal tumors and significantly impacts cancer survival [46]. The host’s immune-inflammatory response is crucial in the development of cancer-related malnutrition [47]. Impaired immune status leads to malnutrition, which further diminishes immune and anti-tumor functions, adversely affecting treatment outcomes and prognosis, resulting in reduced survival rates in colorectal cancer patients [48, 49]. This study explored the association between the CONUT nutritional score and colorectal cancer prognosis, offering updated evidence to inform the development of a prognostic model for malnutrition in this population.

This meta-analysis of 15 cohort studies found that CONUT significantly predicts OS, DFS, and RFS in colorectal cancer patients, with higher CONUT associated with shorter survival. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of CONUT’s prognostic value for OS, DFS, and RFS, unaffected by any individual study. Significant heterogeneity was observed for both DFS and OS. Subgroup analysis based on CONUT cutoff values revealed reduced heterogeneity in studies with a cutoff < 3, suggesting that variations in cutoff values may contribute to OS heterogeneity. Future studies should investigate the optimal cutoff using a gradient approach to minimize heterogeneity. Publication bias was detected for OS, likely due to factors such as sample size differences and regional selection bias, which may limit the reliability of CONUT in predicting OS. Addressing this issue will require further international multicenter studies.

This study’s findings are consistent with Takagi et al.‘s meta-analysis [30], but it incorporates substantial new clinical data, including colorectal cancer patient outcomes (DFS), and verifies the stability of evidence and potential sources of heterogeneity through sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Consequently, it strengthens the evidence supporting CONUT’s prognostic value for colorectal cancer prognosis. This study provides the most up-to-date and comprehensive evidence for developing a more precise prognostic model. It also emphasizes that clinicians should exercise caution when considering surgery for malnourished colorectal cancer patients with elevated preoperative CONUT scores. These patients may require more extensive resection, closer follow-up, and potentially neoadjuvant therapy or intensified adjuvant chemotherapy to mitigate recurrence risk.

A recent meta-analysis by Vatvani et al. [50] evaluated the value of the CONUT score as a predictor of survival and complications in patients with colorectal cancer. While the previous meta-analysis by Vatvani et al. also explored the prognostic value of the CONUT score for colorectal cancer, our study provides a unique perspective and complementary evidence, further strengthening our understanding of this field. First, we adopted a more focused approach. Although the previous study included a larger number of studies (n = 22), it included univariate analysis data, potentially making its results more susceptible to confounding. This study, through rigorous screening, focused on analyzing multivariate-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) from 15 high-quality studies, ensuring the independence of the prognostic value of the CONUT score and providing more robust effect size estimates. Second, this study further explored the sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses clearly identified and confirmed that differences in CONUT score cutoffs were the primary factor contributing to the heterogeneity in OS results (I² values ​​decreased from 70% to 49–61%), a key finding not quantitatively emphasized in previous studies. This conclusion has important implications for promoting the standardized application of the CONUT score. Third, we conducted more comprehensive validation. We quantitatively assessed and corrected for publication bias using the Egger’s test and trim-and-fill method, verifying the robustness of the combined results. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the OS, DFS, and RFS results were independent of any single study, significantly strengthening the reliability of the conclusions.

The nutritional status and prognosis (both short- and long-term) of colorectal cancer patients are frequently assessed using the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), each of which is closely linked to cancer prognosis [51–53]. CONUT assesses nutritional and immune status using three indicators: serum albumin, total lymphocyte count, and total cholesterol. Albumin, the key marker of nutritional status, reflects protein reserves, maintains capillary osmotic pressure, and indicates protein synthesis. It is also affected by inflammation and medications [54]. Hypoproteinemia signifies malnutrition, hypermetabolism, and systemic inflammation, often triggering hypercytokinemia and weakening immune defenses against cancer cells [55]. Serum albumin correlates with colorectal cancer prognosis, with hypoproteinemia contributing to adverse outcomes [56]. Lymphocyte count, a critical determinant of the CONUT score, precisely reflects immune status [57]. T lymphocytes orchestrate anti-cancer immunity by initiating cytotoxic responses and inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, ultimately inducing apoptosis [58]. The immune response to cancer cells hinges on lymphocytes, and lymphocytopenia can drive tumor progression. A diminished lymphocyte count compromises immune defense, exacerbating prognosis. Studies associate tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte levels, including CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, with cancer prognosis [59]. Lymphocytopenia in advanced cancer patients arises from tumor immune evasion mechanisms, including lymphocyte destruction by tumor cells expressing pro-apoptotic ligands [60]. Furthermore, IL-6 lowers total cholesterol levels [61], making the CONUT score in cancer patients a marker not only of nutritional status but also of immunosuppression and the effect of proinflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate lymphocytopenia by enhancing protein catabolism during cachexia [62].

The CONUT score includes serum cholesterol measurement, with low cholesterol levels associated with poor clinical outcomes in cancer patients. In cancer tissues, elevated mRNA expression of the low-density lipoprotein cholesterol receptor increases cholesterol uptake, reducing serum cholesterol and stimulating tumor growth [63]. Serum cholesterol correlates with tumor progression and patient survival in various cancers, including colorectal cancer [64]. Thus, all three components of the CONUT score reflect cancer progression and serve as comprehensive indicators of nutritional status, systemic inflammation, and immune response.

This study demonstrates that the CONUT score reflects the long-term prognosis of colorectal cancer patients and informs surgical strategies. It is a reliable, straightforward, and effective tool for prognosis assessment, offering considerable clinical benefits. Nutritional intervention for preoperative malnutrition reduces postoperative complications, shortens hospital stays, and lowers healthcare costs [65–67]. Our results support nutritional intervention for the high CONUT group. The CONUT score directs intervention initiation and evaluates its effectiveness. A CONUT-based nomogram can identify high-risk groups early, facilitating prompt immunonutritional intervention, early detection and treatment of malnutrition, and enhancing prognosis while preventing postoperative complications in colorectal cancer patients. In clinical practice, patients with high CONUT scores should be informed of their heightened risk of complications. Physicians should prioritize improving preoperative nutritional status and ensure meticulous management.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, it predominantly comprises retrospective cohort studies, with only one prospective study. Retrospective studies are prone to confounding factors and biases. Furthermore, most studies are from Asia (primarily China, Japan, and South Korea), with limited data from Europe, the Americas, and Africa, restricting the generalizability of the findings. The high heterogeneity observed in this study (I² = 70%) is a core limitation that requires careful consideration when interpreting the results. This is due to multiple factors: First, all included studies were observational in design, with only one prospective cohort and the rest retrospective studies, which inevitably introduces selection and confounding bias. Second, there was significant clinical heterogeneity in population characteristics, including geographic distribution (the vast majority of studies were from Asia, particularly Japan and China, with a lack of data from European and American populations), ethnicity, and a diverse age structure (ranging from all age groups to studies specifically targeting the elderly). Another limitation of this study is that only a few studies reported cancer-specific survival (CSS), thus preventing a pooled analysis of this important outcome measure. Future studies should prioritize reporting CSS to more purely reflect the association between CONUT score and oncological outcomes. Third, the disease itself is highly heterogeneous, with included studies encompassing a wide range of patient populations, from stage I to stage IV and from non-metastatic to liver metastatic, with distinct risk of disease progression and prognostic profiles. Fourth, and most critically, the definition of CONUT score cutoffs varied significantly across studies (ranging from 2 to 7). Most studies used statistical medians or receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to determine cutoffs, rather than standardized criteria based on clinical significance. This directly led to inconsistent patient classification and risk stratification. Finally, although the treatment strategy primarily focused on radical surgery, the lack of detailed information regarding the use of adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy (for rectal cancer) may have influenced the final prognosis. Furthermore, Egger’s test revealed significant publication bias (P = 0.0001), suggesting that small studies with negative or neutral results may have been under published, leading to an overestimation of our combined effect size. This bias may be related to the Asian tradition of favoring the publication of positive results and the lack of large-scale, prospective registries in this field.

To address these limitations, future research should focus on the following areas to provide higher-level evidence: First, prioritizing large-scale, multicenter, prospective cohort studies with predefined CONUT measurement time points and statistical analysis plans during the study design phase to minimize confounding bias. Second, large-scale studies are urgently needed to explore and validate an optimal cutoff value for the CONUT score based on clinical outcomes (such as mortality and recurrence) to facilitate comparison and integration of results across studies. Third, to address current heterogeneity and further explore moderating factors, the ultimate future direction is to conduct meta-analysis of individual patient data (IPD). IPD meta-analysis allows us to re-stratify and analyze the original data in a unified manner, thereby more precisely answering the following questions: Does the prognostic value of CONUT differ among patient subgroups with different races, TNM stages, and treatment modalities? Is its prognostic power modulated by age or tumor location? By answering these questions, our ultimate goal is to advance the CONUT score from a broad prognostic indicator to a precise tool capable of guiding personalized clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

CONUT is an easily accessible, cost-effective, and noninvasive nutritional marker with potential prognostic value for OS, RFS, and DFS in patients with colorectal cancer. Due to potential publication bias, heterogeneity, and the retrospective design, additional large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies are required to further validate the prognostic value of CONUT in colorectal cancer patients.
