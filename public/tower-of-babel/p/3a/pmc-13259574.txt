International flight–associated splenic infarction in a 33-year-old man with sickle cell trait
Authors: Auriane Chadelat, Marc Tassart, Claude Bachmeyer
Published: 2026

A 33-year-old Black Malian man with no notable personal or family medical history had sudden and intense abdominal pain during an international flight and presented to an emergency department 24 hours later with ongoing pain. His vital signs were normal. The pain was isolated to the left upper quadrant and increased on palpation. His laboratory investigations indicated a hemoglobin level of 129 (normal > 130) g/L, leukocyte cell count of 15.4 × 109/L (neutrophils 13.2 [normal < 7] × 109/L and lymphocytes 0.93 [normal > 1.5] × 109/L), platelet count of 94 (normal > 150) × 109/L, unconjugated bilirubin level of 24 (normal < 5) μmol/L, lactate dehydrogenase level of 378 (normal < 150) IU/L, and C-reactive protein level of 112 (normal < 5) mg/L. Thoracoabdominal computed tomography (CT) appeared normal without contrast but revealed splenic infarction after administration of contrast (Figure 1). Thrombophilia testing and cardioembolic workup were unremarkable. For 5 days, the patient was treated with subcutaneous enoxaparin, but we discontinued it when we received the results of hemoglobin electrophoresis, which showed hemoglobin A1 48% (normal 95% to 98%), hemoglobin A2 3.3% (normal 2% to 3%), and hemoglobin S 39.2% (normal 0%), indicating the presence of sickle cell trait. We administered intravenous analgesics and fluids, and the patient was discharged from hospital 7 days after presentation. One month after hospital discharge, he was free of pain, routine laboratory tests were normal, and CT findings were unchanged. His mother and sister were also found to also have sickle cell trait.

(A) Coronal and (B) axial views from thoracoabdominal computed tomography of a 33-year-old man after intravenous iodinated contrast administration at portal time, displaying a circumferential subcapsular defect of splenic enhancement (arrows), related to isolated splenic infarction.

Patients with sickle cell disease, an autosomal recessive disease responsible for complications such as vaso-occlusive crises and acute chest syndrome, inherit 2 copies of the hemoglobin β gene with sickle cell mutation, 1 from each parent.1 Patients with sickle cell trait inherit only 1 sickle cell gene, usually remain asymptomatic, and can be unaware of their status.2 Many countries, including Canada, now include sickle cell disease and trait in newborn screening programs. Although symptoms or complications from sickle cell trait are rare, papillary necrosis, exertional rhabdomyolysis, and splenic infarction can develop. Splenic infarction has been reported in 85 cases between 1970 and 2020, mainly in women with hemoglobin S percentages higher than 35%; it can develop because of hypoxia and dehydration in altitudes beyond 3000 m and airplane flights.3 Treatment is supportive, with intravenous fluids to maintain adequate hydration and analgesia.2 Anticoagulants are not indicated, since this complication is considered a vaso-occlusive event.
