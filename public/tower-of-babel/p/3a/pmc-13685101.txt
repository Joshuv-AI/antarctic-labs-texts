Scapular dyskinesis with preserved structure and function in largely asymptomatic junior elite tennis players: a 2D video and multimodal cross-sectional study
Authors: Jaemin Lee, Seung Gyu Yang, Doo Sup Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScapular dyskinesis is prevalent in overhead athletes, yet whether it reflects early pathology or physiological adaptation in largely asymptomatic adolescent tennis players remains unclear. In an unselected cohort of junior elite tennis players, we first established scapular dyskinesis prevalence and then examined its relationships with clinical scores, ultrasonographic integrity, shoulder-line tilt, and age. We tested whether shoulder-line tilt displacement during arm elevation differs from zero (the shoulder line moving from a resting obliquity toward level), whether it relates to clinical scores and age, and whether dyskinesis coexists with normal ultrasonographic structure and preserved function rather than structural or functional impairment.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 20 male junior elite tennis players (mean age 16.0 ± 1.7 years) who underwent bilateral range-of-motion assessment, ultrasonography, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) and Constant-Murley (CMS) questionnaires, and posterior two-dimensional video analysis. Two orthopedic surgeons classified scapular dyskinesis by consensus using the Kibler system; shoulder-line tilt angles were quantified by markerless pose estimation. We compared dominant with non-dominant range of motion and related tilt displacement to clinical scores and age.ResultsScapular dyskinesis was present in 65%, predominantly Type I; all 20 athletes had normal rotator cuff integrity on ultrasonography. The dyskinesis subgroup had mean ASES and CMS scores of 99.2 and 98.6, and the groups did not differ in clinical or range-of-motion variables; CMS scores were equivalent, though ASES equivalence was inconclusive given a ceiling effect. Shoulder-line tilt decreased from a resting obliquity toward level at peak elevation, and age correlated strongly and negatively with this tilt displacement.ConclusionsIn this single-program, male-only cohort of largely asymptomatic adolescent elite tennis players, scapular dyskinesis with normal ultrasonography and preserved function is compatible with a physiological adaptation, suggesting an adaptive window; the findings are not generalizable to female athletes, adults, or other sports. Because dyskinesis is linked to future injury risk, long-term follow-up is warranted, interpreted alongside structural and functional status.Keywords: Scapular dyskinesis, Tennis, Adolescent athletes, Physiological adaptation, 2D video analysis, Kibler classification, Shoulder-line tilt, Ultrasonography

Background

Scapular dyskinesis — altered scapular position or motion during scapulohumeral movement — is best understood not as a pathology in itself but as an impairment of scapular mechanics that may become clinically relevant under functional demands; the 2013 Scapular Summit consensus found no evidence that dyskinesis is a pathological process or an inevitable precursor to injury [1], and subsequent work has emphasized its role as an adaptation to loading [2, 3]. It is nonetheless observed alongside symptomatic shoulder conditions, including impingement, rotator cuff dysfunction, and labral lesions [1, 2]. In overhead athletes, repetitive sport-specific loading drives musculoskeletal adaptation in the dominant shoulder. Tennis is especially demanding: repeated serving and groundstrokes impose asymmetric loading from an early age [4] and produce altered range-of-motion (ROM) patterns such as glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD), increased external rotation (ER), and shifts in scapular positioning [5, 6]. In professional tennis, scapular dyskinesis has been reported on the dominant side in 57.7% of players [7]; broader overhead-athlete samples show comparable figures, including 28.7% in basketball [8], a pooled estimate of 61% [9], and rates as high as 68% in healthy college-aged participants [10], and a meta-analysis linked dyskinesis to a 43% increased risk of future shoulder pain [11]. Yet dyskinesis does not invariably indicate pathology. Several authors regard altered scapular positioning in overhead athletes as a sport-specific adaptation to repetitive loading rather than an inherently pathological change [3, 12–15]; differences in scapular muscle activity in symptomatic shoulders [16] underscore that dyskinesis should be interpreted within its clinical context rather than equated with injury. A systematic review of 34 studies (2,365 participants) found scapular dyskinesis in 48% of asymptomatic individuals and 42% of asymptomatic athletes, raising whether the sign represents a normal variant rather than a uniform marker of pathology [17]. The Kibler classification (Type I–IV) remains the most widely used method for visual assessment [1]. Three-dimensional motion analysis is most precise, but its cost and limited accessibility restrict clinical use [18]; two-dimensional (2D) video analysis has emerged as a scalable alternative for quantifying aspects of scapular position [19, 20]. A shoulder-line tilt angle derived from pose estimation indexes shoulder-girdle leveling relative to gravitational horizontal rather than direct scapulothoracic rotation.

Most evidence, however, derives from adult professional athletes or mixed-sport populations. Xie recently identified scapular dyskinesis as a significant risk factor for shoulder injury in adolescent tennis players [21], yet data on its clinical significance in largely asymptomatic adolescent elite players remain scarce, and few studies have simultaneously evaluated scapular dyskinesis, ultrasonographic integrity, and validated clinical outcomes in the same adolescent cohort. A fundamental question therefore persists: in this population, does dyskinesis coexist with intact structure and preserved function — a pattern compatible with an adaptive window — and how does it relate to clinical and age-related factors? Although a cross-sectional design cannot differentiate adaptation from pathology, simultaneous multimodal characterization can establish whether the conditions for an adaptive interpretation are met.

Using 2D video analysis, this study examined the relationship between shoulder-line tilt, scapular dyskinesis, and clinical and ultrasonographic parameters in junior elite tennis players. We tested four hypotheses: (1) shoulder-line tilt displacement from rest to peak elevation differs significantly from zero; (2) shoulder-line tilt displacement correlates with clinical outcome scores (the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons [ASES] Score and the Constant-Murley Score [CMS]); (3) in athletes with dyskinesis, ultrasonographic structure is intact and clinical function is preserved relative to non-dyskinetic athletes (a pattern compatible with a sport-specific adaptive window rather than structural or functional impairment); and (4) with increasing age, a proxy for cumulative training exposure, internal rotation (IR) decreases and the magnitude of shoulder-line tilt displacement increases within normal ranges.

Methods

Study design

The Institutional Review Board of Wonju Severance Christian Hospital (IRB No. CR326028; approved 22 April 2026) approved this retrospective cross-sectional study, conducted per the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments; because the analysis used pre-existing clinical data, the board waived informed consent. Reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. We extracted and analyzed data over the one-month period following approval (April–May 2026).

Participants

All male elite tennis players aged 14–19 years who visited our Department of Orthopedic Surgery and completed all of the following were enrolled: bilateral shoulder ROM measurement, bilateral shoulder ultrasonography, posterior 2D video recording during forward flexion (FF) and scaption (SC; arm elevation in the scapular plane, approximately 30°–40° anterior to the coronal plane), and clinical outcome questionnaires (ASES and CMS). Athletes with acute trauma (fracture, dislocation) precluding normal ROM measurement were excluded. Of the 22 athletes evaluated, 2 were excluded for incomplete multimodal data and none under the acute-trauma criterion, leaving 20 (complete enumeration; census) drawn entirely from a single regional elite training program at Yanggu Middle and High School (Gangwon Province, Republic of Korea); the resulting exclusively male, single-program sample limits generalizability. Of the 20, 9 attended the high school and 11 the affiliated middle school. All were registered with the Korea Tennis Association as competitive junior athletes, and 19 of 20 held a national junior ranking (U14, n = 8; U16, n = 6; U18, n = 5). Athletes trained a median of 30 h per week (range, 24–30) over 6–7 days. They had collectively won team-event titles at national-level junior tournaments; 11 of 20 (55%) had competed in International Tennis Federation (ITF) or Asian Tennis Federation (ATF) international junior tournaments (J30/J60 or C1/C2 grade), and 10 of 20 (50%) achieved a top-four finish in singles or doubles. Subjects presented for routine pre-season medical screening, not symptomatic complaints. We defined the dominant arm as the tennis racket arm, classifying the one ambidextrous player as right-dominant. Five subjects had a documented injury history: 1 with shoulder impingement and a superior labral anterior-posterior (SLAP) Type I lesion, 1 with left elbow osteochondritis dissecans (postsurgical), 2 with resolved rotator cuff inflammation, and 1 with a left clavicle fracture. We retained all 5 in the primary analysis and ran a sensitivity analysis excluding them.

Clinical assessment

Range of motion

Bilateral shoulder ROM — FF, abduction, ER and IR in neutral (side ER, side IR), ER and IR at 90° abduction (Abd ER, Abd IR), and IR level — was quantified with a two-dimensional digital video markerless motion-tracking system in routine clinical use at our institution, with each measurement independently re-confirmed by two orthopedic residents. We defined total ROM as the sum of side ER and side IR, and GIRD as non-dominant IR minus dominant IR. A side-to-side internal-rotation difference exceeding 18°–20° conventionally defines classic GIRD in adult overhead athletes [22]; in this young, structurally intact cohort, we adopted a more sensitive 10° threshold to capture subclinical asymmetry, defining classic GIRD when dominant internal rotation was reduced and reverse GIRD when non-dominant internal rotation was reduced. We report the continuous mean deficit alongside these dichotomized rates.

Clinical outcome measures

Both patient-reported outcome scores were recorded for the dominant (racket) shoulder. The ASES Standardized Shoulder Assessment Form comprises a visual analog scale (VAS) pain component and 10 function items (each 0–3), totaling 0–100. The CMS spans 4 domains — Pain (15 points), Activities of Daily Living (ADL; 20 points), ROM (40 points), and Power (25 points) — for a total of 0–100.

Ultrasonography

Bilateral shoulder ultrasonography was performed by an orthopedic surgeon with shoulder subspecialty training and more than 5 years of musculoskeletal ultrasonography experience, using a GE LOGIQ P9 system (GE HealthCare) with a 6–15 MHz linear transducer. The biceps tendon, rotator cuff, and glenoid labrum were assessed in standardized positions: biceps tendon and subscapularis in elbow flexion, supraspinatus in the Modified Crass position (hand on the buttock or hip with the elbow flexed), and infraspinatus in cross-body adduction. Findings were classified as normal or abnormal based on tendinopathy, partial- or full-thickness tears, or other structural pathology.

Video-based shoulder-line tilt and dyskinesis assessment

Scapular dyskinesis classification

We assessed scapular dyskinesis from the posterior 2D videos during FF and SC. Two board-certified orthopedic surgeons (J.L. and D.S.K.) independently assigned a Kibler type to each shoulder [1] (Type I, inferior angle prominence; Type II, medial border prominence; Type III, superior border prominence; Type IV, normal/symmetric); discordant cases were discussed until a single consensus classification was reached, and when features of more than one pattern coexisted the shoulder was assigned the single predominant prominence by consensus. We evaluated each side independently and classified a subject as having dyskinesis if at least 1 side showed Type I, II, or III. The 2-surgeon consensus design follows precedent for expert visual scapular-dyskinesis assessment in sport [8, 23–25]. To assess reproducibility, a third orthopedic surgeon (S.G.Y., a co-author not involved in the original consensus) — blinded to identifiers, side dominance, and the original classification — independently re-classified all 40 shoulders from the archived videos, all of which matched the consensus (40/40, 100% concordance). Because this re-rating was scored against the fixed consensus rather than by independent paired raters, it reflects confirmatory concordance and does not estimate chance-corrected inter-rater reliability.

Shoulder-line tilt angle measurement

A single posterior camera (Sony HDR-CX450; 1440 × 1080, ~ 30 frames/s) recorded each athlete at a fixed 480 cm distance and 130 cm height. We measured shoulder-line tilt angles with Google MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (version 0.10.33, Heavy model; minimum detection confidence 0.7), which detected the left and right shoulder landmarks (model landmark indices 11 and 12) — estimated body keypoints approximating the lateral shoulder, not palpated acromial or scapular bony landmarks. MediaPipe-derived shoulder landmarks have been validated against universal-goniometer reference angles for shoulder elevation [26], although that validation addressed unilateral elevation angle rather than the inter-shoulder asymmetry used here. We defined the shoulder-line tilt angle as the angle, relative to horizontal, of the line connecting the 2 shoulder landmarks, extracted at rest (arms at sides) and at peak arm elevation; the measurement procedure (landmark detection, the tilt angle relative to gravitational horizontal, and the rest and peak-elevation frames) is illustrated in Fig. 1. The resting baseline was the mean tilt across the first 10 frames with successful pose detection; the peak frame was that of maximum arm elevation (highest mean wrist position). Frames with failed detection were excluded rather than interpolated. Detection succeeded for 98.4% of frames overall (per-video median failure rate, 0%; range, 0%–15.1%), and 3 of 40 videos exceeded a 10% failure rate. Three parameters were computed: tilt at rest, tilt at peak, and tilt displacement (peak minus rest), the last representing the within-movement leveling of the shoulder line from rest toward horizontal — a within-shoulder dynamic change distinct from any left–right (side-to-side) asymmetry. We analyzed both FF and SC, measuring tilt in an anatomical reference frame in which a positive value indicates the left shoulder is higher than the right. Because the dominant (racket) arm was the right in 18 of 20 athletes (the 2 left-dominant players excepted), a positive resting tilt corresponded to a lower dominant shoulder at rest in most of the cohort. Shoulder-line tilt is a surrogate index of inter-acromial levelness, influenced by trunk lean and symmetric shoulder-girdle elevation, and does not isolate scapulothoracic motion.

Statistical analysis

Primary analyses used Python 3.12 with SciPy 1.17.1; the supplementary power, equivalence, and false-discovery-rate analyses used Python 3.11.9 with SciPy 1.17.1 and NumPy 2.4.4. We set α = 0.05 and applied Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with range and 95% confidence interval (CI), categorical variables as frequency and percentage, and normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Dominant versus non-dominant ROM was compared with paired t tests when normally distributed and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests when not, with effect sizes reported as Cohen d and rank-biserial correlation (r), respectively. Given the non-normal clinical-score distribution (ceiling effect) and the ordinal nature of certain variables, all correlation analyses used Spearman rank coefficients. The two confirmatory correlation families — shoulder-line tilt displacement versus clinical scores (Hypothesis 2) and the age-correlation family (Hypothesis 4) — were Bonferroni-corrected, and prespecified exploratory analyses (below) reported uncorrected. We compared continuous variables between the dyskinesis group (≥ 1 side Type I–III) and the normal group (bilateral Type IV) using Mann-Whitney U tests with rank-biserial r, and compared dyskinesis prevalence between sides with the exact McNemar test. Two sensitivity analyses were run: one excluding the 5 subjects with injury history, and one recomputing the primary tilt findings after excluding the 3 videos that exceeded a 10% pose-detection failure rate. One-sample t tests assessed whether tilt displacement differed from zero, and a paired t test compared FF versus SC tilt displacement. Several analyses were exploratory (hypothesis-generating) and excluded from the confirmatory Bonferroni families: CMS Power versus tilt displacement, the dyskinesis-versus-normal group comparisons (Table 6), and the dichotomized age-group comparison (14–15 vs. 16–19 years). We treated the age-correlation family (Tables 6 and 7 comparisons) as confirmatory and Bonferroni-corrected; within it, significant correlations remained robust to Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction, whereas correlations significant only before correction are reported as exploratory. Because the census design precluded an a priori sample-size calculation, we report the minimum detectable effect at 80% power for the achieved sample size (one-sample Cohen d ≈ 0.66; Spearman ρ ≈ 0.59; α = 0.05) as a design-level benchmark, interpreting underpowered comparisons as hypothesis-generating using effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals. To operationalize the Hypothesis 3 claim of preserved function, between-group equivalence for ASES and CMS was tested with the Two One-Sided Tests procedure, using margins informed by the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) literature: ±10 points for CMS [27] and, given the pronounced ASES ceiling effect, ± 6.4 points for ASES (the reported MCID) [28], with ± 10 points as a wider sensitivity margin. The ASES equivalence conclusion is sensitive to the exact margin at the MCID width.

Scapular dyskinesis: Kibler classification

By 2-surgeon consensus, scapular dyskinesis (Kibler Type I–III) appeared in 13 of 20 subjects (65.0%) on at least one side. Type I was the most common dyskinetic pattern, and Type III was not observed (Table 2; Fig. 2). Bilateral symmetry was present in 17 of 20 subjects (85.0%): 10 showed the same dyskinetic pattern on both sides and 7 were bilaterally normal (Type IV). The remaining 3 had a mixed pattern, dyskinesis on one side and normal on the other. Dyskinesis prevalence did not differ between the dominant and non-dominant sides (McNemar exact test, P = 1.000).

GIRD analysis

Mean GIRD was − 5.6° ± 13.2° (95% CI, − 11.8 to 0.5). Classic GIRD greater than 10° occurred in 2 of 20 athletes (10.0%) and reverse GIRD greater than 10° in 7 of 20 (35.0%).

Hypothesis 1: dynamic shoulder-line tilt displacement during arm elevation

Shoulder-line tilt decreased from a resting obliquity of approximately 4° toward a near-level line at peak elevation during both forward flexion and scaption, with tilt displacement differing significantly from zero in each (both P < 0.001; Table 4; Fig. 3). The two movements did not differ significantly (paired t, P = 0.392, Cohen d = 0.20).

Hypothesis 4: age-related changes

Age correlated significantly and negatively with tilt displacement during both forward flexion and scaption (Table 7; Fig. 5). Non-dominant side internal rotation also decreased with age, though this exploratory association did not survive correction (Table 7). An exploratory age-group comparison showed lower non-dominant internal rotation in the older group (16–19 years, n = 11; 52.6° ± 9.9°) than in the younger group (14–15 years, n = 9; 64.8° ± 13.6°; Mann-Whitney P = 0.023, rank-biserial r = 0.62).

Sensitivity analysis

Excluding the 5 subjects with injury history left 15. Dyskinesis prevalence remained 66.7% (10/15), all with normal ultrasonography; ASES was 100.0 ± 0.0 and CMS 98.5 ± 1.1, and the dyskinesis subgroup (n = 10) showed the same perfect ASES with CMS 98.7 ± 1.2. The dominant-versus-non-dominant internal rotation difference retained its direction (65.1° ± 10.2° vs. 60.5° ± 10.6°; P = 0.153; d = 0.390). Excluding instead the 3 videos with a pose-detection failure rate above 10% (1 forward-flexion and 2 scaption videos) left the primary tilt findings materially unchanged: forward-flexion tilt displacement still differed from zero (n = 19; −4.1° ± 3.8°; P < 0.001; d = − 1.09), scaption tilt displacement remained significant (n = 18; −4.2° ± 4.8°; P = 0.002; d = − 0.87), and both age correlations were retained (forward flexion, ρ = −0.63, P = 0.004; scaption, ρ = −0.70, P = 0.001).

Discussion

This study examined shoulder-line tilt displacement and scapular dyskinesis and their relationship with clinical and ultrasonographic parameters in 20 male junior elite tennis players aged 14–19 years. Five principal findings emerged. First, expert consensus identified scapular dyskinesis in 65% of subjects, most commonly Type I. Second, 2D video analysis revealed a significant change in shoulder-line tilt from rest to peak elevation during both forward flexion and scaption, the resting obliquity diminishing toward a level line; because bilateral arm elevation tends to level the shoulder line, this within-movement change is partly an expected geometric consequence rather than a lateralized asymmetry, and the two movements produced concordant tilt behavior (paired t, P = 0.392). Third, tilt displacement did not correlate with clinical scores, but given the pronounced ceiling effect this null is hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of no relationship. Fourth, the dyskinesis and normal groups did not differ in the measured clinical scores or selected range-of-motion variables, and ultrasonography was normal in both. Fifth, age correlated strongly and negatively with tilt displacement during both movements.

Our 65% subject-level dyskinesis prevalence approaches the 61% pooled estimate of Burn et al. [9], although cross-study comparison is constrained by differing prevalence denominators and classification systems. Reported figures span shoulder-level rates of 28.7% in professional basketball [8], subject-level rates of 46% in elite swimmers [29] and 57.7% on the dominant side in professional tennis [7], and 68% in healthy college-aged participants [10]. Comparable subject-level rates occur even in clinical and other overhead populations — 65.7% after rotator cuff repair [30], with dyskinesis likewise documented in asymptomatic overhead athletes [31] — underscoring that a 65% rate need not by itself signify pathology. Few studies have reported prevalence in asymptomatic adolescent elite tennis players. Lee et al. reported scapular dyskinesis in 70.9% of shoulders in male elite tennis players (79.1% dominant, 62.8% non-dominant), with higher grades accompanying greater dominant-side internal-rotation loss; our 65% is broadly concordant, though comparison is constrained by differing classification systems (Kibler Type I–IV consensus versus a three-grade visual test) and denominators [32].

Among dyskinetic patterns, Type I predominated (32.5% of sides), followed by Type II (25.0%) — a sport-specific contrast, since Type II was most prevalent in basketball (54.8%) [8]. The absence of Type III may be sample-specific in this small cohort but accords with tennis biomechanics, in which overhead serving generates primarily anterior tilt and internal rotation rather than superior migration [4]. Chen et al. [33] linked Type I dyskinesis in overhead athletes to altered supraspinatus musculotendinous architecture, with greater scapular upward rotation predicting supraspinatus fiber-bundle lengthening, and Deniz et al. [34] offered a complementary mechanism: young tennis players with dyskinesis showed lower ascending trapezius activation and altered viscoelastic muscle properties.

Bilateral symmetry was high (85%; 17 of 20 athletes showed the same Kibler type on both sides), and dyskinesis prevalence did not differ between sides (McNemar exact test, P = 1.000), so this cohort showed no substantial lateralized side-to-side asymmetry. López-Vidriero Tejedor et al. [7] observed a similar bilateral pattern in professional tennis players (dominant, 57.7%; non-dominant, 45.9%; not significant), attributing it partly to 2-handed backhands. This bilateral pattern is compatible with load-related changes that are not confined to the racket arm rather than with a unilateral pathological process; it is distinct from the within-shoulder dynamic tilt displacement examined above, and our cross-sectional design cannot isolate the contributing mechanism.

Four observations are consistent with interpreting scapular dyskinesis in this population as a load-related adaptation accompanied by preserved structure and function at this age. First, the high prevalence (65%) in a largely asymptomatic cohort argues against a purely pathological interpretation and is consistent across overhead sports [8–10, 17, 29, 35]. Second, all 20 subjects — including all 13 with dyskinesis — had no rotator cuff structural abnormality on screening ultrasonography, dissociating the finding from the structural shoulder conditions with which it is sometimes observed [1–3, 36]. This contrasts with senior tennis players, in whom Vervaecke et al. [37] found structural rotator cuff abnormalities in 78.8% despite ≈ 75% reporting no current pain, indicating that our adolescent cohort is structurally intact. Our uniformly normal findings accord with prior high-resolution ultrasonography in non-elite junior tennis players, in whom only 15% showed abnormalities — all minor and not significantly different from age- and sex-matched controls (P = 0.71) — supporting a low structural-pathology burden in adolescent tennis shoulders [38]. Third, clinical scores in the dyskinesis group were near-perfect with essentially no pain, paralleling Paksoy et al. [8], whose statistically significant between-group Subjective Shoulder Value difference nonetheless fell below the MCID. Fourth, in a single uncorrected exploratory analysis, greater CMS Power was associated with a larger-magnitude (more negative) scaption tilt displacement (Spearman ρ = −0.597), an association of uncertain direction that did not survive correction; causality cannot be inferred. Because ASES and CMS exhibited a pronounced ceiling effect (90% and 30% of subjects at the maximum), the absent group difference and equivalence findings — ASES equivalence being inconclusive at the MCID-width margin — should be read as compatible with, rather than proof of, preserved function. These observations align with frameworks that interpret altered scapular motion in overhead athletes as a response to sport-specific loading rather than an inherently pathological disorder, and that caution against equating dyskinesis with pathology [3, 12, 13, 39].

Dyskinesis is not without potential consequence, however. Xie [21] reported a hazard ratio of 2.76 for future shoulder injury in adolescent tennis players with dyskinesis, and 18.3% of initially normal players developed new-onset dyskinesis over 24 months; specific subtypes have also been associated with altered glenohumeral kinematics during throwing in other overhead athletes [40]. Because dyskinesis carries a reported association with future injury risk, only prospective follow-up can establish the longer-term significance of these load-related findings.

The reverse GIRD pattern was unexpected: a mean GIRD of − 5.6° indicates the dominant side maintained greater internal rotation than the non-dominant side, contrasting with the classic GIRD attributed to posterior capsular contracture in adult overhead athletes [5]. Vervaecke et al. [37] confirmed reduced dominant-arm IR in senior tennis players, and Intelangelo et al. [41] reported greater non-dominant IR in amateur overhead athletes, both consistent with classic GIRD. A likely explanation is timing: at ages 14–19 the musculoskeletal system may be in early-phase remodeling, with increased external rotation acquired but posterior capsular tightening not yet developed [42, 43]. Consistent with this, dominant internal rotation did not decline with age; the exploratory finding of lower non-dominant internal rotation in older athletes would, if anything, accentuate the reverse pattern rather than signal a shift toward the classic adult deficit, which was not evident in this cross-sectional sample. As Vervaecke et al. [37] noted, GIRD is not inherently pathological when total rotation remains symmetrical. A meta-analysis of 2,195 overhead athletes likewise found that no range-of-motion metric, including glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (mean difference, 3.11°; 95% CI, − 0.13° to 6.36°; P = 0.06), reached a statistically significant association with upper-extremity injury [44].

Shoulder-line tilt displacement was larger in older athletes. While age is a risk factor for shoulder injury in adolescent tennis players (Xie [21]: hazard ratio, 1.42), the present cross-sectional association concerns a shoulder-line tilt measure and should not be read as evidence of injury risk. Age proxies cumulative training exposure (career data were unavailable) but also biological maturation and skeletal growth, which can themselves alter a shoulder-line angle, so this cross-sectional association cannot distinguish training load from maturation.

Two clinical implications follow. First, scapular dyskinesis in adolescent tennis players should not automatically trigger concern or treatment when ultrasonographic findings are normal and clinical scores preserved [12, 13]; Intelangelo et al. [41] showed that rotator cuff strength imbalance, rather than dyskinesis alone, was the most prevalent risk factor for overuse shoulder injuries, so evaluation should extend beyond visual scapular assessment. Second, the cross-sectional age association warrants longitudinal monitoring, given Xie’s [21] finding that dyskinesis increases injury risk over time. The 2D video approach offers a practical, low-cost tool for team-level screening, consistent with growing interest in accessible, non-three-dimensional measurement methods [19, 26, 45].

Limitations

The census design precluded an a priori power calculation; for this sample the minimum detectable effect at 80% power was a one-sample Cohen d of ≈ 0.66 and a Spearman ρ of ≈ 0.59 (α = 0.05). These benchmarks apply to the one-sample tilt and Spearman analyses; the paired dominant-versus-non-dominant ROM contrasts and the small dyskinesis-versus-normal comparison are likewise small-sample and interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The cross-sectional design prevented causal inference, and pronounced ceiling effects constrained the correlation and equivalence analyses. We lacked tennis career duration (using age as a proxy, which also reflects maturation and growth) and body measurements (height, weight, body mass index). Our 2D approach measured shoulder-line tilt — a surrogate index of inter-acromial levelness influenced by trunk lean and symmetric shoulder-girdle elevation — rather than true scapulothoracic kinematics; the test–retest reliability and accuracy of our specific pose-estimation pipeline were not formally established in this retrospective dataset, so absolute tilt magnitudes should be read as a surrogate index rather than against a validated clinically meaningful threshold; the resting baseline used the opening valid frames rather than a standardized posture, and peak tilt was read from a single frame. Bilateral ROM was obtained with a two-dimensional digital video markerless motion-tracking system in routine clinical use at our institution and independently re-confirmed by two orthopedic residents; although this provides an objective, instrument-based measurement, its independent test–retest reliability was not formally established in this retrospective dataset, so the smaller side-to-side differences — none of which survived Bonferroni correction — warrant cautious interpretation and may lie within measurement error. The third surgeon’s blinded re-rating assessed agreement with the original consensus rather than independent dual-rater reliability, so it reflects confirmatory concordance rather than chance-corrected inter-rater reliability. Because all 20 athletes were male from a single regional program, the findings are not normative and should not be extrapolated to female athletes, adults, or other sports. Prospective longitudinal studies incorporating three-dimensional kinematics and direct measures of training load are needed to clarify the longer-term clinical significance of these load-related findings, including their relationship to future injury risk.

Conclusions

Scapular dyskinesis affected 65% of these 20 male junior elite tennis players, mirroring rates in other overhead sports [7–9]. Yet all retained normal rotator cuff integrity and near-perfect clinical scores (ASES, 98.7; CMS, 98.0), and the dyskinesis and normal groups did not differ significantly. These findings are compatible with — though they cannot establish — a sport-specific physiological adaptation, consistent with these largely asymptomatic adolescent elite athletes being in an adaptive window at this age; because the cross-sectional design cannot distinguish adaptation from clinically silent pathology, this interpretation requires longitudinal confirmation. Because dyskinesis carries an established association with future injury risk [11, 21], clinicians should interpret it alongside symptoms, structural integrity, and functional status rather than dismiss it, and longitudinal follow-up is needed to clarify its prognostic significance.
