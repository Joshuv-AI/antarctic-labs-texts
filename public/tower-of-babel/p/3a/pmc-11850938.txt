Racial and gender disparities in patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion at a comprehensive stroke center
Authors: Olivia Duru, Ryan G. Eaton, Nathan Ritchey, Sharon Heaton, Ciarán J. Powers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) is the standard of care for acute ischemic stroke for select patients with large vessel occlusion (LVO). Although racial disparities in the utilization of thrombectomy have been previously identified, disparities in the utilization of thrombectomy in a single center with a standardized patient selection protocol have not been described in the literature.METHODS:Using the American Heart Association Quality Improvement Programs Registry, we retrospectively reviewed the records of 1,143 patients with LVO between December 1, 2014, and May 31, 2021. Patient records were assessed for demographic data, stroke risk factors, process metrics, and success of thrombectomy. A Pearson’s Chi-Squared and an independent two-sample t-test were used to determine the significance. Following this, a multivariate logistic regression was run to determine predictably of thrombectomy outcomes.RESULTS:Of the 1,143 LVO patients, 567 were male (49.6%), 576 were female, (50.4%), 963 were white (84.3%), and 180 were nonwhite (15.7%). Based on our Pearson’s Chi-squared analysis, female patients were more like to undergo thrombectomy compared to male patients (62.4% vs. 48.9%; P < 0.001). White patients were also more likely to undergo thrombectomy compared to nonwhite patients (58.7% vs. 39.7%; P < 0.001). After the multivariate logistic regression analysis and after controlling for comorbidities, insurance status, age, time to presentation (last known well to arrival), transfer from outside hospital, and zip codes, white patients were 2.29 times more likely to receive a thrombectomy compared to nonwhite patients (odds ratio [OR], 2.29, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.33, 3.944). Patients with Medicare insurance were 33.57 times more likely to receive a thrombectomy compared to those without medicare (OR, 33.57, 95% CI, 20.37, 55.327). In the regression model, sex did not contribute significantly to the likelihood of receiving a thrombectomy.CONCLUSIONS:White patients were more likely to undergo MT. Female patients tended to have higher rates of MT, accounting for the fact that other variables could have influenced this. These disparities may result from a multitude of other factors such as eligibility for MT, delayed presentation, and adequate diagnosis of LVO in the emergency department. This study highlights the importance of and potential causes of these disparities. Further investigation with data from multiple centers is necessary to validate these findings and identify strategies for improving utilization of thrombectomy.

Introduction

Several large, prospective, randomized studies have demonstrated the benefit of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for select patients with acute large vessel occlusion (LVO).[12345] Importantly, race and gender were not used as selection criteria for these studies, and it is reasonable to conclude that there is a benefit irrespective of these factors.

Racial and gender disparities do exist in the incidence, prevalence, morbidity, and mortality related to stroke. In regard to race, the incidence of stroke is higher in blacks compared to whites in all categories of stroke.[6] Non-Hispanic black males and females have a higher mortality rate compared to other races.[6] After stroke, non-Hispanic blacks were less likely to be discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility, more disable when admitted, and have less improvement in functional status per inpatient day compared to non-Hispanic whites.[7] Furthermore, racial disparities in the utilization of thrombectomy have been described in a review of a national database.[8] Nationally, thrombectomy was performed for 7% of nonwhites as compared to 9.8% for whites.

For gender disparities, more females die of stroke each year than males[9] and 58% of stroke deaths are females.[6] Each year, over 55,000 more women than men have strokes,[10] and the lifetime risk of a stroke is 1 in 5 for females while only 1 in 6 for males.[11] The incidence rates of ischemic stroke have been declining for men but not women.[12] After strokes, females have higher rates of disability and are less likely to be independent.[13] For our study, we used the American Heart Association Quality Improvement Programs Registry to investigate racial and gender disparities in the utilization of MT with a standardized patient selection protocol at our institution between 2014 and 2021.

The purpose of our study includes various reasonings. The first purpose was to investigate the gaps in surgical stroke in hopes of optimizing systems to improve patient outcomes. Another purpose of our study was to assess further factors or causes that may be associated with the disparities found and understand ways to address these factors. And lastly, to understand ways that we can improve patient outcomes, reduce mortality and morbidity within the population of patients affected by LVO and thrombectomy outcomes.

Methods

Using the American Heart Association Quality Improvement Programs Registry, we retrospectively reviewed the records of 1,143 adult patients with LVO between December 1, 2014, and May 31, 2021, at our comprehensive stroke center. Patients over the age of 18 years old were included. Patients who were inmates or were pregnant were excluded from the study. After narrowing down the selection of patients, patient records were assessed for demographic data, stroke risk factors, process metrics, and success of thrombectomy.

Patient selection for thrombectomy was determined by a protocol agnostic to gender and race. Assignments of race and gender were based on self-reported results (or selected by the patient’s next-of-kin). No effort was made to validate these self-reported variables either phenotypically or genetically. As such, we refer to gender rather than sex as the former as a social construct associated with the identity of “woman” or “man,” while the latter is defined as a set of biological attributes that are associated with certain physiological and physical features.

This protocol was developed collaboratively with Vascular Neurologists and Endovascular Neurosurgeons based on available published data and expert opinion. Patients with an LVO (defined as basilar, internal carotid, and middle cerebral arteries proximal to M3), a premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score <4, and a National Institutes of Health Stroke Score >2 were taken for thrombectomy if they arrived <6 h from last known well (LKW) and their ASPECTS score was >3. Between 6 and 24 h from LKW, the patients were taken for thrombectomy if perfusion imaging revealed a core infarct volume of <100 ml, a mismatch ratio of >1.8, and a mismatch volume of >15 ml.

We compared demographics, process metrics, and outcome measures between male and female patients and between white and nonwhite patients. Descriptive and comparative statistics were used for this study and values were presented as percentages. For each category, each subgroup of patients was compared against another. For example, the overall proportion of female patients was compared to the overall proportion of male patients receiving a MT for LVO. A Pearson’s Chi-Squared and an Independent two-sample t-test were used to calculate the significance. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine the statistical significance. To further the analysis, a multivariate logistical regression model was run with certain variables including sex, race, LKW to arrival, BMI, Medicare status, transfer from OSH, zip codes, age, and sum of comorbidities (history of atrial fibrillation, history of diabetes, and history of cerebrovascular accident).

Clinical trial registry

This work is a retrospective analytical study. No clinical trials were involved.

Results

Patient population and demographics

Results: Of the 1,143 LVO patients, 567 were male (49.6%), 576 were female, (50.4%), 963 were white (84.3%), 180 were nonwhite (15.7%). After performing a Pearson’s Chi-squared analysis, males were more likely to have a history of diabetes compared to females (22.8% vs. 17.9%, P = 0.044) and to be on medication for diabetes (25% versus 17.5%, P = 0.001). Conversely, females were more likely than males to have a history of atrial fibrillation (39.6% vs. 28.6%, P < 0.001), were older (70 years vs. 64 years, P < 0.001), and had a higher rate of pre-stroke mRS >2 (19.3% versus 7.5%, P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in race for diabetes and atrial fibrillation; however, nonwhites were more likely to be younger (63 years vs. 68 years, P < 0.001) and live in our local county (58.1% vs. 8.3%, P < 0.0001) [Table 1].

Race, sex, and thrombectomy utilization

Based on our Pearson’s Chi-squared analysis, female patients were more like to undergo thrombectomy compared to male patients (62.4% vs. 48.9%; P < 0.001). White patients were also more likely to undergo thrombectomy compared to nonwhite patients (58.7% vs. 39.7%; P ≤ 0.001) [Table 3]. After the multivariate logistic regression analysis [Table 4] and after controlling for several comorbidities, insurance status, age, time to presentation (LKW to arrival), transfer from outside hospital, and zip codes, white patients were 2.29 times more likely to receive a thrombectomy compared to nonwhite patients (odds ratio [OR], 2.29, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.33, 3.944). Patients with Medicare insurance were 33.57 times more likely to receive a thrombectomy compared to those without Medicare (OR, 33.57, 95% CI, 20.37, 55.327). In the regression model, sex did not contribute significantly to the likelihood of receiving a thrombectomy. However, females tended to be older and more likely to have Medicare [Table 5].

Discussion

In our analysis of LVO patients, female patients and white patients were more likely to undergo MT when compared to male and nonwhite patients. The female gender was not significant in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, given that confounding variables were controlled for however, it was significant in our Chi-squared models where confounding factors such as age, medicare status, etc., likely influenced the association between females and thrombectomy. Although the sigfnicance varies, it is still important to understand.

Although these disparities are significant based on statistical results, these disparities may result from a multitude of underlying factors. Since race and gender did not factor into the initial selection, we suspect that these disparities are due to other factors as stated previously, especially factors such as size of the core infarct that likely resulted males and non-whites less likely to be treated with MT. For example, the higher incidence of diabetes in males may have resulted in those patients presenting with a larger core infarct, making them ineligible for thrombectomy.

Although sex did not significantly contribute to the multivariate logistic regression model, females were more likely to receive a thrombectomy based on the Pearson’s and Chi-squared analysis. After analyzing the multivariate logistic regression model, Medicare patients were 33.29 more likely to receive a thrombectomy. Out of those with Medicare, females were more likely to have Medicare compared to males. Based on this, it is likely that Medicare status was a cofounding factor that contributed to females being more likely to receive a thrombectomy. Female patients also tended to be older which likely contributed to their higher rates of thrombectomy in the sex group. In a study done by Brinjikji et al.,[14] socioeconomic status played a significant role in the utilization of thrombectomy, the study showed that MT rates were higher among privately insured patients when compared to Medicare and uninsured patients. This varied from our results but showed the outcome disparity between insured patients and uninsured patients. Our particular study did not assess specific insurance types and only assessed Medicare versus Non-Medicare status.

Despite not being statistically significant, nonwhite and male patients had on average a longer LKW to arrival time when compared to white and female patients which possibly could impact patients’ eligibility status for a MT. In a similar manner, although nonwhite LVO patients were more likely to come from within our local county, they had a longer time from LKW to presentation, which may also have resulted in a larger core infarct due to a delay in treatment. In understanding, it has been shown that white patients are more likely to present to smaller, nonendovascular centers, then transferred to endovascular centers, whereas nonwhite patients often initially presented to larger endovascular centers. Often these larger endovascular centers are in more accessible areas (and urban areas) for nonwhite patients making it more likely that they are arriving at these centers rather than at nonendovascular centers.[15] Even though transfer rates were not significantly different between white and nonwhite patients, this could potentially explain why whites were less likely to originate from within the county and were more likely to be transfers in comparison to nonwhite patients.

Our results demonstrated that nonwhite patients were less likely to undergo MT when compared to white patients; previous studies have also demonstrated this trend that was seen in our study. This disparity could be due to many underlying factors arising from differences in baseline characteristics of our patients such as hospital accessibility, past medical history, time of hospital presentation, or time of diagnosis of an acute ischemic stroke due to LVO. In a study done by Smith et al., they found that race and ethnicity played a major role when it came to delays in hospital presentation for an acute ischemic stroke. The study found that Mexican Americans were less likely to use EMS in comparison to non-Hispanic whites, and usually patients being transported with EMS are handled with more urgency.[16] This could potentially explain the difference in the utilization of MT between nonwhites and white patients. If patients are delayed in hospital presentation due less utilization of emergency services after symptom onset, patients are less likely to meet the window for MT.

Longer LKW to arrival times may also be due to a delay in the recognition of stroke symptoms, lack of stroke awareness within a community, or decreased stroke literacy for an individual. A gap in knowledge regarding how stroke symptoms may present can significantly delay patient presentation to the hospital. It has been shown that women are more likely to recognize symptoms which could potentially explain why females are more likely to undergo MT, since they are recognizing symptoms earlier and able to present to the hospital in a timely matter to meet the criteria for receiving an MT.[17] Previous studies have also shown that there is a disparity in knowledge of stroke along with myocardial infarction symptoms that are influenced by race/socioeconomic factors. In a study conducted by Lutfiyya et al., African American males, poorer individuals, and those with lower levels of education had significantly lower scores.[18] Minority patients tend to present with increased severity of stroke symptoms and increased risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and more likely to have lacunar strokes making them less eligible for thrombectomy.[1920]

In recognizing the implications of these disparities and understanding what can be done moving forward, it is crucial to highlight the importance of addressing gaps in stroke awareness within various communities.[18] Addressing these gaps not only can improve the overall knowledge of stroke in the community but could influence delayed process metrics (LKW to arrival, arrival to arterial puncture, and arrival to revascularization times) and encourage patients to seek treatment quicker. Addressing the disparity in thrombectomy rates in both racial and gender realms require a multifaceted approach that involves systemic policy intervention, community engagement through education, and changes in resource availability. With the knowledge that the use of EMS is associated with an increased urgency of being medically evaluated, efforts can be made to address the screening protocols for stroke patients in the ED. To further enhance our knowledge in this area, investigating stroke screening rates between arrival in emergency department to the operating room can be an essential start to understanding how to accurately target current protocols. Within various communities that may be affected by these disparities, community engagement through educational interventions can be instrumental to increasing stroke awareness. Educating patients, along with family and friends, about the warning signs of stroke can significantly improve the likelihood of early recognition, thereby enhancing the chances for timely treatment. In a study conducted by Wallace et al., community engagements such as HipHop Stroke have been used in communities with an effort to increase education about stroke in black communities.[21]

The disparity rates as witnessed by multiple studies extend beyond an “access solution,” many of these disparities can be pushed to be addressed at national level where policy enforcement and re-allocation of resources can happen, such as increasing the amount of mobile stroke units available.[22] At our institution, the use of the mobile stroke units has been implemented in hopes of accelerating patient access to treatment. Studies such as ours can highlight the disparity issue and urge for stronger action to combat health disparities, especially in a disease process that affects a large amount of the general population. In health-care settings on a hospital level, the implementation of cultural competency training and educational initiatives aimed at addressing equitable stroke care can be a strategy used to ensure patients are receiving fair and unbiased treatment and screening within the ED.

Study limitations

The major limitation of our study is that it is based on a single institution. Further investigation with data from multiple centers will be necessary to validate these findings and identify strategies for improving utilization of thrombectomy. Although many institutions may have presented their outcomes and data, combing the data to obtain a generalized statistical analysis could be helpful in creating solutions. Another limitation for our study is that it is a retrospective study which enhances the likelihood of selection bias occurring and narrowing the window that these findings represent the statistics of the general population. Further investigation to improve this would be retrieving data from various centers to again validate the findings.
