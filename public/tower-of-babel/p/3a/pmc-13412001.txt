Institutional educational climate and MIR examination outcomes: a multicenter ecological study in Spanish medical schools
Authors: Jesús Millán, Beatriz Gal, Montserrat Esquerda, Joaquín García-Estañ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Spain, access to specialized medical training is determined through a highly competitive national examination (MIR). While the educational climate of medical schools has been associated with students’ learning outcomes and well-being, its relationship with performance in high-stakes national examinations remains unclear.MethodsA multicenter ecological study was conducted including 31 public medical schools in Spain. Educational climate was assessed using the Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM). Institutional MIR outcomes, including pass rates and residency placement rates, were obtained from official national data (2023–2025). Associations between DREEM scores and MIR outcomes were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation.ResultsA total of 3,632 students participated. Institutions with higher DREEM tended to show higher MIR pass and residency placement rates, however, no statistically significant correlations were observed between overall educational climate scores and MIR outcomes. Similarly, none of the DREEM subscales showed statistically significant associations with MIR indicators.ConclusionsEducational climate showed a limited and non-significant association with MIR outcomes, suggesting that performance in high-stakes national examinations is influenced by a complex interplay of factors beyond the institutional learning environment.

Introduction

Access to specialized medical training represents a critical milestone in physicians’ professional development. In Spain, admission to residency programs is determined through a highly competitive national examination known as the Medical Intern Resident (MIR) exam, which establishes a nationwide ranking system for specialty and training location allocation. Final ranking is based on a combination of examination performance and undergraduate academic records, making success in this process a key determinant of future professional opportunities.

Given the competitive nature of this system, identifying factors associated with success in national licensing examinations is of considerable interest for medical educators and academic institutions. Performance in high-stakes examinations is influenced by multiple individual and contextual variables, including prior academic ability, learning strategies, access to preparatory resources, and institutional educational characteristics.

Among institutional factors, educational climate has gained increasing attention as a determinant of students’ learning experiences and outcomes. Educational climate refers to students’ shared perceptions of teaching quality, academic support, institutional atmosphere, and social environment within medical schools. These aspects are typically evaluated using validated instruments that measure learners’ self-perceptions across various dimensions [1–3]. A positive educational climate has been associated with enhanced student engagement, deeper learning approaches, improved academic performance, and better psychological well-being [4].

The Dundee Ready Educational Environment Measure (DREEM) [1] is one of the most widely used instruments for assessing educational climate in health professions education. This validated tool evaluates multiple dimensions of the learning environment, including perceptions of learning, teaching quality, academic self-perception, institutional atmosphere, and social self-perception. Its international use has facilitated cross-institutional comparisons and contributed to quality assurance processes in medical education.

Although the relationship between educational climate and academic performance has been examined in various contexts, evidence linking institutional learning environments with outcomes in national licensing examinations remains limited. Understanding whether educational climate contributes to institutional differences in examination performance may inform strategies aimed at improving both educational quality and student achievement.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore institutional-level associations between educational climate, as measured by DREEM, and outcomes in Spain’s national MIR examination for access to specialized medical training. Specifically, we examined whether medical schools with more favorable educational environments tended to show higher pass rates and residency placement rates in the national selection process.

Methods

Study design and setting

An institutional-level, multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across public medical schools in Spain. The study examined associations between institutional educational climate and aggregated outcomes in the national Medical Intern Resident (MIR) examination for access to specialized medical training.

Participants and data collection

Educational climate data were collected from medical students enrolled in clinical years (years 4–6) across participating institutions. Students were invited to participate voluntarily through their respective student delegation offices. Data were gathered using an anonymous online questionnaire administered via Google Forms. The survey was administered between March and May 2022. Participating institutions were anonymized and coded numerically to ensure confidentiality.

A total of 31 public medical schools in Spain were included in the study, representing the 100% of available institutions. Private medical schools were excluded by design due to greater variability in curricular structures and potential differences in student selection processes, which could limit comparability across institutions.

A total of 3,632 students completed the survey. The average response rate per college was 11.75% (117,2 students per faculty) in all the 31 participants universities (Table 1).

Educational climate measure

Educational climate was assessed using the validated Spanish version [5] of the original Dundee Ready Educational Environment Measure (DREEM) [1]. This instrument consists of 50 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), yielding a maximum score of 200 points.

The DREEM evaluates five domains:

Higher scores indicate a more favorable educational climate. In accordance with established interpretation guidelines, total scores above 100 reflect a generally positive learning environment, scores above 150 indicate an excellent educational climate, scores between 51 and 100 suggest areas requiring improvement, and scores below 50 are considered indicative of a very poor environment.

Both overall institutional DREEM scores and subscale scores were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for institutional educational climate scores and MIR outcome indicators. Institutions were ranked according to overall DREEM scores. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Trend analyses were performed to visually explore relationships between educational climate and MIR outcomes. Because variables did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric analyses were conducted.

Results

Institutional educational climate and MIR outcomes

A total of 3,632 students from 31 public medical schools completed the DREEM questionnaire. The mean number of respondents per institution was 117.2 students (range: 33–295). Based on estimated enrollment figures derived from the national numerus clausus system, participants represented approximately 11.75% of the total medical student population across participating institutions.

Overall institutional DREEM scores varied considerably across medical schools, reflecting differences in students’ perceptions of the educational environment. Institutions were ranked from highest to lowest according to their total DREEM scores (Table 1). The table also presents the corresponding institutional MIR pass rates and residency placement rates.

Trend analyses

Trend analyses were conducted to visually examine associations between educational climate scores and MIR examination outcomes. Institutions with lower overall DREEM scores tended to exhibit lower MIR pass rates and lower residency placement rates.

Graphical representations suggested a gradual decline in examination performance indicators as educational climate scores decreased (Fig. 1). The trend line for MIR pass rates intersected the educational climate curve at approximately 90 DREEM points (Fig. 2), suggesting that institutions below this threshold tended to demonstrate comparatively poorer examination outcomes. The 90-point DREEM value should not be interpreted as an established cutoff from the original literature [1], but rather as an empirical observation specific to our dataset.

Correlation analyses

Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were performed to assess associations between institutional educational climate scores and MIR outcome indicators.

A total of 3,632 students participated across 31 medical schools. Although institutions with higher overall DREEM scores tended to show higher MIR pass rates and residency placement rates, no statistically significant correlations were observed between overall DREEM scores and either outcome.

Similarly, analysis of the five DREEM subscales did not reveal statistically significant associations with MIR outcomes. In particular, the “students’ perceptions of teachers” subscale showed a positive but non-significant trend (pass rate: p = 0.08; residency placement: p = 0.14). (Tables 2 and 3).

Internal consistency of MIR indicators

A strong and statistically significant association was observed between MIR pass rates and residency placement rates across institutions. This finding reflects internal consistency between the two outcome measures and supports the reliability of institutional MIR performance indicators.

Variance Explained by Educational Climate

Regression analyses indicated that institutional educational climate accounted for a small proportion of the variability in MIR outcomes. Coefficients of determination (R²) showed that less than 10% of the variability in MIR pass rates and residency placement rates could be explained by differences in educational climate across institutions.

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that the educational climate within Spanish medical schools shows a limited and non-significant association with institutional outcomes in the MIR examination. Although institutions with more favorable educational environments tended to demonstrate higher pass and residency placement rates, these trends did not reach statistical significance.

These findings highlight the complexity of factors influencing performance in high-stakes national examinations. While educational climate has been consistently associated with students’ academic achievement, engagement, professional identity formation, and psychological well-being [7–13] its direct relationship with standardized examination outcomes appears to be more limited. This is consistent with the notion that performance in licensing examinations is influenced by a wide range of variables, including individual student characteristics, external preparation strategies, and assessment design. In fact, the MIR and the Objective Structured Clinical Examination assess different domains of clinical competence, highlighting the complementary and exploratory nature of the skills evaluated by each test [14]. Additionally, participation in external preparatory courses for the MIR examination was not controlled, which may have influenced performance independently of the educational climate, potentially attenuating observed associations.

An important observation in this study is the relatively low DREEM scores across most participating institutions. Previous studies conducted in Spanish medical schools using the DREEM instrument have generally reported moderately positive educational climates, with global scores typically above 100, although several problematic areas persist, particularly during clinical training. For instance, a multicenter study across five Spanish medical schools reported mean scores of 116.2 in preclinical years and 104.8 in clinical years, highlighting a decline in perceived educational climate and the persistence of traditional teaching limitations [2]. Of note, DREEM questionaries used were derived from a Spanish version validated in a Latin-American country [5]. The consistently lower scores observed might also suggest that further investigation is required to validate DREEM measurements in a language and country specific contexts.

A supportive learning environment may enhance motivation, foster deeper learning strategies, and promote reflective practice, all of which are relevant to academic success [11, 12]. However, performance in national licensing examinations such as the MIR is likely determined by a complex interplay of multiple factors, including prior academic ability, individual study strategies, access to preparatory resources, institutional academic support systems, and personal socioeconomic circumstances [15–18]. Within this multifactorial context, institutional educational climate represents only one contributing dimension.

At the subscale level, a non-significant positive trend was observed for students’ perceptions of teachers. Although this did not reach statistical significance, it may suggest that aspects related to teaching quality, feedback, and faculty–student interaction could play a role in shaping learning experiences. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The relatively small proportion of variance explained by educational climate further supports the interpretation that MIR performance is influenced by numerous academic and non-academic determinants beyond the perceived learning environment [17, 19].

These results contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the extent to which institutional educational characteristics influence measurable academic outcomes. While educational climate remains an important indicator of institutional quality, student satisfaction, and well-being [7–12], its direct relationship with standardized national examination performance appears limited [19]. Therefore, policies aimed at improving examination outcomes should adopt a comprehensive approach that integrates curricular design, programmatic assessment strategies, academic support programs, faculty development initiatives, student mentoring, and equitable access to learning resources [15, 16].

From a quality assurance perspective, assessing educational climate continues to provide valuable information for identifying institutional strengths and areas for improvement [11, 12]. A positive educational environment supports students’ professional development and may indirectly contribute to academic achievement through enhanced motivation, engagement, and psychological well-being [7–10]. Consequently, efforts to optimize educational climate remain justified as part of broader institutional strategies to improve medical training.

Future research should explore longitudinal and multilevel designs to better clarify the temporal and causal relationships between educational environment and academic outcomes. Combining institutional indicators with individual-level academic trajectories, psychological variables, and learning strategies may provide a more comprehensive understanding of how educational experiences shape professional development and licensing examination performance [10, 13].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the design precludes individual-level inference and may be subject to ecological fallacy, as associations observed at the institutional level may not reflect relationships at the student level [20, 21]. Second, the relatively small number of responding students per institution (~ 11%) could limit statistical power, potentially contributing to the absence of more significant associations. Additionally, educational climate was measured through voluntary student participation, which introduces the possibility of selection bias and limits the ability to determine whether respondents were fully representative of the overall student population in each institution. The absence of response rate data further restricts assessment of representativeness.

Third, educational climate was evaluated using students’ self-perceptions, which may be influenced by subjective experiences, expectations, and cultural factors rather than objective institutional characteristics. Fourth, MIR outcomes were aggregated at the institutional level and may be influenced by variations in cohort academic ability, differential access to preparatory academies, institutional academic support systems, and socioeconomic factors not accounted for in this study.

Additionally, although MIR results corresponded to recent examination years, educational climate was assessed at a single time point. Consequently, the extent to which current perceptions accurately reflect the educational environment experienced by the examined cohorts cannot be fully established. Another limitation that must be considered is that the Spanish version of DREEM used was validated in a Latin-American institution [5], but its validation in Spain requires a more thorough analysis [2]. Other external factors such as participation in preparatory courses for the MIR examination were not controlled and may have influenced outcomes independently of institutional educational climate.

Finally, the relatively small effect sizes observed suggest that educational climate explains only a limited proportion of variability in MIR outcomes, indicating that other unmeasured factors likely play a more substantial role.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a national-level overview of the relationship between educational climate and MIR outcomes, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutional factors in medical education.

Conclusions

This multicenter study found that institutional educational climate showed a small and non-significant association with performance outcomes in Spain’s national examination for access to specialized medical training. Although medical schools with more favorable learning environments tended to demonstrate better examination indicators, educational climate accounted for only a limited proportion of the variability in institutional results.

These findings suggest that performance in high-stakes national examinations is influenced by a complex combination of academic, institutional, and individual factors, among which educational climate represents only one contributing dimension. Nevertheless, evaluating and improving the educational environment remains important for promoting student well-being, professional development, and institutional quality.

Future research using longitudinal and multilevel approaches is needed to better understand how educational experiences interact with individual learning trajectories and institutional characteristics to shape academic and professional outcomes.
