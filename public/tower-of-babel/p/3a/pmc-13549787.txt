Effects of Knee Osteoarthritis on Compensatory Mechanisms Post-Spinal Deformity Correction
Authors: Yoon Ha Hwang, Dongkyu Kim, Hyun-Jun Jang, Bong Ju Moon, Jeong-Yoon Park, Kyung-Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe investigated the relationship among regional alignments, the chronological sequence of compensatory mechanism changes, restoration of sagittal malalignment, and the effect of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) on resolution of compensatory mechanisms and clinical outcomes in deformity correction surgery. MethodsWe reviewed medical records and images of 75 patients with adult spinal deformity undergoing thoracolumbar fusion, with a minimum of 2 years of postoperative follow-up. Data collection focused on changes in various spinal and lower extremity alignments, including global angle, pelvic shift, knee angles (KAs), and ankle angles (AAs). We conducted propensity-matched comparisons to elucidate the impact of OA on resolution of compensatory mechanisms between the KOA group and the non-KOA (NKOA) group based on the Kellgren-Lawrence grading (KLG). Additionally, the study assessed patient-reported outcome measures. ResultsA significant linear correlation was observed between the change in T1 pelvic angle and the changes in various spinal and lower extremity parameters during follow-ups. Statistically significant correlation coefficients for thoracic kyphosis, sacro-femoral angle, and AA, initially not observed, were observed during the final follow-up. The KOA group exhibited limited restorative changes in compensatory spinal and lower extremities (KA : 1.1±5.5 vs. 7.1±9.3; p=0.015). Clinical outcomes showed improvement in both groups, with the NKOA group demonstrating more significant progress in 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (p<0.05). ConclusionRestoring sagittal alignment leads to the disappearance of compensatory mechanisms in a temporal pattern, with lower extremity improvements continuing for years. Severe KOA over KLG 3 impedes this restoration and results in poorer clinical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

In an aging society, the incidence of adult spinal deformity (ASD) and related degenerative conditions, including osteoarthritis (OA), is increasing. ASD often results from advanced spinal degeneration or multiple prior surgeries and affects not only the spine but also the lower extremity joints, which are essential for upright locomotion. OA involving the hip, knee, and ankle commonly leads to restricted joint motion, including flexion and extension contractures. Consequently, it is common to encounter patients experiencing spinal sagittal malalignment and lower extremities contracture [25].

Alignment can deteriorate due to various causes such as degenerative, iatrogenic, or traumatic, resulting in compensation mechanisms to maintain an efficient erect position and steady horizontal gaze. These compensatory mechanisms include loss of lumbar lordosis (LL), increased pelvic tilt (PT), cervical hyperlordosis, knee flexion, and ankle dorsiflexion [17,18,31]. Additionally, it was observed that after surgery, changes not only occur at the surgical site but also reciprocally affect other regions. These reciprocal changes seem directed at improving the global angle and reducing compensation that has accumulated in each region. Consequently, postoperative reciprocal change enables patients with ASD to better align their centers with the gravity line and maintain a horizontal gaze [3,30]. Moreover, restoring the sagittal vertical axis (SVA), PT, and mismatch between pelvic incidence and LL (PI-LL) significantly enhances postoperative quality of life (QOL) [34]. In the context of this complex compensatory chain reaction, surgical decision-making for each anatomical region requires careful consideration, as the impact of correction in one region cannot be evaluated independently of others.

This study explores postoperative reciprocal changes in sagittal alignment, focusing on cervical, thoracic, spino-pelvic, and lower extremities parameters following ASD surgery. Using the EOS imaging system (EOS imaging, Paris, France), we evaluated the impact of knee OA (KOA) on these postoperative alignment changes. The objective of this study was to clarify the relationships among spinal alignment, compensatory mechanisms, and clinical outcomes, emphasizing the importance of considering KOA in the management of ASD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gangnam Severance Hospital (IRB No. 3-2023-0382).

Patient population and data collection

In this single-center retrospective study, we reviewed the medical records of 421 adult patients with sagittal spinal deformity who underwent stereoradiography (EOS imaging) between 2018 and 2022. Our patient enrollment and data collection protocol were approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution. Inclusion criteria comprised adults (aged 18 years and above) presenting with radiographic findings of ASD as defined by the sagittal modifier of the Scoliosis Research Society (SRS)-schwab classification (PI-LL >10 degrees, PT >20 degrees, and/or SVA >40 mm) [34]. Patients meeting these criteria were included if they underwent instrumented thoracolumbar correction surgery and excluded if they had previously undergone thoracolumbar fusion for the reason of ASD, all types of cervical spine surgery, or had a history of hip, knee, or ankle arthroplasty or arthrodesis.

Standard demographic information was recorded for each patient, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and bone mineral density (BMD). Additionally, basic surgical information, such as spinal segments fused, number and type of osteotomies, and estimated hemorrhage, were recorded. We collected data at three distinct time points: baseline, first follow-up (3 months after surgery), and final follow-up (2 year after surgery). The patients were categorized into two groups based on the severity of KOA measured in Kellgren-Lawrence grading (KLG) scale [12]. The grading system is as follows : grade 0 (normal) shows no features of OA. In grade 1 (doubtful), a minute osteophyte is present with doubtful significance. Grade 2 (mild) indicates a definite osteophyte with a normal joint space. Moving to grade 3 (moderate), there is a moderate reduction in joint space. Finally, grade 4 (severe) reveals a greatly reduced joint space along with subchondral sclerosis. Non-KOA (NKOA) group consisted of the patients graded KLG 0, 1, or 2 (n=53), while the KOA group consisted of the patients graded KLG 3 or 4 (n=22). To ensure comparability between groups, we controlled patient factors (age at surgery, sex, BMI, BMD, and other medical comorbidities), as well as baseline radiographic parameters and clinical scores of each group, using propensity score matching (PSM).

Surgical considerations

LL targets were determined using the Global alignment and proportion score [37] based on PI. The planned LL was achieved through a combination of osteotomy techniques and selection of interbody cages with appropriate lordotic angles. Rather than pursuing maximal lordosis correction, surgical correction prioritized clinically sustainable sagittal alignment that could be realistically maintained in daily activities. Consequently, mild under-correction relative to the calculated ideal LL was considered acceptable in some cases. Selection of the lower instrumented vertebra (LIV) was based on lumbosacral pathology and stability. In patients with L5-S1 pathology, interbody fusion at L5-S1 with sacral fixation was routinely considered. The upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) was selected with consideration of the thoracolumbar inflection point and inclusion of a stable vertebra with true rib attachment, most commonly T10. This strategy aimed to reduce junctional stress while maintaining physiological thoracic alignment. Overall, the surgical approach allowed for flexibility and variation depending on individual patient anatomy and deformity characteristics. Adjustments in osteotomy degree, use of staged procedures including lateral approaches, and selection of UIV and LIV were made to accommodate spinal rigidity, sagittal imbalance severity, and intraoperative correction response.

Radiographic and clinical evaluation

All sagittal full-body radiographs were analyzed using dedicated and validated software (Surgimap; Nemaris, Inc., New York, NY, USA). The spinopelvic parameters measured included : PI, LL, sacral slope (SS), PT, PI minus LL mismatch (PI-LL), thoracic kyphosis (T2-T5, T5-T12 Cobb angle) and C2-C7 lordosis (CL, C2-C7 Cobb angle). The global alignment parameters measured included : T1 pelvic angle (TPA; the angle between the line from the femoral head axis to the center of the T1 vertebra and the line from the femoral head axis to the middle of the S1 superior endplate), SVA, and odontoid hip axis angle (ODHA; the angle between the odontoid to hip axis). In addition, pelvic angles referenced to other spinal landmarks were assessed, including the T4 pelvic angle (T4PA) and L1 pelvic angle (L1PA), which were defined analogously to TPA using the centers of the T4 and L1 vertebral bodies, respectively. The T4-L1PA mismatch (T4-L1PA) was calculated as the difference between T4PA and L1PA to evaluate the distribution of sagittal alignment along the thoracolumbar spine. The lower extremity parameters measured included sacro-femoral angle (SFA; angle formed between the bicoxofemoral axis and the line tangent to the superior endplate of S1 and the line between the bicoxofemoral axis and the femoral axis), knee angle (KA; angle between the mechanical axis of the femur and the mechanical axis of the tibia), ankle angle (AA; angle between the mechanical axis of the tibia and a vertical line), posterior pelvic shift (P-shift; the sagittal offset between the posterosuperior corner of the S1 endplate to the anterior cortex of the distal tibia) [5]. In addition, KOA was assessed using the anteroposterior (AP) and lateral (LAT) views of the knee region available on EOS full-body radiographs. The severity of KOA was graded according to the Kellgren-Lawrence classification. Coronal lower limb alignment was evaluated by measuring the femorotibial angle (FTA) on AP images, with varus alignment defined as medial angulation of the femorotibial axis. Standardized health-related quality of life measured included a Visual analog scale for back and leg pain, the Oswestry disability index, the SRS 22-question Questionnaire, and 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36). Two neurosurgeons, blinded to the study, assessed radiologic parameters and reviewed medical records.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using validated statistical software (R, version 4.1.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). First, we correlated postoperative changes in TPA with changes in regional spinopelvic parameters, cervical spine parameters, and lower extremity compensatory mechanisms using Pearson’s correlations to illustrate the disappearance of compensatory mechanisms. We performed ztest to verify the significant difference between the immediate and final follow-up [33]. Subsequently, we evaluated the correlation coefficient for changes during both immediate and final postoperative periods from the baseline. We balanced the factors that could be prognostic factors by conducting PSM. We used the matchit function in R’s Matchit package. For the frequency analysis of categorical variables across the groups, chisquare test was used, while Student’s t-tests was used to assess differences of continuous measures. In addition, correlation analysis between the Kellgren-Lawrence grade and FTA was performed to examine whether radiographic KOA severity was associated with coronal lower limb alignment. Comparison between the two groups involved perioperative changes in radiologic parameters and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). Mean values are represented as mean±standard deviation. p-value <0.05 was considered significant for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

A total of 75 patients who met the criteria were included in the trial. To investigate the tendency of the resolution in compensatory mechanisms, we analyzed data from all 75 individuals, and PSM was used to compare groups based on the presence of KOA. Following the PSM procedure, 22 patients were assigned to both the KOA and NKOA groups, respectively. Perioperative changes (Δ, delta value) were used to compare outcomes.

According to the SRS-schwab classification (PI-LL <10 degrees, PT <20 degrees, and/or SVA <40 mm) [34], both groups achieved corrections approximating the classification at final follow-up after surgery. In the KOA group, the PI-LL values were 12.5±18.1, PT values were 28.3±10.6, and SVA values were 32.2±41.2. In the NKOA group, the corresponding values were 13.2±11.7 for PI-LL, 27.8±7.7 for PT, and 34.6±35.2 for SVA. No significant differences were observed in demographic, baseline radiographic, clinical characteristics, or surgical-related outcomes among the matched groups. An exception was baseline femorotibial alignment, with varus alignment more frequently observed in the KOA group than in the NKOA group (Table 1). Consistently, the Kellgren-Lawrence grade showed a significant correlation with the FTA (r=0.72, p<0.001).

Analysis of postoperative change in TPA with change in regional parameters (the tendency of the resolution in compensatory mechanisms).

We investigated the correlation coefficients between the changes in TPA, representing the degree of correction after the surgery, and the changes in regional parameters. Table 2 presents changes from baseline to immediate and final follow-up for each period. Significant correlations were observed across all areas of spino-pelvic, cervical, and thoracic regional parameters up to the final follow-up period. These values represent the delta (Δ), calculated by subtracting the preoperative values from the postoperative values for each parameter. Postoperative TPA tended to decrease during correction surgery, with reciprocal changes observed in several parameters, indicating a decrease in their values.

Consequently, significant positive correlation was observed among change in PT (r=0.70, p<0.001), PI-LL (r=0.69, p<0.001), C2-C7 (r=0.36, p=0.002), ODHA (r=0.54, p<0.001), SFA (r=0.39, p=0.001), KA (r=0.60, p<0.001), AA (r=0.24, p=0.040), and Pshift (r=0.64, p<0.001). Conversely, LL exhibited a significant negative correlation, indicating a tendency that its reciprocal change progresses in the direction of an increase (r=-0.80, p<0.001). Although thoracic kyphosis increased, it demonstrated a positive correlation due to its kyphotic nature (T2-T5 : r=0.28, p=0.015; and T5-T12 : r=0.37, p=0.001). The correlations for each regional parameter are depicted in Fig. 1 and Table 2. The results of changes from baseline to immediate and final follow-up at each period are presented in Table 2. For thoracic kyphosis (T2-5, T5-10), SFA, and AA, statistically significant correlation coefficients, not observed initially, were observed at the final follow-up. Additionally, we conducted a z-test to determine whether there was a significant difference in the correlation coefficients between the two time points. In the case of KA, a significant difference between immediate and final follow-up was observed, with values of 0.33 and 0.60, respectively (p=0.041).

Comparison of radiographic and clinical outcome (the influence of KOA)

Perioperative changes between the final follow-up period and the baseline were compared to assess the differences. Regarding the radiologic results, a higher delta value indicated greater resolution of compensatory mechanisms and more significant restoration in sagittal balance (Table 3). Notably, for KA, a significant difference was observed between the two groups (1.1±5.5 vs. 7.1±9.3; p=0.015). With respect to spinopelvic and global sagittal alignment, postoperative correction achieved through surgery was comparable between the two groups. Changes in PT, LL, and PI-LL did not differ significantly. Likewise, global alignment parameters, including L1PA and T4-L1PA, demonstrated comparable postoperative changes, indicating a similar degree of surgical sagittal correction in both. Although not statistically significant, differences were observed in parameters T2-T5 (0.7±7.3 vs. 3.1±7.8), SFA (3.3±6.3 vs. 7.9±9.7), and AA (0.6±4.3 vs. 3.8±7.0). The KOA group underwent limited restoration in most other parameters. The consistent trend is graphically depicted in Fig. 2. The temporal variation in the graph represents the extent of restoration. Lesser variation was observed for the aforementioned parameter in the KOA group. Clinical outcomes assessed through PROMs were also evaluated using delta values to determine the extent of improvement. Both groups demonstrated sufficient progress after surgery. However, the improvement in SF-36 was notably higher in the NKOA group. Significance was observed in the following SF-36 items : role-physical, -5.4±31.6 vs. 24.9±33.5 (p=0.001); general health, -2.5±14.1 vs. 7.3±22.7 (p=0.028); role-emotional, -14.8±42.7 vs. 18.9±42.2 (p=0.003); physical component summary, 6.8±14.5 vs. 18.7±20.3 (p=0.015); and mental component summary, 0.4±19.2 vs. 14.2±19.3 (p=0.006) (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Human bipedal locomotion relies on the coordination of the alignment of the spine, pelvis, and lower extremities [17,28]. Whole-body evaluation, including the lower limbs, using EOS imaging is therefore essential for understanding sagittal alignment compensatory mechanisms, and several preliminary studies have been conducted in this regard [1,35]. Thoracolumbar malalignment can lead to global sagittal decompensation, prompting a cascade of compensatory mechanisms such as cervical hyperlordosis, pelvic retroversion at the hip joints, knee flexion, and ankle dorsiflexion. These compensations exhibit reciprocal restoration following spinal correction surgery [3,32]. During the chain reaction of realignment, knee motion is expected to influence both regional and global balance.

KOA leads to knee flexion contracture as a result of a combined effects of pain, degeneration, and inflammation in the adjacent tissues [14,21]. Murata et al. [21] proposed the concept of “knee-spine syndrome” suggesting an association between knee flexion contracture in patients with OA and decreased LL as well as lower back pain. Tsuji et al. [35] reported a relationship between patellofemoral joint pain and SS. Lee et al. [20] demonstrated an association between knee flexion and spinal alignment with normal joint conditions. In addition, several studies have investigated the impact of KOA on surgical outcomes in patients with lumbar degenerative disease [15,19].

This study specifically focused on the impact of KOA during the resolution of compensatory mechanisms following corrective surgery in patients with ASD, representing a distinct emphasis compared with previous studies. First, we sought to confirm whether postoperative restoration of alignment occurred in our cohort and whether the follow-up duration was sufficient to capture reciprocal changes in the knee and other lower extremity parameters. Accordingly, we analyzed delta values for each radiographic parameter at multiple time points—immediately after surgery and at the final follow-up—relative to baseline measurements. To quantify the degree of surgical correction, changes in the TPA were used as the primary reference, and correlations between changes in TPA and each regional parameter were evaluated. TPA remained stable and was not influenced by variations in PT or by supported standing during long spinal fusion procedures. Because TPA incorporates the concept of truncal inclination, it has been reported to reflect true thoracolumbar deformity [3,24]. As shown in Fig. 1, significant linear correlations were observed between changes in TPA and changes in most regional parameters, indicating effective resolution of compensatory mechanisms and successful postoperative restoration. Furthermore, as presented in Table 2, correlation coefficients for thoracic and lower extremity parameters at the final follow-up were greater than those observed immediately after surgery. Notably, KA demonstrated a more pronounced difference on z-test analysis, suggesting that restoration of knee and lower extremity alignment became more evident over time. However, this finding does not indicate a precise chronological sequence of restoration among unfused segments; rather, it suggests that reciprocal changes require a certain postoperative period to fully manifest.

Research has been conducted on how knee joints can influence global sagittal balance. Wang et al. [36] and Katsumi et al. [11] asserted that severe KOA had a significant impact on the sagittal alignment of the spine-pelvis-lower extremity axis. Sato et al. [26] enrolled patients with KOA to investigate improvement in the 3D alignment of the lower extremity following spinopelvic correction surgery. In this study, we categorized the groups based on severity to understand the impact of KOA. We conducted PSM to balance patient factors, radiographic parameters, and clinical scores between the two groups. Importantly, postoperative spinal correction assessed using conventional spinopelvic parameters, including PT, LL, and PI-LL mismatch, was comparable between the KOA and NKOA groups. More recently, sagittal realignment concepts have emphasized restoring a standing alignment that minimizes engagement of compensatory mechanisms and is directly modifiable during surgery. In this context, trunk-based parameters such as the T4PA and L1PA have been proposed as global descriptors of thoracolumbar alignment [8,9,23]. Consistent with the spinopelvic findings, postoperative changes in these global alignment parameters were also similar between the two groups, indicating that a comparable degree of sagittal realignment was achieved surgically. Despite equivalent spinal and sagittal correction, the KOA group, the change in KA was significantly reduced. Additionally, our findings revealed that angles or values in other regional and global parameters remain stagnant and fail to decrease compared to the NKOA group (Fig. 2). In essence, the residual of compensation in the KOA group resulted in less achievement of sagittal restoration. Representative images of the perioperative changes associated with thoracolumbar deformity correction in each group is depicted in Fig. 4.

Persistent compensatory mechanisms may affect the postoperative QOL negatively [6,13,27]. Our findings demonstrated postoperative improvement in both groups; however, a significant intergroup difference was observed in the degree of improvement as assessed by the SF-36 questionnaire. KOA-related factors, including knee flexion contracture, appeared to hinder the reduction of knee flexion, a key compensatory mechanism in the lower extremities. As a result, these limitations exerted secondary radiological effects on other regional and global sagittal alignment parameters. Moreover, such radiological differences may have a substantial impact on patients’ clinical outcomes. We illustrated the differences in sagittal balance restoration associated with KOA using schematic diagrams (Fig. 5). Given that both ASD and KOA are highly prevalent degenerative conditions in the aging population, it is essential not to overlook either condition in clinical practice. Therefore, comprehensive preoperative evaluation of both spinal alignment and knee pathology should be considered crucial when formulating an optimal treatment strategy.

This retrospective study has several limitations. First, the follow- up period was relatively short, which may have limited our ability to fully characterize the timing and patterns of reciprocal changes across all regional parameters. As suggested by the trend graphs, a longer follow-up period could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of postoperative changes, particularly in the cervical and thoracic spine. Second, the study population was skewed toward older patients. Diebo et al. [4] asserted that age influences sagittal spinal malalignment due to the stiff spine commonly observed in the elderly, leading to significant compensations in the lower extremities. Although our analysis of knee and lower extremity compensation was not substantially affected, we acknowledge that age-related factors may have limited the assessment of reciprocal changes in other spinal regions. Additional subgroup analyses stratified by age could have enabled a more detailed evaluation of these effects.

In our retrospective analysis of knee-related parameters and KOA, the limitation of relying solely on recorded medical information presented challenges, leading to a simplified interpretation. Owing to the application of already captured EOS AP and LAT images, we relied solely on flexion and extension angles. Employing multi-angle imaging, including inversions, would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the spine’s impact. Additionally, using the Kellgren-Lawrence grade as the primary diagnostic criterion for KOA, it is essential to note that an increase in severity of the KLG does not necessarily imply worse knee flexion due to contracture. Other symptoms, such as pain, also determine severity [29]. Assessing this information from patient records was challenging. Due to the inherent spatial resolution limitations of EOS imaging, detailed evaluation of osteophytes and subtle degenerative changes of the knee joint was not feasible in this study. Therefore, we utilized the FTA, a parameter closely correlated with the KA as reported in prior studies, which reflects coronal deformity and knee contracture, and can be reliably assessed on EOS imaging as a clinically meaningful indicator of compensatory mechanisms influencing sagittal alignment [16]. Although the Kellgren-Lawrence grade has known limitations, we intentionally maintained it as a representative parameter because of its simplicity, intuitive interpretation, and widespread clinical use. To address the potential discrepancy between radiographic severity and functional knee limitation, we strengthened the representativeness of the Kellgren-Lawrence grade by demonstrating its correlation with the FTA. In addition to the knee, other lower extremity joints, such as the hip and ankle, may also contribute to compensatory mechanisms and influence postoperative restoration. A more comprehensive analysis incorporating these joints could have provided further insight into the relative contributions of each lower extremity joint to overall compensation. However, the primary objective of the present study was to isolate and clarify the specific influence of the knee on sagittal alignment and compensatory changes. Accordingly, our analysis was deliberately focused on knee-related parameters. Moreover, dedicated questionnaire for lower extremities, such as Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index [2] and Harris hip score [7]. It is plausible that our hypothesized concern with the restoration of global balance may not be the predominant consideration; outcomes could be influenced solely by the condition of the lower extremities. We excluded individuals who underwent total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Many studies indicate that TKA increases the range of motion in the knee [10,22]. We did not longitudinally assess changes in KOA severity during the follow-up period. Incorporating serial evaluations of KOA progression could have allowed a more detailed assessment of the reciprocal influence of surgical interventions across different anatomical regions and provided valuable insights into the optimal sequencing of surgical treatment in patients with concomitant spinal deformity and knee pathology.

CONCLUSION

Sagittal alignment restoration following spinal surgery is accompanied by the resolution of compensatory mechanisms, including reductions in cervical malalignment and adjustments in knee, hip, and pelvic alignment. These reciprocal changes appear to follow a temporal pattern, with continued improvements in the lower extremity alignment persisting for several years. However, severe KOA impedes this restorative process by sustaining knee flexion and other lower extremity compensations. This results in difficulties in achieving global balance and improving other regional parameters such as cervical and thoracic alignment, often leading to poorer clinical outcomes in patients with KOA.
