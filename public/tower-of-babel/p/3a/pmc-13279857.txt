Plastics and labor: The case of disposable medical plastics
Authors: Gauri Pathak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlastics are ubiquitous in the contemporary practice of medicine, where they are tied to notions of hygiene and quality of care. However, when plastics first infiltrated global medical practice, they did so because of considerations related to patient comfort and durability. It was only after developments in the sterilization of plastics and the aggressive marketing of single‐use plastics that medical plastics came to be emblematic of hygiene as they are today. In this article, drawing from historical data and fieldwork in India, I argue that dependence upon disposable medical plastics is not just about infection control but also about enabling reductions in labor at the site of use. This labor is displaced into the future—as new plastic production—and downstream, onto waste management. Medical plastics thus function as a materiality of displaced labor in clinical settings. Recognizing this displacement effect is crucial to plastic control interventions within sustainable healthcare.

INTRODUCTION

The Essential Plastics Coalition is a USA‐based plastic‐industry body founded to “defend important consumer products from unnecessary restrictions,” and its website contains numerous mentions of single‐use medical plastics as high‐value plastics that should be exempt from plastic control restrictions. This stance is not uncommon. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, I attended webinars with representatives of Indian plastic‐related industry bodies where I heard refrains regarding the critical value of disposable plastics as found in, for instance, personal protective equipment (PPE) and packaging. These plastics were deployed as metonyms for a larger plastic apparatus and its importance to hygiene and the saving of human lives. In general, this association between plastics and hygiene (and infection control) has not been countered in public discourses. Policymakers across the world have tended to treat disposable medical plastics as exceptional and therefore immune from plastic control regulations. An initial draft of the global plastics treaty being negotiated (negotiations have thus far proven inconclusive) included a blanket exemption on plastic products for medical and health applications (United Nations Environment Programme, 2024). In practice, too, although medical plastic dependence varies across regions, there has been a trend away from reusables toward an increased use of disposable medical equipment, much of which contains plastics (Smith et al., 2023).

I am a medical anthropologist studying human–plastic entanglements, and in 2023, I found myself having two experiences of hospitalization in Mumbai, India—the first, in late January and early February, was for sudden and severe pneumonia for ten days (of which seven were in the ICU), and the second, in July, involved one day of hospitalization for a gynecological surgical procedure. Until then, my research had not been focused on medical plastics, but the exceptional status accorded to them had been hard to ignore. During my hospitalizations, I therefore found my attention being drawn to the plastics being used and discarded during my care, and I mentally chronicled the processes involved in their use.

This was not a case of planned fieldwork—I am not a hospital ethnographer, and I had not expected either of these hospitalizations. Instead, it was an instance of life preceding and guiding fieldwork. Although within medical anthropology we try to listen to patients, we rarely speak as patients. My hospitalizations led to my experiencing plastics as a patient, and those experiences form the basis for this article. I draw from them, against the backdrop of my interest in and earlier fieldwork on plastic use, regulation, and disposal, to meditate upon the role of disposable plastics1 in contemporary medical practice. I argue that a high degree of dependence upon disposables is not just about infection control but crucially also enables reductions in labor at the site of use, at the hospital or clinic; in such settings, disposable plastics function as a materiality of displaced labor. In doing so, I bring work on the anthropology of plastics into the context of clinical settings to highlight an important role for medical anthropology within the emerging field of sustainable healthcare.

METHODOLOGY

My hospitalizations left me with experiences that could not be easily codified in research designs and ethics. The usual hierarchies under which I conduct research were subverted—as a patient, I was dependent upon medical authority. Getting consent for the observations I conducted then would have required a different relationship to hierarchy, but excluding those observations would mean being dishonest about the inspiration for my later exploration and excluding a valuable form of experiential knowledge. I have therefore included those observations while taking care to anonymize hospital and staff details and treating the auto‐ethnographic material differently from other forms of participant–observation.

India has a multi‐payer healthcare model with a mix of public hospitals (which provide low‐cost care) and private hospitals, but public spending on healthcare in India is some of the lowest in the world, at 1.28% of GDP (Central Bureau of Health Intelligence, 2019, 14). India therefore has one of the highest proportions of household out‐of‐pocket healthcare expenditures globally, the expanding private sector is the predominant provider of inpatient care and high‐end curative services (Balarajan et al., 2011), and 55% of the country's hospital bed capacity comes from private hospitals (Rajagopalan & Choutagunta, 2020, 12). Doctors and healthcare providers also tend to be concentrated in urban areas. Within this system, Maharashtra—which houses Mumbai—has a relatively higher density of skilled healthcare workers compared to the national average (Motkuri & Mishra, 2020), but data from 2005 suggest that 83.4% of hospitals within the state were private hospitals and total bed capacity in private hospitals was around twice that of public hospitals (Rajagopalan & Choutagunta, 2020, 23–24). Moreover, Maharashtra is one of the country's most populous and industrialized states, but its economic growth is heavily reliant on the Mumbai district. Mumbai is also known for its corporate (for profit) hospitals, owned by Indian companies but enjoying support from the government through land subsidies and tariff reductions on high‐tech equipment, that offer high quality care, including to foreign medical tourists (Baru, 2005; Sengupta & Nundy, 2005).

Both my hospitalizations (and auto‐ethnographic observations) occurred in such a private facility. T.S.H. is a large corporate multispecialty hospital, and its brand has been built on high quality care (undeniably, the care I received was exceptional); it has won several awards and been certified by the National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and healthcare providers. The hospital caters to healthcare seekers within and beyond Mumbai, including international medical tourists; it even has an overseas patient center and help desk. At the same time, it is registered as a private charitable trust hospital, which requires subsidized treatment for a quota of low‐income patients (Office of Charity Commissioner Maharashtra State, 2018).

My observations at T.S.H. were supplemented by a review of historical research on the use of disposables. They also formed the basis for a series of in‐depth unstructured interviews with key interlocutors (n = 7) in the winter of 2023–2024. Of these, four were healthcare practitioners involved with hospitals in Mumbai. One was an infection control specialist at a hospital, another was a clinician and hospital administrator, and one was a surgeon who consulted for T.S.H. but also ran a private orthopedic hospital. All three had been practicing for over four decades and had witnessed shifts in the use of disposables in Mumbai and in Indian healthcare. The two clinicians also had considerable hospital administration experience. The fourth Mumbai‐based key interlocutor was an executive nurse at T.S.H. in a senior leadership position and familiar with hospital procedures.

The two other key interlocutors were clinicians from a smaller city, which I call Surajpur, also in Maharashtra. Surajpur is a growing industrial city within the larger administrative Surajpur district, far from Maharashtra's established medical hubs in Mumbai, Pune, Nashik, and Nagpur. Surajpur city's hospitals and medical establishments thus end up catering to a large population from the surrounding rural and semi‐rural areas, and it is an emerging medical center. My interlocutors—a gynecologist running his own hospital and a consultant anesthetist who visited several hospitals on calls—had both been practicing for over forty years and were active members of the branch of the Indian Medical Association in Surajpur. They could therefore speak to changes in dependence upon medical disposables, both with time and as Surajpur has increasingly industrialized; data from them forms an important complement to the Mumbai case. Finally, in Mumbai, I also spoke to a senior executive in the medical device industry, to understand patterns in the sales and marketing of reusable medical equipment. Interviews were conducted in English, although there was frequent code switching between English, Hindi, and Marathi. The post‐hospitalization research was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Aarhus University, and informed consent was obtained from all key interlocutors. All interlocutor and hospital names are pseudonyms.

DISPOSABLE MEDICAL PLASTICS AND HYGIENE

Plastic packaging comprises a significant proportion of plastic production; data from Europe suggest it accounted for around 44% of annual global production in 2021 (Plastics Europe, 2022). In hospital settings in Europe, however, packaging waste is only a fraction of total medical plastic waste. Instead, the bulk of plastic waste is composed of (1) gloves, gowns, aprons, and PPE, (2) incontinence products, and (3) “contact devices” such as tubing and syringes (Ivanović et al., 2022; Health Care Without Harm, 2021). These data suggest that most disposable plastic items used in hospitals have medical—rather than packaging—applications.

Writing about the rise of disposable face masks in healthcare, Bruno Strasser and Thomas Schlich (2020) chronicle a shift to disposable materials—syringes, needles, trays, and surgical instruments—in hospitals in the USA in the 1960s. Initially, plastics entered medical practice in high‐income countries because of their convenience and durability rather than because of concerns regarding hygiene (Borowy, 2020; Hodges, 2017). Plastic blood bags were easier to transport than glass bottles (Hodges, 2017, 322). Plastic syringes were better positioned for delivering vaccines over wide areas than glass ones (Hodges, 2017, 322). Other plastic equipment was more resilient than alternatives, making it better suited to patient comfort (Hodges, 2017, 322). By the 1970s, equipment made from plastics infiltrated hospitals in greater volume, including in prosthetics, dialysis tubes, and intravenous bags (Borowy, 2020, 234). Single‐use plastics also came to be symbols of sterility because of the advent of chemical sterilization, the development of plastics that could withstand high temperatures, and the marketing of disposables as hygienic (Borowy, 2020; Greene et al., 2022; Hodges, 2017). The 1978 Declaration of Alma‐Ata brought increased emphasis on primary health care and the use of “appropriate technology” (Borowy, 2020, 235), and the Universal Immunization Program relied on single‐use plastic syringes in low‐income settings, such as India, as they obviated a need for cleaning and sterilization (Hodges, 2017, 323). This, in turn, encouraged the use of other medical disposables. Then, the rise of new infectious diseases (such as HIV/AIDS and Ebola) brought fears regarding transmission through improperly sterilized medical equipment. This, along with increasing antimicrobial resistance and more extensive infection transmission because of international travel brought a new preoccupation with infection control, and single‐use plastics acquired significance as a modern hygienic technology (Borowy, 2020; Greene et al., 2022; Hodges, 2017).

These shifts nevertheless brought misgivings about dependence upon medical disposables. From the 1980s onward, financial (rising landfill costs) and environmental (toxicity and pollution) concerns about high volumes of waste prompted studies into medical waste reduction in the USA, which was at the forefront of the use of disposables (Gilden et al., 1992). The NGO Health Care Without Harm was also started in 1996 in North America—although it has since expanded across the globe—to find ways to combat the environmental costs of healthcare, and healthcare waste is at the core of its efforts (Health Care Without Harm, n.d.).

More recently, within the emerging field of sustainable healthcare, which addresses the environmental costs of healthcare delivery, clinicians have called for better waste segregation for enhanced recycling, more reliance on reusables, reductions in packaging, and increased medical device reprocessing such that device manufacturers reuse components (e.g., MacNeill et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2023). Sustainable healthcare advocates point out that although more comprehensive data are lacking, studies suggest that the extensive use of disposables is not always necessary for infection control. Hospital‐based infection, for example, can be effectively combated through handwashing with soap and water for 20 s in a regular context and 2–5 min in a surgical context as opposed to requiring the use of gloves (Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, 2023). One study found that reusable medical gowns offer better barrier protection than disposable ones (McQuerry et al., 2021). Another reported no evidence to support a difference in risks of surgical site infection between the use of disposable and reusable surgical drapes in orthopedic and spinal surgery (Kieser et al., 2018). A comparison of cataract operations in the UK and India also revealed that infection rates were lower in India, where there is less reliance on disposables, than in the UK (Haripriya et al., 2019).

GEOGRAPHIES OF DISPOSABLE MEDICAL PLASTIC DEPENDENCE

Dependence upon disposal plastics in the worldwide practice of medicine is uneven. I had been rushed to T.S.H. complaining of breathlessness and a galloping pulse rate. A (plastic) oxygen mask was placed over my nose, and a chest X ray revealed I had a severe case of multilobar pneumonia. I was administered a battery of tests to determine its cause. I was also given a corticosteroid injection—via a plastic syringe—and started on a broad‐spectrum antibiotic—via a plastic IV line. In that short span of time, several plastics were used and discarded—gloves, syringes, tubes, oxygen masks, packaging, tubes, and others. By the end of my ten‐day hospital stay, they would be far more—in the hospital bill, I counted 126 syringes (varying types), 248 gloves, 26 pressure monitoring lines, and 16 IV lines.

Nevertheless, the degree of dependence on disposables that I witnessed at T.S.H. was in fact much lower than that found in other settings. For example, in the Singapore public hospital system, which comprises over 80% of the country's bed capacity (Tan et al., 2021), disposable drapes, gowns, and PPE are the norm. The reliance on disposables even extends beyond plastics, with disposable surgical packs that contain steel implements (Pathak, 2025). This is not unusual. Medical waste generation tends to be higher in high‐income countries. Estimates suggest rates of medical waste generation of 0.02 kg per patient per day in urban dispensaries in Tanzania compared to 10.7 kg per occupied bed per day in metropolitan general hospitals in the USA (Borowy & Aillon, 2017). In India, studies have found rates to be on the lower end, ranging from 0.25 kg per occupied bed per day to 2.31 kg per bed per day (Thakur & Ramesh, 2018).

When I was admitted to T.S.H. for my surgery, I noticed that the anesthetist and the nurses in the operating theater were dressed in green fabric gowns. My nurse interlocutor later confirmed that the hospital relied on reusable gowns and drapes for surgeries considered low risk, such as gynecological or general surgeries. For general surgeries, the hospital also reused equipment such as trocars and laparoscopic instruments. For major and supra‐major surgeries (such as orthopedic and transplant surgeries), considered high‐risk surgeries where the stakes of infection are much greater for patients, T.S.H. used disposable gowns and drapes.

At Arun Bapat's gynecological hospital in Surajpur, dependence upon disposable plastics was even lower than at T.S.H. This much smaller hospital relied upon reusable gowns and drapes, and staff reused gynecological surgical equipment such as suction cannulae and trocars. Dr. Bapat told me that prior to 2000—which is when latex gloves became more easily available—they even autoclaved gloves for reuse. However, compared to T.S.H., Dr. Bapat's hospital generated less plastic waste; the nurses autoclaved cotton bundles to make them into swabs, dressings, and so forth, resulting in fewer packaging discards.

REUSABLE ALTERNATIVES

When I first started thinking about disposable medical plastics, I thought their reduction would involve updating practices before the proliferation of single‐use plastics. After all, syringes can be washed or autoclaved and reused. Glass bottles can replace blood and IV bags. Laundries can take care of reusable PPE; plastic wipes (usually made of blends containing polypropylene or polyester) used for sponging can be replaced with washcloths; medication can be dispensed into patient envelopes; and so on. I put these thoughts to Dr. Dawda, the infection control consultant for a Mumbai hospital. Her retort was unequivocal: “Our hospital won't have the staff to do that. Most hospitals will not have the staff to do that. Our turnover is too high. Already I am continuously training the nurses in infection control.” When I asked about the days prior to the easy availability of disposables, she paused for a moment. “They used to have sterilizers,” she said, as she gestured in the shape of a box. “But in those days infection rates were higher. That was there.”

It is telling that Dr. Dawda's first thought was not about infection control but labor. When I spoke to Dr. Mukund Kshirsagar, an orthopedic surgeon with his own hospital in Mumbai who is also a consultant at T.S.H., there were similar shifts between notions of infection control, risk, and labor. Dr. Kshirsagar had started switching over to disposables, including surgical gowns and drapes, around 2015; at the time of our conversation, his practice consisted entirely of disposable PPE. The hospital had once used reusable gowns and drapes, but, he said, “the cost of infection is definitely not worth it.” Orthopedic surgery is considered high risk, and infections can lead to permanent suffering. Disposables cost about 25% more, but “with present rates of electricity, labor, it [cost of reusables] is getting higher and higher. It is then worth considering the benefit of disposable vs. material locally autoclaved.” Dr. Kshirsagar explained further, “It [reusables] is a lot of employment,” and even with what he termed modest rates of staff turnover at his hospital, “train karaayla traas asto (it is a lot of trouble to train).”

When I asked about centralizing sterilization and cleaning services for smaller hospitals, he replied: For that, you will have to collect from hospitals. You have to collect it from the operating theaters. Then—just sterilization is not enough. You need to wash it. You will need to use—first you have to do a social wash, with soap and simple detergent; a social wash, to remove debris, blood clots. You can't wash and only autoclave….you will have to supervise. Mansa employ karayla laagtil (you will have to employ people). Facilities karayche (you will have to create facilities), it will become like dhobi ghats (open air laundries). Mumbai will become full of dhobi ghats! It becomes too much. [The use of] Disposables compensates for all this.

For bigger hospitals such as T.S.H., the use of reusables, even when it does not extend to glass syringes or blood bottles, requires extensive training, effort, and oversight. At T.S.H., gowns and drapes are separated in the operating theater and cleaned in antibacterial solution. After this, they are sent to be laundered. Steel tools are put through an enzyme wash and an ultrasonic machine. Then, materials are autoclaved as per manufacturer instructions. The entire process is audited and checked, overseen by the hospital's Central Supply Sterilization Department. The scale of sterilization activity at T.S.H. makes this cost competitive when compared to Dr. Kshirsagar's smaller facilities. Moreover, orthopedic surgery, as a high‐risk operation, requires more infection control precautions than other procedures.

For Dr. Bapat's gynecological hospital, however, the smaller scale was less of a barrier. My interlocutors told me that in Surajpur, salaries for nurses (“sisters”) and cleaning staff (“mavshi”) were lower than those in Mumbai. Dr. Bapat's hospital had a single‐drum autoclave machine that ran on electricity, much of which was derived from rooftop solar panels. Stored, unused surgical linens, gowns, drapes, and tools were autoclaved every Thursday and Sunday (steel for 30 min; cotton fabrics for 35 min) to maintain sterility. A green label was used to mark autoclaved products, and records were made in a register of which products had been autoclaved, by whom, and when. The extra labor still resulted in lower costs; the cost differential between reusables and disposables was about 80%. “A delivery [birth] that costs 25,000 rupees [around USD 300] here would cost 80,000 rupees [around USD 960] in a corporate hospital in Mumbai,” Dr. Bapat explained. The 80% cost differential between reusables and disposables was also far more favorable than the 25% differential at Dr. Kshirsagar's hospital. This meant that at Dr. Bapat's facilities, disposables were only used for cases with higher risks to staff, such as when a patient tested positive for Hepatitis B.

RISK AND LIABILITY

In a piece in the Journal of the American Medical Association urging action to reduce single‐use products in healthcare, Smith et al. (2023) call for better reporting of medical device‐related infection risk, especially decontamination challenges and infection risk factors, as such data would allow for comprehensive risk stratification guides to help clinicians make more sustainable and safe choices. They also note that the onus of gathering data regarding reuse is on manufacturers, who are not incentivized to reduce disposables: For a manufacturer to bring a medical device to market with a “reusable” label, it provides data demonstrating to FDA's satisfaction that the device can be cleaned and disinfected or sterilized without impairing its function. However, the “single‐use” label is self‐designated by manufacturers, not FDA. Thus, a device may be labeled as single‐use because the manufacturer believes it cannot be safely and reliably used more than once, or because the manufacturer chooses not to conduct studies needed to demonstrate to the FDA that it is reusable. Meanwhile, certain manufacturers and third‐party companies collect used so‐called single‐use devices, clean and sterilize them, and sell them back to hospitals for reuse as FDA‐approved “reprocessed” medical devices (Smith et al., 2023, 1948).

This resonates with what I found in my conversation with Dr. Kshirsagar. He would have been willing to use reusable devices (such as artheroscopes, which are thin tubes with attached cameras that are used during surgery), he told me, if manufacturers took on the responsibility of sterilizing and endorsing them as safe. “At present,” he lamented, “the industry is not supporting this in India.” My medical device interlocutor suggested that this was due to a perception from marketing departments of a lack of commercially viable demand. However, I heard mention of such reuse occurring in India without certification by the original manufacturers. Studies and surveys have borne out that reuse, often poorly regulated, occurs in‐house at hospitals in India, rather than through certified third‐party suppliers or original manufacturers (Jacobs & Akpinar, 2018). This, Dr. Kshirsagar asserted, was unsafe and therefore unethical, and without manufacturer guarantees, he was unwilling to engage with device reuse.

My nurse interlocutor mentioned that manufacturer representatives visited T.S.H. to train staff on how to clean, sterilize, and reuse equipment such as laparoscopes. Equipment which was not certified by the manufacturer for reuse was a liability, and the law required that patients and their families were informed of reuse, as in the case of the indigent patients that must be treated for free by private charitable hospitals. Nevertheless, given the liability and the risk of reputational damage, T.S.H., did not reuse equipment that was not certified as reusable.

The issue of liability was also mentioned by Dr. Kshirsagar. Reusables meant taking responsibility for any infection‐related outcomes. The nominally higher costs of disposables relieved Dr. Kshirsagar of this worry: Triple Hs—that is HIV, HBsAg [Hepatitis B], HCV—have taken a front stage. It is menacing…. We used to do it [use reusable equipment], but the liability is not worth it. The marginally increased costs—about 25%—compensates for the headache, it brings peace of mind to the surgeon. If there is litigation or investigation, if you can make a firm statement that you used disposables, you are acquitted immediately.

DISPOSABLE PLASTICS AS LABOR SAVING

Interlocutor comments reveal two types of labor that disposable medical plastics reduce in clinical settings: (a) physical labor as effort (primarily that of janitorial staff, nurses, trainers, and infection control specialists/auditors) and (b) mental labor in terms of thought and attention (primarily that of nurses, clinicians, and administrators). Reusables require materials to be picked up, inspected, and segregated in operating theaters or wards. They must be cleaned to remove debris and soaked in enzymatic cleaners to remove biological matter such as blood. They must then be sterilized. Sterility must be maintained through regular autoclaving or chemical fumigation (to tackle airborne pathogens). All these processes must be recorded, checked, rechecked, audited, and certified. Training is necessitated at every stage to ensure that procedures are being accurately followed and for the appropriate amounts of time.

My experiences of being hospitalized at T.S.H. revealed the hectic schedules of the nurses and janitorial staff there. In the ICU, nurses were constantly monitoring patient parameters, responding to patient calls for assistance, cleaning dressings, administering medication, or answering questions from residents, intensivists, or other clinicians. They had to record readings, medication delivery, and equipment use in a file that was scrutinized every day at the end of their shifts by charge nurses. Infection control specialists audited their files and their procedures, questioning any seeming discrepancies. The janitorial staff were similarly fetching bed pans and commode chairs, emptying catheters, preparing beds for tests in radiology, fetching medicines from the hospital pharmacy, and sponging patients and changing linens. Outside the ICU, protocols for the testing and monitoring of patient parameters were less rigid, but nurses and janitorial staff had more charges. A shift toward more reusables would require far more labor. Who would perform the cleaning; who would pay for additional training? What would the audits of sterilization look like, who would they be conducted by, and how would the costs of all this labor be borne?

In resource‐rich settings such as the USA and UK, the ease of maintaining sterility, along with issues of managing supplies and healthcare workers’ desires for disposables fostered by aggressive marketing campaigns, were only part of the reason for a shift to disposables (Strasser & Schlich, 2020). Significant reductions in labor formed the clinching argument. Sidney Peimar, a hospital administrator in Detroit, USA, noted that the “advent of the disposable package” relieved nurses of supply responsibilities (Peimar, 1969, 118). At Peimar's hospital, preparing a reusable spinal tray for use required eight steps prior to reaching the patient's bedside and another ten steps before items on the tray could be reused for another patient. Importantly, Peimar noted, “Of these steps, only the tasks performed at the nursing station and in the patient's room are commensurate with the nurse's professional training” (Peimar, 1969, 119). Disposable trays required only four steps. This was a significant saving of time and costs, especially of nurses having to bear non‐specialist labor.

The other, less obvious, form of labor is of thought and attention. Peimar observed that tasking nurses with supply responsibilities meant that “nursing personnel had to be constantly concerned about having sufficient quantities of each item on the cart available at all times” (Peimar, 1969, 122). My time in hospital made me realize how substituting disposable plastics with reusables would require multiple levels of planning, coordination, hiring, and oversight. Interlocutor comments, however, revealed another dimension: the privileging of disposables for sterility means that the use of reusables brings a sense of responsibility for this choice. This is not just a case of legal liability but also of ethical obligation and anxieties about patient outcomes and perceptions of quality of care. In the absence of data on risk factors, reusables‐related infection outcomes, and certifications for safety, clinicians and hospital administrators are left with the mental labor of worrying about infection potential and liability. This becomes an added and seemingly unnecessary burden. This is not to say that unregulated reuse does not occur; as mentioned, such reuse happens—sometimes to save costs for low‐income patients but also to increase profit margins—but it is acknowledged as not being best practice and as being ethically murky (Jacobs & Akpinar, 2018). As I have described elsewhere, an interlocutor in Singapore explained the difficulties in talking to clinicians about reducing their use of disposable plastics. When asked to try to move toward more sustainable medical practices, many clinicians emphasized that they had a duty of care to their patients, not to the public at large (Pathak, 2025). For them, the use of disposables was part of a quality assurance. Meanwhile, disposable plastics replace that labor of thought (and worry), just as they replace labor as effort.

PLASTICS AS LABOR INTERMEDIARIES

In the last few decades, plastics have increasingly become associated with pollution, and their rampant use and disposal has emerged as an urgent environmental challenge. As mass‐manufactured anthropogenic substances, plastics have become emblematic of the Anthropocene, and much attention has been trained on the “bads” they bring across their life cycle, from a dependence upon fossil fuel economies and issues of disposal and waste colonialism to harms to ecologies from plastic litter and contamination from micro and nanoplastics or endocrine disrupting additives (e.g., Liboiron, 2021; Marriott & Minio‐Paluello, 2013; North & Halden, 2013; Prata et al., 2020; Velis & Cook, 2021; Vethaak & Legler, 2021).

An emerging “anthropology of plastics” (Abrahms–Kavunenko, 2023; Pathak & Nichter, 2019), at the intersections of medical and environmental anthropology and science and technology studies (STS), draws attention to how humans and more‐than‐humans interact with plastics. Such work makes the case that to address the “bads” of plastics, it is necessary to understand the “goods” they are associated with—the advantages they are seen to bring and the constraints associated with the use of alternative materials or practices. Plastics, for example, have freed design from the limitations of natural materials (Vincent, 2013), made goods cheaper to produce (Meikle, 1995), enabled market and trade expansion (Andrady & Neal, 2009), and implicated themselves in life possibilities in the medical world (Roberts, 2010).

Plastics, especially disposable plastics, are also associated with “convenience,” which is extolled as their major advantage (Latkar, 2025). Disposable products can be classed as part of “convenience consumption,” a form of consumption that saves time and effort (Campanelli, 2003; Oka, 2021). Convenience technologies were initially thought to reduce labor time and lead to gains in leisure time. However, such technologies rarely save overall labor or time—rather, they generate new tasks and new standards. Total working time does not change and paradoxically acquires greater intensity in terms of the number of tasks to be done in that time (Campanelli, 2003; Cowan, 1989; Vanek, 1974). This is especially true of care work, which is often feminized and devalued (Lane, 2017). The use of disposable plastics in healthcare seems to follow a similar trend. My observations of the nurses and janitorial staff at T.S.H. certainly suggest that their use of disposable plastics did not translate into more breaks or leisure time.

Amid a rich body of work on plastics from the perspective of STS, Gay Hawkins (2011, 2012, 2018) has explored how plastics are enacted as market devices. In a key article, Hawkins (2017) examines how plastics, known for their durability and resistance to easy biodegradation, came to be associated with disposability. Disposability, Hawkins contends, is about anticipating and accepting wasting as built into single‐use plastics and relations with them from the outset rather than as something that happens after use and after the exhaustion of value. To illustrate this point, Hawkins engages with a meme by Max Temkin (2011; see Figure 1). The meme points to the effort required to manufacture a disposable plastic spoon, from oil extraction to conversion to plastic to shaping into a spoon and transport to destination of use. This effort is contrasted with that required to wash a spoon for reuse. Hawkins points to the network of associations and structures that make disposability possible and unavoidable, such that plastics become ethical intermediaries that foreground disposable modes of entanglement with the world.

Hawkins's focus is on disposability as a form of ethical blindness—the ability to waste without care—fostered by plastics. Nevertheless, the point about plastics as intermediaries and about the effort—in effect, labor—involved in the materialization of carbon economies is an important one. In the case of medical plastics, by saving clinicians, nurses, janitorial staff, and other healthcare practitioners the labor of collecting, cleaning, sterilizing, and worrying about reusable equipment, disposables are acting not just as ethical intermediaries but also as labor intermediaries.

When it comes to diagnostic testing usability, Alice Street and colleagues note that availability need not imply the quality of being “ready to hand,” described as the immediate handiness of medical equipment and resources to staff, “without the need for them to undertake further cognitive or physical effort to make them useable” (Street et al., 2023, 2). Thought of in this way, disposable medical plastics are more “ready to hand” than their reusable alternatives. They help healthcare practitioners avoid the labor required for the safe deployment of reusable equipment at the site of the hospital or clinic. Such labor saving becomes particularly salient in high‐income—or rather higher labor cost—settings, such as in Mumbai (and especially at T.S.H., as a premier hospital within Mumbai) when compared to Surajpur and the smaller hospitals within it. This is also borne out in the trend that generally, dependence upon disposable medical plastics tends to be higher in high‐income settings, where labor is more expensive (e.g., Morris et al., 2013; Thiel et al., 2017).

DISPOSABLE PLASTICS, DISPLACED LABOR, AND SUSTAINABLE HEALTHCARE

Literature on the anthropology of health systems examines the social organization and power relations of health systems to challenge dominant techno‐managerial perspectives (Closser et al., 2022; Pfeiffer & Nichter, 2008; Storeng & Mishra, 2014). Much of the focus within sustainable healthcare is on such techno‐managerial perspectives and the establishing of metrics, material substitutions, technical solutions, and awareness raising. Within this literature, interventions related to disposable plastics revolve around life cycle assessments, procurement, material substitutions, product redesign, and better waste segregation, without considering sociocultural and political dimensions (Rizan et al., 2020). In their review of the medical anthropological literature on health systems, Svea Closser and colleagues make the case for “critical ethnographic scholarship to interrogate the interconnectedness of one specific aspect of care provision with the larger systems at play” (2022, 9). Through this article, I heed that call in the context of sustainable healthcare provision to show how dependence upon disposable medical plastics is implicated in larger social and political–economic structures of labor organization and plastic production. Disposable medical plastics’ quality of being “ready to hand” rests upon a gathering of plastic production and device manufacturing systems, chemical sterilization procedures, supply chains, human resource operations, and legislative and regulatory schemes that privilege the use of disposables.

The need for labor, although reduced at the site of the clinic, is not obviated. Disposable medical plastics form a materiality of displaced labor, such that this labor is displaced upstream, into the future, as the labor of extracting and refining fossil fuels, manufacturing them into polymer resin, and processing this resin into new disposable plastic products for use (e.g., Pathak, 2023). These products must then be chemically sterilized prior to packaging and transported to their destinations. All this occurs at scale, allowing for greater efficiencies and cost savings. The labor of disposable medical plastics is also displaced downstream, away from the specialist labor of healthcare professionals and janitorial staff—onto the labor of correctly managing medical waste, often as externality. Dr. Bapat stated that although he pays for medical waste from his hospital to be collected for incineration, he has reservations about the eventual fate of this waste. An investigative report, he told me, had found medical waste in Surajpur being openly burned (that is, in community settings without adequate controls). Studies suggest that in India, incinerators often operate beyond capacity, much waste is disposed of through deep burial rather than incineration, and most healthcare facilities have no credible biomedical waste management system in place (Dehal et al., 2022; INCLEN Program Network Study Group, 2014). Indeed, most medical waste across the world is inadequately managed, and common issues include open burning, the tipping of untreated wastes into dump yards, and a small but non‐negligible trade in used medical equipment (Borowy, 2020; Cook et al., 2022).

Given the role of disposable medical plastics as an infrastructure within healthcare (Pathak, 2025), reducing dependence upon them would require reimagining healthcare provision and care work. It would also entail pursuing a strategy of “shifting the focus of medical care away from material to non‐material methods and, generally, to reducing the resource burden of therapy” (Borowy & Aillon, 2017, 354). Such changes would mean emphasizing health promotion over cure, a social rather than biomedical approach to health, an emphasis on labor productivity rather than resource productivity, and reversing the medicalization of birth and ageing, among other things. As of now, systems of plastic production, the role of plastics as market devices, and notions of disposability mean that the labor of disposable plastic avoidance is individualized whereas that of its use is built into structures of production, manufacturing, and waste management (see also Liboiron & Lepawsky, 2022). Until more systemic change can be effected, interventions targeting disposable medical plastics will therefore need to tackle that burden by developing structures that help achieve labor efficiencies in the deployment of reusables. This would mean an initial outlay of other forms of labor—research on the risk outcomes of reusable equipment, the testing of reusables by medical device manufacturers, and the categorizing and certifying of reusables for their specific contexts of safe use. This would then have to be reflected in legal structures evaluating risk, liability, and notions of duty of care, as well as in medical education to combat perceptions of disposables as preferable to reusables. Crucial, though, would be a reprocessing and reuse industry that can achieve economies of scale in the cleaning and sterilizing of reusables.

CONCLUSION

Disposable medical plastics are ubiquitous in healthcare. This ubiquity often goes unquestioned because of assumptions regarding the hygiene and effective infection control that disposable plastics bring. Nonetheless, a reliance on disposable plastics is not necessarily or always the best infection control practice. Drawing from auto‐ethnographic and ethnographic fieldwork in India and a review of the historical research on the use of disposables in medicine, I have argued that the major barrier to an increased reliance on reusable alternatives is not infection control but labor. Disposable plastics replace labor in clinical settings, displacing that labor into the future, in the upstream production of new disposables, and downstream into waste management. They also obviate the need for the initial labor outlay of research and testing required for the safe deployment of reusables.

I do not mean to imply that the contemporary practice of medicine relies on disposable plastics (however uneven this dependence may be) solely to save specialist labor and displace it onto more undervalued labor. Undoubtedly, there are infection control considerations that should not be downplayed. My argument here—through insights from Mumbai and Surajpur—is instead regarding the need to center the degree of dependence upon disposables, and how much of that dependence—when clinically relevant—can be tackled by addressing sociocultural and political–economic issues, particularly those of labor.

Aarti Latkar (2025) writes about how escape from the labor of caring for and cleaning reusable materials is integral to the convenience attributed to disposable plastics. The case of disposable medical plastics helps illuminate this dimension as a major barrier to a shift to reusable alternatives. Disposable plastics form a majority of the plastics produced annually, and they are an obvious starting point in tackling plastic pollution. Nevertheless, for interventions targeting disposable plastics to be effective, they must reckon with how they are perceived as saving labor. As of now, the labor of disposable plastic avoidance is individualized whereas the role of disposable plastics as labor intermediaries is built into global structures of production, consumption, and disposal. A true move away from a high degree of reliance on medical disposables will require new labor structures and new ways of provisioning and conceptualizing healthcare. However, in the short‐term, plastic control interventions within healthcare will have to tackle the added labor burden by developing processes to achieve labor efficiencies in the use of reusables. Only by acknowledging the labor displacement effect of disposable plastics can sustainable gains toward plastic control be made.
