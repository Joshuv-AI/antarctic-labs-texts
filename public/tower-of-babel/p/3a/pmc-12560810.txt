Trajectories of Well‐Being and Burnout Among Health and Social Services Community Workers During the COVID‐19 Pandemic: A Longitudinal Study of Job Demands and Resources
Authors: Ariane Paquin, Alexandra Giroux, Stephanie Radziszewski, François Lauzier‐Jobin, Simon Coulombe, Janie Houle, Sophie Meunier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHealth and social services nonprofit workers face important occupational challenges that were exacerbated by the COVID‐19 pandemic and that may have impacted their psychological health during that time. In Quebec, Canada, many of these workers are employed in community‐based organizations providing essential services to vulnerable populations. This longitudinal study examined the temporal evolution of well‐being and burnout, as well as their associated predictors, among health and social services community workers across three time points: November 2019 to January 2020 (Time 1), June to September 2020 (Time 2), and January to March 2021 (Time 3). Findings revealed significant decreases in well‐being and increases in burnout over time. Reduced opportunities to use one's strengths at work was associated with declining well‐being, while increased overcommitment was associated with rising burnout. Contrary to expectations, perceived workload and dissatisfaction with pay decreased during the pandemic. Similarly, higher baseline resources and demands were unexpectedly associated with a greater decline in well‐being and increase in burnout. This is one of the few studies to have longitudinally examined the well‐being and burnout of health and social services community workers. The results show that, among these workers in Quebec, in a crisis situation such as COVID‐19, well‐being decreases while exhaustion increases. Strengths used at work and overcommitment seem to be important variables to consider in explaining these results, and such insights can be used for developing targeted interventions to support the psychological health of these essential workers.Keywords: burnout, community organizations, COVID‐19 pandemic, health and social services, job demands‐resources model, multilevel growth curve analyses, well‐being [at work]

Introduction

Health and social services workers play a crucial role in supporting vulnerable populations. In Quebec, Canada, many of these services are provided by nonprofit community‐based organizations, commonly known as community organizations, which currently employ 54,000 workers and account for 1.4% of all jobs in Quebec (RQ‐ACA 2025). Although they work in collaboration with public services (e.g., hospitals, clinics), they are distinct from these insofar as they emerge from and are governed by the communities they serve. Their employees, often referred to as community workers, offer essential services such as mental health support, housing assistance, and food security. These workers are employed for community organizations that are mission‐driven and frequently created to meet critical needs unmet by public or private systems, or to advocate for marginalized populations (Smithwick et al. 2023; Chevalier et al. 2013). Community organizations are primarily funded through a mix of public grants and community fundraising (CSMO‐ÉSAC 2024).

Because of the way they are structured, community organizations are both uniquely positioned and especially vulnerable in times of crisis, such as during the COVID‐19 pandemic. For example, in Quebec, Canada, the COVID‐19 crisis significantly disrupted the service delivery, staff coordination, and funding stability of nonprofit community‐based organizations (Couturier and Fortin 2021; RQ‐ACA, & TNCDC 2022). Several reports from the grey litterature documented how these organizations faced increased demands while managing staff shortages, emotional exhaustion, and a lack of institutional support (Couturier and Fortin 2021; RQ‐ACA, & TNCDC 2022). Many workers indicated having to take on new tasks, extend their hours, and operate under conditions of chronic uncertainty—all of which amplified psychological strain (Couturier and Fortin 2021; RQ‐ACA, & TNCDC 2022). While some peer‐reviewed studies conducted in the rest of Canada have found similar results (e.g., Kerman et al. 2022; Schiff et al. 2024), most of these were cross‐sectional in nature and overlooked the distinct organizational and sociopolitical context of Quebec's community sector. Given the fluctuating context of the COVID‐19 pandemic and the likelihood that similar situations will occur in the future, it seems important to examine the evolution of health and social services community (HSSC) workers' well‐being and burnout during the pandemic to develop targeted interventions and sustain this essential workforce.

In sum, while there is a growing body of research on the psychological health of healthcare workers in Canada during the COVID‐19 pandemic (e.g., Dall'Ora et al. 2020; Wilbiks et al. 2021), relatively fewer studies have focused specifically on HSSC workers. Some recent Canadian studies have examined the well‐being and working conditions of social workers, homeless service providers, and personal support workers (e.g., Hapsari et al. 2022; Kerman et al. 2022), but longitudinal research remains scarce, particularly in the community sector. The present study seeks to fill this gap by contributing to the growing evidence base on HSSC workers and uses a longitudinal design that spans the period before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Using the Job Demands–Resources (JD‐R) model (Demerouti et al. 2001), the current study examines the trajectories of well‐being and burnout among HSSC workers across three time points. By identifying the work‐related demands and resources that predict these trajectories, this study aims to provide practical recommendations for supporting the psychological health of HSSC workers during prolonged crises.

Psychological Health at Work

The World Health Organization (2024) defines health as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well‐being and not merely the absence of disorders. In this regard, psychological health includes emotional, psychological, and social well‐being, and influences how individuals think, feel, act, and cope with stress. In the workplace context, several authors have suggested that psychological health at work should likewise be viewed as having a positive and a negative dimension and be assessed in a way that reflects the specific challenges of the work environment (Dagenais‐Desmarais and Savoie 2012; Gilbert et al. 2011). In this regard, psychological well‐being at work (PWBW) and burnout are two key constructs that help to better understand workers' mental health.

PWBW refers to the subjective positive experiences and perceptions that individuals have of their work setting. According to Dagenais‐Desmarais and Savoie (2012), it comprises five dimensions: interpersonal fit at work, thriving at work, feeling of competency at work, perceived recognition at work, and desire for involvement at work.

Burnout, on the other hand, is a psychological state resulting from prolonged exposure to unmanaged work stress (World Health Organization 2024). It includes emotional exhaustion (lacking the energy to display empathy to others), physical fatigue (feelings of tiredness and low energy), and cognitive weariness (feelings of reduced mental agility) (Shirom and Melamed 2006), and is distinct from clinical disorders such as depression or anxiety because it is specific to the occupational setting.

Job Demands‐Resources Model

Over the past two decades, the JD‐R model has become a widely used theoretical framework to explain how work characteristics affect employee well‐being and burnout (Demerouti et al. 2001; Bakker and Demerouti 2017). This model structures the intricate relationships between job characteristics and employees' psychological health (i.e., exhaustion/burnout and engagement/well‐being) and offers valuable insights into the complexities of the modern workplace. The JD‐R model categorizes work conditions into two fundamental components: job demands and job resources (Bakker and Demerouti 2017).

Job demands refer to aspects of work that require sustained effort and are associated with certain costs, such as workload, emotional strain, or role conflict (Demerouti et al. 2001). According to the JD‐R theory, job demands have a direct and positive relationship with burnout, notably by contributing to exhaustion (Bakker and Demerouti 2017).

Job resources, on the other hand, are the physical, social, or organizational aspects of work that help achieve work goals, reduce job demands, and stimulate growth and development (e.g., social support, job autonomy; Bakker and Demerouti 2017). They are associated with increased well‐being and motivation (Bakker and Demerouti 2017). Although this model has been applied widely across various sectors, few studies have used it to examine longitudinal changes in the health and social services community sector during a crisis such as the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Job Demands and Resources Among Community Workers

Job demands and resources vary across occupations, and community organizations present a unique configuration of them. Among HSSC workers, common job demands include a chronic overload of tasks, role ambiguity, and emotional labor associated with supporting vulnerable populations (Fauvel et al. 2020; Giroux et al. 2022; Kim and Stoner 2008). Financial insecurity, lack of benefits, and systemic underfunding further exacerbate the strain placed on this workforce (Couturier and Fortin 2021; Kosny and MacEachen 2010; Ormel et al. 2019). Furthermore, many HSSC workers report feelings of guilt for not doing enough for their clients or colleagues, especially when taking time off (Fauvel et al. 2020; Cleary et al. 2023). Overcommitment is also prevalent, with some workers struggling to disconnect from work even during personal time (Fauvel et al. 2020; Sonnentag and Fritz 2015). In sum, HSSC workers often face complex and cumulative job demands that can challenge their psychological health. On the other hand, the community sector also offers important job resources for HSSC workers. Support from colleagues and supervisors, job autonomy, and the opportunity to use one's personal strengths at work have been identified as important protective factors for their well‐being (Fauvel et al. 2020; Giroux et al. 2022; Forest et al. 2012). Many community workers describe their teams as tightly knit, which can foster a sense of solidarity and shared purpose (Fauvel et al. 2020; McClure and Moore 2021). They also reported that engaging in projects aligned with their individual interests and strengths not only enhanced their job satisfaction but also bolstered their sense of competence and overall well‐being at work (Giroux et al. 2022).

However, these resources can be fragile and highly sensitive to organizational instability, resource scarcity, or external crises. For example, during the pandemic, support systems were disrupted, and the ability to use one's strengths or benefit from collegial feedback was often compromised (Banks et al. 2020). As such crisis are likely to occur more frequently in the future (Marani et al. 2021), it is crucial to understand how they affect the psychological health of front‐line workers, such as HSSC workers, to better support them in future crises.

Objective and Hypothesis

The present study aimed to examine the temporal evolution of well‐being, burnout, and their related demands and resources among HSSC workers during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The study also aimed to determine whether fluctuations in work demands and resources were associated with burnout and well‐being trajectories. The following hypotheses were tested:

Well‐being of HSSC workers will have decreased over time during the pandemic;

Burnout of HSSC workers will have increased over time during the pandemic;

The job resources (autonomy, the opportunity to use one's strengths at work [meaningful work], and social support from supervisors and colleagues) of HSSC workers will have decreased during the pandemic;

The job demands (workload, dissatisfaction with pay, overcommitment, and guilt) of HSSC workers will have increased during the pandemic;

A decrease of job resources during the pandemic will be associated with a decrease of well‐being during the same period;

An increase of job demands during the pandemic will be associated with an increase of burnout during the same period.

Furthermore, as an exploratory objective, this study aimed to examine how job demands and resources at baseline were associated with changes in psychological health at work among HSSC workers during the pandemic.

Method

This study was conducted in partnership with a network of health and social services community organizations in the province of Quebec, Canada. The partner was involved throughout the research process, in accordance with a participatory research approach (Cargo and Mercer 2008; Jagosh et al. 2012) aiming to ensure contextual relevance and applicability of the study. Specifically, the partner validated the appropriateness of the measures used in the questionnaire to the context of HSSC workers, facilitated participant recruitment through its communication channels, and provided contextual insights to support the interpretation of findings.

Participants and Procedures

This study was approved by the Université du Québec à Montréal (UQAM) Institutional Committee on Ethics in Research Involving Humans. Participants were recruited through advertisements in the newsletters or on the social media platforms (e.g., Facebook) of community organizations in the province of Quebec, Canada. Eligibility criteria included being 18 years or older and employed in a health and social services community organization—this excluded the public sector. Participants had to be actively employed in a nonprofit community‐based health and social services organization at each time point to be eligible. The survey was available in both French and English, with back‐translation used for scales that were not previously available in both languages (Brislin 1970). For all validated scales, total scores were computed using the mean of item responses, unless otherwise indicated. Initial recruitment took place between November 2019 and January 2020 (Time 1), with subsequent measurements between June 2020 and September 2020 (Time 2), and between January and March 2021 (Time 3). A total of 1425 community workers, aged between 19 and 70 years of age (M = 38.17, SD = 11.33), participated in at least one of the three data collection waves. As the questionnaires were independent across time points, participants could join the study at Time 2 or Time 3 without having completed the initial survey. For the purposes of the present longitudinal analysis, we retained only participants who completed the baseline survey (Time 1) and at least one follow‐up (Time 2 and/or Time 3), resulting in a final analytic sample of 287 participants. Participants retained for the longitudinal analyses (n = 287) did not differ from those excluded (n = 847) on most baseline characteristics and outcomes, except for psychological well‐being at work and autonomy. Participants retained in the longitudinal analyses reported slightly higher baseline well‐being (M = 4.41, SD = 0.52 vs. M = 4.27, SD = 0.62, t(674.23) = −3.28, p = 0.001), and slightly higher autonomy (M = 5.26, SD = 1.54 vs. M = 4.98, SD = 1.61), t(606.82) = −2.38, p = 0.018) than those not retained. This suggests a potential retention bias whereby individuals experiencing greater difficulties at baseline may have been more likely to discontinue participation. Retained participants reported an average weekly workload of 33.05 h (SD = 6.59) and an average tenure of 9.71 years (SD = 8.39) in the nonprofit sector. Measures

Psychological Well‐Being at Work

Psychological well‐being at work was assessed using the Index of Psychological Well‐Being at Work (Dagenais‐Desmarais and Savoie 2012), which comprises 25 items (e.g., “I find meaning in my work”; “I feel that my work efforts are appreciated”) to be rated on a six‐point Likert scale ranging from (0) “Disagree” to (5) “Completely agree”. Indices of internal consistency for this scale were very satisfactory, with α = 0.93 to 0.94.

Burnout

Burnout was measured using the Shirom‐Melamed Burnout Measure (Sassi and Neveu 2010; Shirom and Melamed 2006), which consists of 14 items that assess physical fatigue, cognitive weariness, and emotional exhaustion. Sample items include “I feel burned out” and “I have difficulty thinking about complex things”. Responses range from (1) “Never or almost never” to (7) “Always or almost always” (α = 0.94 to 0.96).

Autonomy, Social Support From Supervisors and Colleagues, Quantitative Workload and Dissatisfaction With Pay

Autonomy, social support from supervisors and colleagues, quantitative workload, and dissatisfaction with pay were evaluated using items from the Questionnaire on the Experience and Assessment of Work (QEAW) (Lequeurre et al. 2013). Autonomy was measured with the Independence in Work Subscale (4 items; “Can you decide the order in which you carry out your work on your own?”; α = 0.85 to 0.89). Meanwhile, social support from supervisors (4 items; “Do you get along well with your superior?”; α = 0.94 to 0.95) and from colleagues (4 items; “In your work, do you feel appreciated by your colleagues?”; α = 0.88 to 0.90), along with quantitative workload, were measured with the Pace and Amount of Work Subscale (4 items; “Do you have too much work?”; α = 0.83 to 0.94). Responses are on a seven‐point Likert scale ranging from (1) “never” to (7) “always”. Participants evaluated dissatisfaction with pay (4 items; “Do you think you are paid enough for the work that you do?” α = 0.81 to 0.84) on a Likert scale ranging from (1) “strongly agree” to (7) “strongly disagree”.

Strengths Use

Strengths use at work was assessed using an adapted short version of the Strengths Use at Work Scale (Forest et al. 2012), comprising six items (α = 0.89 to 0.91). Statements such as “I use my strengths every day” or “My work gives me a lot of opportunities to use my strengths” are rated on a seven‐point Likert scale ranging from (1) “Strongly disagree” to (7) “Strongly agree”.

Work Guilt

To measure guilt toward work, participants completed a five‐item scale developed by Cleary et al. (2023) (α = 0.80 to 0.84). Sample items include “I feel bad when I take time off from work”, “I feel guilty when I feel I'm not doing enough for my organization”, and “The feeling of not doing enough for service users makes me feel guilty“ and are rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from (1) “Strongly disagree” to (5) “Strongly agree”.

Overcommitment at Work

Overcommitment at work was evaluated using the six‐item Effort‐Reward Imbalance (ERI) scale (α = 0.84 to 0.86) (Niedhammer et al. 2000), with items like “As soon as I get up in the morning, I start thinking about work problems”. Responses are scored on a four‐point Likert scale, ranging from (1) “Strongly disagree” to (4) “Strongly agree”.

Analyses

Preliminary Analyses

Preliminary examinations and data cleansing were performed using R statistical software. The skewness and kurtosis values for all variables were within the acceptable range of −2 to 2 (George and Mallery 2010). Univariate outliers were winsorized to be within three median absolute deviations from the median, following the procedure recommended by Leys et al. (2013), to reduce the influence of extreme values while preserving all cases. The use of the performance package, which provides tools for assessing, comparing, and validating the statistical model (Lüdecke et al. 2021), revealed no multivariate outliers. This approach ensured the integrity of the data set (Leys et al. 2013).

Main Analyses

Multilevel growth curve analyses were conducted in Mplus (Muthén and Muthén 2012). Maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR) was used to prevent non‐normality issues with the data (Hox and Van de Schoot 2013). A multilevel modelling framework (MLM) was chosen to manage missing data and unequally spaced time measures (Burchinal et al. 2006; Hox and Van de Schoot 2013; Singer and Willett 2003). In this modelling framework, the repeated observations are nested within each individual, which makes it possible to study intraindividual changes over time (level‐1; within each worker) but also interindividual changes in baseline scores and growth rates (level‐2; between workers; Heck and Thomas 2015). Interindividual changes can also be predicted by variables of interest. Each worker constitutes a level‐2 observation. Since these analyses can be performed with as few as 30 to 50 level‐2 observations, adequate statistical power was achieved (Burchinal et al. 2006).

Intraindividual growth curves of well‐being at work and burnout were modeled first. Intraindividual growth curves were also modeled for the eight demands and resources considered to be predictors of the psychological health of HSSC workers in the present study. Linear models with all random terms were tested for each growth curve. We then tested the associations between the trajectories of the job demands and resources and psychological health, with the aim of assessing whether the increase or decrease of a predictor was associated with the increase or decrease of the trajectory of psychological health. Finally, we tested the association between the demands and the resources at baseline, on the one hand, and the burnout and well‐being trajectories, on the other.

Results

Well‐Being at Work Growth Curves

We tested a linear model for well‐being at work with all random effects (Figure 1). Model parameters revealed that well‐being at work decreased significantly over time (B = −0.018, p < 0.001). On average, nonprofit workers' well‐being at work decreased by 0.018 points per month (0.22 points per year). Nonprofit workers' well‐being at work started at a level of 4.40. The covariance between the slope and intercept was significant (B = 0.001, p < 0.001), which indicates that nonprofit workers who had a higher level of well‐being at work at baseline showed a faster decrease over the months of the study than those who had a lower level of well‐being at work at baseline.

Linear model of the evolution (time points) of well‐being at work among HSSC workers.

Burnout Growth Curves

We tested a linear model for burnout with all random effects (Figure 2). Model parameters revealed that burnout increased significantly over time (B = 0.033, p < 0.001). On average, nonprofit workers' burnout increased by 0.033 points per month (0.40 points per year). Nonprofit workers' burnout started at a level of 3.53. The covariance between the slope and intercept was not significant (B = 0.001, p = 0.592), which indicates that the evolution of burnout is not associated with its level at baseline. These results indicate a progressive increase in burnout symptoms, which suggests a deterioration in psychological health among community workers during the pandemic.

Job Demands and Resources at Baseline as Predictors of Change in Psychological Health at Work

As an exploratory analysis, we tested the job resources at baseline (i.e., strengths use, colleague and supervisor support, and autonomy) as potential predictors of the trajectory of well‐being at work. We also controlled for job position (subordinate or supervisor). In this model, colleague support (B = 0.16, p < 0.001), supervisor support (B = 0.09, p < 0.001), and strengths use (B = 0.20, p < 0.01) positively and significantly predicted the trajectory of well‐being at work, indicating that when workers perceived these job resources to be more present in their workplace, the decrease of well‐being at work was more pronounced. However, autonomy (B = −0.005, p = 0.720) and job/occupation (B = 0.040, p = 0.349) were not significant predictors of the trajectory of well‐being at work. This indicates that higher levels of perceived strengths use, colleague support, and supervisor support at baseline were associated with steeper declines in well‐being over time.

We tested job demands at baseline (i.e., quantitative workload, dissatisfaction with pay, work guilt and overcommitment) as potential predictors of the trajectory of burnout. We also controlled for job/occupation. In this model, quantitative workload (B = 0.15, p = 0.001), work guilt (B = 0.23, p = 0.001), and overcommitment (B = 0.35, p = 0.003) positively and significantly predicted the trajectory of burnout, indicating that when workers perceived these job demands to be more present in their workplace, the increase of burnout was more pronounced. However, dissatisfaction with pay (B = 0.07, p = 0.073) and job/occupation (B = −0.08, p = 0.486) were not significant predictors of the trajectory of burnout. This indicates that workers who initially reported higher levels of quantitative workload, work guilt, and overcommitment were more likely to experience a steeper increase in burnout symptoms over time.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to assess the temporal evolution of well‐being, burnout, and their related demands and resources among HSSC workers during the COVID‐19 pandemic. A significant decrease of well‐being and an increase of burnout were observed over time, confirming our first two hypotheses. These findings are consistent with previous research that highlights the detrimental effects of prolonged stress and increased workload on the psychological health of healthcare workers (e.g., Dall'Ora et al. 2020).

The observed trends are especially pertinent to the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic, corroborating other research that has documented the heightened challenges faced by healthcare and social service workers during this global crisis (Gur et al. 2022; Yella and Dmello 2022; Wu et al. 2022). Our research confirms these results for the nonprofit community sector. Findings highlight the importance of monitoring HSSC workers over time to collect valuable insights into the long‐term impacts of health crises on their psychological health and the potential for their recovery. Future research should investigate whether levels of burnout and well‐being have returned to pre‐pandemic baselines following the resolution of the crisis.

Interestingly, only a few job demands and resources changed significantly over time during the pandemic, partially supporting hypotheses 3 and 4. We observed decreases in strengths use, quantitative workload, and dissatisfaction with pay. These changes may reflect the complex and evolving nature of the pandemic‐related adjustments in terms of work structure, expectations, and support systems. The various restrictions imposed by the health crisis and the urgency of the situation may have restricted the latitude these workers had to use their strengths at work. Conversely, the perceived reduction in workload we observed could be explained by increased efficiency, particularly among those who had the opportunity to telework. Previous research has shown that teleworking can be associated with lower perceived workload (Turja et al. 2022).

Finally, one possible explanation for the observed decrease in dissatisfaction with pay over time is the temporary financial incentives provided by the government to HSSC workers during the pandemic. However, this decrease in dissatisfaction may have been short‐lived since subsequent reports have noted that these incentives were often perceived as insufficient, temporary, and sometimes subject to taxation, which may have generated mixed feelings and frustration among workers (SEIU Healthcare 2022; Hapsari et al. 2022).

In line with hypotheses 5 and 6, analyses revealed that a decline in strengths use was associated with a parallel decrease in well‐being. This finding aligns with current literature suggesting that strengths use is associated with increased satisfaction and well‐being at work (Miglianico et al. 2020). Together, these findings highlight the importance of fostering strengths use in organizations by helping workers to identify their strengths (with tools like the VIA survey; VIA Institute on Character n.d.) as well as opportunities to use them. By emphasizing strengths use, organizations may provide a crucial protective factor for employee well‐being, especially during challenging times, such as a global pandemic or other crisis.

Conversely, overcommitment was positively associated with increases in burnout, suggesting that overinvestment in work may contribute to long‐term psychological strain. This finding is in line with the recovery paradox (Sonnentag 2018), which posits that workers are the least likely to engage in recovery activities (e.g., rest, detachment) precisely when they need them most. Recovery activities can include participating in hobbies, spending time with family and friends, or practicing relaxation techniques—these are all activities that help individuals mentally and emotionally disconnect from work‐related concerns. In line with the recovery paradox, during a period of stress such as COVID‐19, some HSSC workers may have been less inclined to engage in recovery activities and more inclined to invest themselves in their work.

Unexpectedly, higher baseline levels of job resources (e.g., colleague support, supervisor support, strengths use) were associated with steeper declines in well‐being. While this may seem counterintuitive, one possible explanation is that workers who initially benefited from strong relational and motivational resources may have been more affected when these were disrupted during the pandemic. For instance, the sudden loss of informal peer interactions, the shift to virtual work, and reduced opportunities for collaboration may have disproportionately affected those who relied heavily on social connections at work. This hypothesis warrants further investigation, but it is consistent with previous research suggesting that disruptions to social communities can be particularly challenging for socially connected individuals (Gur et al. 2022). Another possible explanation lies in the statistical phenomenon of regression to the mean, as individuals with higher levels of initial well‐being may have been more likely to show greater declines over time regardless of external conditions. Future research will be needed to determine whether this pattern can be replicated in other samples and contexts.

From a practical standpoint, these findings emphasize that interventions should be considered at multiple levels to support the psychological health of HSSC workers. At the individual level, it is important to identify and support at‐risk workers—that is, not only those with high demands and low resources, but also those with high initial engagement who may be more sensitive to change. Organizations may consider implementing routine mental health screenings or resource assessments (e.g., validated checklists, brief psychosocial tools). Offering access to peer support groups, reflective practice spaces, and strengths‐based coaching can also help sustain well‐being. At the supervisory level, training managers to recognize early signs of overcommitment and burnout, such as excessive overtime, emotional exhaustion, or difficulty detaching from work, may be key. Guidelines like the Psychological Health and Safety in the Workplace standard (CSA Group & the Bureau de normalisation du Québec 2022) could serve as the basis for action. Recent evidence also highlights the importance of specific management practices in supporting remote workers' psychological health in the context of the COVID‐19 crisis; these include work structure, flexibility, and consideration (Bouchard and Meunier 2022). Integrating these approaches into community‐based settings may help mitigate risks of burnout and reinforce workers' sense of connection and support.

At the organizational level, structural policies such as ensuring workload balance between team members, encouraging recovery time (e.g., implementing a right‐to‐disconnect policy), and offering formal recognition mechanisms (e.g., acknowledging employees' efforts during meetings) could buffer the impact of chronic demands. Moreover, during crises, contingency planning should include strategies to maintain essential resources like supervision, peer communication, and workload distribution.

Beyond individual, interpersonal, and organizational dynamics, several recent Quebec‐based studies point to structural factors that may have intensified the psychological risks faced by community workers during the pandemic. These include unstable funding, weak employment protections, and managerial practices that often exacerbate job precarity and emotional overload (Fauvel et al. 2020; Fauvel et al. 2024). While autonomy and mission‐driven engagement are often described as protective resources among HSSC workers, these same features can become stressors when embedded in precarious or under‐resourced environments (Fauvel et al. 2020). Supporting HSSC workers' well‐being therefore requires not only interventions at the individual level, but also systemic changes to how the sector is funded, managed, and politically recognized.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, all data were self‐reported, and may therefore be subject to common method bias and social desirability effects. Future research could incorporate other indicators of psychological health that are particularly important for workers in the health and social services sector (e.g., compassion fatigue), as well as objective measures, such as physiological markers (e.g., cortisol levels), behavioral indicators (e.g., work attendance or turnover), or multi‐source data, including peer or supervisor ratings. Second, although the sample is diverse, it only represents community workers in the province of Quebec, Canada. It would be interesting to carry out further studies to see if these results also apply to other regions or activity sectors. Third, while this study focused on specific job demands and resources, other variables, such as leadership, organizational culture, and individual coping mechanisms, may also influence psychological health and merit further exploration. Fourth, although individuals on medical leave were eligible to participate in the study, some of them may not have received the invitations to the follow‐up surveys, particularly if these were sent to their professional email addresses. This may have led to an underrepresentation of workers experiencing higher levels of psychological distress or burnout. In this regard, participants retained in the longitudinal analyses reported slightly higher levels of autonomy and well‐being at baseline than those not retained, suggesting a potential self‐selection bias that may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study contributes to the growing body of literature on occupational health in healthcare and social services, particularly in the context of global crises. It provides valuable insights into the temporal evolution of well‐being and burnout among HSSC workers during the COVID‐19 pandemic, as well as the influence of fluctuating job demands and resources. Our findings highlight the important challenges faced by this essential workforce, particularly the decrease of well‐being and increase of burnout over time. It is particularly concerning that this population, which already faced occupational stress before the pandemic, has experienced such marked changes in their psychological health and work environments. Addressing the well‐being of these essential workers is crucial not only for their individual health and the quality of the care they provide to vulnerable populations, but also for the overall resilience and well‐being of society.
