Targeting tumor-associated bacteria in digestive system cancers: carcinogenic mechanisms and nano-regulate platform design
Authors: Hongyu Liu, Xin Wang, QI Xi, Tianren Li, Hanwei Huang, Jia Zhao, Lin Guan, Funan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor-associated bacteria (TAB) and their products can reprogram host signaling and metabolism, disrupt tissue homeostasis, and remodel antitumor immunity, collectively creating a cooperative protumor ecosystem. Accumulating evidence indicates that TAB promote the initiation and progression of digestive system malignancies and can also influence therapeutic outcomes. Although conventional bacterial modulation approaches can alter the composition and functions of TAB, their clinical application is hindered by major limitations, including poor in vivo stability, limited targeting specificity, difficulty in penetrating mucosal and tumor barriers, insufficient local persistence, and safety concerns related to dysbiosis. Nanotechnology-based engineering strategies—such as introducing specific ligands, designing biomimetic and stimuli-responsive coatings, improving adhesion and sustained-release performance, and integrating multifunctional modules—can help overcome these constraints, enabling selective elimination of TAB or rebalancing of bacterial communities to enhance anticancer efficacy. This review summarizes the carcinogenic mechanisms of TAB in digestive system cancers and outlines the features and shortcomings of conventional bacterial modulation tools. We further integrate recent advances in nanotechnology-based delivery platforms. Finally, we discuss considerations for translation, emphasizing that multifunctional systems must balance mechanistic synergy with manufacturability and safety, and that future progress may depend on multi-omics–guided patient stratification and modular, biocompatible platform design.

Introduction

The human body harbors an estimated 100 trillion microorganisms colonizing the gastrointestinal tract, skin, oral cavity, and almost every organ and tissue [1]. These communities consist of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, with bacteria representing the major component. In a 70 kg adult, the total bacterial load is approximately 3.9 × 10¹³, a magnitude comparable to the number of human cells [2, 3]. Among these microbial communities, gut bacteria constitute the largest and most functionally diverse population. They play indispensable roles in nutrient metabolism, drug metabolism, and the regulation of inflammation and immune status [4–7], and their functions are closely linked to the maintenance or disruption of homeostasis across multiple organ systems [8, 9], particularly within the digestive tract [10–12].

Under physiological conditions, gut bacteria are spatially segregated from host tissues by the mucosal barrier. Dysbiosis, characterized by the overgrowth and heightened activity of pathobionts, can damage this barrier and allow bacteria to translocate into adjacent tissues, then spread to distant sites through the bloodstream, lymphatic circulation, or anatomical cavities [13–15]. Once barrier integrity is compromised, gut bacteria and their products—including cytotoxins, outer-membrane vesicles, and diverse metabolites—can induce genomic and epigenomic alterations and disrupt the balance between epithelial proliferation and apoptosis. In parallel, these signals amplify inflammatory pathways while suppressing antitumor immunity, collectively fostering protumor conditions within the gut and extraintestinal organs [16–18]. The resulting shifts in tissue homeostasis reshape cellular behavior and can alter responsiveness to therapy [19–21]. Bacteria that directly or indirectly contribute to tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, or therapeutic response are collectively referred to as tumor-associated bacteria (TAB) [22–24]. They may colonize within tumors or on mucosal surfaces. The recognition of TAB has reshaped classical views of oncogenesis, highlighting a form of bacteria–cancer “symbiotic pathogenesis” and providing a biological basis for new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Modulating the composition and functional activity of TAB offers opportunities to interrupt carcinogenic processes, correct dysregulated host states, and reprogram the tumor microenvironment.

Current approaches to regulate these bacterial communities rely primarily on antibiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), prebiotics, and probiotics. In addition, bacteriophages and inorganic nanoparticles have attracted interest as tools for reshaping bacterial community structure and activity, thereby expanding the range of bacteria-directed interventions in oncology [25–28]. Despite their promise, these agents still face notable translational constraints. For oral administration, the gastrointestinal milieu is highly hostile: steep pH gradients and digestive enzymes can degrade or inactivate payloads, and first-pass hepatic clearance may further diminish effective exposure. Furthermore, epithelial tight junctions, the mucus layer, and other mucosal barriers restrict contact with resident gut bacteria and epithelial cells, collectively blunting the efficacy of orally administered agents [29, 30].For intravenous administration, interactions with blood components lead to protein-corona formation and opsonization, while phagocytic clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system markedly reduces circulation time [30, 31].Within tumors, aberrant vasculature, dense stroma, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure generate heterogeneous perfusion and limited permeability, constraining intratumoral accumulation and penetration of bacteria-modulating agents [28, 31, 32]. Bacteria can further attenuate therapeutic activity by forming biofilms and deploying multidrug efflux pumps. Overall, the direct application of bacteria-modulating agents is often limited by poor tissue accumulation and suboptimal selectivity, with relatively modest ability to distinguish harmful taxa from commensal counterparts; when used as monotherapies, these strategies frequently show limited efficacy. Moreover, given their potential toxicity or pathogenicity, such interventions may pose risks of uncontrolled infection in vulnerable individuals or precipitate new dysbiosis, ultimately leading to unfavorable clinical outcomes [33–35]. Together, these constraints suggest that the success of TAB modulation is determined not only by antibacterial or microbiota-regulating activity itself, but also by how effectively such agents can be delivered, protected, and localized in vivo.

Nanotechnology offers a flexible formulation framework to address these delivery-related barriers and to improve the precision and efficacy of TAB-oriented intervention. Nanoscale carriers can exploit the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect to passively accumulate to some extent at tumor sites, thereby reducing systemic exposure and toxicity under a fixed total dose. With years of technological development, nanotechnology now allows optimization of materials, structures, and contact interfaces to improve the biocompatibility and bioavailability of active components. By tuning carrier particle size, morphology, and surface charge, and by introducing “stealth” coatings, it is possible to prolong circulation, reduce protein-corona formation, and limit clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Ligand modification further enables carriers to recognize tumor cells, tumor stroma, and TAB, promoting deeper transport of drugs into tumor tissue, enhancing active uptake by target cells, and exerting more localized effects on specific bacterial niches. In addition, by designing specialized coatings and physicochemical linkers, taking advantage of pathological cues within tumors or TAB-related enzymes, and combining suitable external-field stimulation, nanoplatforms can be engineered to achieve spatiotemporally controlled “on-demand” release and, in some cases, real-time monitoring of therapeutic responses. For environment-sensitive bacteria modulators and drugs, such as probiotics and prebiotics, nanoencapsulation and biomimetic coatings provide physical protection and microenvironmental buffering, helping maintain their biological activity and colonization capacity. Overall, nanotechnology shows considerable potential for overcoming physiological and pathological barriers and for improving the antitumor effects of bacteria modulators and conventional drugs, while providing an engineering framework for tumor treatment strategies based on TAB modulation [36–40].

Carcinogenic mechanisms of tumor-associated bacteria

TAB promote digestive system tumorigenesis through five interconnected dimensions, including sustained proliferative signaling, genomic instability, invasion and metastasis, immune suppression, and chronic inflammation. Although these mechanisms are discussed separately for clarity, they overlap extensively in vivo and together form a self-reinforcing network. Tumor-promoting effects mediated by virulence proteins and bacterial metabolites

This first level mainly corresponds to the direct signaling layer summarized in Fig. 3. TAB can directly drive epithelial proliferation and survival through virulence proteins and metabolites, with Fusobacterium nucleatum representing one of the most illustrative examples. Its Fap2 recognizes galactose/N-acetylgalactosamine (Gal/GalNAc) residues on host cells, promotes bacterial binding and entry, and induces pancreatic cancer cells to secrete granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and interleukin-8 (IL-8), thereby activating phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)–protein kinase B (AKT) signaling and enhancing proliferation [41]. This effect may be further amplified through the establishment of a pro-inflammatory paracrine environment that favors sustained tumor growth [42]. In parallel, FadA binds E-cadherin and activates β-catenin, while related downstream effects converge on the Toll-Like Receptor 4 (TLR4)/MyD88/nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) axis, resulting in miR-21 upregulation, RASA1 suppression, and reinforcement of the Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) cascade [43, 44]. Additional adhesin-linked changes, including RadD-associated matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) induction, BIRC3-mediated apoptosis resistance [45–47]. and KRAS/Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK)/STAT3-dependent strengthening of the DHX15 interaction, further enhance bacterial adherence, inflammatory output, and reduced sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil [48].

Bacteroides fragilis toxin (BFT) similarly couples anti-apoptotic protection with proliferative drive. It activates the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)–FoxO3a axis and autophagy-related programs to delay cell death, while also engaging MAPK, Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2–Related Factor 2 (NRF2), STAT3, and E-cadherin/β-catenin-related signaling to support epithelial proliferation and stress adaptation under inflammatory and oxidative conditions [49–53].

Similar effects are observed in other bacteria. Salmonella reprograms β-catenin, AKT, STAT3, and MAPK signaling through AvrA, SopB, and SopE-family effectors [54–58]. Porphyromonas gingivalis activates β-catenin/TCF/LEF programs through gingipains and suppresses apoptosis via PI3K/AKT [59–61], whereas Peptostreptococcus stomatis drives proliferation through the FBA–integrin α6β4–ERBB2–MAPK axis and also reduces colorectal cancer sensitivity to cetuximab and vemurafenib [62]. By contrast, Campylobacter jejuni mainly perturbs epithelial renewal through cytolethal distending toxin (CDT)-mediated CDK1 disruption and G2/M arrest [63]. Additional taxa, including Escherichia coli, Peptostreptococcus anaerobius, Helicobacter bilis, commensal Clostridium, and Shigella flexneri, further support tumor growth through IL-17 C-, Sterol Regulatory Element-Binding Protein 2 (SREBP2)-, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-, mechanistic Target of Rapamycin Complex 1 (mTORC1)/Sonic Hedgehog (SHH)-, and DNA damage/PI3K-related mechanisms, respectively [64–68].

Despite their different molecular entry points, these bacteria broadly strengthen proliferative signaling, improve stress tolerance and reduce sensitivity to cytotoxic or targeted therapy.

Tumor-promoting effects at the transcriptional level

The second level highlighted in Fig. 3 involves transcriptional reprogramming that stabilizes these abnormal proliferative phenotypes. In CRC, F. nucleatum activates the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis and reshapes host RNA networks. Downregulation of miR-18a* and miR-4802 relieves inhibition of ULK1 and ATG7, thereby enhancing autophagy and chemoresistance [69]. Upregulated miR-31 further targets STX12 and EIF4EBP1/2, impairing autophagosome–lysosome fusion, facilitating intracellular bacterial persistence, and promoting cell-cycle progression [70]. F. nucleatum also induces miR-130a-3p, which suppresses AMPKα/β1 and promotes SREBF2-dependent cholesterol synthesis, thereby supporting metabolic reprogramming and CRC progression [71]. In addition, increased miR-4474 and miR-4717 repress CREBBP and further promote proliferation [72, 73], while enhanced exosomal loading of hsa_circ_0004085 stabilizes GRP78 and attenuates ER-stress–associated apoptosis [74]. Similarly, ETBF activates the BFAL1–miR-155/miR-200a–RHEB axis to stimulate mTOR [75], whereas pks + E. coli enhances p53 SUMOylation through miR-20a-5p-dependent SENP1 suppression, thereby generating a growth-promoting secretory environment [76].

Tumor-promoting effects at the epigenetic level

The third level in Fig. 3 emphasizes epigenetic remodeling. F. nucleatum and H. hathewayi upregulate DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) and DNMT3A, causing promoter hypermethylation of tumor-suppressor genes such as CDX2, MLH1, and CDKN2A [77, 78]. They also activate the PLA2G4A/COX-2 axis and promote ENO1-IT1/KAT7-related histone acetylation [77, 79]. In addition, METTL3-dependent N6-methyladenosine (m6A) processing enhances miR-4717-3p production and suppresses MAP2K4, whereas BFT induces NFAT5 and JMJD2B to remove repressive marks at the NANOG promoter [80, 81].These findings indicate that TAB can stabilize abnormal proliferative states not only through acute signaling events, but also through methylation, acetylation, and RNA modification.

In regulating aberrant cell proliferation, TAB not only provide additional growth stimuli but also superimpose virulence factor–driven receptor signaling, transcriptional rewiring, and epigenetic alterations onto pre-existing oncogenic events. Through the coordination and crosstalk of these mechanisms, they enable tumor cells to escape homeostatic cell-cycle control, increase proliferative activity and tolerance to stress, and gradually adapt to cytotoxic therapies. These convergence helps explain interpatient differences in tumor aggressiveness and treatment responsiveness, and may provide candidate biomarkers for identifying patients at high risk of rapid progression and chemoresistance [82, 83].

Induction of genomic instability and damage

As shown in Fig. 4, genomic instability is a central driver of malignant transformation and progression, and the cumulative burden of DNA damage provides its material basis. When endogenous repair pathways are insufficient to counter persistent insults to the genome, molecular events such as base mismatches and double-strand breaks arise at critical loci, leading to aberrant activation of proto-oncogenes or loss of tumor-suppressor function. Bacteria can accelerate this process by deploying virulence factors that directly assault DNA, by provoking oxidative stress, or by perturbing host DNA-damage repair systems, thereby fostering the accumulation of mutations and chromosomal abnormalities. These gene-level disturbances not only promote oncogene activation and tumor-suppressor silencing but also amplify intratumoral heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance.

Direct DNA damage

The first route shown in Fig. 4 involves direct structural injury to DNA. Oral microorganisms generate acetaldehyde during ethanol oxidation and glucose metabolism, and alcohol intake markedly increases local acetaldehyde exposure in the oral cavity. More importantly, pks-harboring E. coli synthesize colibactin, which forms irreversible DNA interstrand cross-links, induces γH2AX-marked damage, activates the ATM–Chk2–Cdc25C checkpoint, and causes G2/M arrest [84–86]. Even when replication-stress responses and the Fanconi anemia pathway are activated, persistent lesions may remain and can further induce the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), thereby indirectly promoting inflammation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), stemness, and therapy resistance [87]. Similarly, CDT from C. jejuni induces DNA double-strand breaks through the DNase I-like activity of CdtB [88]. Thus, direct DNA injury may act not only as an initiating event, but also as an amplifier of subsequent inflammatory and phenotypic remodeling.

Oxidative stress–mediated damage

The second route in Fig. 4 highlights oxidative stress as an indirect source of genomic injury. H. pylori delivers CagA through its type IV secretion system and induces reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated DNA damage [89]; A. odontolyticus releases membrane vesicles that localize to mitochondria, inhibit electron transport chain activity, and trigger ROS accumulation, DNA double-strand breaks, and p53/p21 checkpoint activation [90]. S. anginosus links oxidative DNA damage to inflammatory transformation through SAA antigen-induced NO and COX-2 overproduction [91, 92], whereas BFT connects infection, chronic inflammation, and tumorigenesis through polyamine catabolism, spermine oxidase (SMO) upregulation and H₂O₂ production [93]. These observations indicate that TAB can convert transient molecular injury into more durable genomic alterations by maintaining an oxidative microenvironment.

Disruption of DNA repair systems

The third route summarized in Fig. 4 involves weakening of the repair systems that normally preserve genome integrity. F. nucleatum suppresses Ku70 and p53, thereby increasing DNA double-strand breaks and weakening damage repair [94]. H. pylori CagA upregulates miR-150-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-3163 while downregulating mismatch-repair proteins and POLD3, thereby promoting replication stress and microsatellite instability (MSI) [95]; related Activin A receptor type I–IRF3–POLD signaling further contributes to mutational burden accumulation [96]. E. faecalis activates ATM/ATR–Chk1/Chk2 while generating extracellular superoxide and COX-2-dependent lipids that suppress poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP-1) and increase chromosomal instability [97, 98]. Enteropathogenic E. coli can likewise deplete MSH2 and MLH1 at the post-translational level and further increase spontaneous mutation rates in the presence of elevated ROS [99].

Taken together, these observations indicate that TAB can disturb genome integrity at multiple levels, thereby converting repairable damage into fixed mutations and chromosomal abnormalities and ultimately accelerates the accumulation of oncogenic alterations and facilitating the emergence of therapy-resistant clones.

Promotion of invasion and metastasis

As shown in Fig. 5, TAB are increasingly recognized as drivers of metastasis. Through adhesins, secreted toxins, metabolites, and exosome-mediated signals, these bacteria initiate, amplify, and sustain epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity, stimulate angiogenic and stromal remodeling, and make local immunity toward a pro-migratory phenotype. In parallel, they erode mucosal and vascular barriers, allowing bacterial products and cancer cells to traverse normally sites and colonize to distant sites. Highlighting the bacteria–host interaction as a target for limiting metastatic progression.

Regulation of EMT and enhanced motility

The first dimension highlighted in Fig. 5 is the acquisition of a motile and invasive phenotype through EMT-related remodeling. TAB can drive this transition by engaging pattern-recognition receptors and oncogenic signaling pathways, thereby inducing EMT programs, reshaping the cytoskeleton, and endowing tumor cells with stem cell–like properties. A broad inflammatory route is represented by LPS-mediated TLR4 activation. By stimulating inflammatory cytokine production and stromal angiogenic signaling, this pathway supports EMT and cancer stem cell (CSC)-like phenotypes; correspondingly, high TLR4 expression is associated with microvascular invasion, early recurrence, and poor prognosis in HCC [100, 101].

Fusobacterium nucleatum and Bacteroides fragilis provides a representative example of how bacterial colonization can be coupled to EMT plasticity and invasive progression, its SusD/RagB–Notch1 axis activates Notch signaling, whereas Dps-induced CCL2/CCL7 secretion strengthens prometastatic inflammatory cues that facilitate EMT and invasion [102, 103]. In parallel, depletion of miR-122-5p in exosomes relieves repression of FUT8 and enhances TGF-β1/Smads signaling, while the miR4435-2HG–AKT2–SNAI1 axis further stabilizes EMT-associated transcriptional programs and may also reinforce CSC-like traits [104–106]. Similar EMT-promoting effects are also observed in oral and gastric pathogens. P. gingivalis promotes EMT through PAR2/PAR4–ERK1/2–ETS1, HSP27, and NF-κB signaling, together with PI3K/AKT-mediated GSK3β inhibition [107–109]. H. pylori also induces gastric epithelial EMT and increases CD44 expression through CagA–ERK/JNK signaling [110]. Overall, despite different initiating signals, these bacteria converge on enhancement of migratory, invasive, and stem cell–like phenotypes.

Construction of a metastatic microenvironment

Figure 5 further indicates that metastatic spread is supported not only by tumor-cell plasticity, but also by construction of permissive local and distant microenvironments. F. nucleatum promotes translational activation of EMT transcription factors and enhances secretion of IL-8 and CXCL1, thereby shaping a prometastatic niche [111, 112]; in oral tumors, P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum can act synergistically through TLR4-related inflammatory and EMT-associated signaling [113]. Within the gut–liver axis, E. coli disrupts the gut vascular barrier via a VirF1-dependent type III secretion system, facilitates portal dissemination, and promotes formation of a liver premetastatic niche characterized by inflammatory-cell infiltration, increased vascular permeability, and TNF-α/TGF-β conditioning [114]. METTL3 reduction and stabilization of KIF26B mRNA further connect bacterial signals to cytoskeletal remodeling and metastasis [115]. Thus, bacterial promotion of metastasis is not occurred in the primary site, but also involves preconditioning of distant organs for disseminated cells.

Disruption of mucosal and vascular barriers

The barrier-related component of Fig. 5 emphasizes how TAB lower the threshold for dissemination across normally restrictive tissue compartments. Mucosal and vascular barriers normally restrict the passage of bacteria and malignant cells across tissue compartments. TAB can weaken these physical and signaling boundaries by altering adhesion molecules, stem cell function, thereby facilitating translocation of bacteria and tumor cells and lowering the threshold for distant colonization.

F. nucleatum strengthens tumor cell–endothelium adhesion and promotes extravasation through the ALPK1/NF-κB/ICAM1 axis [116]. E. faecalis impairs epithelial repair through TyrDC-derived tyramine and suppression of cAMP–PKA and Wnt/β-catenin signaling in Lgr5⁺ stem cells [117]. H. pylori can additionally induce macrophage CCL3 through JAK1–STAT1 signaling, thereby contributing to gastric barrier disruption [118].

Overall, TAB promote metastasis through coordinated effects on EMT plasticity, niche construction, and barrier injury. Clinically, these findings suggest that TAB-related metastatic signaling may be linked to microvascular invasion, early recurrence, organ-specific dissemination, and adverse prognosis. Accordingly, TAB and TAB-associated host responses may help identify high-risk patients and reveal actionable targets for limiting dissemination and recurrence.

Induction of an immunosuppressive microenvironment

As shown in Fig. 6, TAB are key architects of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. By secreting virulence factors and metabolites, releasing exosome-borne signals, and hijacking host receptors, they reprogram innate and adaptive immunity—upregulating immune checkpoints, driving functional exhaustion of effector T and NK cells, and skewing myeloid lineages toward M2 macrophages and MDSCs. In parallel, chemokine networks and stromal crosstalk sustain the recruitment and maintenance of suppressive cell populations, establishing a circuitry of immune suppression. The resulting “immune desert” undermines immune surveillance, accelerates clonal selection and metastatic dissemination, and directly diminishes the responsiveness and durability of immunotherapy.

Immune checkpoint regulation

The first aspect showed in Fig. 6 is amplification of checkpoint signaling that blunts immune cells activity. Through virulence factor–driven signaling in tumor cells and lymphocytes, they increase the expression and activity of checkpoint molecules, thereby strengthening inhibitory signaling and restricting the activation and effector functions of cytotoxic T cells and NK cells. F. nucleatum-Dps promotes PD-L1 transcription through ATF3, while neutrophil-associated PD-L1 induction and IL-17 A-related signaling further impair T-cell function [119, 120]. Methylobacter spp. are associated with reduced TGF-β signaling, impaired TRM maintenance, and increased PD-1 and CTLA-4 expression in CD8⁺ TRM cells [121]. In gastric cancer, H. pylori CagA activates epithelial SHH signaling and induces PD-L1, thereby restricting CTL proliferation and cytotoxicity; in pancreatic tissue, E. faecalis and E. coli can breach the gut barrier, promote M2 polarization, and elevate PD-1 expression [122]. Together, these observations indicate that local inflammatory signaling can be redirected into a checkpoint-dominated state of immune tolerance.

Reprogramming of immune cell function and phenotype

Beyond checkpoint regulation, Fig. 6 also emphasizes broader phenotype reprogramming of Immune cells. TAB affect immune-cell metabolism, survival, and differentiation. By rewiring signaling pathways and metabolic fluxes in macrophages, T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells, they tilt the balance from effector and tissue-resident surveillance toward suppressive subsets that consolidate local immune exhaustion.

F. nucleatum enhances glucose uptake and lactate accumulation in macrophages, thereby promotes GLUT1-dependent lactate production in tumor cells, and the resulting lactate-rich microenvironment facilitates macrophage M2-like polarization [123]. Its virulence proteins also directly suppress effector lymphocytes: CbpF binds CEACAM on T cells, whereas Fap2 binds TIGIT/CEACAM1 and reduces NK-cell degranulation and T-cell cytotoxicity [124]. It can further induce apoptosis in PBMCs and PMNs and stimulate macrophage IDO expression and kynurenine production, thereby further suppressing T-cell function [125, 126]. More broadly, gut bacterial products can activate inflammatory dendritic cells, promote IL-23 release, polarize γδT17 cells, and recruit PMNs and MDSCs, thereby establishing a layered suppressive circuit [127]. DCA and LTA further reinforce immune evasion through COX-2/PGE2-dependent signaling [128].

Recruitment of immunosuppressive cells

A further component highlighted in Fig. 6 is selective recruitment and maintenance of suppressive immune subsets within the tumor microenvironment. LPS from gram-negative bacteria induces epithelial CCL2 and attracts monocyte-like macrophages [129], whereas classical NF-κB signaling links tumor-cell invasiveness with macrophage recruitment through miR-1322/CCL20-related mechanisms [130]. Similarly, F. nucleatum-driven CXCL1, IL-8, and CXCL2 signaling supports migration, M2 polarization, and expression of immunosuppressive mediators [131], while BFT promotes IL-17 production and establishes a distal colonic CXCL1/2/5 gradient that selectively recruits CXCR2⁺ MDSCs [132, 133]. In this situation suppressive populations gradually become dominant within the local immune ecology.

Through these three interdependent dimensions, TAB potentiate immune checkpoint signaling, rewire the function and phenotype of innate and adaptive immune cells, and sustain the preferential recruitment of immunosuppressive populations. This remodeling converts an initially effective antitumor response into a chronically tolerogenic state, in which effector cells are progressively silenced while suppressive cells predominate. Clinically, these findings support the view that TAB composition and TAB-induced immune phenotypes may serve as candidate biomarkers for predicting response to immune checkpoint blockade and other immunotherapies. They also suggest that bacterial modulation may represent a complementary strategy for improving the durability and consistency of antitumor immune responses.

Induction of an inflammatory microenvironment

Figure 7 organizes TAB-associated inflammatory remodeling into two linked stages, namely initiation of inflammatory signaling at mucosal surfaces and long-term maintenance of chronic protumor inflammation. Beyond acting as key triggers of the inflammatory microenvironment, bacterial communities can sustain pro-inflammatory conditions through multiple mechanisms: activating host pattern recognition receptors and initiating inflammatory cascades; recruiting and reprogramming immune cells to secrete pro-inflammatory mediators; disturbing metabolic homeostasis to induce oxidative stress; and remodeling the extracellular matrix to interfere with tissue repair, thereby promoting the transition from normal epithelium to precancerous lesions. Persistent activation of pattern recognition receptors, chronic stress on the epithelial barrier, and the recruitment of inflammatory myeloid and lymphoid cells, together with dysregulated cytokine and chemokine networks, tightly link infection and tissue damage to tumor growth.

Initiation of the inflammatory response

The first process reflected in Fig. 7 involves the initiation of inflammatory cascades through epithelial injury, microbial products, and early immune activation. TAB engage epithelial and PRRs, thereby triggering signaling cascades that disturb epithelial homeostasis and establish a pro-inflammatory milieu in the stomach, colorectum, and oral cavity. H. pylori activate SHP1/SHP2 through the HopQ–CEACAM axis and facilitates CagA translocation, thereby driving cytoskeletal remodeling, IL-8 release, and aberrant NF-κB/STAT3 activation [134]. Chronic infection can also induce hypermethylation of GPR37 and NPTX2, changes associated with neutrophil and monocyte infiltration and supportive of the clinical value of H. pylori eradication [135]. S. anginosus promotes aberrant proliferation and atrophic gastritis through the TMPC–ANXA2–MAPK axis [136].

Within colorectal epithelium, F. nucleatum initiates inflammatory remodeling through multiple routes. FadA-associated epithelial signaling amplifies both pro-proliferative and pro-inflammatory programs [137], whereas its extracellular vesicles suppress miR-574-5p, relieve repression of CARD3, and enhance LC3-II and p62 turnover, thereby promoting hyperactivated autophagy, tight-junction disruption, and barrier leakiness [138]. Its interaction with CD46 further mediates C3b cleavage and activates epithelial signaling, resulting in upregulation of IL-6, IL-8, and MMP-9 [139]. In the oral niche, P. gingivalis rapidly induces ROS and activates JAK2-dependent inflammatory signaling, thereby aggravating periodontal inflammation [140].

Sustaining chronic inflammation

Figure 7 further emphasizes that once acute inflammation is established, how TAB drive its transition to a chronic state via toxins, metabolic modulators, quorum sensing-dependent community behaviors, and cytokine/chemokine networks. TAB can promote its transition to a chronic state through toxins, metabolic modulators, and quorum-sensing-dependent community behavior. E. faecalis hijacks FABP2 and activates quorum-sensing signaling, thereby promoting biofilm formation, gut dysbiosis, and exacerbation of inflammation [141]. In C. jejuni, CdtB acts as a PIP3 phosphatase and activates GSK3β, thereby contributing to pro-inflammatory cytokine production, DNA damage, and G2 arrest [142].ETBF links chronic inflammation to post-transcriptional and epigenetic dysregulation through miR-149-3p suppression, PHF5A upregulation, SF3b stabilization, KAT2A aberrant splicing, and impaired pri-miR-149 maturation, while also promoting Th17 differentiation and elevating IL-17 A and TNF-α [143].

Along the oral–gastrointestinal axis, P. gingivalis reinforces chronic inflammation through protease-driven cytokine amplification and neutrophil-centered remodeling. Its HbR activates p38 MAPK and ERK1/2 and promotes nuclear translocation of ATF-2, CREB, and NF-κB p65, thereby increasing IL-8 expression, neutrophil recruitment, and inflammatory tissue damage [144]. After colonizing pancreatic tumors, it induces S100A8/S100A9 and activates the CXCL1/CXCL2/CXCR2 axis, recruiting TANs and promoting NETs formation through the NE–MPO pathway, thereby establishing a persistent inflammatory milieu that accelerates pancreatic cancer progression [145].

Taken together, these findings suggest that TAB participate in both inflammation initiation and long-term maintenance. By coupling epithelial injury, metabolic stress, and immune-cell reprogramming with dense cytokine and chemokine networks, they help convert transient mucosal insults into chronic, tumor-promoting inflammation. This dynamic crosstalk provides a mechanistic bridge from infection and tissue damage to malignant transformation, and it also highlights inflammatory signaling and TAB themselves as potential targets for interrupting inflammation-driven carcinogenesis. For instance, in inflammatory bowel disease, bacterial-based detection and modulation have become important tools [146, 147].

Overall, current evidence suggests that TAB promote tumor progression through a multidimensional and tightly coordinated network. Sustained proliferative signaling, genomic instability, metastatic remodeling, immune evasion, and chronic inflammation do not act independently, but intersect across time and space to shape tumor initiation, progression, and treatment response. This extensive crosstalk helps explain why TAB may influence multiple clinical phenotypes simultaneously. Importantly, analogous to the existence of distinct clones or molecular subtypes within the same tumor, the pro-carcinogenic mechanisms associated with TAB exhibit differential effect intensities across diverse patients and cancer types. These mechanisms are shaped by factors including primary tumor site and stage, intratumoral spatial distribution of bacteria, baseline bacterial profiles, host immune status, and prior therapeutic exposure [20, 21, 148, 149]. Accordingly, although specific bacteria and bacteria-associated pathways show promise as diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarkers, their clinical translation still depends on more rigorous validation. H. pylori provide the most mature example for the clinical significance of TAB: its carcinogenic effect was gradually established through epidemiological, mechanistic and intervention studies. suggesting that it is entirely possible to move from mechanism discovery to clinical practice of risk reduction and cancer prevention by identifying and intervening in key carcinogenic bacteria at the appropriate disease stage [150, 151].

Conventional bacterial modulation strategies

Building on the preceding sections, we have outlined how TAB influence oncogenesis, progression, and therapeutic responsiveness through multi-stage, multi-step mechanisms. Effective clearance or control of these bacteria is therefore pivotal to improving outcomes. Current modulation strategies each carry distinct strengths and liabilities: probiotics/postbiotics excel at barrier repair and immune/metabolic calibration but are limited by colonization instability and short in vivo lives; prebiotics expand beneficial taxa at the community level and improve treatment tolerability, yet their efficacy depends on baseline microbiota and diet; many natural products derived from plants, such as alkaloids, polyphenols and saponins, also exhibit the ability to regulate TAB. However, because of their poor hydrophilicity and reactive chemical properties, they are difficult to develop into drugs. Antibiotics can rapidly debulk pathogens but risk broad disruption of commensals and resistance; bacteriophages offer strain-level specificity and anti-biofilm activity, but face in vivo neutralization and delivery hurdles; metal nanoparticles combine antibacterial action with potential tumor-sensitizing effects, while contending with protein coronas, narrow safety windows, and dependence on bacteria state.

Probiotics and postprobiotics

Probiotics are live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer health benefits on the host. They reduce the adhesive and virulence burden of oncogenic bacteria through niche competition, quorum-sensing interference, and biofilm disruption [152], reinforce mucus and tight-junction integrity [153, 154], and modulate innate and adaptive immunity [155]. In addition, through short-chain fatty acids, tryptophan derivatives, and bile-acid metabolites, probiotics regulate metabolic and epigenetic programs that constrain abnormal proliferation and EMT [156, 157]. Certain strains can also bind or metabolize mutagens and procarcinogenic metabolites [158, 159]. These effects can be summarized as partial restoration of microbial homeostasis, suppression of tumor-promoting processes, and improved sensitivity or tolerance to conventional therapies [160, 161].

Postbiotics are inactivated probiotics and their cellular components or metabolites that can directly confer health benefits when administered. Compared with probiotics, postbiotics provide defined molecular entities and more reproducible mechanisms of action [162].SCFAs signal through the GPR41 and GPR43 and inhibit histone deacetylases, thereby linking metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming to anti-inflammatory and barrier-strengthening effects [163, 164]. Teichoic acid and lipoteichoic acid preferentially modulate TLR2 signaling and help interrupt excessive inflammation, chemotaxis, and leukocyte recruitment [165]. Exopolysaccharides and cell-wall fragments act as mucosal-protective and immune-homeostatic ligands, indirectly weakening the virulence expression and ecological advantages of tumor-associated bacteria [166].

In clinical application, probiotics face several limitations, including unstable colonization, low survival after oral administration, and marked inter-individual variability. In immunocompromised individuals, safety events, although uncommon, still warrant attention. These considerations indicate that strain selection, formulation design, and the integration of probiotics with standard anticancer therapies require careful and refined planning. For postbiotics, although they offer better batch-to-batch consistency and safety and do not require long-term colonization in the gut, their short in vivo half-life and limited targeting and retention remain major bottlenecks. These features highlight the importance of stabilization and targeted-delivery strategies for improving the effectiveness of postbiotic-based interventions.

Prebiotics

Prebiotics are defined as substrates that are selectively utilized by host microorganisms and thereby confer health benefits. They are mainly fermentable oligosaccharides and soluble dietary fibers, with a smaller group of non-carbohydrate compounds. Specific beneficial gut bacteria possess rich sets of glycoside hydrolases and fermentation pathways, which allow them to preferentially use these substrates and produce bioactive metabolites that support host health [167–169]. They weaken the ecological niche and virulence programs of TAB, restore epithelial and immune homeostasis, and improve treatment sensitivity and tolerability [170–172]. Several lines of evidence support these effects. In vitro fermentation of rice bran fiber reduced the abundance of colorectal cancer–associated Escherichia coli and Fusobacterium, while increasing beneficial taxa and SCFAs. Diets rich in resistant starch suppressed high-fat diet–induced colonic inflammation and lowered tumor burden [173, 174]. Inulin promoted Bifidobacterium growth and slowed tumor progression in multiple murine model [175]. L-fucose inhibited F. nucleatum–induced EMT, reduced cancer-cell proliferation and invasion [172]. Prebiotics can also reshape the DCA pool and modulate the AhR pathway to help maintain epithelial and immune balance. Prebiotics also appear to have potential as adjuncts in cancer treatment. Higher dietary fiber intake has been associated with enhanced efficacy of immunotherapy and a reduced risk of immune-related colitis [171, 176]. As supportive care during cytotoxic therapy, prebiotic supplementation can help lessen mucositis and promote drug-induced apoptosis [177–180]. During pelvic radiotherapy, inulin plus FOS reduced the number of days with watery stools, whereas perioperative inulin increased butyrate-producing metabolism, lowered pH, reduced ETBF and secondary bile acids, and alleviated mucosal inflammation [181, 182]. Several plant-derived natural products, including polyphenols, alkaloids, and flavonoids, have also been reported to optimize the intestinal bacterial spectrum and contribute to tumor prevention and control [183–185].

In practical application, prebiotics are naturally suited for oral administration and can serve as “bacteria-enzyme–triggered” colon-targeting carriers. They can resist digestion in the upper gastrointestinal tract and are then degraded by colonic bacteria, while at the same time protecting the mucosal barrier in proximal segments and reducing off-target exposure of drugs and functional proteins. Compared with conventional synthetic polymers, prebiotic-based carriers are safe and biodegradable, and their fermentation generates SCFAs that provide both barrier-protective and immune-enhancing effects. In addition, co-administering prebiotics with probiotics as symbiotic formulations can form a “fuel–substrate” system that maintains probiotic activity and improves colonization efficiency. Overall, prebiotics represent a scalable and engineerable regulatory module within TAB, and their translational value may be further amplified by nanotechnology and advanced formulation design.

Antibiotics

Antibiotics remain an important tool for controlling bacterial infections and can also be used to deplete TAB. In addition, several natural organic acids and bioactive antimicrobial peptides have been reported to exert bactericidal or bacteriostatic effects through multiple mechanisms [186, 187].

Accumulating data indicate that using antibiotics to modulate TAB can support the treatment of digestive system cancers. In gastric cancer, H. pylori eradication has become the most mature example: antibiotic-based quadruple therapy is standard of care, and in 1,293 H. pylori-positive patients undergoing curative gastrectomy, postoperative eradication was associated with improved 5-year overall survival [188]. In colorectal cancer, metronidazole reduced gut F. nucleatum and serum succinate, partially restored sensitivity to PD-1 blockade [189].and also inhibited primary tumor growth and liver metastasis by depleting F. nucleatum and other protumor taxa [190, 191]. In Apc^Min/+ mice inoculated with ETBF, oral cefoxitin eliminated ETBF, significantly reduced colon tumor burden, and lowered mucosal IL-17 A, supporting the idea that targeting ETBF can restrain IL-17 A–dependent colon tumorigenesis [192]. In hepatocellular carcinoma models, antibiotics increased microbially derived indole-3-acetic acid, which stimulated γδ T cells to release cytotoxic mediators and enhanced their antitumor activity [193]. In metastatic CRC, antibiotic use during bevacizumab treatment correlated with a higher response rate and longer progression-free survival [194]; depletion of cytidine deaminase–expressing intratumoral Proteobacteria restored gemcitabine sensitivity [195], and in a retrospective series, concomitant antibiotic use improved outcomes in the oxaliplatin group but not the irinotecan group [196, 197].

Notably, many antibiotic regimens still rely on broad-spectrum agents with limited selectivity. Depletion of nonpathogenic commensals during treatment may contribute to unfavorable tumor-related outcomes. In addition, bacterial composition and carcinogenic mechanisms differ between segments of the gastrointestinal tract, so the effects of oral antibiotics on cancer risk and prognosis are likely to vary by anatomical site [198, 199]. Thus, when evaluating how a given antibiotic may influence TAB and tumor biology, it is important to consider not only its antibacterial spectrum but also the route of administration and the host’s baseline bacteria spectrum. Beyond using advanced diagnostic tools and large clinical datasets to guide regimen selection, future approaches could combine antibiotics with modern drug-delivery systems to enhance tumor or bacterial targeting or achieve tissue-specific release. Such strategies may help achieve a more favorable balance between safety and therapeutic benefit [200, 201].

Bacteriophage

Bacteriophages are viruses that specifically infect bacteria rather than human cells. They offer a more precise bacteria-directed strategy than antibiotics. By selectively infecting target bacteria, they can remove protumor taxa together with their virulence factors and harmful metabolites, while bacterial lysis also releases PAMPs that may enhance local immune activation. As a “bacteria-free” alternative, fecal virome transplantation (FVT) also shows potential to remodel the gut microecosystem while reducing the risks associated with bacterial transfer [202, 203].

A variety of bacteriophages have been confirmed to have the ability to kill common TAB. In F .nucleatum–associated mouse CRC models, both engineered and naturally isolated phages ØTCUfn3, FNU1, and FnpΦ02 reduce bacterial burden, slow tumor cell proliferation, and reverse chemotherapy resistance and EMT phenotypes [204–206]. Phages EFA1 and vB_ZEFP, which target E. faecalis, disrupt established biofilms and their growth-promoting signals, thereby suppressing CRC cell proliferation [207, 208]. Phages such as STWB21 and LPST153 limit Salmonella invasion of the liver and spleen, disrupt biofilm structure, and reduce overall Salmonella burden, and are being explored as candidate agents for the control of acute infection [209, 210]. Phage VA7 selectively depletes B. fragilis and alleviates B. fragilis–mediated chemoresistance [211]. The C. difficile phage ΦCD27 reduces toxin production with minimal disruption of commensals [212].While H. pylori phages HPy1R and PhiHp33 remain acid-stable and exhibit bacteriostatic activity in vitro [213, 214]. Phages targeting Enterococcus and Klebsiella are also being explored to modulate hepatic inflammation and metabolic injury [215, 216].

The core advantage of bacteriophages lies in their host specificity: they can selectively clear pathogenic bacteria while causing relatively limited disturbance to resident bacteria. Their highly programmable genomes and flexible capsid-display capacity also make it possible to incorporate antitumor payloads or tumor-homing ligands into phage particles. However, as obligate bacterial viruses with modest stress tolerance, phages still face several bottlenecks, including efficient in vivo delivery, maintenance of activity within complex environment, and neutralization by host antibodies. Designing delivery systems that improve their localization and retention in target tissues while reducing systemic exposure and immune clearance may provide a relatively practical route for translating phage-based interventions targeting the tumor-associated microecology into clinical practice [217, 218].

Metal nanoparticles

Nanoscopic inorganic particles are widely present in foods, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and pigments. Because they have a high specific surface area, good biocompatibility, and modifiable surfaces, and because many of them also possess magnetic, optical, or photothermal properties, they are frequently used in drug-delivery systems and multifunctional therapeutic platforms. A range of pathogen-diagnostic tools and anti-infection systems has also been developed on this basis, suggesting that inorganic nanoparticles can be engineered to interact closely with bacteria.

Silver, copper, and ZnO nanoparticles exhibit broad antibacterial activity against TAB and drug-resistant bacteria such as H. pylori, F. nucleatum, and B. fragilis [219–221]. and silver and copper nanoparticles can suppress multidrug-resistant strains even without extra functionalization [222, 223]. Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles also have favorable biocompatibility and are already approved for clinical iron supplementation and as MRI contrast agents; for example, intravenous ferumoxytol has proven safe and effective for anemia, providing a rationale for exploring intestinal applications [224]. In one study, octahedral Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals tightly adsorbed to bacterial spores and chelated dipicolinic acid, a key component of spore cores, thereby blocking germination, and left commensal bacteria relatively spared [225]. At the community level, metal nanoparticles can also remodel the gut bacteria and help build a more antitumor microecology [225–227]. Some inorganic nanoparticles further act directly on tumor cells by inducing oxidative damage and apoptosis, which provides additional antitumor effects and makes them candidates as adjuvants to chemotherapy or immunotherapy [228–230].

However, in biological fluids, unmodified particles rapidly acquire molecular coronas that weaken effective bacteria contact [231]. Bacterial secretions can further induce changes that resemble drug resistance [232], and bacteria–nanoparticle aggregates are readily phagocytosed by immune cells [233]. The physiological state of the gut bacteria also affects the activity of silver nanoparticles [234]. Toxicity is another important concern. Zinc and iron generally have a wider safety window, but high doses can disrupt trace-element balance and induce oxidative stress. TiO₂ appears relatively safe with short-term exposure, but long-term animal studies suggest pro-inflammatory and tumor-promoting risks [235–238]. Therefore, the value of metal nanoparticles may not mainly lie in their use as independent “broad-spectrum antibacterial agents”, but rather in their modification through engineering plans to alter their biocompatibility and selectivity, and further integration into multifunctional therapeutic platforms. Practical examples include milk-protein–coated silver nanoparticle [239]; Sugar-ligand silver(I) complexes [240], photocatalytic TiO [241] and polyethyleneimine-functionalized ZnO nanoparticles [242], all of which improve antibacterial performance, biocompatibility, or controllability.

In this section, multiple studies demonstrate that bacterial modulation strategies can effectively interfere with TAB-mediated carcinogenic processes discussed in the previous chapter and can further sensitize tumors to conventional therapeutic strategies. However, major obstacles still remain, including poor stability, limited targeting capacity, insufficient penetration across tissue and tumor barriers, inadequate in vivo persistence, and suboptimal biocompatibility. These limitations not only affect therapeutic efficacy but may also disturb the symbiotic bacteria, disrupt mucosal homeostasis or interfere with immune balance. Therefore, it is still necessary to introduce advanced formulations and engineered designs to match the in vivo process of therapeutic media with specific physiological and pathological barriers and treatment processes, or to introduce multifunctional therapeutic modules to exert a synergistic effect.

Harnessing nanotechnology to modulate TAB and enhance cancer therapy

As outlined above, TAB participate in virtually every stage of carcinogenesis, progression, and therapy resistance, yet conventional bacterial modulation strategies are limited by suboptimal targeting to bacteria and tumor tissue, poor in vivo stability, insufficient local retention, and difficulty in crossing tissue barriers—all of which constrain their therapeutic efficacy. To overcome these obstacles and improve the treatment of bacteria-related cancers of the digestive system, nanotechnology provides a series of formulation-level engineering solutions. By tuning nanoparticle size, surface charge, and ligand composition, nanoplatforms can simultaneously recognize tumor cells and TAB while reducing off-target effects; biomimetic membranes and coating strategies further enhance drug stability and immune evasion; pH-, enzyme-, or redox-responsive materials and specialized surface layers enable on-demand drug release in defined microenvironments or under external stimuli; and adhesive scaffolds can be incorporated to prolong retention at sites of disease. By integrating these functions into a single therapeutic platform, nanomedicines can substantially improve both TAB modulation and antitumor efficacy. Enhance targeting and selectivity

In TAB-oriented intervention, precise targeting is the primary principle for enhancing local effective exposure while reducing off-target effects. This is particularly important in digestive system cancers, where the mucus layer, epithelial barriers, and inflammatory remodeling together hinder effective contact between therapeutic agents and target bacteria. Current nanoplatforms improve selectivity mainly through two approaches: receptor-directed recognition of bacterial or host molecules, and biomimetic or homologous coating strategies that promote lesion enrichment. Whether targeting bacteria or tumors, both strategies can enhance intratumoral concentrations and mitigate damage to non-target bacteria, independent of the dosages of bacterial modulators or antitumor agents. Overall, targeting-oriented designs are most valuable when pathogenic taxa, lesion-associated receptors, or bacteria-related products are sufficiently defined, but their broader applicability may still be constrained by interpatient heterogeneity in receptor expression, bacterial localization, and lesion biology.

Molecular recognition and receptor-directed targeting

One representative example is the UBI/PEG/PPa micellar system developed for γ-Proteobacteria-enriched pancreatic tumors. Because intratumoral E. coli-like bacteria can inactivate gemcitabine through cytidine deaminase, this platform uses the antimicrobial peptide UBI to confer selective bacterial binding, and then employs PPa to generate ROS under laser irradiation, thereby disrupting bacterial membrane potential and restoring gemcitabine sensitivity [243]. This design precisely targets the bacteria that affect the efficacy of chemotherapy, highlighting the significant clinical importance of targeting TAB.

The design developed by the Gao research group emphasizes dual targeting of both bacteria and tumors. In this platform, LA, boronic acid–modified oligoethyleneimine, and an oxaliplatin prodrug are assembled through dynamic boronate ester bonds. The phenylboronic acid moiety recognizes sialic acid residues overexpressed on tumor cells, whereas the acidic tumor microenvironment triggers synchronized release of LA and oxaliplatin [244]. The advantage of this design resides in the concurrent occurrence of antitumor and antibacterial effects within the same spatiotemporal context. This not only reduces the TABs burden but also enables precise elimination of tumor cells that are highly colocalized with pathogenic bacteria.

Targeting strategies can also exploit physiological transporters or virulence factor. UreI-targeted nanoparticles improve gastric localization in H. pylori infection while reducing disruption to commensal bacteria [245]. Deoxycholic acid–modified B. subtilis spore-based carriers exploit bile acid transport pathways to enhance transepithelial uptake [246]. Lipid–protamine–DNA nanoparticles encoding LPS-binding proteins shift the target from bacteria themselves to pathogenic bacterial products, thereby attenuating LPS-related immune dysregulation and enhancing antitumor immunity [247].

The recognition targets of such systems typically correspond to well-defined pathophysiological processes, including colonization, virulence signaling, and drug transport. This effectively enhances the control of pathogenic bacteria while improving the efficacy of conventional tumor therapeutic strategies.

Biomimetic membranes and homologous co-targeting

Compared with single-ligand modification, biomimetic membrane strategies enable lesion localization and retention via adhesion or homing behaviors mediated by the “homologous targeting” principle, leveraging natural cell or bacterial membranes. This approach exhibits superior efficiency and biocompatibility.

A representative example is the neutrophil membrane–coated platform co-delivering metronidazole and oxaliplatin. Because inflamed CRC lesions display increased expression of adhesion molecules and active immune-cell recruitment, the neutrophil membrane not only provides partial immune evasion, but also confers inflammatory-site homing and lesion enrichment. As a result, antibacterial and chemotherapeutic agents are simultaneously delivered to the tumor–bacteria interface, thereby reversing F. nucleatum-associated EMT and chemoresistance [248].

Other biomimetic systems support the same design logic. AGS cell membrane–coated PLGA nanoparticles and α-linolenic-acid-modified liposomes improve local targeting to H. pylori by mimicking gastric epithelial adhesion settings or bacterial membrane properties [249, 250]. In CRC, liposomes containing F. nucleatum membrane components and cancer-cell-membrane–coated melittin-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles enhance enrichment at the tumor–bacteria interface while relatively reducing effects on non-target bacteria [251, 252].

These evidences indicate that biomimetic membranes harbor diverse recognition cues and can serve as a biocompatible protective modality for therapeutic agents. Constrained by challenges such as membrane component sourcing, preservation of functional protein activity, and batch consistency, the biomimetic membrane strategy is more suitably applied in scenarios where the membrane itself confers irreplaceable targeting advantages.

Augmenting tumor enrichment and deep tissue penetration

For TAB-targeted therapy, reaching the tumor does not equate to accessing the truly relevant microbial niche. Intratumoral bacteria are frequently localized in hypoperfused, mucus-coated, necrotic, or biofilm-like regions, while tumors themselves exhibit features including heterogeneous vascular permeability, dense stroma, and elevated interstitial pressure. Thus, this section focuses on strategies such as controllable particle size, functional modules, particle coatings, and biological carriers to overcome in vivo transport bottlenecks. Specifically, it describes nanodesign strategies that enable therapeutic components to achieve more uniform and sustained effective exposure at the tumor-bacteria interface. These enrichment- and penetration-oriented strategies are particularly useful when access to the relevant lesion niche is the dominant bottleneck.

Structure tuning and size adaptation

The hyperbranched polyglycerol/CB[7] system carrying oxaliplatin(IV) and lauric acid platform maintains approximately 200 nm through CB[7]-mediated supramolecular assembly during circulation, thereby favoring prolonged circulation and tumor accumulation. After reaching CRC lesions, however, overexpressed spermine competitively disrupts the assembly, causing the particles to further disassemble into smaller units of about 10 nm and thus enhancing intratumoral penetration [253]. The size-tunable nanogel developed by Xie et al. addresses the classical size paradox in drug delivery by first using a larger construct to improve lesion retention and then releasing smaller functional units through local enzyme-triggered degradation to facilitate deeper diffusion and cellular uptake [254]. Chen et al. adopted a more compact strategy by generating silver nanoparticles in situ within dendritic mesoporous silica, enabling chemotherapeutic agents and antibacterial silver ions to be integrated and released within the same nanoscale space [255]. These strategies improve lesion coverage by coordinating selective activation, retention, and deeper intratumoral penetration, making them well suited to dense and heterogeneous TAB-associated tumors.

Coating- and ligand-mediated deep infiltration and adhesion

By modulating the interactions of nanoplatforms with mucus, tumor surfaces, and cell membranes through coating layers, ligands, and interfacial materials, it is possible to improve lesion-surface adhesion, local entry, and effective contact with target bacteria and tumor cells.

Zhao et al. loads therapy agents into mesoporous silica nanoparticles and coating with a metal–polyphenol network and dispersion in carboxymethylcellulose to form a colonic perfusion gel [256]. Under acidic conditions, the polyphenol shell dissociates, exposing the active payload at the tumor surface; meanwhile, carboxymethylcellulose allows the nanosystem to adhere more readily to the mucosa interface, thereby increasing short-range entry. This design is especially suitable for addressing the lesion surface as the first barrier. Similarly, the silver-nanoparticle hydrogel designed by Zheng et al. can closely conforms to irregular tumor surfaces, establishes a sustained local contact interface, and slowly releases silver ions for continuous action on the intratumoral bacteria [257].

In HA-coated platforms such as PAMAM-Pt-LA@HA and the gemcitabine/ciprofloxacin co-delivery system [258, 259], HA mainly acts by improving interfacial compatibility and local recognition. In the former system, it enhances interaction with hyaluronan-rich perilesional stroma, thereby promoting lesion-margin localization and near-surface entry. In the latter, HA layer degradation in hyaluronidase-rich environments, facilitates local drug exposure in lesion region. Overall, the strength of these coating/ ligand mediated strategies lies in improving lesion accessibility by reshaping interfacial behavior, particularly in settings where the mucus layer, tumor surface, or perilesional matrix constitutes barrier.

Bacteria-mediated tumor infiltration

Their motility, stress resistance, chemotactic behavior, ecological niche competition, and immunometabolic regulatory capacity determine that they can also serve as tools for TAB modulation and antitumor drug delivery.

C. butyricum spores can tolerate the harsh conditions of the gastrointestinal tract and germinate in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment. While promoting the enrichment of chemotherapeutic agents in dense pancreatic tissue, they can also achieve TAB control through ecological niche competition as well as metabolic and immune regulation [260]. Similarly, a system using L. rhamnosus GG as a probiotic chassis in combination with the antibacterial activity of gallium ions also demonstrates the value of exploiting biologically driven homing [261]. Compared with conventional nanoparticles, this strategy does not rely solely on passive diffusion, but instead takes advantage of bacterial motility and chemotaxis toward hypoxic and acidic features of the tumor microenvironment to penetrate mucus and dense stroma and enter bacteria-associated tumor regions, thereby improving the efficacy of antibacterial agents. Jiang et al. further strengthened this capability by introducing external-field guidance in their oral oncolytic magnetotactic bacteria platform [262].

Overall, bacteria-mediated infiltration strategies offer unique advantages in overcoming complex tissue barriers, but their safety still requires careful consideration, including variable in vivo colonization, immune perturbation, and the potential risk of dysbiosis.

Maintain and enhance the in vivo stability of the active components

Many active agents used for TAB modulation, particularly natural products, metabolites, and bacteriophages, possess clear antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, or microecological regulatory potential, yet their in vivo application is often limited by poor stability, rapid inactivation, short local persistence, and insufficient lesion accessibility. Accordingly, this section focuses on how nanotechnology improves the availability of these agents in complex biological environments through protection, shielding, and lesion-oriented delivery, thereby enhancing their value for TAB modulation and cancer therapy. Stability-enhancing strategies are especially attractive for biologically fragile agents such as natural products and bacteriophages, but excessive stabilization may also compromise timely release or add formulation complexity.

Stable delivery and efficacy conservation of natural products

Through protection, sustained release, and lesion-oriented delivery, nanotechnology can convert pharmacokinetically unfavorable natural products and metabolites into more effective tools for TAB modulation.

Butyrate has clear microecological regulatory and antitumor effects, yet the free molecule is limited by poor stability, rapid absorption, and short local persistence. A study adopted a dual-route design combining liposomal delivery with Eudragit S100 encapsulation: the former improved systemic delivery stability and lesion accessibility, whereas the latter prevented premature inactivation in the upper gastrointestinal tract and enhanced local colorectal delivery [263] .They offer insights for the intravenous and oral administration of SCFAs.

A second type of design uses carriers that actively participate in regulating the bacterial and tumor microenvironment rather than serving only as inert shells. The inulin-based micellar system carrying tanshinone IIA uses lipoic-acid-modified inulin to construct GSH-responsive micelles while retaining prebiotic function [264]. Likewise, the dextran–carbenoxolone platform uses dextran as a prebiotic polysaccharide backbone and conjugates it with carbenoxolone for bacteria-associated hepatic inflammation and dysbiosis [265].

Fe@Fe₃O₄-Rg3 represents an organic–inorganic hybrid strategy. Nanoparticle conjugation enhances the in vivo stability and functional exposure of ginsenoside Rg3 and links its activity to bacteria regulation and metabolic remodeling in HCC [266]. Overall, the value of these systems lies not merely in “nanoformulating” natural products, but in preserving local activity while amplifying their microecological significance. Natural small-molecule substances with tumor microecology and bacterial spectrum regulatory properties are derived from diverse sources. Enhancing their in vivo stability and lesion-targeting capability holds significant implications for their application in TAB modulation and cancer therapy.

Preservation of bacteriophage activity

Compared with antibiotics, bacteriophages offer higher bacterial specificity and can reduce collateral damage to resident bacteria. However, as obligate bacterial viruses, phages are highly vulnerable to gastric acid inactivation, immune neutralization, and rapid in vivo clearance. Therefore, this subsection focuses on how to preserve phage infectivity during transport and release while maintaining effective contact with target bacteria.

Liposomal or polymer-based encapsulation can improve the oral survival of phages targeting Salmonella and E. coli [267, 268]. Although these studies were mainly conducted in acute infection settings, they provide a clear pharmaceutical basis for phage-based TAB clearance.

In tumor-related situations, the phage–dextran nanoparticle system targeting F. nucleatum in CRC uses biocompatible polymeric nanoparticles to reduce premature phage inactivation and clearance in the gastrointestinal and tumor-associated environment while increasing the probability of effective phage–bacterium contact at the lesion site [269]. By comparison, the M13 phage–AgNP composite places emphasis on maintaining functional phage integrity [270].

The DNA nanopatch–bacteriophage platform combines S. gallolyticus-specific phages with DNA origami nanopatches. On the one hand, the nanopatch structurally stabilizes and locally enriches the phages, thereby improving their availability in inflamed intestinal environments. On the other hand, its ROS-scavenging capacity reduces ROS-related phage functional loss. Thus, the platform preserves lytic activity against S. gallolyticus .For IBD-associated CRC, this design is more suitable than simple gastric protection alone [271].

Furthermore, colon-targeted phage microspheres designed for Salmonella demonstrate that pH-responsive and sustained-release architectures can preserve phage activity within the digestive tract, mitigate physical clearance caused by intestinal contents and peristalsis, and thereby enhance effective dose [272]. Overall, preservation of phage activity and bacterial recognition/infectivity is a prerequisite for applying phages in bacteria-associated tumors.

Microenvironment-responsive on-demand release

Compared with normal tissues, tumors and TAB-enriched sites show marked differences in pH, enzyme activity, and redox status. These endogenous gradients provide natural “switches” that can be built into nanomedicine design to achieve site-specific drug release and selective TAB modulation. By coupling payloads to pH-, enzyme-, or redox-responsive linkers or prodrugs, formulations remain stable during systemic transport but are activated only after entering diseased, bacteria-rich microenvironments. This strategy helps concentrate effective doses at target lesions, reduces systemic exposure and peak toxicity, and limits unnecessary disruption of commensal bacteria, thereby improving both the precision and the controllability of treatment. Microenvironment-responsive systems can improve spatiotemporal precision, but their true translational potential depends on identifying physiologically and pathologically specific cues or components capable of triggering release, and on designing engineered modules that can interact with them effectively.

pH-responsive release

pH-responsive release is based on the fact that tumor tissues, inflammatory lesions, and intracellular vesicular compartments generally exhibit a lower pH than normal tissues. In TAB-associated lesions, such acidification is closely related not only to tumor metabolism, but also to local microenvironmental changes caused by bacterial colonization and inflammation. A core–shell nanoplatform constructed by Liu et al. uses Q-P-A as the functional core and CP[5]A as a pH-responsive gatekeeper, so that the active core remains shielded during transport but is exposed in the acidic tumor microenvironment, thereby confining its action more specifically to the tumor–bacteria interface [273] In the carbonized polymer dot microspheres designed by Zhang et al., pH-sensitive microspheres are used to release ROS-scavenging activity in the distal gut, thereby alleviating radiotherapy-associated intestinal injury and maintaining bacteria homeostasis [274]. This suggests that pH-responsive platforms can also be used to correct treatment-associated microenvironmental damage. Overall, pH-responsive systems are more suitable for lesions with marked acidification and relatively defined local release windows, although their selectivity still depends on the extent of the pH difference between diseased and normal tissues.

Enzyme-responsive release

In TAB-associated tumors, enzyme-responsive delivery often offers stronger lesion specificity because many enzymatic signals are closely linked to bacterial colonization or tumor invasion, allowing drug activation to be more tightly coupled to TAB functional status. The hydrogel designed by Cheng et al. more directly reflects this concept by using F. nucleatum-associated nitroreductase as the trigger. After release in the lesion, the active component further forms nanofibers with biofilm-interfering capacity, while reversing protective autophagy associated with resistance [274, 275]. Similarly, the remotely controllable supramolecular nanomedicine designed by Yan’s team undergoes bond cleavage and disassembly with participation of the abundant azo reductase produced by intratumoral bacteria, which enables preferential activation in F. nucleatum-associated drug-resistant lesions and allows the antibacterial module to directly suppress F. nucleatum, thereby integrating bacterial clearance with chemotherapy sensitization [276]. A C. butyricum-based delivery system further uses tumor-associated MMPs to trigger local release [277]. While suppressing the growth of Proteobacteria, it concentrates the effects of gemcitabine and vactosertib in pancreatic lesions and improves drug penetration by reducing extracellular matrix production.

Redox-responsive release

Redox-responsive platforms are based on the elevated GSH, ROS accumulation, and disturbed redox homeostasis commonly observed in tumors and inflammatory lesions. In TAB-associated tumors, these abnormalities are related not only to tumor metabolic reprogramming, but also to bacterial colonization, persistent inflammation, and mucosal injury. The fluorouridine–metronidazole disulfide-linked prodrug integrates antibacterial and antitumor functions into one cleavable prodrug and enables synchronized release after tumor accumulation through GSH triggering, thereby achieving both antibacterial and antitumor effects [278]. The ROS-responsive dextran–aspirin nanodrug reflects a more anti-inflammatory and microecology-oriented design [279]. Its advantage lies in using inflammation-associated ROS to trigger release while also taking advantage of the prebiotic property of dextran to help restore microbiota balance, suggesting that redox-responsive platforms can also improve TAB-associated tumor environments indirectly by correcting inflammatory and microecological imbalance.

Multifunctional and multimodal synergy

The intricate crosstalk of multiple factors within the tumor ecosystem means that a single drug or a single bacterial-modulation strategy is seldom sufficient to achieve satisfactory efficacy. Harnessing nanotechnology, conventional TAB-modulating tools can be integrated into metal or inorganic nano-scaffolds with defined optical, electromagnetic, and catalytic properties. These hybrid systems gain controllable responsiveness to external physical stimuli, incorporating imaging modules that enable image-guided and disease assessment. The high specific surface area, energy-conversion capacity, and nanozyme-like activity of metal nanoparticles provide the physical basis. On this basis, multifunctional platforms can, simultaneously exert antibacterial and antitumor effects and allow treatment monitoring. By harnessing external physical triggers, they further enhance those effects in a noninvasive manner. Of course, although multifunctional and multimodal systems can generate strong mechanistic synergy, their value should be assessed cautiously if the added modules do not provide clear and irreplaceable therapeutic benefit.

Collaborative platforms for external field stimulation

For TAB-associated tumors, improving the spatiotemporal controllability of antibacterial and antitumor effects is particularly important, because pathogenic bacteria are often unevenly distributed within tumors, while conventional drugs are readily compromised by physiological and pathological barriers or by bacteria-mediated resistance. In this context, light, ultrasound, and magnetic stimulation can all be applied noninvasively from outside the body and activated locally when needed, thereby compensating for insufficient local drug delivery, enabling selective activation, and reducing damage to non-target tissues. Moreover, physically mediated bacterial killing and tumor control may offer additional advantages in reducing resistance and recurrence.

Gastric acidity and rapid gastric emptying can markedly weaken the local effects of many conventional formulations, whereas the graphite-shelled FeCo nanoparticle platform retains magnetic-to-heat conversion capability under acidic gastric conditions. Under an alternating magnetic field, it achieves localized thermal antibacterial activity and suppresses H. pylori without relying on conventional antibiotics [280].

The lecithin-coated PLGA nanoparticles encapsulating verteporfin are constructed from clinically familiar materials. After oral administration, the protein corona formed on the particles helps neutralize CagA, while ultrasound-triggered ROS generation enables selective eradication of H. pylori and avoids obvious microbiota disruption or resistance pressure. Compared with more complex inorganic systems, this design more directly integrates drug protection, bacterial virulence intervention, and externally triggered activation into a single engineering strategy [281].

External-field platforms therefore play a synergistic role in attenuating pathogenic effects while enhancing therapy in TAB-associated settings. Nb₂C nanosheets combined with Au nanoparticles and TNF antibodies integrate photothermal effects, oxidative injury, and immune modulation [282]; the biomineralized Au/CuPpIX platform uses sonodynamic activation to reverse F. nucleatum-associated autophagy [283]; the HA-coated gemcitabine/IR1048 platform employs photothermal effects to reduce bacteria-mediated drug degradation and improve the immune microenvironment [284]; and the acid-responsive gold-based anti–H. pylori system combines pH-triggered antibody release with photothermal killing [285]. Overall, these platforms improve spatiotemporal control over antibacterial and antitumor effects through external stimulation, although their treatment procedures are more complex, their dependence on equipment is stronger, and the uniformity of activation in deep or heterogeneous tissues still requires further evaluation.

In-vivo imaging synergy platform

Imaging-enabled platforms use in vivo imaging to answer key therapeutic questions, such as whether the formulation truly reaches the lesion, whether it releases as intended, and whether the intervention actually occurs at the tumor–bacteria interface.

Li and colleagues combined oleic acid-modified SPION with an amphiphilic polymer carrying a platinum prodrug and lauric acid to construct a nanodrug directed against both F. nucleatum and CRC. The SPION component provides MRI visibility, allowing tumor accumulation to be directly monitored. At the same time, SPION also exhibits peroxidase-like activity, promotes ROS generation, enhances F. nucleatum killing and biofilm disruption, and induces lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [286].

In the Cu₂O/BNN6@MSN-Dex platform, dextran-decorated mesoporous silica serves as the scaffold for Cu₂O and the NO donor BNN6. After reaching CRC lesions, endogenous H₂S converts Cu₂O into CuS in situ, thereby endowing the platform with photoacoustic imaging and photothermal properties. The imaging signal arises from lesion-associated in situ conversion, making it more directly informative for local activation, whereas the antibacterial effect mainly relies on the combined action of photothermal heating and NO against F. nucleatum [287].

Conjugated polymer nanoparticles modified with Tachyplesin I and associated with gold nanoclusters combine bacterial targeting, optical imaging, and photothermal killing [288]. By contrast, SulBC gel uses micro-CT to monitor local distribution and exerts a more indirect antibacterial effect by modulating bacteria and alleviating radiotherapy-associated intestinal injury [289].

Prolonged circulation and retention

Prolonging the in vivo retention duration of payloads and enhancing their retention capacity at lesion sites represent critical steps in translating effective exposure into robust therapeutic efficacy. Nanoparticulate formulations function via rational selection of carrier materials and precise modulation of surface chemical properties, thereby providing efficient protection for active ingredients during transit in the bloodstream and gastrointestinal tract, improving their in vivo pharmacokinetic profiles, and enabling stable, sustained local action. Soft particulate carriers contribute to reduced clearance rates, enhanced tumor accumulation, and improved in situ stability. Conversely, degradable polysaccharides, biomimetic matrices, and adhesive materials facilitate site-specific release and adhesive retention at colonic and mucosal sites. In this context, prolonged circulation and local retention are meaningful not as ends in themselves, but because they provide the temporal basis for effective interaction with the tumor–bacteria interface. Their ultimate value depends on whether extended residence can improve functional lesion exposure rather than simply prolong drug presence in vivo.

Liposome/albumin nanocarriers

Liposomes and albumin-based carriers have a relatively mature pharmaceutical foundation. Multiple liposomal anticancer formulations have already entered clinical use, and albumin nanomedicines have likewise been translated into oncology, indicating that these soft nanocarriers are practically useful for improving pharmacokinetics, prolonging circulation, and reducing nonspecific toxicity.

A liposomal platform co-loading silver ions and tinidazole prolonged systemic exposure and improved lesion accumulation, allowing both agents to more effectively eliminate carcinogenesis-associated bacteria; subsequent bacterial lysis further released relevant antigens and promoted antitumor immune activation [290].

Albumin nanoparticles delivering selenium alleviated cisplatin-associated intestinal mucosal injury while maintaining bacteria homeostasis [291]. A magnetic mesoporous silica liposome platform incorporating P127, 6-gingerol can enhanced immunotherapy-associated effects by increasing local exposure [292]. In the design reported by Wang’s team, albumin served not only as a delivery shell but also as the protein coordination microenvironment for copper atoms. Atoms formed natural enzyme-like coordination motifs within the protein scaffold, which improved dispersity and stability while preserving peroxidase-like activity. Nanozyme catalyzed ROS generation and simultaneously depleted GSH, thereby amplifying local oxidative stress, disrupting bacterial membranes, impairing bacterial survival, and further promoting lipid peroxidation and apoptosis in tumor cells [293]. Taken together, these systems suggest that prolonged circulation is valuable not merely for raising blood drug levels, but for providing a sufficient time window in which antibacterial, immune, and tumoricidal modules can establish synergy within lesions.

In situ mucosal adhesion and degradable scaffolds

For orally administered systems, local retention is often more important than prolonged systemic circulation. This is because many TAB-associated lesions are located at or near the luminal side and mucosal surface, so whether a formulation can remain locally for a sufficient period directly affects its ability to cross mucus, contact bacteria, reshape the metabolic microenvironment, and trigger subsequent therapeutic processes.

Through host–guest chemistry, dextran was coated onto C. butyricum spores to form a core–shell structure [294]. After oral administration, the dextran shell first increased mucosal adhesion and intestinal residence, allowing the spores to accumulate more efficiently at colonic lesions. Once the system entered the tumor-associated environment, the spores germinated, and the outer dextran layer was further fermented to generate butyrate and other SCFAs, thereby directly supporting subsequent bacterial activation, local colonization, and metabolite output.

In addition, a spirulina-based amifostine system reduced radiotherapy-associated injury by prolonging luminal residence [295]. Chitosan/pectin-encapsulated anthocyanins improved bacterial ecology and exerted anticancer effects by increasing contact time [296]. Inulin–ursodeoxycholic acid and xylan–stearic acid systems likewise extended local residence through adhesive or degradable materials and thereby strengthened the combined regulation of tumors and bacteria [297, 298]. These oral scaffolds emphasize sustained contact on the luminal side, creating conditions for subsequent bacteria modulation, metabolic reconstruction, and accumulation of local pharmacological effects. At the same time, stronger local persistence still needs to be balanced against insufficient penetration, local irritation, and sustained disturbance to non-target bacteria.

Overall, the studies discussed in this section indicate that the value of nanotechnology in TAB-oriented intervention lies in addressing clearly defined biological bottlenecks at the tumor–bacteria interface. Although the above studies have demonstrated definite antibacterial and antitumor efficacy in short-term observations, their translational value will still depend on stable performance across heterogeneous lesions, acceptable biosafety, and reproducibility in manufacturing. Therefore, beyond pursuing multifunctional designs to enhance efficacy, future progress in this field may also depend on modular design and on the rational selection of platforms according to lesion biology, the major physiological and pathological barriers, and the therapeutic object.

Conclusion and prospect

TAB participate in almost every stage of carcinogenesis, progression, and therapeutic resistance in digestive system cancers. Rather than merely accompanying tumors, TAB should be regarded as functional components of the tumor ecosystem. Through virulence factors and metabolites, TAB interact with epithelial cells, immune cells, and the tumor microenvironment, sustaining aberrant proliferation, promoting genetic alterations, driving invasion and metastasis, and shaping immunosuppressive or inflammatory niches, ultimately leading to unfavorable clinical outcomes. Biological behavior of tumors is thus determined by a coordinated host–bacterium–microenvironment network, which explain the limited efficacy of traditional single-modality therapies. Accordingly, the significance of TAB regulation lies not only in reducing bacterial burden, but also in interrupting bacteria-driven carcinogenic processes and altering the microecological conditions that support tumor progression. As shown in Fig. 10, the translational path of this field extends from target definition to model-based validation, patient-oriented treatment design, and manufacturable implementation, rather than being defined by platform construction alone.

TAB are highly dynamic, bacterial composition and function are influenced by age, geography, diet, disease status, medications, and circadian rhythm [299–303]. Strains traditionally considered “probiotic”, such as Lactobacillus and some E. coli lineages, can blunt antitumor immunity or promote DNA damage, whereas prototypical TAB such as F. nucleatum may enhance responses to immunotherapy in selected patients [304–307]. Marked interpopulation and interindividual differences in bacterial composition, functional redundancy, and environmental adaptability make it difficult for any single species to serve as a universal diagnostic marker or therapeutic target [308, 309]. Therefore, correlations between gut or intratumoral bacteria and tumor occurrence, progression, or prognosis remain insufficient to guide clinical decision directly, and reliable conclusions must instead rest on clearly defined tumor types and host backgrounds together with rigorous causal evidence linking specific bacterial species or consortia, virulence factors, and pathogenic effects [310, 311].

Methodologically, the field is shifting from descriptive abundance-based correlation analysis toward research models with clearer causal inference and greater clinical interpretability. Future studies should rely on large-scale, multi-cancer cohorts together with multi-source specimens, so as to capture the heterogeneity and dynamic variation of TAB more comprehensively [312–316]. Meanwhile, integrated multi-omics analyses will help link bacterial features to tumor behavior and treatment outcomes [317, 318]. Although bacteria-feature-based diagnostic and prognostic models have already shown relatively high accuracy across multiple cancers, the identification of pathogenic bacteria still requires careful attention to reproducibility, with confounding factors such as age, diet, and medication use taken into account, and must ultimately be validated in high-quality prospective clinical cohorts [319, 320]. As represented by the upper part of Fig. 7, integrating large-scale patient inclusion, multi-source specimen analysis, and data interpretation can help define which bacterial communities, species, or related pathways are truly pathogenic, actionable, and clinically meaningful in specific cancer types and disease stages, thereby providing a basis for patient stratification, selection of the intervention window, and subsequent precision therapeutic design.

From a translational perspective, the key challenges of TAB-directed nanotechnology lie not only in platform design, but also in selectivity, safety, generalizability across models and patients, manufacturability, and clinical applicability. Because TAB regulation acts on a highly dynamic microecological system in which different components influence one another, broad interventions with insufficient selectivity may not only reduce pathogenic bacterial burden, but also disturb commensal bacteria, mucosal homeostasis, and immune balance. Multiple metallic nanoparticles have already been shown to exert such effects [321], and even biodegradable non-metallic carriers are not necessarily benign; intravenously administered PLGA nanoparticles have likewise been shown to acidify the cecum and induce marked bacterial changes [322]. In addition, nanoparticle entry into solid tumors is itself heterogeneous, with delivery efficiency varying across tumors and patients [323]. Thus, even if a given platform shows favorable accumulation and efficacy in a specific model, its performance may not naturally extrapolate to other tumor types or to more complex human settings. Beyond efficacy, scale-up production also faces practical challenges, including the definition of critical quality attributes, control of batch-to-batch consistency, and sterilization process compatibility. FDA guidance has identified particle size distribution, physical stability, manufacturing control strategies, and process validation as core quality issues in the development of drug products containing nanomaterials [324].

Against this background, the lower part of Fig. 7 indicates that once clinically meaningful bacterial targets have been defined, the next step is not only to select appropriate antibacterial payloads and functional modules for specific settings, but also to establish a translational pathway linking model-based evaluation, patient-oriented treatment design, and scalable production. Complementary in vitro and in vivo models are needed to assess lesion accessibility, bacterial selectivity, therapeutic efficacy, ecological impact, and safety. On this basis, treatment design should be matched to patient and lesion characteristics, including tumor type, disease stage, dominant barriers, therapeutic timing, and the ecological consequences of intervention. Accordingly, functional modules and therapeutic components should be selected according to the needs of different patient subsets and treatment settings, rather than being applied uniformly. Only after such biological and therapeutic matching does scalable production and quality control become truly meaningful for translation. In this process, nanotechnology helps render active but application-limited components more selective and controllable, while linking lesion biology, delivery requirements, and therapeutic goals.

Even within such a framework, translational feasibility still requires rigorous standards. Clinically successful nanomedicines such as Doxil and Onpattro were developed from systems with clear principles, scalable manufacturing, and mature quality control [325, 326]. By contrast, although active-targeting and multimodule designs remain attractive in preclinical studies, approved products are still mainly passively targeted nanocarriers, and clinical experience with platforms such as BIND-014 suggests that complex designs face a much stricter test [327, 328]. Therefore, added modules should be introduced only when they provide clear and indispensable therapeutic value. At the same time, the biodistribution, degradation, long-term retention, and immune effects of nanomaterials remain insufficiently understood. PEGylation may trigger accelerated blood clearance through anti-PEG antibodies [329],, positively charged nanomaterials may increase the risks of hemolysis, platelet activation, and coagulation disturbance [330], and complement activation-related pseudoallergy (CARPA) remains an important immune safety concern for some liposomal and lipid-based nanomedicines [331].

In summary, as shown in Fig. 7, future progress in this field will likely depend on coordinated advances across three closely linked levels: more rigorous target identification and causal validation at the biologcal level, more selective and biologically matched platform design at the technical level, and better patient stratification together with standardized manufacturable systems at the clinical level. Ultimately, only when deeper mechanistic microbiome research is combined with more pragmatic nanoengineering design will TAB-oriented precision intervention be more likely to truly serve the clinical treatment of digestive system cancers [332, 333].
