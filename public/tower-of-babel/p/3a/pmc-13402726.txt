Combined carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 50, and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin distinguish benign and malignant pleural effusions: a Bayesian analysis
Authors: Su-Na Cha, Yan Niu, Jian-Xun Wen, Cheng Yan, Xu-Lei Hao, Wen-Jie Hou, Li Yan, Ya-Nan Xu, Wen-Qi Zheng, Zhi-De Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe value of pleural fluid neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) in distinguishing malignant (MPE) and benign pleural effusions (BPE) remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of pleural fluid NGAL in differentiating MPE from BPE. We prospectively enrolled patients presenting with undiagnosed pleural effusion at the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University between September 2018 and July 2021. Their pleural fluid NGAL levels were measured using a Luminex platform. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of NGAL, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and carbohydrate antigen 50 (CA50) for MPE. Bayesian likelihood ratio analysis was employed to calculate each biomarker’s likelihood ratios (LRs). A total of 153 participants were included, comprising 66 cases with MPE and 87 cases with BPE. Pleural fluid NGAL levels were significantly higher in MPE than in BPE. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) of NGAL for diagnosing MPE was 0.72 (95% CI: 0.63–0.80). When combined with CEA and CA50, the AUC increased to 0.88 (95% CI: 0.82–0.94), with a sensitivity of 0.82 (0.70–0.90) and specificity of 0.87 (0.79–0.94). The posterior probability of MPE based on the combined use of the three biomarkers was 0.967. Pleural fluid NGAL demonstrates moderate diagnostic performance for MPE and can serve as an auxiliary biomarker in clinical practice. The combination of NGAL, CEA, and CA50 represents a useful diagnostic tool for MPE.Keywords: NGAL, Bayesian analysis, Biomarkers, Pleural effusionSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Pleural effusion is a common clinical sign associated with a wide range of diseases, including pneumonia, malignancy, tuberculous pleurisy, and heart failure1,2. Among them, pleural effusion caused by malignancy is referred to as malignant pleural effusion (MPE), and the pleural effusions caused by other diseases are termed as benign pleural effusion (BPE). The most common malignancies causing MPE include lung cancer, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, mesothelioma, and lymphoma1. A multicenter survey conducted in China indicated that MPE accounts for approximately 23.3% of all pleural effusion cases2. MPE is typically associated with a poor prognosis, with a median survival between 6 and 12 months3,4. A timely and accurate differentiation between MPE and BPE is critical for determining appropriate treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes5.

Currently, the diagnostic standards for MPE are cytological examination and pleural biopsy. While cytological examination has advantages such as low cost, rapid turnaround, and high specificity, its diagnostic sensitivity is approximately 60%, and its accuracy depends on the experience of the pathologist6,7. Pleural biopsy can be performed under image guidance or via thoracoscopy, and the latter is the most accurate method for diagnosing MPE8. However, both imaging-guided and thoracoscopic biopsies are invasive procedures and may result in complications such as bleeding and infection9,10. In contrast, the detection of biomarkers in pleural fluid offers several advantages, including minimal invasiveness, objectivity, low cost, and ease of implementation, making them promising alternatives for diagnosing MPE11. For example, our previous studies revealed that effusion carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 50 (CA50) are useful diagnostic markers for MPE12,13.

The neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), also known as lipocalin-2, is a small molecular protein secreted by activated neutrophils and macrophages during inflammation14. NGAL has been recognized as a biomarker for acute kidney injury for a long time15,16. Recent studies reported that it is involved in tumor-related processes such as proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis17. Elevated serum NGAL has been observed in a variety of cancers, including lung, breast, ovarian, and brain cancers, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker18–22. Several previous studies have indicated that NGAL may aid in distinguishing MPE or parapneumonic effusions23–25. However, these findings still require further validation due to the limitations of the small sample size and the representativeness of the participants.

Bayesian analysis methods, which dynamically update disease probabilities by integrating prior probabilities with likelihood ratios, have been increasingly applied in diagnostic research26,27. Compared to traditional threshold-based classification approaches, Bayesian frameworks allow the incorporation of continuous-variable data, thereby aligning more closely with real-world clinical decision-making processes. In the present study, we employed a Bayesian analysis to evaluate the diagnostic performance of pleural fluid NGAL, CEA, and CA50. This study adheres to the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines28.

Methods

Participants

The study participants were from a prospective, preregistered, double-blind diagnostic test accuracy study named SIMPLE, which was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University between 2018 and 202029. The aim of the SIMPLE was to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers in pleural fluid and serum29. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients with undiagnosed pleural effusions and (ii) patients who underwent diagnostic thoracentesis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients whose diagnoses remained unclear at discharge; (ii) pregnant women; (iii) patients aged < 18 years; (iv) patients who developed pleural effusion during hospitalization due to medical intervention; and (v) patients with trauma-related pleural effusion. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (2018011). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnostic criteria

After enrollment, final diagnoses were confirmed by the attending physicians based on a combination of cytological examination, acid-fast staining, bacterial cultures, pleural biopsy, and treatment response. MPE was defined as a positive effusion cytology or pleural biopsy. In cases with negative cytology who were unable or unwilling to undergo pleural biopsy, an MPE diagnosis could be made based on evidence of a known primary malignancy (particularly at advanced stages), radiological features consistent with MPE, and exclusion of alternative benign causes.

The diagnostic criteria for tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE) included positive acid-fast staining, positive culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, or a positive pleural biopsy. In patients highly suspected of having TPE but would not undergo biopsy, the diagnosis could be made based on pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) activity > 35 U/L, a favorable response to anti-tuberculosis therapy, and exclusion of other potential causes.

The diagnostic criteria for parapneumonic effusion (PPE) were based on positive pleural fluid bacterial culture, imaging features (e.g., loculation), purulent pleural fluid, and a positive response to antibiotic treatment9.

Congestive heart failure (CHF)–related pleural effusion was diagnosed based on biochemical tests (especially elevated serum NT-proBNP levels), echocardiographic findings, clinical history, and treatment response to diuretics30.

All diagnoses were confirmed by two senior physicians based on clinical records and follow-up data. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Biomarkers assay

Pleural fluid was collected upon patient admission in plain tubes without anticoagulants, left at room temperature for about 4 h, and then centrifuged. The supernatants were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C to − 70 °C until analysis. Previous studies indicated that NGAL and CEA are stable at −80 °C for long-term storage and at room temperature for up to one hour13,31–33. CEA concentrations in pleural fluid were measured using the Abbott I2000SR immunoassay system. CA50 concentrations were measured using the MAGLUMI 4000 analyzer (Shenzhen New Industries Biomedical Engineering Company, China). NGAL concentrations were measured using a Luminex-based method with the Luminex® 100/200TM system. Reagents consisted of two custom-premixed human multiplex assay kits (R&D Systems).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and their normality was assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Two-group comparisons were conducted using independent-sample t-tests or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on their distribution. In addition, likelihood ratios (LRs) were estimated using the Bayesian framework proposed by Simel et al., via the following formula: LR = exp [𝛽 × (x – x′)], where 𝛽 was derived from a logistic regression model, x represented the NGAL concentration of a participant, and x′ was the concentration at which the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) equals 134. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC, AUC) was used to assess the performance of biomarkers in distinguishing MPE and BPE. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/).

A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 170 patients with undiagnosed pleural effusion were recruited in this study. Among them, 17 patients were excluded due to the following reasons: 11 patients with unclear clinical diagnoses, two patients who withdrew consent during subsequent treatment, and four patients who had been diagnosed with pleural effusion based on their medical history prior to recruitment. Ultimately, 153 participants were included in the study, consisting of 66 cases with MPEand 87 cases with BPE. The BPE group included 32 cases of PPE, 20 cases of TPE, 23 cases of CHF-related pleural effusion, and 12 cases of other types of pleural effusion. The MPE group had 56 cases of lung cancer, five cases of malignant mesothelioma, one case of breast cancer, two cases of gastric cancer, one case of lymphoma, and one case of an undetermined malignancy. The clinical characteristics of the participants have been described in our previous publication12.

Pleural fluid NGAL levels

As shown in Fig. 1, the median (IQR) pleural fluid NGAL levels were 37.50 (31.61–44.30) ng/mL and 27.45 (21.46–34.29) ng/mL in MPE and BPE groups, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001).

Diagnostic accuracy of pleural fluid NGAL

To facilitate the interpretation of Bayesian analysis with multiple markers, we also analyzed the diagnostic accuracy of CEA and CA50. These two markers have been investigated in our previous works12,13. Figure 2 shows the ROC curve for NGAL in diagnosing MPE. Discussions

This study assessed the diagnostic performance of pleural fluid NGAL for MPE in the SIMPLE cohort. The main findings of this study include: (i) NGAL has moderate diagnostic performance when used alone; however, its diagnostic efficacy improves when combined with CEA and CA50; (ii) we further analyzed the diagnostic threshold of NGAL based on Bayesian theory, which provides clinicians with a practical tool to assess the patient’s risk of MPE.

To date, only three studies have explored the diagnostic value of pleural fluid NGAL in verifying the causes of pleural effusion23–25. In a study, the authors used ELISA to measure pleural fluid NGAL levels in various types of patients and found that PPE patients had significantly elevated NGAL, suggesting that NGAL acts as a diagnostic biomarker for PPE25. In another study, the authors utilized Luminex technology to measure pleural fluid NGAL and found that MPE patients had elevated NGAL levels, which could assist in diagnosing MPE23. Our results are similar to those of the second study. We speculate that these inconsistencies may primarily be due to differences in the NGAL assays used. The elevated NGAL in MPE may be attributed to tumor-associated inflammation, which activates neutrophils and macrophages, leading to NGAL secretion, or IL-1β and TNF-α released by the malignancy, which induce pleural epithelial cells to express and release NGAL35. Additionally, NGAL can form complexes with matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which may promote tumor invasion and metastasis, a process that could also be associated with increased NGAL expression23,35. However, it should be noted that NGAL in body fluids is influenced by various factors, such as infection, acute or chronic renal disease, and metabolic complications35. Therefore, the diagnostic value of effusion NGAL should be interpreted in light of these factors.

This study proposed a Bayesian analysis-based approach discriminating between BPE and MPE. Particularly, the diagnostic value of effusion NGAL was evaluated. Although no single effusion biomarker can adequately confirm or rule out MPE when used alone, determining effusion biomarkers is clinically valuable because it can help pulmonologists select further diagnostic procedures, such as histological examination36,37. Therefore, incorporating these markers into the diagnostic pathway for undiagnosed pleural effusions using Bayesian analysis may improve diagnostic efficiency, shorten hospital stay, and save costs. This is particularly important for patients when first-line, simple diagnostic tests (e.g., effusion acid-fast staining, cytology) yield inconclusive results. In addition, it should be emphasized that, in the era of immunotherapies and targeted therapies, diagnosing MPE alone is insufficient; it can only help manage MPE. The molecular phenotype of primary cancer should be determined to facilitate better management.

Traditional diagnostic accuracy experiments typically rely on metrics derived from ROC analysis, such as sensitivity, specificity, and the AUC. However, these metrics are not straightforward in clinical practice. For example, the AUC lacks straightforward clinical interpretation. Clinicians are more concerned with the probability of the target disease in a given patient. The advantage of Bayesian algorithms is their ability to calculate the posterior probability based on the likelihood ratios of biomarkers, which helps clinicians assess the patient’s risk and facilitate further decisions. In this study, we developed a formula based on Bayesian likelihood ratios to facilitate the clinical evaluation of patient risk.

Our study also has some limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, and it is a single-center study, so the conclusions need to be validated by studies with large samples and a multicenter design. Second, although our previous study demonstrated the long-term stability of pleural fluid CEA under − 80 °C to − 70 °C storage conditions13, and the long-term stability of NGAL has been reported by previous studies32,33. However, to our knowledge, the long-term stability of pleural fluid CA50 has not been fully investigated.

Conclusions

Pleural fluid NGAL, CEA, and CA50 may help differentiate MPE from BPE. In particular, combining NGAL with CEA and CA50, along with Bayesian analysis, can help pulmonologists estimate the probability of MPE, facilitating the selection of additional diagnostic tools. This proof-of-concept study also demonstrates that Bayesian analysis is a useful and straightforward tool for investigating the diagnostic value of effusion biomarkers.
