Major Bleeding During Factor Xa Inhibitor Therapy: Lessons from the Pre-Andexanet Era in Japan
Authors: Manabu Minami
Published: 2026

See article vol. 33: 1157-1179

Warfarin was approved for human use in the United States in 1954 1) and it has remained the cornerstone of oral anticoagulation therapy for decades, substantially reducing the risk of stroke and systemic embolism in patients with atrial fibrillation. However, its efficacy and safety depend on adequate control of the prothrombin time–international normalized ratio (PT-INR), and treatment is complicated by a narrow therapeutic range, interactions with food and drugs, and intracranial hemorrhage. Stable anticoagulation can be particularly difficult in patients with a high thromboembolic risk or impaired hepatic and renal functions 2) . These limitations are especially important in East Asian patients, who have a higher risk of warfarin-associated intracranial hemorrhage 3) .

The introduction of direct factor Xa inhibitors (FXa inhibitors), including apixaban, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban, has transformed anticoagulant therapy. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), including FXa inhibitors, are administered at fixed doses without routine PT-INR monitoring and reduce intracranial and fatal bleeding compared with warfarin 4 , 5) . These benefits may be greater in Asian populations than in Western populations 6) . However, as the use of FXa inhibitors expands, the clinical course after bleeding has become an increasingly important consideration.

Kohsaka et al. 7) examined 8,011 patients hospitalized for bleeding while receiving an FXa inhibitor using a large Japanese hospital-based administrative claims and Diagnosis Procedure Combination database. The median age was 80 years, reflecting an elderly population that is often underrepresented in randomized trials. Gastrointestinal bleeding is the most common presentation. However, the outcomes vary markedly by bleeding site. Overall, the in-hospital mortality rate was 7.1% and reached 17.8% in patients with intracranial hemorrhage. Thus, despite the improved safety profile of FXa inhibitors, intracranial hemorrhage remains a serious complication. Oral anticoagulation was resumed during hospitalization in 67.9% of patients, with a median time to resumption of 3 days. While this finding provides useful descriptive information on Japanese practice, it should not be interpreted as evidence in favor of early resumption.

The main value of this study lies in describing the hospital course after bleeding, rather than estimating the incidence of bleeding. Previous trials and database studies have largely focused on bleeding rates and comparisons with those of warfarin. Instead, this study reported mortality, thromboembolic events, rebleeding, and anticoagulant resumption according to the bleeding site. As the study period preceded the introduction of andexanet alfa in Japan, the results may also serve as a benchmark for the reversal agent era.

This study has several limitations. First, major bleeding was not defined according to the standardized International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis criteria. Data on hemoglobin decline, transfusion volume, imaging findings, and hematoma volume were limited, and clinically heterogeneous events may have been grouped together. Second, the exact event dates were unavailable. It was not possible to determine whether rebleeding occurred before or after the resumption of anticoagulation, and comparisons between patients who did and did not resume treatment could not establish the safety of resumption. Thromboembolic and rebleeding events may have been misclassified as pre-existing or incident events. Third, the analyses of anticoagulant resumption are prone to selection bias. Patients had to survive and become clinically stable before treatment could be restarted. Therefore, survivor bias, immortal time bias, and confounding by indication preclude the interpretation of the median resumption time of 3 days as evidence that early resumption is safe and beneficial. Fourth, follow-up ended at the time of discharge. Death after discharge, rebleeding, thromboembolism, and functional outcomes were not captured, and outcomes after inter-hospital transfer may have been missed.

The database also lacked key clinical details, including information on the timing of the last dose of FXa inhibitor, renal function, and use of concomitant antithrombotic therapy. Therefore, the contribution of active factor Xa inhibition to each bleeding event could not be determined in this study. Additionally, because no group of FXa inhibitor users without bleeding was included for comparison, the study could not identify risk factors for FXa inhibitor–associated bleeding.

The present study reflects the practice prior to the availability of andexanet alfa. Andexanet alfa rapidly reverses FXa inhibition and improves hemostatic control in FXa inhibitor–associated major bleeding and intracerebral hemorrhage 8 , 9) . However, ANNEXA-I also demonstrated an increased risk of thrombotic events, particularly ischemic stroke 9) . Better hemostatic control does not necessarily translate to lower mortality or better functional outcomes. In the era of andexanet alfa, reversal alone should not be used to define treatment success. Management should also include surveillance for thrombotic complications and repeated assessment of when and whether anticoagulation should be resumed after hemostasis.

FXa inhibitors offer safer and more convenient anticoagulation than warfarin. However, major bleeding, especially intracranial hemorrhage, still carries a poor prognosis. Because the thrombotic consequences of treatment interruption vary depending on the indication, future studies should analyze patients separately according to the reason for anticoagulation, including non-valvular atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, cancer-associated thrombosis, and postoperative venous thromboembolism prophylaxis.

Prospective registries should record the last dose and dosing regimen, concomitant antithrombotic therapy, renal function, bleeding site and severity, imaging findings, reversal and hemostatic treatment, time to hemostasis, anticoagulant resumption, mortality, functional outcomes, rebleeding and thromboembolic events. Such data are needed to improve prognostic stratification, guide hemostatic treatment, and define the timing of anticoagulant resumption. Extending this study to broader cohorts, including FXa inhibitor users without bleeding, should help identify patients at the greatest risk and support more individualized strategies for bleeding prevention.
