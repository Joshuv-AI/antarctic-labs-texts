Ictal phase–amplitude coupling supports surgical decision-making in MRI-negative epilepsy with non-localizing SEEG: an illustrative case
Authors: Kazuki Nishioka, Hiroharu Suzuki, Takumi Mitsuhashi, Tetsuya Ueda, Kazuki Nomura, Madoka Nakajima, Hidenori Sugano, Yasushi Iimura, Akihide Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a 14-year-old boy with MRI-negative drug-resistant frontal lobe epilepsy presenting with the chapeau de gendarme sign. SEEG showed diffuse low-voltage fast activity with rapid bilateral propagation, precluding reliable visual localization. Ictal phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) analysis revealed a right-dominant increase in modulation index at seizure onset, localizing to the anterior insula and adjacent prefrontal cortex. Informed by these findings, right frontal disconnection with operculo-insular resection was performed, resulting in seizure freedom at 1 year. Introduction

MRI-negative drug-resistant epilepsy remains a major challenge in presurgical evaluation, as the absence of structural abnormalities limits delineation of the epileptogenic zone (EZ) and is associated with less favorable surgical outcomes than MRI-positive epilepsy [4]. While subtle focal cortical dysplasia may underlie some cases, precise localization is often difficult due to widespread or poorly defined epileptogenicity [5, 11, 12].

Stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) plays a central role in evaluating MRI-negative epilepsy [8]; however, accurate EZ localization can remain limited even with extensive electrode implantation. Low-voltage fast activity (LVFA) is a classic ictal marker of epileptogenic tissue, but when it appears diffusely or propagates rapidly, its spatial specificity may be limited, reflecting early network spread rather than the seizure-generating core and thereby limiting visual localization [1]. This uncertainty poses a critical challenge for surgical decision-making, as treatment strategies must often be determined despite non-localizing SEEG findings.

Quantitative analysis of SEEG has been proposed to address this limitation. Phase–amplitude coupling (PAC), quantified using the modulation index (MI), reflects interactions between low-frequency phase and high-frequency amplitude and has been suggested as a biomarker of epileptogenicity [2, 3, 7, 10]. However, its role in guiding surgical decision-making in cases with inconclusive SEEG findings remains unclear.

Ictal semiology may provide complementary localization clues. The “chapeau de gendarme” (CdG) sign has been described in frontal and operculo-insular seizures [9], but its integration with quantitative electrophysiological markers has not been established.

Here, we report a case of MRI-negative frontal lobe epilepsy presenting with the CdG sign, in which ictal PAC analysis provided actionable localization when visual SEEG interpretation was non-localizing, contributing to network-oriented surgical decision-making and seizure freedom.

Case description

A 14-year-old right-handed boy had drug-resistant epilepsy since the age of five years. His seizures occurred predominantly during sleep and were characterized by symmetrical contraction of the lips and chin, consistent with the CdG sign, and were occasionally followed by predominantly left-sided tonic posturing. Neurological examination was normal. Seizures occurred daily despite multiple anti-seizure medications, including lamotrigine, perampanel, lacosamide, and clobazam.

Epilepsy-protocol 3.0-T MRI revealed no structural abnormalities (Fig. 1A, B). FDG-PET demonstrated hypometabolism involving the right frontal operculum, anterior insula, and adjacent frontal regions (Fig. 1C, D). Magnetoencephalography revealed clustered dipoles in the left frontal cortex. Scalp EEG monitoring showed bilateral frontal epileptiform discharges with slight left predominance, while ictal EEG demonstrated diffuse attenuation followed by generalized rhythmic activity.

To further delineate the epileptogenic zone, SEEG was performed with bilateral frontal implantation. Eight platinum depth electrodes (AD-TECH, Racine, WI, USA) were stereotactically implanted using a ROSA robotic guidance system. SEEG was recorded at 2,000 Hz. Interictal recordings showed frequent epileptiform discharges in the right dorsomedial premotor cortex and AIC, with early contralateral propagation. Ictal SEEG was characterized by diffuse LVFA with rapid bilateral spread, precluding reliable visual identification of a focal seizure onset zone (Fig. 2A). Electrical stimulation of the right AIC consistently reproduced habitual seizures.

To overcome the limitations of visual SEEG interpretation, ictal PAC analysis was performed. MI was calculated from bipolar SEEG signals using 1-s sliding windows (80% overlap), coupling 3–4 Hz phase with ripple and fast ripple amplitudes. The peri-ictal period from − 30 to + 90 s was analyzed. Interictal PAC was examined as a comparator; detailed methods and results are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Ictal PAC analysis revealed a localized increase in MI at seizure onset, most prominently in the right AIC, despite diffusely distributed LVFA on SEEG (Fig. 2B). Heat maps of MIRipples/3–4 Hz showed right-dominant peri-ictal increases, with the most prominent onset-related peak in the right AIC, followed by ipsilateral prefrontal regions. During the 0–15 s onset phase, the right AIC showed the highest median ictal MI, with additional elevations in the right VMPF, DMPF, and VLPF (Supplementary Materials). These findings were concordant with FDG-PET hypometabolism and habitual seizure induction by electrical stimulation.

Based on this integrated interpretation, right frontal disconnection combined with resection of the frontal operculo-insular region was performed (Fig. 3). Intraoperative electrocorticography (ECoG) was performed before and after resection and frontal disconnection. Pre-resection/disconnection ECoG demonstrated widespread epileptiform discharges extending across the disconnection line. Post-resection/disconnection ECoG showed only occasional residual spikes within the preserved frontal lobe. Histopathological examination revealed focal cortical dysplasia type I. The postoperative course was uneventful except for transient left facial weakness, which resolved within one month. At the 1-year follow-up, scalp EEG showed only occasional sharp waves at C4, without recurrence of the bilateral frontal epileptiform discharges observed preoperatively. The patient has remained seizure-free for 1 year with unchanged antiseizure medications.

Discussion

Ictal phase–amplitude coupling in seizures presenting with the CdG sign

In previous studies based on visual analysis of SEEG recordings, the CdG sign has been associated with seizures involving mesial, lateral, and basal frontal regions as well as the operculo-insular cortex, suggesting that this semiology reflects activation of a distributed cortical network rather than a single focal generator [9].

In the present case, ictal PAC analysis demonstrated the highest MIRipples/3–4 Hz at seizure onset in the AIC, with additional elevations in adjacent prefrontal regions (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Materials). Rather than indicating a strictly localized onset, this pattern is consistent with a network-level process in which the AIC may represent a functionally important node within a broader frontal–operculo-insular network.

This interpretation aligns with prior concepts of the CdG sign as a manifestation of coordinated activation across interconnected cortical regions, including the insulo–cingulate system [6]. Within this framework, ictal PAC may provide complementary information by identifying regions showing relatively stronger coupling dynamics at seizure onset when visual SEEG changes are diffuse.

Contribution of ictal PAC to surgical decision-making

Visual identification of the seizure onset zone can be inconclusive when LVFA emerges near-simultaneously across multiple electrodes [1], as observed in the present case. Although SEEG suggested involvement of a right frontal–operculo-insular network, rapid bilateral propagation precluded reliable prioritization of the core epileptogenic region based on visual interpretation alone.

In this context, ictal PAC provided additional discriminatory information at seizure onset. Specifically, MI for ripples/3–4 Hz demonstrated a focal and temporally early increase in the right anterior insular cortex and adjacent prefrontal regions, allowing differentiation of relatively dominant regions within a broadly activated network. In contrast, interictal PAC showed more spatially diffuse and less specific elevations, including contralateral regions not targeted for resection (Supplementary Materials), highlighting the greater spatial specificity of ictal PAC for surgical decision-making.

Importantly, ictal PAC did not simply confirm SEEG findings but enabled prioritization of regions for intervention within a non-localizing electrophysiological context. The preferential involvement of the right anterior insular and adjacent frontal regions supported a network-oriented surgical strategy, including frontal disconnection combined with operculo-insular resection.

These findings suggest that, in MRI-negative epilepsy with inconclusive visual SEEG onset patterns, ictal PAC may provide actionable information by identifying regions with relatively greater early ictal involvement, thereby supporting surgical planning within a multimodal framework.

Limitations

This report describes a single case, and validation in larger cohorts is required to determine generalizability. The spatial distribution of PAC may have been influenced by SEEG electrode coverage, and the analysis focused on a specific MI frequency pairing. Because no universally accepted threshold for clinically meaningful MI exists, ictal and interictal MI distributions were statistically compared, and relative spatial patterns were interpreted in this case (Supplementary Materials). We did not directly compare PAC with other quantitative SEEG measures, including the Epileptogenicity Index [2, 7]; therefore, the incremental value of ictal PAC remains to be clarified. Despite these limitations, ictal PAC provided anatomically coherent localization concordant with FDG-PET and electrical stimulation mapping, contributing to surgical decision-making when visual SEEG interpretation was inconclusive.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that ictal PAC can provide actionable localization in MRI-negative epilepsy when visual SEEG findings are non-localizing due to diffuse or rapidly propagating ictal activity. By identifying regions with relatively greater early ictal involvement within a distributed epileptogenic network, ictal PAC supported surgical decision-making and contributed to seizure freedom. These findings suggest that ictal PAC may serve as a useful adjunct within multimodal presurgical evaluation in challenging cases.
