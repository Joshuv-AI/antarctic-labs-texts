Ureterorenoscopy performed by residents under expert supervision: a training approach ensuring patient safety and effectiveness
Authors: Emanuele Serafin, Andrea Franceschini, Francesco Ditonno, Endri Toska, Alberto Baielli, Antonio Raiti, Damiano D’Aietti, Francesca Montanaro, Luca Rahmati, Mattia Ronca, Peres Fokana Pongmoni, Sonia Costantino, Vincenzo Vetro, Alessandro Veccia, Riccardo Rizzetto, Alberto Bianchi, Alessandra Gozzo, Vincenzo Lacola, Maria Angela Cerruto, Riccardo Bertolo, Alessandro Antonelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectivesto evaluate the outcomes and postoperative complications of ureteroscopy (URS) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) for urolithiasis performed by residents under supervision compared to those performed by specialists.Materials and methodsThe institutional endourology database was reviewed. Patients, stone characteristics – including Guy’s stone (GSS) and S.T.O.N.E. scores to describe the complexity of renal stones – perioperative and postoperative data, complications, and stone-free rate (SFR) were retrieved. Procedures were stratified based on whether a specialist or a supervised resident performed them. Multivariate logistic regression was used to analyze the association between stone characteristics and/or operator, and SFR.ResultsOut of 280 patients analyzed, 164 (58.6%) were operated on by specialists vs. 116 (41.4%) by supervised residents. Specialists treated larger stones (p < 0.04), but GSS and S.T.O.N.E. scores showed no significant differences. Supervised residents performed more URS than specialists (23.3% vs. 13.4%, p = 0.038). Operative time was shorter for supervised residents (70 vs. 75.5 min, p = 0.02). Patients operated by specialists were more likely discharged with indwelling ureteral stent (82.3% vs. 69.8%, p = 0.02), for a longer time (p = 0.004). Complication rates were similar, but patients operated by specialists had more stone-related events within 90 days (29.9% vs. 16.4%, p = 0.01). SFR was 58.5% for specialists and 63.8% for supervised residents (p = 0.4). Stone volume and stones’ multifocality were predictors of a poorer SFR, while an operation performed by a supervised resident was not a predictor (p = 1).ConclusionsUnder the controlled environment and careful case selection typical of a supervised residency training program, resident-performed ureterorenoscopy for urolithiasis is safe and yields satisfactory postoperative outcomes.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01909-1.

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a condition that is widespread globally, with a prevalence ranging from 1–20% [1–4]. The disease is constantly encountered in urology practice and, therefore, is an integral part of urology resident training. In the era of minimally invasive endourological surgery, technical challenges make the initial learning curve for ureteroscopy (URS) or retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) particularly steep [5, 6].

The need for surgical training must be balanced with evolving patient attitudes toward inexperienced surgeons performing procedures, raising significant ethical and legal considerations [7, 8].

Currently, available evidence does not yet precisely define the learning curve of URS/RIRS [9–11] because its exact definition also depends on the selected endpoints, which may include measuring outcomes, complication rates, patient safety, procedural costs, and surgical efficiency (e.g., operative time, fluoroscopy time, radiation dose, or stone volume removed per minute) [12–14].

Over time, various surgical training methods have been proposed and developed [15]. Recently, the European Association of Urology has implemented a standardized simulated training pathway [16–18]. Training models based on simulators offer a safe environment where basic skills can be practiced without worrying about potential iatrogenic harm [5, 15]. Nevertheless, supervised surgical training in the operating room remains crucial, as performing surgery is the ultimate goal [14].

At the main campus of our University Hospital’s residency program, our training approach does not include simulation, relying instead on direct supervision by a staff urologist in the operating room upfront.

This study analyzed URS and RIRS data for urolithiasis performed by supervised residents and staff urologists to determine if significant differences exist in outcomes and postoperative complications.

Materials and methods

Study population

Patients who underwent endourological treatment for urolithiasis between December 2020 and January 2024 at the primary site of our urology residency program were included in the study.

Data were collected into a prospectively maintained institutional database and then analyzed retrospectively.

Informed consent was collected from each patient. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data regarding retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) and ureteroscopy (URS) procedures were extracted. Surgeries were performed by either a staff urologist or a resident in training (attending from the 3rd to the 5th year of residency). All procedures performed by residents were conducted under the direct supervision of a staff urologist present in the operating room alongside the resident, providing verbal guidance and, if necessary, taking over the instrument to assist the resident in the tricky passages of the procedure. URS indicates the retrograde endourological treatment of ureteral stones, up to the ureteropelvic junction, performed with a semi-rigid ureteroscope. RIRS refers to ureterorenoscopy using a flexible instrument for the treatment of kidney stones, and in some cases, of proximal ureteral stones, if the procedure also involves exploring the renal cavities to complete lithotripsy or remove the produced fragments.

Only patients who had preoperative abdominal CT scans available to evaluate both stone burden and stone characteristics were considered. Patients without follow-up or those with missing data were excluded from the analysis.

Outcome measurements

Baseline characteristics included age, sex, BMI, preoperative anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy, age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), ASA score, history of urolithiasis, and anatomical abnormalities. Data on stone characteristics included laterality, location, number, density (Hounsfield units), size, hydronephrosis, and chemical composition. For renal stones, stone complexity was assessed using Guy’s stone (GSS) [19] and the S.T.O.N.E. scores [20]. Stone size was evaluated as linear (mm), surface (mm2), and volume (mm3). Perioperative data included preoperative urine culture, presence of previously placed ureteral stent or nephrostomy, preoperative creatinine (mg/dl), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, ml/min/1.73 m²), type of surgery (RIRS vs. URS), operative time, use of ureteral access sheath (UAS), postoperative ureteral stent placement, length of hospital stay, postoperative blood tests, presence of indwelling stent or nephrostomy at discharge and time to their removal. Postoperative complications (within 30 days from surgery) were collected and classified according to Clavien-Dindo [21]. Data about postoperative infections, stone-related events within 90 days from discharge, and stone-free rate (SFR) were also recorded. Complete clearance (stone-free status) was defined as the absence of fragments ≥ 2 mm on postoperative imaging (ultrasound, CT scan, or KUB (kidney, ureter, bladder) X-ray).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range [IQR]. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables.

Multivariate logistic regression models evaluated the association between operating surgeon (supervised resident vs. staff) and stone characteristics and SFR. Results were presented as odds ratio (OR), 95% C.I.

Statistical tests were conducted using Stata® 17.0 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 329 patients underwent endourological treatment for upper urinary tract stones within the time span considered. Forty-nine patients were excluded due to missing follow-up data.

Finally, 280 patients remained for the present analysis. Of these, 164 patients (58.57%) were operated on by staff urologists, while 116 (41.43%) were operated on by supervised residents in training.

Patients operated on by staff urologists were more often discharged with a stent in place [135 (82.32%) vs. 81 (69.83%), p = 0.02], held for a longer time (30 [IQR 23–50] days vs. 28 [IQR 2–40] days, p = 0.004).

Table 4 summarizes the postoperative outcomes. Patients operated on by staff urologists experienced more stone-related events within 90 days [49 (29.88%) vs. 19 (16.38%), p = 0.01]. However, there was no significant difference in SFR, with 96 (58.54%) specialist-treated patients and 74 (63.79%) supervised resident-treated patients achieving stone-free status (p = 0.4), though the imaging methods used for evaluation differed between groups (p = 0.006).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis (overall population, n = 280) found that stone volume (OR 0.64, 95% C.I. 0.495–0.827, p = 0.001) and multifocality of stones (OR 0.261, 95% C.I. 0.112–0.615, p = 0.002) were predictors of poorer SFR. Conversely, operation by a supervised resident was not a predictor of poorer SFR (OR 0.993, 95% C.I. 0.570–1.729, p = 1).

The same results were obtained when focusing the analysis on patients who exclusively had renal stones at preoperative presentation; no statistically significant predictor of SFR was found when focusing the analysis on patients who only had ureteral stones. Complete results are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the outcomes of ureterorenoscopy for urolithiasis performed by either staff urologists or supervised residents in training. Under the controlled environment and careful case selection typical of a supervised residency training program, supervised resident-performed ureterorenoscopy interventions were safe and yielded satisfactory postoperative outcomes.

This is a reassuring message. Ideally, training should begin with preclinical models such as dry lab, wet lab, cadaver lab, animal models, and simulators. However, these methods often involve costs that are not budgeted for in residency training programs. As a result, especially in programs with a high number of residents, trainees are directly exposed to endourology under strict supervision from the very beginning.

The study was not designed for a head-to-head comparison of outcomes between residents and staff urologists. However, we conducted a cohort comparison to assess the degree of case selection for procedures performed by residents. Not surprisingly, staff urologists treated larger stones, whether measured by stone diameter, stone surface area, or stone volume. On the other hand, there were no differences between the two groups in stone complexity, as measured by both the GSS and S.T.O.N.E. scores. This realistically means that specialists entrusted residents with performing endourological treatment on complex stones, provided they were in a supervised setting.

Our data show that supervised residents performed proportionally more URS and had shorter operative times. Also, patients operated on by supervised residents were less often discharged with a ureteral stent in place, and if the device was still in place at discharge, this was removed earlier. Again, these results are not surprising but representative of the type of patients operated on by the staff urologists, characterized by larger - often multifocal - renal stones, which, therefore, require longer treatment times and a double-J stent after surgery. Moreover, the need for repeated treatments or postoperative imaging to evaluate the stone-free status often leads to a longer dwelling time of the stent before deciding on its eventual removal.

A notable finding of our study is the lack of statistically significant differences between the two groups in early postoperative complications, both infectious and non-infectious, as well as complications within 30 days. Additionally, most of the complications that occurred were minor, according to Clavien-Dindo [21, 22]. Only in terms of stone-related events within 90 days there was a difference between the two groups (29.88% of patients operated on by a staff urologist vs. 16.38% of patients operated on by a supervised resident, p = 0.01). However, when revising the type of reported event, 51% of the events related to staff urologists vs. 31.6% of the events related to residents were attributable to symptoms caused by the indwelling stent. This difference is, therefore, justified by the higher proportion of patients treated by specialists who were discharged with an indwelling stent.

Another important finding from our analysis concerns the SFR. Not only did the descriptive analysis show no differences between the two groups, but multivariate logistic regression also confirmed that being operated by a supervised resident was not a predictor of a poorer SFR, whether analyzing all stone types or separately assessing ureteral and kidney stones. On the contrary, an increasing stone volume and multifocality were found to be predictive factors of a poorer SFR both in the multivariate analysis conducted on any stone and on kidney stones.

Similar results have been reported in the literature, with evidence suggesting equivalent outcomes between residents working under direct supervision and experienced surgeons. A retrospective study conducted by Al-Naimi et al. involved 891 patients (for a total of 1182 URS) divided into two groups, the first operated on by residents in a tutored setting and the second by trained surgeons [23]. This study observed comparable results between the two groups regarding SFR (90% for both residents and trained surgeons) and complication rate (10.5% vs. 13% for residents and trained surgeons, respectively). Similar 30-day complication rates were observed within the setting of our study (16% for staff urologists vs. 15.5% for residents).

Our SFR was slightly lower than what was reported by Al-Naimi, likely because we used a stringent definition of stone-free status, allowing only fragments up to 2 mm. In contrast, the literature often adopts a 4 mm cut-off. Another factor that may explain this result is the relatively infrequent use of the ureteral access sheath in our series, which was placed in only half of the patients who underwent RIRS. Lastly, to put our SFR into perspective, only 13 out of 111 non-stone-free patients (11.7%) required reoperation. Therefore, in the end, our outcomes are not far from the nearly 90% SFR reported in some more “accommodating” literature series.

The literature further supports data about complications, reporting an overall complication rate ranging from 4–25% [1, 24–29]. In another study comparing the surgical outcomes between surgeons in training and residents, Leijte et al. demonstrated that the group of tutored residents reported better SFR and lower complication rates than the group of less experienced specialists (< 10 procedures performed) [30].

In another study, Netsch et al. evaluated the impact of surgical experience on the outcome of ureteroscopy performed for single upper urinary tract stones, demonstrating that being a resident did not compromise the SFR and overall complication rate. However, the ureteral perforation rate was higher in the resident group (4.3% vs. 1.3%, p ≤ 0.027) [31].

Also regarding the impact of multifocality of urolithiasis and stone volume on SFR, our results are consistent with the literature. In their case series of 100 patients, Cho et al. reported that the presence of multiple stones was a predictor of poorer SFR, both in terms of number of stones (OR 0.118, 95% C.I. 0.020–0.691, p = 0.02) and multiple stone locations (OR 0.124, 95% C.I. 0.028–0.547, p = 0.006) [13]. In a study conducted on 238 cases of RIRS, Ito et al. demonstrated that the volumetric determination of the stone by CT-scan was inversely associated with the stone-free status (p < 0.001) [32]. Regarding ureteroscopy for stones > 20 mm, stone volume was found to be a stronger predictor of SFR than maximum stone diameter and cumulative diameter [33–35].

When searching the literature about the learning curve of ureterorenoscopy procedures, the heterogeneity in reporting outcomes, anatomical differences, stone burden, and prior experience of surgeons performing the procedures can impede an exact definition of the learning curve itself [10]. However, several studies suggest that approximately 50–60 RIRS procedures are necessary to reach the plateau of the learning curve [9, 12, 13, 15, 36, 37]. In a review of surgical training in URS, Skolarikos et al. suggested adopting the threshold of 50 procedures as the goal for achieving a good competence profile during the residency program [14]. While this number seems reasonable, achieving such a volume of URS and/or RIRS per resident might be difficult in low-volume centers or teaching hospitals lacking dedicated endourology fellowships. The result may be that the experience level achieved for such procedures might be inadequate or not uniformly attained by all trainees by the end of the residency program [12, 14].

These considerations led to a first limitation of our study, as it was conducted within a residency program involving several residents. This resulted in many different operators, significantly reducing the number of cases performed by each individual surgeon, well below the threshold of 50 procedures described in the literature. Additionally, the 24 residents who operated during the study period did not have the same experience level, as they began the residency program in different years. These limitations prevented us from constructing and analyzing a learning curve for the residents.

Another limitation concerns the follow-up criteria, which were heterogeneous. The interval between the patient’s discharge and re-evaluation varied from days to months, according to the operators’ discretion. Moreover, the imaging used to assess the stone-free status was arbitrarily chosen by the operator. The difference between the groups (staff urologists vs. supervised residents) in the imaging technique used to evaluate the stone-free status might have influenced the SFR.

Given the retrospective design and the characteristics of the dataset, we were unable to perform matching procedures (e.g., propensity-score or coarsened exact matching) to control for baseline differences in case complexity between the resident and specialist groups, and this represents a limitation of our study.

The study does not fully compare the residents’ performance with that of the staff urologists. Indeed, the residents were constantly under the direct supervision of a tutor, who guided them and could intervene at any step of the procedure. Unfortunately, we did not account for recording how many minutes of the total surgery duration were performed independently by each resident and how many required the tutor’s intervention, nor did we assess the significance of the tutor’s involvement at various stages of the procedure. This could be the goal of future prospective efforts.

Finally, while the intent was to remain consistent with prior literature, we acknowledge the limitation of using PCNL-derived scoring systems to stratify stone complexity.

Notwithstanding the limitations of the analysis, our study highlighted the educational environment promoted during tutored endourological surgeries at our institution. It suggested that a university hospital with a residency program could be a safe and effective choice for patients seeking treatment for urolithiasis, regardless of whether the procedures are performed by ‘expert hands’ or ‘hands in training.

Conclusions

Our data would confirm that, under the controlled environment and careful case selection typical of a supervised residency training program, supervised resident-performed ureterorenoscopy for urolithiasis is safe and yields satisfactory postoperative outcomes. The tutoring activity implemented at a university hospital would ensure adequate patient outcomes while allowing the residents to increase their surgical experience as first surgeons.
