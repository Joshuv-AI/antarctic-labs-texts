Is there a relationship between morphokinetic parameters and obstetrical complications? An analysis of singleton live births after single fresh embryo transfer
Authors: Avital Wertheimer, Onit Sapir, Alyssa Hochberg, Avi Ben-Haroush, Eran Altman, Tzippy Shochat, Eran Hadar, Yoel Shufaro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the advancement in embryology and the introduction of time-lapse monitoring system, the embryologists' goal might be to find not only the embryo with the highest probability of live birth, but also the embryo with the highest probability to progress to a healthy full-term delivery. Thus, we aimed to investigate the association between morphokinetic time-lapse parameters and obstetrical and perinatal complications.MethodsA cohort study reviewing fertility and delivery files of all singletone births from IVF patients whose embryos were cultured in a time-lapse monitoring system and had a single fresh embryo transfer at our center between 2013–2019. We conducted multiple comparisons between complicated and uncomplicated pregnancies of each perinatal complication, including: gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM); small for gestational age (SGA); pre-eclamptic toxemia (PET); preterm labor < 37 weeks of gestation (PTL); and third stage of labor complications. A comparison between pregnancies with and without a composite outcome of placental complications including GDM, SGA, PET and PTL was also conducted. Baseline characteristics, treatment and morphokinetic parameters in complicated and uncomplicated gestations were compared. Logistic regression analysis was utilized to adjust results for potential confounders.ResultsOne hundred seventy-six single embryo transfers resulted in 176 live births. Morphokinetic time-lapse parameters were similar between the groups, except for a shorter time to full blastulation in the SGA group (tB-tPNf = 75.5 ± 1.3 h vs. 79.5 ± 4.8 in the non-SGA group, p < 0.001), and shorter third cell cycle duration in the PET group (CC3 = 12.4 ± 1.1 h vs. 13.6 ± 2.9 in the non-PET group, p = 0.02). On multivariate regression analysis, none of the morphokinetic parameters were found to be significantly correlated with any of the perinatal complications.ConclusionTime-lapse morphokinetic parameters of the embryo transferred are not associated with adverse obstetric and perinatal outcomes.Keywords: Morphokinetics, Time-lapse monitoring system, Perinatal complications, KID Score

Background

The introduction of the time-lapse monitoring system (TLMS) has enabled continuous assessment of embryonic morphological changes that could assist embryologists in embryo evaluation and selection for transfer [1–3]. The concept of morphokinetic parameters regarding embryo cell dynamics and their association with implantation and clinical pregnancy rates is well studied [4–6], while less is known regarding their association with obstetrical complications. Previous studies have shown that TLMS might improve In-Vitro fertilization (IVF) clinical outcomes by increasing clinical pregnancy and live birth rates and decreasing early pregnancy loss rate [7, 8]. Only one previous study has investigated the relationship between TLMS and perinatal complications and demonstrated different time intervals to be associated with different obstetrical complications [9]. If a relationship between early embryonic morphokinetics and complications of the third trimester does exist, this would be of great theoretical and practical importance. In such a scenario, embryos could be selected not only to implant but also to produce a pregnancy with the lowest possible rate of complications. Additionally, the early molecular nature of complications like growth restriction or pre-eclampsia could be investigated. On the other hand, if such a relationship does not exist, patients could be reassured that the relevance of morphokinetic data is limited only to the first trimester.

Singleton pregnancies conceived through IVF are known to have an increased risk of adverse perinatal outcome including preterm delivery (PTD), preeclamptic toxemia (PET), small for gestational age (SGA) neonates, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and third stage of labor complications [10–15]. It is not clear whether the high incidence of perinatal complications is related to the patients’ baseline characteristics, subfertility, the IVF technology, the hormonal treatment, or embryo quality [13, 16]. The improved obstetric and perinatal outcomes of pregnancies conceived through frozen-thawed embryos could be partially explained by the transfer of good quality embryos [17, 18], However, no association has been found between the traditional embryo quality grading and perinatal complications [19].

With the advancement in embryology and the introduction of TLMS, the embryologists' goal might be to find not only the embryo with the highest probability of live birth, but also the embryo with the highest probability to progress to a healthy full-term delivery [7, 20]. Theoretically, the progression of the embryonic culture might have an impact on the implantation and placentation of the in vitro cultured embryo, which might translate later during pregnancy into gestational complications. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between the morphokinetic parameters provided by the TLMS and perinatal complications in singleton pregnancies conceived through IVF following single fresh embryo transfer (ET).

Methods

Study population

This was a cohort study conducted at the IVF unit of a university-affiliated tertiary hospital. The study included all IVF patients who had a singleton live birth after a single fresh ET in which the embryos were cultured in a TLMS between June 2013 and December 2019. Patients whose embryos were not cultured exclusively in a TLMS and patients with multifetal gestations were excluded, as were patients for whom data regarding pregnancy and obstetrical complications were missing. The cohort included all embryos that gave a singleton live birth after single fresh embryo-transfer and had a detailed information regarding the patient treatment, early and late morphokinetic parameters of the embryo, and obstetrical and perinatal complications of the pregnancy—from pre-laboratory to post-partum. This detailed information enabled us to differentiate between obstetrical risk factors and potential IVF-related risk factors.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The retrospective anonymous data collection for this study was approved by the Rabin Medical Center IRB. Informed consent was waived by the Rabin Medical Center IRB (Study number RMC-19-0697).

Ovarian stimulation

A flexible Gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist protocol was used in the majority of patients, whereas the long GnRH agonist protocol represented a smaller proportion of cases. Treatment protocols, FSH dosage and the addition of LH were determined according to individual clinical considerations. Oocyte retrieval was carried out 36–38 h after triggering with HCG (Ovitrelle® 250 µg,Merck, Kenilworth, New Jersey) or a GnRH agonist (Decapeptyl® 0.2 mg, Ferring, Saint-Prex, Switzerland), or both, administered once the leading three follicles measured ≥ 18 mm. Oocyte fertilization was carried out either by conventional insemination or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) according to sperm parameters, previous treatment outcomes, or the use of preimplantation genetic testing (PGT).

Embryo culture, assessment and time-lapse monitoring

After fertilization (immediately after ICSI or a day after conventional insemination), embryos were placed in individual wells of pre-equilibrated EmbryoSlides of the time lapse system TLMS (EmbryoScope™, Unisense Fertiltech, Vitrolife, Viby, Denmark), overlayed with mineral oil (Irvine Scientific, USA) in an atmosphere of 5.0% O2, 5.6%CO2. Embryos were cultured alternatively in either LifeGlobal Media® (LifeGlobal, Brussels, Belgium) or CSC™, (Irvine Scientific, Santa Ana, CA, USA) and annotated using the embryoscope software. The timing of early morphokinetic parameters—including time of appearance of the second polar body (tPB2), time of pronuclei appearance (tPNa) and fading (tPNf), cleavage timings from a zygote to a 9-cell embryo (t2-t9)—as well as late morphokinetic parameters–including full compaction at the morula stage (tM), start of blastulation (tSB) and time to full blastocyst (tB)—were determined by three experienced embryologists using the TLMS21 Morphological evaluation at 16–18 h post insemination to check for normal fertilization and at 44 and 68 h to assess blastomere number and size and degree of fragmentation, was also recorded.

In order to control for fertilization method (conventional insemination or ICSI), we calculated the time interval from tPNf to the time of all other events. Cleavage timings were determined when the cells were separated by different cytoplasmic membranes. tM was determined when cell boundaries were indistinguishable. tSB was defined when an initial cavity was observed and tB when the trophectoderm pushed the zona pellucida. tEB (expanded blastocyst) was defined when the embryo volume increased, expansion of the blastocoel cavity appeared and the zona pellucida was markedly thinned [21, 22].

Other parameters we investigated included the known implantation data (KID) score of day 3 and 5 embryos as provided by the manufacturer of the Embryoscopetm, the synchrony of the divisions of the second and the third cell cycles (s2 = t4-t3 and s3 = t8-t5, respectively), and their duration(CC2 = t3-t2 and CC3 = t5-t3, respectively).

Embryo selection and transfer

The day of embryo transfer was determined based on the quantity and quality of embryos either during the cleavage stage or after blastulation. Embryos were selected for transfer based on their KIDScore™, approved by morphological evaluation. The KID Scoretm model is embedded in the annotation software of the Embryoscopetm and offered as a tool for deselection of embryos showing erratic morphokinetic patterns. The model is based on cumulative known clinical outcomes from a large number of IVF centers [23]. Because it requires day-3 annotations, embryos transferred on day 2 were excluded from this comparison. KID Score was used as a standardized scale (values ranging from 1 to 5), and was stratified as high (4–5) or low (1–3). A day 5 KID score (ranging from 1 to 9.9) was determined for embryos that were cultured to day 5 by the annotation software of the Embryoscopetm, and was stratified as high (≥ 8) or low (< 8).

Embryos were transferred on day 2–5 of culture under abdominal ultrasound guidance. Luteal support was provided starting the day after oocyte retrieval, using vaginal progesterone: Endometrin® 100 mg twice a day (Ferring, Saint-Prex, Switzerland) or Crinone® one application a day (Merck, Kenilworth, New Jersey). The dose was doubled (Endometrin® 200 mg twice a day or Crinone® one application twice a day) and oral estradiol was added (Estrofem®, Novo, Denmark 2 mg, 3 times a day) if Decapeptyl® alone was used for triggering. Pregnancy was first assessed by serum βHCG measurement 14 days after oocyte aspiration. Clinical pregnancy was confirmed by transvaginal ultrasound that demonstrated an intrauterine gestational sac with a fetal heartbeat during the 7th gestational week.

Data collection

Clinical characteristics and treatment parameters and outcomes including perinatal complications were collected from the hospital's computerized database and were analyzed anonymously. Time-lapse morphokinetic assessment was performed by one of our trained embryologists.

Perinatal complications that were assessed included: gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM, International Classification of Diseases [ICD] 10 code O.24.4), small for gestational age neonates (SGA, defined as < 10th birthweight percentile according to national population-based live born infant birthweight curves [24]), preterm labor (PTL, defined as delivery < 37 weeks of gestation), preeclamptic toxemia (PET, ICD 10 codes O.14, O.15), and 3rd stage of labor complications (including post-partum hemorrhage estimated at > 500 ml of blood loss, manual removal of the placenta or revision of the uterine cavity). A composite outcome of placental complications was defined as existence of any of the following: GDM, SGA, PTL or PET. For each perinatal complication, the studied pregnancies were divided into two groups of complicated (cases) and uncomplicated (controls).

Statistical analysis

SAS software, version 9.4, was used for statistical analysis. The studied embryos were divided into two groups of complicated and uncomplicated pregnancies for each of the studied perinatal complications. Continuous variables were compared by student t-test and are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were compared by chi-squared test and are presented as number and percent. Univariate analysis was used to compare baseline and treatment characteristics, as well as morphokinetic parameters between cases (complicated) and controls (uncomplicated). To control for potential obstetrical risk factors, morphokinetic variables that were found to be significantly different in the univariate analysis between complicated and uncomplicated pregnancies, were analyzed in a multivariate logistic regression model defining the perinatal complication as a dependent variable, and maternal age, fertilization method, the day of embryo transfer and the morphokinetic variable as independent variables. We also used univariate analysis to compare baseline characteristics and perinatal complications of embryos with high (4–5) and low (0–3) day 3 KID scores. In order to control for the effect of PGT, we conducted a separate statistical analysis excluding PGT cases (N = 138). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 176 fresh single embryo transfers that resulted in 176 singleton live births met the inclusion criteria. The prevalence of the different obstetrical complications investigated were in accordance with published literature [25–29]: seventeen patients (9.6%) were diagnosed with GDM, 14 (7.9%) with SGA, 22 (12.5%) with PTL, 11 (6.3%) with PET, 16 (9.1%) with third stage of labor complications, and 51 (28.9%) with the composite outcome of placental complications.

Maternal baseline and treatment characteristics of the control versus study groups are presented in Table 1. There were no inter-group differences in baseline characteristics, including age, BMI, and infertility diagnosis, excluding a lower BMI in the SGA group only (21.4±3.3 vs. 24.1±6.0 in the non-SGA group, p=0.024). IVF treatment characteristics, including: treatment protocol; type of trigger; FSH dosage; number of aspirated oocytes; performance of PGT; fertilization method; culture media and day of ET, were also comparable between groups.

Morphokinetic parameters and embryo grading of the different study groups are presented in Table 2. Early morphokinetic parameters, including pronucleus fading, cleavage timings (t2-t9), second and third cell cycle duration (CC2, CC3) synchrony (S2, S3), and late morphokinetic parameters, did not differ between groups, except for a shorter time to full blastocyte in the SGA group compared to the control group (tB-tPNf= 75.5±1.3 hours vs. 79.5±4.8 in the non-SGA group, p<0.001) and a shorter third cell cycle duration in the PET group compared to the control group (CC3= 12.4±1.1 hours vs. 13.6±2.9 in the non-PET group, p=0.02). Table 3 represents the multivariate logistic regression analysis controlling for maternal age, day of embryo transfer and fertilization method. Following multivariate regression, tB-tPNF was not significantly associated with SGA (adjusted OR 0.884, 95% CI 0.696-1.121) and CC3 was not significantly associated with PET (adjusted OR 0.875 95% CI 0.702-1.091). There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in the average day 3 and day 5 KID scores, nor in the distribution of embryos' grading according to day 3 and 5 KID scores, except for a higher KID3 score in the SGA group (4.83±0.39 vs. 4.50±1.0 in the non-SGA group, p=0.027). Following multivariate regression analysis controlling for maternal age, day of embryo transfer and fertilization method, day 3 KID score was not found to be significantly associated with SGA (adjusted OR 1.201, 95% CI 0.535-2.696, Table 3).

Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate the existence of an association between morphokinetic parameters and obstetric and perinatal complications, and after controlling for potential confounders, no significant association between them was demonstrated.

The higher reported prevalence of obstetrical complications in singleton IVF pregnancies might be related to embryonic factors - structural, genetic or epigenetic; maternal factors - baseline characteristics, uterine and endometrial factors, and the cause of infertility; or endocrine factors – development and implantation in supra-physiological levels of estradiol [30–32]. The timeline of events in the embryonic culture may theoretically affect the events of implantation which, in turn, can be associated with the occurrence of placental complications.

Fresh and frozen IVF pregnancies differ in their obstetric complication profiles. For example, the prevalence of SGA and PTL is higher in fresh IVF cycles, whilst the prevalence of PET and macrosomia is higher in frozen-thawed cycles [31, 33–35]. This suggests that the endocrine environment, as well as the presence of a corpus luteum, have an important role in determining the risk for obstetrical complications [33, 36]. On the other hand, another explanation for the improved perinatal outcome in Frozen-Thawed embryo transfer cycles could be the selection of good quality embryos [19]. Moreover, other obstetrical complications such as third stage of labor complications, are more prevalent in both fresh and frozen-thawed IVF cycles compared to spontaneous pregnancies [15, 29, 37], suggesting an embryonic effect on obstetrical outcomes.

In order to better understand the association between obstetrical complications and embryonic factors, we conducted a cohort study of all the embryonic cultures performed in a TLMS which resulted in live births following a fresh single ET. The TLMS currently provides the most abundant non-invasive information on embryonic development in vitro, so we sought to examine if any association exists between these parameters and the occurrence of obstetrical complications, providing implantation occurred. Only cases in which full data regarding both the IVF treatment and the delivery were available were included. Within a large quantity of data, we did find several isolated associations between morphokinetic parameters and obstetrical complications which reached statistical significance in a univariate analysis. However, none of these associations remained significant after controlling for confounders using a multivariate analysis. This implies that there is no specific visual embryonic factor, other than the in vitro culture itself, to which the higher prevalence of obstetrical complications in IVF gestations can be attributed. This insight is consistent with previous studies that demonstrated a correlation between obstetrical complications and non-embryonic parameters such as endometrial thickness [38–40] or estradiol level [41] on the day of ovulation triggering.

Different obstetrical complications such as PET, SGA and preterm birth are associated with impaired implantation and pathological trophoblastic invasion [42–45]. Despite the reported association between morphokinetic parameters and implantation rates [4–6, 23], our study did not demonstrate an association between morphokinetic parameters and implantation quality, as reflected by a comparable obstetric complication profile in pregnancies conceived through transfer of low or high KIDscore embryos. This finding is consistent with a previous study that demonstrated no association between embryo quality in terms of traditional embryo morphology and obstetrical complications [19].

In contrast to the single previous study that investigated this issue9, we found only scattered statistically significant differences between time lapse parameters and obstetrical complications in the univariate analysis, and no statistically significant correlation after controlling for patient's age, fertilization method and day of embryo transfer in a multivariate regression model. The differences between the two studies may result from lack of controlling for fertilization method in the other study, a different population of embryos included (only blastocytes in the previous study compared to blastocytes and cleavage-cell embryos in our study), or different sample sizes (85 embryos compared to 176 embryos in our study).

Our results suggest that the morphokinetic data is relevant only to the implantation and the early gestational periods. Patient and physician concerns regarding the transfer of eligible embryos with suboptimal morphokinetic evaluation as a risk factor for obstetrical complications are absolutely not supported by our study, which is the largest in the published literature.

While Doron-Lalehzari et al. [9] demonstrated an association between longer time lapse interval durations and obstetrical complications, our study showed an association between SGA and shorter time to full blastulation and a high KID3 score, and between PET and a shorter third cell-cycle duration. Theoretically, we would expect obstetrical complications known to be related to poor placentation [43–45] to be associated with morphokinetic parameters which are known to be related to a decrease in implantation potential. In our study, the univariate analysis demonstrated an opposite association - morphokinetic parameters that are known to be related to increased implantation potential such as a high KID score and shorter time to blastulation [23, 46], were found to be associated with SGA. Third cell cycle duration (CC3=t5-t3) is an important parameter in the KID score model [23] and known to be associated with implantation potential. Interestingly, the univariate analysis in our study revealed an association between lower CC3 and PET. However, this association wasn’t confirmed in the multivariate analysis after controlling for the relevant confounders. Therefore, we believe that these few apparently significant associations in the univariate analyses scattered in a large data set, which make no biological sense and were not found to be significant in multivariate analysis, are of no importance.

The strengths of our study lie in the detailed information collected regarding each embryo and pregnancy, and the fact that all included embryos resulted in live births. Limitations of our study include its' retrospective nature, sample size, different days of embryo transfer, and the inclusion of PGT embryos. Our sample size was determined by the number of cases of singleton live births after single fresh embryo transfer of an embryo that was cultured in TLMS. The fact that we conducted multiple comparisons between different raw and calculated morphokinetic parameters, and the fact that this is the largest study in the literature investigating this association, allows us to safely suggest, that no association between morphokinetic parameters and perinatal complications does exist.

Regarding the inclusion of PGT embryos, embryo biopsies are routinely performed on day 3-5 of culture, suggesting they had no effect on the interpretation of early morphokinetic parameters and day 3 KIDscores. Moreover, the percentage of embryos that underwent PGT was comparable between the groups with and without obstetrical complications, and a separate statistical analysis excluding PGT cycles/pregnancies also demonstrated similar results.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that time lapse parameters of embryos that were transferred and resulted in live births are not predictive of obstetrical complications and seem not to be associated with impaired placentation. This may reassure clinicians, embryologists and patients, that although the chance of a clinical pregnancy with a single low KIDscore embryo may be lower than that of a high KIDscore embryo, once a clinical pregnancy is achieved, the chances for obstetrical complications such as PET, PTB, SGA, GDM and third stage of labor complications are not increased just because an embryo of “lower quality” was transferred.
