Longitudinal recovery of physical function after lung transplantation: a comparative analysis of ECMO and non-ECMO recipients
Authors: Woo Chul Son, Jong Yoon Chang, Ji Yeon Park, Jun Hyeok Lee, Geon Woo Kim, Jung Hwa Do, Sang-Bum Hong, Ho Cheol Kim, Won Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLung transplantation (LT) improves survival in patients with end-stage lung disease; however, recovery of physical function after transplantation remains insufficiently characterized, particularly in critically ill recipients bridged with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Understanding longitudinal functional recovery and potential differences between ECMO and non-ECMO recipients is essential for optimizing post-transplant rehabilitation strategies.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 84 adult patients who underwent lung or heart–lung transplantation between 2018 and 2023. Physical function was assessed at 6 and 12 months using the 6-minute walk test, gait speed, and strength measures. Changes over time and group differences were evaluated using longitudinal statistical models.ResultsFifty patients (59.5%) required ECMO support. Overall physical function significantly improved from 6 to 12 months, with 93.4% achieving independent ambulation by 12 months. ECMO recipients had a lower proportion of independent ambulation at 6 months compared with non-ECMO recipients. At 12 months, no statistically significant between-group difference was observed in the proportion of patients achieving independent ambulation.ConclusionsLT recipients showed significant improvement in physical function between 6 and 12 months after transplantation. ECMO-bridged recipients had lower independent ambulation rates at 6 months, while no statistically significant between-group difference was observed at 12 months. Further studies incorporating pretransplant and early postoperative assessments are needed to better characterize functional recovery after lung transplantation.

Introduction

Lung transplantation is a life-saving procedure for patients with end-stage lung disease, significantly improving survival and quality of life (QOL) [1]. As survival rates following lung transplantation increase, attention has shifted toward enhancing the exercise capacity, independence, and overall QOL of these individuals [2].

Patients who undergo lung transplantation often experience a slow recovery of their physical function because their functional status is already reduced before surgery due to their advanced lung disease [3, 4]. Furthermore, long-term hospitalization, physical inactivity, immunosuppressive therapy, and graft rejection negatively affect the recovery of physical function after transplantation [5]. The reduced physical function interferes with daily activities and diminishes the QOL post-transplant [6, 7]. However, few studies have described how long reduced physical function persists after lung transplantation and how physical function changes during the post-transplant follow-up period [8].

Pretransplant physical function is closely associated with post-transplant outcomes [9]. Thus, a good physical function should be maintained before lung transplantation. In Korea, however, lung allocation is based primarily on medical urgency, with the highest priority given to patients requiring mechanical ventilation (MV) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) [10]. Consequently, illness severity among transplant recipients has increased; currently, > 40% of recipients require MV or ECMO before surgery [11]. Such preoperative deterioration, often accompanied by a poor physical function, may delay postoperative recovery and prolong ICU and hospital stays [12]. Only a few studies have examined the effect of ECMO on physical recovery after lung transplantation and compared patients with and without ECMO [13]. Studies on functional recovery in critically ill patients, including those supported by ECMO, remain limited.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate changes in physical function between 6 and 12 months after lung transplantation. The secondary objective was to compare physical function at these follow-up time points between recipients with and without ECMO bridge to transplantation.

Methods

Study protocol

This study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in Seoul, Republic of Korea. Data were retrospectively collected from patients who were 18 years or older and underwent lung or heart–lung transplantation between August 2018 and August 2023. Patients were included if they underwent at least one physical function evaluation at 6 or 12 months postoperation (Fig. 1). Patients who refused or failed to undergo evaluation because of various causes such as major neurological deficits were excluded.

Enrollment of the patients

All patients were subjected to individualized pulmonary rehabilitation five times per week whenever feasible. With the goal of early mobilization, rehabilitation was initiated in the ICU and tailored to each patient’s tolerance as assessed by the attending physicians. After the patients were transferred to the general ward, rehabilitation continued until discharge. The program included breathing exercises, aerobic training, and resistance training within a tolerable range. After discharge, patients were advised to continue home-based aerobic and resistance exercises according to their functional status. Outpatient rehabilitation or pulmonary rehabilitation was prescribed when clinically indicated. However, detailed information regarding the frequency, intensity, adherence, and duration of post-discharge physical therapy was not systematically recorded because of the retrospective study design.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval number: S2024-0370-0002). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study involving anonymized data.

Clinical characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, height, weight, and primary diagnosis leading to transplantation. Operative and perioperative data included the type of transplantation (single, bilateral, or heart–lung), ECMO use and duration, preoperative and postoperative ICU stay, total hospital length of stay, and number of readmissions. Total hospital length of stay was defined as the number of days from admission for transplantation-related care to hospital discharge.

Physical function measure

Physical function was assessed at 6 and 12 months by using multiple measures: 6-minute walk test (6MWT), gait speed, functional ambulation category (FAC), five-times sit-to-stand test (FTSST), grip strength, knee extensor strength, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1).

The 6MWT and gait speed were used to assess the overall functional ability because they reflect cardiopulmonary and musculoskeletal performance [14]. The 6MWT was conducted in a 20 m straight corridor. Although the American Thoracic Society (ATS) recommends a 30 m course [15], shorter distances, such as 20 m, are frequently used in clinical and research settings and considered acceptable alternatives, particularly when space is limited [16, 17]. In this study, patients were classified into high- and low-function groups by using a threshold of 400 m walked during the 6MWT [18]. For gait speed assessment, the 10 m walk test was used [19, 20]. The participants were instructed to walk at their usual pace along a designated 10 m course, and the total time required to complete the distance was recorded. Gait speed, expressed in meters per second, was then calculated for analysis. According to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 guidelines, a gait speed of less than 1.0 m/s is considered indicative of a low physical performance [21]. On the basis of this criterion, patients with gait speeds of < 1.0 m/s were categorized in the low-function group.

The Functional Ambulation Category (FAC) was also used to assess the ambulation ability [22]. In this scale, results were interpreted as follows: FAC 0, non-functional ambulation; FAC 1, dependence on continuous physical assistance; FAC 2, dependence on intermittent or light touch assistance; FAC 3, dependence on supervision although the patient could walk without physical assistance; FAC 4, independent ambulation on a flat ground but possible need for assistance on stairs or uneven terrains; and FAC 5, independent walking in all environments, including stairs and uneven terrains. For the purposes of this study, the patients were classified on the basis of the threshold of FAC 4, which denotes independent ambulation without supervision. Patients with FAC scores of 4 or 5 were categorized as the high-function group, while those with scores of 0 to 3 were classified as the low-function group. Because FAC is an ordinal scale, the distribution of the original FAC categories was additionally summarized in the supplementary Table 1.

For strength evaluation, the FTSST, grip strength testing, and knee extensor strength testing were conducted. The FTSST was used to assess the lower limb strength, balance, and mobility [23]. In accordance with the AWGS 2019 guidelines, which define a performance time of ≥ 12 s as indicative of a low physical function, this threshold was adopted for classification in our study [21]. Grip strength was measured using a Takei handgrip dynamometer (Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). During the test, the patients were seated; their shoulder was adducted and in neutral rotation, and their forearm and wrist were in a neutral position. They were instructed to squeeze the dynamometer as forcefully as possible for 3–5 s. Two trials were conducted for each hand, and the highest recorded value was used for analysis. Knee extensor strength was assessed using a leg strength analyzer (IB-LS, InBody Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea). In isometric peak torque measurement, the patients were seated upright, and their hips were flexed at 90°. During the extension phase, they were instructed to push as hard and as quickly as possible. Two trials were conducted for each leg, and the highest value was recorded for analysis.

For respiratory function evaluation, MIP and FEV1 were measured. MIP was assessed using a digital spirometer (Pony FX, Cosmed Inc.). During the assessment, the patients were seated upright, fitted with a nose clip, and instructed to gently bite the mouthpiece. This procedure was demonstrated by the examiner first. The patients were then instructed to exhale slowly and completely, maintain a tight seal around the new mouthpiece, and subsequently inhale as forcefully as possible. Each test was repeated twice, and the highest value was recorded for analysis [24]. FEV1 was measured via spirometry, which was performed in accordance with the guidelines established by the American Thoracic Society [25].

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were compared using two-sample t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Certain outcomes, including the 6MWT, gait speed, FAC, and FTSST, were categorized into high- and low-function groups based on the criteria described above, and generalized estimating equations analyses were applied. For FAC, the original ordinal category distribution was also presented as supplementary data to reduce information loss from dichotomization. The patients who were unable to independently perform the respective assessments were included in the low-function group, thereby minimizing the risk of overestimating their recovery status.

The patients were stratified into ECMO and non-ECMO groups to evaluate the effect of ECMO. Differences in recovery rates between the two groups were assessed using key functional outcomes, namely, the 6MWT, FAC, and grip strength. The group means of 6MWT and grip strength were analyzed as continuous variables; the 6MWT and FAC were also analyzed as categorical variables to account for patients who were unable to perform the 6MWT because of a limited ambulatory ability. Linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts were used for continuous outcomes (6MWT and grip strength), while generalized estimating equations were applied for categorical outcomes (6MWT and FAC). All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median values, whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages.

Results

Of the 127 patients initially screened, 12 patients who expired before 6 months postoperation and 1 patient who had a postoperative major neurologic deficit were excluded. In addition, 17 patients refused evaluation, and 13 patients were not assessed at either the 6th or 12th month of follow-up. As a result, 84 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the overall cohort was 54.73 years, and 92.9% of the patients underwent bilateral lung transplantation. The proportion of women in the ECMO group and their BMI at the time of transplantation were significantly higher than those of the non-ECMO group. Furthermore, the proportion of patients with interstitial lung disease was higher in the ECMO group than in the non-ECMO group. Preoperative and postoperative ICU stays were significantly longer in the ECMO group than in the non-ECMO group. The median total hospital length of stay was also significantly longer in the ECMO group (104 days) than in the non-ECMO group (46.5 days).

In Tables 2 and 3, the physical function of the overall cohort of lung transplant recipients was compared at 6 and 12 months postoperation. Non-dichotomized physical function measures at each time point are provided in Supplementary Table 2. All measures of physical and respiratory functions indicated statistically significant improvements at 12 months. Specifically, the proportion of patients in the high-function group increased over time (Table 3); the proportion of patients with FAC ≥ 4 reached 93.4% at 12 months, indicating that most patients achieved independent ambulation 1 year after transplantation. The proportions of the patients in the high-function group at 12 months in the 6MWT and gait speed test were 67.2% and 74.5%, respectively. These findings showed that a considerable proportion of patients achieved favorable functional outcomes.

Tables 4 and 5 present physical function measures at 6 and 12 months according to ECMO use. Additional non-dichotomized physical function measures stratified by ECMO use are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Table 4 shows the changes in the 6MWT and grip strength between the two groups. The 6MWT and grip strength of both groups significantly improved, but their degree of improvement did not significantly differ. The proportion of patients classified in the high-function group based on the 6MWT was numerically lower in the ECMO group than in the non-ECMO group at both 6 and 12 months, but the between-group differences were not statistically significant (Table 5). However, the proportion of the patients achieving independent ambulation on level surfaces (FAC 4 or 5) at 6 months was significantly lower in the ECMO group than in the non-ECMO group. After adjustment for age and sex, the increase in the proportion of patients achieving FAC ≥ 4 from 6 to 12 months was greater in the ECMO group than in the non-ECMO group.

Discussion

This study evaluated physical function at 6 and 12 months after lung transplantation and compared these outcomes between recipients with and without ECMO bridge to transplantation. Previous studies primarily evaluated the overall prognosis after transplantation or focused only on a limited domain such as ambulation when assessing functional outcomes [26–28]. The present study is among the first to comprehensively evaluate physical function at 6 and 12 months after lung transplantation, specifically comparing outcomes according to ECMO use. We also assessed not only muscle strength but also gait performance and respiratory function; thus, we more comprehensively evaluated the patients’ physical performance. Our findings provide descriptive information on functional changes between 6 and 12 months after lung transplantation and may help identify recipients who require continued functional monitoring after discharge.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population. Of the 84 patients, 50 (59.5%) required ECMO support. The mean BMI of the entire cohort was 22.29, indicating a relatively low baseline BMI. Postoperative ICU stay and hospital length of stay were significantly longer in the ECMO group than in the non-ECMO group. The high proportion of ECMO-bridged recipients in this cohort reflects the case mix of a high-volume transplant center caring for critically ill patients. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to centers with different recipient profiles or lower ECMO use may be limited. However, functional recovery after lung transplantation in recipients requiring ECMO support remains insufficiently characterized, and this study provides descriptive data on physical function at 6 and 12 months in this population [29].

Consistent with previous findings, the present results revealed that overall, the physical function of lung transplant recipients significantly improved at 12 months compared with that at 6 months (Table 2) [6, 30]. When functional thresholds were applied, at 6 months, 33% of the patients had an FAC of ≤ 3, indicating that they still required assistance during ambulation. By 12 months, however, < 10% of the patients had not achieved independent ambulation (Table 3). This finding was in contrast with previous reports suggesting that no additional functional improvement occurred beyond 6 months post-transplantation [31]. This discrepancy may be related to differences in baseline illness severity, perioperative course, and post-transplant management across cohorts. However, because pretransplant physical function was not available in this study, this explanation remains speculative. Given the critical status of these patients, longer recovery periods are typically required, adequate recovery may not be observed at 6 months, and improvements continue over a longer period [12]. Despite the significant improvement by 12 months, approximately 25%–30% of patients still met the AWGS criteria for a low physical function when they were assessed using the 6MWT, gait speed, and FTSST. Taken together, these results suggest that although physical function improved between 6 and 12 months, approximately 25%–30% of patients still had low physical performance at 12 months [21].

Tables 4 and 5 present the comparisons between the ECMO and non-ECMO groups in terms of functional outcomes measured as continuous and categorical variables. At 6 and 12 months, the mean values of 6MWT and grip strength were higher in the non-ECMO group than in the ECMO group, but these differences were not statistically significant after adjustment for age and sex. This finding might be due to the relatively small sample size of the non-ECMO group and the higher proportion of women in the ECMO group. Conversely, when the FAC was used as a categorical measure, the proportion of the patients achieving independent ambulation (FAC ≥ 4) was significantly higher in the non-ECMO group than in the ECMO group at 6 months. However, by 12 months, the two groups had no significant differences; specifically, > 90% of the patients in both groups achieved independent ambulation. These findings suggest that ECMO-bridged recipients had lower independent ambulation rates at 6 months, but many achieved independent ambulation by 12 months [12, 13].

ECMO-bridged recipients showed lower physical function at 6 months in some measures, particularly FAC-based independent ambulation, but the between-group differences were attenuated at 12 months (Tables 4 and 5). Physical functions, including gait speed and SPPB, are associated with metabolic complications, falls, healthcare utilization, survival, and healthy life expectancy in patients with chronic disease and older adults [21, 32]. Physical functions at the time of assessment reflect not only current health status and behaviors but also predict long-term health outcomes [32, 33]. Therefore, low physical function at follow-up may indicate a subgroup requiring closer clinical monitoring (Table 5). Further studies are needed to determine whether structured post-discharge rehabilitation improves subsequent functional outcomes in patients with low physical function after transplantation.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and the inclusion of only patients who survived at least 6 months and underwent follow-up functional evaluation may have introduced selection bias and overestimated functional recovery. Second, pretransplant and early postoperative physical function were not available, limiting our ability to characterize the full course of functional change after transplantation. Third, some outcome data and detailed post-discharge rehabilitation information, including exercise intensity, adherence, and outpatient therapy frequency, were not systematically recorded. Fourth, although FAC was dichotomized using a clinically meaningful threshold, this approach may have resulted in loss of information from the original ordinal scale. Finally, information regarding awake status, invasive mechanical ventilation, ambulatory status, and participation in physical therapy among ECMO-bridged recipients was not systematically collected. Future prospective studies should include these variables to better characterize functional recovery after lung transplantation.

Conclusion

Lung transplant recipients showed significant improvements in physical function between 6 and 12 months after transplantation. Most patients achieved independent ambulation by 12 months, although approximately one-fourth continued to exhibit low physical performance. ECMO-bridged recipients had lower independent ambulation rates at 6 months, whereas no statistically significant between-group difference was observed at 12 months. Further studies including pretransplant and early postoperative functional assessments are needed to better characterize recovery trajectories after lung transplantation.
