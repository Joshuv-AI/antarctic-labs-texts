Ferulic acid promotes hair growth via estrogen receptor alpha activation in cultured human dermal papilla cells
Authors: Heena Rim, Jaeyoon Kim, Jang Ho Joo, Sung Hyun Kim, Yun-Ho Choi, Jae Young Shin, Mi Hee Kwack, So Young Lee, Seung-Hyun Jun, Young Kwan Sung, Eui Taek Jeong, Nae Gyu Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent alopecia treatments have side effects and limited efficacy, highlighting the need for better alternatives. Estrogen and estrogen receptor alpha (ERα)-targeting therapies have shown promise in addressing female-pattern hair loss, outperforming some conventional options. This study investigated the hair growth-promoting effects of Cnidium officinale extract (COE) and its active compound, ferulic acid (FA), focusing on their role in activating ERα. Using human dermal papilla cells (DPCs) and cultured human hair follicles, we assessed cell proliferation, mitochondrial function, gene expression, and protein levels including transcriptome analysis. Both COE and FA significantly enhanced DPCs proliferation and mitochondrial activity, and extended hair follicle length during the anagen (growth) phase. Transcriptomic comparisons between FA- and estrogen-treated cells revealed upregulation of genes involved in energy metabolism, particularly those related to ATP regeneration. FA also elevated c-JUN mRNA expression, indicating estrogen-like ERα activation, and increased the expression of MAPK pathway-associated proteins, along with key growth factors. Additionally, co-treatment with FA and NMN, a cellular activity regulator, enhanced mitochondrial activity in a concentration-dependent manner beyond NMN alone. These findings suggest that COE and FA stimulate hair growth by enhancing DPCs function, with FA’s ERα-activating properties offering potential as a novel therapeutic option for alopecia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-36774-x.Keywords: Dermal papilla cells, Cnidium officinale extract, Ferulic acid, Hair growth, Estrogen, Estrogen receptor alphaSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Endocrinology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Alopecia is a common hair disorder, referred to simply as hair loss1. Although it was long considered a sign of aging, various other causes of alopecia such as stress, nutritional deficiency, and hormone imbalances have been discovered2–4. However, to date, only three drugs, minoxidil (MNX), finasteride, and baricitinib, have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for alopecia treatment5.

MNX, the most widely used drug for alopecia, is a potassium channel opener that promotes hair growth in both male- and female-patterned alopecia6. However, MNX has shown limited efficacy against female androgenic alopecia in previous reports7. Furthermore, MNX has side effects, such as dermatitis, irritation, and pruritus8. Baricitinib, a reversible inhibitor of Janus kinase 1/2, has been approved as a new hair loss treatment and has been reported to have side effects, such as nausea and reactivation of herpesvirus infection9.

Finasteride, a drug for androgenic alopecia, works as a competitive inhibitor of type 2 5-alpha reductase, blocking the conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), thereby increasing anagen hair10,11. Androgenetic alopecia is a progressive hereditary thinning of hair caused by androgen-dependent processes due to the binding of DHT to the androgen receptor12. However, the hormonal disruption effect of finasteride means it is contraindicated in patients with female-patterned hair loss13. Symptoms of alopecia have been reported in patients with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)—a common endocrinopathy affecting women—and those experiencing menopause14,15. In addition, administration of the aromatase inhibitor tamoxifen was reported to cause alopecia as a side effect16,17. Administering estrogen to the scalp could help treat female patients with alopecia18. Therefore, estrogen and estrogen receptor-targeted therapy, rather than DHT-targeted approaches, are effective therapeutic strategies for female alopecia. Since the number of people experiencing hair loss is increasing, and the causes and symptoms are diverse19,20, it is essential to develop appropriate and effective treatments.

There are several types of estrogen and the major female sex hormones, including estrone (E1), estradiol (E2), and estriol (E3)21. Estradiol, the most common form of estrogen, regulates the female reproductive cycle and is important for the development and maintenance of female reproductive tissues. Estradiol acts as an agonist of the estrogen receptors (ERs), and activated ERs regulate the expression of several genes, not only in the reproductive organs but also in the adipose tissue and brain22. ER consists of two types: alpha (ERα) and beta (ERβ)23. ERα is mainly specialized in regulating skeletal homeostasis and metabolism, while ERβ mainly regulates the central nervous and immune system24. Especially, ERα and Estrogen-related receptor alpha are known to regulate the expression of mitochondrial DNA and oxidative metabolism which can affect energy homeostasis and body weight25. In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between ERα and hair cycle by focusing on the activity of ERα.

The developmental cycle of hair follicles consists of three stages: anagen (proliferation), catagen (regression), and telogen (resting)26. Hair loss can be defined as the result of a decrease in hair follicles at the anagen stage and an increase in follicles at the telogen stage27. Dermal papilla cells (DPCs), which are differentiated from mesenchymal stem cells and located at the core of the hair follicle, are major hair cycle regulators that control external signals and stimuli delivered through cytokines and junctions28. Several growth factors from DPCs, such as bFGF, KGF, and VEGF stimulate hair follicle development and prolong the anagen phase through cellular proliferation29,30.

Cnidium officinale is a perennial herbaceous plant belonging to the family Umbrelliferae that has been used in traditional medicine in Korea, China, and Japan. In cosmetics, Cnidium officinale extract (COE) is mainly used for its whitening effect to relieve spots and freckles, or to clean the skin by removing impurities31. Compounds extracted from the rhizomes of Cnidium officinale contain large amounts of phenolic substances, are known to facilitate blood circulation, and have analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects32. However, our knowledge of the hair growth-promoting effects and active ingredients of COE remain limited.

Here, we investigated the various effects of COE and its individual components on alopecia and aimed to explore the rational correlation between the regulation of ERα activity and alopecia treatment. We showed that energy metabolism was increased by COE and ferulic acid—a single component of COE—in DPCs. Furthermore, we showed that ferulic acid alone could activate ERα. Through this process, we confirmed that COE and ferulic acid can promote hair growth by increasing ERα activity.

Results

COE increases energy metabolism in cultured DPCs

The results of the CCK-8 assay using various concentrations of COE showed that COE increased the proliferation of DPCs. When treated with 10, 20, and 50 µg/mL of COE, cell viability increased by 28, 23, and 29%, respectively compared to the control group (Supplementary Figure S1a). Since CCK-8 responds to the dehydrogenase activity of cell mitochondria, COE can be predicted to affect mitochondrial activity. To confirm this, a JC-1 MMP assay was performed by treating DPCs with COE. In Fig. 1a, mitochondrial activity, or mitochondrial membrane potential (∆Ψ), is expressed as the ratio of the aggregate form to the monomer form of JC-1 dye. When treated with COE concentrations of 100 µg/mL, mitochondrial activity of DPCs increased up to 52.4% compared to the control group. Because mitochondrial activity and mitochondrial membrane potential indicate the capacity for energy generation in the mitochondria, these results indicate that COE is associated with the promotion of energy metabolism in DPCs cultures.

Next, we confirmed the expression of Wnt-related genes, an important signaling pathway in DPCs, to explore the effect of COE on energy metabolism in more detail. In Fig. 1b, the mRNA expression of Fzd5 gene increased in a COE concentration-dependent manner; at a COE concentration of 100 µg/mL, the Fzd5 gene expression increased more than 3.4-fold compared to the control group. In Fig. 1c, when DPCs were treated with 100 µg/mL of COE alone, LEF-1 gene expression did not change; however, when co-treated with 50 ng/mL of Wnt3a and 100 µg/mL of COE, LEF-1 gene expression increased more notably than when Wnt3a was applied alone.

Additionally, changes in the expression of several growth factors after COE treatment were confirmed using an antibody array membrane kit. DPCs were known to secrete diverse growth factors for regulation of hair follicles, and Fig. 1d shows that compared to control samples, the expression of EGF, FGF-7, PDGFRα, and IGF-1 increased in samples treated with 40 µg/mL of COE. Taken together, these results suggest that COE promotes hair growth by enhancing cellular energy metabolism and activating hair follicles in cultured DPCs.

COE promotes hair follicle growth via increasing anagen stage

We used a hair follicle ex vivo organ culture model to investigate the direct effect of COE on the hair follicle stage. Hair follicles in the anagen stage were cultured for 9 days and treated with test compounds every 3 days. After 9 days, 52.6% of the hair follicles in the untreated (control) group were in the anagen phase (Fig. 2). In contrast, 73.7% in the MNX-treated positive control group, and 85.7% in the group treated with 50 µg/mL COE were in the anagen stage. In other words, the number of hair follicles in the anagen and proliferation stages increased after COE treatment, which can be interpreted as direct evidence that COE promotes hair growth.

Ferulic acid binds to ERα and is involved in energy metabolism in cultured DPCs

In general, because extracts contain many substances, they can have various effects. Likewise, COE is composed of various ingredients, and these substances work comprehensively to promote hair growth, as shown above. However, because the amount of each effective ingredient is limited and the ingredients vary greatly depending on the conditions, the efficacy of extracts may be reduced compared to that of a single ingredient. Thus, it is important to identify ingredients that exert the main effects among the many single ingredients contained in COE. Thus, we performed a phytochemical analysis of COE. The results showed that the 50% ethanol extracts of Cnidium officinale contained ferulic acid, sekyunolide A, and (Z)-ligustilide (Supplementary Figure S2).

The structure of ferulic acid is shown in Fig. 3a. It is a derivative of hydroxycinnamic acid, featuring a benzene ring substituted with a methoxy group (-OCH₃) and a hydroxyl group (−OH). In addition, it contains a side chain with a trans double bond, which contributes to its strong antioxidant properties33. In addition, as a result of using the molecular interaction prediction method provided by SwissDock34,35, the predicted value of the binding between ferulic acid and the estrogen receptor alpha ligand-binding domain in complex with estradiol (5WGD) is very high. The SwissParam score, an estimate of the binding free energy of the ligand and target, was - 7.0856, showing a similar binding prediction value compared to estradiol at - 8.5607. A simulation model, using ChimeraX, is shown in Fig. 3b36.

As confirmed in COE, DPCs were treated with ferulic acid to determine its effect on cell proliferation using the CCK-8 assay. When treated with 10, 25, and 50 µM of ferulic acid, the proliferation of DPCs increased by about 26.88, 27.00, and 26.11%, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1b). Therefore, ferulic acid increased dehydrogenase activity of NADH inside the cultured DPCs, similar to COE. By confirming the mitochondrial membrane potential of ferulic acid through the JC-MMP assay, mitochondrial activity increased approximately 1.2-fold when treated with 10, 20, and 50 µM of ferulic acid compared to the control group (Fig. 3c). When treated with estradiol, the proliferation and mitochondrial activity of DPCs also increased (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Figure S3). Considering these results, ferulic acid can be considered to play an important role in the efficacy of COE in energy metabolism, playing a role similar to that of estradiol in cultured DPCs.

Transcriptome analysis revealed ferulic acid and estradiol have similar effects on energy metabolism in cultured DPCs

RNA was extracted from DPCs treated with 10 µM of ferulic acid or 100 nM of estradiol, and DEG and GO analyses were performed on the next-generation sequencing data derived from RNA sequencing and library production. In Fig. 4a, the DEGs identified between ferulic acid and the control groups are shown using a volcano plot. When the cut-off for p-value was 0.05 and the cut-off for fold change was 2.0, 138 genes showed increased expression and 81 genes showed decreased expression in the group treated with ferulic acid. Among the genes with increased expression, ALDH1L2, ARHGEF7, ACSL3, and SYF2 were included, and among the genes with decreased expression, PRPF31, PGM3, MAPK8, and ACOX3 were included.

Among the GO groups that showed significant differences in RNA expression in groups treated with ferulic acid or estradiol, genes related to oxido-reduction and ATP regeneration were commonly identified. This suggests that ferulic acid may be involved in the energy metabolism of cultured DPCs and has an effect similar to that of estradiol.

Ferulic acid increases ERα activity and promotes hair growth

Activating protein-1 (AP-1) regulates transcription through functional interference with several nuclear receptors, including ER, and is involved in various cellular processes. It comprises several combinations of JUN (c-Jun, JunB, and JunD) and FOS (c-Fos, Fra-1, Fra-2, and FosB). In particular, it has been revealed that this activity is controlled by estrogen and that a physical interaction exists between ERα and c-Jun protein45. Therefore, we treated DPCs with estradiol and observed changes in JUN expression. We confirmed that JUN mRNA expression increased after treatment with 200 nM estradiol. Additionally, when 100 nM tamoxifen, an ER inhibitor, was co-treated simultaneously with estradiol, the mRNA expression of JUN(c-Jun) was reduced to the untreated level (Fig. 5a). The mRNA expression of JUN, which was decreased by simultaneous treatment with estradiol and tamoxifen, recovered in proportion to its concentration after treatment with ferulic acid (Fig. 5a). In other words, the specific ER-mediated downstream pathway inhibited by tamoxifen was reactivated by ferulic acid, indicating that ferulic acid affects ER activity.

More directly, examination of ER activation shows that estradiol binding induces an agonist conformation, enabling nuclear translocation and subsequent DNA binding to activate transcription of target genes. During this process, phosphorylation mediated by kinases such as MAPK further enhances transcriptional activity by promoting ligand-independent activation of ER. Thus, an increase in phosphorylated ER reflects an increase in ER activity. In Fig. 5b, estradiol treatment elevated phospho-ER (S118) levels, and treatment with ferulic acid or COE produced a comparable increase. These findings indicate that, similar to estradiol, both ferulic acid and COE induce ER phosphorylation and contribute to its activation.

In addition, to investigate the changes in the phosphorylation pathway, we extracted proteins from DPCs treated with estradiol and ferulic acid and performed a MAPK phosphorylation antibody array assay. The expression of GSK3β decreased and p63 increased in DPCs treated with 100 nM estradiol (Fig. 5c). Similarly, when treated with 50 µM of ferulic acid or 40 µg/mL of COE, we observed that the expression of GSK3b decreased while that of p63 increased.

As shown in Fig. 1d, it was confirmed that the expression of various growth factors was increased in COE-treated DPCs. Similarly, when treated with 50 µM ferulic acid and 100 nM estradiol, the expression of EGF, FGF-7, PDGFRα, and IGF-1 also increased. (Fig. 5d) These growth factors promote the hair cycle in DPCs, leading to hair growth, so it can be seen that both ferulic acid and estradiol can promote hair growth.

Furthermore, Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is a precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), which plays a crucial role in energy transfer and production within cells and participates in various metabolic reactions. It is well established that NAD and NMN enhance mitochondrial activity in DPCs46, and in this study, we evaluated mitochondrial activation following co-treatment with NMN and ferulic acid. We found that combining ferulic acid at concentrations of 2, 5, and 10 µM with NMN at 20, 50, and 100 µg/mL led to a greater increase in mitochondrial activity than treatment with each compound alone (Fig. 5e). These findings suggest that ferulic acid and NMN may exert synergistic effects on mitochondrial function in DPCs. Further studies delineating the precise mechanisms and pathways underlying this synergy will help clarify how these two agents interact to enhance cellular activity.

Ferulic acid promotes hair follicle growth via increasing anagen stage

We also used an ex vivo culture model to determine the effect of ferulic acid on the hair follicle stage. As with COE, hair follicles were cultured for 9 days and the materials were treated at 3-day intervals. After 9 days, 46.7% of hair follicles in the untreated (control) group werein the anagen stage (Fig. 6). Meanwhile, 71.7% of hair follicles in the MNX group and 82.5% of hair follicles in the 200 µM ferulic acid treatment group were in the anagen stage. In other words, just like COE, ferulic acid also increases hair follicles in the anagen stage, which can be direct evidence that it promotes hair growth.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of ferulic acid on ER activation and its therapeutic potential for scalp health. Scalp skin health, which encompasses factors such as hair density, hair thickness, scalp skin barrier integrity, and elasticity, diminishes with age, akin to the changes observed in facial skin47. Previous studies have shown that the pattern of decline in each scalp parameter varies with senescence. In addition, age-related changes in scalp parameters have been observed to differ between male and female participants29. Clinical reports suggest that the characteristics of female-patterned hair loss are dependent on estrogen-related symptoms, but independent of a family history of alopecia48. Ferulic acid has been proposed as a beneficial ingredient for promoting healthy aging. As a phenolic substance with well-known antioxidative effects, it influences several signaling pathways in the body. It is also known to downregulate the NF-κB signaling pathway and reduce neuro-inflammation49. In addition, as an antioxidant, it may scavenge free radicals and regulate the activity of several enzymes. Therefore, ferulic acid has been widely used in cosmetics for its ability to protect the scalp skin barrier, enhance hair growth, and provide a whitening effect by inhibiting melanin production.

Hair follicles are among the most energy-consuming organs in humans50. Therefore, energy generation processes are highly activated in human hair follicle cells, particularly during the anagen stage51. In cutaneous tissues, ERs and their mediated signaling pathways contribute to the regulation of cellular energy metabolism23. Furthermore, mitochondrial energy production in the hair bulb strongly contributes to hair follicle function51 and affects hair growth in vitro and in vivo52,53. In this study, treatment with COE and its active ingredient, ferulic acid, increased cellular viability and mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψ) in cultured DPCs. (Fig. 3) In addition, transcriptomic analysis of ferulic acid and estradiol showed significantly increased oxidoreduction and ATP regeneration activities (Fig. 4). Therefore, we could conclude that ferulic acid stimulates mitochondrial function and its energy metabolism via ERα pathway signaling.

In this study, we confirmed that ferulic acid stimulates ER phosphorylation and signaling pathways, thereby increasing the expression of downstream genes, including c-Jun (Fig. 5). ER-mediated activation of ERK and JNK signaling has been reported in previous studies54. Furthermore, the direct physical interaction between ERα and c-Jun has also been examined45. Activation of c-Jun and JNK signaling strongly contributes to intercellular communication during aging, such as wrinkles and alopecia55. The β-catenin Wnt signaling pathway (canonical Wnt signaling pathway) is essential to maintaining the growth and hair-inducing activity of DPCs56. Frizzled-5 (Fzd5) protein is a receptor of Wnt protein57, and when the Wnt protein is present, it binds to the Fzd5 receptor and inhibits the degradation of β-catenin allowing β-catenin to activate the transcription of target genes. Meanwhile, LEF-1, a member of the lymphoid enhancer factor-1/T-cell factor (LEF-1/TCF) transcription factor family, mediates a nuclear response to the Wnt signal via β-catenin58. Previously, LEF-1 was shown to regulate dermal-epidermal interactions, and its expression increased in DPCs treated with Wnt3a59.

In addition, COE enhanced the activation of growth factors in DPCs. Notably, COE treatment activated several important growth factors, including EGF, KGF (FGF-7), PDGFRα, IGF-1, which were also significantly increased by ferulic acid treatment (Figs. 1 and 5). These activated growth factors play crucial roles in stimulating the hair cycle and promoting hair growth, as documented in previous studies18, and are secreted by DPCs. In particular, KGF is known to trigger signaling pathways in DPCs that are essential for hair germ proliferation and initiation of new hair cycles60. As shown in Fig. 2, COE treatment extended the anagen phase in a dose-dependent manner. This increase in growth factors, which helps prolong the anagen phase, can be anticipated to support hair bulb growth at higher levels following ferulic acid treatment. Our data demonstrate that ferulic acid may be an essential active ingredient of COE, with potential to maintain hair follicles in the anagen state by enhancing growth factor secretion.

The composition of plant extracts is characterized by a diverse array of chemicals, the specific makeup of which is influenced by environmental conditions during the harvesting of the original plants. Additionally, the effects of these extracts are harmonized by a variety of plant metabolites. Thus, it is plausible that the optimal concentrations of plant extracts, such as Houttuynia cordata, Polygonum multiflorum, and Platycladus orientalis, vary across different phenotypic parameters61–63. The effects of plant extracts result from the coordinated actions of numerous compounds, each of which exhibits a unique molecular signature.

Although both COE and ferulic acid increased the proportion of anagen-stage hair follicles in ex vivo assays (Figs. 2 and 6), such statistically significant changes may not necessarily be sufficient to induce substantial hair regrowth in vivo. The biological relevance of ex vivo anagen extension is well recognized, as it may indicate a potential hair-growth–promoting effect27; however, the translation of these findings to actual in vivo hair growth remains inherently limited due to differences in the follicular microenvironment, systemic regulation, and pharmacokinetics64. Therefore, the effects observed in this study should be interpreted as evidence of physiological activity rather than as indicators of a transformative therapeutic impact on alopecia. Further studies employing in vivo models or additional ex vivo analyses—such as direct measurements of hair-shaft elongation—will be essential to more fully assess the translational relevance of these findings.

In this study, we found that ferulic acid is the major active component of COE and stimulates hair growth by enhancing cellular energy metabolism and increasing the secretion of growth factors (Figs. 3 and 5). Furthermore, COE and ferulic acid administration resulted in elongation of the anagen stage in cultured ex vivo human hair follicles (Figs. 2 and 6). We also found that the enhancement was accompanied by ERα activation and ERα-mediated signaling activation, such as that of the JNK and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways (Figs. 1, 3 and 5). Together, these findings indicate that COE and its major component, ferulic acid, modulate key cellular and molecular processes associated with hair follicle activation.

In conclusion, our data strongly suggest the possible therapeutic potential of ferulic acid for the treatment and/or prevention of hair loss. This study may provide a direction for research on drugs and cosmetics that treat hair loss symptoms associated with estrogen and ERs. Since our study suggests that ferulic acid can mimic estrogen’s effects without the hormonal side effects of traditional hormone therapies, ferulic acid may be a safer alternative compared to current treatments, especially for female-patterned hair loss. Finally, while this study provides evidence of the efficacy of ferulic acid and COE in DPCs and cultured hair follicles, further clinical trials are necessary to confirm its safety and effectiveness in human patients. Long-term, in vivo studies are also needed to assess the sustained effects and any potential side effects of ferulic acid as a hair growth treatment, underscoring the broader relevance of our findings for the development of future therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Preparation of extract

Dried rhizomes of Cnidium officinale were purchased from Humanherb (Daegu, Korea). Dried rhizomes (100 g) were extracted with 2000 mL of 50% aqueous ethanol for 2 d at room temperature and filtered through Whatman No. 4 filter paper. The filtrate was evaporated using a vacuum evaporator under reduced pressure to obtain 15.5 g of 50% aqueous ethanol extract.

Characterization of COE

The extract was diluted 100-fold with 80% methanol. An external standard method was used for quantification65. Standard stock solutions of ferulic acid (1), senkyunolide I (2), senkyunolide A (3), and Z-ligustilide (4) were prepared with 80% methanol just before use. The standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (ferulic acid, St. Louis, MO, USA) and Chengdu Biopurify Phytochemicals Ltd. (senkyunolide I, senkyunolide A, Z-ligustilide, Chengdu, China). The high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of COE was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system. An HPLC-DAD detector was used, and the wavelength was set at 280 nm. The column used YMC J’sphere ODS-H80 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 4 μm) at 30 °C. Injection volume for sample and standard solutions was 10 µL.

Human dermal papilla cells culture

Human dermal papilla cells (DPCs) were purchased from Promocell (PromoCell GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). DPCs were cultured in basal medium supplemented with 4% fetal calf serum, 0.4% bovine pituitary extract, 1 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and 5 µg/mL insulin (Supplement Mix, Promocell). Cultured DPCs from 7 to 10 passages were used for the experiments. Cells were maintained in a humidified environment at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Cell proliferation assay

The effects of COE or ferulic acid on DPCs proliferation were examined using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The DPCs (3 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 1% FBS and 1 ng/mL bFGF (Sigma Aldrich). Cultured cells were treated with various concentrations of COE, ferulic acid, or estradiol (Sigma Aldrich) and 100 nM of MNX (Sigma Aldrich) as a control for 24 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using a JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). DPCs were seeded at 3 × 103 cells/well into 96-well plates. The cells were treated with COE, ferulic acid, estradiol in DMEM containing 1% FBS and 1 ng/mL bFGF. After 24 h, cells were stained using 10 µM JC-1 Solution. The JC-1 aggregate form was measured at 590 nm for emission (535 nm for excitation) and the monomer form at 530 nm for emission (475 nm for excitation) using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was used to study the effects of COE or ferulic acid on Wnt signaling-related genes. DPCs were seeded in 6-well plates (1.5 × 105cells/well) and incubated for 24 h. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 1% FBS and 1 ng/mL bFGF for 24 h, followed by treatment with COE, Wnt 3a(Sigma Aldrich), ferulic acid, or estradiol were treated for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy RNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (Philekorea, Seoul, Korea) in a ThermoCycler (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA samples obtained from the control and treated cells were subjected to quantitative real-time (qRT) PCR analysis.

The TaqMan probes for qRT-PCR were as follows: GAPDH assay ID 4352934E; frizzled-5 assay ID Hs00258278_s1, LEF-1 assay ID HS01547250_M1; JUN assay ID Hs01103582_s1. For the PCR reaction, TaqMan™ Universal Master Mix II, with UNG (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) was used. PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems StepOne Real Time PCR system following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting data were analyzed using Applied Biosystems StepOne software v2.3.

Quantification assay of phospho-estrogen receptor alpha (S118)

Phosphorylated Estrogen receptor α at serine 118(phospho-ER S118) was measured using a commercially available sandwich ELISA kit (Abcam). DPCs (2 × 105cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Cells were treated with 100 nM of estradiol, 20 or 50 µM of ferulic acid, or 50–100 µg/mL of COE for 24 h. After treatment, the culture supernatants were collected for estrogen receptor analysis. For the assay, lysate samples were added to microplate wells pre-coated with an antibody recognizing total ERα, enabling selective capture of ERα proteins. After washing, phosphorylated ERα was detected using a specific primary antibody followed by an HRP-linked secondary antibody. A chromogenic substrate produced a colorimetric signal proportional to the level of phosphorylated protein. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek).

Dot blot array of growth factors and MAPK phosphorylation antibody

A human growth factor antibody array membrane kit (Abcam) was used to elucidate changes in the growth factor profiles of DPCs following estradiol, ferulic acid, or COE treatment. A total of 41 types of human growth factors were analyzed simultaneously. Briefly, DPCs (1 × 105cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Cells were treated with 100 nM of estradiol, 50 µM of ferulic acid, or 40 µg/mL of COE for 24 h. After treatment, the culture supernatants were collected for growth factor analysis. Fresh medium and culture supernatant from untreated cells were used as the blank and control, respectively. Conventional immunoblotting was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Biotin-conjugated anti-cytokine antibodies were used as primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated streptavidin was used for chemiluminescence detection. The resulting blots were analyzed under the same conditions using an iBright FL1000 (Invitrogen) and ImageJ v1.51j8. Likewise, cultured DPCs treated with 100 nM of estrogen, 50 µM of ferulic acid, or 40 µg/mL of COE were prepared; next, 17 phosphorylation targets of MAPK were analyzed using a MAPK phosphorylation antibody array membrane kit (Abcam).

Human hair follicle organ culture and hair cycle scoring

Human scalp skin specimens were obtained from patients undergoing reconstructive plastic surgery after obtaining informed consent following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHA Bundang Medical Center (IRB No. 2018-09-009). Anagen human hair follicles (hHFs) were isolated by microdissection and maintained in William’s E medium (WelGENE, Kyungsan, Korea) supplemented with 10 µg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 ng/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich), 20 mM HEPES (Invitrogen-Gibco-BRL, NY, USA), and 1× antibiotic-antimycotic (Invitrogen-Gibco-BRL, NY, USA) for 1 day. Each group of 20 isolated hHFs were cultured with medium containing COE at concentrations of 2, 20, 50 µg/mL. Every third day, the medium was replaced, and hHFs were photo-documented. The hair cycle stages of cultured human hair follicles were determined on day 0 and 9 according to hair cycle guidelines66–68. In the same manner, hair follicle culture and hair cycle scoring were performed for 50, 100, and 200 µM of ferulic acid. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyungpook National University (IRB No. 2025-0421).

Transcriptome analysis

Transcriptome analysis was performed to investigate changes in gene expression after treatment with ferulic acid or estradiol. DPCs were seeded in 6-well plates (3 × 105cells/well) and incubated for 24 h. Then cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 1% FBS and 1 ng/mL bFGF. The cell samples consisted of three groups: an untreated group and another two groups treated either with 100 nM of estradiol or 10 μm of ferulic acid for 4 h. After harvesting, each cell was stored in TRIzol and rapidly frozen at − 80 °C for transport. RNA extraction and transcriptome analysis was performed at Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) using Trimmomatic v0.38, HISAT2 v2.1.0, StringTie v2.1.3b, DESeq2 v1.38.3 and g; Profiler ve111_eg58_p18_f463989d. The results were analyzed by mapping, expression profiling, and differentially expressed gene (DEG), protein-protein interaction (PPI) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses. Figures such as the PPI network were created using STRING database v12.0 (STRING: functional protein association networks), Cytoscape Web v1.0 (Cytoscape Web) and SRplot (SRplot- Science and Research online plot).

SwissDock simulation

To simulate the binding of ER and ferulic acid, we used the SwissDock platform (https://www.swissdock.ch/). The SMILES structure of ferulic acid was entered as the ligand and the target protein, 5WGD (estrogen receptor alpha ligand binding domain in complex with estradiol and SRC2-LP1) was selected. The chain labeled “A-Estrogen receptor” was chosen to prepare a modified PDB file. Next, the binding space defined with the coordinates (54, − 5, 60), and the dimensions (15, 15, 15) and random initial conditions (RIC) were set to 1. Finally, docking was performed to simulate the binding of ferulic acid to ERs in the estrogen-bound structure. The simulation results obtained in this way were visualized using the Chimera X program v1.8.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism v10.0.2 (GraphPad Software, MA, USA). Data are presented as average ± standard deviation. We assessed the data for normal distribution and similar variance between groups. Statistical significance (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001) was evaluated using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups and a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with relevant post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. All in vitro experimental data are presented as the average ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments.
