French National Guidelines for the Appropriate Use of Opioids: Analgesia, Opioid Use Disorder, and Overdose Prevention—Part 2: Prevention and Management of Opioid Use Disorder and Overdose
Authors: Célian Bertin, Julie Dupouy, Nicolas Authier, Nathalie Michenot, Sidonie Verdier, Mathieu Chappuy, Anne-Priscille Trouvin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Opioid use disorder (OUD) and overdoses represent critical challenges linked to the widespread availability of opioid medications. To address these risks, the French National Authority for Health convened a multidisciplinary working group to develop comprehensive guidelines on the prevention and management of OUD and overdoses. These recommendations expand on the second part of the guidelines, which specifically addressed the prevention and management of OUD and overdoses. Grounded in the highest level of international evidence and developed through a rigorous formal consensus process, the guidelines emphasize appropriate opioid prescribing as the cornerstone of prevention. They provide practical recommendations for identifying patients at risk, detecting problematic use early, and initiating opioid agonist treatment when indicated. The role of naloxone as an essential tool to prevent fatal overdoses is highlighted, along with strategies for coordinated and multidisciplinary care across diverse healthcare settings. By integrating prevention, treatment, and harm reduction measures, these guidelines seek to balance legitimate access to opioid analgesics with robust safeguards against opioid use disorder and overdose. Although developed within the French healthcare system, their strong evidence base and underlying clinical principles make them applicable to international practice. Together with the first part on pain management, they constitute one of the most comprehensive and up-to-date sets of recommendations on the safe and effective use of opioid medications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40122-026-00852-9.

Introduction

The first part of these guidelines from the French National Authority for Health (HAS) emphasized the essential role of opioid medications in pain management, while underscoring the importance of appropriate use to limit their potentially severe adverse effects. Experience from some countries, particularly the USA, has shown that broad access to opioids, when not accompanied by prevention and monitoring measures, can result in a marked rise in opioid-related harms, including opioid use disorder (OUD) (see definitions in Appendix 1—Supplementary Material) and fatal overdoses. This highlights the need for a comprehensive approach to care that integrates analgesia, prevention, early identification, and management of emerging difficulties in patients who are candidates for, or already receiving, opioid analgesics.

This second part focuses on the fundamental principles for identifying, preventing, and managing OUD—including situations requiring opioid agonist treatment (OAT)—as well as overdoses.

These recommendations build on the framework established for analgesia, ensuring that legitimate access to opioids is preserved while strengthening the safety of prescribing practices and improving coordination among healthcare professionals caring for patients at highest risk.

The aim of these guidelines is to support clinicians in the safe prescribing of opioids and in the prevention, identification, and management of opioid use disorder and overdose, while preserving appropriate access to opioid therapies when clinically indicated.

Method

Data Sources

The guideline development process was based on a systematic literature review conducted by the French National Authority for Health (Haute Autorité de Santé [HAS]). The search covered clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized or controlled trials, and observational studies published between January 2009 and December 2021. Literature was accessed through PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and major guideline repositories. The list of websites consulted (publishing clinical guidelines, health technology assessments, ethical or economic reports, and professional society recommendations) is provided in Table S3 (Supplementary Material). Full details of the search strategy and selection process are available in the Appendix 2 (Supplementary Material) and in the HAS guidance document “Development of Clinical Practice Guidelines—Method for Clinical Practice Recommendations [1].”

Target Patient Population

These guidelines apply to patients over 15 years of age treated with opioid analgesics for acute or chronic pain, cancer-related or noncancer-related. They also apply to patients receiving OAT for physical dependence, as well as those with physical dependence and/or OUD related to prescribed or illicit opioids.

Target Healthcare Professionals

The guidelines are intended for all healthcare professionals involved in the care of patients treated with opioid medications and/or presenting with OUD. This includes professionals working in outpatient or inpatient settings, in primary or secondary care, in social and medico-social institutions, as well as in prison healthcare units.

The French National Authority for Health (Haute Autorité de Santé [HAS]), an independent public body with a scientific mandate, aims to improve the quality of healthcare and social services for the benefit of patients. In this context, it was tasked with developing national guidelines to enhance professional practices concerning the appropriate use of opioid medications. This mandate encompassed both their role in pain management and the prevention and management of OUD and overdoses, through the development of evidence-based national recommendations using a formal consensus method [1].

Implementation

Detailed implementation procedures are provided in Appendix 2 (Supplementary Material).

These guidelines were developed in accordance with the methodology outlined in the HAS manual Development of Clinical Practice Guidelines [1]. This process is rigorous and transparent, ensuring independence and appropriate management of potential conflicts of interest.

A multidisciplinary working group (27 members, including healthcare professionals, patient representatives, and public agencies) conducted a systematic literature review (2009–2021, 4453 references), resulting in the selection of 223 high-level evidence sources (Grade A). On the basis of this review, a first draft of the guidelines was prepared and submitted to an extended reading group (49 members) for evaluation of clarity and applicability. Their feedback was incorporated into the final recommendations and summary sheets, which were validated by the HAS College and published in March 2022.

These guidelines are based on previously conducted studies and existing recommendations and do not contain any new studies involving human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

These recommendations are primarily based on high-level evidence (including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials) when available and are complemented by expert consensus in areas where evidence is limited.

Prevention of Opioid Use Disorder

Definitions of the terms Addiction, Craving, Misuse, Opioid Use Disorder, and Physical Dependence are provided in the glossary in Appendix 1 (Supplementary Material).

The pharmacological class of opioids includes all substances with affinity for endogenous opioid receptors. These substances differ in their therapeutic indications, legal framework (controlled substances, whether legal or illicit), and pharmacological characteristics.

Adverse Effects of Opioids

The analgesic and adverse effects of opioids are primarily mediated by activation of opioid receptors. While these effects are common to this class, their frequency and intensity vary depending on the specific molecule, administered dose, and individual patient characteristics.

Some opioids also act on nonopioid receptors. More broadly, the endogenous opioid system interacts with multiple neural circuits, contributing to a wide spectrum of adverse effects, the full description of which lies beyond the scope of this work [2–7].

Certain adverse effects warrant particular attention in the context of preventing OUD and overdoses [7, 8]:Respiratory depression: Opioids reduce the sensitivity of respiratory centers to hypercapnia and suppress the cough reflex. These mechanisms can reduce respiratory rate and increase the risk of respiratory depression. This risk is amplified by concomitant use of other substances such as alcohol, benzodiazepines, or gabapentinoids [9]. Combined with the sedative properties of opioids, these effects can be life-threatening.Reinforcing effects: through activation of dopaminergic pathways and inhibition of GABAergic activity, opioids promote continued use beyond pain relief. Physical dependence develops through a physiological process of progressive tolerance, whereby the body adapts to the persistent presence of the drug. Tolerance reduces treatment efficacy and leads to the need for progressively higher doses to achieve the same effect [10, 11].

Abrupt discontinuation disrupts this balance, triggering a withdrawal syndrome with distressing physical and psychological symptoms. These symptoms can drive patients to maintain or escalate opioid use, thereby increasing the risk of accidental and potentially fatal overdose. Clinically, opioid overdose is typically identified by the triad of miosis, impaired consciousness, and respiratory depression [7, 8]. However, miosis alone is insufficient to indicate overdose, as it merely indicates opioid exposure.

Evolution of Nosographic Framework

Chronic use of opioids, whether licit or illicit, can lead to a range of problems that vary according to individual factors, duration of exposure, dosage, and the pharmacological properties of the specific molecule.

Among these problems, physical dependence is common. Clinically, it manifests as a need to maintain opioid intake to prevent the onset of withdrawal syndrome, which is characterized by multiple unpleasant symptoms including yawning with rhinorrhea and lacrimation, diffuse joint and muscle pain with cramps, gastrointestinal disturbances, chills, tremors, hyperhidrosis, irritability, agitation, anxiety, and insomnia.

Some patients develop opioid “addiction,” defined as a chronic disorder characterized by compulsive drug-seeking behaviors, an irresistible desire to consume the substance (craving), and loss of control over use despite negative socio-professional and personal consequences. Mood disorders often accompany this condition.

From a diagnostic perspective, the concepts of misuse, abuse, and addiction have been merged into a single entity in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), graded as mild, moderate, or severe depending on the number of diagnostic criteria met [12–14]. Physical dependence on opioid analgesics remains described separately from OUD, as patients in this category exhibit only withdrawal symptoms, tolerance, and continued use, without craving or negative consequences of substance use.

Applied to opioids, the diagnosis of OUD better reflects clinical reality, particularly by integrating the concept of craving—an intense and irresistible desire to consume the substance—which is considered a major factor in the persistence of use and the risk of relapse [14, 15].

Impact studies have shown that pre-DSM-5 diagnostic categories remain clinically valid but tended to underestimate certain substances, particularly opioid analgesics, because they did not include less easily measurable criteria such as craving [16].

Target Populations

These guidelines apply to all patients exposed to opioids, whether through analgesic treatment for acute or chronic pain, or among those with OUD. OUD may develop after treatment with opioid analgesics, occasional or regular exposure to illicit opioids, or use of opioid agonist medications, whether prescribed or self-administered.

Although the modalities and purposes of opioid use can influence the risk of OUD or overdose, these risks primarily depend on the pharmacological properties of the opioids themselves. Accordingly, the guidelines adopt a comprehensive approach, independent of therapeutic indications, to address the diversity of clinical situations encountered by practitioners, patients, and their families.

When specific data are available for certain populations, these have been incorporated into the guidelines to ensure a nuanced consideration of particular cases.

Finally, appropriate opioid prescribing forms the foundation for the prevention and management of OUD and overdoses. While these issues are presented separately in the guidelines for clarity, they remain closely interrelated in clinical practice.

Most preventive measures are directed at prescribers and apply to all patients for whom opioids may be considered, particularly in the context of long-term prescribing.

Before Any Opioid Prescription

In the context of confirmed or suspected painful conditions, the underlying cause must be investigated.

In the context of confirmed or suspected OUD, the diagnosis must be established by precisely identifying the associated clinical features—isolated physical dependence, OUD, or overuse in an attempt to relieve pain or withdrawal symptoms (historically referred to as “pseudo-addiction”) [18–22].

A comprehensive clinical interview is recommended, with the aim of building a strong therapeutic alliance. This promotes patient adherence to care and reduces the risk of treatment discontinuation. Discontinuation may have serious consequences such as relapse, worsening of the underlying condition, development of substance use disorders, or the emergence of risk behaviors [23].

The interview should explore the patient’s expectations regarding treatment, personal and family medical history, substance use history, and prescribed treatments, particularly benzodiazepines [21–24]. A global assessment covering somatic, psychiatric, legal/forensic, and environmental aspects is essential to identify comorbidities and social vulnerabilities that may contribute to pain and to OUD. These issues require multidisciplinary management involving specialists from the relevant fields [20–23, 25–29].

This assessment also serves a pretherapeutic purpose, allowing the exclusion of potential contraindications to the planned treatments [21, 29]. The therapeutic strategy must be discussed with the patient, presenting all available options. Treatment objectives should result from shared medical decision-making, taking into account the patient’s preferences and circumstances, the risk–benefit balance—including the risks of OUD and overdose—and the available scientific evidence [20–22, 24, 25, 28].

The identification of patients at risk for OUD may be facilitated by specific risk stratification tools, such as the Opioid Risk Tool (ORT) [18, 24, 30].

An integrated, multidisciplinary approach, involving coordination among professionals from different specialties, is essential to simultaneously address potential comorbidities [22, 24, 29].

In the Presence of an Existing Opioid Prescription

When a patient presents with an existing opioid prescription, it is first necessary to verify that the general recommendations outlined above have been followed. The appropriateness of the indication should be assessed in relation to the clinical presentation and the available scientific evidence [31].

It is recommended to assess for signs of physical dependence on opioids by screening for withdrawal symptoms. If needed, validated tools such as the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS) or the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS) may be used [20, 25, 29, 32–34].

Screening for OUD should also be performed, either through clinical evaluation or with validated tools such as the Prescription Opioid Misuse Index (POMI) [35, 36], or by applying the diagnostic criteria for substance use disorder from the DSM-5 [18, 20, 23, 26]. The ICD-11, which adopts a categorical rather than a dimensional approach, is considered less applicable to clinical practice in this context.

In the Case of First-Time Opioid Prescription

When an indication for prescribing an opioid is confirmed, it is recommended to start at a low dose and titrate gradually to reach the optimal dose [22, 23, 29].

The patient and their relatives must be informed about the benefits and risks of the prescribed substance, taking into account the patient’s perception of treatment, their risk profile, and the clinical context [21, 25, 29, 31]. This information should cover both common and mild adverse effects, as well as more serious risks, including use disorders, overdoses, and the risk of fatal outcomes [21, 25, 29, 31]. In the event of treatment interruption, patients must be reminded of the rapid loss of opioid tolerance and the increased risk of overdose if resuming at the previous dose. Reintroduction at a lower dose may be considered under medical supervision to reduce this risk [20, 21, 25, 29].

Safety measures for opioids must also be ensured, particularly to prevent accidental overdose among household members, especially children. Medications should be stored out of reach, in their original safety packaging (e.g., blister pack, child-resistant cap) [26, 29].

In some cases, urine testing for opioid metabolites may be useful to confirm prior opioid use, detect associated use sometimes unknown to the patient (e.g., adulterants in illicit substances), or reduce the risk of prescription diversion by individuals seeking to supply illicit markets [21, 23, 25, 29]. However, this method should not be used as a diagnostic criterion.

It is essential to inform the patient and their relatives—who are most likely to witness an overdose—about the clinical signs of overdose, namely reduced consciousness and respiratory depression. It is recommended to provide them with a ready-to-use take-home naloxone (THN) kit, the opioid antidote, which allows emergency treatment of an overdose and reduces associated mortality while awaiting emergency medical services [20, 21, 25, 27, 29, 37]. Since naloxone has a short half-life, its use must always be accompanied by an immediate call to emergency services [20, 23–27, 29, 38–40]. All individuals likely to intervene in the event of an overdose (relatives, healthcare professionals, firefighters, law enforcement, etc.) must be trained in its administration. The kit should be stored in an accessible location [38, 40].

Finally, prescribers must systematically reassess the effectiveness and tolerability of the treatment to adjust management as needed [21, 23–25, 27, 29, 31].

First-Time Prescription of Opioid Analgesics

In pain management contexts, opioid titration should begin with the lowest effective dose appropriate to the clinical situation [23, 24, 41, 42]. The choice between short-acting and long-acting opioids must be individualized according to each patient’s clinical circumstances.

International guidelines caution against the use of long-acting formulations during initial titration, since analgesic response and opioid tolerance are often unpredictable early in treatment [23, 24].

Opioids should be prescribed for the minimum duration necessary, tailored to the patient’s clinical situation and the treatment objectives defined in advance, particularly in acute pain [23, 24].

First-Time Prescription of OAT

In the context of OUD, scientific evidence consistently supports the prolonged use of OAT, which is preferred over nonpharmacological approaches or rapid detoxification strategies [20–22, 25, 27–29]. However, refusal of pharmacological treatment should not deprive patients of access to equivalent multidisciplinary care [20, 26, 29].

In patients without pain, oral morphine equivalent conversion tables are not recommended. These equivalences were developed primarily for opioid rotation in analgesia, not for substitution treatment [25]. Gradual titration remains the recommended standard in these contexts [20–22, 25, 27, 29].

Guidelines report no difference in efficacy between buprenorphine (with or without naloxone) and methadone. The choice should be individualized, taking into account clinical characteristics, patient preferences, and risk profile [21, 22, 28]. Buprenorphine (with or without naloxone) is considered safer because of its partial agonist profile, which reduces the risk of overdose. It is therefore more suitable in nonspecialized addiction settings. However, it should not be co-administered with full opioid agonists. Initiation should occur only after the onset of early withdrawal symptoms to avoid precipitating withdrawal [22].

Methadone lacks this safety profile but may be cautiously combined with full opioid agonists, when treating acute pain. Methadone requires slow and careful titration, respecting its pharmacological properties, particularly by spacing dose escalations to allow plasma concentrations to stabilize and thereby limit the risk of overdose [21].

The use of immediate- or extended-release opioids indicated exclusively for pain is strongly discouraged in the management of OUD [27, 28].

Supervised dispensing of OAT should be discussed on a case-by-case basis with the patient, rather than applied systematically. While supervision can enhance adherence, reduce misuse and overdoses, and promote treatment routines, it may also limit patient autonomy, increase costs, and reinforce links to environments associated with OUD [21, 29].

Context of Co-Occurring Pain and OUD

In situations of comorbidity between pain and OUD, multimodal analgesia should be prioritized [22, 25]. If this approach is insufficient, the OAT dosage may be adjusted given its intrinsic analgesic potential linked to opioid receptor activity. This may involve splitting doses or, in some cases, increasing them [20, 22].

Patients with both pain and OUD generally show reduced pain tolerance alongside increased opioid tolerance. It is advisable to seek the expertise of a pain specialist and/or addiction specialist to confirm the diagnosis and optimize the therapeutic strategy for these complex clinical profiles [20–22, 27, 29].

Before Any Potential Renewal of an Opioid Prescription

Renewal of opioid prescriptions must be conditional on the persistence of a validated and justified clinical indication [31]. Before any renewal, it is essential to assess the effectiveness and tolerability of the previous prescription [23, 24].

Particular attention should be paid to withdrawal symptoms, particularly if they occur before each scheduled dose. Their identification can be facilitated by validated tools such as the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS) and the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS) [20, 25, 29].

Prescribers must remain vigilant regarding risk factors for OUD, including psychiatric comorbidities, a history of substance use disorders, an average daily dose exceeding 120 mg oral morphine equivalents, male sex, and patient age (with OUD risk decreasing as patients get older) [18, 23, 31, 42]. Screening for OUD, performed clinically or using tools such as the Prescription Opioid Misuse Index (POMI), should be repeated throughout care [18, 20, 23, 26].

A comprehensive, multidisciplinary assessment of the patient, integrating somatic, psychiatric, legal/forensic, and environmental dimensions, should be carried out regularly during follow-up. This process allows for evaluation of progress, confirmation of the continued indication for opioid treatment, and assessment of patient progress relative to the initial therapeutic objectives [24, 28, 29].

Depending on the risk of OUD and overdose, repeat urine testing for opioid metabolites may be performed to confirm opioid use and detect potential co-use of other substances [21, 23, 25, 29]. It is also important to periodically remind the patient, and potentially their relatives, of the benefits and risks of the prescribed opioids [21].

In cases of treatment interruption, medical reassessment of the OAT reintroduction dosage is necessary. Restarting at a lower dose than previously administered may be considered to prevent overdose owing to rapid loss of tolerance, followed by titration adapted to the patient’s clinical situation [21, 25, 29]. This adjustment should never be perceived as a sanction, but rather as a preventive measure against overdose. The patient must be informed of this approach to prevent compensatory behaviors such as illicit drug purchases [26, 29].

Increased vigilance is required during critical periods such as discharge from hospitalization or release from incarceration, which are often associated with reduced opioid use and may result in overdose upon resumption of prior dosing, owing to loss of tolerance [21, 29].

Depending on the evolving risk of overdose, prescribing or renewing THN kits may be considered to enhance safety in these high-risk situations [20, 23, 25–27, 29, 38, 40].

The key elements for preventing OUD and overdoses are summarized in Table 1.

Management of Opioid Use Disorder

To prevent or limit OUD, appropriate prescribing of opioid analgesics from the start of treatment is essential, accompanied by patient education on safe use as outlined in Part 1 of these guidelines and in the previous chapter [20, 21, 26, 27, 29].

The management of OUD requires a multidimensional approach, combining clinical and psychosocial assessments, appropriate pharmacological treatments, and nonpharmacological interventions [19, 22].

International guidelines, developed across different geographical and healthcare contexts, highlight convergent principles as well as national specificities that must be considered in clinical practice. In all cases, OAT is not recommended for the treatment of use disorders involving other psychoactive substances (e.g., alcohol, cannabis, benzodiazepines, psychostimulants) [19, 20, 26, 27].

Clinical Evaluation and Diagnosis of OUD

Clinicians must diagnose OUD using the DSM-5 criteria. The ICD-11 classification, which retains a categorical rather than a dimensional approach to substance use disorders, is considered less suitable for clinical practice [19, 21, 22].

Prolonged use of opioid analgesics beyond what would normally be expected for acute peri- or postoperative pain requires careful reassessment of pain [19–22].

The diagnosis of OUD is primarily based on patient history, together with a comprehensive evaluation that includes a physical examination, with particular attention to potential injection sites [19, 21].

Patients with OUD should systematically be assessed for associated pain, as inadequate pain management may exacerbate the existing disorder [19, 21, 29].

In the context of chronic pain management in primary care, simple tools such as the Prescription Opioid Misuse Index (POMI) can be used to monitor for OUD [26].

Patients being assessed for OUD should also be evaluated for mental health status and potential psychiatric comorbidities [19, 21, 29]. In addition, women of childbearing age should be asked about contraceptive methods and offered a pregnancy test [19].

Urine testing can document recent opioid use but is not pathognomonic for the diagnosis of OUD [19, 21, 26].

Given the high prevalence of comorbidities and risk behaviors, initial blood tests are recommended: complete blood count, electrolytes, creatinine, liver function tests, and infectious disease screening (hepatitis B and C, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), sexually transmitted infections, and tuberculosis) [19, 21]. Additional specialized assessments may be indicated depending on clinical findings [19, 21], together with verification of vaccination status [19, 29] and consideration of smoking cessation [19].

If initiation of OAT is planned, an electrocardiogram is recommended to evaluate the QT interval before treatment initiation in cases of hypokalemia or concomitant use of medications known to prolong this interval [21].

Choice of Pharmacological Treatment

Patients with OUD should preferably be managed in primary care, with coordination to specialized services when necessary [20, 26, 29].

Before initiating any OAT, therapeutic objectives must be defined with the patient within the framework of shared decision-making [19, 22, 29]. The possibility and conditions of reducing or discontinuing OAT should be discussed at the outset, so that the patient considers this option as legitimate, without it being the ultimate goal of treatment [19, 21, 27].

Psychosocial interventions and support by user associations (experiential approaches: self-help groups, peer support, patients with lived experience) should be offered in addition to OAT, without being perceived as conditional or mandatory for access to OAT [19, 20, 27, 29].

Transition to opioid substitution is recommended when the clinical situation requires it and the patient agrees. Direct withdrawal (without substitution) is not recommended, as it is associated with high relapse rates and an increased risk of morbidity and mortality [20, 21, 27].

Initiation of OAT should not occur in an emergency setting without first ensuring overall management [19, 21, 22], but it should not be unduly delayed due to lack of patient adherence or delays in completing the clinical workup [19, 20].

When co-occurring pain may exacerbate an existing OUD, multimodal analgesia should be prioritized. If ineffective, OAT dosing may be split and the dose increased if necessary; consultation with a pain specialist is recommended [19, 21, 22, 25, 29].

The appropriateness of prescribing and dispensing THN should be systematically assessed in situations of life-threatening overdose risk, and both patients and their relatives must be informed about its use [19, 20, 29, 37, 38].

The two OAT medications available in France are buprenorphine (with or without naloxone) and methadone. In all cases, patients must be informed about prescribing modalities, dispensing procedures, monitoring, and safe use [19, 21, 29].

While both methadone and buprenorphine are highly effective in reducing heroin use and overdose mortality, they differ in their clinical utility. Methadone generally shows superior retention rates, making it a robust choice for patients requiring higher levels of structure and stability. Conversely, buprenorphine, owing to its partial agonist profile, demonstrates a more favorable safety profile, particularly regarding the risk of fatal overdose, and may be preferred for patients with different risk profiles or specific clinical needs [43].

If the goal is a simple reduction in opioid use, methadone is recommended, as buprenorphine is not suitable given its pharmacological properties [20–22].

If the goal is complete cessation of opioid use and the patient has no preference regarding OAT, buprenorphine is recommended, as it is safer than methadone with respect to overdose risk [19–22, 27, 29].

If cessation follows an initial phase of opioid use reduction, it is recommended to continue the OAT initially prescribed if it is well tolerated by the patient [19, 27, 29].

The buprenorphine–naloxone combination is indicated when concerns about diversion are paramount [19, 20, 22, 27, 28].

The sublingual buprenorphine lyophilizate formulation, which dissolves more quickly than the sublingual tablet, may be offered in specific situations (e.g., supervised administration in prison), provided the noninterchangeability of the two formulations is respected and retitration is performed if switching [21].

Methadone substitution is recommended when buprenorphine (with or without naloxone) is not the patient’s preferred option or has proven insufficiently effective [19–21, 27–29].

Beyond methadone and buprenorphine, sustained-release morphine and diacetylmorphine are also utilized in some European countries as a second-line maintenance treatment for OUD, reflecting the need for diversified therapeutic options [44–47].

Dose reductions or discontinuation must never be punitive, but based strictly on safety considerations [20, 27].

In patients requiring high buprenorphine doses without satisfactory clinical outcomes, supervised dispensing is recommended to verify proper sublingual intake consistent with the marketing authorization. If these administration requirements are difficult for the patient to comply with, the sublingual lyophilizate formulation should be considered as an alternative [19, 21, 29].

Methadone

Methadone is often associated with higher treatment retention rates compared with buprenorphine, providing a robust therapeutic structure for patients who require sustained clinical stability; this difference should be considered when tailoring treatment strategies to individual patient needs [43].

When initiating methadone, regular, and sometimes daily, supervision is recommended. The first weeks are critical to stabilize the patient and ensure that opioid use (outside OAT) is discontinued if this is among the defined objectives. Once treatment is proceeding well, greater flexibility should be allowed, with more spaced dispensing intervals, within regulatory limits, to minimize disruption to the patient’s social, professional, and family life [20, 21, 29].

The level of supervision required is determined by evaluating the information exchanged with the patient, including efficacy, tolerability, craving, adherence to protocol, and achievement of objectives [19–21, 29].

Long-Term Follow-Up

Common Principles for OAT

Treatment should be considered long term, although its optimal duration remains unknown, and the possibility and conditions of treatment discontinuation should be regularly discussed with the patient [20, 21, 26, 27, 29].

Throughout treatment, within the framework of a therapeutic alliance between clinician, pharmacist, and patient, individualized follow-up should help identify difficulties (e.g., co-use, diversion) and provide appropriate support [19–22, 29].

Consultation with an addiction specialist is advisable when diversion of opioid analgesics is suspected or confirmed, and for advice on harm reduction strategies [19, 20, 29].

Doses must be tailored to each patient and adjusted according to efficacy, tolerability, adverse effects, persistence of craving, patient satisfaction, and the risk of overuse or diversion [19, 21, 22, 28, 29].

In the long term, treatment should be reassessed regularly, taking into account the risk–benefit balance, evolving patient objectives, drug-drug interactions with concomitant treatments, age-related physiological changes (QT prolongation, decreased renal or hepatic function), and associated substance use (alcohol, benzodiazepines, illicit drugs, etc.) [19, 21, 22, 29].

Urine testing may be used to tailor follow-up and monitor opioid and other psychoactive substance use, at least every 6 months [19, 21, 26, 29, 51].

Occasional concomitant use of another opioid does not contraindicate continuation of OAT, but the associated overdose risk must be discussed with the patient [20, 21, 29].

Buprenorphine

Dose adjustment with buprenorphine is more flexible than with methadone and may be performed on a daily basis [19, 21, 22, 28, 29].

For the sublingual formulation, treatment efficacy is generally optimal between 16 and 24 mg/day, which allows control of illicit opioid use. The maximum recommended dose for the lyophilizate formulation is 18 mg/day [19, 21, 22, 28].

Given the risk of diversion of buprenorphine through alternative routes of administration (injection, snorting), patients should be asked about their mode of administration [20, 21, 29].

Methadone

Although a few patients stabilize on a methadone maintenance dose of 30–60 mg/day, most achieve better stabilization with 60–120 mg/day, which minimizes euphoric effects if patients self-administer additional opioids [19, 21, 22, 28, 29, 49].

During follow-up, dose adjustments, whether upward or downward, should be made in 5–10 mg increments with a stabilization phase of 3–5 days [19, 21, 28].

Any dose above 120 mg/day requires investigation of potential drug–drug interactions, closer monitoring for adverse effects (including QT prolongation), and increased supervision to avoid diversion [19, 21, 28, 29].

Switching from one OAT to Another

From buprenorphine (sublingual) to methadone: the switch is simpler than the reverse. Methadone may be administered 24 h after the last dose of buprenorphine, without exceeding 40 mg/day at initiation. For patients stabilized on ≤ 4 mg/day of buprenorphine, it is recommended to start at a low dose and titrate appropriately. The patient should be reassessed the following day to adjust the methadone dose accordingly [19, 21, 22, 28, 29].

From methadone to buprenorphine (sublingual): the methadone dose should be tapered and stabilized at 30 mg/day or less to minimize the risk of abrupt withdrawal. Clinicians must inform patients that switching from methadone to buprenorphine carries a risk of precipitated withdrawal if the methadone dose is not sufficiently reduced or if buprenorphine is initiated too early after the last methadone intake. Patients should be thoroughly educated about the symptoms of precipitated withdrawal and the importance of respecting the recommended stabilization dose and the necessary waiting period prior to the switch. Switching at doses above 30 mg/day should only be considered in a specialized hospital setting under addiction specialist supervision. The first dose of buprenorphine should be administered at least 24–36 h after the last methadone dose, preferably when mild to moderate withdrawal symptoms are present. Extending this interval further reduces the risk and severity of precipitated withdrawal [19, 21, 22, 28, 29, 49].

Discontinuation of Substitution Treatment

Decision to Discontinue OAT

The decision to discontinue OAT should be discussed with the patient, and tapering should be supported by the relevant healthcare professionals. If the decision is made jointly, informing relatives may be helpful [19, 21, 22, 29, 49, 50, 52]. Discontinuation of OAT should not be considered an absolute objective, as treatment may be maintained in the long term [20, 27, 29].

Stopping OAT is best considered when patients are clinically stable (cessation of opioid use other than OAT, stable living conditions, professional activity, etc.). For stable patients wishing to discontinue OAT, a gradual tapering of the dose should be evaluated [19, 21, 27].

A successful discontinuation of OAT consists of safely and comfortably stopping opioid medications without relapse into OUD or other substance use [19, 21, 27].

The consequences of an unsuccessful attempt at withdrawal due to premature discontinuation may include relapse into opioid or other substance use (notably alcohol), deterioration of physical and mental health, worsening of social circumstances, and an increased risk of overdose and death [19, 21, 27].

Patients considering OAT discontinuation should be informed about how to approach tapering and the conditions for success. This is essential for shared decision-making and planning of the process (see Table 2) [19, 21, 22, 29, 49, 50]. For stable patients treated with low doses of OAT (< 30 mg/day of methadone or ≤ 2 mg/day of buprenorphine) who may be reluctant to further dose reductions or discontinuation, it is recommended to discuss withdrawal strategies and address their concerns or fears without actively encouraging discontinuation. A pause in dose tapering may also be considered if the patient wishes, and dose increases can be made if necessary [19, 21, 27, 29, 52].

Because of the risk of relapse and the increased risk of overdose following reduced opioid tolerance, it is recommended to implement a program of regular follow-up with more frequent assessments immediately after complete cessation of opioid use. This follow-up allows ongoing supervision of the post-withdrawal period, monitoring for relapse, and identification of emerging comorbidities [19, 20, 29]. Peer support associations can contribute to this follow-up, particularly in cases of social isolation and interpersonal deficits resulting from the disorder [20, 27].

In addition, prescribing or dispensing THN is essential at the time of discontinuation, with patient and family education on its proper use [19–21, 27, 29].

Withdrawal Procedures

It is recommended to gradually taper OAT over several months, or even years depending on the patient, to allow adaptation to the necessary physiological, behavioral, and social changes [19, 21, 22, 27–29, 49, 50, 52].

The intensity of withdrawal symptoms increases as the OAT dose approaches zero, with peak discomfort occurring 1–4 weeks after treatment discontinuation. Symptoms such as poor sleep, mood disturbances, and craving may persist at low intensity or frequency for several weeks or months [19, 21, 22, 28, 29].

As with any attempt at gradual medication tapering, careful follow-up is required to identify relapse or deterioration in the patient’s condition, which may indicate the need to reconsider the treatment plan [19, 21, 22, 27–29].

Methadone

It is recommended to reduce methadone doses by 5–10% every 1–4 weeks (i.e., a 5–10 mg reduction for doses > 50 mg, and 1–5 mg for doses < 50 mg). The tapering rate may vary depending on the indication, the timeframe for withdrawal, and the patient’s clinical stability [19, 21, 28, 29].

For patients unable to reduce their methadone dose further (often between 20 and 60 mg) owing to withdrawal discomfort, increased use of other medications, or somatic and psychiatric deterioration, it is recommended either to increase the dose to the previously effective level that re-stabilizes the patient [19, 21, 29].

Buprenorphine

Because incremental dose reductions are better tolerated with buprenorphine than with methadone, it is recommended to reduce buprenorphine doses by up to 25% every 1–4 weeks (i.e., a 4–8 mg reduction for doses > 16 mg, and 0.4–4 mg reduction for doses below 16 mg) [21, 22, 28, 29].

Urine drug testing serves as a tool to support the achievement of the therapeutic objectives defined with the patient [19, 21, 26, 29, 51].

These tests provide information on psychoactive substance use in the days preceding the consultation [21, 26, 51].

When initiating OAT, urine drug testing is mandatory prior to methadone initiation and recommended prior to buprenorphine initiation [19, 21, 26, 29, 51].

It is recommended to use urine dipsticks to detect opioid use, which provide immediate results and support clinical decision-making during the consultation. However, clinicians must be aware of their inherent limitations. Immunoassays are presumptive; they are prone to false-negative results for many semi-synthetic and synthetic opioids (e.g., oxycodone, fentanyl, tramadol) owing to limited antibody cross-reactivity with these chemical structures [51, 59]. Conversely, false-positive results can occur owing to cross-reactivity with other substances, such as certain antidepressants, antipsychotics, or over-the-counter decongestants [51, 59]. Therefore, results should be interpreted within the context of the patient’s clinical presentation. The purpose of urine drug testing must be clearly explained to the patient to avoid misunderstanding or mistrust [21, 51]. The use of urine dipsticks specific for these opioids (e.g., tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl) remains possible.

If more detailed investigations are required (e.g., substances not covered by dipsticks, quantitative measurements, disputed dipstick results), referral to a medical biology or toxicology laboratory via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) is recommended [19, 21, 29].

Supervised urine sample collection by a healthcare professional is not recommended, as it is intrusive and negatively impacts the therapeutic alliance [21, 29, 51]. Random urine drug testing is recommended, as its unpredictability is more likely to provide clinically useful information than scheduled testing [19, 21, 26, 29, 51].

The frequency of urine drug testing—higher during the initiation period of OAT—should be individualized according to the clinical situation, co-use, and other patient risk behaviors [19, 21, 26, 29, 51].

Preventive Measures and Patient Environment

Patients and their relatives—who are most likely to witness and respond to an overdose—must be informed about the characteristic clinical signs of overdose (miosis, impaired consciousness, and respiratory depression) [19, 27, 29].

The risk of overdose among relatives, due to accidental or intentional ingestion of an opioid medication by an opioid-naïve person, must also be addressed through specific prevention measures [19, 20, 29]:Secure storage of medications, kept strictly out of the reach of children, in their original protective packaging (e.g., blister packs, childproof caps).Awareness of the danger of giving one’s medication to another person, even if they present with similar symptoms.

Systematic reviews confirm that community-based THN distribution programs are effective in reducing opioid-related mortality. Evidence indicates that early bystander intervention, combined with timely naloxone administration, significantly improves the chances of clinical reversal of opioid toxicity, provided that appropriate resuscitation support is simultaneously implemented [38, 62].

THN enables emergency treatment of an opioid overdose and reduces associated mortality while awaiting emergency medical services, although the amount of naloxone in a kit may be insufficient to fully reverse an overdose [19, 20, 27, 29, 37].

To anticipate overdose management, it is recommended to prescribe or dispense THN to patients—particularly those at risk of overdose—as well as to their relatives. Patients and relatives must also be instructed in how to administer naloxone and the importance of first contacting emergency medical services [19–21, 27, 29, 37, 63]. The THN should be stored in a location that is easily accessible to all potential responders [64, 65].

Law enforcement and first responders (firefighters, paramedics) are often the first to arrive on site; therefore, they should have facilitated access to naloxone and be trained in its administration while awaiting transfer of care to a medical facility [64–66].

Management of Opioid Overdoses

An opioid overdose can be identified by the association of three symptoms (the "opioid overdose triad”): miosis, unconsciousness, and respiratory depression [19–21, 27, 29, 37, 67]. However, miosis alone, when not accompanied by somnolence or respiratory depression, indicates opioid exposure rather than overdose.

In the event of suspected opioid overdose, the first recommended action is to ensure the person’s safety and promptly call emergency medical services [19, 20, 27, 29, 65, 67].

It is recommended to administer an initial dose of naloxone using a THN kit, including in pregnant women, to save the mother’s life. The kit is intended to prevent death while awaiting emergency services, as the naloxone dose it contains may be insufficient to fully reverse an overdose [19–21, 27, 29, 68].

Subsequently, it is then recommended [19–21, 27, 29, 37, 67, 68]:To perform basic emergency procedures: assess consciousness and respiration, place the person in the recovery position if breathing resumes; or perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation if breathing is absent.To monitor the patient while awaiting emergency services. A second dose of naloxone should be administered after 2–3 min if there is no improvement, or to prolong the antidote effect if emergency responders have not yet arrived, given naloxone’s short half-life. Other causes of respiratory depression or concomitant use of substances for which naloxone is ineffective cannot be excluded.

Pregnant and Breastfeeding Women

Pregnant Women

Prevention of Opioid Use Disorder

All preventive measures for opioid use disorder apply to pregnant women.

In this population, additional measures may be implemented, such as early detection of pregnancy and referral, in coordination with healthcare providers already involved in her care, to the appropriate specialist (obstetrician, midwife, pediatrician, pain physician, addiction specialist, etc.), depending on the clinical situation [19, 29, 70, 71].

Management of Opioid Use Disorder and Overdose Prevention

In the context of OUD, owing to the high relapse rates associated with heroin use, it is recommended to maintain or initiate opioid agonist treatment (OAT) to prevent overdose and reduce the risk of prenatal complications linked to alternating cycles of intoxication and withdrawal, as well as neonatal abstinence syndrome [19, 29, 49, 71, 72].

The choice of OAT should be made in consultation with the patient, as methadone and buprenorphine have comparable efficacy during pregnancy [49, 71, 72].

Dosage and administration schedules must be regularly reassessed and adjusted according to pharmacokinetic and physiological changes during pregnancy, particularly in the third trimester [19, 29, 49].

When initiating methadone treatment, it is recommended to increase the dose in increments of 5–10 mg per week, with the aim of maintaining the lowest effective dose that controls withdrawal symptoms and reduces craving for additional opioids. Divided dosing (twice daily) is often more effective and better tolerated than once-daily dosing. As pregnancy progresses, the gradual decrease in plasma methadone concentrations and increased clearance may necessitate dose escalation or splitting into two daily doses. Dose readjustments may also be required in the postpartum period [19, 29, 49].

Women on methadone or buprenorphine OAT who choose to breastfeed should be supported in the absence of contraindications such as untreated HIV infection or concomitant medications excreted in breast milk that may be harmful to the infant [19, 49, 70, 71].

Limitations

These recommendations have several limitations. First, the underlying literature review covered publications up to December 2021 and therefore did not formally integrate all subsequent evidence or guideline updates. Nevertheless, several major international recommendations and updated versions of previously cited guidelines were incorporated into the manuscript when considered clinically relevant. Second, although the methodology prioritized high-level evidence, some recommendations rely on expert consensus or lower levels of evidence, particularly in areas where randomized controlled data remain limited, such as harm reduction strategies, supervised dispensing practices, or specific opioid agonist treatment modalities.

Conclusions

These national guidelines, developed under the auspices of the French National Authority for Health, provide comprehensive recommendations on the prevention and management of opioid use disorder (OUD) and overdoses. Building on the first part, which addressed the use of opioids for acute and chronic pain, they emphasize early identification of at-risk patients, appropriate prescribing practices, and the central role of opioid agonist treatment and THN. Multidisciplinary coordination and integration of psychosocial support are highlighted as essential components of care.

Together, the two parts constitute one of the most comprehensive sets of recommendations currently available, with the overarching goal of balancing legitimate access to opioid analgesics with strong safeguards against dependence, opioid use disorder, and overdose. These guidelines may contribute to improving the safe and appropriate use of opioid medications internationally while reducing the burden of opioid use disorder and overdose.
