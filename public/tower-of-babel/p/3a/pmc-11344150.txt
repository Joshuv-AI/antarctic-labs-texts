The effects of acoustic-light-thermal environment quality parameters on pedestrians’ overall comforts in residential districts
Authors: Fanxi Zeng, Shuang Liang, Jian Zhang, Yanyi Chen, Shuangyan Feng, Quanzhang Mo, Tianru Zhou, Yumao Lai, Ting Liu, Siqi Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study explored subjective responses towards various environmental quality parameters. It would be helpful to approach an overall comfort improvement relating to physics. In order to investigate the combined effects of sound, light and heat on the overall comfort of pedestrians, which could be evaluated by overall comfort vote, two residential areas in Yubei District, Chongqing (a Cfa city in China) were selected for field measurements and questionnaires. Three were three key findings were concluded based on subjective responses to environmental parameters via multiple linear regression. First, the average outdoor neutral temperature, mean neutral sound level and neutral illumination intensity were determined to be 26.6 °C (determined by physiologically equivalent temperature), 56.5 dBA (determined by A-weighted equivalent continuous sound level) and 21.4 klx (determined by LUX), respectively. Second, considering the effects of both physiology and psychology, thermal perceptions varied for acoustic and light environments. Residents partially had lower neutral temperatures at the noisy condition. Third, environmental quality factors had a significant effect on overall comfort. Positive correlation between physiologically equivalent temperature and overall comfort vote was found (sig < 0.000); while the effects of sound pressure and light intensity were not always significant. Hence, thermal stress played a significant role in people’s overall comfort. This study has explored the effects of three environment quality parameters on human perceptions. It provided better understanding against dwellers’ feelings under complex circumstances. Future urban design and planning works should consider the cooling factors regarding contextual acoustical and visual environments.

Introduction

The rapid urban development (sprawl, industrialisation, and transportation) has resulted in environmental pollution and poor living qualities1. They are present in various aspects, including thermal2, acoustic3 and visual4 discomforts. They pose a risk to human physical and mental health, with heat-related deaths accounting for about yearly 489,000 globally and about 45% in Asia per year5. It is estimated that currently more than 80% of the world’s population is affected by the prevalence of light pollution6. In September 2014, the World Health Organization7 has released a comprehensive report "The Burden of Disease Due to Noise Pollution", regarding noise pollution triggering diseases. People live longer in urban residential areas. Hence it is necessary to improve the outdoor environments for their daily lives8.

Currently, researchers are exploring ways to address issues relating to poor environments and poor perceptions. The first crucial step is to comprehend how different settings elicit subjective responses. Outdoor open spaces in complex environments are composed of diverse elements that are integrated, facilitated, and constrained9. Each of them could affect the environmental quality, further impacting human perceptions10. Earlier studies have investigated the relationship between individual perceptions and corresponding environmental factors, including thermal11, acoustic12, and visual environments13. Temperature and solar radiation in the thermal environment can determine the state of outdoor thermal comfort11. Noises caused thermal discomforts. Studies have shown that the acoustic environments in urban open spaces had significant effects on thermal perceptions and noises caused thermal discomforts14. There are many physical factors affecting the light environment, including illuminance, relevant color temperature, glare, and color rendering, etc., which affect people's outdoor comfort13. In addition, temperature and noise have a common effect on human perceptions. Noise could affect thermal perception differently by various background environments15. However, the interactive effects between light and acoustic sensations is irregular16.

Researchers have explored many ways to improve human comfort levels9. They intended to find subjective responses towards various environmental conditions (acoustic, light, and heat17) as well as physical factors (greening factors18 etc.). Yan et al.19 reported that vegetation can reduce noise levels by 2 to 3.9 dBA. Lau et al.20 found that sound absorption effects increased with the increases in incidence angles and vegetation thickness. They were also effective on thermal comforts21. In addition, the shadow casting effect of buildings helped to reduce the radiation loads on pedestrians while improving thermal comfort, especially in high-density cities, even if the ventilation is reduced22,23. Many countries like China have issued laws and regulations to limit the effects of artificial lights at night, e.g., China24. Gălăţanu25 revealed that certain facade design methods have effectively reduced light pollution. Yang et al.26 reported sustainable ways to mitigate urban heat. Similar results were also reported27–29. Also, people could responded differently towards various environment qualities30.

Previous studies have extensively examined the single physical factor that influences human comfort. They have provided instructions for urban design strategies to improve environmental quality in any single aspect. Nevertheless, environmental quality is influenced by a combination of factors17; Therefore focusing on one aspect is hard to achieve overall improvements31. According to Torresin et al.32, different comfort domains are interrelated and therefore should be studied under the same conceptual framework33. However, only a few studies have investigated multi-domain comfort33. They mostly focused on indoor spaces, i.e., climate chambers33 and classrooms34. Overall discomforts were more likely to take place outdoor due to the lack of shelters and facilities for protection Höppe35. Residents’ comforts were commonly affected by these factors. Meanwhile, some of the perceptive factors are interactively influential, i.e., the thermal perception difference resulting from the variation of acoustic and light contexts36. For achieving an overall improvement, it is necessary to have the comprehensive investigation involving diverse factors.

This study aimed to find subjective responses towards visual, acoustic, and thermal environments by field measurements. The analysis results could be beneficial for future environmental design work to meet pedestrians’ needs. The whole work was carried while considering the following steps:Finding out a certain site with multiple environmental conditions (acoustic, light, & thermal) that affect subjective overall perceptions.Selecting a variety of points with various physical characteristics as sample subsites for field surveys.Measuring selected points about their environmental qualities and interviewing pedestrians for subjective responses towards them.Statistically associating subjective response data with environment quality parameters for defining the conditions that suit people’s physiological preference.

Methodology

Sample city

Chongqing (29.7°N, 106.6°E) is a municipality city, located in the southwest of China. Chongqing belongs to the Cfa climate zone and has special climatic characteristics. The Cfa refers to humid subtropical climate, which has rainy-hot summers and dry-cold winters37. Nevertheless, Chongqing usually has hotter summers compared to other cities in this zone38. This may be due to its geographical features, such as mountainous and riverside cities39. The seasonal maximum temperatures achieved 43 ℃ during 202240. There were significant altitude differences between different locations around the city, which might induce special climate. The poor climate could result in environmental problems in a variety of aspects, such as noise41 and light pollution42. They could degraded local citizens’ living qualities.

Site selection

Residents spent more than 2/3 of their lifetime in residences (one’s own home)43. The environment quality in the residential area could significantly impact domestic lives. It is necessary to understand the side factors and their effects on dwellers, e.g., noises, glare, and heat stress44. This study selected two residential districts situating in the Yubei District (a key metropolitan district) of Chongqing as the cases for dwellers’ comfort research (Fig. 1). There are social problems around the sample areas such as noises of transportation, business behaviors, and leisure activities. They take places in most other residence spaces in Chongqing. This might be a common metropolitan phenomenon in this city. Subjective comfort levels in the districts were negatively affected by surrounding areas although well planned and forested. There are an express way, a public square, and a business area nearby (Figs. 2 and 3). Subjective activities produce a lot of noise, including urban traffic, nature, and social activities causing noises around the residential areas45. The direct sunlight causes glare, which causes visual discomfort46. Previous studies have confirmed that vegetation and artificial design factors were adjustable on comforts47–49. It is the percentage of visible sky from the ground in a hemisphere, which impacts the environmental quality in various aspects, including heat52 as well as sound level53. Sites selected in this study had different SVF. This ensured different environmental qualities, causing various comfort levels. Environmental quality parameters

The environmental quality is significant for people’s comfort levels. Generally, residents are sensitive to heat, light, and acoustic environments17. All of these parameters are considered in this study.

Thermal environment/comfort indices

The thermal comfort is a complex perception. Any parameter about the meteorology could be effective54. Hence, factors relating to that need to be considered. OTC studies usually use complex indices to evaluate that comprehensively. Each index contains several meteorological parameters. The PET is a comprehensive index developed by Höppe35. It represents the temperature of a certain given place (outdoor or indoor), which is equal to the Ta for thermal balance of human body in a typical indoor environment (Ta = MRT, relative humidity = 50%, Va = 0.1 m/s, etc.). Leq refers to a single sound level value (dB) that quantifies the noise environment into any required duration. It is also called the average sound level LAeq (dBA); T, Pt, and P0 are time period(s), sound pressure (Pa, N/m2); and reference sound pressure (2 10–5 Pa, N/m2). People are sensitive to the noise level. Strong noises could result in poor work efficiency61, bad sleeping quality62, and mental discomforts63. Numerous recent studies have investigated ways to reduce the negative effects of noise. Studies have shown that LAeq above 55 dBA could negatively affect individuals studies and works. People would be wakened up by that above 60 dBA, which is that being higher than 70 dBA might cause heart diseases64.

Light environment

Light has positively influenced on human civilisation65. The light intensity (lx) affects human perception66. Numerous studies have been conducted to understand the light and its side effects. The calculated illuminance is the amount of light reaching a certain point on the surface, which is measured in lux (lumens/m2) or foot candle (lumens/foot2)67. Outdoor light levels were much higher (11,080–18,176 lx) than indoors (112–156 lx). Outdoor illuminance levels vary significantly depending on weather, geographic location, and elevation, with illuminance levels reaching 130,000 lx on a clear day to 15,000 lx under shaded or cloudy conditions68. Illuminance depends on the intensity, distance and angle of the light source. Humans are very sensitive to illumination. More than half of individuals are satisfied with the Lux level of 103–5 * 103 Lux69.

Questionnaire survey

The questionnaire survey in this study contained two parts, subjective responses towards various environmental quality parameters (Part 1) and individual factors (Part 2) that could affect the responses (Table 3). In Part 1, subjective perception votes, including thermal sensation vote (TSV), acoustic sensation vote (ASV), light sensation vote (LSV) and overall comfort vote (OCV) were assessed. In Part 2, personal information of the volunteers was collected, including gender, height, weight, age, clothing and main type of activity. All the parameters were followed as per ISO70 and ASHRAE Standard 55 (Table 271). The questionnaire took place in the residential areas and hence most volunteers were regional dwellers. The questionnaires were approved by Southwest University of Science and Technology. All research was performed in accordance with human rights. Consents were obtained from all subjects and/ or their legal guardians. Volunteers were assured they would not be harmed before the interviews. Dwellers of the two areas walked around the sample sites frequently. The passersby were invited to participate in the questionnaire voluntarily. A total of 1563 questionnaire sheets were collected. Data analyses

This study utilised multiple linear regression (MLR) to predict the linear correlation between the dependent variable and independent variables73. Subjective perceptions were affected by environment quality, which is available as dependent variables. Their influencing elements (meteorology, LAeq, light etc.) were defined as independent variables. Pedestrians could have different responses followed by the changes in any environment parameters. Multiple and Bayesian regressions were used in different steps of the whole analyses depending on the quantity of variables. The Bayesian regressions were used for associating each single sensation vote with its corresponded environmental parameter, such as the linear association between TSV and PET74. This was made available to find dwellers’ neutral points. Furthermore, human overall comforts were commonly affected by various elements. This needed to be analysed by multiple models. OCV could be affected by thermal, visual, and acoustic environment parameters36.

Results

Subjective responses towards various environmental parameters

People’s thermal, acoustic, and light sensations presented different trends in environmental conditions (Fig. 7). The PET rise of 10 °C caused TSV to increase by 2.4 (District 1) and 2.1 (District 2). Passersby felt thermal free at the PET point of about 26.6 °C average (26.8 °C in District 1 and 26.4 °C in District 2), which is their NTs. They could be acoustically comfortable if mean LAeq is lowered beyond 56.5 dBA (56.3 dBA in District 1 and 56.9 dBA in District 2). Meanwhile, if the mean sun-lighting level achieved 21.4 klx (20 klx for District 1 and 22.7 klx for District 2), they would be perceptive over-light. Values in the two zones were very close. Their mean neutral points were calculated as 26.6 °C (thermal), 56.5 dBA (acoustic), and 21.4 klx (light), respsectively.Figure 7Linear correlation between subjective responses and various environmental parameters in the two districts.

Roles of subjective perceptions on overall comfort vote

Discussion

This study has investigated citizens’ comforts in various aspects under different environmental contexts. Their thermal responses varied for sound pressure levels and light intensities75. The thermal environment played a more significant role in pedestrians’ overall comforts. These phenomena resulted from the characteristics of human physiology, psychology, and seasonal stress76. Numerous researchers have proved that thermal comfort is affected by the acoustic environment, while thermal sensation is not affected by the acoustic environment. The overall comfort is not only affected by the main effects of temperature and sound pressure level of the main sound source, but also by the interaction between temperature and sound pressure level77. Scholars have also found the outdoor neutral points of pedestrians for various environmental parameters through linear regression analysis. Similarly, residents’ thermal responses (expressed by NT) were also different for acoustic and light environments. There are different degrees of negative correlation between dwellers’ overall comfort and these parameters78. There were similar studies conducted previously. They were compared with the present study (Table 8). Sounds could affect citizens’ living qualities in diverse aspects, e.g., psychological enjoyment79 etc.. Their effects on mental conditions could result in different feelings under the same physical context. Dwellers would feel thermally unacceptable in noisy environments. Individuals would be uncomfortable under noisier or quitter situations80. This would negatively influence thermal acceptances9. The present study has revealed that subjective thermal responses varied for acoustic background. However, they showed insignificant linear correlation. Local dwellers have adapted to these types of noises, for long-term. Perhaps there are many uncontrollable factors (traffic, insects, and social noise) in the experimental environment, and there are no good control variables. Because of the season and living environment, dwellers’ comfort for background sound could be different5.

Subjective perceptions vary for light environments. The lowest NTs emerged at the LSV between 1 and 2 in both two districts (26.2 ℃ abd 27.6 ℃). Lighting levels were influential on human thermal perceptions. Giorgia Chinazzo et al.81 evaluated the effect of light intensity on dwellers subjective thermal sensation and distinguished thermal sensation from thermal evaluation. Their results showed that illumination has a significant effect on citizens thermal sensation, but only in terms of thermal evaluation, and had no significant effect on thermal sensation. Golasi et al.82 studied the effect of lighting color temperature on indoor thermal comfort. The color temperature of lighting was found to have certain effect on individuals thermal sensation, but it was lower than the effect related to gender. Mayes et al.83 reported that the perception of the environment was the overall integration of human senses. In some cases, humans give priority to visual information. Their research results showed that illumination had a significant effect on pedestrians thermal sensation and was reflected in thermal evaluation.

Tables 6 and 7 show the significant correlation between OCV and various environmental factors (heat, acoustic, and light). While some of them showed crucial impacts. Meanwhile, most of them had negative correlations. Therefore, stronger lighting, noise, and heat levels could degrade pedestrians’ comforts. There were natural heat stresses in this season. Any factor increasing heat stress would reduce the comfort level69. Nevertheless, people’s responses towards some environment factors were insignificant. The acoustic environment showed poor effects. This was expressed in Models 1 and 2. This might result from individual physiological adaptation in permanent noise84. The LSV linearly changed with the light intensity. This conflicted with the results of Liu85 and Guo et al.86 (polynomial correlated). It might result from the special contextual experimenting conditions. The measurement was conducted during the summer, mostly in the daytime. Volunteers might be insensitive to darkness in outdoor spaces during summers. Moreover, the physical environment of each measuring point is different and vegetation as well as architectural shadows may block the light intensity.

There were some strategies about urban design obtained based on the results of this study. They might be available to create comfortable outdoor environments. In summer, the thermal environment could affect the visual comfort. There could be different neutral temperatures under the same sound and light conditions. Thermal comfort is the most important factor affecting the overall comfort. Therefore, the green layout, artificial shelter and waterscape design that can reduce noise and cool down the residential area are necessary.

There were also limitations in this study. Although people’s thermal responses have been found to vary for acoustic and light environments, slight linear correlations between them were found to be present. People partially preferred being cooler under noisier conditions. This might result from the shortages of samples. In addition, there were other aspects of environmental qualities, such as air quality. They would be perceptively influential, which is unconsidered by this study. Factors temporarily uninvolved would be research opportunities for future studies.

Conclusion

The comfort investigation of outdoor environments of residential areas were carried out in the Yubei District of Chongqing (China). The thermal response of pedestrians under different environmental conditions (expressed by NT) were discussed by using the method of subjective questionnaire survey and objective measurement. A prediction model was established to evaluate the overall comfort of residents. The main results are as follows.The neutral points of residents from the sample sites towards thermal (PET), acoustic (LAeq) and visual environments were 26.6 ℃, 56.6 dBA, and 21.4 klx on average. Slight differences were found to be present between the two sample districts. People in the two districts had different thermal perceptions since the sound level variation, and the sound pressure level had an impact on people 's TSV. The NTs of the Districts 1 and 2 were 24.2 and 28.4 ℃ respectively as the ASV ranged from − 1 to 1. Thermal perceptions also varied for light environments. The lowest NTs emerged at LSV ranging from 0 to 1, 26.2 ℃ (District 1) and 27.6 ℃ (District 2), respectively.Multiple regression models were established to predict the overall comfort of subjective parameters (ASV, TSV, and LSV) and objective parameters (LUX, PET and LAeq) as predictors. The prediction results of the model showed that the environmental quality parameters were significant for subjective perceptions. Compared to acoustic and light parameters (sig. < 0.000), the thermal index was relatively significant. Similarly, in terms of subjective perception, TSV was the most significant in all regions and negatively correlated with OCV (sig < 0.000). LSV was partially effective. The objective physical environment parameters and subjective perception in the model were significantly correlated with the overall comfort, indicating that it can effectively predict the overall comfort of people.
