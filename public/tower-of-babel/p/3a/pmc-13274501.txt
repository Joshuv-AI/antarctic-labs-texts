Digital Competence in Managing Digital Resources Among Health Professions Faculty in Ethiopian Public Universities: Implications for CME/CPD Digital Transformation
Authors: Amare Equlinet Misganaw, Awoke Giletew Wondie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContinuous medical education and continuing professional development (CME/CPD) have traditionally emphasised pedagogical effectiveness, yet the rapid digital transformation of health professions education now requires faculty to demonstrate advanced competencies in technology integration, digital resource management, accessibility, and online instruction. Faculty digital proficiency is increasingly essential for designing, delivering, and sustaining high-quality CME/CPD programmes that support workforce readiness and equitable access to learning opportunities. Evidence from resource-constrained settings remains limited. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 498 faculty members across nine Ethiopian public higher education institutions offering six priority health programmes. Data were collected using the DigCompEdu framework (Area 2: Digital Resources). Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics and proficiency levels. Associations between categorical variables and expertise (Expert+ vs. Below Expert) were examined using Chi-square and Fisher’s Exact tests. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of expert-level proficiency, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Only 34 faculty (6.8%) reported expert-level digital proficiency, with most clustered at novice or explorer levels. Expertise was more common among faculty from research universities (12.9%), non-clinical units (13.3%), doctoral degree holders (33.3%), female faculty (23.1%), and those with ≥10 years of teaching experience (17.0%). Regression analysis revealed that male faculty had significantly higher odds of expertise (OR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.35–4.45, p = 0.003), while higher academic rank was inversely associated (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.15–0.68, p = 0.002). Institutional type was strongly predictive (OR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.06–0.35, p < 0.001). Academic qualification and teaching experience were not significant predictors. Experts play a pivotal role in advancing educational practice by modelling innovation in teaching and learning. Yet, this study reveals a significant deficiency in faculty digital skills, with most lacking expertise in managing digital resources for student learning. Targeted, tiered faculty development is therefore essential to strengthen digital competence and enhance CME/CPD delivery in health professions education.

Background

Continuous professional development (CPD) in higher education institutions (HEIs) is widely acknowledged as a cornerstone for sustaining and enhancing faculty competence. In this manuscript, continuing medical education/continuing professional development (CME/CPD) refers to structured lifelong learning activities that support health professionals in maintaining and improving competence after initial qualification; CPD is the term used in Ethiopia’s regulatory framework, while CME is retained to align with terminology commonly used in medical continuing education literature. Traditionally, CPD initiatives have been informed by needs assessments that prioritise pedagogical domains such as curriculum design, instructional strategies, and assessment methods [1]. While these areas remain indispensable, the accelerating digital transformation of higher education has introduced new demands that extend beyond pedagogy, requiring faculty to develop advanced digital competencies, including accessibility, online teaching modalities, and integration of emerging technologies [2–4]The rapid digital transformation requires faculty to demonstrate proficiency in integrating technology into teaching, research, and administrative tasks.

Digital proficiency among faculty members has emerged as a cornerstone of effective higher education, particularly as institutions increasingly adopt e-learning and blended learning models [5]. When educators are skilled at managing digital resources, they can curate, organise, and integrate high-quality materials into their teaching, thereby enriching students’ learning experience. This competency ensures that learners have access to relevant, interactive, and up-to-date content, which enhances both engagement and comprehension [6,7]. Faculty digital proficiency also promotes inclusivity and equity in education. By effectively using digital platforms and accessibility features, educators can tailor resources to diverse student needs, reducing disparities in learning outcomes. This is especially important in health professions education, where equitable access to knowledge directly influences workforce readiness [8].

Global frameworks such as DigCompEdu and UNICEF’s Educators’ Digital Competency Framework underscore digital literacy as a core requirement for educators, yet many institutions have not embedded these standards into faculty evaluation or development processes [9,10]. The absence of reliable data on faculty digital proficiency, therefore, constitutes a critical problem, creating uncertainty about the institution’s readiness for digital transformation. Although international frameworks have advanced the conceptualisation and assessment of faculty digital proficiency, most empirical studies have been conducted in high-income settings [9,10]. These studies provide valuable insights into faculty readiness but fail to capture the realities of resource-constrained environments. In particular, there is a lack of empirical evidence on faculty digital proficiency levels in developing countries, where infrastructural limitations, uneven access to technology, and contextual challenges may significantly influence competence [2]. Moreover, existing assessments frequently rely on standardised tools that are not adapted to local institutional, cultural, or regional contexts, limiting the relevance of the findings and overlooking unique challenges faced by faculty in diverse educational environments [4]. Finally, the literature reveals a notable gap in understanding how faculty digital proficiency varies across disciplines, institutions, and regions, particularly within resource-constrained settings. Addressing these gaps is essential for advancing a more contextually grounded and inclusive understanding of faculty digital proficiency in higher education [2,4].

Although this study focuses on faculty engaged in pre-service health professions education, its relevance to CME/CPD is direct. In Ethiopia, Health Professions Education Institutions (HPEIs) are legally mandated providers of CPD for health professionals. As a result, university faculty contribute not only to undergraduate and postgraduate training but also to the design, development, delivery, and evaluation of CPD activities. The same educators often serve as trainers, facilitators, mentors, and content developers within national and institutional CPD programmes. Consequently, faculty digital competence is critical for both pre-service education and the effectiveness, quality, and digital transformation of CME/CPD systems.

This study provides timely, contextually grounded evidence on faculty digital proficiency in Ethiopian higher education institutions offering health training programmes. Specifically, it aimed to assess faculty self-reported digital proficiency levels and examine variations across disciplines, institutions, and regions. By identifying faculty strengths and perceived gaps in digital competence, the study contributes to the limited evidence from developing-country contexts. It provides actionable insights for designing targeted faculty development initiatives, strengthening digital learning capacity, and supporting the digital transformation of CME/CPD systems.

Methods and Materials

Study Design

We conducted an institution-based cross-sectional survey between December 2022 and February 2023 in Ethiopian public higher education institutions (HEIs) offering health training programmes.

Study Setting and Population

The study was conducted in Ethiopian public HEIs offering six priority health programmes (medicine, nursing, anaesthesia, midwifery, laboratory sciences, and pharmacy. The Ministry of Health has identified these programmes as priority health programmes. At the time of the study, 16 public HEIs offered the six priority academic programmes, and more than 5,000 health professional educators were employed in these HEIs. The target population for this study consisted of all health professional educators working in these public HEIs that provide the six priority health training programmes.

Sampling

To ensure representation of educators from all types of institutions across the 16 targeted HEIs offering the six priority academic programmes, we employed a stratified sampling approach. The HEIs were first stratified according to their primary mission, as defined by the Ministry of Education – research universities, universities of applied sciences, and comprehensive universities [11]. From these strata, nine HEIs (50% of all eligible institutions) were randomly selected for inclusion. Within each selected HEI, faculty participants were then sampled proportionally using simple random sampling to achieve balanced representation across institutional types.

Because comparable studies were lacking in similar low-resource settings, the minimum required sample size was calculated using the standard formula for estimating a proportion in a finite population:n=Z1α/22⋅P1−Pd2

where Z corresponds to the 95% confidence level, P was conservatively set at 0.50 due to the absence of prior prevalence estimates, and d was set at 0.05. This yielded a minimum sample size of 383 faculty members. To account for additional sampling variance introduced by the stratified design, a design effect (DEFF) of 1.5—consistent with current survey methodology recommendations – was applied. After this adjustment, the final target sample size was 577 faculty educators.

Instrument (DigCompEdu)

Data were collected using the Digital Competence Framework for Educators (DigCompEdu). DigCompEdu builds on the European Digital Competence Framework (DigComp) and focuses on the digital competencies educators need to integrate technology into teaching. Developed by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre in 2017, it serves as a widely recognised reference for self‑assessment, professional development, and evaluation [12]. We employed DigCompEdu 2.1 for its structured six‑level proficiency model (Newcomer to Pioneer). The framework comprises six domains; this study focused on Area 2: Digital Resources (selecting, creating/modifying, managing/protecting/sharing). DigCompEdu 2.1 provides descriptors and performance indicators for each level, guiding professional development [13]. For contextual relevance, Area 2 was adapted for local use through expert review by five specialists in digital education and health professions training. Minor terminology adjustments were made, followed by a pilot with 15 faculty at a non‑participating HEI, which provided support for face and content validity (Supplementary File 1). The digital resource-use items assessed faculty self-reported competence in selecting, creating, modifying, managing, protecting, and sharing digital resources for teaching and learning broadly. The instrument did not ask participants to distinguish whether these resources were used for pre-service teaching, in-service training, or CME/CPD delivery. Therefore, CPD-specific digital resource use was not analysed separately. In addition, Specific data on the tools or platforms used (e.g. learning management systems, communication platforms, content authoring tools) were not collected. However, Ethiopian HEIs commonly employ Moodle-based LMSs, institutional e-learning portals, and basic authoring tools such as PowerPoint and PDFs for teaching and CPD activities.

Data Collection

Data collection occurred from 15 December 2022, to 28 February 2023. It was administered by trained data collectors using Open Data Kit (ODK), an open‑source Android‑based platform suitable for large‑scale surveys in resource‑constrained contexts [14].

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using R. Associations between faculty characteristics and digital proficiency (Expert+ vs. Below Expert) were examined using Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact tests, and independent predictors were identified through multivariable logistic regression. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p values.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 498 faculty members were included in the analysis. Nearly half were affiliated with research universities (48.2%), while applied and comprehensive institutions each accounted for 25.9%. The majority worked in clinical units (80.1%), with 19.9% in basic sciences. Most participants held a second degree (90.4%), whereas 6.0% had a doctoral qualification and 3.6% a first degree. Academic rank was evenly distributed, with 47.6% lecturers and 52.4% assistant professors or above. The sample was predominantly male (92.2%), with females comprising 7.8%. Teaching experience varied: 12.0% reported ≤ 5 years, 59.6% reported 5–10 years, and 28.3% reported ≥ 10 years. The mean age of participants was 34.2 years (SD = 5.4). Discussion

This study sought to determine the proficiency of health professions educators in managing digital resources, an area increasingly recognised as essential for both pre-service health professions education and technology-enabled CME/CPD delivery. As one of the first national studies in Ethiopia to generate empirical data on educators’ digital skills within health professions education, the findings provide HPEIs and CPD systems with critical, data-driven insights to guide digital learning initiatives, strengthen teaching capacity, and support the digital transformation of continuing professional development [15,16].

In particular, expert-level and advanced digital literacy skills are critical, as prior studies have linked higher educator digital competence with educational quality, learner engagement, and learning outcomes. Educators who achieve higher levels of digital competence are better equipped to identify reliable resources, design high-quality digital content, and integrate technology into educational practice – key elements that can enhance learning experiences for both pre-service learners and health professionals participating in CME/CPD activities [12,16,17].

Consistent with the developmental perspective of the DigCompEdu framework, our findings indicate that nearly 9 out of 10 educators were at the Novice, Explorer, or Integrator levels of proficiency in selecting, creating, modifying, and managing digital resources. This distribution suggests that most faculty possess only basic to intermediate digital competencies and have not yet progressed to the advanced levels required for strategic and pedagogically sophisticated use of digital technologies. Similar patterns have been reported internationally, where most educators cluster at intermediate levels of digital competence and relatively few attain Expert or Pioneer status. Studies applying the DigCompEdu framework across higher education and educator development contexts have likewise demonstrated that advanced digital proficiency remains uncommon, even within technologically mature educational systems [9]. These findings underscore the global challenge of moving educators beyond functional technology use towards pedagogically meaningful digital integration.

The predominance of Novice, Explorer, and Integrator-level educators has important implications for health professions education and CME/CPD delivery. At these proficiency levels, educators are generally capable of locating, using, and sharing digital resources, and some may incorporate basic multimedia or interactive elements into their teaching. However, they may have limited capacity to critically evaluate digital resources, create sophisticated learning materials, implement accessibility standards, or systematically manage and curate digital content. Consequently, the design of online learning experiences may remain largely content-centred rather than learner-centred, potentially limiting opportunities for engagement, collaboration, and self-directed learning [12,18].

These limitations are particularly relevant for CME/CPD programmes, which increasingly rely on digital platforms to support flexible, lifelong learning among health professionals. Faculty operating below expert proficiency may encounter challenges in developing high-quality online CME courses, facilitating meaningful asynchronous learning, and utilising advanced digital assessment approaches such as automated feedback, learning analytics, competency tracking, and adaptive assessment strategies. As a result, the educational potential of digital CME/CPD platforms may not be fully realised, even where technological infrastructure is available. However, because the survey did not distinguish digital resource use in pre-service education from use in CME/CPD or in-service training contexts, these findings should be interpreted as indicators of faculty digital capacity relevant to CME/CPD systems rather than as direct evidence of CPD-specific digital practice.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that educators frequently lack the advanced digital skills required for effective integration of technology into teaching and learning [19]. Research has identified particular weaknesses in areas such as digital communication, online information management, and the creation and modification of audiovisual learning materials [20]. These deficiencies can influence both the quality of educational experiences and learners’ opportunities to develop their own digital competencies.

The finding that only 6.8% of participants reported expert-level proficiency warrants particular attention. Expert educators play a critical role in leading digital innovation, mentoring colleagues, and supporting institutional adoption of technology-enhanced learning. Although this study did not directly assess CME/CPD programme performance, learner outcomes, or platform utilisation, the low proportion of expert-level faculty suggests a potential capacity limitation for the scalability and sustainability of online CME/CPD initiatives. A limited pool of digitally proficient faculty may reduce the interactivity of digital learning platforms and slow broader digital transformation efforts within health professions education. In resource-constrained settings, where digital CME offers opportunities to overcome geographic and infrastructural barriers, insufficient faculty expertise may further limit institutional readiness to expand equitable access to high-quality continuing professional development opportunities. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of faculty capacity and institutional readiness, rather than as direct evidence of CME/CPD effectiveness or learner outcomes.

This proficiency profile also aligns with findings from other settings, where educators often struggle with advanced competencies such as creating interactive multimedia resources, applying accessibility standards, and managing digital resources securely and systematically. Previous research has consistently linked higher levels of educator digital competence with improved learner engagement, enhanced educational quality, and better learning outcomes, highlighting the importance of progressing beyond basic technology use towards pedagogically grounded digital integration in both health professions education and CME/CPD delivery [21,22]

The developmental trajectory described by DigCompEdu provides a useful framework for addressing these gaps. Educators at lower proficiency levels require structured support, mentorship, and opportunities for guided practice, while those at intermediate levels benefit from collaborative learning, experimentation, and peer exchange. These differentiated needs suggest that tiered faculty development strategies may be particularly effective for advancing digital competence and strengthening institutional capacity for digitally enabled teaching and CME/CPD delivery [23]

Multivariable regression revealed that male faculty had significantly higher odds of expert-level digital proficiency compared with females (OR = 2.45, p = 0.003. This finding is consistent with prior studies reporting greater ICT confidence and frequency of use among male academics, reflecting differences in self-efficacy and exposure [24,25]. Although some recent evidence suggests that gender gaps may narrow with structured institutional ICT training [25]. Our adjusted model indicates that sex remains an independent predictor. For HPEIs and CPD systems, this finding highlights the importance of ensuring equitable access to digital training, mentorship, and technology-supported teaching opportunities across faculty groups involved in both pre-service and continuing professional education

Faculty with higher academic rank demonstrated significantly lower odds of expert proficiency (OR = 0.32, p = 0.002), supporting frameworks that highlight generational and structural differences in ICT adoption [26,27]. Senior academics, often trained before widespread digital integration, may have had fewer opportunities for structured ICT training, while increased administrative responsibilities may limit engagement with emerging technologies. In contrast, academic qualifications and teaching experience were not significant predictors, underscoring that digital competence is a distinct skill set requiring continuous development rather than being automatically acquired through academic advancement or tenure [28]. This has direct relevance for CME/CPD systems, where senior faculty may serve as subject-matter experts, mentors, or CPD facilitators but may still require targeted support to design and deliver digitally enabled continuing education.

Implications for Practice

The findings of limited expert-level digital proficiency among faculty have important implications for health professions education and CME/CPD systems, particularly in contexts where HPEIs serve as legally mandated CPD providers. Although this study did not directly assess CME/CPD effectiveness, learner outcomes, clinical practice change, or patient care outcomes, it identifies faculty digital competence as a foundational institutional capability that may influence the quality, reach, scalability, and sustainability of technology-enabled CME/CPD delivery.

For CME/CPD providers, these findings suggest the need to embed digital competence expectations into CPD provider requirements, quality assurance mechanisms, and accreditation standards. Such expectations could include faculty capacity to select and adapt evidence-based digital resources, design accessible online learning materials, facilitate asynchronous learning, and use digital assessment and feedback tools. Integrating frameworks such as DigCompEdu into CME/CPD provider standards may help institutions move beyond ad hoc technology use towards systematic digital capacity building.

For CME/CPD programme designers, the results highlight the importance of designing tiered faculty development programmes that are explicitly aligned with CME delivery contexts. Novice-level faculty may require guided support in locating, evaluating, and sharing digital resources for CPD activities. Explorer-level faculty may benefit from mentorship in developing interactive online materials and moving beyond static presentations or email-based resource sharing. Integrator-level faculty may require advanced training in learning management system use, multimedia content development, accessibility, learner analytics, digital assessment, and online facilitation. This tiered approach would allow faculty development to respond to actual proficiency levels rather than assuming that all educators require the same type of training.

The findings can also inform institutional strategies for learning management system adoption, digital CME content development, and the expansion of remote or online CPD. Institutions with a limited pool of expert-level faculty may need to prioritise developing digital CME/CPD champions who can mentor colleagues, support course design, review the quality of digital content, and guide the integration of interactive platform features. In resource-constrained settings, such strategies may be particularly important for expanding access to CPD opportunities for geographically dispersed health professionals while maintaining educational quality. Broader impacts on workforce readiness, institutional innovation, educational outcomes, and patient care remain potential implications that require further empirical study.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

Using DigCompEdu 2.1 (Area 2: Digital Resources) provided a validated, internationally recognised framework for assessing competence, with clear descriptors across six proficiency levels. Contextual relevance was enhanced through expert review and a pilot, which supported face and content validity. However, the gender imbalance in the sample limits the precision of inferences for female faculty, and the adapted tool was not subjected to formal psychometric validation (e.g. construct validity), which should be prioritised in future work. The study did not collect profession-specific data, educator roles, CPD engagement, or whether reported digital resource use occurred in pre-service, in-service, or CME/CPD delivery contexts. This limits direct assessment of how faculty digital competence translates into CPD-specific course design, delivery, assessment, or learner outcomes. Future research should address these gaps to enhance applicability for both pre-service health professions education and CME/CPD practice.

Importantly, this study relied on self-reported measures that capture perceived competence rather than observed digital skills. Such measures may introduce bias through over- or under-estimation and lack objective validation. To strengthen rigour, future studies should triangulate self-assessment with performance-based evaluations and incorporate qualitative insights into barriers and enablers of digital competence. In addition, future research should integrate professional-level and CPD data to enhance the relevance of findings for practice. Collecting tool-specific usage data – such as faculty engagement with learning management systems, authoring platforms, and communication tools – would provide a more granular understanding of digital proficiency. Taken together, these approaches would yield a more comprehensive and contextually grounded picture of faculty capacity, ensuring that findings translate more reliably into both pre-service digital education and CME/CPD programme design, delivery, and evaluation.

Conclusion

Expert educators play a crucial role in advancing digital education across health professions education and CME/CPD systems by modelling innovation in teaching, learning, and technology-enabled professional development. However, this study highlights a substantial deficiency in faculty digital proficiency, with most participants lacking expertise in managing digital resources for pre-service and continuing professional learning. Targeted, tiered faculty development is therefore essential to strengthen digital competence and enhance institutional capacity for digital teaching and technology-enabled CME/CPD delivery.

At the Novice stage, educators require guidance and encouragement to apply existing digital skills in educational contexts. Explorers benefit from peer modelling and structured mentorship, while Integrators need opportunities for experimentation and reflection within collaborative communities to progress towards the Expert level. These staged needs provide a practical blueprint for faculty development programmes that support both health professions education and CME/CPD systems.

Although CME/CPD-specific delivery outcomes were not assessed, the findings suggest that limited faculty digital proficiency may represent an important capacity constraint for the implementation, scalability, and digital transformation of continuing professional development programmes. Future research should empirically examine how platform-specific proficiency, including faculty use of learning management systems, authoring tools, and communication systems, translates into effective CME/CPD delivery, learner engagement, institutional innovation, and educational quality.
