MRA and Doppler ultrasound of laryngeal blood flow under hormonal and hydration modulation
Authors: Taylor W. Bailey, Niharika Narra, Naila Cannes do Nascimento, M. Preeti Sivasankar, Craig J. Goergen, Abigail Cox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePhonation is a complex process requiring an appropriate tissue environment of the vocal folds, with laryngeal blood flow serving as one factor that contributes to tissue homeostasis. Effects of dehydration and reproductive hormone cycling to blood flow and vascular dynamics are described in the literature, but there is a gap in our understanding how these factors may influence the laryngeal blood supply directly. The present study has used a mild systemic dehydration and hormone manipulation paradigm in rats to evaluate the related impacts to the superior thyroid artery using Doppler ultrasound and time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography.ResultsThis study provides evidence that hormone status can modify the manifestation of mild systemic dehydration as reflected in bodyweight loss. The imaging methods employed did not reliably identify perturbations to blood flow related to either hydration or hormone status, which we attribute to their practical resolution with respect to the superior thyroid artery and subtlety of the hypothesized effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07817-3.

Introduction

Phonation (also known as voice production) is a complex process that depends on the coordinated activity of the respiratory, laryngeal, and supralaryngeal systems, and a healthy vocal fold environment. Ample research has demonstrated that systemic dehydration of the body can negatively impact phonation [1, 2], an effect that may be explained collectively by changes in tissue viscoelasticity, oxidative stress, and other molecular changes that have been identified [3–6]. The primary blood supply of the vocal folds is the superior thyroid artery (STA) that branches off the external carotid artery (ECA) a short distance distal to the carotid bifurcation. The STA was the target vessel of the present study and is well described in humans via various imaging modalities [7–10]; however, studies specifically inclusive of the rat STA are limited, particularly outside of the context of the thyroid gland and surgical intervention to facilitate cerebral ischemia. The impacts of hormonal signaling in cycling human females has been shown to influence STA vascular dynamics [9, 10] and have implications to the physical manifestation of systemic dehydration [11, 12], but characterization of the potential interacting effects of hormone signaling with mild systemic dehydration are limited. Here we have used female rats as an animal model and applied both magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and pulse wave Doppler ultrasound to evaluate the impacts of ovariectomy with and without estrogen supplementation in combination with mild water-restriction induced systemic dehydration on cervical vasculature and STA blood flow, respectively.

Methods

Animal care and experimental manipulation

Forty-eight female, Sprague-Dawley rats, 21 weeks old, were obtained from Envigo (Indianapolis, IN) and were either kept intact (n = 16) or ovariectomized (OVX; n = 32) prior to arrival. The experimental timeline is summarized in Fig. 1. OVX groups were randomly assigned to a subcutaneous estradiol (E2) pellet or a placebo pellet (Placebo), which were implanted five weeks prior to the start of the experiment. Half of the rats were subject to water-restriction (4 ml water/100 g bodyweight) to induce mild systemic dehydration, while the euhydration group received ad libitum water at all times. Water restriction was maintained for 5 days and was repeated for a second 5-day period following a week of ad libitum water access for all animals. Bodyweight was tracked as a proxy metric for hormone status and hydration. Imaging was obtained for all animals before and after the restriction period, under isoflurane anesthesia on room air, titrated as necessary. Rats were euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation to preserve the structural integrity of the larynx for histological analysis for a separate study, not reported in the present manuscript.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Imaging was performed with a 7T MRI system (BioSpec 70/30, Bruker Corporation) using a transmit-only volume 1 H RF coil (86-mm inner diameter, Bruker). Three-dimensional time-of-flight angiography was completed with sequential imaging planes along the longitudinal axis with the following parameters: repetition time 15 ms, echo time 3 ms, flip angle 20°, averaged over two acquisitions, and voxel size of 0.2 mm isotropic. A saturation band was placed superior to the excitation field of view to reduce signal associated with venous blood flow.

Vascular geometry measurements and 3D modeling

SimVascular (Catalina-2023-05) [13, 14], an open-source software, was used to create 3D models of target vessels and quantify vascular parameters: cross-sectional area of the STA, cross-sectional area of the external carotid artery (ECA) at the STA branch point and at the occipital artery (OA) branch point, and the distance from the carotid bifurcation to the STA branch point. The scan analysis protocol is detailed in Additional file 1.

Ultrasound

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed on a heated pad in a supine position. Fur along the ventral neck was removed by shaving and depilatory cream. Data were acquired with a Vevo 3100 high frequency ultrasound imaging system (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto, ON, Canada) using an MX550D transducer (center transmit 40 MHz, axial resolution 40 μm, default settings for mechanical index). The left STA was located using color Doppler mode, and the pulse wave Doppler mode was used to acquire flow velocity measurements with focus adjusted to the dept of the target measurement. (Additional file 2.A). Data was exported and individual waveforms manually annotated using Vevo Lab (5.9.0, FUJIFILM VisualSonics); three full cardiac cycles were used to calculate an average for each measurement at the given timepoint. Metrics included volume time integral (VTI), peak velocity, mean velocity, resistivity index (RI), and pulsatility index (PI). Animal heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature were monitored throughout the imaging session.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.5.2). Linear models were applied to all analyses. For datasets with repeated measurements, models included a random intercept for individual rats to account for within-subject correlation. For ultrasound measurements, models additionally included a random slope for time within each rat to account for subject-specific changes over time (lme4 [15]). The performance package was used to visually assess modeling assumptions, followed by Type III ANOVA (car [16], lmerTest [17]) and estimated marginal means (emmeans [18]) for post-hoc analysis. Alpha was set as 0.05 for statistical significance with Tukey’s adjustment for full pairwise comparisons and simulated multivariate t-distribution for multiple comparisons where otherwise appropriate. Bodyweight was analyzed as raw values but are reported also as percent change for relative interpretation. Practical interpretation of statistically significant effects for imaging-derived measurements followed the rationale of the experimental design: [1] at the beginning of water restriction period, all animals were euhydrated capturing baseline variation and [2] time-matched repeated measurements were collected for the euhydrated controls to assess thresholds for interpretable differences in the dehydrated groups. A cross-over design was employed to adjust for the effect of repeated dehydration (“Restriction”) on outcome measures, but the limited sample size and large measurement and between-individual variance limit reliable interpretation of the associated highest order interaction effect directly. Results for the entire statistical analysis are available in Additional file 3.

Results

Hormone and dehydration model

Body and organ weights are summarized in Fig. 2. Both OVX animal groups were significantly heavier (Week 0: 45 g, p < 0.001; Fig. 2A) than the intact group prior to estrogen supplementation, after which the estrogen supplementation group (E2) rapidly lost weight initially and subsequently gained bodyweight consistent with the intact group with a negligible difference at five weeks post pellet-implantation (Week 5: 2 g, p = 0.905; Fig. 2A). The Placebo group remained approximately 45 g heavier than both intact and E2 rats (Week 5: both p < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Uterus weight at euthanasia was significantly different between all three groups (Intact: 0.540 g; Placebo: 0.112 g; E2: 0.287 g; all pairwise comparisons p ≤ 0.002; Fig. 2B) corroborating ovariectomy and estrogen supplementation (Fig. 2B). During the experimental period, all dehydrated groups lost bodyweight relative to their euhydrated state (Intact: -14.5 g, -5.2%; Placebo: -12.1 g, -3.8%; E2 -15.8 g, -5.7%, all p < 0.001) and to their respective euhydrated controls (Intact: -13.4 g; Placebo: -8.5 g; E2: -18.0 g) as shown in Fig. 2C. Between hormone groups, these relative changes were statistically significant between the Placebo and E2 groups (p = 0.0092) only (Fig. 2D). Bodyweight for dehydrated animals rapidly restored to baseline with ad libitum water access between restriction periods.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Data from MRA with significant effects are summarized and representative three-dimensional renders shown in Additional file 2.B, C and Fig. 3, respectively. Gross anatomical exceptions were observed in the vasculature of some rats. As confirmed by tracking vessel progression toward their anatomic targets on the three-dimensional TOF-MRA scan, typically the STA is the second artery to branch from the ECA distal to the carotid bifurcation, while the OA is the first. Two scans showed branching of the STA from below the carotid bifurcation, and one scan showed branching of the STA before the OA. While most scans were suitable for quantitative analysis, some lacked the resolution needed to reliably estimate the cross-sectional area of the STA. No statistically significant main effects or target two-way interactions were identified for the cross-sectional areas of the STA, the ECA at the OA branch point, or the distance from the carotid bifurcation to the STA branch point. A significant Hormone: Hydration: Time interaction (F(4.38, 2, 115.7), p = 0.015) was observed for the area of the ECA at the STA branch point, corresponding to an apparent decrease in vessel diameter in the intact dehydrated group relative to an higher magnitude increase in the euhydrated controls, not seen in the other hormone groups.

Ultrasound

Data from ultrasound with significant effects are summarized in Additional file 2D, E. No statistically significant main effects or target interactions were identified for VTI, mean velocity, or peak velocity. A significant Hormone: Hydration: Time: Restriction interaction was observed for both RI (F(5.34, 2, 33.4), p = 0.001) and PI (F(4.38, 2, 34.32), p = 0.020). For both measures, this corresponds to an apparent reversal of the direction of effect between first and second restrictions in the E2 group; however, the absolute magnitude of changes in the restricted groups are smaller than the largest observed for the euhydrated control group.

Discussion

The results demonstrate a successful water restriction and hormone perturbation model in rats. The ~ 5% bodyweight loss is consistent with realistic translationally relevant dehydration, that in the present study is uncoupled from enteric pathogenic etiology [19] and the confound of exercised-induced thermoregulation [20]. The lower relative and absolute water restriction-induced bodyweight loss in the Placebo group highlights the value of endocrine considerations when evaluating other biological implications of even mild systemic dehydration. We note that while the target hormone of the present study is estradiol, ovariectomy results in a collective deficiency of sex hormones and that estradiol supplementation alone does not fully restore the Intact phenotype. Neither MRA nor Doppler ultrasound demonstrated robust or reproducible alterations in STA geometry or blood flow under this experimental paradigm. There is preliminary evidence that RI and PI may be differentially influenced by repeated dehydration, but the small sample size for the highest order interactions in the present study warrant caution in the physiological interpretation. While there is evidence that these parameters of the STA can be influenced by hormone cycling [10], it is possible that compensatory systems to the acute dehydration employed mask any modifying effect with dehydration and that inter-subject variability is augmented by un-synced estrus cycles at the times of measurement in the Intact groups. Future research may be informed by exploring both higher magnitudes or chronic states of dehydration, and the vocal fold capillary interface as a direct mediator of interstitial hydration downstream of the blood flow measured in the present study could be evaluated directly.

Limitations

The primary limitations of the present study stem from the imaging techniques where practical resolution approaches the lower limits needed to capture the small scale of the rat STA and perturbations to blood flow parameters in this model. Published estimates for the diameter of the female rat STA based on corrosion casts (0.25 mm) provide cross-sectional areas on the same order but universally smaller than those obtained in the present study [21]. That many MRA scans did not provide reliably estimable areas at the 0.2 mm resolution coupled with the relatively limited visualized perfusion into the distal aspect of the STA suggest that a higher spatial resolution is necessary and that TOF-MRA may be an impractical approach to assess the STA in this model, particularly in the absence of a contrast enhancement agent. The spatial resolution was sufficient for estimates of the ECA. The ultrasound measurements demonstrate considerable variation between and an inherent variability within individuals as noted by changes seen within control groups. Additionally, the blood flow velocities measured in the present study are on average higher than those reported for female rats of similar size utilizing a different anesthesia protocol highlighting a potentially confounding effect [22]. Thus, ultrasound as employed in the present study may be underpowered to detect true but subtle physiological arterial changes associated with hormone and mild hydration manipulation in this model despite the grossly observed physiological differences.
