A mutation in PTPN6 associated to emphysema alters B-lymphocyte biology in humans and mice
Authors: Marie Pineault, Nadia Milad, Gabrielle Bouffard, Félix Tremblay, Joanie Routhier, Marie-Josée Beaulieu, Sophie Aubin, Ariane Lechasseur, Yohan Bossé, François Maltais, Mathieu C Morissette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur group discovered a mutation in a French-Canadian family causing a severe and early-onset form of emphysema. The heterozygote mutation located in the PTPN6 gene causes an alanine to threonine switch near the catalytic site of SHP-1, leading to a reduction of its enzymatic activity. In this study, we aimed to characterize the respiratory and immune abnormalities caused by this mutation in human carriers (PTPN6Ala455Thr) and mice carrying the equivalent mutation (Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+). Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice spontaneously developed pulmonary tertiary lymphoid tissues (TLTs). They also exhibited a higher proportion of B-1 cells in both lung and spleen. Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice had lower IgG1-specific antibodies to T-independent immunization, while their T-dependent response was normal. Human carriers showed reduced circulating naive B lymphocytes and increased memory B lymphocytes, a phenotype mainly observed in men, and had lower circulating levels of IgG1 and IgG4 as well as higher levels of IgG3. Spontaneous TLT formation in mice and alterations of B-cell biology in both humans and mice are caused by the mutation. This reinforces the link between emphysema, pulmonary TLT formation, and B cells; however, the exact mechanism involving B cells and pulmonary TLT in the development of emphysema remains elusive.

Introduction

Tobacco smoking is the main risk factor for the development of emphysema, and genetic variants in genes such as SERPINA1 encoding for alpha-1-anti-trypsin can greatly predispose to emphysema development in both people who smoke and people who do not smoke.1 Recently, a point mutation in PTPN6 gene encoding for the protein phosphatase Src homology region 2 domain-containing phosphatase-1 (SHP-1) was linked to early-onset and severe emphysema in a French-Canadian family.2 The penetrance of the mutation is nearly absolute, with almost all heterozygote carriers being afflicted with emphysema. The mutation causes an alanine (Ala) to threonine (Thr) switch at position 455 (PTPN6Ala455Thr) of the SHP-1 protein, reducing the phosphatase activity.

SHP-1 is a protein phosphatase involved in the regulation of numerous immunological pathways.3 Mice carrying mutations in Ptpn6, such as “motheaten” mice (me/me),4 “viable motheaten” mice (mev/mev),5 or Ptpn6Spin mice, exhibit immunological abnormalities due to either a loss of SHP-1 protein,6 a decrease in its activity,7 or a disruption of SHP-1 signaling.8

Of interest, Me/me and mev/mev mice manifest several abnormalities in the lymphoid compartment.9 These mice have high levels of serum autoantibodies10 and immunoglobulins.11 An expansion of B-1 lymphocytes is observed in both me/me and mev/mev,11,12 and specific deletion of CD5 limits this expansion as well as immune cell accumulation in the lungs and improves survival of mev/mev mice.13 More recently, a CD19-driven deletion of SHP-1 in B cells caused an accumulation of B-1a cells in the spleen, peritoneal cavity, bone marrow, and lymph nodes.14 These mice also exhibited an altered immunization response to T-independent antigen but a normal immunization response to T-dependent antigen, suggesting a particular defect in B-lymphocyte functions.

While the impact of disruption in SHP-1 function on immune cells and autoimmune diseases is well documented, the link between the human PTPN6 mutation discovered in the French-Canadian family and adverse respiratory outcomes remains poorly understood. Using blood samples collected from carrier and noncarrier members of the family as well as tissues harvested from mice with the same mutation in the SHP-1 catalytic site (Ptpn6Ala457Thr), we demonstrated that this mutation leads to alterations in B-lymphocyte populations. Furthermore, we found that Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice have an impaired immunization response to T-independent antigen and spontaneously develop tertiary lymphoid tissues (TLTs) in the lungs. However, the immune response to cigarette smoke exposure was not exacerbated by the mutation, and emphysematous lung damage remained similar to that in wild-type (WT) mice. These findings suggest that the recently identified PTPN6 mutation primarily affects B-lymphocyte biology, but the link to the development of severe and premature emphysema remains unclear.

Materials and methods

Sex as a biological variable

Both female and male mice were included in the study and compared. In addition, data and samples from both females and males were used in the study and compared.

Transgenic mouse model and housing

Male and female mice of the C57BL/6N background carrying the specific mutation in the Ptpn6 gene were generated using CRISPR/Cas 9 technology, where guanine was replaced by an adenine at position 1363 (McGill Integrated Core for Animal Modeling). This change caused an alanine (Ala) to threonine (Thr) switch at position 457 in the amino acid sequence of the SHP-1 protein, corresponding to the amino acid at position 455 in humans. Heterozygote male founders were crossed with WT C57BL/6N females and F1 heterozygote males with WT C57BL/6N females up to F6 to generate Ptpn6Ala457Thr heterozygote pups for experimentations. The mouse colony is maintained by crossing Ptpn6Ala457Thr heterozygote males with WT females. Male and female heterozygote and WT littermates were used for experimentation. Mice homozygous for the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation were not viable (in utero death at embryonic day 15.5) and therefore could not be used for experimentation. Mice were housed following guidelines from the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC) at the Institut universitaire de cardiologie et de pneumologie de Québec—Université Laval (UL), and protocols were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of UL (#2022-984, #2021-964).

Characteristics of participants

All participants were members of the French-Canadian family in which the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation was discovered. Sequencing and clinical assessments were performed as previously described.2 Findings are summarized in Table 1. Blood samples from participants were used to perform flow cytometry on circulating cells (n = 63) and to measure circulating immunoglobulins (n = 70). Immunoglobulin subtypes (total IgG, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgM, and IgA) were measured in the serum by nephelometry.

Mouse model of cigarette smoke exposure

Mice were exposed to the primary smoke of 24 cigarettes (3R4F reference cigarettes; University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY, USA) in a whole-body exposure system (SIU24; Promech Lab Holding AB, Vintrie, Sweden) for 2 hours per day in the morning, 5 days per week, as previously described.15–17 Male and female mice of various ages at exposure onset were exposed for up to 6 months. In specific protocols, mice exposed to cigarette smoke also received a repeated challenge once every 2 weeks of heat-inactivated nontypeable Haemophilus influenza (hiNTHi; ATCC #49766 [L-378]) (2 × 106 CFU in 35 µL of PBS).

Lung function assessment

Mice were anesthetized using a mixture of ketamine, xylazine, and acepromazine to perform mechanical ventilation, while muscles were paralyzed with pancuronium. Lung functions were measured using the FlexiVent (SCIREQ, Montreal, QC, Canada) as previously described.15,18 The following sequence of measures was performed 3 times: deep inflation, snapshot-150, quick prime-3, and pressure/volume loop.

Lung tissue and cell harvesting

Mice were euthanized and the whole lung was removed from the chest cavity. A cannula was inserted and secured into the trachea. The right multilobed lung was sutured and kept for flow cytometry and/or snap-frozen. For histological analysis (no bronchoalveolar lavage), the left lobe was inflated with and kept in 10% formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin.

Ex vivo µCT analysis

In independent experiments, formalin-fixed mouse lungs were dehydrated sequentially in 50% ethanol (2 hours), 70% ethanol (2 hours), 90% ethanol (overnight), and 100% ethanol (2 hours) and transferred in hexamethyldisilazane (2 hours). Finally, lungs were air-dried overnight. Lungs were imaged in a Skyscan1174 µCT scanner (Bruker MicroCT, Kontich, Belgium) at 50 volts and 800 microamperes with a voxel size of 11.38 µm. Lungs were scanned using a 0.5° rotation with a 1,600 ms exposure. Reconstructions were performed using SkyScan NRecon software (Bruker Micro CT).

Immunohistochemistry

Sections of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded mouse left lungs were stained for B220 by immunohistochemistry. Sections were deparaffinized 3 times in toluene and then rehydrated as follows: 100% ethanol, 95% ethanol, 70% ethanol, and distilled water. Citrate buffer antigen retrieval was performed (40 minutes) in a steamer. Staining was performed according to the instruction provided by the DAB anti-rat staining kit from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA), except that serum block was replaced by 1% goat serum in TBS 0.01% Tween. Sections were incubated with rat anti-mouse B220 (clone RA3-6B2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 1:300 dilution in 1% goat serum in TBS 0.01% Tween overnight at 4 °C.

Tertiary lymphoid tissue and alveolar size quantifications

Sections of paraffin-embedded unlavaged mouse lungs were cut at 5 µm thickness to perform hematoxylin and eosin staining. The entire sections were scanned at 20× magnification to quantify the area of the lung occupied by TLTs. Using ImageJ software, lung area (µm2) and TLT area (µm2) were quantified. Data are shown as TLT area per lung area (TLT area/lung area). Alveolar size measurements were performed on whole-lung sections using homemade software. Bronchus, blood vessels, and nonalveolar section were removed manually and areas <50 µm2 were excluded from the analysis to remove intercellular spaces or artefacts. Alveolar size was stratified in predetermined intervals.

Flow cytometry

Mouse lungs and spleen

Single-cell suspensions from mouse right lung lobes and spleen were generated by enzymatic digestion (lung) or mechanical dissociation (spleen) followed by separation using a cell strainer as previously described.17 Cells were stained with anti-CD19-PE (clone 6D5), anti-CD11b-PeCy7 (clone M1/70), anti-IgM-APC (clone RMM-1), anti-IgD-PECY7/BV711 (clone 11-26c.2a), anti-CD90.2-FITC (clone 30-H12), anti-CD4-PERCP (clone RM4-5), and anti-CD8-BV785 (clone 53-6.7) (all from BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA).

Human blood

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood by density gradient using lymphocyte separation media (WISENT, Saint-Bruno, Québec, QC, Canada). PBMCs were incubated for 1 hour on plastic at 37 °C, 5% CO2 to separate mononuclear cells from lymphocytes. Nonadherent cells were collected, washed twice with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS 1X; WISENT), and counted. Cells were stained with anti-CD19-BV711 (clone SJ25C1, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), anti-CD27-BV421 (clone M-T271, BD Biosciences) anti-IgD-Pecy7 (clone IA6-2, BD Biosciences), anti-CD3-V500 (clone UCHT1, BD Biosciences), anti-CD4-PerCP (clone OKT4, BioLegend), and anti-CD8-PE (clone SK1, BioLegend).

For both humans and mice, single-cell suspensions were run on the BD LSRFortessa system (BD Biosciences) and results were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.9.0; Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA).

Immunization and plasma NP-specific immunoglobulin determination

Mice were immunized with either Nitrophenyl (NP)-Ficoll or Nitrophenyl-Chicken Gamma Globulin (NP-CGG) (50 µg/mouse; Biosearch Technologies, Teddington, Middlesex, UK) by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection on day 0. Plasma was collected from lateral tail vein before immunization at day 0 and on days 3, 7, 10, 14, and 21 postinjection. Mice were challenged with 50 µg of NP-Ficoll or NP-CGG i.p. on day 29 and plasma was collected on days 29, 31, 34, and 39. Plasma levels of NP-specific IgM, IgG1, IgG3, and IgA were measured by ELISA. Plates were coated with 100 µL of NP-BSA (5 µg/mL; Biosearch Technologies) in PBS overnight at room temperature. Plates were washed 3 times with PBS-Tween 0.05% and blocked with PBS 2% BSA for 2 hours at room temperature. Plasma samples were diluted in PBS 2% BSA 0.05% Tween as follows: IgM (1:3,000), IgG3 (1:3,000), IgG1 (1:1,000), and IgA (1:200) for NP-Ficoll ELISA. For samples from NP-CGG–immunized mice, plasma was diluted as follows: IgM (1:1,000), IgG3 (1:100), IgG1 (1:1,000), and IgA (1:100). Plates were washed 3 times with PBS-Tween 0.05%, then incubated with HRP-coupled secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature (IgM and IgG3 1/10,000, IgG1 and IgA 1/5,000; Jackson Immunoresearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA). Plates were washed again and 100 µL of TMB substrate reagent BD OptEIATM (BD Biosciences) was added for 15 minutes. The reaction was stopped with 50 µL of H2SO4 2N, and optical densities at 450 and 570 nm were measured by a plate reader (Synergy H1 hybrid reader, BioTeK, Winooski, VT, USA).

Real-time quantitative PCR

RNA was extracted from snap-frozen lung tissue of 12-month-old female and male mice, as previously described.15 Transcriptional levels were determined by qPCR with gene-specific primers to amplify Cxcl9, Cxcl10, Cxcl13, Ccl20, Lta, and Ltb.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism software (version 9.5.1; GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). Two-group comparisons were made using a 2-sided t-test (P < 0.05). Multiple comparisons were performed using a one-way ANOVA followed by preselected Šídák multiple comparison post hoc tests. For immunization protocols, 2-way ANOVA followed by Šídák multiple comparison post hoc tests were used for each time point.

Study approval

Mice were housed following guidelines from the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the CCAC at the Institut universitaire de cardiologie et de pneumologie de Québec—UL, and protocols were approved by the Animal Protection Committee of UL (#2022-984, #2021-964). Human studies were approved by the Ethics Board of the Institut universitaire de cardiologie et de pneumologie de Québec—UL (2020-3286, 21811).

Results

Mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation spontaneously develop TLTs in the lungs

Older Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice presented more pulmonary TLTs in the lungs compared to WT mice of the same age (Fig. 1A, B). Tissue histology and ex vivo computed tomography confirmed the spread distribution of these structures in the lungs, while immunochemistry for B220 confirmed high density of B lymphocytes (Fig. 1B). Expansion of these structures was remarkable in mice aged between 4 and 12 months (Fig. 1C). Both male and female Ptpn6Ala457Thr mice presented this progressive phenotype, but female had more pulmonary TLTs overall compared to males (Fig. 1C). Transcriptional levels of Ccl20, Cxcl9, Cxcl13, Cxcl10, Lta, and Ltb encoding for protein implicated in lymphocyte recruitment and TLT formation/maintenance were also greatly increased in Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice (Fig. S1A–F). Increased proportion of pulmonary B-1 compared to B-2 lymphocytes in Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice was noticeable at 8 months of age in females but was higher in both sexes at 12 months of age (Fig. 1D, E). Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice also showed altered T-lymphocyte populations, with reduced proportions of CD8+ T cells in the lungs (Fig. 1F, G).

Mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation have systemic manifestations of altered B-lymphocyte homeostasis

Both female and male mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation progressively developed larger spleens than WT mice over time (Fig. 2A, B). Similarly to the lungs, we observed increased proportion of B-1 lymphocytes in the spleen of both female and male mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation (Fig. 2C, D). Decreased proportions of CD8+ T lymphocytes were also very consistent in the spleen of Ptpn6Ala457Thr mice starting at 8 months of age for both females and males (Fig. 2C, D).

Ptpn6 Ala457Thr/+ mice also exhibited increased levels of circulating IgM and IgG compared to WT mice, while IgA levels were similar (Fig. 2E, F). Following immunization with NP-CGG (Fig. 3A), a T-dependent antigen, NP-specific IgG1 and IgG3 were either similar or higher in Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice compared to WT mice in both males and females, while NP-specific IgM levels were higher (Fig. 3B, C). However, following immunization with NP-Ficoll (Fig. 3A), a T-independent antigen, both female and male Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice had lower NP-specific IgG1 compared to WT mice, and increased NP-specific IgA production was observed in females only (Fig. 3D, E).

Humans carrying the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation have altered circulating B-lymphocyte populations and immunoglobulin levels

Since the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation alters immunoglobulin levels and B-lymphocyte populations in mice, we assessed whether humans carrying the same mutation also exhibit changes in B-lymphocyte biology. Measurement of total serum immunoglobulins revealed that men with the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation had lower total IgG, IgG1, and IgG2 while IgG3 concentrations were increased in the serum of women carrying the mutation (Fig. 4A). IgG4 levels were lower in both men and women carrying the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation. IgM and IgA levels were not affected (Fig. 4A). When assessing circulating lymphocytes by flow cytometry, the proportion of B lymphocytes was similar between carriers and noncarriers of the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation. However, we found a decrease in the percentages of naive B lymphocytes and an increase in isotype-switched memory B lymphocytes in the circulation of men carrying the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation compared to noncarriers (Fig. 4B). The ratio of naive B lymphocytes to isotype-switched memory B lymphocytes and the ratio of naive B lymphocytes to non-isotype-switched memory B lymphocytes were lower for men carriers of the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation (Fig. 4B). T-lymphocyte populations, plasma cells, and transitional B lymphocytes were similar between groups (Fig. S2A–F).

Cigarette smoke exposure does not worsen the effects of the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation

Female and male mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation did not develop altered lung functions or enlarged alveoli over time (Fig. S3). Since smoking precipitated the development of emphysema in human carriers of the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation,2 we sought to investigate in mice the interaction between cigarette smoke exposure and the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation. Exposure to cigarette smoke for 24 weeks induced TLT formation in WT female mice but not in males (Fig. 5A, B). Interestingly, cigarette smoke exposure limited TLT formation in female Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice, whereas there was no difference in males (Fig. 5A, B). With regard to emphysema assessment, mean linear intercept was increased following a 6-month exposure to cigarette smoke in males only, with no clear worsening caused by the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation (Fig. 5C). While alveolar size measurement confirmed mean linear intercept measurement in females (Fig. 5D), it also showed a shift from smaller to larger alveoli in male mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation exposed to cigarette smoke compared to their WT counterparts (Fig. 5E).

In terms of lung function assessment, differences between WT and Ptpn6Ala457Thr mice exposed to room air conditions were limited (Fig. 5F, G and Fig. S4). Exposure to cigarette smoke affected both male and female WT mice with increased inspiratory capacity and hysteresis (Fig. 5G). Male and female Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice exposed to cigarette smoke followed similar trends as their WT counterparts, except for male Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice showing higher hysteresis (Fig. 5G) and lower Newtonian resistance (Fig. S4).

Immune cell recruitment in response to cigarette smoke exposure is unaffected by the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation in mice

Among room air–exposed animals, male and female Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice had fewer neutrophils and higher dendritic cell proportions in the lungs compared to WT mice (Fig. 6A–C). Similarly, room air–exposed male and female Ptpn6Ala5457Thr/+ mice had higher B-1 lymphocyte and CD4+ T-lymphocyte proportions as well as lower B-2 and CD8+ T-lymphocyte proportions in the lungs (Fig. 6D–I). However, cigarette smoke exposure did not exacerbate the effects of the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation on any or the proportion of immune cells populations in the lungs (Fig. 6).

The Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation in mice does not affect the response to repeated challenges with heat-inactivated NTHi with or without cigarette smoke exposure

We investigated if the outcomes of repeated hiNTHi intranasal challenges in combination, or not, with exposure cigarette smoke could be different in mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation (Fig. 7A). In female Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice, repeated challenges with hiNTHi did not impact lung TLT formation or mean linear intercept under room air or cigarette smoke exposure conditions (Fig. 7B–E). While hiNTHi reduced inspiratory capacity and increased elastance under room air conditions in both WT and Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ female mice, no differences in lung function were observed between the WT and Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ genotypes (Fig. 7F, G). In male Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice, hiNTHi exposure increased lung TLT formation under room air condition but not following cigarette smoke exposure (Fig. 7H, I). However, no differences between WT and Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ male mice were observed with regard to mean linear intercept, alveolar size, or lung function (Fig. 7J–M).

Discussion

In this study, we found that mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation (PTPN6Ala455Thr in humans) have significant immune alterations. The most obvious was that they exhibited spontaneous formation of pulmonary TLTs, organized immunological structures mainly composed of B lymphocytes at the center surrounded by T lymphocytes and infiltrated by dendritic cells and macrophages.19 Although TLT development is known to occur naturally with advanced age,20,21 we found that the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation greatly promotes formation of TLTs over time as the 12-month-old Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ females had 10 times more TLTs than their WT counterparts. It has been shown that CD19-driven specific deletion of SHP-1 in B lymphocytes (Ptpn6f/f;CD19-cre mice) leads to B220+ infiltrates resembling TLT in the lungs and the liver of these mice.14 This supports that reduced SHP-1 activity in B lymphocytes and/or bone marrow progenitors can lead to this progressive increase in pulmonary TLT. In mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation, TLTs developed over time in both female and male mice. However, significantly more TLTs were observed in females. This sex difference has also been reported in WT mice exposed chronically to cigarette smoke as well as in humans with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).22 Differences in B-lymphocyte SHP-1 activity and associated signaling could therefore represent a significant force driving TLT formation as well as the previously described sex difference in mice and humans.

As mentioned previously, cigarette smoke can induce TLT formation in the lungs after months of daily exposures.21,23,24 Therefore, we could expect the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation in mice and chronic cigarette smoke exposure to at least generate a combined additive effect on TLT formation in the lungs. However, we found that exposure to cigarette smoke does not exacerbate the formation of TLTs in the lungs of mice carrying the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation. We even observed reducing effects of cigarette smoke on pulmonary TLT formation in females. A possible explanation could be the potent immunomodulatory effects of cigarette smoke on the adaptive immune system, notably through the generation of xenobiotics and aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling.25,26 While cigarette smoke did not exacerbate TLT formation in Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice, repeated hiNTHi challenges did not interfere with TLT formation, even causing an increase in Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ male mice. We could not identify the mechanism(s) behind the lack of additive effects between cigarette smoke and the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation in terms of lung TLT formation. However, we can conclude that the mechanisms driving TLT formation by cigarette smoke exposure likely differ and possibly interfere with those induced by the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation in mice, adding to the complexity of lung TLT biology.

We showed that the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation disrupts B-1/B-2 lymphocyte balance in the lungs and spleen of Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice, which is progressive and, like TLT formation, sex-dependent in intensity. This phenotype has been reported in other mouse models carrying mutations in Ptpn6.14,27 B-1 lymphocytes normally originate from fetal liver and are mainly located in the peritoneal cavity, with relatively low levels detected in other organs like the lungs or spleen.28 The Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation, by reducing SHP-1 activity, may lead to stronger BCR signaling and favor the B-1 lineage,29,30 an effect already documented in mice lacking SHP-1 in B lymphocytes14 and in other mutations in genes coding for other BCR regulators.31,32 Changes in B-lymphocyte populations were also observed in human carriers of the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation. Carriers of the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation showed a reduction in circulating naive B lymphocytes and increase in memory B lymphocytes, observations previously reported in aging.33–35 These observations were also much more pronounced in men than women. Interestingly, old mice have lower pro–B-lymphocyte numbers in the bone marrow,36,37 and older humans have reduced numbers of B-lymphocyte precursors in the bone marrow,38 suggesting that aging could affect B-lymphocyte populations similarly to what we observed in individuals carrying the PTPN6Ala457Thr mutation. Overall, the PTPN6Ala455Thr and Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutations in humans and mice, respectively, alter B-lymphocyte populations in the lungs and circulation with marked sex differences.

Both mice and humans show changes in circulating immunoglobulin levels. Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice had elevated IgG and IgM in the serum, which could be a reflection of the B-1 lymphocyte expansion occurring with age.30 This phenotype has previously been observed in viable motheaten mice and Ptpn6f/f;CD19-cre mice.11,14 Furthermore, young Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice exhibit lower production of IgG1 following immunization with T-independent antigen, but similar levels of IgM, IgG3, and IgA, an effect that has also been reported in Ptpn6f/f;CD19-cre mice.14 Similarly, the response to immunization is dampened in older humans.39 Conversely, we found that T-dependent antigen responses were not dampened but were in fact slightly enhanced by the Ptpn6Ala457Thr mutation. The same pattern was observed in mice lacking CD22, a protein upstream of SHP-1 in BCR signaling.40 The significant alterations in immunoglobulin levels noted in patients carrying the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation, especially in men, similarly suggest that B-lymphocyte functions are altered in these individuals. Members of the family carrying the mutation had decreased levels of IgG1 and IgG4 as well as increased IgG3, indicating a potential disruption of immunoglobulin class switching. In line with these findings, decreases in IgG have previously been documented in COPD patients41 and patients with immunodeficiency.42 Adding to the observed alterations in B-lymphocyte populations in the lungs and the circulation, the fact that mouse carriers present altered responses to T-independent antigens and that human carriers have altered circulating immunoglobulin isotypes suggests that changes in B-lymphocyte populations driven by the mutation can translate into functional alterations.

Despite aging and cigarette smoke exposure, Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice did not develop emphysematous lesions or airspace enlargement beyond WT levels, thus differing from what was observed in humans. Since the PTPN6Ala455Thr mutation leads humans to develop early and severe emphysema, especially in individuals with a history of tobacco smoking, we postulated that the same would be true in the mouse model. This hypothesis was further supported by a previous study where overexpression of Ptpn6 in a mouse model of aluminum oxide–induced emphysema protected against lung tissue destruction.43 However, even 12-month-old female and male Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice did not develop emphysema, and exposure to cigarette smoke, either acute or chronic, with or without repeated lung bacterial challenge (hiNTHi) did not trigger emphysema development. Therefore, our results do not support a direct link between the presence of TLTs in the lungs and the development of emphysema in mice. We observed no exacerbation of pulmonary emphysema in Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mice despite a marked increase in TLT formation with age, even in the context of cigarette smoke exposure. TLTs have been associated with severe COPD in humans19,44,45 and in mouse models of cigarette smoke exposure.23 Furthermore, it has been shown that mice lacking B lymphocytes (µMT) were somewhat protected from emphysematous lung lesions,46 suggesting that B lymphocytes and TLTs could be involved in emphysema development. Mechanistically, some posit that B lymphocytes indirectly lead to lung tissue damage by skewing macrophage activation and stimulating the expression of mmp-12, previously linked to COPD pathogenesis.46 In addition, when TLT formation is blocked by inhibiting the Lta-Ltb/Ltbr axis in mice exposed to cigarette smoke, there is a significant improvement in emphysema, possibly via activation of the Wnt regeneration axis.47 Conversely, CXCL13 blockade, essential to TLT formation in the lungs, does not completely prevent cigarette smoke emphysema, as the mean linear intercept remained elevated with the treatment while the destruction index was improved.48 LTα−/− mice, in which lymphoid aggregates accumulate in the lungs, do not exhibit increased mean linear intercept compared to WT mice following cigarette smoke exposure.49 Finally, D’hulst and colleagues demonstrated that Scid mice, which lack mature B and T cells, still develop emphysema following cigarette smoke exposure, suggesting that emphysema development does not require adaptive immunity.50

The Ptpn6Ala457Thr/+ mouse represents a new human-relevant dominant negative model to study the biology of SHP-1. While the exact mechanism remains to be confirmed, it is very likely the mutated SHP-1 protein can perform the same protein–protein interactions as the WT protein given its intact SH2 domains, however, without providing the same phosphatase activity due to the point mutation in the catalytic site of the phosphatase domain2 and leading to an overall reduction in cellular SHP-1 phosphatase activity for a given signal.

In conclusion, the mutation PTPN6Ala455Thr linked to early and severe emphysema in humans also induces significant alterations in B-lymphocyte circulating population and immunoglobulin levels and, in mice, leads to spontaneous TLT formation in the lungs as well as specific functional alterations in B lymphocytes. However, we have yet to determine the link, if any, between these alterations and early emphysema onset. We also have to contemplate the possibility that not all of the effects associated with the mutation in humans are being reflected in mice.
