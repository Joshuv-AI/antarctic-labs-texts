Transformer-assisted broad learning for hybrid intelligence-based skin cancer segmentation
Authors: Yisa Cai, Lizheng Cai, Ao Song, Chao Yang, Wanli Yang, Ruihua Yu, Tianjing Wang, Feng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the rise of Transformer architectures, deep learning applications have gradually shifted from traditional convolutional neural networks to Transformers based on self-attention mechanisms. In tasks such as image classification, segmentation, and detection, pure Transformer models (such as ViT, Swin Transformer, PVT) and hybrid architectures (such as ConViT, BoTNet) have become mainstream. At the same time, Broad Learning Systems (BLS) exhibit unique advantages in real-time scenarios with limited resources and small sample sizes, thanks to efficient training, strong dynamic scalability, and global optimization mechanisms. However, BLS has shortcomings in feature design, computational complexity, representation capability, and incremental learning stability. Transformer architectures effectively address these deficiencies through self-attention mechanisms, hierarchical structures, and parameter sharing. This paper proposes a hybrid network structure, VIWDNet, that combines the advantages of Transformers and BLS. The network retains the efficient training characteristics of BLS and incorporates the powerful feature abstraction capabilities of Transformers, making it suitable for skin lesion segmentation tasks in medical image analysis—a key step in early skin cancer screening and diagnosis. Experiments on four public medical image datasets (ISIC 2016, ISIC 2017, ISIC 2018, and PH2) show that VIWDNet improves Dice and mIoU metrics by 1.48–2.23% over the most advanced existing models, providing reliable technical support for computer-aided dermatological diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19465-x.

Introduction

Skin cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide, primarily including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and malignant melanoma1. Malignant melanoma is considered the most dangerous type due to its high invasiveness and propensity for metastasis2,3. In recent years, the incidence of skin cancer has been steadily increasing, posing a significant challenge to public health4,5. Research has shown that the occurrence of skin cancer is closely related to various factors, including genetic susceptibility, skin type, and immune function. Among these, ultraviolet (UV) radiation is the primary external cause6. As greenhouse gas emissions have led to the depletion of the ozone layer, more harmful UV rays can penetrate the atmosphere and reach the Earth’s surface, significantly increasing the risk of skin cancer7. Although skin cancer is usually curable with surgery in its early stages, advanced lesions generally have a poor prognosis, are costly to treat, and severely affect patients’ quality of life8. Therefore, enhancing the early screening and diagnosis rates of skin cancer has become a focus of current dermatological research and public health efforts.

In the early diagnosis of skin cancer, accurately segmenting the lesion area is one of the key steps9. Image segmentation techniques aim to precisely extract the lesion areas from clinical or dermoscopic images, providing a foundation for feature extraction, classification diagnosis, and treatment decisions10. Precise segmentation helps doctors observe the boundaries, shapes, and color changes of lesions more clearly, and improves the accuracy and consistency of disease identification11,12. Traditional manual segmentation methods rely on the experience and judgment of professional dermatologists, which is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and easily influenced by subjective factors, making it difficult to meet the demands for efficient and standardized diagnosis and treatment13,14. Therefore, automatic segmentation methods based on deep learning have gained much attention in recent years. These methods, by learning lesion features from large datasets, achieve high accuracy and robustness in automatic segmentation, which is significant for building efficient computer-aided diagnostic systems. Their application not only reduces the burden on doctors but also enhances early screening coverage in resource-limited areas, thus improving the overall diagnostic level of skin cancer.

In recent years, with the development of deep learning, an increasing number of studies have focused on applying convolutional neural networks (CNN), fully convolutional networks (FCN), U-Net and its variants to the task of automatic segmentation of skin cancer images15. Due to the excellent performance of U-Net and its improved versions (such as U-Net++ and Attention U-Net) in medical imaging, they are widely used16,17. Additionally, transformer-based models have gradually been introduced into the medical image segmentation field, demonstrating strong global modeling capabilities, which effectively enhance segmentation accuracy18,19. Despite these methods achieving significant results on public datasets (such as ISIC 2018 and PH2), skin cancer image segmentation still faces numerous challenges20,21. Firstly, the lesion area exhibits high variability in color, shape, and boundary, with some lesions having low contrast with surrounding skin tissue, making it difficult for models to accurately locate the edges22. Secondly, real-world images are often accompanied by interference such as hair, noise, and lighting changes, further affecting segmentation performance23. Moreover, data imbalance and annotation difficulties also limit the generalization ability of models24. Therefore, constructing an efficient segmentation model that can capture local details and possesses global contextual awareness remains a focus and challenge in current research.

We propose a novel dual-stream skin lesion segmentation framework, which combines the advantages of Vision Transformer (ViT) and Wide Residual Networks (WideNet), achieving complementary fusion of multi-scale features through a cleverly designed attention mechanism. In the encoding stage, the ViT branch captures long-range dependencies and semantic contextual information using a global self-attention mechanism. Its multi-layer Transformer structure consists of 12 encoder layers and 8 attention heads, outputting 256-dimensional patch embedding features. Meanwhile, the improved WideNet branch employs a lightweight multi-scale module constructed in four stages of residual block structures, with each stage containing two convolutional branches with different dilation rates, and aligns features with ViT through 1×1 convolution for channel adjustment. In the feature fusion stage, we designed a DualFusionAttention module, which includes channel attention (SEBlock) and spatial attention submodules. The channel attention uses global average pooling and two fully connected layers to generate channel weights, while the spatial attention generates spatial masks through a squeeze-and-excitation structure, and both work together to achieve adaptive weighted feature fusion. The decoder adopts an SE-enhanced U-shaped structure, comprising three upsampling stages, where each stage progressively restores spatial resolution through 3×3 convolutions and bilinear interpolation, and finally calibrates output features using SEBlock. With this model design, we have improved segmentation accuracy on multiple skin cancer databases and provided a new perspective for future models. Our main contributions are as follows:

Relevant work

Based on deep learning method

In recent years, significant progress has been made in skin cancer segmentation based on deep learning, primarily due to the integration and optimization of CNN and Transformer architectures25,26. Traditional CNN approaches (such as U-Net and its variants) effectively extract local features through an encoder-decoder structure but are limited when dealing with diverse lesion shapes and fuzzy boundaries15. To address this, researchers have introduced attention mechanisms (such as Attention U-Net) and Transformer modules (such as Swin Transformer) to enhance global context modeling capabilities17,27. For example, FAT-Net utilizes feature adaptive Transformers and multi-scale fusion to achieve high-precision segmentation on the ISIC dataset, while GFANet employs gated fusion and channel-spatial attention to improve boundary detail processing28,29. Additionally, lightweight design has gradually become a trend, with MobileUNETR and MambaU-Lite employing hybrid architectures and parameter optimizations to reduce computational costs while maintaining performance30,31. These methods not only improve segmentation accuracy (e.g., Dice coefficient exceeding 90%), but also enhance the model’s practicality in clinical settings, providing reliable support for the early diagnosis of skin cancer.

Based on broad learning method

Research on Broad Learning Systems (BLS) in the field of medical imaging primarily focuses on disease diagnosis and image classification, especially the early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). Han et al. proposed a novel Convolutional Broad Learning System (CVBLS) which effectively classifies AD, mild cognitive impairment, and normal control groups using MRI images32. Experiments on the ADNI dataset demonstrate that this model outperforms existing methods in terms of accuracy and training efficiency. Additionally, they developed a BLS variant with multi-scale 3D convolutional features, enhancing the model’s ability to analyze complex structures in MRI images, achieving accuracy rates of 91.83% and 75.52% for AD and MCI diagnosis tasks, respectively33. These studies highlight the advantages of BLS in medical imaging, such as lightweight architecture, fast training speed, and good cross-dataset generalization capabilities. Jin et al. further introduced a BLS model with grouped label enhancement, which significantly improved image classification performance by optimizing intra-class similarity and inter-class differences34. A review by Gong et al. systematically summarized the applications of BLS in computer vision and biomedical engineering, emphasizing its potential in feature extraction and end-to-end learning35. These studies indicate that BLS, by integrating the multi-scale feature learning of convolutional neural networks with the efficient incremental learning traits of BLS, provides a new method combining accuracy and efficiency for medical image analysis, and future exploration of its applications in multimodal image fusion and real-time diagnosis is promising.

WideBackbone module

The module proposes an improved width residual network architecture for local feature extraction, with its core innovation being the introduction of lightweight multi-scale modules and a width expansion strategy. The network input first goes through a 7 × 7 convolutional layer (stride = 2, padding = 3) for preliminary feature extraction, increasing the number of channels to 64. Then, it is processed through a BatchNorm layer and ReLU activation function, followed by spatial downsampling using a 3 × 3 max pooling layer (stride = 2). The main part of the network consists of four hierarchical structures, each made up of several lightweight multi-scale modules, with the number of channels in each layer expanding to 128, 256, and 512, and the spatial resolution gradually reduced through convolution with stride = 2.

The lightweight multi-scale module is designed with a parallel branch structure, including four feature processing paths: (1) 1 × 1 standard convolution, (2) 3 × 3 regular convolution (padding = 1), (3) 3 × 3 dilated convolution with a dilation rate of 2 (padding = 2), and (4) 3 × 3 dilated convolution with a dilation rate of 3 (padding = 3). The output channel number for each branch is one-fourth of the target channel number. The discrete transformation is selected for its capacity to process grid-based image features and support efficient numerical computation, which is critical for end-to-end training. Leveraging the DWTForward class from the pytorch_wavelets library, this module achieves efficient feature dimensionality reduction via frequency domain analysis and a channel attention mechanism. DualFusionAttention

We integrated the DualFusionAttention mechanism to achieve adaptive fusion of multi-scale features from different modules. This module enhances feature representation capabilities through the synergistic action of channel and spatial attention. The core architecture includes feature addition operations and parallel attention branch processing. The input feature pair (x₁, x₂) is first combined using element-wise addition to obtain the initial fused feature (x = x₁ + x₂), which is then processed by the channel attention branch and the spatial attention branch separately. The channel attention branch uses the SEBlock structure to capture inter-channel dependencies through global average pooling. Experimental results

Dataset

This experiment employs four publicly available skin lesion datasets for model validation: the International Skin Imaging Collaboration 2016–2018 Challenge Datasets (ISIC2016, ISIC2017, ISIC2018) and the PH2 dataset20,21,36,37. The ISIC2016 dataset includes 900 dermoscopic images along with corresponding lesion segmentation annotations. ISIC2017 and ISIC2018 contain 2,150 and 2,694 annotated images, respectively, while the PH2 dataset provides 200 high-resolution dermoscopic images. To maintain experimental consistency, all datasets are divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 6:2:2 ratio. Specifically, ISIC2016 comprises 540 training images, 180 validation images, and 180 test images; ISIC2017 includes 1,290 training images, 430 validation images, and 430 test images; ISIC2018 consists of 1,616 training images, 539 validation images, and 539 test images; and the PH2 dataset is divided into 120 training images, 40 validation images, and 40 test images. This partitioning strategy ensures sufficient training data while enabling hyperparameter tuning through the independent validation set, and ultimately allows objective evaluation of model performance on unseen test sets. During preprocessing, all images are uniformly resized to a resolution of 224 × 224 pixels and standardized (mean = 0.5, standard deviation = 0.5).

Implementation details

The experiment uses a fixed input size of 224 × 224 pixels, with all input images scaled to this resolution using bilinear interpolation. During training, the batch size is set to 8, and it runs in an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU environment. Training starts from epoch 0, with a maximum of 1000 iterations, using the AdamW optimizer (weight decay of 1e-4) and an initial learning rate of 1e-4. The loss function selected is BCEWithLogitsLoss, which combines Sigmoid activation with binary cross-entropy loss. The learning rate scheduler adopts the ReduceLROnPlateau strategy, reducing the learning rate to 0.1 of its original value when the validation loss does not decrease for 5 consecutive epochs. An early stopping mechanism is set up to terminate the training if no improvement in validation loss is observed within 100 epochs. The best model weights are saved after each validation, with all experiments implemented in the PyTorch 2.2.1 + cu118 framework, using CUDA 12.1. Data augmentation strategies include random horizontal flipping (probability 0.5), ± 15-degree rotation, and brightness/contrast jitter (magnitude 0.1). Data augmentation is disabled during validation, with only normalization (mean of 0.5, standard deviation of 0.5) performed.

Performance comparison of models on the ISIC2016 dataset

We evaluated the impact of model parameter count and GFLOPS changes on mIoU using the ISIC2016 dataset. Fig. 2 shows that the relationship between computational complexity (GFLOPS), parameter count, and segmentation performance (mIoU) is nonlinear. For example, when GFLOPS increase from 7.25 to 152.92, the mIoU of the compared models only improves by 4.09% (from 75.43 to 79.52%), indicating that merely increasing computational resources has limited effects on performance enhancement. Conversely, when the parameter count fluctuates between 31.04 M and 57.16 M, the mIoU variation is even smaller (from 75.43 to 77.29%), suggesting that model efficiency relies more on structural design rather than parameter scale. Notably, our proposed method achieves an mIoU of 85.05% using 38.93 M parameters and 50.77 GFLOPS, demonstrating excellent performance. This indicates that a well-designed network architecture can optimally balance computational resources and model performance.

Grad cam analysis

According to the GradCAM visualization results, the Wide branch, through parallel multi-scale convolution and residual aggregation, is capable of accurately focusing on lesions in the spatial domain while suppressing high-frequency noise such as hair(Fig. 7). The Wavelet downsampling module uses DHWT to separate low-frequency (lesion morphology) from high-frequency (hair, texture noise) components, enhancing low-frequency signals and suppressing high-frequency interference through a learnable channel attention mechanism. Finally, DualFusionAttention fuses these two noise-resistant paths with the global context of ViT, ensuring both global coherence and a high focus on lesions. This result confirms that each module in the VIWDNet model achieves a synergistic optimization effect, enabling the model to stably and accurately locate lesion areas even in complex backgrounds and demonstrating exceptional anti-interference capability. The effectiveness of each module in improving segmentation performance is further quantitatively verified by the ablation experiments in Sect. 4.9: Table 6 shows that VIWDNet (integrating all modules) achieves the highest mIoU (81.09%) and Dice (88.27%) on the ISIC2018 dataset, outperforming sub-models lacking partial modules (e.g., VITNet with mIoU 78.95%, WideNet with mIoU 77.67%), and Fig. 8 intuitively reflects this performance advantage, fully validating the rationality of our model architecture design.

Ablation experiment

To further verify the contribution of each proposed module to the overall prediction accuracy of the model, we conducted a series of ablation analyses on VIWDNet using the ISIC 2018 dataset. All the ablated models follow a consistent training strategy and hyperparameters to ensure the consistency of the evaluation, and their performance is compared according to key parameters. As shown in Table 6, we split VIWDNet into four sub-models, namely VITNet, WideNet, VWNet, WideDNet, and VIWDNet. As shown in Table 6, we constructed four sub-models by sequentially excluding or retaining core components of VIWDNet, with clear definitions as follows:

VITNet: Only the ViTBackBone module (Sect. 3.2, 12-layer Transformer encoder + 16 × 16 patch embedding) and the basic U-shaped decoder are retained; the WideBackBone, dual wavelet transform, and DualFusionAttention modules are excluded. This sub-model is used to evaluate the independent performance of ViT-based global context extraction.

WideNet: Only the WideBackBone module (Sect. 3.3, an improved wide residual network with 4 parallel multi-scale convolution branches) and the basic U-shaped decoder are retained; it does not include the ViTBackBone, dual wavelet transform, and DualFusionAttention modules. This sub-model is used to evaluate the independent performance of local multi-scale feature extraction based on WideBackBone.

VWNet: VWNet integrates the ViTBackBone, WideBackBone, and DualFusionAttention modules while retaining the basic U-shaped decoder; it does not include dual wavelet transform. This sub-model is designed to evaluate the synergistic interaction between global context (from ViT) and local multi-scale features (from WideBackBone) without frequency-domain refinement.

WideDNet: WideDNet integrates the WideBackBone and Haar wavelet transform module (ordinary wavelet transform module) while retaining the basic U-shaped decoder. This sub-model is used to evaluate the performance difference between wavelet transform and dual wavelet transform in feature extraction.

The ablation results indicate that each module in VIWDNet has a significant impact on the segmentation of skin lesion areas. The combination of all modules yields the highest overall segmentation performance, thus confirming the effectiveness of the proposed modules in terms of accuracy. Fig. 8 more intuitively demonstrates these results through a bar chart, clearly indicating that the proposed method achieves excellent performance. Based on the above results, we believe that the performance advantage of VIWDNet stems from its two-way combination of deep features and width features of pathological images, as well as the multi-level frequency-domain refinement of the dual wavelet transform mechanism. This is an ability that has not been fully achieved by existing methods.

Discussion

Since the emergence of Transformers, deep learning applications have gradually shifted from convolutional neural networks to Transformer architectures based on attention mechanisms42. In tasks such as image classification, segmentation, and detection, many excellent models have emerged, including pure Transformer architectures like VIT, Swin Transformer, and PVT, as well as hybrid architectures (CNN + Transformer) such as ConViT and BoTNet43–46. Currently, mainstream models mainly use these two methods to handle image tasks. Broad Learning provides a new approach to this.

BLS exhibit unique advantages in training efficiency, dynamic scalability, and global optimization through random feature mapping and incremental learning mechanisms47,48. They have adaptability to small sample sizes and model interpretability, making them particularly suitable for resource-constrained real-time scenarios49. However, their inherent limitations include reliance on manual design for feature extraction, increased computational complexity due to width expansion, insufficient representation capacity for complex tasks, and stability issues in incremental learning50. These shortcomings are especially evident when handling complex tasks. The introduction of Transformer architectures aptly compensates for these inadequacies: the self-attention mechanism replaces manual design through dynamic feature weight allocation, hierarchical structures (e.g., Swin Transformer) optimize computational complexity, deep representation learning significantly improves the performance of nonlinear tasks, and parameter sharing and pretraining mechanisms enhance stability51,52. The resulting BLS-Transformer hybrid model retains the efficient training characteristics of Broad Learning while incorporating the strong feature abstraction capabilities of Transformers, showcasing unique value in scenarios requiring rapid response and high precision.

By combining Transformer and Broad Learning architectures, we designed the VIWDNet network, which does not replicate BLS’s incremental learning but integrates its core advantages (efficiency, global optimization, small-sample adaptability) into a segmentation-specific architecture. This integration is achieved through three key design choices: First, in terms of efficient feature mapping (a core advantage of BLS), the WideBackBone’s “parallel multi-branch convolution” (4 branches with different dilation rates: 1 × 1, 3 × 3, dilated 3 × 3 with rates 2 and 3) draws on BLS’s idea of “random feature mapping without deep stacking”. Unlike BLS’s fully random mapping, our branches use structured convolutions to generate discriminative features—this retains BLS’s efficiency (reducing deep layer redundancy) while improving feature quality. For example, the 4-branch design reduces parameter redundancy by 30% compared to a single deep convolution branch (38.93 M parameters vs. 55.2 M in VMUnet), aligning with BLS’s lightweight nature. Second, regarding global optimization (a key trait of BLS), which emphasizes global feature calibration to avoid local optimal solutions, VIWDNet reflects this in two aspects: (a) The SEBlock (in WideBackBone) and ECA-Net (in Double Wavelet Transform) perform channel-wise global attention, similar to BLS’s global weight adjustment; (b) The DualFusionAttention module fuses ViT’s global context and Wide’s local features, achieving global feature optimization—consistent with BLS’s “global mapping instead of local stacking” principle. Third, for adaptability to small samples (another advantage of BLS), although VIWDNet does not directly adopt BLS’s incremental learning mechanism (since skin lesion segmentation requires end-to-end training), it retains BLS’s small-sample adaptability. For example, on the PH2 dataset (200 images, small sample), VIWDNet achieves a Dice of 95.30%—1.48% higher than VMUnet. This is because the Wavelet module’s frequency-domain feature extraction reduces reliance on large-scale data (compensating for small samples), and the efficient parameter design (38.93 M) avoids overfitting—both inherited from BLS’s small-sample optimization logic. Notably, VIWDNet addresses BLS’s inherent limitation of poor complex feature representation via Transformer’s self-attention, forming a “BLS-Transformer synergy” that balances efficiency and performance. Experiments on four public datasets (ISIC 2016, ISIC 2017, ISIC 2018, and PH2) and comparative analysis with state-of-the-art methods demonstrated the effectiveness of this hybrid architecture: VIWDNet improved Dice and IoU metrics by 1.48–2.23% over current advanced models, while maintaining efficient training and inference (38.93 M parameters, 50.77 GFLOPS), suitable for clinical application scenarios.

We hope our research can serve as a solid foundation in the field of medical diagnostics because the potential for innovation in neural networks, including deep learning and broad learning, is limitless. We anticipate that our work will pave new paths for innovative architectures in both computer science and medicine.
