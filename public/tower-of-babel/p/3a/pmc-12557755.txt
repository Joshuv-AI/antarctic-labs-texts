Profile of Jean-Philippe Avouac
Authors: Tinsley H. Davis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Jean-Philippe Avouac, elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2025, explores deformations in Earth’s surface in an effort to understand the formation and behavior of earthquakes. Using geology, satellite data, radar imagery, and modeling, Avouac has characterized the strain along the Himalayan Arc and at other faults. In his Inaugural Article, Avouac characterizes the magnitude 7.7 earthquake along the Sagaing fault in Myanmar in 2025. The enormous 510 km rupture traversed a section of the fault assumed to be under strain since it had not erupted since 1839. However, the rupture also included sections of the fault that were not part of this seismic gap.

The shifting plates of Earth’s crust rip, buckle, and perturb the surface. The surface deformations—and the insights they could lend into the earthquake cycle—have long captured the interest of geologist Jean-Philippe Avouac. Elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2025, Avouac has spent his career measuring and modeling the physical forces that shape the tension tied to crustal deformations. In his Inaugural Article (1), Avouac, a professor of mechanical and civil engineering at the California Institute of Technology, analyzed the 2025 earthquake along Myanmar’s Sagaing Fault. The magnitude 7.7 earthquake ruptured along a 510 km stretch of the fault’s surface, which included sections known to be under built-up strain as well as those that had ruptured more recently. “While this complexity is a challenge for earthquake forecasting, it can be reproduced in a dynamical model of the earthquake cycle,” says Avouac.

Getting Started

Avouac grew up in Paris, France. He enjoyed math, chemistry, and physics but was not particularly studious. “In my last year, I got meningitis, and I was out of school for quite a long time. After that, I [decided] to focus. I worked hard to make sure that I would graduate.” Upon graduation, Avouac’s path seemed preordained. “In the French system, if you are a good student, you don’t make choices, the system takes you to the next step,” he says. “It worked well for me.”

However, Avouac had choices in the internships he pursued while at Ecole Polytechnique, and a serendipitous mistake shaped his future in research. “I got off the lift on the wrong floor. There were pictures of Tibet everywhere on the wall.” Avouac, who had hiked in Tibet the previous year with a friend asked during the internship interview, “’What are these people doing on the first floor?’” The interviewer made an introduction, and Avouac ended up interning in geologist Paul Tapponnier’s lab. “I was sent to the basement to do some experiments in the sandbox to create a fault and see how the faults would actually interact with a fluvial system at the surface,” he recalls. “I think I would’ve preferred to go to Tibet, which I did eventually during my PhD, but for the internship, actually, it was a fun way to get started.”

In 1991, Avouac earned a doctoral degree in geology from Institut de Physique du Globe in Paris, where he worked on the deformation of Central Asia from the collision of India and Eurasia. “India is moving north at about four centimeters per year,” he explains. “This has created the Himalaya and Tibet, and it’s the reason for all those faults in Central Asia.” Drawn back to Tibet for his field studies, Avouac characterized the myriad faults to determine how fast they were slipping, thus creating a kinematic model (2). “There was no GPS at the time. You had to do it with geology,” he recalls. Avouac used satellite images to look for natural features that were displaced by a fault. “If you have a clear moraine that is offset by a fault, you measure the offset,” he describes. “Then, you date the moraine, and you get the slip rate on the fault.”

The funding for Avouac’s doctoral program had come from the French government with the stipulation that he work for the government after graduation. Avouac was subsequently employed by the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission to assess seismic hazards at nuclear facilities in France and also maintained an academic courtesy appointment at École Normale Supérieure in Paris. Avouac and his students began using optical and radar satellite images and newly developed GPS technology to track ground deformation. Unlike seismometers, GPS could measure slow movements of the surface that could be tracked over time. Avouac used the techniques to measure the rate at which faults were being loaded between earthquakes and document deformation during large earthquakes. “We were able to measure how fast the shortening is occurring across the Himalaya and derive implications for seismic hazards,” he says. “It’s about two centimeters per year.” (3)

Seismic Gaps

In the 1990s, Avouac began to use satellite images to measure displacement captured before and after earthquakes (4). While interferometry from radar images was the technique of choice at the time, Avouac’s group worked on using only the amplitude information. He says the approach resulted in a less accurate but more robust method that was also applicable to optical images. “At the beginning, we needed to have about one meter of slip to measure it,” he explains. “Nowadays, we can measure to a few centimeters, maybe 10 centimeters.” His team applied the technique to the 1992 Landers earthquake in southern California (5, 6). “That was the first earthquake to be imaged with satellite imagery,” he says. “We’ve applied it to nearly all the large continental earthquakes which have happened since. It’s been a great source of information.”

Avouac’s Inaugural Article uses optical and radar techniques to examine the 2025 Mandalay earthquake in Myanmar (1). Correlation of optical and radar images to characterize the rupture created by the earthquake provided better resolution of the larger displacements found closer to faults, compared with interferometry. The Mandalay rupture extended 510 km. “That’s the longest rupture ever observed on a continent,” says Avouac. It also exceeded the length of the seismic gap, or the portion of the fault expected to be under strain, given the time since its last rupture in 1839, and included portions of the fault that had ruptured recently, between 1930 and 1956.

“We see a complexity here that we need to understand for seismic hazard assessment, because you cannot simply assume that future earthquakes are going to be the same as the past earthquakes that we observed on that fault or that they are going to rupture gaps left by previous earthquakes,” he says.

“It’s not a metronome,” he explains. “There’s a complexity that is self-maintained because each time you have a rupture it redistributes stress on other segments of the fault.” Much of Avouac’s recent work has focused on modeling the complexity (7).

Earthquake Weather

In 2003, Avouac joined the faculty at Caltech, where, a year later, he became director of a tectonics observatory. He retained focus on the Himalayas, continuing to work on the measurement of crust deformation and adding 27 new GPS stations in Nepal between 2003 and 2004. “The first big thing that we learned from those measurements is that the fault along the Himalayan Arc was fully locked from the surface to a depth of about 20 kilometers,” he explains (8, 9). He estimates that an earthquake of magnitude larger than 8.5 would be needed every 1,000 years on average to relieve the strain. The high seismic hazard produced by this locking assumes that the rate of accumulation of elastic strain remains constant over 1,000 years. “I hope we are wrong,” he adds.

Avouac’s team observed early on that seismicity in the Himalayas changes over time. “It’s faster in the winter than in the summer.” He initially thought that the variation was due to seasonal variations of the noise. However, together with student Laurent Bollinger and colleague Frédéric Perrier, he correlated the increased earthquakes with the monsoon season. Water from the summer rains accumulates in the vast Indo-Gangetic plain. “It’s like a trampoline,” Avouac explains.” The increase in water slows down the convergence, releasing the tension along the fault. “In the winter, the groundwater level drops, and there’s a rebound that puts back the Himalaya in compression and enhances seismicity” (10, 11).

Not all earthquakes originate from natural processes. In 2018, Avouac and colleagues created the Center for Geomechanics and Mitigation of Geohazards at Caltech to understand the impact of injecting or extracting fluids from Earth’s subsurface. “We are developing physics-based models where we use information about the wells: Where they are, how fast we’re injecting fluids or extracting fluids,” Avouac explains. “Once we understand how faults are responding to stress variation due to anthropogenic activities, then we can use the same models to assess how faults are responding to natural stress variations.“

The now-unused Groningen gas field in the Netherlands has provided a model that combines the study of such induced seismicity with seasonality. “A few decades after production began at the site, earthquakes began to be observed,” Avouac explains. “What is interesting here is that, first, there is this delay. It means that the faults initially were not critical. It took time to bring them to the point of failure.”

Gas production typically increased each winter to meet the demand for heating fuel. Measuring the pressure of the gas reservoir allowed Avouac to calculate the stress changes due to the seasonal variation in gas production. “Before they started doing anything at Groningen, the process would’ve been very, very slow for an earthquake to nucleate. We’re talking many centuries, maybe a thousand years, “says Avouac.” But with the production, suddenly the loading is much faster. It’s more on the order of years. These data have been great to actually test our model of nucleations, which is a very key element in our physical model of the seismic cycle” (12).

Predicting Earthquakes

Avouac says predicting the exact location, time, and magnitude of earthquakes is currently far-fetched. “The question is whether we know with enough accuracy, the state of the system and all the sources of stress so that we can make a prediction. It’s just like weather forecasting,” he explains. “It’s a chaotic system, so you cannot make prediction[s] over a very long period of time because you don’t know with enough accuracy the state of the system today. And the small differences, with time, are going to result in quite different outcomes.”

Avouac’s team is addressing the difficult problem of calculating what it would take for an entire fault to rupture as a path toward predictive simulations. “It looks very promising, at least for numerical simulations,” he says. “Next is to demonstrate that we could do that to systems that would be a little more complicated—still synthetic—and then move to natural examples. We’ll see how it goes, and I think it will be time for me to retire when we get to that point.”
