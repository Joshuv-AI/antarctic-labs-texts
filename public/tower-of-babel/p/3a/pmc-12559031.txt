Effect of multi-artery renal grafts on patient outcomes in living donor kidney transplantation
Authors: Amil Huseynov, Halil Şengul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple renal arteries (MRA) in a donor kidney can increase surgical complexity in living donor kidney transplantation. However, the impact of MRA on early transplant outcomes, including delayed graft function (DGF) and early graft failure, remains unclear.MethodsWe performed a single-center retrospective cohort study of 1,200 living donor kidney transplants conducted between 2019 and 2024. Donor kidneys were classified as having a single renal artery or multiple renal arteries (two or more). Recipient and donor characteristics, intraoperative details (ischemia times, vascular anastomosis technique), and postoperative outcomes (delayed graft function [DGF], primary nonfunction, and graft survival) were compared between the two groups. Logistic regression analyses were used to examine associations between MRA and the risk of DGF or graft failure within the first 90 days, both before and after adjusting for prespecified covariates.ResultsOf the 1,200 transplanted kidneys, 882 (73.5%) had a single artery and 318 (26.5%) had multiple arteries. Although the median cold-ischemia time was longer for multi-artery grafts (35.2 min vs. 29.4 min, p = 0.004), there were no significant differences in donor or recipient baseline characteristics. Multivariable logistic regression revealed no significant association between MRA and either DGF or early graft loss. Kaplan–Meier curves, censored for death, demonstrated similar graft survival in both groups (log-rank p = 0.25).ConclusionsIn this single-center cohort of living donor kidney transplants, grafts with multiple renal arteries did not exhibit an elevated risk of DGF or early graft loss. While additional surgical complexity is often required for MRA grafts, our findings suggest that these kidneys remain a safe and effective option for transplantation, supporting their broader use to expand the donor pool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03880-9.Keywords: Multiple renal arteries, Living donor kidney transplantation, Delayed graft function, Early graft failure, Vascular anastomosis, Graft survival

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the definitive therapy for end-stage kidney disease, providing the greatest gains in survival and quality of life [1–3]. To mitigate the persistent organ shortage, we and other centers have increasingly relied on living donor kidney transplantation (LDKT), which shortens waiting times and delivers superior outcomes [4]. Turkey ranks among the world’s leading nations for LDKT, with roughly 3,000 living-donor transplants and 500 deceased-donor procedures performed each year; our center contributes approximately 200 and 30 of these, respectively [5].

Despite the well-documented advantages of LDKT, certain anatomical variants complicate surgery. Chief among these is the presence of multiple renal arteries (MRA), which can make back-table preparation more demanding, necessitate vascular reconstruction, and prolong both cold and warm ischemia times [6]. Extended ischemic intervals and extra anastomoses have been linked to early complications such as graft thrombosis [7–12]. Only a few studies have stratified risk by specific MRA configurations—particularly grafts with three or more arteries—or systematically compared the anastomotic techniques used to address them [13–17].

In this single-center study, conducted by our transplant team, we evaluate whether MRA in living-donor kidneys is associated with delayed graft function (DGF) and graft loss within 90 days.

Materials and methods

We performed a single-center retrospective cohort study to determine whether MRA increase the risk of DGF or 90-day graft failure after living-donor kidney transplantation. All adult recipients (≥ 18 years) who underwent living-donor transplantation at our center between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2024 were screened. Transplants were included if complete donor vascular-anatomy data were available, intra-operative arterial counts were recorded, peri-operative outcomes were documented and at least 90 days of follow-up had elapsed. Recipients of deceased-donor grafts, kidneys lacking definitive arterial anatomy or cases missing key data on ischemia times, DGF status or 90-day graft survival were excluded (n = 47; 14 had no definitive arterial anatomy documented and 33 had missing data on ischemia times, DGF status, or 90-day graft survival), leaving 1,200 transplants for analysis.

Donor computed-tomography angiography or magnetic-resonance angiography was reviewed to determine arterial number. Where operative notes were incomplete, grafts were retained in the MRA cohort only if pre-operative imaging or intra-operative Doppler unequivocally showed two or more arterial trunks; these grafts were omitted from technique-specific analyses to avoid misclassification. Anastomotic strategies were classified as single artery, unified arterial patch, spatulated multiple arteries, or other MRA techniques (sequential anastomoses, mixed approaches or undocumented details). Recipients were deemed pre-emptive when they had not required dialysis before transplantation. DGF was defined as dialysis within the first postoperative week, and 90-day graft failure as graft loss (or patient death with a functioning graft) within 90 days. Death-censored graft survival was measured from transplantation to resumption of chronic dialysis or re-transplantation, censoring deaths with a functioning graft.

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS Statistics v25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD if normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test, Q–Q plots and histograms) and as median (inter-quartile range) if non-normal; categorical variables are given as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used the independent-samples t-test for normal data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normal data and the χ² or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data with sparse cells. Multivariable logistic regression estimated the association between artery group and (i) DGF and (ii) 90-day graft failure, adjusting for prespecified donor, recipient and transplant covariates; results are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Kaplan–Meier curves depicted death-censored graft survival and were compared with the log-rank test. Proportional-hazards assumptions were violated, and although Schoenfeld residuals were examined, limited event counts precluded alternative time-to-event models. A prespecified sensitivity analysis compared grafts with three or more arteries against single-artery grafts, using the same approach but without multivariable adjustment because of sparse events. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis, with excluded cases omitted from all models.

The study was approved by the İstanbul Sabahattin Zaim University Institutional Review Board (Approval No. ET-FR-675, 29 January 2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all living donors and recipients, and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Istanbul.

Results

A total of 1,200 living-donor kidney transplants met the inclusion criteria, and all recipients were undergoing their first graft. Of these, 882 grafts (73.5%) had a single renal artery, whereas 318 (26.5%) had multiple renal arteries (MRA): 277 grafts with two arteries, 38 with three, and three with four. Kidney side (left vs. right) was distributed similarly in the single- and multi-artery groups, and donor characteristics did not differ significantly (Table 1). Recipient demographic and clinical variables were likewise comparable.

Urological complications—ureteric leak, ureteric obstruction/stricture, or lymphocele requiring intervention—occurred in 49/882 single-artery grafts (5.6%) and 19/318 MRA grafts (6.0%); none led directly to graft loss. Median cold-ischemia time (CIT) was longer in the MRA cohort (35.2 min, IQR 23–37) than in the single-artery cohort (29.4 min, IQR 19.8–37.3; P = 0.004). Despite this difference, DGF incidence was not statistically different (9/882 [1.0%] vs. 11/318 [3.5%], P = 0.07). 90-day graft failure remained low in both groups, and graft thrombosis rates were similar (11/882 [1.2%] vs. 6/318 [1.9%], P = 0.38). Side-specific analyses (right vs. left allografts) revealed no significant variation in vascular complications, including thrombosis (P = 0.44).

Multivariable logistic regression showed no association between MRA and either DGF or 90-day graft failure after adjustment for donor age, recipient age, pre-emptive transplant status, peak PRA, and CIT (Table 2). Death-censored graft survival did not differ between groups (log-rank P = 0.25). Within the MRA cohort, grafts with ≥ 3 arteries (n = 41) exhibited 12-month survival comparable to double-artery grafts (n = 277): 0.96 (95% CI 0.92–1.00) versus 0.97 (95% CI 0.95–0.99), log-rank P = 0.58. Because proportional-hazards assumptions were violated, adjusted Cox models were not fitted; thus, Kaplan–Meier curves should be interpreted cautiously.

Sensitivity analysis comparing grafts with ≥ 3 arteries (n = 41) against single-artery grafts (n = 882) showed DGF in 3/41 (7.3%) versus 19/882 (2.2%) and 90-day graft failure in 1/41 (2.4%) versus 11/882 (1.2%), respectively, differences that did not reach significance, likely reflecting limited statistical power.

To explore residual confounding, 120 paired donors—each providing one single-artery and one MRA kidney—were examined. Baseline recipient characteristics were balanced across pairs. Although median CIT was longer for MRA grafts (34 min, IQR 23–38) than for single-artery grafts (25 min, IQR 16–35; P < 0.001), DGF (2.5% vs. 1.7%) and 90-day graft-failure rates (0.8% vs. 0.8%) remained equivalent.

Of the 318 MRA grafts, 114 (36%) were transplanted using a common arterial patch, 53 (17%) via spatulation into a single orifice, and 151 (47%) fell into an “other” category owing to incomplete operative details or mixed techniques (including nine hybrid reconstructions, two sequential anastomoses, and three polar-artery ligations). These heterogeneous cases were excluded from technique-specific analyses but retained in the overall MRA group because pre-operative imaging confirmed ≥ 2 arteries.

Discussion

Despite the well-documented advantages of living-donor kidney transplantation—shorter waiting times, lower cold-ischemia exposure and superior graft survival—surgeons remain cautious when anatomical variations such as multiple renal arteries (MRA) are encountered [12]. In the present series we assessed early outcomes associated with MRA grafts and found that, although these kidneys demanded more intricate vascular reconstruction, they did not confer higher rates of delayed graft function (DGF) or 90-day graft loss when compared with single-artery grafts [13].

Approximately one quarter of our living-donor grafts displayed MRA, yet neither DGF nor early graft failure differed significantly between groups, even though cold-ischemia time was longer in the MRA cohort. This apparent paradox may reflect protective factors we did not measure—shorter warm-ischaemia periods, meticulous microsurgical reconstruction or preferential selection of low-risk donors—and is consistent with earlier reports showing comparable early outcomes in MRA and single-artery grafts [8, 9, 14]. Although MRA grafts required additional back-table reconstruction and modestly lengthened operative time [15], these technical challenges did not translate into more thrombosis or stenosis during follow-up. Stringent donor selection, generally healthier vasculature and the routine use of modern microsurgical techniques—including arterial patching, spatulation and sequential anastomosis—likely attenuated risk [16, 17]. Importantly, our center applies uniform protocols for arterial reconstruction, which may limit variability in outcome; whether these findings are reproducible elsewhere warrants prospective study.

Urological events, often a concern when polar arteries are sacrificed or ureteric blood supply is jeopardised, were infrequent and comparable between groups [18, 19]. Several limitations merit discussion. First, although our overall sample was large, the subgroup with three or more arteries comprised only 41 cases, limiting power to detect modest risk increments [20]. Multi-center collaboration will be required to amass sufficient numbers for definitive analysis. Second, 151 MRA grafts (47%) were categorised as “other” because detailed intra-operative notes were lacking. Pre-operative imaging confirmed ≥ 2 arteries in every case; nonetheless, misclassification of reconstruction technique cannot be excluded, representing a source of bias we expressly acknowledge. Third, the retrospective design permits residual confounding: surgeons may have been more willing to accept MRA kidneys from donors with otherwise favorable characteristics, potentially masking small risks [21]. Fourth, our analysis was confined to early outcomes; we did not examine chronic allograft dysfunction, late graft loss or long-term recipient survival. Prospective studies with extended follow-up are needed to determine whether MRA predisposes to late complications such as transplant renal artery stenosis or chronic ischaemic nephropathy [22]. Fifth, operative reports varied in the granularity with which issues such as polar-artery ligation, donor-artery diameter or total warm-ischaemia time were documented, precluding detailed adjustment for these factors [23, 24]. Finally, we captured recipient characteristics comprehensively but did not record donor morbidity. Adhering to the principle of primum non nocere, future investigations should report donor complications explicitly.

In summary, our single-center experience indicates that, when modern reconstruction techniques are applied, living-donor kidneys with multiple renal arteries yield early graft outcomes comparable to those with a single artery. While longer cold-ischemia is unavoidable, careful surgical planning and microsurgical expertise appear to offset the theoretical risks. Conclusion

In summary, our single-center experience indicates that, when modern reconstruction techniques are applied, living-donor kidneys with multiple renal arteries yield early graft outcomes comparable to those with a single artery. While longer cold-ischemia is unavoidable, careful surgical planning and microsurgical expertise appear to offset the theoretical risks. Confirmation in larger, multi-center cohorts with long-term follow-up will further define the safety profile of these anatomically complex but potentially valuable grafts. Despite these constraints, our findings have meaningful implications. Multi-artery grafts from living donors seem to represent a safe and viable option, and concerns about short-term adverse outcomes alone do not justify excluding these kidneys from transplantation. Future studies should focus on detailed reporting of ischemia and anastomosis times, as these may influence outcomes independent of the number of arteries.
