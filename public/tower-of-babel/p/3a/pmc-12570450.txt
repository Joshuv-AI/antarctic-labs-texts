Massive left atrial myxoma uncommon presentation in an aged Man, an educational case report and literature review

Background

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, and approximately 90% of them are benign, with left atrial (LA) myxoma being the most prevalent subtype [1]. Despite accounting for over half of all non-metastatic cardiac tumors, the overall incidence of myxomas remains extremely low, estimated at 0.5 per million population annually [2]. Histologically, these tumors are of mesenchymal origin, arising from unregulated proliferation of subendocardial or multipotent mesenchymal cells, often located at the fossa ovalis [3, 4]. An estimated 75–80% of cardiac myxomas occur in the left atrium, with 15–20% found in the right atrium and the remaining 5% distributed within the cardiac ventricles [4]. Myxomas typically present between the fourth and sixth decades of life, with a female predominance — the incidence in women is approximately 1.5 times higher than in men. Pediatric cases are exceedingly rare and often suggest a familial or syndromic form, such as Carney complex, an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by cardiac and extracardiac myxomas, endocrine abnormalities (e.g., Cushing syndrome), and cutaneous pigmentation.

The presentation of this condition depends on the patient’s tumor size, location, and age. It is mostly asymptomatic, and in symptomatic cases, it most commonly presents with constitutional symptoms such as fatigue and fever. In its familial form, myxoma is caused by mutations that inactivate genes encoding the cAMP-dependent protein kinase type 1α regulatory subunit (PRKAR1A) [5].

While left atrial myxomas are well documented, this case is notable due to several atypical features: advanced age, male sex, unusually large tumor size, and misleading initial symptoms. The patient—a 76-year-old man—presented with embolic stroke and recurrent syncope, symptoms initially attributed to insulin-induced hypoglycemia, resulting in diagnostic delay. Such a demographic and clinical profile is rare in the literature, underscoring the importance of considering cardiac etiologies in elderly patients with unexplained neurological events. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by myxomas and reinforces the critical role of early echocardiographic evaluation in similar scenarios.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a sudden onset of aphasia following a transient loss of consciousness. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension, for which he had been on insulin therapy for four months. Over the previous two months, he had experienced four unexplained syncopal episodes that were misattributed to hypoglycemia, despite consistently normal glucose readings. During this period, his family also noted progressive speech deterioration, which had not been previously investigated. Additionally, he reported persistent malaise and headaches over the past three months.

At presentation, the patient was alert but exhibited global aphasia, severely limiting communication. The neurological examination revealed preserved motor strength and cranial nerve function, with no focal deficits except for the language disturbance. Vital signs were stable: blood pressure 140/90 mmHg, heart rate 80 bpm, respiratory rate 17 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 95% on room air.

Cardiac examination revealed a low-frequency mid-diastolic murmur, best heard over the apex in the left lateral decubitus position—a finding that raised suspicion of a cardiac pathology. A non-contrast brain computed tomography (CT) was initially unremarkable. However, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed multiple cortical infarcts in the temporal and parietal lobes, consistent with embolic stroke. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further evaluation and management of a suspected cardioembolic stroke.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed revealed a large, mobile echogenic mass attached to the interatrial septum, measuring approximately 59 mm in its largest diameter in the apical four-chamber view. The mass occupied over 90% of the left atrial cavity and was observed to prolapse through the mitral valve during diastole, with no significant increase in MV inflow gradient at rest (Fig. 1).

Comprehensive echocardiographic findings are summarized in Table 1. The left ventricle had normal size and systolic function (ejection fraction (EF) 55%) with mild diastolic dysfunction. The mitral valve was structurally normal, showing only mild regurgitation and a normal diastolic gradient. The left atrium was moderately dilated (left atrial volume index (LAVI) 42 cc/m²), and the mass showed a smooth border with no evidence of calcification. Right heart function and valves were preserved, and pulmonary pressures were within normal range. These findings were in favor of a large left atrial myxoma. They suggested that the patient’s prior syncopal episodes and current embolic stroke were due to tumor-related embolization and intermittent mitral inflow obstruction.

Surgical management and histopathology

The patient was transferred for urgent surgical excision of the mass. Via median sternotomy and left atriotomy, a pedunculated, gelatinous mass was excised (Fig. 2). The mass measured approximately 6 cm in diameter, with a smooth surface and areas of hemorrhage. Histopathological examination (Fig. 3) confirmed the diagnosis of left atrial myxoma, demonstrating a myxoid stroma populated by stellate and polygonal cells, interspersed with hemorrhagic zones and hemosiderin deposits (Gamma-Gandy bodies).

Postoperative course and Follow-Up

The patient spent three days in the ICU and an additional two days in the coronary care unit (CCU). His neurological symptoms improved progressively, and by postoperative Day 7, complete resolution of aphasia was observed. He was discharged in stable condition without residual neurological deficits. At one- and three-month follow-up visits, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no recurrence of syncope, aphasia, or other neurological symptoms. Follow-up echocardiography showed no signs of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

Primary cardiac tumors, though exceedingly rare, are of significant clinical importance. Autopsy studies reveal an incidence of 0.001% to 0.3% [1], with cardiac myxomas representing the most common type of primary cardiac tumor. Myxomas typically arise in the left atrium and are predominantly sporadic, accounting for 95% of cases, with the remaining 5% being familial [2, 3]. This is consistent with the patient’s presentation, where no familial history of myxomas was identified. Left atrial myxomas most commonly affect middle-aged females, with peak incidence between the fourth and sixth decades of life and a female-to-male ratio of approximately 1.5:1 [2, 3]. Our case deviates from this pattern, involving a 76-year-old male, an age and gender combination less frequently reported in the literature. Similar demographic outliers were described by Bajraktari et al. [6] and Shabab et al. [7], underscoring that clinicians should not exclude cardiac myxoma based on age or sex alone.

Cardiac myxomas typically present with obstructive symptoms, embolic phenomena, or constitutional signs. In our case, the patient initially experienced recurrent syncope and developed aphasia due to an embolic stroke. These symptoms were initially misattributed to insulin-induced hypoglycemia, which led to a delay in diagnosis. Such diagnostic misdirection is not uncommon; previous studies by Kebede et al. [8] and Nicholls et al. [9] have documented similar cases where embolic events were initially misdiagnosed. Our case reinforces the importance of including cardiac sources in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neurological symptoms in elderly patients.

Cardiac myxomas are classified into two main types: smooth-surfaced and villous-surfaced. Smooth myxomas are often larger and associated with obstructive symptoms due to their mass effect, whereas villous myxomas, characterized by their friable and irregular structure, are more prone to embolization [10]. In our case, the patient exhibited embolic symptoms, which are potentially life-threatening complications and are not commonly associated with smooth-surfaced myxomas. These embolic events occur in up to 30–40% of myxoma cases and are frequently associated with neurological or retinal complications [11, 12]. Interestingly, the consistency of the tumor also appears to influence the risk of embolism. Smaller and softer myxomas are more likely to cause embolic complications, as demonstrated by Wang et al., who reported that 15.5% of patients with myxomas smaller than 4.5 cm had embolic events before surgery [11].

Echocardiography remains the gold standard for diagnosing cardiac myxomas, as it provides high sensitivity for detecting well-demarcated, mobile masses with a pedunculated attachment [3]. However, advanced imaging techniques like cardiac MRI and CT can offer additional details on tumor morphology and better define the extent of the lesion, which may be particularly helpful in surgical planning [6]. Early diagnosis and prompt surgical intervention are critical, as timely resection of the myxoma can prevent life-threatening complications, such as embolism and sudden cardiac death [4, 10]. The post-surgical prognosis for cardiac myxoma patients is generally favorable, with a survival rate of nearly 90% at 10 years [11].

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large, mobile mass measuring 59 mm, attached to the interatrial septum and prolapsing through the mitral valve. This is among the largest myxomas reported in the literature. Although Wang et al. [11] noted that smaller, villous tumors have higher embolic potential, our case demonstrated embolic complications despite the tumor’s smooth surface and large size, suggesting additional risk factors such as mobility and systemic inflammation may be involved.

Recurrence of myxomas after surgical resection is rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. However, familial forms, such as those associated with Carney complex, exhibit higher recurrence rates [13, 14]. Current guidelines suggest echocardiographic follow-up for up to 4–5 years in sporadic cases. Lifelong follow-up is generally unnecessary unless familial or genetic syndromes (e.g., Carney complex) are present [15]. Regular echocardiographic surveillance is crucial for early detection of recurrence, which, if managed promptly, can further reduce morbidity and improve long-term outcomes [16].

To ensure consistency and clinical relevance, we reviewed the literature and included only confirmed cases of left atrial myxoma that presented with embolic complications, obstructive symptoms, or delayed diagnosis. Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights the diagnostic complexity of left atrial myxoma in elderly patients presenting with recurrent syncope, embolic strokes, and nonspecific systemic symptoms. Early implementation of transthoracic echocardiography in such clinical scenarios can expedite diagnosis, enabling timely surgical excision and favorable neurological recovery. For sporadic myxomas, postoperative echocardiographic follow-up for 4–5 years is typically sufficient to monitor for recurrence.
