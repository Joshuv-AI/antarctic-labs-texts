Palmitoleic acid promoted by BMPR2 signaling primes CD169 macrophages and alleviates liver fibrosis
Authors: Hong Ma, Jinkun Zhong, Qilin Peng, Mengyuan Dai, Linyuan Wang, Jianan Wang, Danchun Huang, Yang Liu, Yan Tang, Wei Xu, Qi-qun Tang, Shuwen Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) participate in the energy metabolism. BMP receptor type 2 (BMPR2) is expressed in the liver. Whether BMPR2 is involved in the pathophysiology of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has not been studied. In this study, we analyzed the RNA-sequence data of several patient cohorts and found that BMPR2 expression decreases at the stages of advanced fibrosis. A mouse model featuring BMPR2 knockout demonstrate that BMPR2 knockout in the liver produces profibrotic effect upon long-term high-fat and high-fructose (HFF) diet challenge. BMP signaling promotes biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, which is repressed under BMPR2 knockout condition. The most affected unsaturated fatty acid, palmitoleic acid (POA), is found to activate CD169 macrophages. Moreover, CD169 macrophages is revealed to have high levels of lysosomal enzyme along with matrix metalloproteinase 8, which degrades collagen and alleviates fibrosis. Our study demonstrates that BMPR2 influences over the progression of liver fibrosis induced by the HFF diet through fatty acid-regulated CD169 macrophages. POA-primed macrophages might be a potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of liver fibrosis.Subject terms: Cell biology, Liver fibrosis

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) encompasses a spectrum of disorders, ranging from simple steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocarcinoma1. Pure steatosis is a benign condition, that has been strongly associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome (MS)2. Approximately 25% steatosis cases are complicated by NASH, a severe form of NAFLD that involves inflammation and predisposes the patient to liver cirrhosis3,4. The dynamics of NASH progression also indicate chance for regression5,6. Inflammation is an important feature of NASH progression, in which macrophages are involved7. Liver macrophages consist of tissue-resident Kupffer cells and monocyte-derived macrophages recruited from systemic circulation8. When progressing to the NASH state, the resident Kupffer cell content decreases, followed by the infiltration of monocytes, which turn into subsets similar to Kupffer cell or lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs)9–11. Hepatic LAMs are located in fibrotic zones and thought to participate in NASH and fibrosis12–14. However, LAMs do not necessarily exhibit impaired function or an increased pro- inflammatory phenotype; some show antifibrogenic and protective effects10,15–17.

Epidemiological data indicate that the etiology and pathogenesis of NAFLD are complex. The multiple-hit hypothesis, which considers multiple insults to act together, provides a more reasonable explanation for NAFLD pathogenesis18. Therefore, it is necessary to consider more than one factor when analyzing outcomes. Genetic and environmental factors are generally thought to be the two main contributors to the pathology of NAFLD.

Bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2 (BMPR2) is type II receptor for BMP2/4, BMP5/6/7/8, BMP9/10, growth differentiation factor (GDF5/6), and BMP15/GDF9, which are transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) family members19,20. Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) act by binding to type I and type II BMP receptors to activate downstream P38/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) or suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD) pathways and play important roles in embryonic development, organogenesis, and structural and functional homeostasis of various tissues in adulthood21,22. Genetic deficiency of BMPR2 is associated with familial pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH)23. In PAH, abnormalities are not only restricted to the pulmonary circulation but also involve multiple organs, such as the systemic circulation, nervous system, kidney, skeletal muscle, and liver24,25. Liver abnormalities in PAH patients manifest as congestive hepatopathy and hepatic fibrosis26,27. Although abnormalities are generally regarded as the consequences of hemodynamic insufficiency, genetic and molecular events, such as metabolic programming and immunity, may be common pathophysiological mechanisms in multiple organs28,29.

Although of BMPR2 expression is comparable to that in other well-studied tissues, the role of BMPR2 in the physiology and pathology of the liver has not been specifically studied. We checked some RNA-sequence datasets for the liver samples from patients with NAFLD and analyzed expression of BMPR230–32. The level of BMPR2 in liver exhibited a stage-dependent variation. Downregulation was observed in the steatosis and fibrosis stages but not in the NASH stage. These data suggest a role for BMPR2 in the progression of NAFLD and imply the involvement of other synergetic factors. Therefore, we used albumin-Cre to drive BMPR2 knockout in the liver along with a high-fat and high-fructose (HFF) diet challenge to observe the role of BMPR2 in the pathogenesis of NAFLD and explore the underlying mechanism. BMP signaling regulated the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, which was promoted under BMPR2 knockout condition along with a moderate diet challenge. Elevated BMP7 and activin receptor type 2a (ACVR2a) were detected to account for this compensation, which was disabled if the HFF diet was sustained for up to 1 year. The most affected unsaturated fatty acid was palmitoleic acid (POA), which activated CD169 macrophages. These activated macrophages had high lysozyme activity accompanied by matrix metalloproteinase 8 (MMP8), and was able to degrade collagen and alleviate liver fibrosis. Adoptively transferred peritoneal macrophages primed with POA showed antifibrotic effects. Therefore, the genetic deficiency of BMPR2 is dynamically affected by dietary factors, which might explain the stage-dependent variation of BMPR2 in NAFLD. Our findings on POA-primed CD169 macrophages also suggest their therapeutic potential in combating liver fibrosis.

Results

Liver BMPR2 affects lipid metabolism and NAFLD

Expression level of BMPR2 was less than that in the lungs, but was still considerable based on data from a human dataset (Fig. S1a)33. We determined the protein levels of BMPR2 in various tissues of mice using western blotting. BMPR2 levels were was most abundant in the lungs, followed by the liver and kidney, indicating the potential role of BMPR2 in the biological function of the liver (Fig. 1a). We then analyzed the RNA sequence data of liver biopsies from cohorts from three studies (GSE167523, GSE213621, and GSE48452) and found that BMPR2 expression in liver of patients with steatosis or fibrosis (stage 2 to stage 4) was lower than that in the corresponding normal control (Fig. 1b, c). Unexpectedly, in the NASH state, BMPR2 expression tended to be higher than that in steatosis (Fig. 1d). Therefore, BMPR2 may be associated with the progression of NAFLD. To test this hypothesis, BMPR2 was knocked out in the liver by crossing albumin-Cre and BMPR2 floxed mice to observe biological alterations in the liver (Fig. S1b). Bmpr2 knockout did not change the body weight of mice fed a chow diet (Fig. S1c). RNA sequence analysis of the liver tissue showed significantly downregulated genes enriched in the signaling pathway of the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 1e), including acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (Acox1), acyl-CoA thioesterase 1 (Acot1-4), elongation of very long-chain fatty acids (Elovl6), and fatty acid desaturase (Fads1 and Fads2) (Fig. 1f). The decreased protein level of ACOT1, FADS2 and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD1, another enzyme catalyzing the desaturation of fatty acid), as well as BMPR2 was verified by western blotting (Fig.1g, h). FADS1 and ELOVL6 did not change after calculating the gray density of the bands (Fig. 1h). Although the ratio of liver to body weight did not change (Fig. 1i), the amount of triacylglycerol (TG) increased in the liver of BMPR2 knockout mice (Fig. 1j). But the total cholesterol level remained unchanged (Fig. S1d). The free fatty acid profile of liver showed no obvious alteration (Fig. S1e). The serum TG and cholesterol showed no difference between knockout and control mice (Fig. S1f and S1g); however, random glucose levels in serum were elevated in the knockout mice (Fig. S1h). As BMPR2 knockout in the liver altered lipid metabolism, the possibility that liver BMPR2 levels are related to NAFLD was suggested.

BMPR2 regulates biosynthesis of POA

We further challenged the BMPR2 knockout and control mice with a diet of high-fat and 5% fructose in water (HFF) to induce liver steatosis and progressing to fibrosis. Because of the alteration of genes involved in lipid metabolism shown by previous RNA sequencing (Fig. 1e), we examined the profiles of fatty acids in the liver tissue. The levels of the unsaturated fatty acid POA (16:1, n7) were significantly elevated in BMPR2 knockout mice (Fig. 2a). Oleic acid (18:1, n9) and eicosenoic acid (20:1) marginally increased (Fig. 2a). We determined the levels of enzymes for biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acid found the levels of SCD1 increased, levels of FADS2 decreased and level of FADS1 and ELOVL6 did not change (Fig. 2b, c). According to the unsaturated fatty acid synthesis pathway, changes in these enzymes was consistent to the increase of POA (Fig. 2d). ACOX1 levels increased in the knockout mice, whereas the ACOT1 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 2b, c). When we extended the diet challenge to 12 months, SCD1, FADS2, FADS1, and ACOX1 in knockout mice were all lower than those in the controls at this end point (Fig. 2e, f). No obvious differences were observed in stearic acid (18:0) levels between the knockout and control mice (Fig. 2g). The levels of the saturated fatty acid palmitic acid (PA) increased more than 10-fold from 6 months to 12 months and were even higher in knockout mice than in the control (Fig. 2a, g). POA levels decreased to undetected levels using the method described in the present study (Fig. 2g). Oleic acid decreased about 2-fold and eicosenoic acid decreased about 10-fold in WT mice, and decreased more in knockout mice, lead to no difference between two genotypes on 12-month long diet (Fig. 2g). The collective findings indicate that BMPR2 knockout in the liver affected unsaturated fatty acid metabolism depending on the duration of the dietary challenge, in which SCD1 was one of the important regulator enzymes. We further analyzed SCD1 expression in the human datasets. The liver samples grouped in NASH and fatty liver (NAFL) (GSE 167523) showed the slightly higher level of SCD1 in NASH state (Fig. S2a). These grouped according fibrosis (GSE 213621) showed fibrotic liver also had higher level of SCD1 extremely in F0/F1 stage (Fig. S2b). Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r) of the expression levels of SCD1 and BMPR2 was about 0.25 with statistically significance in the two datasets (Fig. S2c and S2d).

As BMP signaling involves multiple ligands and receptors, there could be compensatory regulation if BMPR2 levels were lowered. In hepatocytes of BMPR2 knockout mice, we detected an elevation in another type 2 receptor, Acvr2a, but not Acvr2b (Fig. 2h). The Bmp7 in liver tissue of BMPR2 knockout mice increased significantly, and Bmp9 tended to increase, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 2i). Since we observed that the alteration in unsaturated POA caused by BMPR2 deficiency differed with the duration of the dietary challenge, we further examined the expression levels of these ligands and receptors at different diet-treating times. Six months of the HFF diet increased the expression of Acvr2a and Bmp9. If the diet was maintained for another 6 months, Bmpr2, Acvr2a, Acvr2b, and Bmp7 decreased (Fig. 2j). Bmp9, Bmp7 and Acvr2 expression levels were higher in knockout than WT mice at 6-month timepoint, but the elevation disappeared at 12-month timepoint. Bmpr1a expression did not change much by HFF diet and BMPR2 knockout (Fig. 2j). We also determined the protein levels of BMPR2 and its downstream P38 and suppressor of mother against decapentaplegic (SMAD) family protein 1/5/8, as well as enzymes involved in the desaturation of fatty acids, and we found 3-6 months HFF diet feeding significantly elevated phosphorylated P38 (P-P38), phosphorylated SMAD1/5/8 (P- SMAD1/5/8), SCD1, and FADS2. These molecules, as well as BMPR2 and FADS1 decreased when the diet was maintained for 12 months (Fig. 2k). Moreover, downregulation of P-P38 and P- SMAD1/5/8 was confirmed in the liver of BMPR2 knockout mice fed the HFF diet for 12 months (Fig. 2i, m). These results indicated a compensated and decompensated state by other receptors and ligands in BMPR2 knockouts during the courses of diet challenge.

BMPR2 deficiency in liver conditionally aggravates fibrosis

Because the duration of diet feeding influenced the lipid profile and enzymes participating in lipid metabolism, we anticipated that dietary factor cooperated with BMP signaling to generate downstream effects. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the liver showed that control mice became steatotic after 6 months of HFF diet feeding but to a less extent in male BMPR2 knockout mice (Fig. 3a). The lipid droplets in control livers were enlarged after 1 year of diet challenge and were even more extended in BMPR2 knockouts (Fig. 3a). Quantification and calculation of the TG content in the liver revealed changes consistent with the phenotype indicated by H&E staining (Fig. 3b). No difference was observed in the cholesterol content between the control and knockout mice at either diet treatment timepoint (Fig. 3c). The ratio of liver to body weight decreased in knockout mice at 6 months, but no difference was noted at 12-month timepoint compared to the control (Fig. 3d). The knockout mice gained more body weight after feeding on the diets for 6 months (Fig. 3e). These phenotypes of the liver and body weight were similar in female mice, except that the TG content in the liver of BMPR2 knockouts was higher than that in control mice fed the HFF diet for 6 months, and had no difference at 1 year (Fig. S3a–d).

As the HFF diet was supposed to induce fibrosis, we then examined the collagen expression in the liver by immunohistochemistry (IHC) for collagen 1 and Sirus Red staining. Positive staining was seen for collagen in the livers of wild type mice after 6 months of diet treatment, and the degree was comparably lower in the knockout mice (Fig. 3f–i). At 12-month timepoint, collagen increased and appeared scar-like, particularly in the livers of knockout mice (Fig. 3f–i). In order to explore what the synthesis and degradation of collagen were like. We also examined the expression levels of types of collagens, Acta2 indicating the hepatic stellate cells, and metalloproteinase inhibitor, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (Timp1). Collagen Ⅰ (Col1a), Ⅲ (Col3a), and Ⅳ (Col4a) all increased with the duration of diet treatment and the elevation was especially great for Col1a at 12-month timepoint. The expression levels of Col1a and Col3a in knockout mice were lower than that in control at 6-month timepoint, and the deviation no longer existed at 12-month timepoints (Fig. S3e). The alteration was similar for Acta2 (Fig. S3f). There was no variation for Col4a expression between two genotypes (Fig. S3e). Timp1 expression in knockout mice was lower than that in WT mice for 6-month diet on, which was reversed when the diet maintained for 12 months (Fig. S3g). The fibrosis indicated by collagen staining were similar in females (Fig. S3h). Glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) were less impaired in both male and female BMPR2 knockouts fed the HFF diet for 6 months (Fig. S3i and S3j). The inflammation levels by indicated NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (Nlpr3), and interleukin 18 (Il18) were lower in male knockouts than in control mice fed the 6-month-term HFF diet (Fig. 3j), whereas these genes, along with Il1b and Il6 were higher in knockout mice in the 1-year term groups (Figs. 3k). Liver function was worse in these mice 1 year after the diet challenge, as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) levels were higher in knockouts than in controls (Figs. 3l and S3k). ITT tended to be impaired after 1 year of HFF diet treatment, which was opposite to the GTT reaction in females (Figs. S3l and S3m). This trend was similar in males, although the difference was not statistically significant (Figs. S3l and S3m).

CD169 macrophages decrease in the liver of BMPR2 knockout mice fed the HFF diet for 1 year and MASH patients

Except for the inhibition of the pathway for the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, RNA sequence analysis revealed the activation of infection, indicating a potential inflammatory reaction (Fig. S4a). As the levels of inflammatory cytokines increased after 1 year of the HFF diet, we examined the macrophages in the liver using flowcytometry and IHC. An increased number of F4/80 positive macrophages in the livers of knockout mice was revealed by flow cytometry (Fig. 4a) and IHC (Fig. 4b, c). Due to the involvement of CD169 (Siglec1) gene in the differentially expressed genes caused by the BMPR2 knockout (Fig. S4b), CD169 macrophages were also examined. These cells were decreased in BMPR2 knockouts as shown by flow cytometry (Fig. 4d) and IHC staining (Fig. 4e, f). We also checked in Liver Single Cell Atlas (LiverSCA) based on 248442 cells from 63 patients (https://patholiver.hku.hk/liverp/) and found that the ratio of macrophages in health group (14.11%) is higher than that in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) group (8.67%) (Fig. 4g left). Notably, CD 169 positive macrophages account for 17.63% macrophages of healthy individuals, which is about 3-fold of that of MASH patients (5.45%) (Fig. 4g middle). However, the macrophages defined by the marker, CD68, do not differ much between heathy (65.41%) and MASH (69.85%) groups (Fig. 4g right). The human dataset of bulk RNA-sequence (GSE48452) with more finely divided groups shows CD169 expression increases in the steatosis stage comparing to control and health obesity, then decreased when progressing to NASH stage (Fig. S4c). These data indicate that CD169 identify a subset of macrophages that may curb the pathological progression to severe NASH/MASH and fibrosis stage.

To determine the factors influencing the recruitment of CD169 macrophages, we rechecked the RNA-sequence data and screened those molecules for adhesion (Fig. S4b). We postulated that intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (Icam1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (Vcam1) were more likely to expressed in parenchymal hepatocytes (Fig. S4b). We checked the expression of Icam1 and Vcam1 in the isolated hepatocytes and found Icam1 was highly expressed in hepatocytes (Fig. S4d) and further increased in BMPR2 knockout mice (Fig. 4h). Because POA and PA was the most altered fatty acid after BMPR2 knockout and diet challenge, we treated hepatocytes with unsaturated POA and saturated PA to observe Icam1 levels. Western blotting showed that POA elevated Icam1 levels to a greater extent while PA did not (Fig. 4i). We co-cultured peritoneal macrophages with hepatocytes pretreated with PA or POA (Fig. 4j). Flow cytometry analysis showed that POA-treated hepatocytes stimulated more CD169 cells than PA-treated cells, which could be dampened by ICAM1 neutralization with an antibody (Fig. 4k, i).

POA primes macrophages for CD169 expression and high level of phagocytosis

Because fatty acids synthesized in hepatocytes can be released and affect other tissues or cells, we investigated whether POA directly regulates macrophages. We treated peritoneal macrophages with POA and PA and found that POA significantly increased the percentage of CD169-positive macrophage (Fig. 5a, b). To determine the potential function of the POA-primed macrophages, we subjected those cells to sequencing. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analysis based on the differentially expressed genes between POA and PA showed that the efferocytosis pathway was the most significantly activated by POA (Fig. 5c). Among the genes involved in the efferocytosis pathway, we identified phospholipase A2 group XV (Pla2g15) (Fig. 5d), which encodes a lysosomal enzyme34. Therefore, we examined the expression of Pla2g15 as well as two lysozymes (Lyz1 and Lyz2) using quantitative PCR. Lyz2 was also increased in POA-primed macrophages compared to PA besides Pla2g15 (Fig. 5e). The phagocytosis assay ex vivo showed POA increased the ability of phagocytosis of macrophage (Fig. 5f, g). High lysozyme activity in CD169 macrophages was further evidenced by immunofluorescence staining of the mouse livers (Fig. 5h). Using the human liver single-cell atlas (https://patholiver.hku.hk/liverp/anno/), we verified that PLA2G15 was enriched in macrophages (Fig. 5i) and was less extended in patients with MASH than in healthy controls. This variation was similar for CD169 (SIGLEC1) (Fig. 5i). These results supported the hypothesis that CD169 macrophages exhibit high levels of phagocytosis and lysozyme activity.

CD169 macrophages synthesize MMP8 to decrease collagen

Considering the metalphilic characteristic of CD169 macrophages35, we postulated that MMPs may be also be activated in CD169 macrophages. To find out which types of MMP may be activated in CD169 macrophage, we used the expression data of the human liver (GSE 48452) that has MMPs to observe the correlation of CD169 and MMPs participating in collagen (Figs. 6a and S5a). Taking the R and P value together into consideration, MMP8 was mostly likely associated with CD169. Moreover, MMP8 has been reported to associate with lysozyme in some specific granule lumens36. So, we focused on MMP8 and tested in mouse liver by co-staining of CD169 and MMP8. The staining showed that CD169 macrophages highly expressed MMP8 (Fig. 6b). MMP8 also exist in CD169-negative cells, which was less strong as in CD169-positive cells (Fig. 6b).

Since CD169 macrophages highly express MMP8 (Fig. 6b), CD169 macrophages might contribute to the degradation of collagen by MMP8. This hypothesis was also supported by the in-situ distributions of CD169, MMP8 and collagen that the collagen levels were low at the site where CD169 and MMP8 protein level were high (Fig. S5b). Moreover, in liver tissue of BMPR2 knockout mice, CD169 and MMP8 staining was low, and collagen staining was high (Fig. 6c, d), which was also evidenced by western blot (Fig. 6e).

As POA-primed macrophages had a higher percentage of CD169 cells, we investigated whether these cells could alleviate live fibrosis. Peritoneal macrophages cultured with POA had higher levels of MMP8 (Fig. 6f). We collected and adoptively transferred these fatty acid-nurtured macrophages to fibrosis livers induced by high-fat diet and CCl4. The macrophage survived in the liver when examined two days after transfer (Fig. S5c). The treatment of macrophage did not change the collagen production (Fig. S5d). POA-primed macrophages efficiently alleviated the degree of fibrosis compared to injection of PBS, as evident by either positive staining of collagen and Sirus Red (Fig. 6g, h) or western blot detection of collagen levels (Fig. 6i). PA-treated macrophages slightly relieve the fibrosis to the degree less than POA-treated macrophages (Fig. 6g–i). AST levels elevated in the group of PA-treated macrophage (Fig. S5e). One more cohort were performed to explore whether fatty acids themselves would influence the onset of fibrosis. The results showed that PA aggravated fibrogenesis. On the contrary, POA alleviated the fibrogenesis, which seemed to be less effective than the direct macrophage transfer (Fig. S5f-h).

Discussion

Several studies have been conducted to determine the role of BMPs in NAFLD. Based on clinical research data, BMP2, BMP7, and BMP9 appear to have protective effects against the progression of cirrhosis37–39, as their expression decreases in severe cirrhosis. By contrast, BMP8B appears to be a risk factor for disease progression, as BMP8B continues to increase as NASH progresses40. However, the expression levels of BMPs also seem to be related to the stage of the disease, such as the levels of BMP7 in liver and serum, which are elevated in patients with early cirrhosis39,41, but downregulated in later stage of cirrhosis. Studies using mouse models to investigate the role of BMPs or receptors in NASH and fibrosis have produced inconsistent conclusions42–44. One of the reasons for this is that the methods for creating the mouse model are different, including the composition of fat in food and whether other factors are included, such as fructose, methionine/choline deficient diet, and CCl4. The duration of the dietary challenge should also be considered. In our study, different durations of HFF diet challenge produced opposite phenotypes in BMPR2 knockout mice. Six-month diet challenge resulted in BMPR2 knockouts with resistance to fibrogenesis, which were disabled when the diet challenge was continued for over 1 year. In the relatively short-term-diet-challenged groups, we found a compensatory mechanism involving other members of the BMP family, among which BMP7, BMP9 and ACVR2a mostly participated. The administration of BMP7 or adenovirus-expressing BMP7 has been reported to have antifibrotic functions39,45. Therefore, the compensatory activation of BMP signaling may explain the beneficial effects of resisting fibrosis induced by BMPR2 knockout. BMPs and their receptors are closely associated with energy, particularly lipid metabolism46–50. In metabolic organs, such as the liver and adipose tissue, these molecules are usually increased by a high-fat diet, which is consistent with our results that a 3- and 6- month diet challenge activated BMP signaling. However, most studies did not maintain the diet challenge for more than 1 year, which was maintained due to the shutdown of lab during the COVID period in our studies. The accidental long period actually mimicked the dietary habit people would usually hardly change and obsess over for most of their life-time. As BMP signals respond to the synthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, long-term consumption of food, particularly those rich in saturated fatty acids and fructose, totally represses BMP signaling, resulting in extremely high levels of PA but no POA. The phenotypic outcome by BMPR2 knockout in liver in this study were actually include compensation of BMP signaling and food adaption. The regulation of BMP signaling by diet complicated the compensations in BMPR2 knockout liver by ACVR2a and its ligands BMP7 and BMP9, leading to whether genetical deficiency BMPR2 was the risk or protective factor depended on the stage of NAFLD (Fig. 7).

The promotion was due to the compensation by BMP7, BMP9 and ACVR2a, and decompensation occurred after 12-month HFF diet feeding. In some human cohorts, low BMPR2 expression levels did not necessarily correlate with NAFLD progressing. Although BMPR2 expression is decreased in patients with steatosis or fibrosis, it tends to increase in patients with NASH. Such patients are thought to be at a higher risk of developing cirrhosis; however, mitigation can occur. Therefore, NASH represent a state that progressing from steatosis when the immune cells combatting fibrosis also change dynamically. We found that CD169 macrophages have anti-fibrosis potential. CD169 is typically expressed in metallophilic macrophages residing in the marginal zone of the spleen and the subcapsular sinus and medulla of the lymph nodes51. CD169 macrophages across tissues are different from M1- or M2- like macrophages, and are reported to function in phagocytosis and antigen presentation, thus being involved in anti-tumor immunity, as well as in defense against infection by pathogens, such as viruses, bacteria and plasmodia52–54. We found that CD169 macrophages had high lysozyme activity associated with MMP8, which degrades collagen and alleviates fibrosis.

CD169 macrophages were activated by unsaturated fatty acid POA through two possible ways: recruitment by ICAM1 on POA-enriched hepatocytes, or the direct action of POA. POA has metabolic benefits, including antiinflammation functions fulfilled through macrophages. For example, POA promotes the M2a polarization of myeloid cells and reduces high fat diet-induced liver inflammation, prevents PA-induced macrophage activation and consequent skeletal muscle insulin resistance, and reduces the inflammation in LPS-stimulated macrophages55–57. Herein, we identified an unrecognized function of POA on CD169 macrophages and their activity of enzymolysis for degrading collagen.

The collective findings of the present study show how genetic factors entangled with environmental factor influence the progress of NAFLD, contributing to the multiple-hit hypothesis of NAFLD etiology. Clinical implication may exist for individuals with genetic deficiency of BMPR2, for whom consumption of food with mild levels of fat might have beneficial outcomes when BMP signaling is compensatorily activated and synthesis of unsaturated fatty acid is improved. However, food habit favoring HFF diet should be restricted to avoid ultrahigh level of saturated PA and trace levels of unsaturated POA in the liver. It would also be inspiring if POA-primed macrophages could be a potential therapeutic strategy to treat liver fibrosis or cirrhosis.

Methods

Animals

To generate mice with a liver-specific knockout of BMPR2, BMPR2LoxP/LoxP (generously provided by En Li, Cardiovascular Research Center, Massachusetts General Hospital, Harvard Medical School) were crossed with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the hepatocyte-specific promoter albumin (Jackson Laboratory). Studies were performed in albumin-Cre- BMPR2LoxP/LoxP mice (LKO) and littermate controls, BMPR2 LoxP/LoxP (labeled as WT). Mice were maintained under 12-h light/12-h dark cycles with unlimited access to food and water. The high fat diet contains 52 kcal% fat and 1.25% cholesterol (Dyets, D190723). The mice were challenged with the high fat diet plus 5% fructose in water for duration ranging from 3 month to 1 year. An alternative method for inducing fibrosis is feeding the mice the high fat diet for 2 weeks, followed by CCl4 (20% in olive oil, 2 μL/g body weight, 1 time/week) treatment i.p. for 6 weeks, the diet was maintained to the end. At the end of experiments, mice were anesthesia with isoflurane (300 mL-500 mL/min) and sacrificed at around 2:00 pm without withdrawing food. The mice not applicable for experiments were euthanized with carbon dioxide. All studies were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Medical College, Fudan University with the approval number 20180302-027. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Cell culture

Primary mouse hepatocytes were isolated by collagenase digestion. The livers were perfused from the portal vein to the incised inferior vena cava with Ca2 + - and Mg2 + -free Hanks’ balanced salt solution (CMF-HBSS) composed of 138 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 4.0 mM NaHCO3, 5.6 mM glucose, 0.3 mM Na2HPO4 and 0.4 mM KH2PO4 (adjusted to pH 7.2). This perfusion was carried out at a rate of approximately 7.46 mL/min for 3 minutes. Subsequently, the perfusion solution was switched to 0.04% to 0.08% collagenase type IV (Sigma-Aldrich, C5138) in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS), supplemented with 0.7 mM MgSO4⋅7H2O and 1.26 mM CaCl2 (adjusted to pH 7.4). This solution was perfused at a rate of 4.66 mL/min for 5 minutes. Following perfusion, the liver was excised, rinsed with PBS, and placed in HBSS, where hepatocytes were liberated. Cells were recovered by centrifugation at 50 × g for 3 minutes at 4°C with maximal braking. Live hepatocytes were subsequently isolated using a 50% Percoll gradient (prepared by first resuspending the cells in 5 mL of PBS, followed by the addition of 5 mL of 100% Percoll), washed with PBS and resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics (1% penicillin and 1% streptomycin). Cells were seeded on collagen-coated plates at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. After allowing the cells to adhere for 6 hours, the medium was replaced to remove nonadherent cells.

For peritoneal macrophage isolation and culture, 3% thioglycolate broth was injected i.p. (1.5 mL/day) into male C57BL/6 mice for 4 days. The macrophages were washed out with PBS from peritoneal cavity. After pelleted, cells were resuspended with red blood lysis buffer to remove red blood, followed by washed with PBS and resuspended in DMEM containing 10% FBS. POA or PA (palmitic acid) was included in the medium at a concentration of 100 μM. After 24 hours, nonadherent cells were washed away and the medium was refreshed. The macrophages were cultured for 2 days for further experiments. The phagocytosis assay was performed using the commercial kit (AAT Bioquest, 21225) according the manufacture’s instruction.

Adoptive macrophage transfer

The 8-week-old male mice were fed on 60 kcal% high-fat diet (Dyets 112252) for the whole experimental procedure. CCl4 (1 µL/g body weight 10% of CCl4 in olive oil) was administrated intraperitoneally twice a week for six weeks after 2 weeks of diet treatment. The adoptive transfer was performed 2 weeks after CCl4 challenge. Fatty acids treated peritoneal macrophage were collected by scraping, and 1 × 106 cells in 100 μL PBS were injected into tail vein. The transfer was performed once a weekly for 4 weeks.

Flow cytometry

Mouse liver tissues were mechanically dissociated by smashing on 100 μm cell strainer (Falcon 352360), and cells were collected by centrifuging. Cultured cells were collected by enzymatic dissociation from dishes and following centrifuging. Fresh cells were fixed with 10% calf serum (CS) in PBS for 30 minutes. Cells were washed with PBS with 0.2% CS and incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against surface antigens in 2% CS- PBS. Stained cells were analyzed with a BDFACSVerse flowcytometer. The data was analyzed with FlowJo v10.7.0. The commercial antibodies used were CD45-APC-Cy7 (BDbiosciences 30-F11), F4/80-PE-Cy7(Biolegend BM8), NK1.1-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BD Biosciences PK136), CD3-FITC (Biolegend 17A2), CD11c-PE (Biolegend N418), and CD169-APC (Biolegend 142417). All the antibodies were used at a 1:200 or 1:500 dilution in1% CS in PBS.

Western blot

Mouse liver tissues or cultured cells were homogenized in 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) tris buffer (60 mM, pH6.8) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). 20–50 μg protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 0.22 μm) membrane. The commercial antibodies used were as following: anti-collagen (Cell Signaling Technology 72026), anti-ACOT1 (Abcam ab133948), anti-FADS1 (Abcam ab126706), anti-FADS2 (Abcam ab232898), anti-SCD1 (Cell Signaling Technology 2794), anti-ACOX1 (Abcam ab128549), anti-P38 (Cell Signaling Technology 9212), anti-P-P38 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9216) and anti-HSP90 (Santa cruz, sc-13119), anti-BMPR2 (Thermo, MA5-15827), anti-SMAD1/5/8 (Cell Signaling Technology, 6944), anti-P-SMAD1/5/8 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9516), anti-ICAM1 (Abcam, ab222736), anti-ELOVL6 (Abcam, ab69857), anti-MMP8 (Abcam, ab81286), anti-TUBULIN (Proteintech, 66240). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies: anti-mouse (Abcam ab6728), anti-Rabbit (Abcam ab6721). All the primary antibodies were used at a 1:1000 dilution in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST. Secondary antibodies were used at a 1:10000 dilution in 5% non-fat milk in TBST.

Histological experiments

Dissected liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. Fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded concentrations of ethanol in water. Sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Sigma-Aldrich). For immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, sections were probed with primary antibody against collagen 1 (CST 72026, 1:500), MMP8 (Abcam ab81286, 1:200), F4/80 (Abcam, ab74383) and CD169 (Novus NB600, 1:200) followed by biotinylated secondary antibody. Binding of second antibodies was visualized by using diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen A (Thermo Fisher).

Statistical and Reproducibility

From animals and ex vivo replicants, data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. All data were analyzed using Graphpad Prism v8.0.2.263 and are represented in the figures as mean values ± SD. Two-tailed Student’s t test was used for comparing two groups. The Pearson Correlation analysis was used to measure the linear correlation between two variables. P values are indicated with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001 on graphs. Where graphs are not labeled with an asterisk, any differences between the test groups and the control groups were non-significant.
