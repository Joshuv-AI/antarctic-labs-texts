Exploring Organ‐Specific Extracellular Vesicles in Metabolic Improvements Following Bariatric Surgery in Youth With Obesity
Authors: Ahlee Kim, Tsuyoshi Okura, Kwangmin Choi, Rupinder Gill, Vishnupriya J Borra, Kazutoshi Murakami, Andrew Poulos, Xiang Zhang, Todd Jenkins, Amy Sanghavi Shah, Michael Helmrath, Takahisa Nakamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveVertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG) promotes significant metabolic improvements, though the underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood. Emerging evidence suggests that small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) contribute to metabolic improvements post VSG, such as improved fatty liver disease or adipose tissue function; however, it is unclear how different organ‐specific sEVs interact with various metabolic parameters. The objective of this study is to establish the role of organ‐specific sEVs in the metabolic improvements post VSG.MethodsDemographic and anthropometric data, metabolic parameters, and sEV samples were collected pre VSG and 3–6 months post VSG in youth with obesity. sEV RNA was sequenced for bioinformatics analyses.ResultsA significant reduction of the liver‐enriched mRNA cargo in sEVs was observed post VSG, whereas adipose‐enriched mRNA cargo showed no such reduction. Liver‐enriched mRNA cargo correlated with BMI, leptin, and resistin 6 months post VSG. Analysis of delta values (post minus pre surgery) revealed that adipose‐enriched mRNA cargo correlated with markers of liver damage, whereas liver‐derived mRNA cargo correlated with branched‐chain amino acids.ConclusionsFollowing VSG, significant changes occur in the liver‐enriched mRNA cargo of sEVs. Liver‐derived sEVs appear to influence adipose metabolism, whereas adipose‐derived sEVs are linked with liver function, suggesting dynamic intertissue cross talk that shapes systemic metabolic outcomes.Keywords: body mass index (BMI), branched‐chain amino acids (BCAA), leptin, metabolic improvements, small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG)

Introduction

Obesity poses significant health risks, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and conditions such as metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic hepatitis (MASH) [1]. Bariatric surgery is a safe and effective intervention to promote dramatic weight loss and metabolic improvements across age groups, including adults, adolescents, and children [1, 2, 3]. Specifically, vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG) is the most commonly performed bariatric surgery in adolescents, demonstrating both safety and effectiveness, with an average 26% weight loss reported 1 year post VSG [4]. As a result, the current clinical guideline for pediatric obesity management now includes bariatric surgery as part of the standard of care [1, 2].

Without a doubt, one of the primary mechanisms behind metabolic improvements associated with VSG is significant weight loss [2, 3, 5, 6, 7]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that these metabolic benefits are not entirely dependent on weight loss alone. Remarkably, metabolic changes appear within just a few days of surgery, well before substantial weight loss occurs, and persist even when some weight is regained [6, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These findings have sparked interest in identifying the molecular mechanisms underlying the weight loss‐independent benefits of bariatric surgery.

While data for cytokines, including adipokines, following bariatric surgery, remain inconsistent [12], some adipokines, such as leptin and resistin, are thought to contribute to metabolic improvements associated with bariatric surgery [13]. For instance, levels of both leptin and resistin decrease after bariatric surgery [14]. The reduction in leptin correlates strongly with changes in body mass index (BMI), suggesting that the decrease in fat mass and improvement in leptin resistance are interconnected [15]. Resistin levels, similarly, are closely associated with weight and BMI, with a downward trend observed following surgery, highlighting its role in obesity‐related metabolic complications [16]. In addition to adipokines, branched‐chain amino acids (BCAAs), a subgroup of amino acids with an aliphatic side‐chain that includes leucine, isoleucine, and valine [17], have emerged as key regulators for human metabolism [18, 19, 20], particularly in the context of obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [18]. Elevated BCAA levels are consistently linked to metabolic dysfunction, including impaired glucose metabolism. Notably, recent studies indicate that bariatric surgery significantly lowers circulating BCAA levels, contributing to the observed metabolic improvements [21].

Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) carry various molecules between tissues, including genetic materials, acting as key mediators of interorgan communication [22, 23, 24]. sEVs are associated with a range of metabolic conditions, including but not limited to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, MASLD/MASH, and cardiovascular disease [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Recent studies suggest that the metabolic benefits of bariatric surgery involve alterations in the circulating sEV profile [33]. Studies have shown that fatty acid binding protein 4 carried by sEVs increases 1 month after bariatric surgery and returns to baseline by 6 months after surgery [34]. Studies have reported a reduction in hepatocyte‐specific sEVs in the blood following bariatric surgery, including those expressing hepatocyte‐specific surface markers in adults with obesity [35, 36].

The objectives of this project were to evaluate changes in circulating sEVs and metabolic parameters in adolescents with severe obesity undergoing VSG and to determine how these changes correlate with metabolic outcomes before and after surgery. In particular, we focused on tissue‐specific mRNA cargo within sEVs to investigate how VSG affects different organs. Furthermore, we explored how tissue‐specific sEVs may interact with BCAA levels and other metabolic parameters to drive robust improvements following VSG.

Methods

Study Population

Adolescents with severe obesity undergoing VSG were recruited and enrolled as part of a single‐center multidisciplinary study at the Pediatric Diabetes and Obesity Center at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC) from 2016 to 2019 [7]. The decision to proceed with VSG was made in accordance with the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (ASMBS) pediatric guidelines [2], involving the subjects, their families, and the multidisciplinary clinical team. All surgery was performed by a single pediatric bariatric surgeon at CCHMC. Participants were assessed and provided blood samples prior to VSG and at 3 and 6 months post VSG. Written informed consent was obtained from participants aged 18 years or older, or from a legal guardian for those under 18 years old, with assent obtained from the minors. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at CCHMC.

Anthropometric Data

Height was measured in centimeters (cm) using a wall‐mounted stadiometer, and weight was recorded in kilograms (kg) using an electronic scale. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

Isolation of sEVs and Extraction of Small RNA Cargo

sEVs were isolated from serum samples using the MagCapture Exosome Isolation Kit (Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan), which utilizes Tim4 protein immobilized on magnetic beads to bind phosphatidylserine exposed on the surface of sEVs. A volume of 500 μL to 1 mL of serum was used for each isolation. The serum was incubated with the Tim4‐coated magnetic beads on a rotator at room temperature for 30 min, allowing efficient capture of sEVs. Following incubation, the magnetic beads were separated using a magnetic stand, and unbound components were removed through several washes with phosphate‐buffered saline. The sEVs were then eluted from the beads with the provided elution buffer and immediately used for RNA extraction. The Qiagen miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for small RNA isolation. Small RNA, including fragmented mRNA, was extracted from sEVs isolated from the same amount of serum across all samples to ensure consistency. The quality and concentration of the isolated RNA were assessed before proceeding to downstream molecular analyses, such as small RNA sequencing (small RNA‐seq).

sEV RNA Cargo Sequencing

Small RNA‐seq of RNA cargo was performed by the Genomics, Epigenomics, and Sequencing Core at the University of Cincinnati per the previously published protocol [37, 38]. To ensure consistency in RNA sequencing outcomes, an identical volume of serum was used for sEV isolation specifically for sequencing of small RNA cargo across all samples in this study. For library preparation, the NEBNext Small RNA Sample Library Preparation Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA) was used with a modified approach for precise small RNA library size selection, allowing the kit to process low‐input RNA (as low as ng levels) with improved library recovery. Specifically, 0.4–4 ng of sEV RNA, quantified by Bioanalyzer QC, was used as input. Following 15 cycles of PCR, libraries with unique indices were pooled at equal volumes (10 μL per sample), cleaned using a column‐based approach, and mixed with a custom‐designed DNA ladder containing 135 bp and 319 bp PCR amplicons. This size range corresponds to small RNA libraries with 16–200 nt inserts, encompassing all small RNA species, including mRNA and miRNAs. Of note, this modified small RNA preparation protocol, which incorporates both adaptors and multiplex primers, enables comprehensive capture of fragmented mRNA species. After high‐resolution agarose gel electrophoresis and gel excision, the library pool ranging from 135 to 319 bp, including the custom DNA ladder, was purified and quantified using the NEBNext Library Quant Kit on a QuantStudio 5 Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). An initial round of sequencing was performed on a NextSeq 550 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to generate several million reads, enabling the relative quantification of each library. The volume of each library was then adjusted, followed by sequencing with single‐end 1 × 85 bp reads to generate ~17 million reads per sample for final data analysis.

Sequencing Data Preparation and Downstream Analyses

Sequencing reads were quality‐checked using FASTQC (v. 0.11.2; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The 3′ adapter sequence (“AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCAC”) was clipped, and reads were trimmed using the FASTX toolkit (v. 0.0.14; http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/). Clipped reads were quantified with the exceRpt tool (v. 4.6.2) on the Genboree Workbench (http://www.genboree.org/). First, reads were aligned using Bowtie2 to UniVec, a library of common contaminant sequences maintained by the NCBI, with valid alignments archived and removed from further analysis. Next, reads were aligned to human ribosomal RNA (rRNA) precursor sequences (including the full 45S, 5S, and mitochondrial rRNAs) using Bowtie2. Reads that passed these filters were aligned sequentially to several databases: Human genome (hg19), miRNA (miRBase; http://www.mirbase.org/), tRNA (gtRNAdb; http://gtrnadb.ucsc.edu/), piRNA (piRNABank; http://pirnabank.ibab.ac.in/), long RNA (GENCODE; https://www.gencodegenes.org/), and circRNA (circBase; http://www.circbase.org/). Aligned read counts were normalized using the TMM (Trimmed Mean of M‐values) method implemented in the edgeR package (v. 3.10; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html). Additionally, down sampling was applied to handle differences in library sizes across samples and batches. Heat maps and PCA plots were generated using the pheatmap (https://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html) and ggplot2 (https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/) packages, respectively. Tissue‐specific and tissue‐enriched genes were collected from the TissueEnrich database's Human Protein Atlas (https://tissueenrich.gdcb.iastate.edu/).

Differential miRNA Expression and Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Differentially abundant miRNAs across time points (pre‐surgery and 3 months and 6 months post VSG) were identified using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Page's trend test to assess consistent directional changes. A total of 17 miRNAs (15 upregulated and 2 downregulated) with significant and directional abundance changes were selected for pathway analysis. KEGG pathway enrichment was performed using miRPath v4.0 [39], applying the “Genes Union” method, which uses the union of all predicted gene targets for the selected miRNAs as the query set. Pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Clinical Labs

Blood samples were collected via venipuncture after participants completed an overnight fast (> 8 h). Fasting glucose level was measured using a Hitachi model 704 glucose analyzer (Roche Hitachi, Indianapolis, IN), and fasting insulin levels were assessed by radioimmunoassay with anti‐insulin serum raised in guinea pigs, 125I‐labeled insulin (Linco, St Louis, MO), and a double antibody method to separate bound from free tracer. The homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated using as: HOMA‐IR = (fasting glucose [mg/dL] × fasting insulin [μU/mL])/405. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was measured using a Siemens Atellica clinical analyzer (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Leptin and resistin levels were measured using the Luminex assay platform (Luminex Corp., Austin, TX) at CCHMC, following the manufacturer's protocol.

BCAA Measurement

BCAAs were quantified using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to a Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole‐Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Of note, the same serum samples used for sEV evaluation were also used for BCAA quantification, ensuring consistency in sample comparisons. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACE C18‐PFP column (100 mm × 2.1 mm) at 30°C, with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (FA) (solvent A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% FA (solvent B). A gradient reduced solvent A from 99% to 10% over 8 min, followed by re‐equilibration. For serum sample preparation, 25 μL of thawed serum was mixed with 100 μL of internal standard solution (5 μg/mL D8‐valine and D10‐leucine in acetonitrile), vortexed, and centrifuged at 21,000g and 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant was dried under nitrogen at 35°C and reconstituted in 300 μL of LC/MS‐grade water with 0.1% FA, and 5 μL was injected for analysis. Calibration standards (0.5–100 μg/mL) and quality control samples (1, 20, 40, 80 μg/mL) were prepared in 0.1% FA. The mass spectrometer operated in positive electrospray ionization mode, with MS2 scans at 70,000 resolutions. Precursor ions of leucine, isoleucine, and valine were quantified using targeted transitions: m/z 118.0865 → 55.0552 for valine and m/z 132.1019 → 86.0970 for leucine and isoleucine. Data were processed using Xcalibur 4.1, with peak areas extracted within a 10 ppm mass window. The assay was linear across a range of 0.5–100 μg/mL.

Statistical Analyses

All available data at each time point, including both RNA‐level and clinical parameter data, were analyzed in an unbiased manner. However, due to inconsistent sample availability across time points, the number of paired data points used in each statistical analysis and plot varied. Correlations (1) between RNA levels and clinical markers or (2) between two clinical markers were assessed by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient in R (v.4.4.0) using paired samples at each time point. The paired version of the nonparametric Kruskal‐Wallis test by ranks was performed in R (v4.4.0) to evaluate whether significant differences exist in RNA or clinical marker levels among paired samples at 0, 3, and 6 months. For miRNA‐level analysis, Page's trend test was applied following Kruskal–Wallis testing to identify miRNAs showing consistent directional changes over time. All plots were generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.2).

Anthropometric and Metabolic Parameters Post VSG

Anthropometric and metabolic parameters were evaluated at multiple time points following VSG to assess the surgery's impact on key metabolic markers. As previously reported, VSG led to a statistically significant reduction in BMI at both 3 and 6 months post surgery [4, 8] (Figure 1a). This result demonstrates that the weight loss trajectory established shortly after surgery is maintained to 6 months following surgery, reflecting effective management of obesity through VSG. In addition to BMI reductions, significant changes were observed in metabolic hormones. Serum leptin levels, which are primarily secreted by adipose tissue and play a role in energy homeostasis [40], were significantly reduced at both 3 and 6 months post VSG (Figure 1b). This reduction correlates with decreased fat mass as leptin levels are closely linked to adiposity. Serum resistin levels, an adipokine implicated in inflammation and insulin resistance [41], decreased following VSG; however, the reduction did not reach statistical significance (Figure 1c). This variability may reflect individual differences in inflammatory responses or may require a longer follow‐up period to observe clear effects.

Serum sEV Analysis and Small RNA Cargo Profiling

To explore whether sEVs are associated with the metabolic improvements observed following VSG, we isolated sEVs from the serum of patients at each time point (Figure 2a,b). Although serum sEV levels showed a decrease following VSG, the reduction was not statistically significant at 3 or 6 months after VSG (Figure 2c). Small RNA cargo was further analyzed by isolating it from the collected sEVs, followed by small RNA‐seq profiling. Among the small RNA cargo, mRNA cargo was first examined specifically to profile its tissue of origin. Although the mRNA species identified in our small RNA‐seq data are expected to be frequently fragmented, many retain informative tissue‐specific signatures. Thus, analysis of mRNA cargo—even in its fragmented form—provides valuable insights into the tissue origin and potential function of sEVs. The mRNA cargo profile exhibited distinct clusters of transcripts that were differentially present in sEVs across pre‐ and post‐VSG time points. The heat map reveals a gradual shift in expression patterns from before VSG (left) to after VSG (right), suggesting that VSG influences RNA loading into sEVs by affecting the cells and tissues from which they originate, thereby reshaping the small RNA cargo profile (Figure S2a).

Using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database, we classified the mRNA cargo based on tissue specificity (Table S2). Notably, the mean abundance of liver‐derived mRNA cargo (Liver‐RNA cargo) was significantly reduced post VSG (Figure 2d and Table S3), suggesting that the liver's mRNA cargo profile is highly sensitive to the metabolic changes induced by VSG. In contrast, this pattern was not observed for sEV mRNA cargo derived from adipose tissue (Figure 2e and Table S4), skeletal muscle, or other organs (Figure 2f and Figure S1). These findings indicate that VSG quantitatively modulates the mRNA cargo profile of circulating sEVs, with the liver emerging as one of the most responsive organs. The data highlight the potential role of liver‐derived sEVs in systemic metabolic regulation and underscore the liver's unique responsiveness to VSG.

In addition to mRNA cargo, we also examined the miRNA content of serum sEVs. The effect of VSG on the miRNA cargo profile appeared less substantial, with more modest and variable changes observed over time (Figure S2b). Nonetheless, using a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Page's trend test, we identified a subset of miRNAs that were significantly altered post surgery, including increased levels of miR‐22‐5p, miR‐27b‐5p, miR‐184, miR‐150‐3p, miR‐345‐5p, and miR‐487b‐3p (Figure S2c) and decreased levels of miR‐1180‐3p and miR‐576‐5p (Figure S2d). To explore their potential biological relevance, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using miRPath v4.0 [39] with the “Genes Union” method. The top 25 significantly enriched pathways (FDR < 0.05) included several signaling pathways implicated in cancer, infection, and immune regulation (Figure S2e). These results suggest that the altered miRNA cargo may influence diverse cellular pathways relevant to metabolic and inflammatory responses post surgery.

Post‐VSG sEV‐RNA Modulation and Metabolic Links

Given that the Liver‐RNA cargo in circulating sEVs was significantly decreased post VSG (Figure 2d) and displayed trends similar to metabolic improvements, such as reduction in BMI (Figure 1a), we next examined the correlation between Liver‐RNA cargo and various metabolic parameters over time. A similar correlation analysis was performed for other metabolic tissue‐specific sEV RNA levels, focusing on small RNA cargo from adipose tissue‐specific genes (Adipo‐RNA cargo), as previous studies have highlighted the association between adipocyte‐derived sEVs and metabolic parameters in obesity [42, 43]. At the pre‐surgery time point, the Liver‐RNA cargo showed no correlations with metabolic parameters such as BMI, leptin, or resistin levels (Figure S3a,b). However, at 6 months post VSG, Liver‐RNA cargo became positively correlated with both BMI and leptin (Figure 3a,b) and, interestingly, negatively correlated with resistin (Figure 3c), all with statistical significance. Adipo‐RNA cargo showed no pre‐surgery correlations with BMI, leptin, or resistin (Figure S3d–f). Post VSG, Adipo‐RNA cargo remained uncorrelated with BMI or resistin at 6 months (Figure 3d,f). However, the Adipo‐RNA cargo developed a significant negative correlation with leptin at 6 months post VSG (Figure 3e). Notably, these trends were not observed at 3 months post VSG. These findings suggest that the hepatic sEV RNA system may play a critical role in regulating metabolic improvements following VSG.

Differential Effects of Adipose and Liver sEVs on Metabolism and Liver Function Post VSG

To further investigate the roles of liver‐ and adipose‐derived sEVs, we performed correlation analyses with HOMA‐IR, a key indicator of insulin resistance that decreases following VSG (Figure 4a). No significant correlations were observed between Liver‐ or Adipo‐RNA cargo levels and HOMA‐IR, either before or after surgery (Figure S4). We then analyzed the delta (Δ) values, calculated as the difference between 6‐month post‐surgery and pre‐surgery measurements, to assess how changes in small RNA cargo levels correlate with improvements in insulin resistance. ΔHOMA‐IR values were positively correlated with ΔAdipo‐RNA cargo (Figure 4c), suggesting that adipose‐derived sEVs may contribute to improved insulin sensitivity following VSG. In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between ΔLiver‐RNA cargo and ΔHOMA‐IR (Figure 4b). Of note, these trends were not observed at 3 months after VSG. These results suggest a specific involvement of adipose‐derived sEVs in enhancing both insulin sensitivity and liver function following VSG, while liver‐derived sEVs may play a distinct, nonoverlapping metabolic role.

A similar pattern was observed with ALT, a marker of liver damage that decreased post VSG (Figure 4d). While adipose‐derived sEVs appear to be associated with liver function (Figure 4f), liver‐derived sEVs do not show such an association (Figure 4e), even though both Liver‐RNA cargo and ALT levels were significantly decreased post VSG (Figures 2e and 4d). This observation may be attributed to the potential role of sEVs in regulating the function of recipient cells, even in distant organs. To delve deeper, we investigated whether liver‐derived sEVs might be linked to parameters related to other metabolic organs, including adipose tissue. This is relevant because Liver‐RNA cargo was associated with leptin and resistin levels, both of which are predominantly secreted by adipose tissue.

Liver‐Derived sEV‐RNA Links to BCAA Metabolism

To further investigate the relationship between Liver‐ and Adipo‐RNA cargo and other metabolic parameters associated with obesity and diabetes, we next focused on BCAAs, which are primarily metabolized in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle [18, 19]. BCAA levels, including combined BCAAs (Figure 5a), valine (Figure 5d), leucine (Figure S5a), and isoleucine (Figure S5d), were quantified from serum samples collected from the same cohort using UHPLC coupled with high‐resolution mass spectrometry. All three BCAAs showed a statistically significant reduction post VSG based on paired analysis. Given these reductions, we conducted correlation analyses between changes in BCAA levels (ΔBCAA) and changes in RNA cargo. Notably, ΔBCAA, ΔValine and ΔLeucine were positively correlated with ΔLiver‐RNA cargo (Figure 5b,e and Figure S5b), whereas no such correlation was observed with ΔAdipo‐RNA cargo (Figure 5c,f and Figure S5c). While isoleucine levels decreased significantly post VSG (Figure S5d), similar to valine and leucine, ΔIsoleucine did not correlate with either ΔLiver‐RNA cargo, despite a nonsignificant positive trend, or ΔAdipos‐RNA cargo (Figure S5e,f). These findings suggest that liver‐derived sEVs may contribute to systemic metabolism regulation through their associations with BCAA metabolism, in addition to the link with adipose tissue‐secreted hormones (Figure 3a,c), highlighting distinct interorgan communication pathways mediated by sEVs post VSG (Figure 6).

Discussion

Bariatric surgery promotes robust weight loss and rapid and dramatic metabolic improvements in patients with obesity [1, 2, 3, 4]. VSG is the most frequently performed bariatric procedure in adolescents due to its effectiveness and favorable safety profile [4]. Growing evidence suggests that these metabolic benefits result from enhanced interorgan communication, with several molecular mechanisms identified as contributors, including alterations in the bile acid profile, gut microbiome, hormones, and adipokines [5, 16, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50]. However, the role of sEVs, particularly their small RNA cargo, remains underexplored despite their emerging importance as mediators of interorgan communication and metabolic regulation. Research into sEVs is essential for understanding how VSG drives systemic metabolic changes. sEVs transport bioactive molecules, such as RNA, facilitating cross talk between distant tissues, thereby influencing metabolic functions. Investigating the small RNA cargo within key metabolic tissue‐derived sEVs, such as liver and adipose tissue, offers valuable insights into how VSG modulates organ‐specific and systemic metabolism.

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing a reduction in Liver‐RNA cargo in sEVs following VSG in adolescents with obesity. Although previous studies have reported a reduction in liver‐derived sEVs following VSG [35, 36], our findings suggest that VSG influences sEV secretion and small RNA cargo profiles in a tissue‐specific manner, with distinct metabolic effects on the liver and adipose tissue. Notably, the sEV mRNA cargo representing other tissues, such as skeletal muscle and the gastrointestinal tract, did not show a similar trend in this cohort. These data indicate that the liver and adipose tissue are key metabolic organs responding to VSG by modulating their sEV cargo system in adolescents with obesity.

One of the key findings from our study is the emergence of statistically significant correlations between Liver‐RNA cargo and Adipo‐RNA cargo with key metabolic parameters, including BMI, leptin, and resistin, at 6 months post VSG (Figure 3), which were absent at baseline. Notably, while VSG did not have a robust effect on Adipo‐RNA cargo levels (Figure 2f), a significant correlation between Adipo‐RNA cargo and leptin levels was observed at 6 months post VSG (Figure 3e). Conversely, Liver‐RNA cargo levels were reduced following VSG, in parallel with decreases in BMI, leptin, and resistin (Figure 1). While no significant correlations were observed between Liver‐RNA cargo and these metabolic parameters prior to surgery, significant correlations emerged at 6 months post VSG. This shift suggests that VSG may re‐establish or strengthen the physiological association between liver‐derived sEVs and systemic metabolic signals, potentially reflecting a correction of disrupted interorgan communication between the liver and adipose tissue. These findings support the idea that liver‐ and adipose‐derived sEVs contribute to the metabolic benefits of VSG through distinct, yet complementary, mechanisms. Specifically, liver‐derived sEVs may be regulated in response to metabolic adaptations occurring within the liver, leading to altered sEV secretion following VSG. These sEVs may, in turn, modulate adipose tissue function—such as the secretion of leptin and resistin—rather than directly influencing systemic insulin sensitivity (Figure 6).

In contrast, adipose‐derived sEV RNA cargo may play a more active role in regulating insulin sensitivity, including both hepatic and adipose responses. Although the overall levels of Adipose‐RNA cargo remained relatively unchanged after VSG (Figure 2e), changes in Adipose‐RNA cargo (ΔAdipo‐RNA cargo) between pre and post VSG positively correlated with improvements in HOMA‐IR (ΔHOMA) and ALT (ΔALT) (Figure 4c,f). This highlights the functional relevance of adipose‐derived sEVs in metabolic recovery, despite minimal changes in total RNA abundance. These findings suggest that qualitative alterations—rather than quantitative changes—in adipose‐derived sEV cargo may contribute to the rapid improvement in insulin resistance [8, 51, 52], a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes, and enhanced liver function following VSG [4]. Notably, this correlation was not observed with changes in Liver‐RNA cargo (ΔLiver‐RNA cargo) (Figure 4), further supporting the tissue‐specific contributions of sEVs. These interpretations are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that adipose‐derived sEVs influence systemic, including hepatic, insulin sensitivity [53, 54], whereas liver‐derived sEVs predominantly facilitate intrahepatic communication [55]. Moreover, the re‐establishment of correlations between sEVs—both liver‐ and adipose‐derived—and key metabolic parameters after VSG may reflect the restoration of interorgan communication, which could be a critical driver of the observed metabolic improvements. In summary, our data underscore the distinct metabolic roles of liver‐ and adipose‐derived sEVs following VSG and suggest that VSG not only mitigates tissue‐specific dysfunction but also enhances interorgan communication—an essential component of systemic metabolic homeostasis (Figure 6).

Individuals with obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes typically exhibit higher plasma BCAA levels compared to metabolically healthy individuals [56, 57]. While skeletal muscle has traditionally been considered the primary site of BCAA catabolism, emerging evidence highlights a critical role for adipose tissue in regulating BCAA metabolism and systemic metabolic homeostasis. Impaired BCAA catabolism, particularly within adipose tissue, can lead to BCAA accumulation and has been implicated in the development of insulin resistance [58, 59, 60]. A recent meta‐analysis further confirmed that bariatric surgery lowers plasma BCAA levels in association with metabolic improvements [61]. Recent studies increasingly suggest that adipose tissue dysfunction in BCAA metabolism may be a key driver of insulin resistance and other metabolic diseases [58], underscoring the importance of adipose tissue as an active metabolic organ beyond lipid storage. Our findings raise the possibility that liver‐derived sEVs may influence BCAA metabolism in adipose tissue (and potentially skeletal muscle), or conversely, that circulating BCAA levels may influence the biogenesis or cargo composition of liver‐derived sEVs. Further investigation is warranted to clarify the interplay between liver‐derived sEVs and BCAA metabolism, and their combined role in systemic metabolic regulation.

In our study, the positive correlation between ΔLiver‐RNA cargo and changes in BCAA levels, with no similar association for ΔAdipo‐RNA cargo, highlights the liver's distinct role in BCAA metabolism after VSG. These findings suggest that interorgan communication via the sEV system plays a critical role in modulating insulin resistance and BCAA metabolism, contributing to the metabolic improvements observed after surgery (Figure 6). Together, these results emphasize the complementary roles of liver‐ and adipose‐derived sEVs in post‐VSG metabolic recovery. The liver appears to regulate BCAA metabolism, while adipose tissue contributes to improvements in insulin sensitivity. This coordinated response through the sEV system suggests that VSG restores molecular interactions and promotes metabolically healthy physiology, both at the tissue level and through interorgan communication.

sEVs carry various species of small RNA. In this study, we focused specifically on fragmented mRNA carried by sEVs, with the goal of leveraging them as tissue‐specific biomarkers of metabolic changes associated with bariatric surgery. Although the mRNA identified in sEVs is often fragmented, it often retains strong tissue specificity [62]. While it remains unclear whether these mRNA fragments are translated or fully functional in the recipient cells [62], their presence in sEVs provides valuable insights into tissue‐specific gene expression patterns and potential intertissue communication. Importantly, a growing body of evidence supports the use of mRNA fragments within sEVs as biomarkers in diverse physiological and pathological contexts [63]. In addition to mRNA cargo, we observed that a subset of miRNA cargo was significantly altered following surgery. Several of the increased miRNAs (e.g., miR‐22‐5p, miR‐27b‐5p, and miR‐184) have been implicated in metabolic and inflammatory regulation [64, 65, 66], while miR‐1180‐3p and miR‐576‐5p, which were decreased, are associated with obesity‐related inflammation and infection [67, 68]. Although the tissue origin of these miRNAs remains uncertain due to their broadly overlapping expression patterns, these changes may reflect sEV‐mediated modulation of recipient cell function that contributes to metabolic improvement associated with VSG. Together, our study highlights that analysis of mRNA and other small RNA cargo, including miRNAs, in sEVs provides complementary insight into systemic molecular adaptations and represents a promising strategy for identifying biomarkers and mediators of interorgan communication.

The strengths of this study include a relatively large cohort of adolescents with obesity undergoing bariatric surgery, longitudinal data capturing changes in key metabolic parameters following VSG, and the analyses of tissue‐specific mRNA cargo in circulating sEVs using an innovative small RNA preparation protocol. Investigating metabolic health in youth provides unique scientific insights that may not be fully captured in adult studies. Metabolic complications often manifest more aggressively in younger individuals, and children and adolescents tend to experience greater metabolic improvements following bariatric surgery compared to adults. These distinct responses may be influenced by ongoing growth, hormonal changes, and developmental plasticity. Furthermore, childhood obesity is highly associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in adulthood [69], underscoring the importance of understanding the mechanisms of metabolic derangement associated with childhood obesity to mitigate lifelong metabolic risks. Despite this clinical significance of and important differences in metabolic health in youth, data on post‐VSG changes in biological function remain limited in the youth population. Our study, which reports changes in small RNA cargo profile in sEVs following VSG in adolescents, offers a novel opportunity to identify noninvasive, tissue‐specific, and accurate biomarkers of obesity‐related metabolic complications at an early stage. These biomarkers could be leveraged to optimize early intervention strategies and reduce the long‐term risk of adverse outcomes, such as cardiovascular diseases.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, limitations in sample availability, particularly inconsistent data across time points, resulted in varying numbers of paired data points for statistical analyses. This affected the interpretation of sEV small RNA cargo and clinical data and limited correlation analyses with metabolic and inflammatory markers. In particular, the small number of male participants precluded robust gender‐stratified comparisons, restricting our ability to assess potential sex‐based differences in sEV RNA cargo. Second, the effect of post‐VSG weight loss was not separated from the analysis of sEV small RNA cargo or metabolic parameters; therefore, we cannot conclude that these observations are entirely independent of weight loss itself. Third, while we used information on mRNA cargo to infer the tissue specificity of sEV origin, the role of tissue‐specific sEVs warrants further investigation using complementary approaches, such as analysis of protein cargos or the use of animal models that enable direct assessment of tissue‐specific sEVs. Finally, this study was conducted at a single center, and its observational design limits both the generalizability of the findings and the ability to draw causal conclusions.

In conclusion, our study provides novel evidence that VSG modulates the sEV cargo of the liver and highlights the interaction between liver‐ and adipose‐derived sEV small RNA cargo and metabolic function. These findings suggest that VSG may restore molecular communication essential for metabolic improvements through the sEV system. These findings underscore the importance of sEV‐mediated interorgan communication in metabolic regulation and highlight the distinct contributions of liver‐ and adipose‐derived sEVs to BCAA metabolism and insulin resistance. It is worth noting that the liver‐sEV system, more so than the adipose‐sEV system, appears to be linked to the metabolic improvements following VSG in this cohort, suggesting the presence of weight loss‐independent mechanisms of metabolic regulation associated with VSG. Future research should explore the functional significance of these interactions to develop highly targeted and noninvasive therapeutic strategies for correcting metabolic dysfunction in obesity.
