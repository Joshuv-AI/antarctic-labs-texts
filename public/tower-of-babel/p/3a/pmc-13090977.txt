Breeding of a higher producer enables identification of a negative transcription factor controlling the biosynthesis of xiamenmycin in Streptomyces xiamenensis
Authors: Wen-Yan He, Jia-Xin Li, Xu-Liang Bu, Run-Tan Cheng, Lei Wang, Sheng-Ce Tao, Jun Xu, Min-Juan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractXiamenmycin, a prenylated benzopyran compound produced by Streptomyces xiamenensis 318, exhibits promising anti-fibrotic activity but suffers from low productivity, limiting its pharmaceutical development. In this study, we obtained a mutant strain N-8 producing 226.3 mg/L xiamenmycin B (28-fold increase relative to the parent strain MT-XN) via N-methyl-N′-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine mutagenesis. Further iterative ARTP treatments yielded mutant strain A5-8 with a titer of 429.4 mg/L. This screening was achieved using a fused antibiotic marker (neo) downstream of the xiamenmycin biosynthetic genes, which enabled kanamycin resistance-based selection. Furthermore, intracellular precursors of xiamenmycin were increased, as indicated by transcriptome analysis to be due to upregulation of biosynthetic pathway genes. Genome re-sequencing revealed deletions of ximA and a 138 bp upstream region of ximB promoter in strain N-8. In situ complementation of the 138 bp region significantly reduced xiamenmycin B production. Mechanistically, we identified a TetR-family transcription factor, XimN1, that specifically binds to the upstream of the ximB promoter and exerts a negative regulatory effect. Deletion of this region alleviated XimN1-mediated repression, upregulates ximBCDE transcription, and enhances xiamenmycin B production. The upstream region also contains the binding sites for AdpA. EMSA analysis further revealed that both proteins bind within the 138 bp region. In summary, we successfully constructed high-yield xiamenmycin mutants and elucidated a novel trans-acting transcription factor, XimN1 control xiamenmycin biosynthesis. This study presents practical strategies for improving the production efficiency of metabolites and offers novel insights into the transcriptional regulatory underlying secondary metabolism in Streptomyces.Keywords: Streptomyces, Benzopyrans, TetR family, Transcription factor, AdpA

Introduction

Natural products are characterized by their diverse structures, which are a valuable source of bioactive molecules and have always attracted considerable attention in the field of medicine. Benzopyrans with complex structures, such as cannabinoid [1], salvinorin A [2], myrsinoic acid C [3] and daphnetin [4] are widely distributed in medicinal plants. They have attracted considerable interest for their bioactivity such as anti-proliferative [5], anti-inflammatory [3], neuro-protective [6], and for prophylaxis of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting [7]. Xiamenmycin is a prenylated benzopyran compound with a threonine substitution [8]. This structural feature contributes to its biological activity and potential as a lead compound, particularly in anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory applications [9,10].

To date, the production of benzopyrans has predominantly relied on either plant extraction [3,11] or chemical synthesis [12,13]. However, the chemical synthesis of xiamenmycin remains significantly challenging due to the presence of two stereocenters in 3-hydroxyl benzopyran skeleton. The synthetic reaction includes multi-step, low yield, and stringent requirement for chiral compound resolution and optical purity of intermediates [14]. Alternatively, xiamenmycin and its derivative compounds can be effectively biosynthesized by the producing strain Streptomyces xiamenensis 318 [15].

It is well established that the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in Streptomyces is tightly regulated by a hierarchical regulatory system that senses and responds to various environmental and physiological signals [16,17]. This system comprises cis-acting elements and trans-acting transcription factors (TFs), which together form a complex network to control the transcription of biosynthetic genes [18]. The promoter region located at the 5′ end of the genes, is a key cis-acting element that initiates gene transcription and receives regulatory signals [[19], [20], [21]]. Regarding TFs that regulate gene clusters involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis, examples include both cluster-situated TFs and pleiotropic TFs located outside the cluster [18,22]. In the case of xiamenmycin, its biosynthetic gene cluster contains five biosynthetic genes [23] and lacks a cluster-situated TF. Our previous study reported that the pleiotropic TF AdpA positively regulates the biosynthesis of xiamenmycin [24]. Despite this finding, the overall regulatory network governing xiamenmycin biosynthesis remains poorly understood and requires further investigation.

Random mutagenesis provides a powerful and practical method to improve the yield of bioactive compounds, with successful applications in the overproduction of FK520 (ascomycin) [25], astaxanthin [26], and ansamitocin P-3 [27]. However, the labor-intensive and tedious processes involved in strain screening present challenges due to the lack of efficient screening systems. Recently, a semi-rational method has been developed that utilizes the promoter of a key biosynthetic gene to drive the expression of a resistance reporter gene (ble) or fuses a reporter gene (neo) to the 3′ end of the biosynthetic core gene, serving as a proxy for production levels of the antibiotics of interest [28,29]. Strain screening has also been achieved using dual reporter systems, such as the co-expression of a chromogenic gene (idgs) and a resistance reporter gene (neo) with the core biosynthetic genes [30]. These reporter-based systems enable more efficient identification of high-yield mutants. Indeed, a variety of mutants with increased titers can be generated through a combination of conventional mutagenesis and rational screening strategies [31].

One of the drawbacks of using random mutagenesis is that it may introduce mutations in key transcriptional elements, such as promoter deletions or mutations in related TFs, disrupting transcriptional repression or activation. This can consequently alter the transcriptional activity of the entire biosynthetic gene cluster, ultimately leading to changes in the yields of products [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Sufficient precursor supply is also a critical material prerequisite for natural product hyper-production [36]. Ideally, mutagenesis that reprograms primary metabolic fluxes could be selected, thereby redirecting carbon sources and energy toward the biosynthesis of precursors, providing adequate material support for the overexpression of biosynthetic genes [37]. Consequently, the synergistic effects of precursor enrichment and optimized transcriptional regulation drive the high-yield phenotype observed in mutants.

In this study, we employed a neo-reporter system to efficiently select high-yield strains through iterative rounds of random mutagenesis during strain breeding. Additionally, we screened and identified a novel pleiotropic TF associated with the high-yield phenotype and investigated its regulatory interaction with AdpA. To further understand the metabolic alterations in the mutants, we quantified the concentrations of intracellular intermediates and analyzed the transcription levels of biosynthetic genes, providing insights into the metabolic changes underlying enhanced xiamenmycin production.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, primers and growth conditions

The strains, plasmids and primers used in this study were listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. S. xiamenensis 318 and its mutants, S. coelicolor M145 and its mutants, S. coelicolor M1146 (M145ΔredΔactΔcdaΔcpk) [38] and its derivative strains were grown on mannitol/soya (MS) agar medium (comprising 20 g/L soybean flour, 20 g/L mannitol, 20 g/L agar) at 30 °C for spore suspension preparation and intergenic conjugation. Trypticase soy broth (TSB, 30 g/L) and GYMO optimized medium (15 g/L d-glucose, 10 g/L yeast extract, 15 g/L malt extract, pH 7.2–7.3) were used for seed culture and fermentation for S. xiamenensis.

The following medium were used for the fermentation of S. coelicolor. Seed medium consisted of the following: 10 g/L d-glucose, 5 g/L yeast extract, 5 g/L malt extract, 5 g/L peptone, 5 g/L NaCl, 1 g/L K2HPO4, and 0.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, with pH adjusted to 7.2-7.4. Fermentation medium consisted of the following: 20 g/L soluble starch, 20 g/L soybean flour, 10 g/L d-glucose, 5 g/L yeast extract, 5 g/L malt extract, 5 g/L peptone, 5 g/L NaCl, 1 g/L K2HPO4, 0.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, and 2 g/L CaCO3, with pH adjusted to 7.2–7.4.

AS-1 medium used for β-glucuronidase (GUS) assays consisted of 0.2 g/L l-lysine, 0.2 g/L l-arginine, 0.2 g/L l-asparagine, 5 g/L soluble starch, 1 g/L yeast extract, 2.5 g/L NaCl, 10 g/L Na2SO4 and 20 g/L agar.

E. coli DH5α was used as host for gene cloning. E. coil BL21(DE3) was used for protein expression. E. coli ET12567/pUZ8002 and S17-1 were served as plasmid donors in conjugative transfer [[39], [40], [41]]. E. coli cells were routinely cultivated in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. Antibiotics were added to the medium at the following concentrations: apramycin (50 μg/mL), kanamycin (50 μg/mL), chloramphenicol (50 μg/mL), hygromycin (25 μg/mL), thiostrepton (100 μg/mL), and nalidixic acid (25 μg/mL).

Mutagenesis and screening of high-yield strains for xiamenmycins

Spores of strain MT-XN were treated with 1 mg/mL N-methyl-N′-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine (NTG) for 1 h and precipitated by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and then washed once with saline solution. The spores were spread on MS agar plate and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, and then flooded with kanamycin solution at the final concentrations of 30 and 50 mg/L. A strain designated N-8 was obtained by selection on the medium containing 30 mg/L kanamycin (Fig. S1).

Subsequently, strain N-8 was subjected to multiple rounds of ARTP (Atmospheric Room Temperature Plasma, Wuxi Tmaxtree Biotechnology, P. R. China) mutagenesis treatment. The spore suspension (10 μL) of the strain N-8 was spread on a sterile steel plate and irradiated using ARTP at a radio-frequency (RF) power of 100 W, a gas flow of 10 standard liter per minute (SLM). The distance between the plasma torch nozzle exit and the sample plate was 2 mm [26]. After 60-s treatment at a lethality rate of 99% [42], the spores were spread on MS plate and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. After incubation at 30 °C for 36 h, the plates were flooded with kanamycin solution at varying final concentrations. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 5–7 days to allow the growth of kanamycin-resistant mutants. The titer of xiamenmycins from each mutant strain was analyzed by HPLC. The highest-yield producers were selected for subsequent rounds of ARTP mutagenesis using the same method.

HPLC measurement of xiamenmycins production

The fermentation broth (0.5 mL) was sampled from each flask. An equal volume of HPLC-grade methanol was added to the sample. The mixture was sonicated for 0.5 h, and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to remove the precipitate. The supernatant was analyzed by HPLC (SHIMADZU, Japan) with an Agilent XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm). UV detection was performed at 254 nm or 325 nm. Gradient elution was carried out using a mobile phase composed of (A) H2O containing 0.5‰ trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and (B) acetonitrile containing 0.5‰ TFA. The gradient program was as follows: 0 min at 25% B, 5 min at 25% B, and 30 min at 90% B, at a flow rate of 1 mL/min.

Determination of intracellular metabolites

Intracellular concentrations of 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (4-HBA) and dimethylallyl diphosphate/isopentenyl diphosphate (DMAPP/IPP) in strains MT-XN, N-8, and A5-8 were determined as previously described [27]. At each sampling time point, cells were immediately filtrated and quenched with the cold methanol/water solution (60% v/v, at −40 °C) for 5 min and then centrifuged at 9500 rpm for 2 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was ground in liquid nitrogen and suspended in 2.5 mL of cold methanol/water (50% v/v, at −40 °C) to extract 4-HBA. The suspension was vortexed for 1 min at 4 °C, frozen at −80 °C for 30 min and subjected to two additional freeze-thaw cycles. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 9500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. An additional 2.5 mL of cold methanol/water (50% v/v, at −40 °C) was added to the cell pellet. The supernatants were combined, lyophilized and stored at −80 °C.

The concentration of 4-HBA was analyzed by HPLC using an Agilent XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm) with UV detection at 254 nm. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (H2O with 0.5‰ TFA) and solvent B (methanol with 0.5‰ TFA) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The gradient programm was as follows: 0–5 min 20% B; 5–30 min 80% B. The authentic 4-HBA was purchased from J&K Chemical (Shanghai, P. R. China). The concentration of 4-HBA was quantified using a standard calibration curve (Fig. S2A).

To measure the concentration of DMAPP/IPP, the quenched cell pellet was mixed with 1.5 mL cold extraction solution (methanol: acetonitrile: water = 2:2:1, at −20 °C) [43,44]. The concentration of DMAPP/IPP was analyzed using an ACQUITY UPLC-Xevo TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (Waters, UK). Chromatographic separation was performed on a 2.1 × 100 mm Acquity UPLC® HSS T3 column. The mobile phase consisted of buffer A (5 mM ammonium formate, NH4FA) and buffer B (acetonitrile). The gradient program was as follows: 0 min 100% A, 1.5 min 100% A, 2 min 100% B, 4 min 100% B, at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The column temperature was 35 °C. Mass spectrometry detection was performed in electrospray negative mode using the transitions 244.968 > 78.900 m/z, 244.968 > 158.849 m/z and 244.968 > 226.932 m/z for DMAPP/IPP. The standards curve and production concentrations were analyzed by using TargetLynx software (Fig. S2B). Authentic DMAPP (95.0%) and IPP (95.0%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR assay (qRT-PCR)

To analyze gene expression, the strains MT-XN, N-8, N-8::ximBp+138 bp and A5-8, were cultured in GYMO liquid medium at 30 °C for 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 days. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and resuspended in PBS buffer. Total RNA was isolated using the RNAprep Pure Cell/Bacteria Kit (TIANGEN, P. R. China). The isolated RNA was treated with DNase I to remove contaminating DNA. Reverse transcription was performed using a cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, Japan).

qRT-PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara, Japan). The amplification program included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a final dissociation curve from 65 °C to 95 °C with a 0.5 °C/5 s increment. The housekeeping gene hrdB was used for normalization. Data was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method [45]. All experiments were conducted with three biological replicates and statistical analysis was performed using a Student's t-test.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the cells of strains MT-XN, N-8 and A5-8 using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) after culturing the cells in GYMO medium at 30 °C for 4 days. The quality of RNA was evaluated using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The concentration and purity of total RNA were detected using Qubit®3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA) and Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, USA). RNA sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 by Sinotech Genomics (Shanghai, P. R. China). Raw RNA-seq data were processed by eliminating low-quality raw reads and mapping them to the reference genome using Fastp and Bowtie 2. Gene expression levels were quantified using the FPKM method by Stringtie software. Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and a logarithmic fold change |log2FC| > 1 were considered differentially expressed. The raw sequencing data have been deposited to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (https://dataview.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/object/PRJNA1086886) under the accession number PRJNA1086886 and Submission ID: SUB14293993. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Screening of TFs using SPIDER assay

The 138 bp sequence of ximB promoter was divided into four 49 bp overlapping oligonucleotide fragments (B1–B4) (Fig. S3A). Biotinylated forward primers and unlabelled reverse primers were used to amplify these nucleotide fragments. The resulting biotinylated DNA fragments were used as bait (Fig. S3B).

A single colony of S. xiamenensis 318 was inoculated in 4 mL TSB broth and cultured to the logarithmic phase. It was then transferred to fresh TSB broth for 48 h of incubation. After incubation, cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 9000 rpm, resuspended in PBS buffer, and lysed by sonication. The protein lysate was collected by centrifugation (Fig. S3C).

Specific pupylation as IDEntity reporter (SPIDER) reaction was performed using the AbCode kit (Cat. No. AC24001) as described previously [46]. The peptide sequences were determined by searching MS/MS spectra against the protein database. Label-free quantification was used to quantify the difference in protein abundance, prey proteins with peptides ratio ≥2 and experimental to control abundance ratio >2 were selected as candidate proteins.

Construction of the gusA reporter systems for GUS assays

The gusA reporter system was employed to investigate the regulatory role of the candidate TFs identified through SPIDER screening [[47], [48], [49]]. The ximB promoter (ximBp) was amplified from the genomic DNA of S. xiamenensis 318 using primers denoted as ximBp-F/R. The gusA reporter gene was amplified from the plasmid pSET-hrdBp-gusA using primers denoted as gusA-F/R. The amplified ximBp and gusA fragments were fused into a single fragment (ximBp-gusA) by overlapping PCR. The ximBp-gusA fusion fragment was cloned into the integrative vector pSET152, which had been digested with XbaI/EcoRV, using Gibson assembly to generate the reporter plasmid pSET-ximBp-gusA. The plasmid was introduced into S. coelicolor M1146 via conjugation and integrated into the ФC31 attB site of the host genome by site-specific recombination. Exoconjugants were selected by inoculating them on MS agar plates supplemented with apramycin. This yielded the construction of the strain M1146/pSET-ximBp-gusA, which served as the negative control strain (Fig. S4A).

Meanwhile, the DNA fragment sxim_51910, under the control of the strong constitutive promoter ermEp∗, was cloned into the EcoRV/SpeI site of the plasmid pRT802, generating the plasmid pRT-ermEp∗-sxim_51910. The recombinant plasmid was introduced into the negative control strain S. coelicolor M1146/pSET-ximBp-gusA via conjugation. The plasmid was integrated into the ФBT1 attB site of the host genome by site-specific recombination. Exoconjugants were selected by inoculating them on MS agar plates supplemented with apramycin and kanamycin. The resulting recombinant strain, M1146/pSET-ximBp-gusA/pRT-ermEp∗-sxim_51910, harbored both XimN1 expression plasmid and the gusA reporter plasmid (Fig. S4B).

For gusA activity assay, spore suspensions of the negative control strain and the recombinant strain were collected, and 5 μL of each strain (OD450 = 0.5) were spotted onto AS-1 agar plates supplemented with 25 mg of 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-d-glucoronide (X-Gluc). After 3 days of growth, β-glucuronidase activity was visualized by the color development of blue precipitates, and photographs were taken. The gusA activity in the presence and absence of XimN1 was compared to evaluate the regulatory role of XimN1 in xiamenmycin biosynthesis.

Construction of gene deletion, complementation, and overexpression mutants

Two 1.5 kb DNA fragments corresponding to the upstream and downstream regions of the ximB promoter were individually amplified from the genomic DNA of S. xiamenensis 318. The 138 bp sequence of ximB promoter was amplified and inserted between the two homology arms to form a fusion fragment. The fusion fragment was cloned into the vector pJTU1278 at the EcoRI/HindIII restriction sites via Gibson assembly generating the plasmid pJTU-UD (ximBp+138 bp). The plasmid was transformed into ET12567/pUZ8002 and introduced into mutant N-8 via conjugation. Exoconjugants were selected on MS arga plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL thiostrepton and 25 μg/mL nalidixic acid. Single-crossover recombinants were isolated from antibiotic-resistant colonies and transferred to MS agar without antibiotics to allow for the resolution of double-crossover events. Double-crossover recombinants were confirmed by colony PCR and DNA sequencing. The resulting strain with 138 bp fragment knock into its natural locus at the ximB promoter was designated N-8::ximBp+138 bp.

The Δsxim_51910 mutant was generated via homologous recombination. The upstream and downstream homology arms of sxim_51910 were fused and cloned into the vector pJTU1278. The aac(3)IV resistance gene, along with its native promoter was inserted at the XbaI/SpeI restriction sites within the plasmid. The resulting gene disruption plasmid, designated pJTU-UD (sxim_51910), contained the upstream and downstream homologous arms flanking the aac(3)IV resistance cassette. The plasmid was subsequently introduced into S. xiamenensis 318 via conjugation, and exoconjugants were selected based on resistance to apramycin.

The sxim_51910 gene was amplified from S. xiamenensis 318 genomic DNA and cloned into vector pIB139 at the restriction sites NdeI/EcoRV using one-step cloning, resulting in the plasmid pIB-sxim_51910. The obtained plasmid was introduced into S. xiamenensis 318 and the Δsxim_51910 mutant by conjugation, generating the overexpression strain WT/pIB-sxim_51910 (OEsxim_51910) and complementation strain Δsxim_51910/pIB-sxim_51910 (Δsxim_51910::sxim_51910). The construction of the overexpression strain M145/pIB-sco7364 followed the same method as described above.

Protein expression and purification

The sxim_51910 and adpA gene fragments were amplified by PCR and cloned into the EcoRI/XhoI sites of the expression vector pET28a, resulting in the recombinant plasmids pET28a-sxim_51910 and pET28a-adpA, respectively. These plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for protein expression. Recombinant strains were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C with shaking until OD600 reached 0.4-0.6. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.2 mM isopropyl β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG), and the cultures were incubated at 16 °C for 16 h to promote expression of soluble recombinant proteins.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) and lysed by ultrasonication on ice. The lysate was centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 60 min at 4 °C. The supernatant, containing soluble proteins XimN1 and AdpA were collected separately. Both recombinant proteins contained a 6 × His-tag were purified using a nickel (Ni) affinity chromatography. Sequential elution was performed using buffer containing 15 mM, 50 mM, 300 mM, and 500 mM imidazole. The eluted fractions were desalted to remove excess imidazole and concentrated using ultrafiltration. The purified protein was assessed by SDS-PAGE analysis, confirming the presence of a single band corresponding to the expected molecular weight of XimN1 (26.4 kDa) and AdpA (42 kDa). The protein concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) and DNase I footprinting assays

EMSAs were performed between the purified proteins and Cy5-labeled DNA probes, following the previously described method [50]. The ximBp and 138 bp sequence were amplified from the genome of S. xiamenesis 318 by PCR using the primer pairs ximBp-F/R and ximBp-F/138 bp-R. Both forward and reverse primers contained a 16-bp universal sequence (AGCCAGTGGCGATAAG) at the 5′ ends. A second round of PCR was performed to incorporate Cy5 labels at the 5′ ends of the DNA fragment using Cy5-labeled universal primers. EMSAs were carried out by incubating the Cy5-labeled DNA probes with proteins in EMSA buffer, containing 100 mM Tris, 25% glycerol, 0.2 mg/mL BSA, pH 7.9, 1 M MgCl2, 0.5 M DTT and 2 μg/mL sonicated salmon sperm DNA at 25 °C for 20 min. The reaction mixture was electrophoresed on a 4.5% polyacrylamide gel in 0.5 × Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer (125 V for 50 min) at 4 °C. The gels were pre-run at 120 V for 30 min in 0.5 × TBE buffer and scanned with the Starion FLA-9000 Scanner (FujiFilm, Japan). DNase I footprinting assays were performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, P. R. China) following the method described previously [51].

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) assays

The binding affinity between proteins XimN1 and AdpA and their respective DNA targets (ximBp or 138 bp DNA fragments) was measured using the NanoTemper Monolith X instrument (NanoTemper Technologies, Germany). The Cy5-labeled DNA fragments served as the targets, and proteins acted as the ligands. A series of 16 dilutions (1:1) of each protein were prepared in the binding buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol and 0.05‰ Tween 20, pH 8.0), starting from an initial concentration of 300 μM for proteins. Each dilution sample was then mixed in a 1:1 ratio with Cy5-labeled DNA (100 nM) and incubated at room temperature. The mixture was loaded into Monolith X standard treated capillaries (NanoTemper Technologies, Germany) and analyzed for MST signal at 27 °C. The equilibrium dissociation constants (Kd) were evaluated and calculated using Monolith X analysis software (NanoTemper Technologies, Germany). The MST dose–response curves were normalized to Fnorm[‰].

5′-RACE for identification of the transcription start site (TSS)

To determine the TSS of ximB, 5′-rapid amplification of cDNA ends (5′-RACE) was performed using a 5′-RACE Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, P. R. China). Total RNA (2 μg) was extracted from S. xiamenensis 318 and reverse-transcribed into cDNA as described in the manufacturer's instruction manual. The finally PCR products were purified by a Gel Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, P. R. China) and directly sequenced using the primer ximB-R2. Sequencing results were analyzed using Snapgene (GSL Biotech LLC, Boston, USA), and the first nucleotide after a stretch of 14 consecutive guanines (poly(G)) was defined as the TSS.

Results

Screening of high-yield xiamenmycin producers using a xim-neo reporter system

The xim biosynthetic gene cluster consists of five genes (Table S3). Among them, ximBCDE form a co-transcribed operon responsible for biosynthesis of the benzopyran skeleton. The biosynthetic pathway is illustrated in Fig. 1A. A xim-neo reporter operon with an overlapping nucleotide between the TGA stop codon of ximE and the ATG start codon of neo was constructed (Fig. 1B). Integrated of xim-neo into the chromosome of a previously constructed xim gene cluster knockout strain (Δxim), generating the recombinant strain MT-XN [42]. This construction establishes a direct correlation between kanamycin resistance and the transcriptional activity of the xim gene cluster in strain MT-XN. Strain MT-XN (8.1 mg/L) was subjected to N-methyl-N′-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine (NTG) treatment, followed by screening for mutants exhibiting increased resistance to kanamycin. One mutant strain, N-8 which was selected on 30 mg/L kanamycin, was found to produce 226.3 mg/L xiamenmycin B, as determined by LC-MS (Fig. 1B and C, Fig. S5). In S. xiamenensis 318, xiamenmycin B was converted into xiamenmycin A via the addition of a threonine residue, catalyzed by the amide synthase XimA. This result suggests that a significant upregulation of ximBCDE, coupled with the downregulation of ximA may have occurred in strain N-8 due to the NTG treatment.

Deletion of the ximA gene and downstream 138 bp region enhances xiamenmycin B production in strain N-8

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the enhanced production of xiamenmycin B in the mutant strain N-8, the ximA and its flanking genomic regions were analyzed. Four DNA fragments (S1, S2, S3 and S4) encompassing the ximA and its flanking sequences were amplified from strains N-8 and MT-XN, respectively (Fig. S6A). Comparison of the PCR products and subsequent DNA sequencing analyses revealed a 4071 bp deletion that spans a 2370 bp fragment upstream of the ximA, a full-length 1563 bp ximA gene and a 138 bp fragment extending into the intergenic region between ximA and ximB (Fig. S6A and S6B). The absence of ximA in the strain N-8 explained the blocked conversion of xiamenmycin B to xiamenmycin A.

Moreover, knocking-in the 138 bp deleted fragment into the intergenic region between ximA and ximB in situ in strain N-8 generated a complemented strain N-8::ximBp+138 bp (Fig. 1B and Fig. S7). The titer of xiamenmycin B decreased to 63.8 mg/L in strain N-8::ximBp+138 bp (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that the deletion of the 138 bp intergenic region contributes to the enhanced biosynthesis of xiamenmycin B in the strain N-8.

Furthermore, we conducted quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) to compare the transcription levels of ximBCDE in the mutant strain N-8 and the complemented strain N-8::ximBp+138 bp. As shown in Fig. 1D, the transcription levels of each gene in the ximBCDE gene cluster were significantly upregulated in strain N-8 compared to MT-XN, with increase of approximately 54-fold, 52-fold, 50-fold, and 23-fold, respectively. In contrast, the transcription levels of the ximBCDE gene cluster in the complemented strain (N-8::ximBp+138 bp) were restored to nearly the levels observed in strain MT-XN. These findings indicate that the 138 bp intergenic region between ximA and ximB plays a critical role in the transcriptional regulation of xiamenmycin B biosynthesis.

Characterization of regulatory protein interacting with the 138 bp region

To explore the regulatory element associated with the 138 bp intergenic region, we employed the Specific Pupylation as IDEntity Reporter (SPIDER) system to capture proteins interacting with this region [46]. One such regulator, XimN1 was found to be highly enriched on the 138 bp DNA probe in vitro (Fig. S8). XimN1, encoded by sxim_51910, is annotated as a member of the TetR family regulatory protein. Homologues of XimN1 were identified in the StrepDB database using BlastP analysis (Table 1). Multiple sequence alignment of these TFs revealed a conserved “helix-turn-helix (HTH)” domain at the N-terminal region of this group of TetR family proteins (Fig. 2A).

We assessed the binding capacity of heterologously expressed XimN1 and its DNA target using the gusA reporter system in S. coelicolor M1146 (Fig. S9). The result demonstrated that the recombinant strain expressing XimN1 exhibited lighter coloration compared to the blue-stained negative control (Fig. 2B). This finding suggests that XimN1 can recognize the ximBp region and exert inhibitory activity in vivo.

Furthermore, we knocked out the sxim_51910 gene in S. xiamenensis 318 (Fig. S10). In the constructed mutant strain Δsxim_51910, the production of xiamenmycin A increased by 95.2% compared to S. xiamenensis 318. However, complementation of the sxim_51910 gene suppressed the enhanced xiamenmycin A production observed in the mutant Δsxim_51910 (Fig. 2C). Additionally, a gene overexpression strain, OEsxim_51910, was constructed by site-specific integration of sxim_51910 gene under the control of a constitutive promoter ermEp∗ (Fig. S11). Xiamenmycin A production in the mutant OEsxim_51910 was 47.3% lower than that in S. xiamenensis 318 (Fig. 2C). Therefore, we conclude that XimN1 negatively regulates the biosynthesis of xiamenmycin.

Other than XimN1, none of its homologues listed in Table 1 have been functionally characterized previously. Here, we investigated the role of XimN1-like protein encoded by sco7364 gene in the model strain S. coelicolor M145. Culture extracts from the wild-type S. coelicolor M145, the empty-vector control strain M145/pIB139 and the mutant strain M145/pIB-sco7364 were prepared after 7 days of incubation and analyzed by UPLC-MS. In the sco7364-overexpressing strain M145/pIB-sco7364, metabolic changes were observed, and the main peak in the chromatograms was inhibited (Fig. S12A). Mass spectrometric analysis revealed the molecular ion corresponding to 3,6-dihydroxy-1-methylanthracene-9,10-diketone, with a molecular formula of C15H10O4 (Fig. S12B), whose peak area was reduced by approximately twofold. It indicates that XimN1-like protein SCO7364 may also perform a negative regulatory function.

Identification of XimN1-binding site on ximB promoter

We determined the promoter structure of the intergenic region between ximA and ximB by identifying the TSS of ximB using 5′-rapid amplification of cDNA ends (5′-RACE). The TSS of ximB was located at a cytosine 134 nt upstream of its start codon (Fig. S13). A putative promoter was predicted based on the identified TSS (Fig. 3A), and it displayed features similar to the −35 and −10 regions found in reported Streptomyces promoters [52].

To further investigate the binding site of XimN1 in vitro, we performed EMSA and DNase I footprinting assays in the presence and absence of XimN1. The footprinting experiment showed a broader protected region of XimN1, which encompasses the upstream sequences of the −35 region of ximBp (Fig. 3A and B). EMSA results demonstrated that XimN1 bound to ximBp in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3C). Upon removal of the identified XimN1-protected region, subsequent EMSA analysis revealed no XimN1-DNA complex formation within the same concentration range of 0-5 μg XimN1 (Fig. 3D). This result suggests the specific binding of XimN1 to the upstream region of the ximBp.

Both XimN1 and AdpA bind to the upstream region of ximB promoter

TFs that typically bind to the downstream of the −35 and −10 regions of a promoter block RNA polymerase and repress gene transcription [[53], [54], [55]]. However, XimN1-binding site is located upstream of the −35 region of the ximBp, suggesting a distinct regulatory mechanism that differs from those of typical negative TFs. Size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200) analysis revealed that XimN1 exists as a dimer in solution (Fig. S14). The binding region of XimN1 overlaps with two previously identified binding sites of AdpA (located at −162 to −152 and −143 to −133) (Fig. 3A), which are known to positively regulate ximBCDE expression [24]. Therefore, we hypothesize that XimN1 interfers the positive regulation by AdpA and functions as a negative TF.

To investigate the potential interaction between XimN1 and AdpA, we performed EMSA using His6-XimN1 and His6-AdpA (Fig. S15) with the 138 bp DNA probe (Fig. 4A). When the concentration of XimN1 was gradually increased from 0 to 0.75 μg while maintaining a constant AdpA concentration of 0.75 μg, the AdpA-DNA complex band and a faint band corresponding to the XimN1-DNA-AdpA ternary complex were observed at low XimN1 concentrations. At 0.25 μg of XimN1, both bands weakened, and a slower-migrating band indicative of the ternary complex appeared. At ≥0.5 μg of XimN1, a distinctly far-shifted band corresponding to the ternary complex was clearly visible. To further confirm this, we performed EMSA using a fixed concentration of XimN1 (0.75 μg) and decreasing concentrations of AdpA (0.75-0 μg). As the AdpA concentration decreased, the migration of the far-shifted band corresponding to the XimN1-DNA-AdpA complex accelerated, and the XimN1-DNA band migrated more rapidly (Fig. 4B). MST analysis further quantified the binding affinities of two proteins with the 138 bp DNA probe. The equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) of XimN1 was 1.47 ± 0.49 μM (Fig. 4C), significantly lower than that of AdpA (10.40 ± 0.13 μM; Fig. 4D), indicating that XimN1 binds to the DNA probe with higher binding affinity.

These results suggest that both XimN1 and AdpA can bind to the 138 bp DNA probe, and that XimN1 binding is concentration-dependent and exhibits a titration effect in vitro. Based on these findings, we propose a schematic model illustrating the interactive regulatory mechanism involving the binding of XimN1 and AdpA to the upstream region of the ximB promoter (Fig. 4E).

Boosting xiamenmycin B yield through iterative ARTP mutagenesis

Strain N-8 was subjected to ARTP mutagenesis for the first round. A total of twenty-two kanamycin-resistant mutants were obtained. Among them, the highest-producing mutant was selected under a kanamycin selection pressure of 110 mg/L and yielded xiamenmycin B at 243.8 ± 20.5 mg/L (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, iterative rounds of ARTP mutagenesis were carried out (Fig. S1), with each round screened at gradually increasing kanamycin concentrations, ranging from 110 mg/L to 410 mg/L. A total of 229 mutants were selected, and the average xiamenmycin B yield progressively increased over five rounds of ARTP mutagenesis. The highest-yield mutant, designated A5-8, produced 429.4 ± 45.6 mg/L xiamenmycin B in shake-flask culture (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the stability of xiamenmycin B production in the high-yield strains N-8 and A5-8 was evaluated through serial subcultivation. Both strains maintained stable xiamenmycin B production after five rounds of sporulation without antibiotic selection (Fig. S16A–B), and showed no significant differences in growth behavior compared to the original strain MT-XN (Fig. S16C).

Deciphering the high-yield mechanism of mutants through metabolic and transcriptome analysis

Since 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (4-HBA) and dimethylallyl diphosphate/isopentenyl diphosphate (DMAPP/IPP) are two key precursors in the biosynthesis of xiamenmycin B, we investigated their intracellular concentrations. As shown in Fig. 5B, the 4-HBA levels in strains N-8 and A5-8 were higher than those in strain MT-XN. Notably, in strain N-8, intracellular 4-HBA reached its peak on day 4, followed by a gradual decrease. In contrast, the 4-HBA concentration in strain A5-8 increased continuously until day 5, promoting an additional sampling on day 6. Similarly, the concentrations of DMAPP/IPP in strain A5-8 were higher than those in strain N-8. The maximum intracellular level of DMAPP/IPP in strain A5-8 was observed on day 4 (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that the increased yield of xiamenmycin B in strain A5-8 is attributed to the elevated availability of the two precursors, which may result from random mutagenesis.

Transcriptome analysis revealed that genes expression patterns involved in precursors biosynthesis differed significantly between strains A5-8 and MT-XN, as well as between strains N-8 and MT-XN using RNA sequencing. As illustrated in Fig. 5D and Fig. S17, the transcription of genes encoding deoxyribose-phosphate aldolase and phosphoketolase in the pentose phosphate pathway and the gene encoding fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (FBP) synthase in the glycolysis pathway was upregulated in two mutants compared to the strain MT-XN (Tables S4 and S5). This upregulation correlated with an increased metabolic flux toward the methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway. Similarly, an increase in phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) directs it toward the shikimate pathway. Additionally, four synthase-encoding genes in the MEP pathway, responsible for GPP synthesis, were upregulated in strain A5-8. Both mutants showed upregulation of three synthase-encoding genes in the shikimate pathway that contribute to 4-HBA production. These transcriptional changes contributed to the development of two mutant strains.

Furthermore, we also validated the transcriptional levels of xiamenmycin B biosynthetic genes in strain A5-8 using qRT-PCR. The results indicated that, compared to strain N-8, several genes exhibited higher expression levels in strain A5-8 (Fig. S18). The ximC gene was upregulated starting from the day 1 and reached a 3.9-fold increase by day 4. The expression level of the ximB gene was 6.5-fold higher on day 1 compared to strain N-8. Moreover, the ximD and ximE genes were upregulated by 3- to 7-fold during the stationary phase. It suggests that iterative mutation further enhances the transcription level of biosynthetic genes.

Discussion

Although conventional mutagenesis and screening methods have been widely used for yield improvement, targeted and high-throughput screening plays a pivotal role in isolating high-yield strains. The reporter-based selection system was first implemented in Aspergillus terreus to identify the lovastatin-overproducing strains [28]. By fusing the lovastatin biosynthetic gene lovF promoter with the phleomycin resistance gene ble, it was demonstrated that increased resistance levels correlated with promoter activity could reliably indicate enhanced lovastatin production in A. terreus. This approach was subsequently adapted for natamycin production in S. gilvosporeus, where the neo resistance gene was fused to the 3′ end of the PKS gene sgnS1 to monitor both sgnS1 expression and natamycin yield [29]. To optimize mutant selection efficiency, scientists developed a dual reporter system by integrating the indigoidine synthetase gene idgS and neo at the upstream and downstream regions of NRPS genes. Remarkably, just a single round of mutagenesis yielded a 6.4-fold increase in daptomycin production, while simultaneously exhibiting reduced false-positive rates and superior operational efficiency compared to single-reporter systems [30]. These findings collectively demonstrate that integrating traditional mutagenesis with rationally designed reporter screening represents an effective strategy for strain improvement.

Through mechanism analysis of the high-yield strain effectively selected using the neo reporter system following NTG mutagenesis, a key role of a transcriptional regulatory element was revealed, leading to the identification of a TetR-family TF XimN1. TetR family proteins have been reported to bind to palindromic or consensus binding motifs [[56], [57], [58]]. For example, BkdR specifically binds to the palindromic sequences, GTTAGCGGTCGTTAAC and GTTAACGGTCATTAAC, within the promoter region of the pccAB operon [59]. As another example, NemR binds to the 18 bp conserved sequence (consensus, 5′-TGGGGTGTATAGGGGGTA-3′) in PA4 [60]. However, the footprinting assay reveals that the protected region of XimN1 spans a relatively wider region, and no obvious binding motif is identified. To precisely map the binding motif, additional experimental approaches such as protein-DNA co-crystallization or chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays may be required [61]. Furthermore, since TetR family TFs are widespread among most Streptomyces species, manipulating XimN1-like proteins to remove negative transcriptional regulation may activate silent gene clusters in these microbes.

As a pleiotropic TF that governs morphological differentiation and secondary metabolism in Streptomyces, AdpA frequently interacts with other TFs by binding cooperatively or competitively to the promoter regions of target genes, thereby enabling precise control of gene expression. For example, AdpA and ClgR can bind simultaneously to the clpP1 promoter and co-regulate the expression of the ClpP protease genes, with ClgR functioning as the primary TF and AdpA acting as an auxiliary TF [62]. In S. roseosporus, the binding sites of PhoP and AdpA on the atrA promoter (atrAp) partially overlap, resulting in competitive binding between the two TFs [63]. In our study, the AdpA-binding site overlaps with the XimN1-binding region, with both proteins targeting the ximB promoter. However, the functional interplay between these two TFs remains elusive. Given that XimN1 exhibits dispersed binding across the 138 bp promoter region, we cannot rule out the involvement of additional TFs binding to this region. Potential TFs identified by SPIDER—such as SXIM_29330, SXIM_08240, and SXIM_21240—may contribute synergistically to this regulatory network.

XimN1 binds to the ximB promoter and exerts negative transcriptional regulation for xiamenmycin B biosynthesis. However, disruption of the sxim_51910 gene did not significantly enhance xiamenmycin B production in S. xiamenensis 318, as observed in the mutant N-8 derived from MT-XN. Meanwhile, the in situ complementation of the 138 bp intergenic region in strain N-8 significantly decreased xiamenmycin B yield to 63.8 mg/L, which was still higher than the 8.1 mg/L observed in the strain MT-XN [42]. This discrepancy may be attributed to a series of mutations in the N-8 genome induced by NTG mutagenesis, including single nucleotide variations (SNVs) and insertions or deletions (InDels) (Table S6), which may enhance the metabolic flux toward xiamenmycin B biosynthesis.

In summary, through random mutagenesis combined with resistance screening of an engineered S. xiamenensis strain MT-XN, which is equipped with biosynthetic gene cluster and resistance marker, we isolated high-yield strains, N-8 and A5-8, that produces dramatically higher levels of xiamenmycins. By performing comprehensive metabolic engineering on strain A5-8 and subsequently conducting optimized fermenter cultivation, we anticipated a substantial improvement in xiamenmycin production, thereby ensuring a reliable supply of this compound for future drug development.
