Performance of optic nerve sheath diameter for predicting mortality in patients with traumatic brain injury: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhi Wei Zhang, Jun Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study explored the performance of optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) for predicting mortality in patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI).Materials and Methods:PubMed, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure databases were searched until October 2025 for studies on the prediction of ONSD for mortality in TBI. The pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and area under the curve (AUC) were used. Subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and Deek test were performed to assess ONSD’s performance in mortality prediction.Results:The systematic search yielded 6 articles involving 516 patients with TBI. A broad ONSD was associated with an increased mortality (OR = 21.86, 95% CI: 12.26–38.99, P < .001), and the AUC of ONSD for predicting mortality in patients with TBI was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.90), showing 86% sensitivity and 78% specificity. Subgroup analysis revealed that ages, gender, study design, sample size, publication location, and publication years had no important effects on heterogeneity. The pooled results were robust according to the sensitivity analysis. Deeks’ test showed no evident publication bias in this meta-analysis (P = .19).Conclusion:This meta-analysis demonstrated that ONSD is a useful predictive method for mortality in patients with TBI.

1. Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a temporary or permanent impairment of brain function due to an external mechanical force to the head.[1] TBI is classified as follows: focal brain damage due to a contact injury causing laceration, contusion, and/or intracranial hemorrhage; and diffuse brain damage from acceleration/deceleration injury, leading to brain swelling and/or diffuse axonal injury.[2–4] According to global epidemiological statistics, the yearly global incidence of TBI is approximately 50 million cases, and the incidence of TBI has been increasing annually.[5] TBI may lead to substantial tissue damage with irreversible functional loss and disrupt intricate neural circuits and connections involved in cognitive and sensor–motor functions.

The optic nerve is ensheathed by 3 layers of meninges (the dura, arachnoid, and pia meninga), collectively known as the optic nerve sheath. The space between the sheath and optic nerve is the continuation of the brain space, which is filled with cerebrospinal fluid. Increases in intracranial pressure are transmitted to the retrobulbar region of the optic nerve sheath via cerebrospinal fluid and leading to distention of the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD).[6] To date, the ONSD can be considered a valuable predictor for mortality in TBI. However, conclusions are inconsistent, the area under the curve (AUC) ranges from 0.722 to 0.884,[7–12] and a systematic review is lacking. To address this discrepancy, the present study aimed to determine whether the ONSD is associated with mortality in patients with TBI.

2. Materials and methods

We performed this systematic review and meta-analysis according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis statement.

2.1. Literature search

Electronic databases, namely, PubMed, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure, were systematically searched from the date of their inception until October 2025 without applying any language filter. The keywords used for the search included “optic nerve sheath diameter,” “traumatic brain injury,” and “mortality.” The reference lists of the identified literature were used to manually search for potentially relevant literature. Two independent investigators conducted the study selection process, and any disagreements were resolved in consultation with the participation of a third reviewer.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following studies were included: studies involving individuals aged > 18 years, published studies on the ONSD and mortality in patients with TBI, and studies with sufficient data for calculating odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The following studies were excluded: nonclinical diagnostic research, such as basic research, review, case report, letter, comment, and meeting summary, and repetitively published studies.

2.3. Data extraction

Two reviewers independently searched and reviewed the title and abstract of each study. Potentially eligible studies were flagged, and full texts were obtained. The following details of the included trials were extracted independently: first author, publication year, age, gender, study region, study design, sample size, and mortality. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus.

2.4. Quality assessment

The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Study-2 (QUADAS-2) tool was used to assess the risk of bias and applicability of each selected study. The QUADAS-2 format involves 4 domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. For each domain, risk of bias and concerns about applicability (the latter does not apply to the domain of flow and timing) were analyzed and rated as of low, high, or unclear. Two reviewers independently piloted the questions to determine between-reviewer agreement. Disagreements between 2 reviewers were resolved by discussion and consensus.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Stata 14.0 software (StataCorp LP, College Station). ORs with 95% CIs were used to calculate ONSD pooled sensitivity and specificity in mortality. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Chi-squared-based Q statistic method and I2, and the level of significance was indicated by P and I2 values of <.05 and >50%, respectively. A random-effects model was used when heterogeneity existed between the studies. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. Factors causing heterogeneity were identified through subgroup analysis, and the robustness of the pooled results was assessed through sensitivity analysis. Publication bias was evaluated with Deeks’ test.

3. Results

3.1. Literature search

A total of 52 studies were identified from electronic databases, and 10 additional records were retrieved through screening article references. After excluding 26 duplicate articles, 36 articles were screened. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 11 full-test articles remained for additional investigation. Two papers were deemed irrelevant (not designed for the outcomes of mortality), and 3 studies were excluded because of unavailable data. Finally, 6 studies[7–12] were included (Fig. 1).

The assessment summary of this study performed using QUADAS-2 is shown in Figure 2. Concerning patient selection, 67% of the studies were identified as having an unclear risk of bias because they did not mention consecutive patients. In the index test, 100% of the studies had a high or unclear risk of bias because they did not mention whether blinding was implemented or did not use a prespecified cutoff. Among the aforementioned index tests, 83% were classified as high risk, and 16% were classified as having an unclear risk status. Applicability concerns were low in all studies across the domains of patient selection, index test, and reference standard.

3.4. ONSD for mortality

The pooled analysis of ORs and their corresponding 95% CIs extracted from the included studies indicated that a broad ONSD was associated with high mortality in patients with TBI (OR = 21.86, 95% CI: 12.26–38.99, P < .001) without obvious heterogeneity (I2 = 49.3%, P = .079), using a fixed-effects model (Fig. 3). The area under curve (AUC) of ONSD for predicting mortality in patients with TBI was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.90), the pooled sensitivity was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.78–0.92), and the pooled specificity was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.72–0.83, Fig. 4). The pooled positive likelihood ratio was 3.8 (95% CI: 2.9–5.0), whereas the negative likelihood ratio was 0.18 (95% CI: 0.11–0.30).

3.5. Subgroup analysis

To explore potential heterogeneity, we performed a subgroup analysis stratified by age (≤50 years or > 50 years), gender (≤60% or > 60%), study design (prospective or retrospective), sample size (≤100 or > 100), publication location (Asia or non-Asia), and publication year (before 2016 or after 2016). The results showed that age, gender, study design, sample size, publication location, and publication year had no important effects on heterogeneity (Fig. 5).

3.6. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

No single study influenced the pooled OR and 95% CI, indicating the robustness of the results of the meta-analysis for mortality (Fig. 6). Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test did not reveal any evidence of publication bias in the present meta-analysis (P = .19, Fig. 7).

4. Discussion

Despite considerable improvements in medical care for serious emergencies, TBI remains a major threat to life, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Overall mortality in patients with TBI ranges from 12% to 38.5%,[13–16] consistent with that (36.7%) in our study. The present study is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that assessed the predictive performance of ONSD for mortality in patients with TBI. The AUC of ONSD for predicting mortality in patients with TBI was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.90), indicating that the ONSD possessed strong predictive performance for mortality in these patients.

The optic nerve is surrounded by meninges and the subarachnoid space, exhibiting elastic properties. A small subarachnoid space (0.1–0.2 mm) separates the dural sheath and white matter, facilitating communication with the subarachnoid space surrounding the brain.[17] Through this communication, cerebrospinal fluid can transfer freely between the intracranial and intraorbital subarachnoid spaces.[18] Thus, an increase or decrease in pressure within the intracranial subarachnoid space should translate to the same pressure changes within the optic nerve sheath.[19] When intracranial pressure increases after brain injury, the sheath expands, and changes in the diameter of the nerve sheath can be visualized through imaging examinations.

A broad ONSD is closely related to mortality. Waheed et al demonstrated a robust relationship between ONSD in patients who were alive (3.7 mm ± 1.0 mm) and in deceased patients (4.7 mm ± 1.1 mm, P < .01).[10] Sekhon et al presented a retrospective cohort study of 220 patients with TBI and demonstrated an association between elevated ONSD and mortality.[20] The result showed that each 1 mm increase in ONSD was associated with a 2-fold increase in hospital mortality (OR = 2.0, 95% CI: 1.2–3.2, P = .007).[20] Consistent with previous study,[9] our results showed that patients with broad ONSD demonstrated a 21.86-fold increase in mortality risk.

Several limitations should be noted in terms of the findings of this meta-analysis. First, the study was based on aggregate published data, and we were unable to perform adjustments for patient-level characteristics. Second, the accuracy of the ONSD measurements depended on the skills of the operators. Third, publication bias was not significant, but it should not be ignored. However, given the limited number of studies, our findings must be interpreted with caution, and publication bias may still occur. Fourth, the meta-analysis included a relatively small number of studies, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Given the limited linguistic abilities, we included only English and Chinese studies.

Therefore, despite the limitations of the methodology, the meta-analysis results indicated the potential use of ONSD as a predictor for the management of TBI.
