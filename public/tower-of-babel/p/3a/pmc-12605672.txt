Nonclinical investigations of a novel heart-type fatty acid–binding protein ligand, [18F]LUF: three-lot process validation, safety assessment, and radiation dosimetry
Authors: Jun Toyohara, Masahiko Ito, Muneyuki Sakata, Tetsuro Tago, Taichi Komoda, Hiroshi Yoshino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart-type fatty acid–binding protein (FABP3) mediates the intracellular transport of fatty acids and is highly expressed in the myocardium. During myocardial infarction, FABP3 leaks from the myocardial cytoplasm into the blood; thus, detection of FABP3 using positron emission tomography (PET) is useful in identifying the site of myocardial damage. 4-(4-Fluoro-2-(1-phenyl-5-(2-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl)-1H-pyrazol-3-yl)phenoxy)butanoic acid (LUF) is a small-molecule organic compound that specifically binds FABP3. We confirmed that fluorine-18–labeled LUF enables clear, high-quality visualization of the myocardium as well as lesions associated with myocardial injury. In this study, we performed a process validation of [18F]LUF production for clinical use and evaluated its nonclinical toxicity and radiation dosimetry estimates using mouse biodistribution data.ResultsThe activity yield of [18F]LUF at the end of synthesis was > 8 GBq, with > 99% radiochemical purity, > 500 GBq/μmol molar activity, and < 1.5 μg/185 MBq total chemical content. All process validation batches complied with the product specifications. An extended single-dose intravenous toxicity study in male and female Sprague–Dawley rats indicated a no-observed adverse effect level for LUF and decay-outed [18F]LUF injection solution of ≥ 25 μg/kg and ≥ 2.5 mL/kg, respectively. These doses are > 100 times the postulated maximum dose of LUF (0.25 μg/kg) and [18F]LUF injection solution (370 MBq). Reverse mutation tests were negative for LUF and the OH form produced after β-decay of [18F]LUF. Neither LUF nor the OH form exhibited cardiotoxicity toward cardiomyocytes derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells at > 100 times the postulated maximum dose. Biodistribution study results demonstrated predominantly hepatobiliary excretion of radioactivity. The most critical organ affected was the heart wall (68.6 μGy/MBq). The estimated effective dose was calculated as 10.2 μSv/MBq. These radiation exposure doses are equivalent to 25.4 mGy (heart wall) and 3.78 mSv for a planned maximum dose of 370 MBq.Conclusions[18F]LUF shows acceptable pharmacological safety at the dose required for adequate PET imaging. The potential risks associated with [18F]LUF injection are well within the acceptable dose limits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41181-025-00404-0.Keywords: Positron emission tomography, Heart-type fatty acid–binding protein, Fluorine-18, Dosimetry, Toxicology

Background

Heart-type fatty acid–binding protein (H-FABP, also known as FABP3) is highly expressed in the myocardium; thus, its measurement as a blood marker of acute myocardial infarction is covered by insurance (Otaki et al. 2017). During myocardial infarction, FABP3 leaks from the myocardial cytoplasm into the bloodstream. However, FABP3 is also expressed in other organs, such as the kidney and skeletal muscle (Binas et al. 1999). Therefore, the injured site or organ cannot be identified using FABP3 as a blood marker. For example, after a fall or other event that occurs during myocardial infarction, it is impossible to distinguish FABP3 originating from skeletal muscle and/or cardiac muscle (van Nieuwenhoven et al. 1995). However, as FABP3 can be detected using positron emission tomography (PET), imaging of abnormal FABP3 expression throughout the body is possible, which makes FABP3 PET useful for identifying the site of injury.

To date, the only reported attempt to detect FABP3 expression through imaging diagnosis utilized an 125I-labeled anti-FABP3 antibody (Fukushima et al. 2022). However, because it involves an antibody, the method is characterized by a high degree of nonspecific background accumulation. Although the radionuclide 123I is used for in vivo imaging diagnosis by single-photon emission computed tomography, PET is preferable because its higher energy produces clearer images. A recent study reported the development of various fluorine-18 (18F)-labeled FABP3–binding peptide derivatives for in vitro evaluations (Dillemuth et al. 2024a). However, no in vivo results using these derivatives have been reported, as they were found to be unstable due to degradation by endogenous metallo-endopeptidases (Dillemuth et al. 2024b).

HY11-8, a small-molecule organic compound that specifically binds to FABP3, was developed by Shiratori Pharmaceutical (Guo et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). The parent compound of HY11-8 (MF1) was developed by Fukunaga et al. at Tohoku University as a derivative of BMS309403, which is selective for FABP4 (Cheng et al., 2019). However, the affinity of MF1 for FABP3 is low (Kd = 303 nM) (Shinoda et al. 2020). HY11-8 is a further modification of MF1, which increased its affinity for FABP3 (Kd = 24 nM; Fig. 1). Although HY11-8 was originally developed as a therapeutic agent for the central nervous system, it is not taken up in the brain. However, it was later confirmed to be useful as a myocardial imaging agent (Toyohara et al. 2024). Fluorine-18–labeled HY11-8 demonstrated significant accumulation in the heart, as observed by PET imaging in healthy mice, with minimal uptake in the lung and liver. The quality of the resulting images was the same as, or superior to, that achieved with the FDA-approved blood flow tracer [18F]flurpiridaz. In vivo studies indicated that HY11-8 remains predominantly as the parent compound within plasma and myocardial tissues in mice, showing negligible metabolic degradation. Analysis of the time-activity curve for cardiac tissue suggested an increase in accumulation over time, indicative of potential fatty acid metabolic processes. Furthermore, reduced tracer uptake was observed in experimental models of acute myocardial infarction and autoimmune myocarditis. Detailed autoradiographic and histological analyses in the myocardial infarction model confirmed reduced tracer uptake in regions corresponding to myocardial damage. In the autoimmune myocarditis model, a positive correlation was identified between tracer accumulation and disease severity, implying the potential utility of fluorine-18-labeled HY11-8 as a marker of myocardial conditions. This led to the further development of HY11-8 as a new PET imaging agent that reflects the density of FABP3 in cardiomyocytes, and the compound name was changed to LUF (Light Up FABP). Before entering initial clinical trials, validation of the production process of [18F]LUF for clinical use is mandatory, in addition to evaluation of its nonclinical toxicity and radiation dosimetry estimation based on mouse biodistribution data.

LUF (formerly named HY11-8) and related compounds

Methods

General

The reference standard for LUF (Yoshino et al. 2024) and its arylboronic precursor (Additional file 1) was provided by Shiratori Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Narashino, Japan). All other chemical reagents were obtained from commercial sources. Male ddY mice were obtained from Japan SLC (Hamamatsu, Japan). Sprague–Dawley (Crl: CD[SD]) rats were obtained from Jackson Laboratory Japan (Yokohama, Japan). Animal studies were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology (approval no. 25003) and Nihon Bioresearch (Hashima, Japan; approval no. 430307). The extended single-dose toxicity study was conducted under the “Guidance on non-clinical safety studies for conduct of human clinical trials and marketing authorization for pharmaceuticals M3(R2)” (PMDA 2010). Reverse mutation tests were performed under the “Guidance on genotoxicity and data interpretation for pharmaceuticals intended for human use” (PMDA 2012).

Process validation

A CFN-MPS-200 multipurpose synthesizer (Sumitomo Heavy Industries, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an integrated high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purification unit and solid-phase extraction (SPE) formulation unit was used for the production of [18F]LUF. A flow diagram of the synthesis apparatus and list of the reagents used are shown in Fig. 2 and Table 1, respectively. Details regarding the radiosynthesis, HPLC purification, and formulation processes were described previously (Toyohara et al. 2024). For validation of the production process, 3 consecutive batches of [18F]LUF were prepared and controlled. Validation runs were performed in our laboratory under the same operating conditions normally set for routine clinical production under Good Manufacturing Practice guidelines. A radioactivity concentration of ≥ 74 MBq/mL and final volume of 15 ± 5 mL were set as acceptance criteria, and each batch was analyzed following the complete quality control program. The shelf life of the formulated product was limited to 3 h. The stability of the final product was assessed in the 3 consecutive batches of [18F]LUF. The product was visually inspected the radiochemical identity, radiochemical purity, and pH determined after storage at room temperature for 3 h after the end of synthesis (EOS).

Quality control

Filter integrity was assessed using the bubble point test (SLTEST000; Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA). The pH of the injection solution was determined using a LAQUA F-72 pH meter (Horiba, Kyoto, Japan). Residual solvents in the injection solution were measured by capillary gas chromatography using a GC-2014 system with LabSolutions Software (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The radionuclide was identified by measuring the half-life using a CRC-55tR dose calibrator (Capintec, Florham Park, NJ, USA) and the 511-keV photopeak was analyzed using a NaI(Tl) radiation detector (US-2000; Universal Giken, Odawara, Japan). Residual tetrabutylammonium in the solution was assessed using an iodine staining thin layer chromatography method with ammonia solution/methanol [0.5/100 (v/v)] as the mobile phase, as specified by the European Pharmacopeia (EDQM 2021). The Limulus amebocyte lysate test was conducted using a Toxinometer® ET-6000 (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan). Finally, sterility after release was tested by direct inoculation of a sample of the product formulation, as specified by the Japanese Pharmacopeia, 18th edition (PMDA 2021).

HPLC analyses were performed on a Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system equipped with a model LC-20AD pump, model SPD-20A UV absorbance detector (set at 254 nm), GABI 3 × 3 inch NaI scintillation detector (Elysia-Raytest, Straubenhardt, Germany), and Titan C18 analytical column (1.9 μm, 50 mm × 2.1 mm inner diameter) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system was controlled using Shimadzu LabSolutions software. For analyses, isocratic elution was applied using acetonitrile/50 mM acetic acid/50 mM ammonium acetate solution = 52.5/23.75/23.75 (flow rate = 0.25 mL/min) as the mobile phase. The retention time of the LUF authentic standard was 8.9 min. Residual copper in the product solution was analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry on an Agilent 7700 × instrument (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) by Shimadzu Techno-Research (Kyoto, Japan) after the decay-out of 18F. The detection limit was 0.1 μg/mL.

Toxicity study

Extended single-dose intravenous toxicity study

An extended single-dose toxicity study was conducted at Hashima Research Center, Nihon Bioresearch, under non–Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) control using healthy male and female Crl: CD(SD) rats. The study involved 3 groups, as follows: rats receiving one dose of vehicle (group 1), rats receiving one dose of unlabeled LUF (group 2), and rats receiving one dose of decay-outed [18F]LUF injection solution (group 3). The dosage and composition for the test groups are shown in Table 2. In group 2, a total of 15 male and 15 female Crl: CD(SD) rats (8 weeks old) were administered physiological saline (12.5 μg/mL) containing 10% (v/v) ethanol and 0.5% (v/v) polysorbate 80 at a dose of 52 nmol/25 μg/kg, corresponding to 100 times the planned maximum dose (0.25 μg/kg). The 15 male and 15 female Crl: CD(SD) rats (8 weeks old) in group 3 were administered a single intravenous dose of 2.5 mL/kg of [18F]LUF injection solution, equivalent to 118 times the planned maximum dose (370 MBq). Control rats (group 1) received 2.0 mL/kg of vehicle physiological saline containing 10% (v/v) ethanol and 0.5% (v/v) polysorbate 80 (formulation matrix solution).

During the study period, the animals were closely monitored each day for signs of toxicity or mortality. The administration site (tail) was visually inspected during general observation the day after administration. Body weight was determined the day before administration (at the time of group allocation) and on days 1, 7, 13, and 14 post-administration. The amount of food consumed was measured on days 7 and 13. On day 1 post-administration, 10 animals from each group and sex were euthanized by exsanguination after sampling of abdominal aortic blood under isoflurane anesthesia and then examined macroscopically and histopathologically. Hematological and blood biochemistry tests were performed using the sampled blood. Details regarding hematological and blood biochemistry test components, weight measurements of organs and tissues and fixation in 10% (v/v) neutral buffered formalin are described in Additional file 1. In order to allow for detection of possible delayed toxicity, 5 animals per group and sex were observed for a period of 14 days following administration and then euthanized for macroscopic, histopathologic, hematologic, and blood biochemistry analyses.

Analysis of mutagenicity

Reverse mutation tests were performed at the Tokyo Laboratory, BoZo Research center (Tokyo, Japan), under non-GLP control. Mutagenicity of the active ingredients LUF and OH produced after 18F decay was evaluated using the Ames test with four histidine-requiring strains of Salmonella typhimurium (S. typhimurium TA98, TA1535, TA100, and TA1537) and one strain of Escherichia coli (E. coli WP2uvrA), with and without the S9 mixture, over a dose range of 78.1–5000 μg/plate, according to the standard method (PMDA 2012).

In vitro pharmacologic studies of cardiovascular safety

Given the rapid intravenous injection of PET ligands, it is crucial to assess their cardiovascular effects. To determine the cardiotoxicity of LUF, its impact on extracellular potentials was investigated using human induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cell–derived cardiomyocytes (Fujifilm Bioscience and Engineering Laboratory, Kanagawa, Japan) (ICH 2022). The concentration range for evaluation was 0.2–200 μM, with the evaluation performed cumulatively over four iterations with a common ratio of 10.

Radiation dosimetry

[18F]LUF (2.0 MBq/8 pmol) was injected intravenously into 8-week-old male ddY mice. Tracer-injected mice were housed individually in filter paper–lined animal rearing cages until the time of euthanasia. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation at 5, 15, 30, 60, 180, and 360 min after injection (n = 4 each). Blood was collected by heart puncture, and tissues were then harvested. Radioactivity excreted into the urine was recovered from the cage floor and via cystocentesis from the urinary bladder. The samples were analyzed for 18F radioactivity using an auto-gamma counter (Hidex-AMG, Turku, Finland) and then weighed. Tissue uptake of 18F was expressed as percentage of injected dose per organ (%ID/organ) or percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g). The tissue distribution data were extrapolated to an adult male phantom using the %kg/g method (Blau 1975). The radiation absorbed dose and effective dose for human adults were estimated using OLINDA/EXM software (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA) (Stabin et al. 2005). Effective dose was estimated using the risk-weighting factor described in ICRP Publication 103 (ICRP 2007).

Off-target binding analysis

Regarding the selectivity of LUF binding, its reported binding affinity (Kd) for FABP3 is 24 nM, and whereas its Kd for other FABP isoforms is ≥ 240 nM, indicating a > tenfold selectivity difference (Guo et al. 2023). To assess off-target binding, the inhibitory effect of LUF on the substrate binding of 28 common receptors was evaluated (Sekisui Medical, Naka, Japan). LUF was tested in all assays at 10 μM.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation for body weight, food intake, hematologic test results, blood biochemistry test results, and organ weights (including relative weights) were calculated for each group. A multiple range test was performed to evaluate the significance of differences in all groups. Specifically, the Bartlett method was used to test for equality of variance, and in cases in which equality of variance was confirmed, differences were evaluated using the Tukey test. However, in cases in which equality of variance was not confirmed, differences were evaluated using the Steel–Dwass test. The significance level was set at 5%, and results are presented as significantly different at < 5% (p < 0.05) and < 1% (p < 0.01). No statistical analyses were performed on general condition, local irritation, or necropsy findings.

Results

Process validation

The 3 production runs exhibited a mean activity yield of 9850 ± 1992 MBq, decay-corrected yield of 22.7 ± 4.2%, radiochemical purity of 99.7 ± 0.0%, molar activity (Am) of 602 ± 145 GBq/μmol, radioactivity concentration of 634 ± 149 MBq/mL, and final volume of 15.6 ± 0.5 mL. The average synthesis time following target bombardment was 82 min. All batches of [18F]LUF injection solution met the quality control criteria listed in Table 3. [18F]LUF was stable up to 3 h after EOS, with acceptable appearance, radiochemical identity of 4.1 ± 1.2%, pH of 6.5 ± 0.0, and radiochemical purity of 99.3 ± 0.4%.

Toxicity study

Extended single-dose intravenous toxicity study

LUF and decay-outed [18F]LUF injection solutions were administered intravenously once to male and female Crl: CD(SD) rats, and potential toxicity was then investigated. Rats in the control group received formulation matrix solution. No deaths were observed in any group, and no abnormalities were observed in terms of general condition, injection site (local) irritation, body weight, food intake, or necropsy findings.

Hematological tests revealed significantly lower eosinophil counts in females in the LUF group compared with the control group on day 14 post-administration, but no significant differences were observed in white blood cell counts or differential white blood cell ratios (Additional file 1: Table S1). Furthermore, because the mean eosinophil count of LUF group was equivalent to the mean value in the control group on day 1 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S2), the difference was deemed to be within the range of physiological variation. Furthermore, the platelet count (PLT) in one female in the LUF group on day 14 post-administration was lower than that in the other animals in the same group (Additional file 1: Table S3). However, no significant differences were observed between the LUF group and the control group (Additional file 1: Table S1), and the PLTs of the other four females in the LUF group on day 14 post-administration were higher than that of the single female in the control group (Additional file 1: Table S4). Therefore, the low PLT in one female in the LUF group was deemed an incidental low value and not due to an effect of the test substance.

Blood biochemistry tests showed significantly lower triglyceride (TG) levels in males in the LUF group compared with the males in the control group on day 14 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S5). However, the difference was within the background variation in the data of the test facility (TGs: 34.5 ± 12.1 mg/dL; range 12.4–79.0, two standard deviation range 10.3–58.7, n = 50, male Crl: CD[SD] rats, 9–11 weeks old) and therefore deemed within the range of physiological fluctuation. Females in the LUF group showed significantly higher gamma-globulin (γ-glb) levels compared with the control group on day 1 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S6). Females in the LUF injection solution group showed significantly higher total protein (TP), albumin (Alb), and total cholesterol (T-Cho) levels compared with the control group on day 14 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S7). However, these differences were all within the background variation in the data of the test facility (γ-glb: 5.2 ± 1.4%; range 3.0–9.1, two standard deviation range 2.4–8.0, TP: 5.65 ± 0.34 g/dL; range 4.98–6.81, two standard deviation range 4.97–6.33, Alb: 2.90 ± 0.23 g/dL; range 2.48–3.65, two standard deviation rage 2.44–3.36, T-Cho: 57.7 ± 13.0 mg/dL; range 32.2–86.9, two standard deviation rage 31.7–83.7, n = 54, Crl: CD(SD) rats, 9 to 11 weeks old) and therefore deemed within the range of physiological fluctuation. Furthermore, females in the LUF group showed significantly lower calcium (Ca) levels compared to the control group on day 1 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S6). However, the mean values were equivalent to those of the control group on day 14 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S7) and therefore deemed to be within the range of physiological fluctuation. Other significant differences were observed between the LUF and LUF injection solution groups, but these were also deemed within the range of physiological fluctuation.

In terms of organ weight, males in the LUF group exhibited significantly higher relative pituitary weight and significantly lower relative lung weight compared with the control group on day 14 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S8), and in females, the relative kidney weight was significantly lower compared with the control group on day 1 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S9). No significant difference in absolute weight was observed in either case, and as the differences were slight, they were judged to be within the range of physiological variation. Furthermore, a significant difference in pituitary weight was observed between males of the LUF group and LUF injection solution group on day 14 post-administration (Additional file 1: Table S8), but this difference was judged to be due to physiological variation.

In conclusion, intravenous administration of 25 μg/kg of LUF or 2.5 mL/kg of LUF injection solution to male and female Crl: CD(SD) rats produced no toxicological effects. Therefore, the no-observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) under these test conditions was ≥ 25 μg/kg for LUF and ≥ 2.5 mL/kg for LUF injection solution.

Analysis of mutagenicity

Inhibition of bacterial growth by LUF was observed at concentrations of ≥ 625 μg/plate for S. typhimurium TA1535 and TA1537, as well as at concentrations of ≥ 1250 μg/plate for S. typhimurium TA100 and TA98, in the absence of metabolic activation. Inhibition of bacterial growth by the OH form was noted at concentrations of ≥ 625 μg/plate for S. typhimurium TA1535 without metabolic activation and E. coli WP2uvrA with metabolic activation, ≥ 1250 μg/plate for S. typhimurium TA1437 without metabolic activation and S. typhimurium TA1533 and TA1537 with metabolic activation, ≥ 2500 μg/plate for S. typhimurium TA98 and TA100 without metabolic activation, and 5000 μg/plate for E. coli WP2uvrA without metabolic activation and S. typhimurium TA98 and TA100 with metabolic activation.

Regardless of the presence of metabolic activation, neither LUF nor the OH form increased the frequency of revertant colonies that exceeded twice the value of the negative control across any of the bacterial strains tested, and no dose–response relationship was observed. Consequently, it was concluded that under the specified experimental conditions, neither LUF nor the OH forms induce gene mutations in bacteria.

In vitro pharmacologic studies of cardiovascular safety

No prolongation of field potential duration corrected by Fridericia’s formula (FPDcF) was observed over the LUF concentration range 0.2–200 μM (Additional file 1: Table S10). By contrast, abnormal waveforms indicative of cardiac arrest were observed at the maximum concentration tested (200 μM; equivalent to 8 mg/kg), but no cardiotoxic effects were apparent at a concentration of 0.89 μM (equivalent to 34.5 μg/kg; 138 times the intended maximum dose). The OH form prolonged the FPDcF at a high concentration, and the estimated 10% concentration of FPDcF prolongation was 26 μM. No abnormal waveforms were observed at any dose of the OH form.

Radiation dosimetry

The tissue distribution of radioactivity after injection of [18F]LUF into mice is summarized in Tables 4 and 5, and time-activity curves for organs with high levels of radioactivity are shown in Fig. 3. The concentration of radioactivity in the blood decreased rapidly after [18F]LUF injection. [18F]LUF is expelled from the body primarily via hepatobiliary excretion; the amount of radioactivity in the liver was highest immediately after administration (12.3%ID/organ) and then decreased over time, with increased accumulation in the small intestine. The amount of radioactivity in the small intestine peaked at 180 min after administration (5.5%ID/organ) and then decreased over time as the radioactivity moved to the large intestine (6.4%ID/organ at 360 min). By contrast, the amount of radioactivity in the urine was low, at 1%ID at 360 min after injection.

Off-target binding analysis

Although LUF exhibited weak binding to GABA A (central BZ), its effect as determined by imaging of peripheral tissues, such as cardiac muscle, was considered negligible. At a concentration of 10 µM, LUF did not exhibit ≥ 50% inhibition of any of the other receptors (Additional file 1: Table S11).

Discussion

The production of [18F]LUF injection solution was validated by 3 consecutive radiosynthesis with quality control. The resulting activity yields (> 8 GBq at EOS) were sufficient for a single-center extrapolatory clinical study. The results of a quality test also met all the established criteria, demonstrating that the final product was of sufficient quality for clinical use.

No toxicological effects were observed after a single intravenous administration of 25 μg/kg of LUF or 2.5 mL/kg of LUF injection solution to male and female Crl: CD(SD) rats. Therefore, the NOAEL for LUF and LUF injection solution is determined to be 25 μg/kg and 2.5 mL/kg or greater, respectively. Mutagenic tests were negative for LUF and the OH form produced after the β-decay of [18F]LUF. Furthermore, LUF did not show cardiotoxicity in cardiomyocytes derived from human iPS cells at 0.89 μM (equivalent to 35.6 μg/kg, 142 times the planned maximum dose) and the OH form did not show cardiotoxicity even at the maximum test concentration (200 μM, equivalent to 8 mg/kg).

The Am of [18F]LUF manufactured in our facility is higher than 500 GBq/µmol, and the total amount of chemical at the planned standard clinically administered radioactivity of 185 MBq is < 1.5 µg even after expiration (0.59 ± 0.21 μg; range 0.40 to 0.81 μg). Therefore, the risks associated with the administration of [18F]LUF injection are considered to be within a toxicologically acceptable range (Koziorowski et al. 2017).

When 370 MBq of [18F]LUF was administered, the effective dose was estimated to be 3.77 mSv and the maximum absorbed dose in organs was estimated to be 25.4 mGy (heart wall). Based on these results, the expected radiation exposure risk for the research volunteers was within the range of 1 to 10 mSv, which is within the social benefit and dose constraints for the volunteers in the ICRP recommendations, and was considered to be within the acceptable range for use in providing useful information for disease prevention and treatment. Regarding absorbed dose, the US RDRC considers that the acceptable range is 30 mGy for radiosensitive organs (gonads, bone marrow, spleen, and lens) and 50 mGy for other organs, and therefore the risks associated with [18F]LUF administration are considered within the acceptable range (Zanotti-Fregonara et al. 2013).

The absorbed doses in the myocardium of other 18F-labeled PET tracers have been estimated at 32.8 μGy/MBq for [18F]FDG (Srinivasan et al. 2020) and between 48 and 60 μGy/MBq for [18F]flurpiridaz (Maddahi et al. 2011; Kawano et al. 2021), which corresponds to 11.1 mGy and 18.5–22.2 mGy, respectively, when 370 MBq is administered. The reason why the absorbed dose of [18F]LUF in the heart wall is higher than these values is thought to be due to the fact that the amount of radioactivity accumulated in the myocardium is high, exhibiting a cumulative pattern, and it is not washed out of the tissue.

A limitation of this preclinical investigation is interspecies variability observed in the biodistribution, which poses challenges for accurately estimating the absorbed dose. For short-lived radionuclides such as 18F and 11C, radioactive decay is the primary factor affecting radioactivity residence time, and the effective dose is generally consistent across species. However, differences in biodistribution tend to result in differences in absorbed dose. In particular, differences in metabolism between humans and rodents result in the activation of different excretion pathways, and it is possible that the dose in organs may be limited by the absorbed dose. For this reason, some have suggested that initial human administration studies should begin with a low dose of radioactivity (Dakanali et al. 2025).

Conclusions

All 3 batches of process validation complied with the product specification. Nonclinical toxicology tests indicated that [18F]LUF shows acceptable pharmacological safety at the dose required for exploratory PET clinical research. A single intravenous injection of 370 MBq of [18F]LUF leads to an effective dose of 3.77 mSv and the highest absorbed dose to an organ of 25.4 mGy (heart wall). The potential risk associated with [18F]LUF injection is well within acceptable dose limits.
