Parents' or Guardians' Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices in the Prevention and Management of Childhood Myopia
Authors: Yifeng Qian, Peirong Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of this study was to assess the knowledge, attitude, and practices (KAP) of parents or guardians regarding the prevention and management of childhood myopia.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology in the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Suzhou, China) between August 2023 and November 2023. Parents or guardians who willingly volunteered to take part in the study were surveyed using a self-designed questionnaire.ResultsA total of 571 participants returned valid questionnaires, among whom 288 respondents (50.44%) fell within the 31- to 40-year age group and 474 respondents (83.01%) were identified as myopic. The mean KAP scores for the knowledge, attitude and practices dimensions were 23.34 ± 3.05 (possible range: 0–26), 46.47 ± 4.02 (possible range: 12–60), and 40.52 ± 7.07 (possible range: 11–54), respectively. Structural equation modeling analysis indicated that education had a direct effect on knowledge (estimate = 0.41, P = 0.038), while knowledge directly influenced both attitude (estimate = 0.40, P < 0.001) and practices (estimate = 0.36, P < 0.001). Also, attitude was found to have a direct impact on practices (estimate = 0.45, P < 0.001).ConclusionsParents or guardians had adequate knowledge, a positive attitude, and proactive practices towards the prevention and management of childhood myopia, which might be affected by their educational level. This comprehensive understanding of parental perspectives highlights the potential for targeted interventions in clinical settings to further enhance pediatric eye care.

Introduction

The escalating prevalence of childhood myopia has become a global public health concern in recent years, prompting an intensified focus on preventive measures and effective management strategies. Myopia, characterized by the elongation of the eyeball, has reached epidemic proportions worldwide, marked by a significant surge in its incidence over the last few decades [1]. In China alone, the total number of individuals afflicted with myopia has soared to approximately 600 million, with a staggering prevalence rate of 95.5% among university students [2]. Moreover, the demographic landscape of myopia is undergoing a concerning shift, with its incidence trending younger and the age of peak myopia growth decreasing [2]. Among urban Chinese children, the prevalence of myopia showcases a noteworthy progression, starting from 3.9% in grade 1 and escalating to 67.3% in grade 7 [3]. This alarming trend emphasizes the critical need for early intervention and comprehensive management strategies. Elucidating the risks associated with myopia, particularly when unaddressed in the formative years of childhood and adolescence, is imperative [4]. Studies highlight the significant link between early childhood myopia and the development of high myopia in adulthood [5]. Recognizing that myopia is often first diagnosed in childhood and can progress throughout adolescence, it becomes evident that the most effective strategy to mitigate myopia-related complications is to delay its progression during childhood [6, 7]. The prevalence of myopia begins to escalate noticeably after the age of 6 years, underscoring the importance of targeted interventions during the crucial early years of visual development [8].

Amid this backdrop, knowledge, attitude, and practices (KAP) surveys have gained prominence as structured assessment tools, offering valuable insights into individuals’ understanding, beliefs, and behaviors related to myopia prevention and management. By employing a structured survey methodology, KAP studies provide a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing the adoption of preventive measures and the implementation of effective management strategies [9, 10]. This approach facilitates the identification of gaps in knowledge, potential misconceptions, and barriers to optimal practices, thereby informing targeted interventions to enhance public health outcomes [11]. Recognizing the pivotal role of parents or guardians in shaping the visual health habits of children, it becomes imperative to investigate the KAP of this group to devise targeted and efficacious interventions.

Despite the increasing importance of KAP surveys in improving current comprehension of the intricacies of behaviors related to myopia, a notable gap persists in the understanding of the distinct factors that shape parental attitudes and practices in this context. While studies have been conducted in various regions of the world, there is a notable absence of such research in the Chinese context [12, 13]. This gap underscores the need for a targeted investigation that addresses the unique socio-cultural, educational, and environmental factors influencing parental perspectives and actions toward the prevention and management of childhood myopia within the Chinese population. This study thus aims to bridge this gap by comprehensively exploring the KAP of parents or guardians regarding the prevention and management of childhood myopia, thereby contributing to the refinement and development of tailored interventions in the field.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study was carried out in the Department of Ophthalmology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Suzhou, China) from August 2023 to November 2023, with parents or guardians selected as subjects through a random sampling process. This work was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2000) of the World Medical Association. The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Suzhou University (No.320-2023), and informed consent was duly obtained from all participating individuals.

Procedures

A convenient sampling method was used to select a cohort of parents or guardians of children for a self-designed questionnaire survey. The questionnaire’s structure was informed by the "Application Standard for the Detection and Prevention of Myopia in Children and Adolescents" established by the Professional Committee of Ophthalmology of the China Medical Equipment Association [Chinese J Ophthalmol. 2018, 8(6):276–288]. A preliminary pilot survey, encompassing 25 responses, was performed, and the reliability and validity test outcomes demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.828, indicating robust internal consistency in the questionnaire.

The final questionnaire, presented in Chinese, encompasses information collection across four dimensions, totaling 34 items. The sociodemographic characteristics section comprises nine items, with item 8 incorporating one sub-item. The knowledge dimension encompasses 13 items, with 2 points awarded for a correct answer, 1 point for "uncertain," and 0 points for an incorrect answer. The potential score range for the knowledge dimension is 0–26 points, with a knowledge score exceeding 70% of the total considered to be indicative of good knowledge. The attitude dimension consists of 13 items and, with the exception of item 7, a 5-point Likert scale is applied. Items 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, and 13 are scored as options a to e, with 5 to 1 points, respectively. Items 3, 4, 10, and 11 are scored as options a to e, with 1 to 5 points, respectively. Item 7 is not scored, and the potential score range for this dimension is 12–60 points, with scores of 12–30 indicating a negative attitude, 31–42 indicating a neutral attitude, and 43–65 indicating a positive attitude. The practice dimension comprises 14 items. Items 1–4 and 7–11 use a 5-point Likert scale, with scores for options a to e being 5 to 1 point, respectively. Item 5 is scored as option a to d, earning 1 to 4 points, respectively; item 6 scores 5 for option a and 1 point for option b; items 12–14 are not scored. The potential score range for this dimension is 11–54 points. Scores of 11–21 indicate negative practices, 22–33 indicate moderate practices, and 34–53 indicate positive practices. Adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices were defined as achieving scores surpassing 75% of the maximum possible score in each respective dimension [11].

The questionnaire was distributed through an online survey that was crafted using the WeChat-based Wenjuanxing mini-program, accompanied by a QR code for participants to access and complete the survey through WeChat. Rigorous measures were implemented to ensure data quality, such as restricting each IP address to a single submission and mandating completion of all items. The research team meticulously assessed all questionnaires for completeness, internal coherence, and rationality. Simultaneously with distribution of the online survey, we informed parents of myopic children or of children with high risk of myopia attending in our outpatient department about the significance of their participation in questionnaires for effective childhood myopia prevention and management. Once informed consent has been obtained, we distributed printed questionnaires under the supervision of a trained research assistant who monitored the process without interfering in the responses. Participants, namely, parents of myopic or high-risk children, were briefed on the intervention and control measures before voluntarily completing the questionnaire on-site at the hospital. Throughout the survey, participants were encouraged to mark "Unclear" if any questions posed difficulty, and meticulous observation ensured the validity of responses by flagging instances of haphazard ticking without prior reading. The researcher further checked for any unanswered items during the collection process, prompting participants to provide missing information and, subsequently, any incomplete or unanswered questionnaires were rendered invalid. During data cleaning, the questionnaires of those participants who chose the same option in the entire section of any of the KAP dimensions and had been completed the questionnaire within 90 s were considered to be invalid.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including mean ± standard deviation (SD) for quantitative variables and frequency [n (%)] for categorical variables, were employed to characterize the study indicators. Group comparisons were conducted through analysis of variance (ANOVA) for quantitative measures. Categorical variables were presented as proportions. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation analysis and structural equation modeling (SEM) were employed to explore relationships and interactions among the variables in the dataset based on the following hypothesis: (1) the educational level, occupation, monthly income, degree of myopia in parent's right and left eyes, and degree of myopia in child's left and right eyes have an effect on knowledge; (2) knowledge, degree of myopia in parent's right and left eyes, and degree of myopia in child's left and right have an effect on attitude; and (3) knowledge, attitude, and degree of myopia in parent's right and left eyes and degree of myopia in child's left and right have an effect on practice. Two-sided P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Initially, a total of 624 responders completed the questionnaire. After data cleaning and quality control, 54 questionnaires were considered to be invalid and removed from subsequent analyses. Consequently, data from 571 questionnaires were included in the study, which is a validity rate of 91.51%. Among the respondents of these 571 questionnaires, 288 (50.44%) were aged 31–40 years; 413 (72.33%) had graduated from college or were undergraduates; 214 (37.48%) reported a family monthly income of 10,000–20,000 Yuan (approximately 1400–2800 USD, with the average monthly income for Soochow residents being around 858 USD); 259 (45.36%) had children in grades 0–3; and 258 (45.18%) were an only child. Also, 474 (83.01%) of the participants were myopic. The mean (± SD) KAP scores for the total study population were 23.34 ± 3.05 (possible range 0–26) for the knowledge dimension, 46.47 ± 4.02 (possible range 12–60) for the attitude dimension and 40.52 ± 7.07 (possible range: 11–54) for the practice dimension. Distribution of knowledge, attitude and practices (KAP) dimensions

With regard to attitudes related to myopia, 39.4% of responders agreed while 37.83% disagreed with the statement that glasses should be worn as long as the vision is unclear (A2). On the other hand, 54.99% disagreed with the statement that vision should be checked on a regular basis when it is clear, but should be checked regularly when unclear (A3). Optimistically, 93.52% of the guardians strongly disagreed or disagreed with the child reading or playing with electronic devices while lying down (A4). Moreover, 68.3% strongly supported the promotion of a publicity campaign on vision science for primary and secondary school students (A8). Further, 49.21% disagreed that myopia prevention and control measures should not be taken in a hurry after a diagnosis of myopia (A10), and 95.27% believed that effective measures should be taken as soon as possible according to the doctor's advice (A11) (Table 2).

In the practice dimension, 46.23% of respondents always paid close attention to the degrees of their children's myopia (P1), and 41.68% always supervised their children to follow proper eye habits (P7). In addition, 33.98% sometimes took their children to outdoor activities for > 2 h a day (P2). However, 33.63% rarely took their children to participate in relevant scientific lectures or activities (P4). Regarding the frequency of taking their children for ophthalmological examination, 37.83% reported once-a-year examinations (P5), and 87.57% would change their children's lenses according to the examination results (P6). Regarding the means of controlling the progression of myopia (P12–P14), more than 45% of the participants would choose to use such measures or not depending on the situation (Table 2).

Discussion

The results of this study reveal that the participating parents and guardians demonstrated sufficient knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices regarding the prevention and management of childhood myopia, which might be affected by their education. These findings underscore the importance of parental involvement in addressing childhood myopia. Given the positive attributes exhibited by parents and guardians, clinicians should leverage and build upon these strengths in developing effective interventions and educational programs aimed at enhancing collaborative efforts between healthcare providers and caregivers in the management of childhood myopia.

The main findings of this study highlight that parents and guardians exhibited commendable knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices concerning the prevention and management of childhood myopia. These outcomes align with the pivotal role parents play in shaping the eye health behaviors of their children, as evidenced by numerous studies emphasizing the significance of parental involvement in myopia-related measures [14, 15]. The identification of statistically significant associations between demographic factors and childhood myopia KAP further enhances our understanding of the nuanced determinants influencing parental engagement in myopia-related behaviors of their children.

The study results revealed that educational level, occupation, and family income significantly influenced parental knowledge regarding childhood myopia. Specifically, higher educational levels were associated with greater knowledge, as supported by both the univariate analysis and SEM results, suggesting that individuals with higher education are likely to be more informed about myopia, possibly due to better access to information and resources [16, 17]. Similarly, the significant association between the occupation and knowledge scores resonates with research indicating diverse health literacy levels across occupational groups. These variations underscore the need for targeted interventions catering to specific educational and occupational backgrounds. Additionally, the correlation between family monthly income and knowledge scores reflects a socioeconomic dimension in understanding myopia-related knowledge. Similar findings have been reported in studies exploring the impact of income on health literacy, suggesting that economic disparities may contribute to differences in health-related knowledge [18–20]. Educational programs should be designed to accommodate diverse educational backgrounds, employing clear and accessible communication strategies to enhance understanding among individuals with varying literacy levels. Additionally, addressing economic disparities through community-based initiatives, subsidies for eye care services, and/or collaborations with social welfare programs could help bridge the knowledge gap associated with family monthly income. Interestingly, no significant differences were found in the knowledge, attitude, or practice scores based on the child’s myopia status or the degree of myopia in the child. This suggests that personal experience with myopia does not significantly influence parental management behaviors, possibly due to a normalization of the condition within families.

The correlation analysis and SEM outcomes elucidated the intricate relationships among knowledge, attitude, and practices. The positive correlations between knowledge and both attitude and practice scores align with established behavioral theories, such as the Health Belief Model and Theory of Planned Behavior, emphasizing the role of knowledge in shaping attitudes and subsequent actions [21–23]. Similarly, the direct impact of education on knowledge, and subsequently on attitude and practice, underscores the multifaceted influence of educational background on health-related behaviors. These findings align with studies emphasizing the role of education as a determinant of health literacy and positive health behaviors [24–26]. The direct impact of attitude on practice reinforces the importance of cultivating positive perceptions and attitudes to drive proactive health practices.

The findings of our research also reveal a nuanced landscape of KAP among parents and guardians regarding childhood myopia. In terms of knowledge, a substantial proportion of respondents demonstrated awareness of key myopia-related concepts, such as the impact of excessive eye development on myopia and the complications of high myopia. However, there were notable gaps in understanding, particularly in areas such as the effectiveness of low-concentration atropine eye drops and the significance of cycloplegic refraction. These findings highlight specific areas that could benefit from targeted educational interventions, and align with studies suggesting the effectiveness of focused knowledge enhancement campaigns in promoting health behaviors. To enhance knowledge, targeted campaigns should focus on clarifying misconceptions, particularly regarding the efficacy of specific myopia control measures. These initiatives can draw inspiration from successful health literacy interventions that utilize clear communication strategies to improve understanding [27, 28]. Collaborations with healthcare providers and educators could facilitate the dissemination of accurate information, building on the positive inclination of parents to participate in vision science campaigns and parent–child activities [29].

In terms of attitude, a majority of respondents exhibited positive inclinations toward myopia prevention and control measures. Noteworthy positive attitudes included the belief in the preventability of myopia and the recognition of the importance of regular optometric examinations. However, some attitudes indicated potential misconceptions, such as the belief that wearing glasses is necessary only when vision is unclear or the perception that it is unnecessary to seek vision checks unless the vision is unclear. Additionally, the positive attitude towards participating in parent–child activities on "protecting eyesight" suggests an openness to community-based interventions, offering an avenue for effective engagement. In terms of attitudes, interventions should address identified misconceptions, emphasizing the importance of early myopia prevention measures and the necessity of regular optometric examinations. Leveraging community-based activities and school initiatives could effectively disseminate accurate information while capitalizing on the willingness of parents to engage in such programs [30, 31].

In terms of practices, the majority of participants reported engaging in positive behaviors, such as paying attention to their child's degree of myopia and encouraging outdoor activities. However, there were notable gaps, such as the relatively low frequency of taking children for optometric examinations and the replacement of lenses based on regular examinations. These findings point towards the usefulness of practical interventions, including educational campaigns emphasizing the importance of regular optometric check-ups and proactive lens replacement strategies. Interventions should focus on promoting regular optometric examinations and the timely replacement of lenses based on professional advice. Educational efforts could utilize various channels, including school-based programs, community workshops, and digital platforms, to reinforce positive practices [32]. Encouraging parental involvement in school and community activities, as indicated by the positive attitudes towards such initiatives, could further enhance the reach and impact of these interventions [33].

While this study offers valuable insights into parental knowledge, attitude, and practices concerning childhood myopia, certain limitations need acknowledgment. Although we considered factors such as parental age, residence, educational level, occupation, family income, child’s school grade/year, number of siblings, and myopia status, there are still numerous unmeasured variables that were not included in our analysis, which could potentially influence the results. The cross-sectional design hinders establishing causal relationships and temporal sequences, suggesting the need for longitudinal investigations. Additionally, relying on self-reported data introduces the possibility of both response and social desirability biases, which may impact the accuracy of our findings, necessitating careful interpretation. The study's setting specificity limits generalizability, urging caution in applying findings universally. Nevertheless, the paper's strengths, including its use of structural equation modeling, a substantial sample size, and a comprehensive exploration of the subject matter, contribute significantly to our understanding of parental roles in childhood myopia. Future research should aim to overcome these limitations by incorporating a variety of study designs, objective measures, and broader participant representation to improve the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, parents and guardians generally possess sufficient knowledge, maintain positive attitudes, and engage in proactive practices regarding the prevention and management of childhood myopia, which might be affected by their educational level. Implementing targeted educational programs within clinical settings ware recommended to enhance parental awareness and promote effective preventive measures for childhood myopia.
