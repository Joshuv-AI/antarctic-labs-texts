Perception of medical students towards virtual learning of pathology courses at the college of medicine, University of Bahri, 2022
Authors: Hind A Elsiddig, Daffalla Alam Elhuda, Maha M Salim, Sahar S Elfakki, Manhal A M Noureddin, Safa S A Omer, Sajida Adam, Tasneem M F, Mozn E Mohammed, Moneeb Y F Tahir, Nour S M
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of virtual learning in medical education. Pathology, a key preclinical course, was delivered online at the University of Bahri. Understanding students’ perceptions of virtual learning in a low resource setting is essential for optimizing educational strategies.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 271 medical students using total coverage sampling and only 160 valid questionnaires were returned. Data were collected via a structured self-administered online questionnaire assessing demographics, socioeconomic status, preferred virtual learning platform, resource availability, attitudes, motivation, interactions, course-specific perceptions, and perceived academic performance comparisons in virtual versus traditional learning. The data were analyzed via SPSS version 25 with a significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsOf the 160 respondents, 75% were female. In general, our students demonstrated a neutral perception of virtual versus traditional learning. The students found virtual learning to be feasible and appreciated the asynchronous access for the Pathology II course. Telegram was the most preferred platform (82.5%). The correlations between perceptions and gender, socioeconomic status, and living situation were not significant. Face-to-face contact during learning is highly appreciated (P < 0.05).ConclusionOverall, our results indicate a neutral perception of virtual versus traditional learning. The students expressed a more positive perception of virtual learning in the Pathology II course than in the Pathology I course. The combination of both traditional face-to-face and virtual learning strategies may be more appropriate for our students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09697-4.Keywords: Virtual learning, Pathology, Medical education, Online learning, Student perception, Sudan

Introduction

Virtual learning has become an important method in education, designed to overcome the limits of time and location. It began with internet-based systems in the 1960s and expanded significantly after the internet was introduced in 1994, allowing accredited online courses. Today, the growth of mobile devices, social media, and internet access has made virtual learning more flexible, enabling learners to study independently, access resources easily, build creativity and confidence, and reduce costs related to travel and physical classrooms [1, 2].

In the context of medical education, virtual learning extends beyond simple correspondence courses to include advanced applications such as computerized virtual patient simulations and interactive pathology courses. Virtual learning environments (VLEs) facilitate these methods by integrating teaching tools, student tracking, and real-time or asynchronous communication [3–7]. Different modes of virtual learning—synchronous, asynchronous, and hybrid—offer tailored approaches to accommodate students’ schedules and learning preferences, with hybrid models combining the strengths of face-to-face and online instruction [8].

Despite its advantages, virtual learning presents challenges, including the need for adequate technological skills, self-discipline, and the potential for limited interaction with instructors and peers [1, 3]. These challenges are particularly relevant in developing countries, such as Sudan, where educational infrastructure may be insufficient for traditional teaching methods, making virtual learning a valuable alternative [1].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the role of virtual learning in medical education became increasingly prominent. Studies conducted in the USA, UK, Pakistan, India, Greece, Saudi Arabia, and Brazil reported generally positive perceptions of virtual learning for medical courses, highlighting benefits such as flexibility, video-assisted lectures, and interactive sessions, while also noting limitations such as screen fatigue, reduced student‒teacher interaction, and the difficulty of separating study from home activities [9–15]. Collectively, these findings support the integration of virtual learning into medical curricula, particularly through hybrid approaches that combine online and traditional methods, enhancing student engagement and educational outcomes. Despite the ongoing crisis in Sudan, which has disrupted many aspects of daily life and higher education, virtual learning has continued to provide a vital alternative for students. Online platforms have enabled the continuation of teaching, learning, and assessments, helping to bridge the gap caused by the closure of campuses and instability. Although challenges such as electricity shortages, internet connectivity issues, and limited resources remain, the persistence of virtual learning has ensured that medical education and other academic programs can still move forward during this difficult period.

The COVID-19 pandemic, along with the current crisis in Sudan, has highlighted the essential role of virtual learning.The integration of virtual learning in medical education, including at the College of Medicine, University of Bahri, has become an unavoidable necessity. While international studies have shown that virtual learning offers flexibility, accessibility, and motivation, it also presents challenges such as technical issues and reduced engagement [16, 17]. In Sudan, data on students’ perceptions of virtual versus traditional teaching—especially in pathology courses—are limited. Evaluating these perceptions can guide faculty in adopting teaching methods that improve learning outcomes, satisfaction, and engagement and help design a context-specific model for pathology education in Sudan.

Although students’ perception of virtual learning has been widely studied, evidence from crisis- affected, low-resource settings remain limited. Unlike studies conducted in stable, well-resourced environments, this study reflects the experience of students learning pathology during prolonged institutional disruption, limited internet stability and reliance on informal platforms such as Telegram rather than structured learning management systems. These contextual constraints shape both feasibility and acceptance of virtual learning and distinguish the present findings from existing literature.

Methodology

This is a cross-sectional, descriptive, analytical study conducted at the College of Medicine, University of Bahri, Khartoum, Sudan, during 2022. The College of Medicine, University of Bahri, which was established in 2011, adopts a traditional discipline-based curriculum with partial integration conducted over six academic years. Basic sciences are taught in the first three years, whereas clinical sciences are delivered in the subsequent three years. The pathology courses included in this study, which comprise general and systemic pathology, are learned in the third and fourth years.

In the University of Bahri curriculum, pathology is delivered through three consecutive courses. Introduction to Pathology and Microbiology is taught during the sixth semester and focuses on basic concepts, general pathology principles, and foundational mechanisms of disease, and was delivered through traditional face-to-face teaching. Pathology I is taught during the seventh semester and introduces the first part of systemic pathology (including Hematology, Cardiovascular, Respiratory and Gastroenterology); it represented the students’ first exposure to virtual learning. Pathology II is taught during the eighth semester and focuses on the rest of the systemic pathology topics and was also delivered using virtual learning methods.

The study population included all male and female fifth-year medical students who completed their pathology courses in 2022 (n = 271). First-, second-, third-, fourth-, and sixth-year students were excluded, as were fifth-year students who refused to participate. The total coverage sampling technique was used, and all eligible students were enrolled; as a response to the study questionnaire depend totally on volunteering, this resulted in response rate of approximately 59% of the target population.

Data was collected via a structured questionnaire designed via Google Forms and distributed through social media platforms. A pilot study was conducted with 10 students to validate the tool. The pilot study demonstrated that the research tool worked as intended and did not require modification before the main study. Participants were able to complete the instrument without significant difficulties, and the data collected were clear and consistent, indicating the tool’s feasibility and appropriateness for the target population. As no major issues or misunderstandings were identified in the pilot phase, the study proceeded using the same tool in the full-scale research, showing that the methodology and procedures are suitable as designed.

The questionnaire included demographic information (gender, living situation, socioeconomic status, and preferred application for virtual learning) and 16 items related to virtual learning. These items covered resource availability (3 items), attitudes toward and acceptance of virtual learning (4 items), degree of motivation and management (2 items), student–teacher interaction (1 item), student–student interaction (1 item), pathology-specific questions (4 items), and performance in virtual versus traditional learning (1 item).

The study’s independent variables included sex, type of university admission, and living situation. The dependent variables were the students’ perceptions of virtual learning, the Pathology I course, the Introduction to Pathology and Microbiology course, and the Pathology II course. Academic performance comparisons between virtual and traditional learning modes were assessed using students’ self-reported perceptions rather than objective examination scores.

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. The internal consistency reliability was performed using Cronbach’s alpha and found to be (0.57). Quantitative variables were expressed as the means ± standard deviations, and a one-sample t-test was performed to examine means versus the hypothetical mean. The “test value” is a hypothesized mean, which is 3 in the case of a 5-point Likert scale. Qualitative variables are described as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square goodness-of-fit test was used to evaluate differences in frequencies, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied to assess relationships in cross-tabulations. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant at the 95% confidence level.

Ethical considerations and consent

Ethical consent was obtained through a Google form from all participants. An administrative letter was obtained from the College of Medicine at the University of Bahri. Data was handled with strict confidentiality.

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Discussion

This study was a descriptive, analytical, cross-sectional, faculty-based investigation conducted at the College of Medicine, University of Bahri. A structured questionnaire designed via Google Forms was distributed to all fifth-year medical students (a total of 271) who had completed their pathology courses by 2022. The questionnaire link was made available online for one month, and 160 students returned a valid questionnaire. In the University of Bahri curriculum, students study three main pathology courses: Introduction to Pathology and Microbiology, PathologyI, and Pathology II, which are taught consecutively during the sixth, seventh, and eighth semesters. The Introduction to Pathology and Microbiology course was conducted before the COVID-19 pandemic through traditional face-to-face teaching, whereasPathology I and Pathology II were delivered virtually after the pandemic.

The findings highlight key insights into students’ demographic characteristics, preferred platforms, attitudes toward virtual learning, and comparisons with traditional learning methods.

Overview of key findings

Overall, the students demonstrated a neutral perception of virtual versus traditional learning (66.3%). Although they acknowledged the convenience and flexibility of online education, face-to-face contact during learning was highly appreciated by a noticeable number of participants. This reflects the transitional nature of virtual learning adoption in Sudan, where both infrastructure and pedagogical adaptation are still developing.

Sociodemographic characteristics

The majority of the participants were female (75%), most of the students were admitted through the general system (66.9%), lived with their families (70%), and were of moderate socioeconomic status (88.8%). This distribution aligns with national trends showing increasing female enrollment in medical colleges in Sudan. The analysis revealed no significant relationships between students’ overall perceptions and gender (p = 0.343), admission type (p = 0.249), socioeconomic status (p = 0.922), or living situation (p = 0.631). These findings suggest that attitudes toward virtual learning are largely independent of demographic differences. These results contrast with those of a study from Saudi Arabia, where female students had significantly more positive perceptions of virtual learning than male students did (p < 0.001) [10]. The absence of gender-based variation in the present study may reflect uniform exposure to technology and similar challenges faced by all students during the pandemic.

Preferred platforms and accessibility

Telegram was the most preferred learning platform (82.5%), followed by WhatsApp (13.1%), while Moodle was the least favored platform (2.5%). The relationship between preferred platform and overall virtual learning perceptions was significant (P < 0.05) indicating that platform characteristics may influence students’ attitudes.

The strong preference of Telegram over Moodle may be explained using the technology acceptance model (TAM) which emphasizes perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness as key determinants of technology adoption [18]. Telegram offers low data consumption, familiarity, rapid communication, and minimal technical barriers, making it more accessible in low resource contexts. In contrast, Moodle requires stable internet access, structured navigation, and institutional technical support, which may reduce its acceptability despite its advantages.

Interestingly, students who used Moodle reported more positive perception of virtual learning possibly reflecting the benefits of its structured learning management features. Similar findings were reported by Pereira (2018), who highlighted Telegram’s ability to enhance real-time interaction and engagement [19].

Most students reported adequate internet access and device experience, indicating that technical barriers were not a major concern. These findings align with studies in the United Kingdom, which reported that online learning saves time, offers flexibility, and reduces costs [12].

Perceptions of virtual vs. traditional learning

The students demonstrated a neutral perception of virtual versus traditional learning (66.3%). These findings don’t mirror findings from Pakistan, where 77% of students expressed negative perceptions of virtual learning [13]. The neutral perception in our study may stem from the lack of sufficient interaction and limited immediate feedback in online environments. The sudden transition to virtual learning during the COVID-19 pandemic likely exacerbated this, as both instructors and students had limited time to adapt to new methods and technologies [9]. Despite recognizing the advantages of virtual learning, some of our students felt that face-to-face contact with an instructor is necessary to learn.

Interaction and motivation

The students claimed that interaction with instructors and peers was limited in the virtual environment, likely due to the predominance of asynchronous sessions. Nevertheless, some participants appreciated the opportunity to revisit recorded lectures and materials, which 46.9% considered a key advantage (p < 0.05).

The feeling that face-to-face contact is necessary for learning was statistically significant (p < 0.05), whereas the statement that virtual learning is more motivating than traditional teaching largely disagreed (p < 0.05). These results indicate that while students valued the flexibility of virtual learning, they did not find it engaging or motivating as classroom-based instruction. This corresponds with a British study across 39 medical schools, where students found virtual learning less enjoyable and less effective for preparation for clinical practice [12]. Conversely, a Saudi study reported that approximately half of the students found virtual learning more flexible than physical classes [10], highlighting contextual differences between countries.

Course-specific perceptions

Students were more comfortable with virtual learning in Pathology II than in Pathology I course. This may reflect a gradual adaptation to the virtual environment over time. Since Pathology I was their first exposure to online learning, initial challenges such as unfamiliarity with digital platforms and reduced interactivity may have influenced their perceptions.

Comparison with international studies

The findings of this study are inconsistent with those from Pakistan and Egypt [13, 20], where most medical students preferred traditional teaching methods. However, studies from Saudi Arabia and Brazil reported higher satisfaction rates with virtual learning, suggesting that contextual factors such as technological readiness, teaching style, and institutional support influence student perceptions [10, 15].

Several international studies have noted that online learning can foster engagement through chat-based questions and interactive platforms [10, 12]. In contrast, the limited technical support and asynchronous nature of learning at the University of Bahri may have reduced these benefits.

Study limitations

When comparing perceived academic performance between virtual and traditional courses, there was no significant difference among the achieved students’ assessment results (p = 0.170). This suggests that despite perceived challenges, virtual learning does not negatively affect perceived academic outcomes. Similar findings were reported by Pei and Wu (2019), who found no significant difference in learning outcomes between online and offline medical education [21].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings, first cross-sectional design and single institution limit the generalizability of the results. Second, the response rate of 59% introduces the possibility of non-response bias, as students with particularly positive or negative experiences of virtual learning may have been more inclined to participate. Third, data collected using a self-administered questionnaire and academic performances were assessed based on students’ self-reported perceptions, which may be influenced by subjectivity and recall bias. Also, while a pilot study was conducted to assess clarity and feasibility, formal psychometric validation of the questionnaire was not performed. Another limitation of this study is the relatively low internal consistency of The Likert-scale questions (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.57). So, the items within the scale may not have reflected the intended structure with high precision and the overall perception score should be interpreted cautiously.

Important issue to notice this study reflects an early phase of virtual learning implementation during crisis conditions, which may have influenced students’ adaptation and perception. Future research should include multiple cohort studies and explore the long-term impact of blended learning approaches on medical education.

A potential limitation of this study is that multiple one-sample t-tests were conducted across several Likert-scale items. Performing multiple statistical tests may increase the possibility of Type I error (false positive findings). Therefore, the results of individual item analysis should be interpreted with caution. The findings are better understood within the context of the overall perception scores rather than relying solely on isolated item-level statistical significance.

Conclusion

Overall, our results indicate a neutral perception of virtual versus traditional learning. The students expressed a more positive perception of virtual learning in the Pathology II course than in the Pathology I course. Although virtual learning has been widely used in pathology education, studies evaluating students’ preferences and the cost-effectiveness of virtual learning technologies in Sudan are lacking. The combination of both traditional face-to-face and virtual learning strategies may be more appropriate for our students.
