Effects of xylazine on blood biochemical, oxidative, and haematological to responses to transport distance stress in day-old broiler chicks
Authors: Hussein Sajid Abdulameer, Kianoosh Zarrinkavyani, Mokhtar Fathi, Zahra Biranvand, Javad Cheraghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransport of day-old chicks is a major concern in the poultry industry, as it induces stress that can impair performance and health. This study investigated whether xylazine, an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist that reduces metabolic rate, could modulate stress responses in day-old broiler chicks during simulated road transport. A total of four hundred day-old broiler chicks were allocated in a 3 × 4 factorial design consisting of three transport distances (0, 100, and 200 km) and four injection treatments (control, saline, xylazine at 0.5 mg/kg BW, and xylazine at 1 mg/kg BW). The results showed that transport distance had significant effects (P < 0.05) on glucose, uric acid, protein, albumin, cholesterol, triglycerides, sodium, potassium, ALT, AST, malondialdehyde, glutathione peroxidase, white blood cell count, heterophil percentage, and heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, with linear trends observed for most parameters. However, xylazine administration at both 0.5 and 1 mg/kg BW did not significantly affect any of the measured parameters (P > 0.05), and no significant interaction effects between distance and treatment were observed for most parameters except for potassium (P < 0.05). Regarding interaction effects (distance × treatment), no significant interactions were observed for the majority of parameters (P > 0.05); however, a significant interaction was detected for serum potassium (P < 0.05), indicating that the effect of xylazine on potassium concentration was dependent on transport distance. In conclusion, transport distance significantly alters biochemical, oxidative, and hematological parameters in day-old broiler chicks, indicating a pronounced stress response, but xylazine at the tested doses failed to mitigate these transport-induced changes.Keywords: Xylazine, Transport, Newly hatched, Oxidative, Haematological

Introduction

Transport of day-old chicks is an inevitable procedure in the modern poultry industry, as hatcheries and broiler farms are often located far apart. However, this process is recognized as a major source of stress that can adversely affect animal welfare, health, and subsequent performance (Mitchell, 2009; Yerpes et al., 2021). Day-old chicks are particularly vulnerable to transport stress due to their immature thermoregulatory system, limited energy reserves, and prolonged periods of feed and water deprivation during transit (Shinder et al., 2007; Jacobs et al., 2016). During transport, chicks are exposed to multiple potential stressors including temperature fluctuations, relative humidity variations, vibration, noise, crowding, and poor air quality, all of which can trigger physiological stress responses (Mitchell and Kettlewell, 1998; Yerpes et al., 2021).

The consequences of transport stress on chick physiology are multifaceted. Yerpes et al. (2021) demonstrated that transport distance and duration are positively associated with weight loss and alterations in blood parameters. Specifically, relative humidity during transport was shown to negatively impact chick body weight, while the percentage of chicks loaded per journey affected first-week mortality. Furthermore, transport stress has been implicated in organ damage, particularly heart injury, through mechanisms involving endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), unfolded protein response (UPR), and autophagy-dependent pathways (Chen et al., 2022). Transport stress has also been shown to induce oxidative stress, disrupt electrolyte balance, and alter liver enzyme activities in day-old chicks (Li et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2018).

The timing of first nutrition also plays a critical role in how chicks respond to transport stress. Hollemans et al. (2018) demonstrated that early access to water and feed immediately after hatch improves body weight gain during the first weeks of life and may influence fear responses in transported chicks. Conversely, delayed access to nutrition exacerbates the negative effects of transport, potentially due to dehydration and depletion of energy reserves (Uni et al., 2003; Hollemans et al., 2018). These findings suggest that management strategies aimed at reducing metabolic demands or supporting physiological homeostasis during transport could be beneficial.

One such potential strategy is the use of pharmacological agents that reduce metabolic rate and physiological activity. Xylazine, an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist, is widely used in veterinary medicine as a sedative, analgesic, and muscle relaxant (Yaygıngül and Belge, 2018; Iacopetta et al., 2025). It acts by reducing sympathetic outflow, decreasing heart rate, lowering body temperature, and inducing a state of metabolic suppression (WHO Food Additives Series 38, 1996; Rimawi and Hamlin, 2025). Given that transport stress is characterized by heightened metabolic demands, increased sympathetic activity, and oxidative stress, it is reasonable to hypothesize that xylazine-induced metabolic reduction could mitigate the adverse effects of transport. However, the effects of xylazine on transport stress responses in day-old broiler chicks have not been systematically investigated. Therefore, the present study was designed to evaluate the effects of xylazine administration on serum biochemical parameters, liver enzymes, oxidative stress markers, and haematological stress indices in day-old broiler chicks subjected to simulated road transport.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal-related experimental procedures were performed in strict compliance with the ethical principles and regulatory guidelines governing the care and use of laboratory animals as approved by Payame Noor University (1404:9:24). The study protocol received formal approval from the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Payame Noor University. Throughout the experiment, appropriate measures were implemented to minimize animal distress and to ensure the use of the minimum number of animals required to achieve reliable scientific outcomes.

Animals and housing

A total of 400 one-day-old male Ross broiler chicks with an average body weight of 41 ± 1 g were used in this study. The chicks were obtained from a 38-week-old breeder flock in their first production cycle (average egg production: 77%). According to the health certificate issued by the company veterinarian, the flock was negative for Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), Salmonella enteritidis (SE), and Salmonella pullorum (SP), and was vaccinated against Mycoplasma synoviae (MS). Maternal antibody titers against Newcastle disease virus (NDV), avian influenza virus (AI), infectious bursal disease virus (IBD), and infectious bronchitis virus (IB) were determined using the AACC (American Association for Clinical Chemistry) recommended standard procedure with an ELISA device. For NDV and AI, specialized kits from Sin Biotech Company (Canada) were used, and for IBD and IB, kits from BioChek (Canada) were used, according to the manufacturers' instructions. The titers were as follows: NDV: 13451; AI: 14450; IBD: 11; IB: 13451

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted using a 3 × 4 factorial arrangements within a completely randomized design, consisting of three transport distances (0, 100, and 200 km) and four injection treatments (control, saline, xylazine at 0.5 mg/kg BW, and xylazine at 1 mg/kg BW). A total of 400 chicks were allocated to four treatment groups (100 chicks per treatment). Each treatment group was further subdivided according to transport distance.

Preparation of animals and injection procedures

Chicks were prepared for the experiment within the first hour of life (approximately 60 minutes after removal from the hatcher vehicle and placement into transport boxes). For individual identification, plastic wing tags were attached to the left wing of all chicks using small locking pins (2 cm length, 0.5 cm width). Each chick was individually weighed using a digital scale (precision: 0.01 g). All injections were administered subcutaneously in the posterior cervical region. The control group received no injection. The saline group received 0.5 ml of saline solution (0.9%). The two xylazine groups received 0.5 and 1 mg/kg body weight of xylazine, respectively. Xylazine mother solution (20 mg/ml, 2%) was obtained from Vet-Agro (Poland). Working solutions were prepared from the mother solution to achieve the desired doses, and a constant volume of 0.5 ml was injected into all chicks. For environmental monitoring, two mercury thermometers and two hygrometers were used, and temperature and relative humidity inside the chick boxes were recorded at two-hour intervals. During transport, the following conditions were maintained constant inside the vehicle: Temperature: 27 ± 1°C, Relative humidity: 55%−65%, Average speed: 70 km/h, Total travel distance: 200 km. Chicks were subjected to simulated transport distances of 0, 100, and 200 km, based on previous studies (Chen et al., 2022; Yerpes et al., 2021).

Blood sample collection and preparation

Immediately after chick delivery and wing tag attachment, individual body weights were recorded. At the hatchery facility, a random sample of 40 chicks was selected. After placing each chick in metal funnels, the neck vessels were severed and 1-2 cm³ of blood was collected from each individual. Blood samples were kept at room temperature for 1 hour and then transferred in a cooler containing ice. This procedure was repeated at the end of 100 and 200 km from the start of the journey. At each stage, 32 chicks (8 chicks per treatment) were randomly selected, weighed, and slaughtered. Blood samples collected in tubes without anticoagulant were allowed to clot and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm to obtain serum. Serum samples were stored at −20°C until further analysis.

Biochemical and electrolyte analysis

Serum biochemical parameters were analysed as follows: Glucose, urea, uric acid, cholesterol, and triglycerides were measured using an autoanalyzer with commercial kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran). Sodium, potassium, calcium, and phosphorus were measured using a flame photometer with specific kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran).

Liver enzyme assays

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activities were measured using commercial kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran) by an autoanalyzer (USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Antioxidant status

MDA levels were measured using the TBARS method (Mousa et al., 2012; Sahin et al., 2009). Samples were extracted with normal butanol, and absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically against a standard curve (Pars Azmoon kits). GPx activity was measured in whole blood using commercial kits (Pars Azmoon, Iran) based on NADPH oxidation measured at 340 nm (Jiang et al., 2014). Results were expressed as U/g Hb (Camkerten et al., 2016). SOD activity was measured in erythrocyte lysate using commercial kits (Pars Azmoon, Iran) based on inhibition of INT reduction by xanthine-xanthine oxidase-generated free radicals (Jiang et al., 2014). One unit causes 50% inhibition. Results expressed as U/g Hb (Camkerten et al., 2016).

Haematological parameters

Whole blood samples were collected by venipuncture into EDTA-K3 anticoagulation tubes for measuring haematological parameters including white blood cells count (WBCc), heterophils (H) and lymphocytes (L) in whole blood were analysed using an automatic blood analyser (Sysmex KX-21 N Automatic blood analyzer, Japan). For differential leukocyte counts (H/L ratio), a drop of blood was smeared on a glass slide, left to dry and then stained with Giemsa stain. One hundred leucocytes were counted on each slide including heterophils and lymphocytes. The H/L ratio was calculated by dividing the number of heterophils by the number of lymphocytes. Two slides were counted and the means were calculated for each bird (Rasha et al., 2017).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using a general linear model (GLM) or mixed model appropriate for the factorial design. Pen or individual chick served as the experimental unit depending on the parameter. Fixed factors included treatment (control, saline, xylazine 0.5 mg/kg, xylazine 1 mg/kg), distance (0, 100, 200 km), and their interaction. When necessary, data were transformed logarithmically or square-root to meet normality assumptions. Differences among means with P ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Discussion

Consistent with this study, increased glucose, albumin, and uric acid after transport have been reported (Hussnain et al., 2020; Ncho et al., 2025). Transport stress activates the HPA axis and stress hormones, leading to glycogen depletion, increased glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, and elevated blood glucose to meet energy demands. Consistent with this mechanism, the significant increase in serum glucose with transport distance reflects stress-induced activation of the HPA axis and enhanced gluconeogenesis, further confirming that the transport protocol imposed a physiological stress response.

Increased albumin is mainly due to dehydration during long journeys, while higher uric acid results from protein breakdown under stress. Corticosteroids further enhance protein degradation and reduce protein synthesis in muscles (Gao et al., 2008). Studies report inconsistent effects of transport on cholesterol and triglycerides, with some showing increases (Collier et al., 2017) and others decreases (Tank and Wong. 2014). These changes are mainly due to stress-axis activation, altered liver metabolism, and changes in lipid-related gene expression (Gao et al., 2008). Elevated corticosteroids increase lipid synthesis and may promote fat accumulation in tissues (Hanauer et al., 2023). Additionally, transport stress induces oxidative stress, damaging the liver and increasing ALT and AST levels (Lin et al., 2021). It also disrupts key metabolic pathways (AMPK, SIRT1, PPARα/γ, Nrf2), leading to increased fat synthesis, reduced fat oxidation, and fat accumulation in the liver (Lu et al., 2021). The severity of these effects depends on transport duration and intensity (Ovsiannikova et al., 2024), as well as bird genetics and nutritional status (Zhang et al., 2017). Overall, these responses raise blood levels of glucose, uric acid, cholesterol, and triglycerides as part of an adaptive energy mechanism.

Studies show that xylazine does not significantly affect blood glucose levels in chickens (Dalir-Naghadeh et al., 2006; Mousa, 2021), unlike in other species where it may cause temporary hyperglycaemia due to reduced insulin secretion (Naghadeh et al., 2006; Elnasser et al., 2018). This suggests chickens are less sensitive to xylazine in terms of glucose metabolism. Findings indicate that increased glucose is associated with increased uric acid, likely due to stress-induced protein breakdown and gluconeogenesis (Xiao et al., 2022; Hanna et al., 2024). Transport stress, dehydration, and lack of water further elevate uric acid levels, while xylazine does not appear to reduce these metabolic processes. Evidence from other species suggests possible decreases due to anaesthesia-related physiological changes, but this is not confirmed in poultry (Ravindran, 2013). Similarly, no direct research has evaluated the effect of xylazine on cholesterol and triglycerides in chickens. Limited evidence from other species (e.g., goats) shows some increase in cholesterol at specific doses, but results are inconsistent (Botreau et al., 2007). Overall, more targeted research in chickens is needed for definitive conclusions.

It has been reported that increasing transport increases serum calcium levels (Zaytsoff et al., 2019). Furthermore, although changes in phosphorus are less frequently reported, research indicates that transport stress can also affect blood phosphorus levels, and these changes are dependent on the duration and severity of stress (Dai et al., 2022). These changes are mainly caused by disruption of ionic homeostasis, reduced activity of ion-transporting enzymes, and changes in the expression of genes related to mineral absorption. Transport stress disrupts the activity of ion-transporting enzymes (e.g., Ca²⁺-ATPase) and reduces their activity in the heart and other tissues, leading to changes in the distribution and levels of blood calcium and phosphorus (Miao et al., 2022). Stress and delayed feeding post-hatch can also alter the expression of calcium and phosphorus transporter genes in the intestine (Li et al., 2022). Currently, no valid scientific findings exist regarding the effect of xylazine on blood calcium and phosphorus in chickens that would confirm or contradict the results of the present study, and information on the effects of other anaesthetic or sedative drugs on mineral metabolism in poultry cannot be generalised or compared with the present study.

Transport stress can alter blood zinc levels in chickens by inducing oxidative stress and altering the expression of zinc transporter genes. Transport increases free radicals and oxidative stress. Zinc plays a key role in the antioxidant system (e.g., superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, and metallothionein). The activity of these antioxidants depends on cofactors such as selenium for glutathione peroxidase and Zn, Cu, and Mn for superoxide dismutase, and the body increases the expression of these proteins to combat stress (Xiao et al., 2022). Therefore, blood zinc levels may decrease or increase depending on the intensity and duration of stress, due to increased absorption and transport to tissues or increased antioxidant consumption.

Transport causes significant changes in blood iron, sodium, and potassium concentrations in chickens. These changes are mainly due to activation of the stress axis, disruption of mineral homeostasis, and altered expression of related genes. Transport stress causes a significant increase in serum iron levels and iron accumulation in the liver of chickens; these changes are accompanied by increased expression of genes and proteins related to iron metabolism, indicating disruption of iron homeostasis and increased absorption and storage in the liver (Liu et al., 2021). Furthermore, it has been reported that transport stress and distance cause significant changes in blood sodium and potassium levels. Typically, with increasing duration and intensity of stress, serum sodium and potassium levels increase (Al-Aqil and Zulkifli, 2009). These results are consistent with and reinforce reports in the literature. Under stress conditions, birds, while gathering the necessary energy for adaptation processes, focus all their efforts on maintaining internal environment homeostasis. Gathering energy generally requires various physiological efforts at different sites. Increased serum iron, sodium, and potassium concentrations in plasma may be a sign of stress and the bird's adaptation process.

Although the findings of the present study did not confirm an effect of xylazine treatments on oxidative indices and antioxidant responses, it has been reported that xylazine can transiently and indirectly increase antioxidant capacity through its sedative effect and reduction of metabolic activity (Sheverini et al., 2024). Consistent with the findings of the present study, it has been reported that transport stress, on the one hand, increases free radical production and increases lipid peroxidation (MDA) and liver enzymes (ALT, AST) and, on the other hand, reduces the activity and capacity of antioxidant enzymes such as GPx and SOD. Reduced antioxidant reserves disrupt the oxidant/antioxidant balance and lead to reduced body defence capacity against free radicals and tissue and cellular damage (Faraguna et al., 2025).

Long-term transport (more than four hours) in animals induces significant changes in blood biochemical indices. It has been reported that this condition causes a significant increase in serum enzymes including ALT, AST, MDA, and creatinine (Sheverini et al., 2024). Furthermore, several other indices such as creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), AST, cortisol (COR), and ACTH have been shown to increase after transport. These changes indicate muscle and liver damage, activation of the HPA axis, and occurrence of an acute stress response (Wang et al., 2020). Our findings also showed a similar increase in these indices, which is in line with the results of Wang et al. 2020 and confirms the role of long-distance transport in inducing systemic stress and tissue damage.

In day-old chickens, transport stress has been reported to induce not only cardiac damage but also severe oxidative stress. Xu et al. (2022) demonstrated that transport stress can disrupt the NO-NOS metabolic system, leading to excessive nitric oxide (NO) production and subsequent cellular damage. Furthermore, these authors reported that in response to such stress, the Nrf2/HO-1/NQO1 antioxidant defense pathway is activated as a compensatory mechanism to mitigate oxidative damage.

Although our study did not directly measure NO, NOS, HO-1, or NQO1, our findings are consistent with the oxidative stress framework reported by Xu et al. (2022). Specifically, we observed that long-distance transport (200 km) significantly increased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and decreased glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities in day-old broiler chicks. These changes indicate a state of oxidative stress, which aligns with the mechanistic pathways described in the literature. However, direct measurement of NO-NOS and Nrf2-related parameters was beyond the scope of the present study and should be addressed in future investigations.

Overall, the results of the present study show that long-distance transport, in addition to causing biochemical changes and tissue damage, also activates antioxidant defence pathways. The similarity of our findings with those of Wang et al. (2020) and Xu et al. (2022) indicates that transport stress not only causes extensive damage at the cellular and tissue levels but also activates antioxidant defence mechanisms as a compensatory response. This consistency strengthens the validity of the present findings and highlights the importance of managing transport conditions in young animals. The intensity and duration of stress influence the extent of tissue damage and antioxidant capacity; in the present study, the peak stress and highest antioxidant capacity were observed at 200 km.

No direct studies exist on the effect of subcutaneous xylazine injection on leukocyte profiles in chickens. In other species (horses, ruminants), xylazine does not cause significant changes in leukocyte counts, or changes are transient and non-significant. Through its sedative effect and reduction of the stress response, xylazine may indirectly induce minor changes in leukocyte profile, but sufficient evidence in chickens is lacking (Ali et al., 2006).

However, the findings of the present study regarding distance are consistent with and show a similar trend to results reported by previous researchers. The consistent findings indicate that transport stress has significant effects on the number and function of white blood cells and specifically increases heterophil counts and the H/L ratio (Huff et al., 2005). These changes are mainly induced through activation of the HPA axis and increased secretion of stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline.

Road transport, as an acute stressor, leads to an increased white blood cell count (leukocytosis). Increased heterophils under these conditions is considered a normal response to stress, observed at distances of 100 and 200 km. We found that with increasing transport distance, a decrease in lymphocyte count also occurred; however, this decrease was not dramatic. It was expected that with longer distances and the transition of stress to a chronic state, the immunosuppressive effect of cortisol would lead to a significant decrease in lymphocyte count. However, at 200 km, the intensity of stress and immune suppression was not sufficient to cause such a marked decrease. Overall, what is clear is that stress alters the distribution of white blood cells in the body, such that cells migrate from the bloodstream to tissues such as skin, lung, and liver to prepare the organism for a "fight or flight" response.

A major limitation of this study is the absence of pharmacodynamic validation data. We did not measure sedation scores, heart rate, or body temperature – parameters that are commonly used to confirm alpha-2 agonist activity. This omission is particularly critical because chickens are known to be relatively less sensitive to alpha-2 agonists compared with mammals. Furthermore, we fully acknowledge that we did not measure serum corticosterone in this study. Although we used the heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H/L) ratio – a well-established, indirect indicator of chronic stress in poultry – we agree that this alone is insufficient to definitively validate the stress model, particularly in the absence of direct glucocorticoid measurement. Without corticosterone (the gold-standard biomarker of HPA axis activation in birds) and without pharmacodynamic confirmation of xylazine activity, we cannot definitively conclude whether the lack of efficacy reflects a genuine absence of anti-stress effects, insufficient stress model validation, or simply that the xylazine doses were too low to produce any physiological effect in chicks. Future studies should investigate higher doses of xylazine alongside direct pharmacodynamic monitoring (sedation scoring, heart rate, body temperature) and direct glucocorticoid measurement (serum corticosterone) to definitively validate both the stress model and the pharmacological activity of the drug.

Conclusion

In summary, transport distance significantly alters biochemical, electrolyte, liver enzyme, oxidative, and hematological parameters in day-old broiler chicks, confirming a pronounced systemic stress response. However, xylazine at 0.5 and 1 mg/kg BW failed to mitigate these changes, with only a significant interaction observed for potassium. A limitation of this study is the lack of pharmacodynamic validation (e.g., sedation scores, heart rate, body temperature), especially given that chickens are less sensitive to alpha-2 agonists than mammals. Therefore, we cannot definitively conclude whether xylazine lacks genuine anti-stress effects or whether the doses were insufficient. Future studies should investigate higher doses alongside direct physiological monitoring.
