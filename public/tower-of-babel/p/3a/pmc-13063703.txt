Characterizing multimorbidity and the risk for hospitalization among people living with HIV from the African Cohort Study
Authors: Remle Scott, Cara H Olsen, Trevor A Crowell, James D Mancuso, Hannah Kibuuka, Jonah Maswai, John Owuoth, Valentine Sing’oei, Emmanuel Bahemana, Zahra Parker, Julie A Ake, Neha Shah, Elizabeth H Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity presents significant challenges for people living with HIV (PLWH) in sub-Saharan Africa given the dual burden of communicable and noncommunicable diseases. We aimed to characterize multimorbidity and examine its association with hospitalization among PLWH.MethodsThe African Cohort Study (AFRICOS) prospectively enrolls participants aged ≥ 15 years with and without HIV at 12 sites in Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and Nigeria. Our analyses were restricted to PLWH and ≥ 18 years of age at enrollment between February 2013 and May 2024. Multimorbidity, defined as the presence of two or more chronic conditions in addition to HIV, was ascertained through International Classification of Diseases version 10 codes on medical history documentation at study enrollment. Hospitalization was ascertained through case reporting forms. Hierarchical clustering was used to describe multimorbidity, and logistic regression reporting adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) was used to evaluate the association between multimorbidity and hospitalization.ResultsOf 3199 PLWH, 1863 (58.2%) were women, and the median age was 37.3 years (interquartile range: 29.2–45.5). Multimorbidity prevalence was 12.4%. Anemia (8.3%), head-related disorders (7.3%), and neuropathy (6.6%) were most common. Hierarchical clustering identified gastrointestinal (n = 80) and neuropathy (n = 212) first. The hospitalization rate was 4.32 hospitalizations per 100 person-years. Multimorbidity was associated with increased hospitalization (aOR = 1.44; 95% CI, 1.06, 1.96) after adjusting for age, sex, country, antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimen, and viral suppression.ConclusionsMultimorbidity was relatively uncommon at enrollment among PLWH in AFRICOS; however, distinct comorbidity patterns—such as gastrointestinal conditions and neuropathy—highlight the need for integrated, context-specific care. Multimorbidity was associated with a higher risk for hospitalization, while factors such as age, ART regimen, and viral suppression point to key intervention targets. These findings emphasize the importance of monitoring multimorbidity over time and adapting HIV care models to address the emerging chronic disease burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44263-026-00260-3.Keywords: Multimorbidity, Hospitalization, HIV, Sub-Saharan Africa, Chronic conditions, AFRICOS, Cohort study, Integrated care, Noncommunicable diseases

Background

There is no unifying definition for multimorbidity, but it is broadly defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic health conditions in an individual [1]. Understanding the prevalence, burden, and impact of multimorbidity in sub-Saharan Africa is important, as noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are collectively projected to become the leading cause of mortality by 2030 [2]. The dual burden of communicable diseases and NCDs, coupled with the syndemic interaction between social, environmental, and structural factors impacting health in the sub-Saharan Africa region, makes studying multimorbidity among people living with HIV (PLWH) challenging, complex, and of crucial importance.

Evidence suggests that PLWH are more likely to have multimorbidity compared to people living without HIV (PLWoH), and as a result, they experience increased risk for higher healthcare utilization and costs, hospitalization, and mortality [3, 4]. However, the available literature on multimorbidity among PLWH is limited to a preselected number of conditions, primarily focusing on cardiovascular, metabolic/endocrine, and urogenital conditions, in high-income countries (HICs) in North America and Europe [1]. When comparing the prevalence of multimorbidity between low- and middle-income country (LMICs) and HICs, there is a marginal difference with pooled estimates of 43.5% and 41.3%, respectively [5]. However, in sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence varies widely from 5.4% in Botswana to 71% in Nigeria [6]. These significant regional differences are a result of diverse health behaviors, population characteristics, and healthcare access. A review of LMICs studies on the prevalence and patterns of multimorbidity found that only six were conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, with 39% of studies using 8–12 chronic conditions to determine multimorbidity [7]. Rarer, complex comorbidities documented among PLWH include mental health conditions and sexually transmitted diseases, as well as symptoms such as peripheral neuropathy, all of which may be associated with a range of multimorbidity outcomes, including hospitalization [1].

Reducing the prevalence of multimorbidity is an important part of achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3.4, which aims to reduce one-third of global premature mortality from noncommunicable diseases by 2030 [8]. On a national scale, a high prevalence of multimorbidity can be an indicator of high mortality and increased healthcare use [9]. Our study addresses a critical void in the literature on multimorbidity among people with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa. The available evidence is limited on which specific chronic conditions tend to cluster in this population and lacks data on the clinical impact of multimorbidity, particularly its association with hospitalization. By providing a comprehensive analysis, this analysis will contribute to a clearer understanding of the burden of multimorbidity in PLWH and the heterogeneity of health outcomes in this region.

The African Cohort Study (AFRICOS) was established in 2013 as an observational study to help guide programming and policy for the US President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief. AFRICOS evaluates the long-term impact of clinical practices, biological factors, and socio-behavioral issues on disease progression and HIV infection in an African context. Previously published data from the multicountry AFRICOS found that HIV was associated with an increased risk for NCDs in this cohort [10]. Our analysis builds on this finding by providing the first characterization of both multimorbidity and hospitalization related to multimorbidity within AFRICOS. Understanding which chronic conditions cluster in PLWH can provide crucial clinical guidance on how to integrate chronic disease management with HIV care. Accordingly, our objectives for this analysis are to describe the epidemiology of multimorbidity and evaluate the relationship between hospitalization and multimorbidity in a cohort of PLWH in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

Study setting and population

AFRICOS is an ongoing longitudinal, prospective cohort study of HIV pathogenesis, comorbidities, and outcomes led by the US Military HIV Research Program (MHRP) at 12 clinics across five programs supported by the US President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR): Kayunga, Uganda; South Rift Valley, Kenya; Kisumu West, Kenya; Mbeya, Tanzania; and Lagos and Abuja, Nigeria [10]. Recruitment began in 2013 from randomized lists of current PEPFAR clinic patients. Enrollment criteria into AFRICOS include individuals who are ≥ 15 years old and who consent to data and specimen collection. PLWH participants must be enrolled for continuous HIV care at a PEPFAR clinic, be long-term residents of the clinic catchment area, and understand English or the local language.

Our analyses were restricted to PLWH who were 18 years or older at enrollment into AFRICOS and had a completed medical history examination between February 2013 and May 2024. All study participants provided written informed consent, and institutional review boards of the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research, Makerere University School of Public Health, Kenya Medical Research Institute, Tanzania National Institute of Medical Research, Nigerian Ministry of Defense, and the Uniformed Services University approved the study. Institutional review boards (IRB) were approved for each AFRICOS site.

Data collection and definitions

AFRICOS visits occurred every 6 months, and participants provided medical history, completed a physical examination, and underwent laboratory assessments. PLWH underwent CD4 T‐lymphocyte count and HIV viral load testing at every visit. Study clinicians reviewed medical records and extracted antiretroviral therapy (ART) start date and medication regimen at every visit. The AFRICOS study employed a robust, dual-source method to capture chronic conditions. At each study visit, AFRICOS clinicians assigned diagnoses based on clinical and laboratory values, and the corresponding International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) codes were recorded on the case reporting form (CRF) [10]. For completeness, information about the participant’s AFRICOS diagnoses was shared with their primary provider. Subsequently, if the primary provider made additional diagnoses between AFRICOS study visits, the AFRICOS study clinician would extract and record those ICD-10 codes as well [10]. Our main exposure of interest was multimorbidity, which we defined as two or more chronic conditions in addition to HIV at enrollment into AFRICOS [1, 11]. We also calculated the total number of chronic conditions and the prevalence of each chronic condition group. The list of conditions reported at enrollment was extracted from medical history CRF using ICD-10 diagnostic codes and was defined as chronic using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Chronic Condition Indicator Refined for ICD-10-CM database [12]. Chronic condition categories were grouped based on the AHRQ clinical classification categories and reviewed by two clinical subject matter experts. The chronic conditions were categorized into the following groups: Allergic rhinitis, anemia, bone/joint/musculoskeletal, cancer, diabetes, diarrhea, epilepsy, eye, gastrointestinal, head related, hemorrhage, heart, hypertension, lipodystrophy, liver, malnourishment, mental health, menstrual, mouth sore, neuropathy, obesity, paralyzed, respiratory, sexually transmitted infections, skin, and thyroid conditions (Supplementary Material 1: Table S1).

Hospitalization was our primary outcome, defined dichotomously as yes, by the presence of at least one hospitalization date following enrollment into AFRICOS. AFRICOS participants were encouraged to notify the study coordinators if they were hospitalized. An AFRICOS team member then completed a CRF, recording clinical details of the visit, any diagnoses, and treatment. If a participant did not immediately notify a team member, they were asked at the next scheduled study visit, and a CRF was filled out at that time. Hospital visits were included in the analysis, provided the visit occurred between the enrollment date and the date of the last visit. We also describe the total number of hospitalization events among PLWH over the analysis period. Demographic variables included biological sex (male, female), age (in years), and country (Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania, Uganda) reported at enrollment.

Several HIV characteristics measured at enrollment were included: duration on ART (ART naive, < 6 months, 6 months to < 5 years, 5 or more years, missing), ART regimen (TLE: tenofovir, lamivudine or emtricitabine, and efavirenz; TLD: tenofovir, lamivudine or emtricitabine, and dolutegravir; zidovudine [AZT] containing; protease inhibitors [PI], other [primarily nevirapine containing], ART naive, and missing), CD4 count and CD4 count nadir (< 200, 200–349, 350–499, 500+ cells/mm3), years living with an HIV diagnosis, and viral suppression (1000 copies/mL, ≥ 1000 copies/mL).

Statistical analysis

We calculated the frequency and percentage of participant demographics, the number of chronic conditions, and chronic condition groups measured at enrollment. Hierarchical clustering was performed on the chronic condition groups. Condition groups with a prevalence < 2.0% were excluded from analyses to minimize the sequential joining of low prevalence comorbidities into existing clusters [13]. We used the Mercator package that facilitates the exploration of binary data sets by first calculating a distance metric and generating a dendrogram to visualize the clusters [14]. The Jaccard similarity index was chosen to calculate distance because it ignores the insignificant non-disease matches and is specifically designed to calculate distances for sparse binary data [15]. We then conducted a sensitivity analysis that restricted anemia to the subset of individuals who were not prescribed AZT, as anemia is a known side effect of AZT. We hypothesized a priori that the anemia subgroups may cluster differently with comorbid conditions [16].

We calculated the prevalence of hospitalization, the total number of hospitalization events, person-time at risk, and the hospitalization rate over the study period. Comparisons by hospitalization (yes/no) were made using Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for continuous variables. Median values and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables were reported. A final, parsimonious logistic regression model was adjusted for age, sex, country, significant variables identified in the bivariate analysis, and known HIV characteristics identified in the literature that may impact the association between multimorbidity (defined as two or more conditions) and hospitalization [10, 16, 17]. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Model convergence, variance inflation factor measurements, and correlation matrices were assessed to test multicollinearity, and variables were removed accordingly for the final adjusted model. Missing data for covariates were managed based on the proportion of missingness observed. For variables with less than 5% missingness, a complete-case approach was utilized. For covariates with 5% or greater missingness, a “Missing” indicator category was created to retain the full cohort and preserve statistical power for the analysis. This approach allowed for the explicit modeling of the distinct risk profile associated with unrecorded clinical values in a programmatic setting. Assessment of missingness patterns for ART-related variables was conducted to identify potential mechanisms of missingness, such as site-specific documentation or data extraction differences.

A post hoc sensitivity analysis assessed potential effect modification by age and country. Hierarchical cluster analysis was implemented using R statistical software, while all subsequent data management and regression modeling were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (Supplementary Material 2: Code) [14, 18].

Results

Study population

There were 3199 PLWH eligible for inclusion in our analysis (Table 1). The majority of the population were women (n = 1863, 58.2%), from Kenya (n = 1671, 52.2%), and had completed no more than a primary education (n = 1780, 55.6%). A third of the cohort was on a TLE-containing ART regimen (n = 1031, 32.2%), and 19% (n = 614) were ART naive at enrollment. The average years of living with HIV prior to enrollment was 2.82 years (IQR, 0.23–6.78). A total of 18.6% (n = 596) of the cohort had a CD4 count < 200 cells/mm3 at enrollment.

Compared to participants who did not report a hospitalization, those hospitalized were women (68.6% versus 56.3%, p < 0.001) and older (median 38.5 years [IQR, 30.1–46.7] versus 37.0 [IQR, 29.0–45.2], p = 0.049) (Table 1). Multimorbidity, defined as two or more conditions, was more prevalent among hospitalized participants (15.2% vs 11.9%, p = 0.04), as were ART-naive individuals (29.6% v 17.2%, p < 0.0001).

Multimorbidity

The prevalence of multimorbidity was 12.4% (n = 397) (Table 1). By condition group, the most prevalent chronic conditions were all forms of anemia (n = 266, 8.3%); head-related conditions including migraine, traumatic brain injury, and neurological headaches (n = 234, 7.3%); neuropathy (n = 212, 6.6%); and sexually transmitted infections (n = 190, 5.9%) (Supplementary Material 1: Table S1). The prevalence of anemia excluding those on AZT was 5.8% (n = 187).

Hierarchical clustering was performed on 10 chronic condition groups with a prevalence ≥ 2% (Fig. 1 and Table 2). Gastrointestinal conditions and neuropathy exhibited the higher degree of co-occurrence, evidenced by the smallest Jaccard distance, and formed the first cluster. A second distinct cluster emerged between head related conditions and mouth sores. Subsequently, bone, joint, and musculoskeletal conditions joined the gastrointestinal-neuropathy pair, forming a larger cluster. To assess the impact of medication-induced anemia, a sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding anemia diagnoses among patients prescribed AZT; the resulting clustering patterns remained consistent with the primary analysis.

Hospitalization

The period prevalence of hospitalization was 16%, and a total of 513 PLWH were hospitalized for 770 hospitalization events in the cohort. There were a total of 17,828.6 person-years at risk for hospitalization and a rate of 4.32 hospitalizations per 100 person-years. Individually, the follow-up period ranged from less than a year to 10.5 years, with a median follow-up of 6.7 years [IQR, 2.3–8.1].

Multimorbidity and hospitalization

PLWH who reported multimorbidity at enrollment had a 1.31 (95% CI, 1.01, 1.72) higher unadjusted odds of being hospitalized over the analysis period compared to those without multimorbidity (Table 3). Age, sex, country, education, ART regimen, duration on ART, and viral suppression were all significantly associated with hospitalization in the unadjusted analyses. Individuals who were ART naive at enrollment had a 2.41 (95% CI, 1.85, 3.13) higher odds of being hospitalized compared to those on a TLE-containing ART regimen. Individuals with a CD4 count < 200 cells/mm3 had a 1.46 (95% CI, 1.13, 1.90) higher odds of being hospitalized than those with a count of 500+ cells/mm3. After adjusting for age, sex, country, ART regimen, and viral suppression, PLWH with multimorbidity had significant 1.44 higher odds of being hospitalized (aOR = 1.44 [95% CI, 1.06, 1.96]). PLWH who were from Tanzania or Uganda (aOR = 5.43 [95% CI, 4.15, 7.11] and aOR = 5.40 [95% CI, 4.08, 7.15], respectively), women (aOR = [1.77% CI, 1.42, 2.20]), and PLWH on a protease inhibitor (aOR = 2.20 [95% CI, 1.38, 3.51]) were significantly more likely to be hospitalized compared to PLWH from Kenya, men, and those on a TLE-containing ART regimen.

Missingness for clinical covariates was most notable for ART regimen and duration on ART, which were unavailable for 9% and 7% of participants, respectively. Further assessment revealed that missingness for ART regimen varied significantly by country, suggesting a mechanism of missing not at random (MNAR). In the final adjusted model, the risk profile for those with missing ART regimen (aOR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.52–1.26) and missing viral suppression (aOR, 1.17; 95% CI, 0.46–3.01) was modeled as distinct categories.

We conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis to assess potential effect modification by age and country. The interaction between age and multimorbidity was non-significant (p = 0.30), and a stratified analyses confirmed that the association between multimorbidity and hospitalization remained consistent across all countries, with no evidence of geographic effect modification.

Discussion

In AFRICOS, among PLWH, we found a total of 12.4% of eligible participants were living with multimorbidity at enrollment, defined as two or more conditions, and 16% reported at least one hospitalization over the study period. Multimorbidity was significantly associated with hospitalization after adjusting for age, sex, country, and viral suppression.

Our cluster analysis grouped gastrointestinal conditions and neuropathy first. There is evidence to support distal symmetric polyneuropathy (DSP) as one of the most common neurologic complications of HIV, and autonomic neuropathy to be a common comorbidity of HIV DSP, and a predictor for morbidity and mortality among PLWH [19, 20]. Common symptoms associated with autonomic neuropathy are gastrointestinal and include diarrhea [21]. Two processes are thought to contribute to the development of DSP including the direct neurotoxicity of HIV and its products and the neurotoxicity of combined ART medications [19, 22]. This comorbidity cluster could be a complication or symptom of ART medication, as the cluster was not associated with hospitalization, but ART was a significant predictor in our regression models.

Despite very limited cluster analyses examining multimorbidity among PLWH, the Africa Wits-INDEPTH partnership for Genomic Studies performed a cluster analysis and found that the most prevalent cluster of four diseases was dyslipidemia, hypertension, HIV, and chronic kidney disease [23]. Their findings were supported by a high prevalence of adults receiving care for diabetes and hypertension, coinciding with a high prevalence of obesity. The prevalence of diabetes and obesity in AFRICOS was < 1% and therefore did not meet the eligibility criteria for inclusion in our cluster analysis. However, we did identify hypertension as a cluster with lipodystrophy. Previous analysis from AFRICOS also found that over a quarter of PLWH aged 50 years or older had elevated blood pressure, and that over 15% of enrollees reported dysglycemia, which are consistent risk factors for diabetes and chronic kidney disease [24–26]. As the cohort ages, clinical management may consider these comorbidity clusters as an important finding that can support the need for integrated chronic care models for PLWH.

These differences in cluster formation may reflect the low prevalence of chronic conditions including diabetes and chronic kidney disease in AFRICOS, which resulted in a multimorbidity prevalence of 12.4%. This finding is notably lower than prevalence estimates reported in the region. Pooled estimates for sub-Saharan Africa reported a prevalence of 28.2%, although this was primarily driven by studies from South Africa [9]. Regional data comparing prevalence rates ranged from 20.2% in West Africa to over 60% in South Africa, although multimorbidity was restricted to seven conditions leveraging laboratory measurements, and populations were restricted to older populations aged 40–60 years [23]. The observed low prevalence of multimorbidity may be a factor in our finding that multimorbidity was not significantly associated with hospitalization in the final adjusted model. Given these analyses were restricted to data at enrollment, and nearly a quarter of the cohort enrolled is aged 15–24 years, multimorbid conditions may not have developed [27]. Multimorbidity may also be less common as a result of healthy user bias, which has been well documented in research, in which case PLWH may be more likely to enroll in AFRICOS compared to the general HIV population [28].

Hospitalization was observed in 16% of our cohort and, as a significant clinical event, requires careful consideration of its association with multimorbidity. The prevalence of this outcome may be lower than in other reported studies due to the relatively younger profile of our cohort. A cohort of PLWH receiving care from 1996 to 2016 examined trends in all-cause hospitalization rates and found older age was associated with higher rates of hospitalization [17, 29, 30]. A cohort study in rural South Africa of adults 40 years or older compared HIV and diabetes status to evaluate healthcare utilization, expenditure, and comorbidity [31]. They found no observed differences in hospitalizations between virally suppressed PLWH and PLWoH, but when compared to those with diabetes, they saw an increased prevalence of chronic conditions and increased hospital visits. These findings suggested that PLWH in South Africa may visit public clinics more often than PLWoH, and that the positive health outcomes observed, such as reduced comorbidities, may be explained by different healthcare engagement patterns when compared to the diabetes group that reported more comorbidities. Our findings also support that with increasing age, PLWH are more likely to be hospitalized, and our analysis describes differences between VL and duration on ART as significant factors impacting hospitalization among PLWH.

Most studies investigating hospitalization and multimorbidity have only compared PLWH and PLWoH in older populations [31]. Our study has characterized multimorbidity clusters and described hospitalization in a multicountry cohort of PLWH in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite the low prevalence of multimorbidity at enrollment in AFRICOS, hierarchical clustering allowed for the exploration of rare comorbidities that have not previously been documented among PLWH. Understanding the prevalence of multimorbidity and how NCDs cluster in a population of PLWH can inform screening, diagnosis, and the integrated management and treatment of NCDs in resource-limited environments. In particular, public health programs that target the prevention of chronic conditions such as hypertension can benefit from understanding HIV-specific co-occurring conditions that may impact clinical health outcomes. While patients with multiple conditions are often excluded from clinical trials, in practice, patients who are experiencing multimorbidity may receive treatments that are beneficial to individual conditions without fully understanding how they may impact the other conditions and recommended treatments [31]. This could lead to issues of polypharmacy and adverse drug reactions [32, 33].

Our analyses had several limitations inherent to the use of secondary program data. First, the data were collected within a clinical program environment, which may have affected overall data quality and consistency in how conditions were recorded across different sites. However, the dual-source mechanism employed by AFRICOS—combining direct study-visit diagnoses with systematic extraction of inter-visit primary provider diagnoses—provides a substantially more complete ascertainment of chronic conditions than typical single-source administrative data. While we lacked the supplemental clinical data required to validate the comprehensive ICD-10 codes used in this analysis, and this reliance on programmatic records may suggest misclassification of hospitalization and comorbidities, we anticipate any under-ascertainment to be non-differential. Consequently, this would likely bias our findings toward a more conservative estimate of the associations between multimorbidity and hospitalization.

Furthermore, our use of programmatic data resulted in notable missingness for key clinical variables, such as ART regimen (9%) and duration (7%). While including “Missing” as a category preserved statistical power, the variation in missingness by country suggests the data may be missing not at random (MNAR), potentially introducing bias into the coefficient estimates for the non-missing groups. Additionally, our cluster methodology examined associations between chronic conditions rather than clustering respondents, which prevented the description of specific multimorbidity phenotypes of individuals. We also limited the cluster analysis to conditions with a prevalence greater than 2% to ensure the stability of the models, though all chronic conditions were included in the broader definition of multimorbidity used for regression.

Additionally, our inclusion of chronic diseases was limited to those reported at enrollment, and our analysis was cross-sectional; therefore, we could only assess the association of multimorbidity and hospitalization, not causality. Residual confounding may also exist if there are additional factors at play that were not available from the data routinely collected by AFRICOS. Previous evidence using longitudinal measures and assessment found PLWH were at an increased risk for comorbidities up to 10 years before HIV diagnosis, revealing other behavioral and social factors were important [34]. These findings may not be generalizable to other populations of PLWH due to health-user bias as study participants may have different health-seeking behaviors.

Conclusions

These analyses offer a detailed assessment of multimorbidity and hospitalization among PLWH in sub-Saharan Africa, using data from the multicountry AFRICOS cohort. Although the prevalence of multimorbidity at enrollment was relatively low, important clinical patterns emerged. Specifically, the clustering of neuropathy and gastrointestinal conditions may reflect the dual burden of HIV-related complications and antiretroviral therapy side effects, highlighting a critical need for tailored chronic disease management in HIV care. Furthermore, multimorbidity was independently associated with an increased risk of hospitalization. When adjusted for age, sex, country, ART regimen, and viral suppression significant risk factors remained—particularly among women, participants in Tanzania and Uganda, and those on protease inhibitor-containing regimens. The low hospitalization rates in this younger cohort suggest that early access to HIV care may reduce severe outcomes, though chronic conditions may become more prevalent as the population ages. These findings underscore the urgency of developing integrated, age-appropriate models of care that address the intersection of HIV and NCDs. Public health strategies and future research should explore how multimorbidity evolves over time and ensure that clinical guidelines evolve alongside the shifting needs of aging PLWH in sub-Saharan Africa.
