Evidence of variations in genetic prevalence for loss-of-function PKP2 variants between individuals with European and African ancestry
Authors: Alexandra Winters, Renae Judy, Choudhary Anwar A Chahal, Ghaith Sharaf Dabbagh, Ravi A Shah, Scott Damrauer, Melissa A Kelly, Cynthia A James, Crystal Tichnell, Brittney Murray, Diane Smelser, H Shanker Rao, David J Carey, Christopher M Haggerty, Eric D Carruth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeArrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is an inherited condition associated with increased risk for ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. Prior ARVC studies contained majority European-ancestry individuals; however, limited data have shown an increase in disease-associated variants in individuals of African ancestry. This study aimed to assess genotype/phenotype differences by ancestry across several cohorts.MethodsWe analyzed genomic and health record data from over 660,000 individuals from 4 population biobanks. We compared the prevalence of predicted pathogenic variants in 4 ARVC-associated genes across ancestries and the association with ARVC-related traits.ResultsWe observed that individuals with a PKP2 predicted pathogenic variant were 2 to 3 times as likely to be of African ancestry than European ancestry in MyCode, All of Us, and the UK Biobank. This difference was not seen in the Penn Medicine Biobank (PMBB). PKP2 predicted pathogenic heterozygotes were more likely to have an ARVC-related trait in the MyCode and All of Us cohorts but not in PMBB or UK Biobank.ConclusionAlthough individuals of African ancestry are more likely to carry a PKP2 predicted pathogenic variant, only European ancestry heterozygotes in MyCode and All of Us had increased odds of an ARVC-related trait, suggesting reduced penetrance or clinical underrecognition of disease in individuals of African ancestry.Keywords: Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), Desmosome genes, Genetic ancestry, Genotype-phenotype correlation, Population genomics

Introduction

Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is an inherited cardiac condition associated with increased risk for ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. Genetically, ARVC is known as a disease of the cardiac desmosome, a structure that is integral to cell-cell adhesion and mechanical integrity, comprising, in part, proteins encoded by the genes PKP2, DSC2, DSG2, and DSP. Previous studies have found that variants in these genes–particularly those causing a putative loss of function (which we will call here predicted pathogenic variants)–account for about 50% of ARVC cases; however, these variants have variable penetrance and expressivity.1,2 Among these genes, variants in PKP2 are the most common cause of ARVC, accounting for roughly 73% of ARVC patients with an identified causal variant.3

Much of our clinical understanding of ARVC is based on studies from referral center populations in the United States and Europe, which have been heavily enriched for individuals of European ancestry. For example, the cohort studied to define the currently recognized diagnostic task force criteria for ARVC was 88% White and <2% Black/African ancestry.4,5 In contrast, there is some limited prior data suggesting a modest enrichment of predicted pathogenic desmosome variants in individuals of African ancestry from a general health-care-based population undergoing research-based sequencing.6 Herein, we report our observations of desmosome variant frequency differences and associated phenotypic manifestations between individuals of African and European ancestry across 4 large cohorts.

Materials and Methods

Study populations

The MyCode Community Health Initiative (MyCode) is an IRB-approved research project that has been recruiting participants since 2007. All patients at Geisinger are eligible, and participants consent to use of their electronic health record (EHR) data, as well as provide biospecimens for research use. Exome sequencing is performed on a subset of participants as part of a collaboration with Regeneron Genetics Center (Tarrytown, NY) as previously described.6,7

All of Us is a NIH-funded study of genetic variation across the United States. Participants provide DNA samples for sequencing, as well as linked health records, personal surveys, and physical measurement data available for research use.

The Penn Medicine Biobank (PMBB) is an academic biobank approved under IRB protocol number 813913 and supported by the Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania, a gift from the Smilow family, and the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences of the National Institutes of Health under CTSA award number UL1TR001878. Imputed genotype data for ∼45,000 individuals are available through a collaboration between the the PMBB and Regeneronr Genetics Center.

The UK Biobank study (UKBB) is a prospective cohort of more than 500,000 individuals aged 40 to 69 years at enrollment, recruited from across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. Participants completed questionnaires and measurements, linked to external sources in the form of ICD-10 diagnostic codes, OPCS-4 operation codes, and national death and cancer registries.8 Follow-up data through February 2021 were included (application 48286).

Variant selection

Variants in the 4 assayed genes: PKP2, DSC2, DSG2, and DSP were determined to be predicted pathogenic variants if they existed in the union of 2 variant sets. The first set was those predicted in ClinVar to be pathogenic or likely pathogenic at any level of evidence. The second set consisted of variants that passed our imposed filters of VEP impact HIGH (VEP version 107),9 not in the last exon of the gene, predicted protein consequence not start-lost or stop-lost, and minor allele frequency less than 0.1%. A pair of frameshift variants identified to occur in cis resulting in restoration of the original ready frame was also excluded. In addition, any variant that did not pass the provided sequencing quality metrics for a given data set was excluded from that data set. Individuals with more than 1 variant were excluded from our study.

Ancestry determination

The determination of ancestry for individuals in MyCode has been described previously.10 Briefly, HapMap3 data were used to train a kernel density estimator which assigned each MyCode participant to 1 of the following ancestry groups: African, Admixed American, East Asian, European, South Asian, or Unknown.

In All of Us, individuals were assigned to 1 of 6 ancestry groups: African, Latino/Native American/Admixed American, East Asian, Middle Eastern, European, Other, and South Asian. Individuals were assigned to these groups by a random forest classifier trained on data from the Human Genome Diversity Project and 1000 Genomes.11

PMBB ancestry was determined by following the same steps as MyCode described above.11,12

In UK Biobank, race/ethnicity was self-reported, with participants selecting 1 from a list of 6 possible ethnic groups (Asian, Black, Chinese, Mixed, White, and Other) at recruitment. The definition of ancestry for UK Biobank uses self-reported race/ethnicity which has been shown by other work to be a reasonable proxy for genetic ancestry.13

Phenotype determination

Individuals with a predicted pathogenic variant in any of the 4 desmosome genes were assessed through review of available phenotype data to document the presence of any ARVC-related traits (defined as an individual having one or more of the ARVC-related phenotypes listed in Supplemental Tables 1-4). For code-based phenotypes, an individual was determined to have a given phenotype if that individual had 2 or more instances of an associated code present in their EHR data.

Tests for association

A Fisher’s exact test was used to examine associations between ancestry and predicted pathogenic variant status, performed separately for PKP2 and the other 3 desmosome genes in each of the 4 cohorts. Logistic regression was performed to test for association with having any single ARVC-related trait as the outcome. Regression was performed among African ancestry and individuals of European ancestry separately and adjusted for age and sex.

Results

Cohorts

This analysis includes over 660,000 individuals from 4 study populations—MyCode, All of Us, PMBB, and UK Biobank. The characteristics of these cohorts are summarized in Table 1. The ascertainment of these 4 study populations varies substantially. The UK Biobank is a national cohort with research-based recruitment and data collection. All of Us is also a national cohort with research-based recruitment but with clinical data pulled from the EHR. Geisinger is a single regional health system also with research-based recruitment and data from the EHR. Finally, PMBB is a single health center with referral-based recruitment and clinical data. Consistent with these differences in ascertainment, the prevalence of any ARVC-related trait (Supplemental Tables 1-4) varies across the cohorts from 23.0% in PMBB and 17.5% in MyCode to 7.2% in All of Us and 4.1% in the UK Biobank. Furthermore, these 4 populations are all multiethnic but differ widely in the percentage of individuals of African ancestry, ranging from 25.7% of the PMBB participants to 1.6% of those in the UK Biobank.

Predicted pathogenic variants

Consistent with earlier research involving fewer participants, we observed an enrichment for individuals of African ancestry among those with predicted pathogenic desmosome variants in MyCode. This difference was driven by the prevalence of PKP2 variants (Fisher’s exact test P = 3 × 10−4) because variants in other genes did not show large differences in genetic ancestry (Supplemental Tables 5 and 6). These findings were consistent with data from All of Us and the UK Biobank, in which African ancestry was more common among participants with a PKP2 predicted pathogenic variant than the full respective study cohorts (Figure 1A, Supplemental Tables 5 and 6; P = 1 × 10−9 in All of Us and P = 5 × 10−3 in UK Biobank). We also confirmed this finding using the gnomAD database (Supplemental Table 6). In contrast, the percentage of participants with a PKP2 variant of African ancestry in PMBB was slightly lower than the overall cohort (P = .7).

ARVC-related traits

As seen in Figure 1B and C, findings of ARVC-related traits differed by cohort and ancestry: in MyCode, individuals of European ancestry with an ARVC-associated variant had significantly higher age- and sex-adjusted odds for the phenotype than those without a variant (OR = 1.78 [1.10, 2.88], P = .02 for PKP2 and OR = 1.97 [1.41, 2.73], P = .00006 for the other 3 genes combined). This genotype/phenotype association was not seen in MyCode participants of African ancestry, acknowledging the limited sample size and statistical power available. In All of Us, there was also higher age- and sex-adjusted odds ratio in individuals of European ancestry for PKP2 alone (OR = 2.01 [1.17, 3.47], P = .01), and a similar but nonsignificant association was seen for individuals of European ancestry with a variant in 1 of the other desmosome genes. There was also no significant association with the outcome of having any ARVC-related trait for individuals of African ancestry with a variant in either PKP2 or 1 of the other 3 desmosome genes in All of Us. Neither PMBB nor UK Biobank showed any significant associations for either individuals of either ancestry with a variant in PKP2 or 1 of the other desmosome genes.

Discussion

In this study, we observed that PKP2 predicted pathogenic variants are more common in individuals of African ancestry than individuals of European ancestry in 3 large, independent population biobanks. However, this observation varied across studies because PMBB did not exhibit this difference. A review of EHR-derived phenotype data showed an increased odds of having any ARVC-related trait among individuals of European ancestry with a predicted pathogenic variant in the MyCode and All of Us cohorts. This association was not seen among individuals of African ancestry in MyCode or All of Us or among those of either ancestry group in PMBB or UK Biobank.

The observed increase in genetic prevalence for PKP2 predicted pathogenic variants in individuals of African ancestry was unanticipated. Although not without precedent (V122I variant in TTR, for example),14 the implications of this finding are difficult to define based on associated phenotypic data because we did not observe an increased risk of ARVC-associated traits in this subpopulation.

One hypothesis that may explain this paradox could be a reduced penetrance for PKP2 and ARVC in African-ancestry individuals compared with European ancestry. That is, despite higher prevalence, if that genotype somehow portends a lower risk of significant disease, then patients would be less likely to require medical attention for the condition, particularly in specialist referral centers. This hypothesis is also supported by the fact that the only cohort not exhibiting an increased genotypic prevalence was PMBB, which also has the strongest referral bias among the included cohorts. However, this hypothesis is undermined by the lack of observed difference in odds ratio for ARVC-associated traits in the UK Biobank by ancestry.

An alternate hypothesis is that racial disparities in total health care access and access to specialist providers and centers may lead to individuals with a PKP2 variant of African ancestry going undiagnosed or misdiagnosed more frequently than individuals of European ancestry. In addition, there is a historical underrepresentation of individuals of African ancestry in ARVC cohorts.4 These findings of increased genetic prevalence with unclear differences in disease risk given a genetic variant and limited representation in disease-specific cohorts represent a clear contradiction that necessitates further study to better understand the genetic architecture and disease risk in non-European ancestry groups.

This study must be interpreted in the context of its limitations. Despite including 660,000 participants, the sample size of those with rare variants—when stratified by ancestry—was limited. Although harmonized to the extent possible, EHR-based phenotypes were not identical because of data availability differences, and some participants had incomplete data. The included cohorts varied in clinical context, recruitment methods, sequencing methods, and ancestry determination. The African ancestry group comprised US- and UK-based populations, limiting generalizability to other global populations, and the lack of an observed effect across ancestries may reflect health care inequity, incomplete phenotyping data, or reduced penetrance. In addition, individuals of African ancestry were on average younger; therefore, it is possible that they have not yet developed an ARVC-related trait but would in the future. Finally, there could exist unexamined genetic differences such as cryptic splicing that may explain the difference in penetrance seen. Future studies will seek to address these limitations and apply more rigorous statistical frameworks.

Overall, we observed that PKP2 predicted pathogenic variants are more common in individuals of African ancestry than individuals of European ancestry across 3 of the 4 large cohorts examined. However, the genotype/phenotype association varied by cohort and ancestry. Recognition of differences in variant prevalence and penetrance by genetic ancestry will improve precision care in individuals with a known PKP2 variant, their relatives, and those with secondary or incidental predicted pathogenic desmosome variant findings.
