Cerebrospinal Fluid Biomarkers in Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy With and Without Spontaneous Lobar Hemorrhage
Authors: Benedetta Storti, Giulia Marinoni, Benedetta Cefaloni, Alessandro Francia, Nicola Rifino, Giorgio Boncoraglio, Antonio Indaco, Giuseppe Di Fede, Mario Stanziano, Isabella Canavero, Anna Bersano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) typically presents as spontaneous lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) or cognitive impairment. While the Boston criteria 2.0 aid CAA diagnosis in ICH, detecting nonhemorrhagic cases remains challenging. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers may offer valuable diagnostic support. This study aimed to evaluate CSF Aβ40, Aβ42, total‐τ, phosphorylated‐τ, Aβ42/Aβ40, and phosphorylated‐τ/Aβ42 in patients with CAA with and without symptomatic ICH.MethodsThis prospective observational study recruited patients with CAA from the cerebrovascular clinic of Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta (Milan, Italy) from January 2021 to June 2024. Patients underwent T2*‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging and lumbar puncture, with CSF biomarkers measured via Lumipulse. Participants were grouped into CAA−ICH+ (prior symptomatic lobar ICH) and CAA−ICH−.ResultsFifty‐four patients were included: 35 CAA−ICH+ (37.1% women) and 19 CAA−ICH− (52.4% men). Groups were similar in mean age at lumbar puncture (63.8 versus 67.9 years) and cognitive impairment prevalence (57.1% versus 47.4%). Median CSF Aβ40 level was significantly lower in CAA−ICH+ than in CAA−ICH− (4355 versus 6507 pg/mL, P<0.001). Median CSF Aβ42 level, although reduced in the entire CAA population analyzed, did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (245 versus 303 pg/mL, P=0.09). Other CSF biomarkers levels were comparable between CAA−ICH+ and CAA−ICH−: total‐τ (P=0.28), phosphorylated‐τ (P=0.16), Aβ42/Aβ40 (P=0.14), and phosphorylated‐τ/Aβ42 (P=0.93).ConclusionsCSF Aβ40 is significantly lower in patients with CAA with prior ICH. Amyloid dysregulation in CAA may follow distinct pathological trajectories depending on previous ICH. CSF Aβ40 shows promise as a biomarker for CAA and merits further studies to explore its prognostic value in disease progression.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT04204642.Keywords: cerebral amyloid angiopathy, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, intracerebral hemorrhage, small‐vessel diseaseSubject Categories: Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Intracranial Hemorrhage

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a disease caused by abnormal vascular amyloid deposition, which produces fragility of cerebral and meningeal blood vessels, eventually increasing the risk of spontaneous intracranial bleedings, above all, lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). 1 , 2 Nevertheless, a patient with CAA may not present with symptomatic ICH (sICH), and other symptoms like cognitive impairment, transient focal neurological episodes, stroke, and seizure may occur at the disease onset. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 This heterogeneous presentation of CAA is only partially captured by the Boston criteria version 2.0: While highly accurate for hemorrhagic presentations, the criteria have a much lower diagnostic yield in patients without sICH. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 To increase the accuracy of a CAA diagnosis and to develop bleeding risk stratification models, molecular biomarkers are needed, 11 , 12 particularly in nonhemorrhagic CAA phenotypes. Moreover, CAA may coexist with Alzheimer disease (AD) pathology. 13 Thus, molecular biomarkers are more relevant than ever in the context of disease‐modifying therapies for AD. These treatments carry known risks of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities, especially in patients with CAA, who are at higher risk of developing ICH‐related complications compared with the general population. 14 , 15 Most recent meta‐analyses further confirmed that the CAA cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker signature is characterized by low CSF Aβ42 and Aβ40 levels when compared with controls and lower Aβ40 levels relative to AD. 16 , 17 However, these investigations had inherent statistical limitations concerning the distribution of biomarker values across study groups and the heterogeneous cohorts of patients with CAA with different clinical presentations and disease duration. In this study, we explored the CSF biomarker profile of patients with CAA with and without a history of sICH, with the aim of investigating whether distinct molecular patterns might contribute to a better understanding of disease manifestations and potentially refine diagnostic approaches.

METHODS

This prospective observational substudy included participants with CAA enrolled in the SENECA (Searching Biomarkers of Cerebral Angiopathy) project, 18 consecutively recruited in the cerebrovascular outpatient clinic of the Fondazione Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta (Milan, Italy) from January 2021 to June 2024. We excluded patients with other causes of cerebral hemorrhage, such as hemorrhagic tumors, vascular malformations, central nervous system vasculitis, and septic emboli, contraindications to brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), pregnancy, and breastfeeding. Inclusion criteria required fulfillment of probable or possible CAA according to the Boston criteria version 2.0. 9 Only patients who had at least 1 brain MRI and lumbar puncture (LP) were included.

Standard Protocol Approval, Registration, and Patient Consent

This study was performed with approval and in accordance with the local ethics committee of Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta, Milan, Italy, and carried out in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients provided written informed consent for the use of their medical information for research purposes.

Clinical Data

For all participants, clinical variables, including cerebrovascular risk factors, and symptoms such as cognitive impairment, seizures, and transient focal neurological episodes were collected at enrollment, at the same time brain MRI and LP were performed. Two experienced neurologists (B.S. and A.B.) examined the patients and assessed data on demographics, medical history, and clinical symptoms including history of ICH, ascertained through medical records. All patients had at least 1 brain computed tomography or MRI scan done before enrollment to document the presence of a previous sICH. Patients were then divided into 2 groups on the basis of whether or not they had a sICH before the LP (groups CAA−ICH+ and CAA−ICH−, respectively). Demographic data were coded and recorded, preserving anonymity. Medical history was reviewed with particular attention to vascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and active smoking. The patient's cognitive status was classified as normal versus impaired (including any grade of cognitive impairment from mild to dementia as assessed by the administration of the Mini‐Mental State Examination or Montreal Cognitive Assessment or through neurological and neuropsychological assessments). The onset symptom/event that led to the suspicion of CAA was identified and recorded.

Neuroimaging Acquisition and Analysis

All recruited patients performed at enrollment 1 brain MRI with T1‐weighted, T2 fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, and T2*‐weighted (or fast field echo) sequences on MRI scanners (1.5 Tesla or superior) between January 2021 and June 2024. Radiological features were recorded, in particular, number of lobar ICHs, focal or disseminated cortical superficial siderosis, number of strictly lobar cerebral microbleeds, Fazekas grade for white matter hyperintensity, white matter hyperintensity in a multispot pattern, and increased perivascular spaces of the semioval center. All MRI markers were rated by an experienced neuroradiologist (M.S.), blinded to clinical and laboratory data, according to the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging 19 and its updated version 2. 20

CSF Biomarkers

All selected patients also underwent LP, after excluding contraindications to the procedure (eg, any acute cerebral hemorrhage in the past 3 months) and signing the informed consent. CSF concentrations of Aβ40, Aβ42, total‐τ, and phosphorylated‐τ181 were quantified through the Lumipulse automated platform G600II according to manufacturer's instructions (Fujirebio Europe, Ghent, Belgium).

Statistical Analysis

The manuscript presents descriptive statistics including means±SD and ranges reported for normally distributed continuous variables, and medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) plus ranges reported for nonnormally distributed variables. Percentages were calculated for categorical variables on the basis of the total population within each group (CAA−ICH+ and CAA−ICH−). The clinical, neuroimaging, and CSF characteristics of the CAA−ICH+ and CAA−ICH− groups were compared using univariate analyses. Levene's test was performed to verify the equality of variances, and the Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of distributions. For normally distributed continuous variables, a 2‐sample t test was used, while for nonnormally distributed variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test where appropriate, with Yates correction applied for small sample sizes to reduce the risk of type I errors. In addition to these univariate comparisons, multivariate analyses were conducted to adjust for age and sex, applying ordinary least squares regression for continuous biomarker outcomes, logistic regression for binary outcomes (eg, cognitive impairment), ordinal logistic regression for ordered categorical variables (eg, Fazekas grade), and Poisson regression for count data (eg, lobar cerebral microbleeds). All tests of significance were 2‐tailed, with a significance level set at P<0.05 for all analyses. These statistical analyses were conducted with Python (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE), using the SciPy and pandas libraries for computation and data manipulation.

Data Availability

Data not provided in the article because of space limitations may be shared (anonymized) at the request of any qualified investigator for purposes of replicating procedures and results.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

From our CAA database of 131 patients, 54 individuals with probable CAA based on the Boston criteria 2.0 had undergone brain MRI and LP at the time of recruitment. Among them, 35 were classified as CAA−ICH+ (37.1% women) and 19 as CAA−ICH− (52.4% women). Baseline characteristics are summarized in the Table. Demographics and vascular risk factors did not differ significantly in the 2 groups (Table). Mean age at LP was not significantly different (63.8 versus 67.9 years, P=0.17; ICH+ versus ICH−, respectively). The onset symptoms/events registered were lobar ICH or subarachnoid hemorrhage (27/54 [50%]), transient focal neurological episodes (8/54 [14.8%]), seizure (5/54 [9.3%]), cognitive impairment (7/54 [13%]), transient ischemic attack (5/54 [9.23%]), and stroke (2/54 [3.7%]). ICH or subarachnoid hemorrhage represented the onset of CAA symptoms in 27 of 35 (77%) patients of ICH+ group. The number of patients with cognitive impairment was higher (though not significantly) in the ICH+ group compared with the ICH− group (20/35 [57.1%] versus 9/19 [47.37%], P=0.69). Cognitive symptoms were reported as the first clinical expression of CAA in 1 of the patients with ICH and 6 patients without ICH. The mean latency between symptom onset and the time of LP was not different between the 2 groups (≈29.1 versus 25.1 months, P=0.86; ICH+ and ICH−, respectively).

CSF Outcomes

In univariate analyses, median CSF total‐τ and phosphorylated‐τ levels did not differ significantly between CAA−ICH+ and CAA−ICH− (337.0 [IQR, 221.15–446.50] versus 439.5 [IQR, 258.5–729], P=0.28; and 43.8 [IQR, 32.3–69.3] versus 65.25 [IQR, 40.03–94.03], P=0.16, respectively). By contrast, median CSF Aβ40 was significantly lower in the CAA−ICH+ group compared with CAA−ICH− (4355 [IQR, 3412–5465] versus 6507 [IQR, 5660.5–7269.5], P<0.001). CSF Aβ42 levels also tended to be lower in CAA−ICH+ (245 [IQR, 179–319] versus 303 [IQR, 229–362], P=0.09) but did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, the ratios Aβ42/Aβ40 (0.05 versus 0.04, P=0.14) and phosphorylated‐τ/Aβ42 (0.21 versus 0.16, P=0.93) were comparable in both groups. In multivariate analyses adjusted for age and sex, only Aβ40 remained significantly lower in CAA−ICH+ (P=0.006), whereas no significant between‐group differences emerged for Aβ42, total‐τ, phosphorylated‐τ, Aβ42/Aβ40, or phosphorylated‐τ/Aβ42 (all P>0.05). Radiological characteristics were broadly similar in CAA−ICH+ and CAA−ICH− (Table). The main exceptions were lobar ICH and convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage, both of which occurred exclusively in the CAA−ICH+ group.

DISCUSSION

Novel biomarkers are needed to refine the diagnostic performance of the Boston criteria version 2.0 for CAA, particularly in the nonhemorrhagic presentations. In this study, we identified different CSF profiles according to the presence of a previous remote sICH in patients with CAA. Our main finding was that the CAA−ICH+ group had reduced CSF Aβ40 levels compared with the CAA−ICH− cohort. All patients had reduced levels of CSF Aβ40 and Aβ42 compared with the reference range, as expected signs of cerebral amyloidosis. Total‐τ and phosphorylated‐τ were elevated in some individuals, respecting the already known possible copresence of CAA and AD pathology. 13 , 21 Cognitive symptoms at clinical onset were more frequent in ICH− than in ICH+, albeit non–statistically significant. Furthermore, neurodegenerative biomarkers (CSF levels of total‐τ, phosphorylated‐τ, Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, and phosphorylated‐τ/Aβ42 ratio) did not differ among the 2 groups. These findings point away from the hypothesis that the 2 subgroups had a different prevalence of concomitant CAA−AD. Interestingly, the CAA−ICH+ group had lower Aβ40 levels than the CAA−ICH− group. This could be interpreted in several different ways: (1) a distinguishing feature of hemorrhage predisposition, (2) a direct consequence of the bleeding, or (3) an indicator of advanced Aβ40 accumulation that has reached the threshold value for ICH induction. Similar considerations could apply for Aβ42, whose mean levels were also quite lower in CAA−ICH+ but without reaching statistical significance. It could be argued that the CAA−ICH+ group consists of patients with a more advanced stage of disease or higher amyloid protein accumulation. Indeed, in both our groups, patients exhibit at least a microhemorrhagic expression of the disease. In fact, the mean time elapsed between the onset of symptoms and the LP was not significantly different in the 2 groups. However, it must be considered that the cohorts represent different clinical settings: patients with at least 1 previous acute cerebral hemorrhagic event, versus patients with only microhemorrhagic small‐vessel disease, the latter often preventing a precise temporal definition of the “clinical onset.” Thus, our findings might support the existence of some features that could intrinsically differentiate CAA−ICH− from patients who are CAA−ICH+ or that may potentially switch during the disease course. Previous observations in the literature concerning CSF biomarkers of CAA are inconclusive. 11 , 17 , 21 , 22 , 23 In particular, the diagnostic accuracy of CSF analysis to differentiate CAA from AD is limited as CAA and AD coexist in more than 20% of cases. 21 , 22 Level of CSF Aβ40 was reported to be lower in CAA than in AD, the latter being characterized more typically by a decrease in Aβ42. 11 , 17 A preclinical study identified Aβ40 as an early biomarker of CAA (before the appearance of signs of hemorrhage). 23 Contrary to this, vascular accumulation of Aβ40 identified postmortem in advanced CAA was found to be higher than in asymptomatic forms. 24 Further supporting the potential role of Aβ40 as a biomarker, a study on hereditary cerebral hemorrhage with amyloidosis–Dutch type, a genetic variant of CAA, demonstrated that both CSF Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels are significantly decreased in presymptomatic and symptomatic mutation carriers compared with controls. 25 Moreover, lower CSF Aβ40 levels correlated with a greater number of lobar microbleeds, increased white matter hyperintensity volume, and the presence of cortical superficial siderosis. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that Aβ40 depletion in the CSF reflects its pathological vascular deposition in CAA and may serve as an early marker of disease progression. To our knowledge, this is the first report focusing on CSF biomarkers comparing CAA with and without sICH. Identifying a hemorrhagic risk biomarker for CAA would make it possible to inform withholding therapeutic interventions, such as antiplatelet agents, that may trigger ICH in this higher‐risk population. In addition, it could allow a more effective recruitment of patients in novel, therapeutically directed clinical trials.

Some limitations must be acknowledged. First, only patients who had a brain MRI and LP at recruitment were included. While this selection criterion was necessary to ensure a standardized data set for biomarker analysis, we acknowledge that it may have introduced a selection bias. Specifically, patients who did not undergo LP might differ systematically due to clinical severity, comorbidities, or contraindications to the procedure. Second, recruited cases did not have biopsy‐confirmed CAA, and we did not systematically collect information regarding ICH volume or clinical trajectory after the ICH event. To partially address this issue, in our study we reported the number of lobar macrohemorrhages as a rough approximation of the ICH burden. Furthermore, although mean latencies were not different between the groups, LP occurred at different time points from the clinical onset: a potential, subsequent conversion of ICH− to ICH+ cannot be ruled out by a single CSF assay. In our regression models adjusting for age and sex, the difference in CSF Aβ40 between CAA−ICH+ and CAA−ICH− remained statistically significant (P=0.006). By contrast, Aβ42 did not differ significantly after adjustment (P=0.14), and we detected no significant differences in total‐τ or phosphorylated‐τ levels between the 2 groups (P=0.92 and P=0.97, respectively). Likewise, neither phosphorylated‐τ/Aβ42 nor Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios were significantly associated with prior ICH after controlling for covariates. In radiological features, lobar cerebral microbleeds remained strongly associated with the ICH+ group in a Poisson regression model (coefficient=−0.48, P<0.001). However, the difference in lobar ICH number, although highly significant in univariate testing, could not be reliably estimated in a Poisson model (P=0.1), likely due to the complete absence of lobar ICH in the CAA−ICH− group. The ordinal logistic regression for cortical superficial siderosis (0, absence; 1, focal; 2, disseminated) showed no significant group effect (P=0.28), nor did the binary logistic models for white matter hyperintensities (P=0.85) or perivascular spaces of the semioval center (P=0.49). Overall, these findings suggest that the lower Aβ40 levels in the CAA−ICH+ cohort persist even after adjusting for age and sex, whereas most other biomarkers and radiological variables do not show robust group differences in our sample. Finally, the study lacked a control group of healthy individuals or patients with unrelated pathology. Therefore, our observations should be validated in a larger sample, including patients with AD and healthy controls without cerebrovascular disease. While our findings suggest a significant difference in CSF Aβ40 levels between patients with CAA with and without a history of ICH at the group level, we acknowledge that this does not necessarily imply its utility as a predictive biomarker at the individual level. Future studies should focus on evaluating the predictive value of CSF biomarkers for ICH risk stratification in longitudinal cohorts, possibly integrating them with imaging markers. Taken together, our observations suggest interesting insights for future research in the biomarker area, which to date has not been included in the diagnostic criteria for CAA.
