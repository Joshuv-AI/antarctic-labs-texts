Antagonistic histone H2A variants and autonomous heterochromatin formation shape epigenomic patterns in Arabidopsis
Authors: Shoko Oda, Sayaka Tominaga, Shumpei Takeuchi, Akihisa Osakabe, Akira Kawabe, Frédéric Berger, Tetsuji Kakutani, Taiko Kim To
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterochromatin formation is pivotal in many eukaryotes with repetitive sequences, such as transposable elements (TEs). However, in plants, where the known de novo DNA methylation mechanism (RdDM) targets euchromatin, how heterochromatin is formed in a region-specific manner remains unclear. We previously reported an RdDM-independent de novo establishment of H3K9me and non-CpG methylation, both of which localize in heterochromatin. Here we show that the mutually exclusive histone H2A variants, H2A.W and H2A.Z, function as guides to initiate heterochromatin formation; H2A.W and H2A.Z promotes and inhibits heterochromatin establishment, respectively, especially in chromosomal arm regions with dispersed TEs. In contrast, pericentromeric TEs demonstrate autonomous heterochromatin formation, less dependently on these H2A variants. Furthermore, H2A.Z protects protein-coding genes from ectopic heterochromatin formation, possibly by preventing its spreading. We propose that the genome indexing mechanism driven by H2A variants, as well as the autonomous formation of pericentromeric heterochromatin, shapes proper epigenomic patterns in Arabidopsis.

Introduction

Most eukaryotic genomes contain a substantial amount of repetitive elements and transposable elements (TEs). TEs are abundant in pericentromeric heterochromatin and are silenced by epigenetic modifications, including the methylation of cytosine (mC) and histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9me)1,2. In plants, mC can be classified into those in CpG sites (mCG) and non-CpG sites (mCH, H = A, T, C)3,4. mCH colocalizes with H3K9me in TEs, whereas mCG is found in both TEs and bodies of active genes5–7. It is crucial for plants to establish and maintain proper distribution of epigenetic information in the genome (epigenome patterns), and to silence TEs specifically8,9. However, in contrast to the intensive studies of vertebrates, which involve sequence-driven heterochromatin formation1,10, our knowledge of how hosts distinguish TEs from genes and initiate heterochromatin formation specifically at TEs is still limited in plants11–13.

Pathways controlling heterochromatin marks have been extensively investigated in Arabidopsis. H3K9me is catalyzed by Suv39H-homolog histone methyltransferases, SUVH4, SUVH5, and SUVH614–16. mCH is further classified into mCHG and mCHH, and is mostly catalyzed by plant-specific CpH methyltransferases, CHROMOMETHYLASE 3 (CMT3) and CMT2, respectively17–20. H3K9me and mCH are maintained by a positive feedback between them, while H3K9me/mCH and mCG are maintained almost independently of each other14,15,21,22. mCG is maintained by METHYLTRANSFERASE 1 (MET1), a homolog of mammalian DNMT123. Unlike in mammals, the de novo DNA methyltransferase DRM2 is recruited in plants via RNA interference (RNAi) machinery in a process called RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM)9,24,25. However, unlike in fission yeast, where RNAi machinery recruits the Suv39H homolog Clr4 to pericentromeric heterochromatin26, DRM2 is guided to short non-coding TEs in euchromatic chromosome arms. How pericentromeric heterochromatin is formed remains unclear.

We previously reported the identification of a novel pathway for establishing de novo mCH/H3K9me specifically to TEs27,28. This pathway is independent of RdDM and is suggested to involve CMTs and SUVHs. It predominantly functions in long TEs, most of which are in pericentromeric heterochromatin. However, how they specifically target TEs is still enigmatic.

Genomic distribution of TEs and genes across chromosomes is associated with that of histone H2A variants. H2A.Z, an evolutionarily well-conserved variant, is localized to protein-coding genes in euchromatic chromosome arms29–31, whereas a flowering plant-specific H2A variant, H2A.W, is specifically localized in TEs, and enriched in constitutive heterochromatin in pericentromeric regions31–33. Recently, DDM1, which has a significant impact on the maintenance of DNA methylation in TEs, binds and deposits H2A.W over TEs and potentially removes H2A.Z at TEs31,34–36. However, DNA methylation is only moderately decreased in h2a.w heterochromatin33, and rarely increases at TEs in h2a.z30, obscuring their roles. In addition, H2A.W and H3K9 methylation jointly suppress expression of TEs, indicating a major role of this variant in silencing37.

Here, we directly investigate the role of H2A.W and H2A.Z in heterochromatin formation. Results from mCH reconstitution in the absence of H2A variants reveal that H2A.W can promote mCH establishment in TEs, while H2A.Z can suppress it in TEs and protein-coding genes. These effects are particularly evident in TE-poor chromosomal arms. In these regions, when mCH is lost, H2A.W is replaced by H2A.Z at hundreds of TEs, attenuating subsequent mCH establishment. In contrast, this replacement is not observed in pericentromeric TEs, where mCH efficiently recovers. However, when both mCG and mCH are lost, pericentromeric H2A.W is also replaced by H2A.Z. The resulting simultaneous loss of H2A.W, mCG, and mCH can all be reversed by complementation of the mCH machinery, but this “autonomous” recovery of heterochromatin marks is only seen in TE-rich pericentromeric regions. These contrasting dynamics of heterochromatin formation between TE-dense pericentromeric and TE-poor arm regions account for the proper differentiation of epigenome patterns in Arabidopsis.

Results

H2A.W is required for mCH establishment in hundreds of TEs

Previously, we developed the mCH/H3K9me reconstitution system to investigate the dynamics and mechanism of mCH/H3K9me establishment genome-wide27. In the system, two mCH/H3K9me-deficient mutants (cmt2 cmt3 (cmt) and suvh4 suvh5 suvh6 (suvh)) were genetically crossed to produce F1 plants (hereafter called F1WT). F1WT plants restore all CMTs and SUVHs genes as heterozygous (Fig. 1a, left), allowing us to assess the dynamics of mCH/H3K9me recovery. As shown previously, F1WT plants restore both mCH/H3K9me at most TE genes (or coding regions within TEs; hereafter referred to as TEs); more than 96% of TEs (3419 out of 3538 TEs with mCHG in wild-type (WT) >0.1; 3389 out of 3471 TEs with mCHH in WT >0.03) show efficient mCH recovery. However, F1WT fail to restore mCH/H3K9me at a small subset of TEs (n = 73 out of 3538 TEs, F1WT/WT <0.1 for mCHG)27. These TEs were called gene-like TEs (GLTs) in our previous study27, but in this study, we call them un-recovered TEs in WT (URTWT). Previous results suggested that the failure of mCH recovery at URTWT in F1WT involves histone H2A variants, because the replacement from heterochromatic H2A.W to euchromatic H2A.Z is specifically observed at URTWT. We first introduced h2a.w-2 (hta6 hta7 hta12) mutation33 into the mCH/H3K9me reconstitution system, and examined the impacts of H2A.W on mCH recovery (Fig. 1a, right). The parental mutants, h2a.w cmt and h2a.w suvh, as well as their F1 progeny in h2a.w mutant background (F1h2a.w), were subjected to BS-seq analysis. The results were then compared with those of F1WT (GSE14875327). Indeed, h2a.w mutation significantly altered mCH recovery (Fig. 1b–g and Supplementary Fig. 1a–f); statistically significant changes in mCHG and mCHH levels were observed at 843 TEs and 1067 TEs in F1h2a.w compared to F1WT (Fig. 1b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 1b, c), respectively. Most of them exhibited hypomethylation in F1h2a.w (mCHG 71.1%; mCHH 84.2%) (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Importantly, as previously reported33, h2a.w mutation alone negligibly affected mCH maintenance (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 1g), demonstrating the contrasting effects of H2A.W on the maintenance and establishment.

The h2a.w mutation increased the number of TEs without recovery by approximately four times in F1h2a.w (301 TEs out of 3511 TEs with mCHG > 0.1 in h2a.w, F1h2a.w/h2a.w <0.1 for mCHG) (Fig. 1d), compared with those in F1WT (73 URTWT; F1WT/WT <0.1 for mCHG). These include most URTWT (69 out of 73) and additional 232 TEs; hereafter, the latter called URTh2a.w. URTh2a.w require H2A.W for the mCH establishment (Fig. 1c–g), while mCH recovery in URTh2a.w was incomplete even in F1WT harboring the wild-type H2A.W genes (Fig. 1e, f, and Supplementary Figs. 1d, e, 2). The h2a.w mutation also suppressed the mCH recovery in a number of TE fragments and TE-like genes with methylation (mCHG in WT > 0.1) (Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). Given that shorter TEs, including fragments, tend to be targeted by RdDM while longer TEs are targeted by CMTs19,27, we also analyzed the relationship between TE length and mCH recovery. No clear association was observed between TE length and mCH recovery in either the F1WT or F1h2a.w, indicating that h2a.w mutation affects mCH recovery irrespective of TE size (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). Taken together, the results indicate that H2A.W can enhance mCH establishment in hundreds of TEs, TE fragments, and TE-like genes.

h2a.z mutation suppresses the failure of mCH establishment in URTWT and URTh2a.w

In our previous study27, the failure of mCH/H3K9me recovery at URTWT in F1WT was associated not only with H2A.W loss but also with H2A.Z gain, suggesting that H2A.Z might also have an impact on mCH/H3K9me establishment. To test this possibility, we tried to create a F1 in h2a.z (hta8 hta9 hta11) background by crossing h2a.z cmt and h2a.z suvh. However, h2a.z suvh exhibited severe developmental defects, including infertility to use for crossing (Supplementary Fig. 4a), possibly due to a more profound loss of H3K9me in suvh than in cmt27. By contrast, h2a.z cmt mutant was slightly fertile while having global mCH loss (Supplementary Fig. 4b), which allowed us to investigate mCH recovery dynamics in the absence of H2A.Z, by complementing CMT genes via transformation. As controls, we firstly created T1 plants in WT background (T1WT) by transforming CMT3 and CMT2 transgene, separately, into cmt (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5a). The resulting transformants (hereafter called CMT3-T1WT and CMT2-T1WT, respectively) were subjected to BS-seq analysis. In the two individual CMT3-T1WT, mCHG was consistently recovered substantially in most TEs (Fig. 2b) as wild-type levels, while mCHH did not largely recover (Supplementary Fig. 4c), which agrees with predominant mCHG activity of CMT317–20. Importantly, in agreement with the observation in F1WT, mCHG recovery was hardly observed in URTWT and was attenuated in URTh2a.w (Fig. 2b, c). In the two individual CMT2-T1WT, mCHH recovered substantially for most TEs (Supplementary Fig. 5b–e). This mCHH recovery was compromised in URTWT and attenuated in URTh2a.w (Supplementary Fig. 5b–d). The same strategy was taken to examine the impact of H2A.Z on mCH recovery. To complement CMT genes in h2a.z background, we transformed CMT3 and CMT2 transgene, separately, into h2a.z cmt mutant (hereafter CMT3-T1h2a.z and CMT2-T1h2a.z, respectively) (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5f). mC was profiled for each of six and three individual T1 plants (T1h2a.z), respectively, as well as their non-transgenic controls. Indeed, h2a.z mutation significantly affected mCH establishment dynamics (Fig. 2e–i, Supplementary Figs. 4d, e, and 5h–j). Many TEs showed significantly altered mCHG recovery in CMT3-T1h2a.z, as well as mCHH in CMT2-T1h2a.z, in comparison to their respective controls with functional H2A.Z (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 5g). Most of them exhibited hypermethylation, indicating that h2a.z mutation enhances mCH recovery in TEs. Strikingly, h2a.z mutation enabled the mCH recovery in URTWT and URTh2a.w, with substantial mCH recovery being observed in T1h2a.z (Fig. 2e–i, Supplementary Figs. 4d, e, and 5h, i). To determine if the enhanced mCH recovery was due to enhanced expression of CMT3 and CMT2 transgenes, we examined their transcript levels. Generally, CMT3 and CMT2 transcript levels in T1h2a.z were mostly comparable to or slightly lower than that in WT and F1WT (Supplementary Figs. 4g and 5k, l), implying that enhanced mCH recovery in h2a.z background was not due to overexpression of CMT transgenes (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Interestingly, however, one T1h2a.z individual, CMT2-T1h2a.z_3, showed approximately four times higher expression of CMT2 than WT. This was associated with an elevated overall mCH recovery, implying a possible dosage effect of CMTs expression on the overall mCH levels (Supplementary Fig. 5k–m, see Supplementary note for detail). Taken together, the results suggest that ectopically increased H2A.Z can cause the failure of mCH recovery in URTWT as well as attenuation in URTh2a.w. h2a.z mutation also significantly enabled mCHG recovery in CMT3-T1h2a.z and mCHH recovery in CMT2-T1h2a.z in TE fragments and TE-like genes (Supplementary Figs. 4h, i, and 5n, o). In contrast, h2a.z mutation alone did not increase mCH maintenance in most TEs, TE fragments, and TE-like genes (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 4f), as previously reported30. These results suggest that, despite its negligible effect on mCH maintenance, H2A.Z has a negative impact on mCH establishment in a small yet significant fraction of TEs, TE fragments, and TE-like genes.

H2A.W and H2A.Z mutually inhibit their accumulation in CMTs/SUVHs mutants

The results so far demonstrate that h2a.w and h2a.z mutations have opposite effects on mCH establishment. The opposite behavior was also observed in terms of their localization in URTWT; H2A.W decrease and H2A.Z increase in cmt, suvh, and F1WT27. Therefore, we next asked whether h2a.w mutation impacts H2A.Z localization in mCH mutants, and vice versa, by performing chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing (ChIP-seq). The antibodies used in this study were previously reported31, and their respective specificity was validated using ChIP-seq in WT (Supplementary Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table 2, 3).

First, we analyzed their localization in WT, cmt, suvh, and F1WT. A decrease in H2A.W and an increase in H2A.Z compared to WT were observed, not only at URTWT but also at URTh2a.w (Fig. 3a–f and Supplementary Fig. 6b–h). Their change levels were negatively correlated with mCH recovery in F1WT (i.e., the highest in URTWT, then URTh2a.w, but barely in the other TEs, while mCH recovery shows the opposite trend). Then, we tested whether h2a.w mutation causes an increase in ectopic H2A.Z localization, particularly in URTh2a.w, where the mCH recovery was lost in h2a.w background, whereas it is enhanced in h2a.z background. Although h2a.w mutation alone rarely changed H2A.Z distribution33,38 (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 6b), it did increase H2A.Z occupancy in URTh2a.w in h2a.w cmt, h2a.w suvh, and F1h2a.w compared to those in cmt, suvh, and F1WT, particularly around TSS and TTS (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 6b–d, h). The change in H2A.Z was negatively correlated with mCG, as observed previously29, and TEs exhibiting both ectopic H2A.Z and mCG loss showed inefficient mCH recovery in F1WT and F1h2a.w (Fig. 3g, h). As for URTWT, strong ectopic H2A.Z was already observed in cmt, suvh, and F1WT, and h2a.w mutation did not cause further increase in H2A.Z occupancy, which could be explained by substantial depletion of H2A.W at URTWT in WT background (Fig. 3a–f and Supplementary Fig. 6b–h). The other TEs showed minimal sensitivity to either h2a.w and cmt/suvh mutations, with H2A.Z levels remaining at low levels in all the plants examined. Next, we examined the impact of h2a.z mutation on H2A.W localization. In h2a.z, H2A.W distribution was not significantly altered. In cmt, suvh and F1WT, H2A.W was lost at URTWT, as well as reduced in URTh2a.w (Fig. 3d–f and Supplementary Fig. 6e–h). Intriguingly, this loss of H2A.W in cmt was strongly suppressed by h2a.z mutation. In h2a.z cmt, H2A.W enrichments significantly increased and became comparable to that in WT (Fig. 3d–f and Supplementary Fig. 6e–g), suggesting that ectopic H2A.Z could decrease H2A.W in cmt, and presumably as well in suvh and F1WT. Furthermore, CG hypomethylation was also suppressed in h2a.z cmt (Fig. 3i, j). Considering that h2a.z mutation also suppressed the failure of mCH recovery in URTWT and URTh2a.w (Fig. 2), these results suggest that H2A.Z, localized ectopically at URTWT and URTh2a.w in CMTs/SUVHs mutants, can antagonize H2A.W and mCG, which could thereby inhibit mCH establishment in F1WT and F1h2a.w.

Taken together, our results suggest that H2A.W and H2A.Z antagonistically affect each other for their localization at URTWT and URTh2a.w, particularly in the absence of mCH/H3K9me. Once this change has been induced, it is sustained even after complementation of CMTs/SUVHs, which would affect the dynamics of mCH/H3K9me establishment in URTh2a.w, and URTWT.

Chromosomal environment and TE family influence the dynamics of mCH recovery

The results above suggest that mutually antagonistic H2A.Z and H2A.W affect mCH recovery dynamics in URTWT and URTh2a.w. Conversely, they barely affected the other TEs in neither their mCH recovery nor the localization of the other variant. We hypothesized that URTWT and URTh2a.w have distinct properties, which would account for their unique dynamics of enrichment pattern in H2A variants and mCH recovery. One possibility would be chromosomal positions, as URTWT are predominantly distributed in chromosomal arms, where TE density is low compared to the pericentromeric regions27 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, URTh2a.w are also enriched in the arms and around the edges of pericentromeric heterochromatin (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). URTWT are the farthest from centromeres, followed by URTh2a.w, and then the other TEs (Fig. 4b) which correlates positively with TE density and negatively with gene density (Fig. 4c). Chromosomal characteristics can vary among chromosomes, such as the length of chromosomes and the existence of pericentromeric knob in Chr 439. To better represent “the degree of heterochromatin,” we introduced an index of TE density (T-index), which is calculated as numbers of TEs/(TEs + genes), in a 100-kb window (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 7b left). As expected, T-index is strongly correlated with heterochromatic marks, such as H3K9me2 and H2A.W (Supplementary Table 1), as well as effectively capturing the pericentromeric knob feature around coordinates 2 Mb in Chr 4. T-index exhibits a steep increase and decrease around pericentromeric heterochromatin (hereafter we call these steep regions ‘boundaries’) (Supplementary Fig. 7b, left). Next, T-index around each TE (50-kb on each side) was calculated, and then compared among URTWT, URTh2a.w, and other TEs to the genomic T-index patterns. T-index for URTWT is low as in chromosomal arms. T-index for URTh2a.w overlaps the steeply changing boundaries. The other TEs show high T-index, as most of them are in pericentromeric regions (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 7b right). These results reflect that URTWT predominantly localize in euchromatic arms, URTh2a.w around the boundaries, and the other TEs in pericentromeric heterochromatin.

Interestingly, even in WT, URTWT, and URTh2a.w already show some euchromatic features; H3K9me2 and H2A.W are the lowest in URTWT, moderately lower in URTh2a.w than the other TEs, while the trend is anti-correlated in H2A.Z (Fig. 4e). The relationship between the heterochromatic features and T-index is seen beyond URTWT and URTh2a.w. LOESS-fitting curve shows that both H2A.W and H3K9me2 are positively correlated, while H2A.Z is negatively correlated with T-index (Fig. 4f). A similar trend was observed when URTWT and URTh2a.w were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7c). The epigenetic features in protein-coding genes were also similarly affected by T-index, while the overall levels differed considerably between genes and TEs (Fig. 4e, g). These results suggest that, in both TEs and genes, epigenetic features can vary depending on the “chromosomal environments,” including surrounding chromatin structure (heterochromatin or euchromatin) and proximity to other genes or TEs. The stronger gene-like features in TEs with lower T-index were further enhanced in cmt, suvh, and F1WT (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 7d). However, they largely reflect the changes in URTWT and URTh2a.w, as the changes diminished greatly when URTWT and URTh2a.w were excluded (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f), indicating that the acquisition of euchromatin-like properties in mCH mutants occurs specifically in URTWT and URTh2a.w.

TE family could be another possibility that may also account for the dependence on H2A variants for mCH recovery in URTWT and URTh2a.w (Supplementary Fig. 8a). URTWT are enriched with COPIA and LINE retrotransposon families27. URTh2a.w are also enriched with COPIAs and LINEs, but also with DNA transposon families. In both URTWT and URTh2a.w, Gypsy retrotransposons are underrepresented. mCH recovery in Gypsy, including Athila elements, were largely unaffected by the h2a.w mutation (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). Mapping to Col-CEN genome40 confirmed the efficient mCH recovery in the centromeric regions in the absence of H2A.W (Supplementary Fig. 8d). On the other hand, COPIAs showed the least and LINEs the second least efficient mCH recovery, especially when H2A.W is absent, which is mostly suppressed when H2A.Z is absent (Supplementary Fig. 8b). These indicate that the dynamics of mCH recovery and its dependence on H2A patterns varied significantly among TE families.

COPIAs and LINEs tend to be distributed in chromosome arms, while DNA transposons are in pericentromeric heterochromatin, and Gypsys are in deep pericentromere8,39,41. By considering the distribution bias, the data was further dissected by T-index. Still, mCH recovery in COPIAs and LINEs was strongly dependent on both T-index and H2A.W (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Even among TE copies with similar sequences, mCH recovery tended to be associated with T-index (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). Furthermore, URTWT and URTh2a.w exhibited a higher degree of enrichment with COPIAs and LINEs than TEs with low T-index (Supplementary Fig. 8h). Taken together, the results suggest that both euchromatic environments represented by low T-index and TE families, COPIAs and LINEs, can best explain the specific displacement of H2A variants, which is associated with the failure of mCH recovery in URTWT and URTh2a.w.

The restoration dynamics of heterochromatin states depend on T-index

The results above illuminate that the enrichment levels of H2A.W and H2A.Z (H2A patterns) are important for mCH establishment in TEs on chromosome arms and boundaries. On the contrary, pericentromeric TEs sustained TE-like H2A patterns (i.e., high H2A.W and/or low H2A.Z) even when both mCH and the other variant are absent. For example, the simultaneous loss of H2A.W and CMTs/SUVHs does not increase H2A.Z enrichment in pericentromeric TEs, obscuring the relationship of H2A variants and mCH recovery in pericentromeric regions. We wondered if the replacement of H2A.W with H2A.Z occurs more genome-wide, including pericentromeric heterochromatin, how does it affect the dynamics of mCH recovery? In order to address the question, we utilized the mCH/H3K9me reconstitution system in met1-1, a weak allele of mCG methyltransferase; the parental mutants met1-1 cmt2 cmt3 mutant (met1 cmt) and met1-1 suvh4 suvh5 suvh6 mutant (met1 suvh), and their progeny F1met142. This is because our previous study shows that mCG decrease by met1-1 mutation significantly attenuates mCH recovery more genome-wide, including pericentromeric TEs42, and because mCG is known to antagonize H2A.Z6,29. To investigate the relationship among chromosomal environments, H2A patterns, and mCG/mCH dynamics, TEs were categorized into three groups based on T-index. Then, ChIP-seq data of H2A.Z and H2A.W were compared against mCG levels as density plots (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 9a), scatter plots (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 9b), as well as LOESS plots (Fig. 5g–j). As shown previously27, in the original system (i.e., cmt, suvh, and F1WT), a decrease in both mCG and H2A.W, and an increase in H2A.Z was observed in a subset of TEs with low T-index (shallow blue spreading; most of them reflect URTWT; Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), which is linked to the absence of mCH recovery (Supplementary Fig. 9b). In met1-1, mCG decreased by half on average, which was commonly observed irrespective of T-index (Fig. 5a, e, g). The met1-1 mutation affected distribution of H2A variants slightly (Fig. 5b, c, e), with the exceptions for some TEs with substantial mCG and mCH loss (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Interestingly, in both met1 cmt and met1 suvh, mCG further decreased (Fig. 5a, e, g), and this was associated with a significant increase in H2A.Z and a decrease in H2A.W, exhibiting a clear anti-correlation (Fig. 5b, c, e, and Supplementary Fig. 9d). Of particular significance was that displacement of H2A.W with H2A.Z occurred across the entire chromosomes, resulting in the majority of TEs acquiring euchromatic epigenetic states, irrespective of T-index (Fig. 5e, h, i). This allowed us to evaluate the impacts of chromosomal environments and H2A variants on mCH recovery, including pericentromeric heterochromatin.

Unexpectedly, although H2A patterns in the parents were similarly disturbed among three T-index groups, mCH recovery in F1met1 was strikingly influenced by T-index. mCH recovered substantially in TEs with high T-index, but scarcely in TEs with low T-index (Fig. 5f, j, and Supplementary Fig. 9e). More surprisingly, other chromatin properties took the same behavior. TEs with high T-index reverted to their original epigenetic states of heterochromatin, losing H2A.Z and reacquiring H2A.W/mCG, with levels in F1met1 becoming comparable with its control, met1-1 (Fig. 5e-green in the right panels, h, i; Supplementary Fig. 9f). On the contrary, TEs with low T-index remained in euchromatic states with high H2A.Z and low H2A.W/mCG in F1met1, which is associated with the absence of mCH recovery (Fig. 5e-blue in the right panels, h, i; Supplementary Fig. 9f). Indeed, of 676 TEs with low T-index, 291 (43.0%), including 40 URTWT and 77 URTh2a.w, failed to recover mCHG (F1met1/met1 <0.1). This is in clear contrast to TEs with middle or high T-index; 187 (including 4 URTWT and 29 URTh2a.w, 26.0%) out of 719 TEs with middle T-index, and 181 (including 1 URTWT and 14 URTh2a.w, 9.97%) out of 1,815 TEs with high T-index, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9i). Importantly, this trend is consistently observed even when both URTWT and URTh2a.w were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9g, h, j), indicating that the inefficiency of euchromatic TEs in reverting to a heterochromatic state could extend beyond URTWT and URTh2a.w. Taken together, these results suggest that, for the recovery of heterochromatic features, local H2A patterns could be more important in TEs on chromosome arms than in pericentromeric regions. In other words, in TE-dense pericentromeric regions but not in TE-poor/gene-dense chromosome arms, autonomous and synchronous recovery of heterochromatic features are triggered upon complementation of CMTs/SUVHs in F1met1, suggesting that chromosomal environments, including TE density, could be pivotal in accelerating the establishment of heterochromatin.

Thus, H2A.Z apparently prevents de novo mCH accumulation in genes. Despite negligible levels of association to T-index (Supplementary Fig. 10e), mCH-gained genes tended to localize in the vicinity of TE fragments (Supplementary Fig. 10a, f). For instance, AT1G30140 is located close to TE fragments with high DNA methylation (Fig. 6d). This gene is hypomethylated in WT, cmt, and the CMT3-T1WT. In h2a.z, mCG is slightly increased around the promoter. In h2a.z cmt, mCG significantly increased towards the coding region of the gene, which is likely to be propagated from nearby TE fragments. Finally, in the CMT3-T1h2a.z, ectopic mCH was observed within the gene where mCG was present in the parent. Similar to this example, mCH-gained genes are frequently found in close proximity to TE fragments with high mCG and mCHG (Supplementary Fig. 10g). The results suggest that the surrounding heterochromatic epigenetic state could affect nearby genes, increasing the chance of ectopic mCH accumulation, and that H2A.Z plays a pivotal role in preventing heterochromatin spreading.

Discussion

In many eukaryotes, chromosomes are composed of distinct territories, euchromatin and heterochromatin. How this differential pattern is established in plants remains obscure, as RdDM predominantly functions in euchromatin. We previously proposed the RdDM-independent heterochromatin establishment mechanism, which precisely targets TEs for de novo mCH/H3K9me27,28,42. In this study, we demonstrate that mutually exclusive histone variants, H2A.W and H2A.Z, play a role in determining the targets of the de novo mCH/H3K9me mechanism in a subset of TEs; H2A.W promotes mCH establishment, while H2A.Z suppresses it. When mCH/H3K9me is lost, transition of histone variants from H2A.W to H2A.Z occurs in a small subset of TEs, particularly in chromosome arms. When mCH/H3K9me/H2A.W is lost, H2A.Z becomes localized in a further subset of TEs, including those in the pericentromeric boundary. In both cases, H2A.Z deposition inhibits heterochromatin recovery. When mCH/H3K9me/mCG is lost, transition from H2A.W to H2A.Z occurs throughout the genome, including pericentromere. Once chromatin becomes euchromatic, heterochromatin is not fully restored in TEs in chromosome arms. By contrast, heterochromatin is restored in TE-dense pericentromere, less dependently on H2A variants. Thus, it could be more general that chromosomal position greatly affects the dynamics of heterochromatin formation and its dependence on histone variants. Moreover, H2A.Z in genes prevents heterochromatin spreading from proximal TEs. Model of specific heterochromatin formation at TEs in Arabidopsis.

Heterochromatin is established specifically at TEs but not at genes. In chromosomal arm regions (TE-poor), H2A.W and H2A.Z, which antagonize each other, can promote and inhibit mCH establishment, respectively, at TEs. H2A.Z at genes can also prevent heterochromatin spreading into genes. Once chromatin becomes euchromatic genome-wide (i.e., with low mCG/mCH/H2A.W and high H2A.Z in met1 cmt and met1 suvh), heterochromatin formation is severely defected in TEs in chromosome arms, while heterochromatin formation can occur autonomously in TE-dense pericentromeric regions. Thus, histone H2A variants play a pivotal role in regulating heterochromatin formation in euchromatin dominated regions of the genome.

Recent studies have shown that a subset of DDM1 functions in remodeling H2A variants by depositing H2A.W and possibly removing H2A.Z, although it is still unknown how DDM1 is recruited only to heterochromatic TEs. Despite the strong impacts shown in this study, how H2A variants contribute to de novo heterochromatin formation remains unclear. As either mutation alone rarely affects mCH distribution, these variants do not seem to directly regulate the activity of CMTs/SUVHs30,32,33. Our results suggest that H2A.Z plays an inhibitory role against heterochromatin spreading into genes. At the TEs in euchromatin, ectopic H2A.Z deposition, caused by the absence of mCH/H3K9me, can inhibit the subsequent mCH establishment. Conversely, in the absence of H2A.Z, heterochromatin is ectopically induced in some protein coding genes located nearby TEs. Specific H2A.Z localization in genes could be supported by euchromatic marks H3K4me2/343,44. H2A.Z is deposited at a proximity of nucleosome-free regions, and to reduce nucleosome stability, while H2A.W has the opposite effect36,45,46. Thus, in domains dominated by euchromatin, H2A.Z may prevent heterochromatin spreading from adjacent heterochromatin to protein-coding genes by modulating nucleosome-free regions. This is supported by observations in other species for a role of H2A.Z in preventing de novo DNA methylation in zebrafish embryos47, and as the boundary against heterochromatin in yeast48. In contrast to the local effects of H2A.Z, H2A.W, which shares the KSPK motif with mammalian macroH2A46,49,50, is more likely to regulate constitutive heterochromatin at the genomic level; higher-order chromatin compaction32,33, chromatin accessibility together with H133, and inhibition of mitotic crossover in heterochromatin51.

The distribution of TEs and genes, or heterochromatin and euchromatin, differs among species. In certain species, including fruit fly, fission yeast, and Arabidopsis, heterochromatin is clustered at some restricted regions of chromosomes26,39,52. In species with larger genome size, including maize, mouse, and human, TEs tend to be dispersed in chromosomes and are often found proximal to genes53,54, while their genomes are often functionally compartmented like TE-rich/poor regions. Important questions remain how H2A variants impact heterochromatin formation in other species, including those with more abundant TEs in their genomes, and the extent to which the current findings apply.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Columbia-0 (Col-0) strain was used as the WT. The mutants h2a.z (hta8 hta9 hta11), and cmt2 cmt3 (cmt), suvh4 suvh5 suvh6 (suvh), and met1-1 are kind gifts from Daniel Zilberman, Judith Bender, and Eric Richards16,19,30,55. The mutants h2a.w-2 (hta6 hta7 hta12), h2a.z cmt, met1 cmt, met1 suvh, and F1met1 were described previously27,33,42. The mutants h2a.w cmt and h2a.w suvh were created in this study by crossing h2a.w and cmt or h2a.w and suvh, respectively. F1h2a.w were created by crossing h2a.w cmt and h2a.w suvh. T1h2a.z were created by transforming CMT3 and CMT2 transgenes, respectively, into h2a.z cmt27 (for CMT3-T1h2a.z_1–6 and CMT2-T1h2a.z_1–3). The plants were grown at 22 °C under 16 h light and 8 h dark conditions for 1–2 weeks on MS agar medium containing 1% sucrose. They were then grown on soil under the same condition.

Plasmid construction and plant transformation

For the transformation of CMT3 and CMT2 genes, the genomic regions from their promoter to just before the stop codon were amplified with the following primers, respectively: 5′-cgggccccccctcgaagcttgtatgattagatatgtgaa-3′ and 5′-ggatccacctccacccccgggtgcaagctcggaaggaagag-3′ for CMT3, 5′-cgggccccccctcgaagcttaacccgtagatatggcacaaag-3′ and 5′-ggatccacctccacccccgggatgaggaatggtttcttgaag-3′ for CMT2. The amplified fragments were cloned into HindIII-digested pGreenII017956 vector with GFP by using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (NEB). The plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens using electroporation, and then into cmt and h2a.z cmt plants by the floral dip. The transgenic lines were selected on MS agar plates with hygromycin and carbenicillin. The selected plants were then grown on soil. The plasmids generated in this study are available from the corresponding author.

RT-qPCR

Rosette leaves from 3- to 4-week-old individual plant were frozen with liquid nitrogen, ground, and total RNA was purified using TRIzol RNA Isolation Reagents (Thermo). RNA was treated with DNase I and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix with ezDNase (Thermo). cDNA was amplified using KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix with the following primers: 5′-gagacaaacgtgagaaacatgacc-3′ and 5′-cgctgacatcttcattgtcctc-3′ for CMT3, 5′-ccataagagaatctgcacggc-3′ and 5′-aagccataccaagcgaataacc-3′ for CMT2, and 5′-gatctccaaggccgagtatgat-3′, and 5′-cccattcataaaaccccagc-3′ for ACT2. qPCR was performed using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo). Two independent biological replicates were analyzed. The expression levels of CMT3 and CMT2 were normalized with the expression levels of ACT2. Bar plots in Supplementary Figs. 4g and 5k, l were created by ggplot2 (v3.3.2).

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing and data processing

Three- to four-week-old rosette leaves of one individual plant were frozen with liquid nitrogen, ground, and the genomic DNA was extracted using Nucleon PhytoPure (Cytiva). The genomic DNA was fragmented using S220 Focused Ultrasonicator (Covaris), and the size of 300–500 bp was gel-extracted. The libraries were prepared using TruSeq DNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina) and KAPA HyperPrep Kit (Kapa Biosystems), and then bisulfite conversion was performed using MethylCode Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Thermo). The libraries were amplified using KAPA HiFi HotStart Uracil ReadyMix (Kapa Biosystems) and purified using SPRIselect (Beckman Coulter). One hundred and fifty-base pair paired-end sequencing was performed using the HiSeq X and NovaSeq sequencer (Illumina) at Macrogen Japan Corp. Two independent biological replicates were analyzed. Raw sequence data were trimmed and quality-filtered using Trimmomatic version 0.3357. The trimmed and quality-filtered sequences were mapped to the reference genome (TAIR1058) for most of the analysis. When mapping to and around centromeres, Col-CEN_v1.240 was used. Deduplication and extraction of methylation data were performed using Bismark version 0.10.159. To calculate methylation levels in genes, TEs (TEs with protein-coding genes) and TEs, including TE fragments, a custom Perl script was used27,42. CG, CHG, and CHH methylation levels within each region were calculated as the number of methylated cytosines divided by the number of total cytosines (weighted methylation level)60. The regions with the number of total cytosines <10 were excluded from the analysis. In addition, for mutants containing the h2a.z mutation, regions in which more than 80% of cytosines had fewer than 5 mapped reads were also excluded from the analysis. Plots were created using Rstudio version 1.3.959. Venn diagrams were created using BioVenn61. TEs with statistically lower or higher mCH in F1h2a.w than in F1WT or mCHG/mCHH in CMT3/2-T1h2a.z than in CMT3/2-T1WT shown in Figs. 1b, 2g, Supplementary Figs. 1b, 4h, i, and 5g, n, o were extracted by using edgeR (v3.32.1)62 following Chen et al.63. For browser views, bedGraph files were created and visualized using IGV (v2.8.9)64. Centromeric and pericentromeric regions in Col-CEN were from Tsukahara et al.65, and mCHG levels were calculated in 10 kb window to generate bedGraph files.

ChIP-seq and data processing

The antibodies used were rabbit anti-H2A.Z27, rabbit anti-H2A.W632, and rabbit anti-H3 (ab1791; Abcam). For H2A.Z ChIP-seq, eChIP method66 was performed. Fourteen-day-old seedlings were frozen with liquid nitrogen, ground, and cross-linked with fixation buffer (PBS containing 1% formaldehyde, 1 mM Pefabloc SC (Roche), and cOmplete protease inhibitor (Roche)) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). To quench the reaction, glycine was added to a final concentration of 200 mM, and the mixture was incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The cell lysate was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was then washed with 1 mL of PBS, resuspended in 0.18 mL of Buffer S (50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate 1% SDS, and cOmplete protease inhibitor). Following a 10-min incubation at 4 °C, 0.72 mL of Buffer F (50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, and cOmplete proteinase inhibitor) was added. Chromatin fragmentation was performed by sonication using Picoruptor (Diagenode). Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Fragmented chromatin samples were incubated with Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen) for 2 h at 4 °C and then washed with PBST buffer (PBS with 0.1% Tween 20). The samples were then incubated with anti-H2A.Z or rabbit anti-H3 overnight at 4 °C with rotation. These were washed twice with low-salt ChIP buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS and cOmplete protease inhibitor), once with high-salt ChIP buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, 350 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS and cOmplete protease inhibitor), once with ChIP wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate and cOmplete protease inhibitor) and TE buffer, and eluted from beads by adding 100 μl of ChIP Elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA and 1% SDS)66 with incubation for 15 minutes at 65 °C. As with H2A.Z ChIP-seq, cross-linking and chromatin fragmentation for H2A.W ChIP-seq were performed as previously66. Fragmented chromatin samples were incubated with anti-H2A.W or rabbit anti-H3 overnight at 4 °C with rotation, followed by incubation with Dynabeads Protein G for 2 h at 4 °C. These were washed once with low-salt ChIP buffer, twice with high-salt ChIP buffer, once with ChIP wash buffer and TE buffer, and eluted from beads by adding 150 μl of ChIP Elution buffer66 with incubation for 6–9 h at 65 °C. DNA was extracted from the eluted samples with Monarch PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (NEB), and libraries were constructed with ThruPLEX DNA-Seq Kit (Takara). The experiments were performed in two independent biological replicates. One hundred and fifty base pair paired-end sequencing was performed using the HiSeq X Ten and NovaSeq sequencer (Illumina) at Macrogen Japan Corp. Two independent biological replicates were analyzed. Raw sequence data were trimmed and quality-filtered using Trimmomatic version 0.3357. The trimmed and quality-filtered sequences were mapped to the reference genome (TAIR10) using Bowtie (version 1.1.2)67 with the option -M 1 -best. BEDTools (version 2.26.0)68 was used to count the reads that overlapped with each gene, TE, or TE including fragments. Plots were created using Rstudio. To create metaplots and heatmaps around and within genes and TEs, 2 kb upstream and downstream of genes and TEs were divided into 10 segments (200 b), and TEs were divided into 20 segments. The mapped reads within each segment of TEs, and the flanking regions were counted using bedtools and normalized as RPKM using Perl script. Metaplots were created with the averaged RPKM within each segment and plotted using R version 4.3.1. Heatmaps were created using gplots (v3.1.1). For browser views, bigwig files were created by deepTools (v3.5.4)69 and visualized using IGV.

Phylogenetic analysis of COPIA copies

COPIA sequences were retrieved from TE annotation of TAIR9.0. Among 1800 annotated regions, copies with RT core domain region (ca. 260 aa) were used. After manual alignment, sequences with more than 100 bp gap were discarded. The remaining 202 sequences were used for the calculation of genetic distances with p-distance. Evolutionary analyses were conducted by MEGA570. TE pairs with a p-distance <0.25 that are each other’s closest in terms of p-distance were selected. The pairs with mCHG in WT and h2a.w > 0.1 and with mCHG in WT and h2a.z > 0.1 were selected.
