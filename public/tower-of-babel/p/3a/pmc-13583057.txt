Individualized cholinergic pathway degeneration in Alzheimer's disease characterized by normative modeling
Authors: Shuoyun Feng, Huixiang Zhuang, Chang Xu, Xinyu Guo, Li Cao, Yaou Liu, Yue Guan, Yao Li, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCholinergic pathway degeneration is implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD), but its individualized characterization in AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) remains limited.METHODSWe constructed lifespan normative models of medial and lateral cholinergic pathways using >6000 diffusion MRI scans from healthy individuals. These models were applied to 1742 scans from 725 participants in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort. Individualized diffusion abnormalities were integrated into a composite cholinergic pathway degeneration index (CPDI). Group differences and longitudinal associations with cognitive decline and progression were evaluated.RESULTSCPDI was higher in MCI and AD participants than in controls, and increased longitudinally in patients with AD and in patients with MCI who progressed to AD. Higher baseline CPDI was associated with an increased risk of progression from MCI to AD and faster decline in global cognition, memory, language, and executive function.DISCUSSIONCPDI provides an individualized biomarker of cholinergic pathway degeneration with prognostic relevance in AD.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by progressive cognitive decline with substantial inter‐individual variability in clinical presentation and disease trajectory. 1 , 2 , 3 Beyond amyloid and tau pathology, disruption of large‐scale neuromodulatory systems, particularly the basal forebrain cholinergic system, plays a central role in cognitive dysfunction. 4 The nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM) provides widespread cortical innervation through medial and lateral white matter pathways, supporting attention, memory, and higher‐order cognitive processes. 5 , 6 Degeneration of this system has long been implicated in AD and forms the basis of the cholinergic hypothesis of cognitive decline. 7

Recent studies have identified NBM atrophy as an early marker of AD‐related neurodegeneration, reflecting loss of cholinergic neurons within the basal forebrain. 8 However, volumetric measures do not directly capture degeneration of long‐range cholinergic projections, where axonal and myelin alterations may precede overt neuronal loss through a dying‐back process. 9 Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), therefore, provides complementary information by enabling assessment of pathway‐level microstructural changes beyond regional atrophy. Prior diffusion MRI studies have demonstrated increased diffusivity within medial and lateral cholinergic pathways across the AD continuum, from subjective cognitive decline to dementia. 9 , 10 These microstructural alterations have been shown to relate more closely to cognitive performance—particularly in attention, executive function, and memory—than traditional measures such as NBM volume. 9 , 10 , 11 Moreover, reduced integrity of cholinergic projections has been associated with accelerated cognitive decline and increased risk of progression from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia, 10 highlighting their potential as early and functionally relevant biomarkers. However, most prior studies have relied on group‐level analyses, which may obscure substantial inter‐individual variability in disease expression and limit their utility for individualized characterization.

Normative modeling offers a framework to address this limitation by establishing population‐based reference trajectories and quantifying individual deviations relative to expected values given age and other covariates. 12 This is particularly important for white matter, which follows highly nonlinear developmental and aging trajectories, 13 making it difficult to distinguish pathological alterations from normal aging processes in AD. Normative modeling has been increasingly applied to characterize individualized patterns of brain atrophy and cerebral perfusion, demonstrating improved sensitivity to disease heterogeneity compared to conventional case–control approaches. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 However, its application to cholinergic white matter pathways, especially using large‐scale lifespan data, remains unexplored.

In this study, we constructed a lifespan normative model of medial and lateral cholinergic white matter pathways originating from the NBM using more than 6000 diffusion MRI scans from healthy individuals. We characterized nonlinear age‐related trajectories of diffusion metrics, including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AxD), and radial diffusivity (RD), to establish lifespan normative reference distributions for cholinergic white matter integrity. This framework was then applied to the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort to derive individualized measures of deviation in patients with MCI and AD. To capture coordinated microstructural degeneration, we integrated pathway‐specific abnormalities into a composite cholinergic pathway degeneration index (CPDI).

We assessed whether individualized markers of cholinergic pathway degeneration derived from normative modeling (1) can track disease trajectories in individuals with MCI and AD; (2) can be used to predict progression from MCI to AD; and (3) are associated with cognitive decline across multiple domains, including global cognition, memory, language, executive, and visuospatial function.

METHODS

Participants and datasets

Participants were derived from three datasets (Figure 1): a template construction dataset, a normative reference dataset, and a clinical test dataset. The template construction dataset included healthy young adults from the Human Connectome Project 18 (HCP; n = 176; age range: 23–38 years; 61.4% female) with 7T diffusion MRI (dMRI) and corresponding 3T T1‐weighted (T1w) imaging, and was used to construct templates of medial and lateral cholinergic white matter pathways. The normative reference dataset comprised cohort‐defined cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals from 11 publicly available cohorts, including UK Biobank 19 and Cam‐CAN, 20 with cohort‐specific inclusion and exclusion criteria detailed in Table S1. Of the participants with available dMRI and T1w scans, a total of 5155 individuals with 6065 scans were retained after preprocessing and quality assessment (Section 2.2) and used as the analysis‐ready normative reference dataset (Table S2). Baseline scans were used for normative model fitting, whereas longitudinal scans were retained for centile score stability analyses. MRI acquisition parameters are provided in Table S3.

MRI data were organized into three datasets: HCP for cholinergic pathway template reconstruction, a multi‐cohort lifespan reference dataset for normative modeling, and ADNI data for clinical analyses. (B) Construction of cholinergic pathway template and feature extraction. Group‐level templates of medial and lateral cholinergic pathways were generated using probabilistic tractography and registered to individual native space to extract diffusion metrics (FA, MD, AxD, and RD). (C) Normative modeling and individualized deviation score calculation. GAMLSS models were used to derive centile scores, from which CPDI was calculated to quantify cholinergic pathway degeneration. (D) Clinical validation. Analyses included group comparisons, longitudinal CPDI trajectories, risk of progression from MCI to AD, and associations with cognitive decline. AD, Alzheimer's disease; AC, anterior commissure; AxD, axial diffusivity; CU, cognitively unimpaired; CPDI, cholinergic pathway degeneration index; dMRI, diffusion magnetic resonance imaging; FA, fractional anisotropy; GAMLSS, generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape; MCI, mild cognitive impairment; MD, mean diffusivity; NBM, nucleus basalis of Meynert; RD, radial diffusivity; T1w, T1‐weighted.

The clinical test dataset was obtained from ADNI (http://adni.loni.usc.edu/), a multi‐center longitudinal biomarker study of AD. Participants from ADNI‐1, ADNI Grand Opportunity (ADNI‐GO), ADNI‐2, ADNI‐3, and ADNI‐4 were included if age, sex, education, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, diffusion MRI, and T1w imaging data were available (n = 725; 1,742 scans). For each participant, the earliest visit with complete imaging data was selected as baseline. Participants were categorized as CU, MCI, or AD according to established ADNI diagnostic criteria, 21 using clinical assessments within 12 weeks of the imaging date. MCI participants were classified as “MCI progressive” if they converted to AD during follow‐up and as “MCI stable” otherwise. Neuropsychological measures were obtained from the ADNI database and matched to each imaging timepoint within a 12‐week window, including the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive Subscale (ADAS‐Cog) 22 and the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), as well as domain‐specific cognition measures of memory (ADNI‐MEM), 23 executive function (ADNI‐EF), 24 language (ADNI‐LAN), 25 and visuospatial function (ADNI‐VS). 25

All contributing studies were approved by the institutional review boards of the participating institutions, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their authorized representatives.

MRI preprocessing

Structural and diffusion MRI data from the normative reference dataset (excluding UK Biobank) and the clinical test dataset were preprocessed using the QSIPrep 26 pipeline. T1w images underwent bias‐field correction, 27 skull stripping, 28 and tissue segmentation. 29 Diffusion‐weighted images were corrected for head motion, eddy currents, 30 and susceptibility‐induced distortion. 31 For UK Biobank, centrally released preprocessed diffusion data were used. These data had undergone comparable preprocessing, including correction for head motion, eddy‐current effects, and susceptibility‐induced distortions. 32 Across datasets, FA, MD, AxD, and RD maps were consistently estimated using FMRIB Software Library (FSL) dtifit 33 from the b = 0 and b = 1000 s/mm2 volumes. Participants were excluded if any subsequently extracted pathway‐level diffusion‐derived metric exceeded 5 standard deviations (SD) from the corresponding cohort‐specific mean. 34 Cohort‐specific numbers and proportions of excluded participants are reported in Table S4. For the HCP dataset, diffusion data were used as provided after the standardized HCP preprocessing pipeline. 18

Construction of cholinergic pathway templates and feature extraction

Cholinergic white matter pathway templates were constructed using the template construction cohort, comprising HCP 7T diffusion MRI data (Figure 1), following a previously described tractography‐based method. 10 The HCP 7T dataset was selected because its high‐resolution, multi‐shell diffusion MRI data provide detailed characterization of complex fiber architecture, including crossing fibers, thereby supporting reliable delineation of the medial and lateral cholinergic pathways. 18 , 35 The young‐adult HCP data, therefore, served as a high‐quality anatomic reference for pathway reconstruction, consistent with previous work using HCP 7T diffusion data to delineate cholinergic projections. 35 Tractography was anatomically constrained using an NBM seed mask, cingulum and external capsule waypoint masks, and brainstem and anterior commissure exclusion masks. All masks used for tractography were transformed into each participant's native diffusion space before fiber tracking. Detailed mask definitions and transformations are provided in the Supplementary Methods SM1.

For each participant, BedpostX 36 and ProbtrackX 37 were used for NBM‐seeded probabilistic tractography, with streamlines generated from each voxel within the NBM region of interest (ROI). Streamlines passing through the cingulum or external capsule waypoint masks were assigned to the medial and lateral cholinergic pathways, respectively, whereas those entering exclusion masks were discarded. Individual tractography maps were transformed to a multimodal unbiased template space constructed from T1w and b0 images. The final group‐level medial and lateral cholinergic pathway templates retained voxels present in at least 70% of individual tract maps. 38

Individual‐level FA, MD, AxD, and RD values were extracted using the constructed cholinergic pathway templates. To account for age‐related anatomic variability across the lifespan, an age‐specific intermediate registration strategy was used to transform the templates into each participant's native diffusion space (Figure S1). Each participant's native T1‐weighted image was nonlinearly registered to the corresponding age‐specific template, 39 whereas the b0 image was affinely registered to the native T1‐weighted image. These transformations were combined with the pre‐estimated transformation between the age‐specific T1‐weighted template and the cholinergic pathway template space, and then inverted to map the medial and lateral pathway templates into each participant's native diffusion space. All nonlinear and affine registrations were performed using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). 40 Transformed pathway masks were intersected with individual white matter segmentations to ensure tissue specificity. White matter segmentations were obtained from the QSIPrep anatomic workflow, in which white matter hypointensity (WMH) labels are retained within the white matter tissue class; therefore, WMH‐affected voxels were not systematically excluded from the pathway masks. This approach preserves pathway regions with potentially age‐ or disease‐related microstructural abnormalities relevant to the diffusion measures of interest and is consistent with previous large‐scale lifespan diffusion MRI reference and normative modeling studies. 13 , 34 Mean diffusion metrics were then calculated by averaging voxel‐wise values within the resulting native‐space cholinergic pathway masks.

Normative modeling

Model construction

Lifespan normative models of cholinergic white matter integrity were constructed using the Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS) framework. 41 Separate models were fitted for each diffusion metric within the medial and lateral cholinergic pathways. For each pathway‐specific metric, age was modeled using fractional polynomials to capture nonlinear lifespan trajectories, sex was included as a fixed effect, and dataset/acquisition‐protocol category, as defined in Table S3, was included as a random effect to account for acquisition‐related variability. 42 , 43 Skewness and kurtosis parameters were specified as intercept‐only terms to improve model stability. 17

Final models were selected based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) across candidate distributions and alternative age and random‐effect specifications, and are summarized in Table S5. Centile curves were then derived to establish normative reference trajectories across the lifespan. 42 , 43 Model adequacy and robustness were evaluated using normalized quantile residual diagnostics, bootstrap resampling, and longitudinal centile‐score stability analyses. In addition, a leave‐one‐study‐out analysis was conducted to assess the sensitivity of the model fitting to any single study cohort. Detailed procedures and results are provided in Supplementary Methods SM2, Figures S2 and S3, and Table S6.

Individualized deviation scores

To estimate individualized deviations while reducing site‐related confounding, CU participants from ADNI were randomly split in half into training and test subsets, stratified by acquisition site. The ADNI CU training subset was incorporated into the normative reference dataset, whereas the remaining ADNI CU participants and all MCI and AD participants formed an independent clinical test cohort.

The fitted normative models were applied to the test cohort to quantify individualized deviations in cholinergic pathway integrity. For each participant, observed diffusion metrics were mapped onto the corresponding age‐, sex‐, and dataset/acquisition‐protocol‐adjusted normative distributions, and centile scores were obtained using the cumulative distribution function of the fitted distributions.

To summarize subject‐level deviations in cholinergic pathway integrity, we calculated the CPDI from the continuous normative z‐scores of FA, MD, AxD, and RD in the medial and lateral pathways. Centile scores were transformed into standardized z‐scores using the inverse normal transformation, 44 with FA z‐scores inverted so that higher values consistently reflected more adverse microstructural deviations. The overall CPDI was calculated as the mean of the resulting eight z‐scores.

To characterize pathway‐specific deviations, medial and lateral CPDI were calculated separately using the same procedure for the four diffusion metrics within each respective pathway. Higher overall, medial, and lateral CPDI values indicate greater diffusion‐defined microstructural damage in the corresponding cholinergic pathways. Hereafter, “CPDI” without a pathway qualifier refers to the overall CPDI.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons of baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared across diagnostic groups (CU, MCI, and AD). Continuous variables were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed data, whereas categorical variables were compared using chi‐square tests.

Cross‐sectional group comparisons of cholinergic measures

Group differences in cholinergic pathway measures were assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 status as covariates. Marker‐level analyses were conducted separately for each diffusion metric within the medial and lateral pathways. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction across three pairwise group comparisons and eight pathway‐metric combinations (24 tests). The same ANCOVA framework was used to compare overall, medial, and lateral CPDI values across groups, with effect sizes quantified using Cohen's d.

Longitudinal analysis of CPDI

Longitudinal changes in CPDI over all available follow‐up visits were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects (LME) models, with CPDI as the dependent variable, time from baseline as a fixed effect, and subject‐specific random intercepts. Separate models were fitted for stable CU, stable MCI, AD, and progressive MCI participants. To compare CPDI trajectories across groups, an additional LME model was constructed including fixed effects for time, diagnostic group, and their interaction (time × group), followed by Tukey‐adjusted post hoc comparisons. Individualized CPDI change rates were calculated as the change from baseline to final follow‐up divided by duration, and compared across groups using multivariable linear regression models, adjusting for baseline age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 status.

To assess the follow‐up duration required to detect CPDI change, cumulative follow‐up‐window analyses were performed using the same LME framework. Stable and progressive MCI were evaluated over 12‐, 18‐, 24‐, 36‐, 48‐, and 60‐month follow‐up windows, whereas CU, stable MCI, and AD were evaluated over 12‐, 18‐, and 24‐month windows. For each window, annualized within‐group CPDI slopes and between‐group slope differences were estimated. To quantify the magnitude of between‐group trajectory separation across follow‐up durations, standardized between‐group differences in cumulative CPDI change were calculated at each follow‐up horizon by dividing the model‐estimated between‐group difference in cumulative CPDI change by the pooled within‐group baseline CPDI standard deviation. 45

Risk of progression from MCI to AD according to baseline CPDI

To assess the prognostic relevance of CPDI, time‐to‐event analyses were performed in participants with MCI at baseline, with progression defined as conversion from MCI to AD during follow‐up. 15 , 17 Participants were stratified into high and low baseline CPDI groups using the 90th percentile threshold derived from the CU reference group. 46 , 47 , 48 Progression‐free survival was estimated via Kaplan–Meier curves and compared using the log‐rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine the association between baseline CPDI group and progression risk, 49 adjusting for age, sex, education, APOE ε4 carrier status, and baseline MMSE. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. All tests were two‐sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Association of baseline CPDI with cognitive decline over time

LME models were used to examine the association between overall, medial, and lateral baseline CPDI and cognitive decline over time in participants with MCI or AD. Cognitive outcomes included ADAS‐Cog, ADNI‐MEM, ADNI‐EF, ADNI‐LAN, and ADNI‐VS, all standardized to z‐scores prior to analysis. Separate models were fitted for each outcome, with subject‐specific random intercepts and slopes. Fixed effects included time, baseline CPDI, and their interaction (time × CPDI). All models were adjusted for baseline age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 status. For visualization, participants were stratified into high and low baseline CPDI groups using the 90th percentile threshold derived from the CU reference group. 46 , 47 , 48

Lifespan trajectories and trajectory‐derived landmark ages of cholinergic pathway integrity

Lifespan normative curves showed age‐dependent trajectories in diffusion metrics across both medial and lateral cholinergic pathways (Figure 2B). FA exhibited an inverted U‐shaped pattern, while MD, AxD, and RD showed U‐shaped trajectories. 34 The lateral pathway reached its trajectory‐derived landmark ages earlier than the medial pathway, as indicated by an earlier FA peak and earlier minima of MD, AxD, and RD (Figure 2C). Across both pathways, these landmarks followed a similar temporal sequence, with FA peaking first, followed by the minima of RD, MD, and AxD. Leave‐one‐study‐out analyses confirmed that these nonlinear trajectory patterns were preserved after exclusion of any single contributing study (Figure S3).

Group differences in cholinergic pathway centile scores and CPDI

Individualized cholinergic centile scores differed across diagnostic groups (Figure 3A). MCI and AD participants showed lower FA and higher MD, AxD, and RD centile scores in the medial pathway than CU (all FDR‐adjusted p’s < 0.05), with more severe abnormalities in AD than MCI (all FDR‐adjusted p’s < 0.05). In the lateral pathway, MD, AxD, and RD differed significantly across groups (all FDR‐adjusted p’s < 0.05), whereas FA did not. Effect sizes were largest for CU versus AD, and were generally greater for diffusivity metrics than for FA. Across pathways, FA, MD, and RD showed larger effects in the medial pathway, whereas AxD showed comparable or larger effects in the lateral pathway (Table S7).

Consistently, CPDI, a continuous composite measure summarizing normative deviations across diffusion metrics and cholinergic pathways, increased progressively from CU to MCI to AD (Figure 3B), with significant pairwise differences between all groups (all p’s < 0.05). The largest difference in overall CPDI was observed between CU and AD (Cohen's d = 0.685).

Pathway‐specific analyses showed that both medial and lateral CPDI were significantly elevated in MCI relative to CU and further increased in AD relative to both CU and MCI (all p’s < 0.05; Figure S4). Across diagnostic contrasts, effect sizes were consistently largest for medial CPDI, followed by overall and lateral CPDI (Table S8).

CPDI increases longitudinally in AD and progressive MCI

Longitudinal analyses using all available follow‐up visits showed distinct CPDI trajectories across clinical stages (Figure 4). In LME models, CPDI increased significantly over time in the AD group (annualized slope β = 0.124, p < 0.001), but not in the stable MCI (β = 0.00002, p = 0.998) or CU group (β = −0.00001, p = 0.999; Figure 4B). Tukey‐adjusted pairwise comparisons from the joint LME confirmed faster CPDI accumulation in AD than in both CU and stable MCI (both p’s < 0.001). Consistently, individualized CPDI change rates differed across groups after adjustment for baseline age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 status (F = 4.272, p = 0.015; Figure 4C). Within the MCI group, CPDI increased over time in progressive MCI (β = 0.081, p < 0.001), but remained stable among non‐converters (β = 0.00002, p = 0.998), with the progressive group showing a significantly faster rate of increase (p < 0.001; Figure 4D).

Cumulative follow‐up‐window analyses indicated that within‐group CPDI increases in progressive MCI were consistently detectable from 24 months onward (all p’s < 0.05), whereas no significant longitudinal change was observed in stable MCI across the examined windows (Table S9). The progressive‐versus‐stable MCI slope difference reached statistical significance at 48 and 60 months. The standardized cumulative trajectory difference at follow‐up horizon H, denoted as dH , was 0.398 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.167–0.629) at 48 months and 0.420 (95% CI: 0.187–0.653) at 60 months, respectively. In the common‐window analyses of CU, stable MCI, and AD, AD showed significant within‐group CPDI increases at 18 and 24 months (both p < 0.01), whereas CU and stable MCI showed no significant change (Table S10). Across the common 12‐, 18‐, and 24‐month follow‐up windows, AD consistently showed a steeper CPDI increase than CU and stable MCI, consistent with the direction of the full‐follow‐up analysis. The AD–CU slope difference was significant at 18 months (p = 0.028), whereas the AD‐stable MCI difference was significant at 24 months (p = 0.025); the remaining comparisons did not reach statistical significance (Table S11).

Higher baseline CPDI is associated with increased risk of conversion from MCI to AD

In multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, education, APOE ε4 status, and baseline MMSE, high baseline CPDI was associated with increased MCI‐to‐AD progression risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.20‐3.07; p < 0.01). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis confirmed shorter progression‐free survival in the high baseline CPDI group (log‐rank p = 0.001; Figure 4E).

Higher baseline CPDI is associated with accelerated decline in global cognition, memory, language, and executive function

Higher baseline CPDI was associated with faster decline in global cognition, memory, language, and executive function over time (Figure 5). DISCUSSION

***In this study, we established lifespan normative models of cholinergic white matter pathways using a large reference cohort and applied them to quantify subject‐specific microstructural deviations in MCI and AD. Centile‐normalized measures revealed graded, pathway‐specific cholinergic disruption across clinical stages. By integrating multivariate diffusion deviations, the composite CPDI captured coordinated microstructural disruption, increased longitudinally in AD and progressive MCI, predicted conversion from MCI to AD, and was associated with accelerated decline in global cognition, memory, language, and executive function.

Our results show substantial inter‐individual heterogeneity in cholinergic pathway degeneration. Although CPDI was significantly elevated at the group level in MCI and AD, its distribution showed considerable overlap across diagnostic categories. This variability was further supported by divergent longitudinal trajectories, with CPDI remaining unchanged in stable MCI individuals but increasing in MCI converters. These findings are consistent with prior normative modeling studies demonstrating marked neurobiological heterogeneity in AD, including individualized deviation patterns in cortical thickness and cerebral perfusion. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Our results extend these observations to the cholinergic system, demonstrating that diffusion‐based measures of pathway integrity also follow individualized trajectories. Compared with prior diffusion MRI studies that primarily emphasized group‐level differences in cholinergic tracts, our approach captures inter‐individual heterogeneity, providing a more nuanced characterization of cholinergic pathway degeneration in AD.

Our findings further indicate a pathway‐specific and metric‐dependent pattern of vulnerability within the cholinergic system. Abnormalities were more extensive in the medial pathway, whereas those in the lateral pathway were detected mainly by diffusivity measures. Pathway‐specific CPDI analyses further supported this pattern: across comparisons among CU, MCI, and AD, effect sizes were consistently largest for medial CPDI, followed by overall and lateral CPDI. The medial predominance is anatomically plausible, as the medial pathway traverses limbic and paralimbic regions affected early in AD. It is also consistent with neuropathological 50 and imaging evidence 34 showing early involvement of medial cholinergic projections. The larger effects observed for diffusivity metrics are consistent with prior in vivo and post‐mortem studies reporting increased diffusivity within cholinergic white matter pathways across the AD spectrum. 9 , 51 Together, these findings support preferential vulnerability of medial cholinergic projections and suggest that diffusivity measures may be particularly sensitive to pathway‐level microstructural disruption, potentially reflecting demyelination, axonal injury, or extracellular changes.

Cholinergic pathway degeneration was also differentially associated with longitudinal decline across cognitive domains. Higher baseline CPDI was significantly associated with accelerated worsening in global cognition, memory, language, and executive function, but not visuospatial function. In pathway‐specific analyses, both medial and lateral CPDI were associated with decline in global cognition, memory, and language, whereas an association with executive decline was observed only for medial CPDI. This domain‐specific pattern is anatomically plausible given the organization of cholinergic projections from the NBM, which densely innervate medial temporal and temporal neocortical regions critical for episodic memory and language processing. 5 , 6 The association with executive function may be particularly relevant to the medial pathway, which courses through the cingulum and innervates cingulate and pericingulate regions involved in cingulo‐frontal cognitive control. 52 , 53 Consistent with this interpretation, human [1⁸F]fluoroethoxybenzovesamicol ([18F]FEOBV) positron emission tomography (PET) studies have linked lower cholinergic terminal integrity in the anterior cingulate cortex to poorer executive performance. 54 Our findings are also consistent with diffusion MRI evidence linking increased diffusivity within cholinergic pathways to impairments in memory, attention, and global cognition, 9 , 51 as well as reduced cholinergic pathway integrity to executive dysfunction. 11 Collectively, the findings support an association between cholinergic pathway degeneration and decline across multiple cognitive domains, while suggesting a potentially distinct contribution of medial pathway degeneration to executive decline.

In addition to characterizing cross‐sectional heterogeneity, our results demonstrate that cholinergic pathway degeneration is informative for disease monitoring and progression. Longitudinal increases in CPDI were most evident in AD and in MCI participants who later converted to AD, whereas CPDI remained stable in those who did not progress. Follow‐up‐window analyses indicated that the detectability of CPDI progression depended on observation duration. In progressive MCI, 12 months provided an early but relatively imprecise indication of change, whereas ≈24 months yielded more consistent evidence of within‐group progression and ≈48 months provided clearer separation from stable MCI. In AD, CPDI progression was more consistently detectable over ≈18–24 months. Longer follow‐up also yielded greater cumulative separation between progressive and stable MCI trajectories. These findings highlight follow‐up duration as an important consideration in longitudinal studies and clinical‐trial design, although dedicated studies are needed to determine whether CPDI can reliably monitor progression at the individual‐patient level. Complementing these longitudinal findings, higher baseline CPDI was associated with an increased risk of progression from MCI to AD and shorter progression‐free survival. Thus, both baseline CPDI and its longitudinal trajectory provide information about subsequent disease course. Extending prior deviation‐based studies of neuroanatomic and perfusion markers, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 our findings identify cholinergic pathway degeneration as an additional, functionally relevant axis of vulnerability in AD. Given the central role of the cholinergic system in cognitive regulation, CPDI may provide pathway‐specific prognostic information beyond traditional structural or vascular markers.

The lifespan normative framework provides important context for interpreting disease‐related deviations by distinguishing pathological abnormalities from expected age‐related variation. Notably, the medial pathway reached its trajectory‐derived landmarks at later ages than the lateral pathway. One possible anatomic basis for this difference is that the medial pathway predominantly courses through the cingulum, a limbic association tract that has been reported to exhibit relatively later diffusion‐metric turning points across the lifespan. 55 , 56 In contrast, the lateral pathway traverses the external capsule and related lateral white matter regions, for which earlier trajectory turning points have been reported in several lifespan diffusion MRI studies. 55 , 56 These prior observations are broadly consistent with the later trajectory‐derived landmarks observed for the medial relative to the lateral cholinergic pathway in the present study. Because diffusion metrics reflect multiple underlying tissue properties, however, these differences should be interpreted as pathway‐specific lifespan trajectory patterns rather than as evidence of a specific biological maturation process.

Several limitations should be considered. First, diffusion MRI provides indirect measures of white matter microstructure and lacks specificity to cholinergic fibers; therefore, the observed alterations likely reflect a combination of demyelination, axonal degeneration, and extracellular changes rather than cholinergic‐specific pathology. Future studies integrating cholinergic PET tracer imaging could provide complementary molecular evidence and help determine whether diffusion‐defined pathway alterations correspond to cholinergic denervation. Second, the lateral pathway was analyzed as an integrated tract, which may obscure branch‐specific effects across its frontal, parietal, and visual projections. Future studies using anatomically defined branch‐specific tractography may provide greater anatomic specificity. Third, CPDI integrates direction‐aligned normative deviations across four diffusion measures with equal weighting and, therefore, does not explicitly account for correlations among them, particularly the substantial overlap between MD and RD. Although sensitivity analyses excluding either MD or RD preserved the principal findings (Figures S6 and S7; Tables S13 and S14), suggesting that this redundancy did not drive the observed associations, the relative contribution of correlated diffusion measures remains to be determined. Future studies could investigate covariance‐aware and data‐driven weighting strategies to better account for correlations among diffusion measures and refine their joint contribution to the characterization of cholinergic pathway degeneration. Fourth, despite modeling dataset/acquisition‐protocol effects within the GAMLSS framework, residual acquisition variability and unmeasured confounders, such as vascular risk factors, may still influence deviation estimates. In addition, the reference and clinical cohorts were derived mainly from North American and European research datasets, which may limit generalizability to more diverse populations. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference and warrants further longitudinal and multimodal investigation.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that individualized deviations in cholinergic white matter pathways, quantified using a lifespan normative modeling framework, capture substantial heterogeneity in neurodegeneration across MCI and AD. The derived CPDI tracks longitudinal progression, predicts conversion from MCI to AD, and is associated with decline in global cognition, memory, language, and executive function. These findings support normative modeling of cholinergic pathways as a promising approach for individualized disease characterization and prognostic assessment.
