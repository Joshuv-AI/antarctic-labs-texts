Cone-beam computed tomography-guided biomimetic management of extensive internal inflammatory resorption with histopathologic correlation
Authors: Z. K. Sincy, Shiji Dinakaran, Keerthana Surendran, C. Leela Krishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Internal inflammatory resorption is an uncommon pathologic condition characterized by progressive loss of intraradicular dentin due to clastic activity within inflamed pulp tissue. Early diagnosis is challenging because clinical symptoms are often absent until significant structural damage occurs. This case report describes the diagnosis and conservative management of extensive internal inflammatory resorption in a maxillary central incisor of a 30-year-old male patient. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) was used to confirm the extent of the lesion and differentiate it from external resorption. Nonsurgical root canal treatment was performed to arrest the resorptive process. The structurally compromised cervical region was reinforced using polyethylene fiber integrated within a fiber-reinforced composite restoration. Histopathological examination revealed fibrovascular connective tissue with chronic inflammatory infiltrate and multinucleated giant cells consistent with odontoclastic activity. Follow-up at 6 months demonstrated satisfactory aesthetic and functional outcomes with radiographic stability. This case highlights the importance of CBCT in accurate diagnosis and emphasizes the role of biomimetic reinforcement strategies in preserving structurally weakened teeth.

INTRODUCTION

Internal inflammatory resorption is an uncommon pathologic condition characterized by progressive destruction of intraradicular dentin due to clastic activity within chronically inflamed pulp tissue.[12] The condition is often asymptomatic and may remain undetected until routine radiographic examination or crown discoloration becomes evident. Continued resorptive activity can result in extensive structural compromise and weakening of the remaining dentin.[1]

Conventional radiography provides limited two-dimensional information and may not accurately determine lesion extent or differentiate internal inflammatory resorption from external cervical resorption.[3] Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) enables three-dimensional assessment of lesion morphology, remaining dentinal thickness, and perforation status, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning.[3]

Management of extensive internal resorption remains challenging because progressive dentinal destruction substantially weakens the tooth, particularly in the cervical region, where stress concentration is greatest.[4] Conventional management approaches include thermoplasticized gutta-percha, calcium silicate-based materials, fiber posts, and composite restorations.[567] Although these methods provide satisfactory sealing ability, they may not adequately reinforce weakened dentin or restore biomechanical integrity.

Recent biomimetic restorative concepts have emphasized preservation and reinforcement of compromised tooth structure using fiber-reinforced composite materials.[56] Polyethylene fibers incorporated within composite resin have been shown to improve stress distribution and reduce crack propagation by functioning as an internal splint.[6] Similarly, the Bio block or Bio-Bulk concept using short fiber-reinforced composite has been proposed for reinforcement of structurally compromised roots affected by internal resorption.[8910] However, reports describing conservative polyethylene fiber reinforcement combined with CBCT-guided diagnosis and histopathologic correlation remain limited.

Histopathologic examination may additionally provide biological confirmation of internal inflammatory resorption through identification of chronic inflammatory infiltrate, multinucleated odontoclast-like cells, and resorptive lacunae adjacent to dentinal surfaces.[1] However, histopathologic correlation is infrequently documented in routine clinical reports of internal resorption.

This report presents a multidisciplinary biomimetic approach incorporating CBCT assessment, histopathologic confirmation, and polyethylene fiber-reinforced composite restoration for management of structurally compromised internal inflammatory resorption.

CASE REPORT

This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines.

A 30-year-old male presented to the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics with progressive discoloration of the maxillary right central incisor noticed over the past few months [Figure 1]. The patient reported no spontaneous pain, and his medical history was non-contributory. On further inquiry, he recalled trauma to the anterior teeth at approximately 6 years of age.

Clinical and radiographic assessment. Clinical examination revealed discoloration of tooth 11 with cervical thinning of the coronal structure, while the incisal enamel remained intact. The tooth was asymptomatic on percussion and palpation, and periodontal probing depths were within normal limits. Thermal pulp testing using refrigerant spray (Endo-Frost; Coltene/Whaledent GmbH, Altstätten, Switzerland) produced an exaggerated but nonlingering response compared with adjacent teeth. No swelling, sinus tract, or abnormal mobility was observed.

An intraoral periapical radiograph demonstrated a well-defined radiolucent enlargement within the coronal and cervical pulp space with a balloon-like appearance suggestive of internal resorption [Figure 1]. The external root contour appeared intact, although the true extent of dentinal involvement could not be accurately determined on the two-dimensional image. Mild widening of the apical periodontal ligament space was also noted.

To further evaluate the lesion and rule out external cervical resorption, CBCT with a limited field of view (6 cm × 6 cm) was performed. CBCT revealed symmetrical enlargement of the pulp space extending to the cemento-enamel junction with preservation of the external root surface and no perforation. Axial, sagittal, and coronal sections [Figure 1] demonstrated thinning of dentinal walls in the cervical region and mild apical periodontal ligament widening without periapical bone destruction. These findings confirmed internal inflammatory resorption involving the cervical third of tooth 11.

According to the American Association of Endodontists diagnostic criteria, the pulpal diagnosis was asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis with asymptomatic apical periodontitis. Nonsurgical root canal treatment was therefore planned to eliminate inflamed pulpal tissue and arrest the resorptive process. Because CBCT confirmed an intact external root surface without perforation, a conservative orthograde approach was selected.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for treatment and publication of clinical details and images.

After obtaining informed consent, local anesthesia was administered using 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Lignox 2% A; Indoco Remedies Ltd., Mumbai, India), and tooth 11 was isolated under a rubber dam. A conventional access cavity was prepared. During access refinement, a small portion of mesio-palatal cervical enamel chipped due to pre-existing thinning, and unsupported enamel was smoothed.

Upon entry into the pulp chamber, vascular pulp-like tissue occupying the resorptive cavity was observed and extirpated using sterile hand instruments [Figure 2]. The specimen was preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathologic examination. Bleeding was controlled with irrigation using 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (Prime Dental Products Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India).

(a) Access cavity (blue arrow) (b) cervical enamel chipping (green arrow) (c) extirpated pulp tissue (d) histopathology showing multinucleated odontoclast-like cell (arrow) (e) polyethylene fiber placement (f) postoperative radiograph-polyethylene fiber (blue arrow) and fiber-reinforced composite (green arrow) (g) final restoration showing reinforced coronal structure (green arrow) (h) 1-week follow-up (i) 6-month radiographic follow-up showing stable periapical status

Working length was determined using an electronic apex locator (Root ZX; J. Morita Corp., Kyoto, Japan) and confirmed radiographically. Cleaning and shaping were performed using a rotary nickel–titanium file system (Orodeka Plex V, size 25/0.06 taper) with a crown-down technique. Irrigation with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (Prime Dental Products Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) was used during instrumentation. A final rinse with 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (RC Help; Prime Dental Products Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India), followed by saline irrigation, was performed. Calcium hydroxide intracanal medicament (RC Cal; Prime Dental Products Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) was placed, and the access cavity was temporarily sealed.

At the subsequent visit, the patient was asymptomatic. The medicament was removed, and the canal was dried with sterile paper points. Obturation was performed using single-cone gutta-percha (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) with a premixed bioceramic sealer (BioRoot RCS; Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France). A postoperative radiograph confirmed satisfactory obturation.

Histopathologic examination stained with haematoxylin and eosin showed fibrovascular connective tissue with chronic inflammatory cell infiltration and dilated blood vessels. Resorptive lacunae adjacent to dentinal fragments and multinucleated giant cells consistent with odontoclast-like cells were observed [Figure 2], confirming chronic pulpal inflammation associated with internal inflammatory resorption.

After obturation, approximately 2 mm of gutta-percha was removed from the canal orifice to create space for reinforcement. A self-etch adhesive system (Clearfil SE Bond; Kuraray, Japan) was applied and light-cured. Flowable composite (Estelite Flow Quick; Tokuyama Dental, Japan) was placed at the orifice. Polyethylene fiber (Ribbond; Ribbond Inc., USA) was adapted within the composite and light-cured.

A fiber-reinforced composite (everX Flow; GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was placed in the cervical region to reinforce the thinned dentin. The remaining coronal structure was restored with bulk-fill composite (Filtek Bulk Fill; 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA). Finishing and polishing restored the natural contour and aesthetics.

At 1-week follow-up, the patient reported no discomfort, and clinical examination showed satisfactory aesthetics and marginal adaptation. At 6th-month review, the tooth remained asymptomatic and functional. Radiographic evaluation showed maintenance of obturation with reduction in apical periodontal ligament widening and no progression of the resorptive defect [Figure 2]. The fiber-reinforced restoration remained intact, and periodic follow-up was advised.

DISCUSSION

Internal inflammatory resorption is initiated by clastic activity within chronically inflamed vital pulp tissue.[1] Trauma is considered a major etiologic factor because damage to predentin and the odontoblastic layer may expose mineralized dentin to odontoclasts.[2] In the present case, childhood trauma was considered the probable initiating factor.

Diagnosis of internal resorption may be difficult because lesions are often asymptomatic. Conventional radiographs may reveal characteristic enlargement of the pulp space, but their two-dimensional nature limits accurate assessment of lesion extent and perforation.[11] CBCT enables precise three-dimensional evaluation and facilitates differentiation from external cervical resorption.[3] In the present case, CBCT confirmed preservation of the external root surface and absence of perforation, enabling conservative nonsurgical management.

Histopathologic examination demonstrated chronic inflammatory infiltrate, multinucleated odontoclast-like cells, and resorptive lacunae adjacent to dentinal fragments, findings consistent with internal inflammatory resorption.[1] Histopathologic correlation is infrequently reported in clinical case reports but provides valuable biological confirmation of the diagnosis and strengthens the correlation between clinical and radiographic findings.

The primary objective of treatment is the elimination of inflamed pulp tissue to arrest clastic activity.[1] Chemomechanical debridement and intracanal medication are effective in halting the progression of the lesion. Similar conservative multidisciplinary management approaches for extensive internal inflammatory resorption have also been reported in the literature.[12] In the present case, obturation with bioceramic sealer provided satisfactory adaptation within the irregular canal anatomy.[13]

Extensive internal resorption significantly weakens dentinal walls, especially in the cervical region where stress concentration is highest.[4] Conventional management strategies include calcium silicate-based materials, thermoplasticized obturation techniques, fiber posts, and composite restorations.[5714] Although these approaches provide adequate sealing and clinical success, reinforcement of severely weakened cervical dentin may remain challenging.[14]

The present biomimetic approach utilized polyethylene fiber incorporated within fiber-reinforced composite to provide conservative internal reinforcement without aggressive post preparation. Polyethylene fibers act as a stress-distributing scaffold capable of limiting crack propagation within weakened dentin.[6] Fiber-reinforced composites have also demonstrated improved biomechanical behaviour and fracture resistance in structurally compromised teeth.[5]

The restorative strategy used in this case was consistent with principles of the Bio block or Bio-Bulk concept described by Fráter et al., in which short fiber-reinforced composite was used to reinforce roots affected by internal resorption.[8] However, unlike the Bio block technique where the canal space was extensively reinforced with fiber composite, the present case incorporated polyethylene fiber reinforcement mainly within the cervical region and canal orifice to preserve remaining dentin and enamel. Similar studies have demonstrated favourable stress distribution and reinforcement using fiber-reinforced restorative materials in weakened teeth.[910]

Although fiber-reinforced restorative strategies have been investigated in endodontically treated teeth, reports describing polyethylene fiber reinforcement combined with CBCT-guided diagnosis and histopathologic correlation in extensive internal inflammatory resorption remain limited. The present case is clinically relevant because treatment was directed not only toward elimination of the resorptive process but also toward biomechanical rehabilitation of the structurally compromised tooth. Unlike conventional restorative approaches focused primarily on obturation or defect repair, the present technique emphasized conservative internal reinforcement and preservation of weakened cervical dentin. The combined use of CBCT-guided diagnosis, histopathologic confirmation, and biomimetic polyethylene fiber reinforcement therefore highlights the novelty and multidisciplinary relevance of the present management approach.

Nevertheless, long-term clinical studies and larger case series are required to further evaluate the durability, fracture resistance, and long-term clinical performance of this conservative biomimetic reinforcement approach.

CONCLUSION

Internal inflammatory resorption may remain clinically silent despite progressive structural compromise. CBCT facilitates accurate diagnosis and differentiation from external resorptive defects, while histopathologic examination provides biological confirmation of the diagnosis. Conservative nonsurgical root canal therapy can arrest the resorptive process; however, reinforcement of weakened dentin is essential for long-term success.

This case demonstrates that a biomimetic approach using polyethylene fiber reinforcement within a fiber-reinforced composite restoration can provide conservative internal structural support while preserving remaining tooth structure. Combined use of CBCT assessment, histopathologic correlation, and fiber-reinforced biomimetic rehabilitation may represent a clinically useful strategy for management of structurally compromised teeth affected by internal inflammatory resorption.
