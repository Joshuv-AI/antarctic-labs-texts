Nanomaterial-induced mitochondrial biogenesis enhances intercellular mitochondrial transfer efficiency
Authors: John Soukar, Kanwar Abhay Singh, Ari Aviles, Sarah Hargett, Harman Kaur, Samantha Foster, Shounak Roy, Feng Zhao, Vishal M. Gohil, Irtisha Singh, Akhilesh K. Gaharwar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMitochondrial dysfunction plays a key role in many diseases, yet treatments to restore function remain limited. Cells naturally transfer mitochondria to help repair damage, but this process is inefficient. Here, we use molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) nanoflowers to boost mitochondrial production, turning donor cells into mitochondrial biofactories. These cells transfer more mitochondria to damaged cells, significantly improving energy production and function. In disease models, this approach restores cell health, offering a strategy for treating mitochondrial-related disorders. By enhancing the body’s own repair mechanisms, this nanomaterial-based method could pave the way for innovative therapies in regenerative medicine.

Mitochondria are essential for cellular health, providing the energy and housing essential metabolic pathways (1–3). Dysfunctional mitochondria can lead to cellular damage and apoptosis, contributing to a wide range of diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and various inborn errors of metabolism (4–6). Existing therapies for mitochondrial disease focus on symptom management, failing to address the underlying mitochondrial dysfunction (7, 8). Fewer than 25% of registered clinical trials for mitochondrial diseases involve new experimental drugs, with only 10 Phase III trials, and just one completed (9). While some experimental therapies show potential in alleviating symptoms, more work needs to be done to identify therapeutic molecules targeting mitochondrial function and formation (10).

Intercellular mitochondrial transfer has emerged as a fundamental biological process whereby cells exchange mitochondria to mitigate stress and promote tissue repair, an extension of mitochondrial movement and cellular communication (11). Occurring in a wide variety of cells, this innate mechanism has the potential to be co-opted to support local energy demands where existing mitochondrial networks struggle. Mitochondrial stressors can arise from environmental and genetic factors that cause defective oxidative phosphorylation (12). Intercellular mitochondrial transfer provides a comprehensive approach to alleviate mutant mtDNA load by altering the mtDNA content of recipient cells while simultaneously restoring respiration and survival through additional energy-generating machinery (13, 14). Transfer has been observed both in vitro and in vivo, under physiological and pathological conditions. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) display a particular propensity for initiating mitochondrial transfer to nearby cells; their mitochondria enhance cellular respiration, induce cell reprogramming, and repair metabolic function in recipient cells (15–20). Due to their lower energy demands, MSCs are favored for mitochondrial transfer to diseased cells with high bioenergetic needs (17). Their immune privilege, availability from various sources, and ease of use render MSCs ideal donor cells for delivering healthy mitochondria (21–25).

However, despite growing recognition of the therapeutic potential of mitochondrial transfer, its widespread adoption is hindered by limited rates of mitochondrial translocation. Existing methods to enhance transfer rates—such as overexpressing mechanistic proteins like the motor protein Miro1 and gap junction Cx43, or engineered techniques like MitoCeption and MitoPunch, are cumbersome and labor-intensive (19, 26–28). Consequently, despite advances in understanding intercellular mitochondrial transfer, current therapeutic strategies often fall short due to limited efficacy and challenges in delivery, underscoring the need for new approaches.

To address these limitations, we have developed a biomaterial-based therapeutic strategy employing molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) nanoflowers with atomic-scale modifications to transform human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) into mitochondrial biofactories. The increased mitochondrial content within MSCs enhances their capacity for intercellular mitochondrial transfer via TNTs. Utilizing nanomaterial platforms allows us to bypass limitations in transfer rates and eliminates the need for complex genetic interventions or extensive use of systemically administered drugs targeting mitochondrial function. This method capitalizes on the natural propensity of MSCs to transfer mitochondria, amplifying this capability through available mitochondrial mass. Our findings underscore the potential of nanomaterial-enhanced intercellular mitochondrial transfer as a viable therapeutic option for treating a wide range of mitochondrial dysfunctions.

Results and Discussion

To engineer hMSCs into mitochondrial biofactories, our primary objective is to boost mitochondrial biogenesis. Traditional approaches rely on small-molecule drugs to enhance this process; however, these compounds often have short half-lives and are rapidly cleared by cells (29, 30). Moreover, dosage requirements may lead to unintended side effects and potential toxicity. To overcome these challenges, we have developed a class of nanoparticles—molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) nanoflowers with atomic-scale vacancies. These vacancy-rich MoS2 nanoflowers uniquely increase mitochondrial biogenesis by activating PGC-1α and TFAM (31). The atomic-level vacancies enable the nanoflowers to scavenge intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn stimulates the expression of genes linked to mitochondrial biogenesis. Utilizing atomic vacancies to transform cells into efficient mitochondrial donors represents a promising and previously unexplored strategy.

Synthesis and Characterization of Size Tunable MoS2 Nanoflowers.

First, we synthesized MoS2 nanoflowers with atomic-scale vacancies in various sizes. The rationale for tuning nanoparticle size was to investigate the impact of surface area-to-volume ratio on cellular processes (32). Smaller nanoparticles are expected to exhibit enhanced cellular uptake due to their size-dependent internalization mechanisms and to achieve prolonged circulation times in vivo by evading clearance (32–34). Building on our previous work, we incorporated atomic vacancies within the MoS2 lattice (which has a 1:2 Mo:S ratio) by using a 1:6 molar ratio of molybdenum (Mo) and sulfur (S) precursors—hexaammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate and thiourea, respectively (31). We posit that varying the temperature and reaction time can modulate particle size, as growth and nucleation processes are strongly influenced by these parameters (34, 35). In this modified synthesis approach, we changed the temperatures from 120 to 200 °C for periods of 6 h (new shorter synthesis time) or 18 h (standard synthesis time), allowing us to obtain nanoflowers of different sizes from 50 to 250 nm as determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1A). At low temperatures and shorter reaction times (120 °C for 6 h), the reactions were incomplete, yielding solutions with poor solubility and altered color (yellow color); therefore, these samples were excluded from further analysis (Fig. 1A). Small MoS2 nanoflowers (~100 and ~130 nm) were obtained by hydrothermal synthesis at 140 and 160 °C for 6 h, while larger nanoflowers (~250 nm) were produced at 200 °C for 18 h. To determine the crystallographic arrangement of Mo and S atoms within the nanoflowers and assess the effect of atomic vacancies on their crystalline structure, we performed X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 1B). The diffraction peaks at (002), (100), and (110) confirm that the MoS2 nanoflowers possess a hexagonal crystal structure, consistent with standard hexagonal MoS2 (JCPDS card No. 73-1508) (Fig. 1B).

TEM was used to characterize the morphology of the nanoflowers, revealing that individual nanosheets stack together due to Ostwald ripening to form the characteristic concentric, hierarchical flower-like structures (Fig. 1C). This morphology was consistently observed in all syntheses performed at temperatures above 140 °C. By maintaining the synthesis time at 6 h, temperature variation above 140 °C resulted a change in nanoflower size without altering their morphology. In contrast, synthesis at 130 °C for 6 h resulted in altered morphology (spherical/oval with 50 to 80 nm in diameter). This change was further confirmed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), where MoS2 synthesized at 130 °C did not exhibit the crisp nanoflower morphology, unlike MoS2 synthesized at 140 °C (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). These findings indicate that temperatures below 140 °C and short hydrothermal durations (less than 6 h) are insufficient for proper formation of the characteristic nanoflower morphology; therefore, these formulations were omitted from further analysis. To simplify and reduce the number of nanoparticle formulations, we focused on 6-h syntheses at temperatures above 140 °C, providing the dual benefits of rapid reaction time and increased surface area-to-volume ratio. We used X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to determine the chemical states, and electronic states of the different size of MoS2 (Fig. 1C). The binding energies (BE) for molybdenum (Mo) and sulfur (S) core-level electrons indicate the presence of Mo4+ (3d5/2 ~ 228 eV and 3d3/2 ~231 eV), and S2− (2p3/2 ~161 eV and 2p1/2~ 162 eV) within MoS2 nanoflowers. The XPS data indicate a high degree of purity in the samples and confirm the synthesized nanoparticles chemically consisted of MoS2 and not a different molybdenum compound. Further deconvolution of the XPS spectra indicates the presence of predominantly 2H phase (hexagonal symmetry) within the trigonal prismatic MoS2 crystal (1 T phase) similar to our previous findings (31, 36). This indicates that change in synthesis methodology does not cause any appreciable changes in the morphology or crystal structure of the MoS2 nanoflowers.

To gauge whether MoS2 nanoflower size affects its physiological stability, we assessed the zeta potential of these nanoparticles in deionized water (DI) and cell culture media containing fetal bovine serum (FBS). All MoS2 nanoflowers exhibited a strong negative surface charge (–35 to –40 mV), however, in cell culture media, the zeta potential increased significantly, attributed to protein adsorption onto the nanoflower surfaces and charge shielding (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that nanoparticle size did not influence the types of proteins adsorbed, indicating that size variations do not affect the formation of the protein corona in biological media. To assess the presence of active sites within the layered structure of MoS2 nanoflowers, we measured their catalytic activity using hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in a biochemical catalase enzyme activity assay. The results indicated that both the smallest and largest nanoflowers synthesized with high atomic vacancies, exhibit catalytic activity regardless of size (Fig. 1D).

To assess the cytocompatibility of MoS2 nanoflowers, we exposed hMSCs to various concentrations of the nanoparticles and measured total DNA content after 24 h. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined by fitting a logarithmic dose–response curve. All MoS2 formulations were highly cytocompatible at concentrations below 100 μg/mL, with IC50 values ranging between 200 to 250 μg/mL regardless of nanoparticle size (Fig. 1E). Given the similar cytocompatibility across sizes, we focused on comparing the smaller 100 nm nanoflowers (hereafter referred to as Small MoS2) with our previously characterized 250 nm nanoflowers (Large MoS2). To evaluate their effect on the cell cycle, hMSCs were treated with 25 μg/mL of MoS2 nanoflowers for 72 h (Fig. 1F). Neither size caused significant alterations in cell cycle progression. Internalization of the MoS2 nanoflowers by hMSCs was confirmed using F-actin and nuclear fluorescence staining, with the nanoflowers visualized via reflective light (Fig. 1G).

To evaluate cellular uptake of nanoparticles, we utilized forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) profiles, which are unique for each cell and particle type. Upon nanoparticle internalization, SSC intensity increases without corresponding changes in FSC intensity. This approach allows evaluation of uptake by flow cytometry without the need for staining or labeling, which could alter nanoparticle size or surface chemistry (37–39). We used a high concentration of MoS2 nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) with a 4-h exposure to promote uptake while minimizing cytotoxicity and ensuring a detectable SSC signal. Flow cytometry confirmed that the majority of cells internalized MoS2, with 100 nm particles showing enhanced uptake as indicated by increased SSC intensity (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). This likely reflects more efficient uptake of smaller particles and/or greater particle numbers per cell. Endocytosis inhibitors Nystatin, Chlorpromazine, and Wortmannin inhibit caveolin-mediated, clathrin-mediated, and micropinocytosis respectively (40–43). Flow Cytometry revealed that both particle sizes rely on clathrin-mediated endocytosis, with macropinocytosis playing a more prominent role for 250 nm particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Collectively, these data demonstrate that we can effectively tune particle size to maintain cytocompatibility while modulating cellular uptake profiles.

Engineering Mitochondrial Biofactories Using MoS2 Nanoflowers.

To investigate the potential for MoS2 nanoflowers to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis, we focused on the activation of the PGC-1α signaling pathway—a central regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis. Mitochondrial biogenesis involves the coordinated replication of both nuclear-encoded and mitochondrial-encoded genes. This process is initiated by the transcriptional coactivator proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α). The activation of PGC-1α can occur via pathways involving sirtuins (SIRTs) or AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). Specifically, SIRT1 responds to cellular oxidative stress and can activate PGC-1α through deacetylation of its inactive form in the cytosol (Fig. 2A).

Given that MoS2 nanoflowers with atomic vacancies have been shown to scavenge ROS within cells, we hypothesized that they could activate the PGC-1α cascade by modulating cellular ROS levels and stimulating the SIRT1 signaling pathway (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, we posited that smaller MoS2 nanoflowers might enhance this effect due to their increased surface area and higher number of active sites. To test this hypothesis, we compared the effects of small (100 nm) and large (250 nm) vacancy-rich MoS2 nanoflowers on mitochondrial biogenesis in human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs). First, we assessed total cellular ROS levels and found that treatment with MoS2 nanoflowers led to a significant decrease compared to untreated cells, with both sizes of MoS2 having a similar effect on ROS (Fig. 2B). Mitochondrial-specific ROS levels were reduced, as indicated by the mitochondrial superoxide indicator MitoSOX (Fig. 2C). Importantly, neither size of MoS2 nanoflowers adversely affected mitochondrial membrane potential, suggesting that mitochondrial health was maintained (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

We then evaluated the expression of PGC-1α and observed an increase following exposure to MoS2 nanoflowers of both sizes. This was corroborated by elevated levels of voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC), a conserved mitochondrial porin indicative of increased mitochondrial mass (Fig. 2D). To quantify this effect, we measured mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number in hMSCs using qPCR analysis. Results revealed a twofold increase in relative mtDNA levels in cells treated with high atomic vacancy MoS2 after 7 d, regardless of nanoparticle size (Fig. 2E). This increase was also replicated in other cell types, such as smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and neonatal human cardiac fibroblasts - ventricular (NHCF-Vs), indicating this effect is not cell type specific (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A).

To confirm whether the observed effects were mediated through the SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway, we utilized PGC-1α knockdown cells reported in our previous study (31). These cells did not show a significant increase in mtDNA copy number following exposure to vacancy-rich MoS2, unlike cells transfected with an empty pLKO vector (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). This finding demonstrates that the MoS2 nanoflowers’ effect mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC-1α. Additionally, to establish MoS2 dependence on SIRT1, we utilized salisistat (Ex-527), an established sirtuin inhibitor known to target SIRT1 (44, 45). Inhibition of SIRT1 through administration of salisistat in culture media significantly reduced the effect of MoS2 on the expression of PGC-1α after 72 h in hMSCs. Further, Small MoS2 treated hMSCs are shown to have increased SIRT1, this effect is reduced in the presence of Salisistat (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The dependence of the SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway was confirmed in C2C12s which showed that salisistat prevented the effect of MoS2 on mtDNA copy number after 72 h of treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D).

Interestingly, we observed that the smaller MoS2 nanoflowers increased mtDNA copy number at lower concentrations compared to the larger ones (Fig. 2F). Suggesting that increased surface area and particle number of the smaller nanoflowers may enhance their effectiveness in stimulating mitochondrial biogenesis. For comparative purposes, we examined other known activators of mitochondrial biogenesis, such as resveratrol and 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR). Resveratrol activates PGC-1α via SIRT1 by reducing oxidative stress, while AICAR stimulates PGC-1α through activation of AMPK (46–49). However, single-dose treatments with resveratrol or AICAR did not produce as robust an increase in mitochondrial biogenesis at 3 and 7 d compared to MoS2 nanoflowers (Fig. 2G).

To assess the functional impact of increased mitochondrial mass, we measured total ATP production in hMSCs treated with MoS2 nanoflowers. Both sizes resulted in enhanced ATP production compared to untreated cells (Fig. 2H). Additionally, OCR measurements using the Seahorse XF24 assay in SMCs showed that MoS2 treatment increased both spare and maximal respiratory capacities (Fig. 2I). Notably, MoS2 nanoflowers did not affect the glycolytic capacity, indicating that the observed increase in cellular bioenergetics is attributable solely to enhanced mitochondrial function (Fig. 2J and SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

In summary, these findings demonstrate that MoS2 nanoflowers with atomic vacancies activate the PGC-1α pathway by modulating cellular ROS levels and stimulating the SIRT1 signaling pathway. This activation leads to increased mitochondrial biogenesis and enhanced cellular bioenergetics, regardless of nanoparticle size. The smaller nanoflowers exhibit increased effectiveness at lower concentrations, likely due to higher surface area-to-volume ratio. The smaller particles also have the benefit of a shorter reaction time, simplifying the synthesis process. These results underscore the potential of both small and large MoS2 nanoflowers in engineering cells as mitochondrial biofactories for therapeutic applications.

MoS2 Nanoflowers Boost Intercellular Mitochondrial Transfer.

Given the ability of hMSCs to transfer mitochondria to other cells, we hypothesized that increasing the intracellular mitochondrial mass in hMSCs would enhance intercellular mitochondrial transfer to recipient cells. We achieve this increase by treating hMSCs with MoS2 nanoflowers, which stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis—as evidenced by a two-fold increase in mtDNA copy number. This elevated mitochondrial content in the donor hMSCs was expected to boost the transfer of mitochondria to other cells. We established this capacity by treating donor hMSCs with small MoS2 nanoflowers (S _ Mo-hMSCs). The donor cells were cocultured with recipient cells at a 3:1 ratio (recipient cells: donor hMSCs) for 24 h. Live-cell microscopy reveals the rapid translocation of mitochondria from hMSCs treated with small MoS2 over the first 16 h (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and Movies S1 and S2). Here, mitochondria can be seen being moved into recipient cells rapidly. Cells reach out with intercellular structures to send mitochondria.

Further confocal microscopy reveals that donor and recipient cells formed cell–cell connections, and active mitochondrial transfer was evident by the presence of stained mitochondria in the unstained recipient cells (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). To validate mitochondrial transfer efficacy across different cell types, we selected various recipients: mouse myoblast cell line (C2C12), NHCF-V, rat cardiomyoblast cell line (H9C2) and human umbilical artery SMC. After coculturing recipient cells with small MoS2 treated-hMSCs, we quantified mitochondrial transfer efficiency using flow cytometry. We measured the percentage of recipient cells labeled with CellTracker Green that also became MitoTracker Red-positive, indicating successful acquisition of mitochondria from donor hMSCs. The results showed that MoS2-treated hMSCs exhibited approximately a two-fold increase in mitochondrial transfer to C2C12 and NHCF-V cells, and a three- to fourfold increase to H9C2 and SMC (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Notably, small MoS2-treated hMSCs demonstrated higher transfer rates in some cell types (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). Interestingly, resveratrol treated hMSCs resulted in lower increase in mitochondrial transfer compared to small MoS2 nanoflowers in C2C12 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). We confirmed that hMSCs were the source of transferred mitochondria by observing a proportional decrease in mitochondrial copy number in hMSCs posttransfer using fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) followed by quantitative PCR on negatively selected, unstained donor cells, eliminating the possibility of mitochondrial dye transfer artifacts (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). This is further evidenced in TFAM knockdown cells, TFAM regulates mitochondrial DNA transcription and its loss results in a decrease in mtDNA copy number in cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B) (50). C2C12s show a significant increase in human mtDNA presence following mitochondrial transfer, showing the ability of mitochondrial transfer to replenish and enhance mtDNA levels in recipient cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C).

In our study, we investigated the mechanisms of intercellular mitochondrial transfer from hMSCs to recipient cells and explored how MoS2 nanoflowers enhance this process. hMSCs have a unique ability to donate mitochondria to various recipient cells, offering a promising solution for reversing mitochondrial dysfunction (51). Intercellular mitochondrial transfer can occur through tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), extracellular vesicles (EVs), or gap junction channels (GJCs) (37, 52, 53). When hMSCs and recipient C2C12 cells were physically separated in Transwell plates with 0.4 μm pore membranes, mitochondrial transfer was nearly nonexistent (Fig. 3D), indicating that cell contact is required. To delineate the specific transfer mechanism, we focused on TNTs, which form through membrane deformation facilitated by actin polymerization. We selectively inhibited TNT formation by adding cytochalasin B (350 nM), an actin polymerization inhibitor, during coculture (16, 38). This treatment dramatically reduced mitochondrial transfer between hMSCs and C2C12 cells in both untreated and MoS2 treated populations (Fig. 3E), with similar reductions observed in additional cell types, confirming that physical cell–cell contact is essential. (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

Further, Miro proteins Miro1 and Miro2, encoded by Rhot1/2 respectively, are Rho-GTPases located on the mitochondrial outer membrane and play a crucial role in mitochondrial transport (Fig. 3F). Acting as adaptor proteins, Miros facilitate the coupling of mitochondria to microtubule motor proteins. Specifically, they bind to the kinesin motor protein KIF5 to form a motor–adaptor complex that regulates mitochondrial movement (25, 39). The well-established role of Miro1 in mitochondrial transfer led us to evaluate its expression levels to gain insight into how MoS2 nanoflowers affect hMSC behavior (38). Interestingly, western blot analysis showed no significant difference in Rhot1 protein expression between untreated hMSCs and small MoS2-treated hMSCs (S _ Mo-hMSCs) isolated from the coculture system by FACS (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). These findings suggest that MoS2 enhances the mitochondrial transfer rate by increasing mitochondrial mass rather than directly influencing the transfer machinery. Additionally, MoS2 nanoparticles within S _ Mo-hMSCs were not transferred to recipient cells, as confirmed by the absence of MoS2 signals in the recipients (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B). Collectively, MoS2 nanoflowers uniquely augment the mitochondrial mass of hMSCs, promoting more efficient transfer through TNTs.

Confocal microscopy and additional SEM confirmed the formation and presence of TNTs in cocultures of smooth muscle cells and hMSCs, mediating the transfer of mitochondria between cells (Fig. 3G). Once inside the recipient cell, transferred mitochondria can have multiple fates: They may act immediately to supplement or replace existing mitochondria, or they may fuse with the recipient’s mitochondrial network. To unequivocally demonstrate mitochondrial uptake by recipient cells, we measured mitochondrial copy number using quantitative PCR. We observed an increased mitochondrial DNA copy number in cells cocultured with MoS2-treated hMSCs, and a decrease in mtDNA in donor hMSCs corroborating observed increased transfer rates (Fig. 3 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). Furthermore, JC-1 dye assays confirmed that these transferred mitochondria remained healthy within the recipient cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B), indicating that the transferred mitochondria maintain their integrity throughout the process and within the recipient.

These results highlight MoS2 nanoflower enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis in hMSCs. By increasing the mitochondrial mass, MoS2-treated hMSCs can more effectively donate mitochondria to recipient cells via TNTs. This mechanism offers a promising strategy for improving mitochondrial transfer efficiency, potentially advancing therapeutic approaches for conditions associated with mitochondrial dysfunction.

Transcriptomic Impact of Intracellular Mitochondrial Transfer on Recipient Cells.

Our results demonstrate that small MoS2-treated hMSCs exhibit enhanced mitochondrial transfer, termed “MitoFactory-transfer”, driven by elevated mitochondrial mass in donor cells, resulting in a significantly higher transfer rate to recipient cells compared to the standard “Mito-transfer” observed in untreated hMSCs when cocultured (Fig. 4A). To further explore this phenomenon, we investigated the impact of mitochondrial transfer on the gene expression profiles of the recipient SMCs (by transcriptome sequencing, RNA-seq). Given the increased transfer efficiency observed with small MoS2-treated hMSCs and the functional similarity between hMSCs and SMCs, we sought to uncover how enhanced mitochondrial transfer might influence recipient cells beyond simply increasing mitochondrial numbers.

Smooth muscle cells, such as human umbilical arterial smooth muscle cells, are vital for vascular contraction, a process that requires a high mitochondrial capacity and respiration efficiency (54–58). To investigate the effect of elevated mitochondrial transfer (MitoFactory-transfer) on recipient SMCs, we sequenced the transcriptome of SMCs after coculturing them with hMSCs—with and without small MoS2 treatment—for 24 h, by positively selecting for prestained recipient cells in FACS. Cells were separated using fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS), with recipient cells prestained uniquely prior to coculture and collected for sequencing. Principal component analysis (PCA) of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed substantial differences in transcriptome profiles between the two groups (Fig. 4B). PCA of the top 50% most variable expressed genes (assessed by median absolute deviation) showed a pronounced distinction between HUASMCs cocultured with MoS2-treated hMSCs and those cocultured with untreated hMSCs. This separation indicates significant transcriptomic changes potentially induced by enhanced mitochondrial transfer.

We identified DEGs between the Mito-transfer and MitoFactory-transfer conditions. Coculture of HUASMCs with untreated hMSCs (Mito-transfer) significantly affected the expression of 2,617 genes—1,256 upregulated and 1,361 downregulated. In contrast, SMCs cocultured with MoS2-treated hMSCs (MitoFactory-transfer) showed significant changes in the expression of 3,625 genes (1,863 upregulated and 1,762 downregulated) (P-adj <0.05, Fig. 4 C and D).

To elucidate the biological processes affected by mitochondrial transfer, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on the DEGs. Notably, when comparing MitoFactory-transfer to Mito-transfer, we observed that GO terms related to biological processes involved in cellular metabolism and respiration were uniquely enriched in the MitoFactory-transfer group (Fig. 4E). For both Mito-transfer and MitoFactory-transfer, enriched GO terms are associated with cell–matrix and cell migration, followed by terms associated with smooth muscle cell morphogenesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

Additionally, we analyzed the affected cellular components (CC) and molecular functions (MF). CC terms reveal the locations of proteins encoded by the RNA within the cell, while MF terms describe their molecular activities. Compared to Mito-transfer alone, MitoFactory-transfer showed enrichment of CC and MF terms associated with mitochondrial localization and OXPHOS processes (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). As the new mitochondria within the cell use up valuable resources and biomolecules, retrograde signaling can signal to the nucleus to help support these new mitochondria and their energy generating machinery (56, 59).

In summary, our results demonstrate that enhanced mitochondrial transfer from MoS2-treated hMSCs not only increases mitochondrial content in recipient cells but also induces significant transcriptomic changes favoring metabolic and bioenergetic pathways. By promoting genes associated with energy production and mitochondrial function, MoS2 nanoflowers may enhance the regenerative capabilities of hMSCs in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine applications.

MoS2-Augmented Mitochondrial Transfer Effects on Cellular Mitochondrial Dynamics.

Our analysis demonstrated that MitoFactory-transfer indicates a promotion of cellular bioenergetics due to the increased mitochondrial content within the recipient cells (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). To delve deeper into this finding, we employed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on smooth muscle cells (SMCs) post MitoFactory-transfer. The GSEA revealed a significant upregulation (normalized enrichment score [NES] > 1) of processes related to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and mitochondrial dynamics, which aligns well with our GO enrichment analysis results. We performed a selective inspection of DEGs using the MitoPathways dataset of MitoCarta3.0, a curated repository of 146 mitochondrial pathways (60). SMCs following MitoFactory-transfer showed consistent enrichment of mitochondrial pathways associated with mitochondrial metabolism compared to standard mitochondrial transfer (Fig. 5B). Among OXPHOS related terms, the gene sets Protein Sorting and Homeostasis (NES= 1.75, P-adj<0.01), Mitochondrial Central Dogma (NES= 1.89, P-adj <0.01), and Oxphos Assembly Factors (NES= 1.76, P-adj<0.01) displayed a positive correlation (Fig. 5C). These findings highlight mitochondrial energy metabolism as key processes affected by the coculture with MoS2-treated hMSCs.

Smooth muscle cells are integral components of contractile tissues and are highly responsive to mitochondrial energetics and ROS levels. Mitochondria are critical for SMC function; their contraction depends on energy from ATP, which is mainly generated through OXPHOS within the mitochondria (56–58). While we observe increased mitochondrial copy number in cells, it is evident from our RNA-seq that transfer has no effect on the transcription of mt-DNA encoded genes (Fig. 5D). However, the majority of mitochondria-associated genes are encoded by the nuclear genome, and an overwhelming majority of these genes are strongly upregulated in response to MitoFactory-transfer, indicating increased mitochondrial transfer induces SMCs to support the received mitochondria. Traditionally, mitochondrial and nuclear genomes produce OXPHOS complexes in a coordinated fashion, synchronized to cellular energy state and growth through transcription rates, turnover, and translation rates (61, 62). Cells maintain fine-tuning mechanisms, tight coexpression at the subunit level as well as subunit turnover. Under transfer mediated by MitoFactory hMSCs, high amounts of these complexes are present in recipient cells, potentially signaling to the nucleus an abundance of mitochondrial gene expression. On short timescales, complex subunits encoded by the mitochondrial and nuclear genomes are often disordinate but at longer time scales correspond precisely (63). It is likely that, in response to elevated rates of intercellular transfer of mitochondria, recipient cells must begin coordinating OXPHOS biogenesis to the perceived imbalance of mitochondrial mass and nuclear encoded mitochondrial genes. Here, subunit turnover within the plethora of new mitochondria could demand a nuclear response.

Our data show the upregulation of mitochondrial respiratory chain assembly factors, encoded by the nucleus. The components of the OXPHOS chain are multiprotein complexes, a plethora of assembly factors are involved in the process that respond to energetic, molecular, and cytosolic cues (64, 65). As the cell begins accepting the transplanted mitochondria into their existing network, OXPHOS proteins/subcomplexes are integrated with the existing cellular components. These assembly factors act to stabilize intermediates, insert cofactors, or ensure integrity (64). Further, the mitochondrial respiratory components have been found to gather into so-called supercomplexes. Assembly factors from each of the 4 respiratory complexes and supercomplex formation are found to be upregulated after mitochondrial transfer, pointing to the adoption of the new mitochondria with existing machinery (Fig. 5E).

To determine how these newly integrated mitochondria from transfer impacts cellular energy production, we measured the OCR of SMCs that received mitochondria from MoS2-treated hMSCs. These SMCs exhibited significantly higher basal respiration levels, owing to the excess mitochondria present within the cells (Fig. 5F). Additionally, we assessed total cellular ATP levels from cell lysates across multiple cell types. The results indicated that mitochondrial transfer elevates total ATP concentrations following coculture, with MitoFactory-transfer showing significantly higher increases in intracellular ATP compared to standard transfer (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that enhanced mitochondrial transfer not only increases mitochondrial content but also promotes a highly energetic phenotype in recipient cells. By boosting oxidative phosphorylation and ATP generation, MitoFactory-transfer has the potential to improve cellular function, especially in energy-demanding tissues like smooth muscle.

MoS2-Mediated Mitochondrial Transfer Replenishes Mitochondria in Damaged Cells.

Our transcriptomic analyses and observations of increased respiration in SMCs cocultured with MoS2 treated hMSCs demonstrate that the transferred mitochondria are adopted, supported, and active within the recipient cells. To assess whether the transferred mitochondria can repair damage to cellular respiration, we induced mitochondrial dysfunction in recipient cells using antimycin A, carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP), and doxorubicin, then evaluated markers of cellular health and function (Fig. 6A) (66). Mitochondria produce approximately 95% of a cell’s ATP; when their function is impaired, cellular metabolism cannot proceed normally, leading to chronic energy deficiencies that threaten cell survival and may induce cell death. Akin to replacing depleted batteries, the intercellular transfer of healthy mitochondria supplements or restores ATP production, supporting the survival of cells with impaired energy metabolism.

After uptake by recipient cells, the internalized mitochondria must effectively integrate with the endogenous mitochondrial network to maintain homeostasis and energy production. Dysfunction in this network can lead to various downstream effects; therefore, integration is essential to demonstrate the functionality and therapeutic potential of the transferred organelles. Key proteins involved in mitochondrial fusion include Mitofusin 1 (MFN1) and Mitofusin 2 (MFN2), which are responsible for outer membrane fusion, and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1), which is responsible for inner membrane fusion 1 (67). MFNs also facilitate the tethering of mitochondria to each other and to other organelles—a necessary first step in mitochondrial fusion (68, 69). Integration can be evaluated by measuring the mRNA expression levels of MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1.

MFN1 and MFN2 were significantly upregulated in SMCs, while MFN1 alone in NHCF-Vs, under physiological coculture conditions after 24 h (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S16A). Notably, OPA1 was significantly upregulated only in the MitoFactory-transfer group, suggesting that MFNs facilitate the initial tethering of mitochondria within recipient cells, with the fusion process becoming prominent when excess mitochondria are present (Fig. 6B). Our results show that mitochondrial dynamics within recipient cells display a gradual decrease in mitochondrial copy number over 48 h posttransfer, indicating that the transferred mitochondria are quickly integrated into existing networks, likely through fusion. Recipient SMCs in the MitoFactory-transfer group retain elevated mitochondrial levels posttransfer due to higher copy numbers (SI Appendix, Fig. S16B).

Interestingly, when recipient cells were under metabolic stress induced by antimycin A, these key mitochondrial fusion genes were further upregulated (Fig. 6B). This supports the hypothesis that newly transferred mitochondria aid in repairing mitochondrial bioenergetics through fusion. Similarly, quantitative PCR revealed nonsignificant increases in Rhot1 (Miro1) expression under physiological conditions in both MoS2-treated and control hMSCs within the coculture system. However, Rhot1 expression increased when recipient cells were injured with Antimycin A, indicating an enhanced role in mitochondrial translocation and reestablishing functional connections during stress (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). These findings align with current studies suggesting that mitochondrial transfer is dependent on Miro1 and preferentially increased when recipient cell mitochondria are damaged (28, 53, 70).

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of mitochondrial transfer from MitoFactory hMSCs, we induced mitochondrial damage using various agents. Antimycin A inhibits electron transport at complex III, leading to elevated superoxide levels, while CCCP uncouples the proton gradient across the mitochondrial membrane, causing depolarization. Posttreatment with these inhibitors, we observed significant improvements in mitochondrial health following MitoFactory-transfer. Mitochondrial transfer from MoS2-treated hMSCs not only boosted total ATP production but also fully restored cellular ATP levels under mitochondrial damage induced by antimycin A (Fig. 6C). Measurements of mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 dye showed improvements to an extent, and assessments of mitochondrial-specific superoxide levels demonstrated significant recovery to basal levels in the MitoFactory-transfer group (Fig. 6 D and E). Impaired mitochondrial function reduces the efficiency of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, resulting in dysregulated ATP metabolism and increased production of ROS. These results suggest that enhanced mitochondrial transfer can address multiple forms of dysfunction, enable the restoration of mitochondrial functions and assist in the rebalancing of redox homeostasis (51). Enhanced transfer results in increased viable mitochondria within recipient cells, allowing them to restore their integrity.

Recognizing the significant efficiency of mitochondrial transfer from MoS2-treated hMSCs, we explored its therapeutic potential in the context of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity. Doxorubicin (DOX), a widely used chemotherapeutic agent, is known to cause cumulative cardiomyopathy through interference with mitochondrial processes (66, 71, 72). DOX induces oxidative stress and epigenetic alterations that damage mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), affecting nontarget tissues like the heart. Treatments such as ROS scavenging and ion chelation show limited efficacy and do not address all aspects of the pathology. Cardiac fibroblasts, comprising a significant portion of the myocardium, are crucial for generating extracellular matrix and supporting heart structure and function. Impaired mitochondrial function in these cells disrupts cellular metabolism, induces metabolic reprogramming, increases ROS production, and enhances autophagy and mitophagy responses to remove damaged components. The mitochondrial transfer capability of MitoFactory hMSCs may offer an opportunity to restore mitochondrial functions and maintain cellular health. To test this, we evaluated mitochondrial function and cellular apoptosis in NHCF-V cells treated with 1 μM DOX for 24 h—a clinically relevant model of cardiotoxicity. DOX treatment resulted in a significant increase in total cellular ROS and a marked decrease in cellular proliferation (SI Appendix, Fig. S18).

Compared with standard transfer, MitoFactory hMSCs achieved significantly higher cell survival in DOX-treated NHCF-V cells, exhibiting greater efficiency in reducing cellular apoptosis and caspase activation through mitochondrial replenishment (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, in the restoration of redox homeostasis and mitochondrial function afforded by transfer, MitoFactory-transfer nearly fully restored injured cells in terms of mitochondrial-specific superoxide levels, showing no statistical differences compared to the healthy control group (Fig. 6G). Additionally, MitoFactory-transfer replenished intracellular ATP concentrations following DOX treatment more effectively than standard transfer (Fig. 6H). These results suggest that mitochondrial transfer from MoS2-treated hMSCs can restore health and function in cardiac fibroblasts after DOX-induced damage, efficiently reversing mitochondrial damage.

Mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway is a well-described mechanism of DOX-induced cell death. Damage to mitochondria and activation of this pathway in cardiac cells following DOX-induced oxidative stress have been reported, particularly involving the release of mitochondrial apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) (66, 71, 72). Our observations indicate that MitoFactory-transfer not only restores mitochondrial function but also significantly reduces levels of AIF in recipient cells compared to hMSCs alone (Fig. 6I). This suggests that enhanced mitochondrial transfer can effectively mitigate mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis, offering a promising therapeutic strategy to protect cardiac cells from chemotherapy-induced damage.

MitoFactory hMSCs, enhanced by MoS2 nanoflowers, significantly improve the efficiency of mitochondrial transfer to recipient cells. The transferred mitochondria integrate into the recipient mitochondrial network, aiding in the restoration of cellular bioenergetics, especially under conditions of mitochondrial dysfunction. This enhanced transfer shows potential therapeutic benefits in addressing mitochondrial-related diseases and mitigating the cardiotoxic effects of agents like Doxorubicin. These data provide a mechanism that could plausibly target mitochondrial disease, indicating mitochondrial transfer’s remedial effects in damaged tissues.

Nanomaterials have demonstrated significant potential for biomedical and therapeutic applications. Direct administration of nanoparticles has been employed to address a range of cellular diseases and pathologies in both in vitro and in vivo settings, with some formulations advancing to clinical trials (73, 74). A key advantage of nanoparticles is their capacity for functionalization with targeting moieties, enabling tissue, organ, and even organelle-level precision. When appropriately functionalized, MoS2 nanoflowers can be engineered to localize within specific tissues or even mitochondria through conjugated targeting molecules (75–77). This versatility, combined with their demonstrated therapeutic effects, positions MoS2 nanoflowers as promising candidates for in vivo therapeutic applications. In the context of intercellular mitochondrial transfer, we demonstrate that MoS2 nanoflowers represent a promising tool for preconditioning donor cells in vitro. While this natural process offers substantial potential to rescue diseased cells, its efficacy remains limited in many conditions. Well-characterized in mesenchymal stem cells, intercellular mitochondrial transfer provides a platform for exploring therapeutic applications. Prior studies have shown that augmenting mitochondrial transfer and transplantation, typically involving in vitro donor cell preconditioning followed by systemic or localized delivery, can enhance outcomes in vivo (19, 78–81). Thus, MoS2 nanoflowers may serve a dual role in regenerative medicine: as a targeted nanotherapeutic for delivery and retention in tissues to promote mitochondrial function and as a preconditioning agent to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis in donor cells. Future in vivo studies are needed to evaluate biodistribution, retention, safety, and the functional integration of donor-derived mitochondria within host tissues in the presence of MoS2.

Conclusions.

Intercellular mitochondrial transfer is a natural process with substantial therapeutic potential to rescue diseased cells, yet its efficacy remains limited in many conditions. In this study, we present a bioengineered strategy to enhance mitochondrial transfer by increasing mitochondrial mass in donor cells. We engineered vacancy-rich MoS2 nanoflowers that stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis, effectively turning hMSCs into mitochondria biofactories. Our findings demonstrate that MoS2 nanoflowers significantly enhance mitochondrial transfer, increasing mitochondrial content in recipient cells. These transferred mitochondria integrate into existing networks and contribute to the recipient cells’ energy-generating capacity. In the present study, therapeutic evaluations targeting mitochondrial damage demonstrated that enhanced mitochondrial transfer from MoS2 treated hMSCs to injured cells effectively mitigates cellular injury. While in vivo validation of our system is ongoing, further studies are needed to elucidate additional therapeutic benefits of hMSCs, including paracrine signaling, immune modulation, ECM remodeling, and the transfer of other biomolecules or cellular components via TNTs (11, 82–85). Our study specifically focuses on and rigorously monitors mitochondrial transfer, clearly establishing its role in cellular repair. Existing approaches for restoring mitochondrial health and function often lack efficacy and are limited in scope, typically addressing only symptom management. In contrast, our MoS2 nanoflower-based strategy offers a broadly applicable platform for pathologies requiring mitochondrial repair and replacement. By enhancing mitochondrial transfer and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis, this nanomaterial approach holds promise as a therapeutic tool for managing mitochondrial dysfunction.

Materials and Methods

See SI Appendix for detailed materials and methods. Detailed methods for nanoparticle characterization, in vitro studies, and RNA-seq can be found in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Generation of Mitochondrial Biofactories and In Vitro Analysis.

Vacancy rich Molybdenum Disulfide (MoS2) nanoflowers were synthesized via hydrothermal synthesis which was adapted and modified for the current study (31, 36). Briefly, a 1:6 molar ratio of molecular precursors of molybdenum and sulfur (Hexaammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate and Thiourea respectively) were added to 15 mL of water. This solution was then transferred to a hydrothermal device and placed at 120 to 200 °C for 6 to 18 h. Human mesenchymal stem cells (ATCC) were treated at 25 µg/mL MoS2 for 7 d to generate MitoFactories. For mitochondrial transfer studies, recipient and donor cells were seeded in a 3:1 ratio, respectively, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Fluorescence Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) was utilized to separate donor and recipient populations for further western, PCR, and transcriptomic processing.
