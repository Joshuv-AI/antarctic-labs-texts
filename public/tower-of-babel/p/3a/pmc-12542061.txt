Fungal dynamics and lignocellulose depolymerization in Pinus koraiensis: impact of longhorn beetle infestation
Authors: Yue Zhu, Sixun Ge, Lili Ren, Youqing Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Xylophagous insects, like Monochamus saltuarius significantly affect tree-stem-associated microbial communities and pose major threats to forest ecosystems. As a vector for the pinewood nematode (Bursaphelenchus xylophilus) and a wood-boring pest, M. saltuarius infests various Pinus species. Yet, the interactions between M. saltuarius-associated fungi and the endophytic fungi of its host tree, Pinus koraiensis, remain uncharacterized. In this study, high-throughput sequencing was used to characterize fungal communities within infested and uninfested host trees. Endophytic fungi with lignocellulose-depolymerizing potential were identified, and their enzymatic activity was experimentally assessed. A significant reduction in fungal diversity was detected in infested samples, indicating that M. saltuarius infestation disrupts the native fungal community, favoring plant pathogens and diminishing the abundance of lignocellulosic depolymerizing fungi. To explore ecological shifts and diagnostic taxa, an integrated biomarker discovery framework combining Random Forest and LEfSe analysis was applied. This model revealed fungal biomarkers enriched in uninfested tissues and potentially involved in lignocellulose degradation based on functional annotation and in vitro assays. These taxa may serve as indicators of infestation status and provide insight into host-microbe-insect interactions. These findings contribute to understanding how xylophagous insect infestations restructure fungal communities and offer a model-based approach for detecting ecological signatures of pest impact in forest ecosystems.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04269-w.

Introduction

Xylophagous insects, particularly longhorn beetles such as Monochamus saltuarius Gebler (Coleoptera: Cerambycidae), play pivotal roles in forest ecosystems as both pests and modulators of microbial communities. Distributed throughout Northeast Asia [1–3], M. saltuarius is not only a xylophagous pest of coniferous forests but also an significant vector for pine wood nematodes (Bursaphelenchus xylophilus) [4]. Among its known hosts plant, Pinus koraiensis (Korean pine) is a dominant conifer in Northeastern China [5–7], where xylophagous pest infestations often coincide with shifts in microbial community structure [8, 9].

While prior research predominantly explored M. saltuarius gut bacterial communities and their roles in host tree’s adaptation [10, 11], interactions between M. saltuarius-associated fungi and host endophytes remain unclear. In related Monochamus-pinewood nematode systems, such vector- and host-associated microbiota have been shown to modulate disease progression and insect fitness [12–15]. Microhabitat characteristics, host resistance levels, and disturbance regimes significantly shape the microbiomes of both beetles and host trees. Yet, whether M. saltuarius-associated fungi engage in symbiosis, competition, or niche displacement within host tissues remains largely uncharacterized.

Insects respond to host tree defense with a variety of different physiological or ecological interactions, including physiological adaptations (e.g., acidic saliva production) and symbiotic associations with microbes, particularly fungi [8, 9, 16]. These fungal symbionts degrade plant polymers for nutrients, enhancing insect survival in ligneous environments. Certain beetles express endogenous cellulases obtained through horizontal gene transfer [17], augmenting their plant tissue digestion capacity.

Plant endophytes serve as multifunctional allies for bolstering herbivore resistance, mitigating environmental stresses, and modulating host susceptibility to pathogens [18, 19]. Insect-associated fungi, depending on their ecological roles, may subvert plant defenses or compete with endogenous endophytes, potentially altering plant–microbe dynamics [20, 21]. For example, longhorn beetles such as M. alternatus harbor symbiotic fungi like Sporothrix spp., which may contribute to successful colonization through modulation of immune responses and potential interactions with host defenses [22]. However, it remains unknown whether M. saltuarius similarly engages in symbiotic recruitment or competitive interactions with lignocellulolytic fungi.

This study tests the hypothesis that M. saltuarius infestation drives structural and functional shifts in the fungal communities of P. koraiensis. By integrating high-throughput sequencing, fungal isolation, enzymatic activity assays, and machine learning-based biomarker selection, we characterized infestation-induced differences in fungal diversity/composition, identified biomarker taxa diagnostic for infestation, and evaluated lignocellulolytic capabilities of isolated indicator fungi. Our findings elucidate tripartite insect-fungus-host dynamics and advance the development of fungal biosignatures for early detection of xylophagous insect infestation.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Samples were collected in 2020 and 2021 from the Cangshi Forest Farm (41°53’N, 124°23’E; 15.003 km2) in Fushun City, Liaoning Province, China. All sampling procedures complied with national and local regulations, and no protected species were involved. A total of 7 trees were sampled as biological replicates, including 4 M. saltuarius-infested trees (I-20–1, I-20–2 in 2020; I-21–1, I-21–2 in 2021) and 3 uninfested control trees (U-20–1 in 2020; U-21–1, U-21–2 in 2021). To maintain spatial independence, sampled trees were spaced at least 50 m apart. From each tree, 1 m-long logs were collected and processed under sterile conditions. 6 microhabitat types were sampled: infested phloem (IP), infested xylem (IX), larval frass (ILF), larval galleries (ILG), uninfested phloem (UP), and uninfested xylem (UX). Phloem-feeding period larval frass and xylem-feeding period larval gallery samples were collected separately [23]. For each tissue type per tree, four replicates were taken, yielding a total of 88 samples (stored at − 80 °C until use). Of these, 37 were used for high-throughput sequencing, and the remaining for fungal isolation.

Total DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and high-throughput sequencing

A total of 37 samples were selected for high-throughput sequencing; sample types and tree sources are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Total genomic DNA was extracted from approximately 500 mg of homogenized plant or frass tissue per sample using the FastDNA™ Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA; Cat. No. 6560–200), following the manufacturer's instructions. Cell lysis was achieved by 40 s of bead beating using Garnet Lysing Matrix E in a FastPrep®−24 instrument, and DNA was purified using silica-based spin columns. The entire extraction process was completed within 30 min per sample. DNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentrations were quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Only DNA with appropriate purity (A260/280 = 1.8–2.0) was used for downstream analyses.

The ITS2 region was amplified using the widely adopted primer pair ITS3F and ITS4R [24]. PCR reactions (20 µL each) were performed in triplicate per sample, containing 10 µL of 2 × Pro Taq Master Mix (Accurate Biology), 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), and 10 ng of template DNA. Thermocycling conditions included: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 ℃ for 30 s, 55 ℃ for 30 s, 72 ℃ for 45 s, and a final extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min.

PCR products were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, USA). Purified products were quantified with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and equimolarly pooled. Library preparation was performed using the NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, USA), including adapter ligation, magnetic bead-based size selection to remove adapter dimers, and PCR enrichment. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 300 bp) was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (China).

High-throughput sequencing data analyses

Given the sensitivity of DADA2 in distinguishing amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), potential over-splitting due to intraspecific ITS sequence variation was considered [25]. Sequencing data were processed using the QIIME2 v2021.2 pipeline. Raw reads were demultiplexed, trimmed, and denoised with DADA2 (https://qiime2.org), yielding ASVs. Taxonomic annotation of ASVs was performed using the Naive Bayes classifier (classify-sklearn) with a confidence threshold of 0.7, trained on two fungal ITS reference databases: UNITE v8.0 and the more recent UNITE v10.0 (fungi_only/ITS). Unless otherwise indicated, analyses presented in the main manuscript were based on UNITE v10.0 to enhance the genus/species level resolution of annotations. Results from UNITE v8.0 are included in the Supplementary Materials for cross-database comparison (UNITE8_Supplementary_Compiled).

The sequence data analysis was mainly performed using QIIME2 (v2022.2), Python packages (v3.13), and the Majorbio BioCloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com/). Alpha diversity was assessed using Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices. Differences between infested (I) and uninfested (U) groups were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test, while comparisons across six microhabitats were performed using Kruskal–Wallis H tests followed by Dunn’s post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction. Beta diversity patterns were visualized using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) to represent compositional differences based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities, without assuming phylogenetic distance. Statistical differences in community composition were assessed by PERMANOVA.

Functional annotation of fungal ASVs was performed using the FUNGuild tool (https://www.funguild.org/) to infer ecological guilds based on taxonomic assignments [26]. Functional assignments (e.g., saprotroph, pathotroph, symbiotroph) were determined at the genus level using QIIME2-based taxonomy output. Only guild assignments with “probable” or “highly probable” confidence scores were retained for downstream analysis.

For each sample, the relative abundance of each trophic mode was calculated based on the summed abundance of all matching ASVs. Functional group differences were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis H tests (for multi-group comparisons) and Mann–Whitney U tests (for pairwise U vs. I contrasts), p-values were reported alongside visualization panels, and key ecological patterns (e.g., enrichment of saprotrophs in infested galleries) were summarized (Supplementary Table 4).

Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under Project number PRJNA1229443.

Fungal biomarker selection model for infested and uninfested host tree samples

Fungal biomarkers differentiating infested and uninfested samples were identified using both Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) [27] and random forest (RF) classification [28]. Model performance was evaluated using ROC curves and AUC metrics. LEfSe was applied at the ASV level to compare infested vs. uninfested samples. The analysis was conducted using the "all-against-all" strategy for multi-class comparison. Relative abundance data were normalized, and unclassified taxa were retained. Differential ASVs were selected using a threshold of LDA > 2 and p < 0.05. We then selected the top 20 LDA score ASVs for more stringent biomarker identification.

A random forest model was implemented in R-3.3.1 (randomForest package), with 500 trees and an AUC-based performance evaluation. Input features were log10-transformed relative abundances of ASVs, with missing or zero values imputed with a small constants prior to transformation. Feature importance was assessed using Mean Decrease Accuracy (MDA). Model performance was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values reported.

To evaluate biomarker precision, we compared four ASV sets (based on ROC): (1) RF_Top4_ASV (selected by random forest), (2) LEfSe_Top20_ASV (top 20 ASVs by LDA score), (3) Wood_Saprotroph_ASV (based on FUNGuild functional annotation), (4) All_ASV (baseline, full ASV set). For each set, ASV-level abundance data were log10-transformed and then averaged across ASVs per sample to generate a single feature. Models were evaluated using tenfold cross-validation, and AUC values were reported for each group to assess diagnostic performance in distinguishing infestation states. The ASV sets (1) and (2) were the biomarker sets analyzed, and the ASV sets (3) and (4) were used as benchmarks and reference controls.

Isolation and identification of fungal strains

Fungal strains were isolated from both infested and uninfested P. koraiensis sample tissues via surface sterilization (5% sodium hypochlorite) and cultivation on PDA medium [29, 30].

Fungal DNA was extracted, and the ITS region was amplified using ITS1 and ITS4 primers [31]. Fungal samples were homogenized, added to the lysate, and heated at 85℃ for 15 min. PCR amplification was conducted in a 25 µL reaction volume with 1 µL of DNA template, 9.5 µL of sterile water, 12.5 µL of r-Taq enzyme, and 1 µL each of primers ITS1 and ITS4. PCR conditions included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 10 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified products were sequenced by Beijing Ribo Biological Company (Beijing, China), and preliminary taxonomic identification was conducted by BLAST searches against the GenBank database [32].

A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree based on ITS sequences was constructed using MEGA 5.0 software [33], incorporating all isolates and 77 reference sequences. Although the ITS region has limited phylogenetic resolution, it is sufficient for genus- and species-level classification in fungi [34, 35].

Screening of lignocellulose-degrading fungal isolates by selective media

We screened all isolated strains on 4 types of selective media to evaluate their ligninolytic, cellulolytic, and hemicellulolytic potential. Each selective medium contained specific substrates and chromogenic indicators.

Ligninolytic potential was screened using 2 selective media: aniline blue medium and guaiacol medium. Ligninolytic activity was indicated by the appearance of clear halos around colonies on aniline blue medium or brown halos on guaiacol medium (Aniline Blue medium: dissolving 10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, 10 g of NaCl, 22 g of agar, and 0.4 g of aniline blue in 1 L of water; guaiacol media: 10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, 10 g of NaCl, 22 g of agar, and 0.4% guaiacol in 1 L of water).

The cellulose selection medium (CMC-Na medium) was prepared by dissolving 10 g of CMC-Na, 2.64 g of (NH4)2SO4, 2.4 g of KH2PO4, 5.7 g of K2HPO4, 1 g of MgSO4·7H2O, 1 g of NaCl, and 13–15 g of agar in water, adjusting the pH to 7.2 [36].

The hemicellulose selection medium (xylan medium) was prepared similarly, using 10 g of xylan, 2.64 g of (NH4)2SO4, 2.4 g of KH2PO4, 5.7 g of K2HPO4, 1 g of MgSO4·7H2O, 1 g of NaCl, 13–15 g of agar, and 1 g of casein, with the pH adjusted to 7.2.

For the Congo red staining method [37], colonies from CMC-Na medium (cellulose) and xylan medium (hemicellulose) were washed with 70% ethanol, stained with 0.1% Congo Red for 30 min, and decolorized with 1 M NaCl for 20–30 min. The presence of transparent hydrolysis zones around the colonies indicated enzyme activity.

Determination of lignocellulose-depolymerizing enzyme activity

We measured 9 lignocellulose-degrading enzyme activities in pre-selected fungal strains with degradation potential. Using spectrophotometric assays [38–42], we quantified 3 cellulases (α-glucosidase, β-glucosidase, exo-β−1,4-glucosidase), 3 hemicellulases (acid/alkaline/neutral xylanases) and 3 ligninases (laccase (Lac), manganese peroxidase (MnP), lignin peroxidase (LiP)). Activity was assessed at 8, 12, 16, 32, and 37 h [43–45] (validation curves and formal statistics are included in Supplementary Table 8).

Significant high-enzyme-activity isolates were identified based on the S-N-K post hoc test. To link these strains to corresponding ASVs, we used a combined taxonomic and phylogenetic matching analysis. For the candidate ASV pool, all ASVs taxonomically annotated (via UNITE v10.0) to the same genus or family as each high-enzyme–activity isolate were collected. 9 neighbor-joining trees of all functional isolates and their candidate ASVs were constructed using MEGA-12 (Supplementary Fig. 1, Kimura 2-parameter model, 1,000 bootstrap replicates). ASVs that clustered with high-enzyme-activity strains both by possessing≥ 97% pairwise identity over ≥ 250 bp (determined by BLASTn) and by phylogenetic proximity were designated as related ASVs (R-ASVs). These R-ASVs were subsequently used to track the distribution of high lignocellulolytic lineages across environmental samples.

Results

Fungal community dynamics in an insect-host tree system

We explored the variation in fungal communities associated with the insect-host tree infestation system. A total of 1,867,222 sequences were generated, revealing 1,276 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) spanning 8 phyla, 34 classes, 93 orders, 201 families, and 325 genera, with 447 species (UNITE v10.0, Supplementary Table 1).

Overlap analysis at the genus level revealed that fungal communities were partially shared but compartmentalized (Fig. 1B). The Venn diagram showed a conserved core of 109 genera shared between uninfested and infested groups, with 199 genera unique to uninfested samples (U) and 17 genera unique to infested samples (I) (Fig. 1B). Core genera are visualized in the intersection; infested samples harbor unique genera potentially enriched by larval activity. The UpSet plot further highlighted that certain microhabitats, especially frass (ILF) and larval galleries (ILG), harbored distinct fungal assemblages not found in surrounding xylem or phloem tissues (Fig. 1B). This habitat-specific exclusivity suggests that larval activity introduces microenvironmental filtering that selectively enriches particular taxa.

β-diversity patterns visualized by PCoA showed clear group-specific segregation (Fig. 1C, genus level). Infestation status explained 22.6% of the variation (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.226, p = 0.001), while environmental sample type accounted for a larger proportion (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.456, p = 0.001), indicating that tissue origin (e.g., phloem vs. xylem, larval frass) plays a more dominant role than infestation alone. Larvae-associated samples (ILF and ILG) exhibited minimal within-group variability and formed tight clusters, contrasting with the broader spread of uninfested tissues (UP and UX).

To identify infestation-responsive taxa, we conducted Kruskal–Wallis tests comparing infested (I) and uninfested (U) groups. The top 10 genera with significant abundance differences were visualized (Fig. 2B). These genera included Hyphoderma, Nakazawaea, Lipomyces, Phaeoacremonium, Graphilbum, unclassified_k__Fungi, Vexillomyces, Apiotrichum, Hyphopichia, and Sporothrix. Apiotrichum and Hyphopichia showed significant enrichment in infested samples (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.001, Supplementary Table 3), suggesting ecological linkages to beetle-mediated wood degradation or saproxylic niches. Conversely, Lipomyces exhibited wider distribution yet maintained significantly higher abundance in infested groups (p = 0.0007). While Phaeoacremonium abundance varied significantly among sample types (p = 0.0179), no consistent variation was observed between infestation statuses (p = 0.6961), indicating context-dependent ecological functions.

To contextualize taxonomic differences in community structure, we performed hierarchical clustering of the top 50 genera (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 3). The heatmap revealed pronounced group-specific clustering, with frass (ILF) and gallery (ILG) samples forming distinct clades separate from xylem and phloem samples. Several genera were selectively enriched in these insect-derived niches, including Sporothrix, Apiotrichum, and Hyphoderma, further suggesting their ecological linkage to beetle activity and wood degradation processes.

We further explored distributional heterogeneity across all 37 samples by mapping the relative abundance of the top 10 genera (Fig. 2B). Significant variability emerged within sample groups, particularly among uninfested xylem (UX) and infested xylem (IX) samples, which displayed distinct microbial community profiles. This suggests fungal colonization depends not only on infestation status but also on microenvironmental factors including host tissue type and insect larval activity.

These results demonstrate significant M. saltuarius-induced restructuring of fungal communities and identify specific genera as ecological indicators of beetle-associated microbial dynamics.

Trophic mode redistribution demonstrated fundamental changes across microhabitats, with a marked dominance of saprotrophs in insect-infested habitats. Saprotrophic fungi increased significantly by 13.2% in relative abundance under infestation (Infested (I): 38.6 ± 8.9% vs. Uninfested (U): 34.1 ± 5.2%; p = 0.0004). This shift was most pronounced in larval frass (ILF: 41.3 ± 3.5%) and galleries (ILG: 32.6 ± 5.1%), where saprotrophs constituted > 40% of functional ASVs in ILF and > 30% in ILG (p < 0.002 for ILF vs. uninfested phloem (UP)).

Mutualistic symbiotrophs fungi exhibited a significant reduction (44.2%) in infested samples (I: 2.9 ± 2.1% vs. U: 5.2 ± 1.8%; p = 0.003). The most pronounced decline occurred in infested xylem (IX: 2.9 ± 2.1% vs. UX: 5.4 ± 0.8%; p = 0.0008). Concurrently, tri-functional fungi (Pathotroph-Saprotroph-Symbiotroph) decreased by 52.8% (I: 5.1 ± 4.7% vs. U: 10.8 ± 4.3%; p = 0.010), with the sharpest depletion observed in infested phloem (IP: 3.9 ± 1.2% vs. UP: 10.6 ± 2.4%; 63.2% reduction).

Pathotrophic fungi demonstrated a significant increase (47.3%) in relative abundance under infestation (I: 13.7 ± 5.4% vs. U: 9.3 ± 3.1%; p < 0.001). This elevation was particularly pronounced in infested xylem (IX: 14.7 ± 2.9% vs. UX: 9.1 ± 2.3%; p = 0.01) and insect galleries (ILG: 32.6 ± 5.1% vs. uninfested xylem and phloem: ~10.4 ± 1.8%). In contrast, frass (ILF) showed consistently low pathotroph representation (≤ 5%) and was dominated by saprotrophic fungi.

Functional microhabitat partitioning significantly differed across sample groups (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.05), with Mann–Whitney U tests confirming pairwise differences (Fig. 3B, C). Tissue types exhibited characteristic functional profiles. Uninfested xylem (UX) preserved symbiotrophs (5.4 ± 0.8%) and tri-functional guilds (7.8 ± 1.2%). Frass (ILF) became saprotroph-dominated microsites (41.3 ± 3.5%). Galleries (ILG) specialized in pathotroph-saprotroph complexes (32.6 ± 5.1%). Infested xylem (IX) developed pathotroph dominance (14.7 ± 2.9%).

The abundance of saprotroph was significantly lower in uninfested samples (U) than in infested samples (I) (Mann–Whitney, p < 0.005), and was especially highest in infested xylem (IX) (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001). Multi-functional guild was significantly more abundant in uninfested phloem (UP) than in infested phloem (IP) (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001).

ROC curves generated by tenfold cross-validation (Fig. 5D) showed that the RF_Top4_ASV set achieved the highest classification (AUC = 0.993), followed by the wood_saprotroph_ASV (based on functional prediction) and the full ASV sets(as a baseline). The LEfSe-based ASV sets scored the lowest classification accuracy (AUC = 0.49).

The top 10 ASVs ranked by mean decrease in accuracy (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 6) exhibited clear enrichment patterns across microhabitats and infestation states. For instance, Trichomerium (ASV148), Apiotrichum (ASV8), and Cladosporium (ASV7) showed highest relative abundance in uninfested phloem (UP) and uninfested xylem (UX), whereas Hyphoderma (ASV1) and Graphilbum (ASV4) were most abundant in larval galleries and frass (ILG, ILF) of infested trees. These ASVs overlapped with LEfSe-detected biomarkers and corresponded to high-enzyme-activity strains or their phylogenetic clades. Their distinct distribution across the six sample types reinforces their potential as habitat- and condition-specific indicator taxa.

Discussion

Our findings reveal that M. saltuarius infestation in P. koraiensis is associated with significant shifts in fungal community structure and function across different wood compartments. These changes show a clear increase in fungal diversity and variability, especially in larval galleries and frass, indicating that insect activity causes localized disturbance and recruits fungi in specific niches. Similar compartment-specific fungal shifts have been observed in bark beetle-spruce systems, where beetle infestation significantly changes the fungal communities in wood, litter, and soil [46]. These consistent patterns suggest that xylophagous insects can reshape the fungal microbiome of conifer hosts in a structured way by disturbing their environment. This spatial heterogeneity, which has also been documented in other insect-tree interactions such as those between pine wood nematodes and different Pinus species [14], likely reflects a compartmentalized influence of the insect on fungal communities.

After beetle infestation, pathotrophic and saprotrophic fungi were increased in infested tissues, especially within frass and xylem. This suggests a shift in the fungal community from endophytic or mutualistic types to groups that promote wood decay and nutrient turnover under beetle-mediated stress. Such a transition likely helps the beetles by accelerating wood decay. Similar findings by previous research has shown that pinewood nematode infection drives deterministic changes in gallery microbiomes, enriching fungi such as Ophiostomataceae that may aid beetle development through lignocellulose degradation [12].

Interestingly, most R-ASVs (ASVs related to high-enzyme-activity strains), identified through combined enzymatic and taxonomic evidence, were more abundant in uninfested samples. This pattern suggests that beetle infestation may suppress lignocellulose-degrading fungal taxa, potentially weakening the host tree’s functional microbiome prior to colonization. Previous studies have proposed that insect-associated microbes assist beetle invasion by compromising plant defense barriers [47]. Our findings raise complementary questions about whether endogenous wood-decaying endophytic fungi contribute to host tree’s resilience in the absence of insect pressure—a question worth further investigation.

Combined Random Forest and LEfSe models were used to find fungi that reliably indicate infestation status. For example, Graphilbum and Hyphoderma thrived in larval galleries(ILG) and frass(ILF), while Nakazawaea was enriched in uninfested phloem. These patterns suggest habitat-specific fungal filtering influenced by insect behavior and wood substrate composition. Despite their functional importance, lignocellulose-degrading taxa were not selected as infestation biomarkers in this combined model, likely due to beetle-induced suppression.

Methodologically, while ASV-level delineation enhances sensitivity to community shifts, the inherent limitations of ITS2—including short length and high variability— can restrict reliable taxonomic assignment below the genus level [48–50]. Functional predictions via FUNGuild inherit this uncertainty, particularly when genus assignments are ambiguous. Recently developed tools such as FungalTraits [51] and FunFun [52] improve functional annotation by integrating phylogenetic structure with curated ecological traits, providing useful directions for future fungal guild prediction. Nevertheless, our R-ASV approach (linking sequences to cultured strains with validated enzymatic functions) mitigates these constraints by prioritizing ecological relevance over taxonomic precision. These caveats necessitate caution in interpreting specific biomarker identities, but crucially, they do not invalidate the community-level patterns revealed here. Future work may integrate long-read ITS sequencing (PacBio) with metatranscriptomics to resolve intra-genus functional heterogeneity as Creedy et al. did for fungal metabarcoding studies [53].

Although the presence of pinewood nematode was not directly assessed, its possible influence is acknowledged. Given that sampling occurred in historically infested areas, separating beetle-driven from nematode-mediated effects remains challenging. This highlights the need for nematode-exclusion experimental designs in future research.

In addition, larval developmental stages were not systematically categorized. However, based on gallery morphology and existing staging data for M. saltuarius [23], frass correspond to phloem-feeding-phases larvae and larval galleries correspond to xylem-feeding-phases larvae. While our primary comparisons centered on infestation status, future studies should incorporate instar-specific profiling to explore fungal recruitment across life stages. These ecological patterns are consistent with broader theories on insect-fungus interactions, where diet breadth and host specificity are influenced by plant defenses and microbial symbioses [54–56].

Finally, the absence of spatial metadata precluded direct testing for spatial autocorrelation. While the two-year sampling design helped mitigate temporal confounding, spatial effects on fungal distribution cannot be ruled out. Future studies should incorporate geospatial data and apply structure-aware models (e.g., Mantel’s r) to further validate spatial independence in biomarker analysis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that M. saltuarius infestation restructures fungal communities in P. koraiensis, disrupting nutrient cycling via suppression of lignocellulose-degrading taxa in healthy tissues while enriching pathogenic taxa in frass/galleries. These shifts suggest that infestation impairs host-tree-associated microbial functions critical for wood decomposition and defense. Habitat-specific biomarkers identified by integrated machine learning/statistical modeling reflect insect-mediated niche shifts, with uninfested-enriched decomposers potentially supporting host tree resilience prior to colonization. Future instar-level profiling under nematode exclusion, combined with spatiotemporal metadata integration, will clarify causal chains and enhance diagnostic applications.
