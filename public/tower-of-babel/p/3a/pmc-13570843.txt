Pre–existing Comorbidities as Potential Risk Modifiers for New-Onset Myocarditis and Pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 Vaccination in Males Aged 18–30 in the United States: A Disproportionality Analysis using VAERS Spontaneous Reporting Data
Authors: Mohammed Salah Salem, Yola Moride, Bernard Bégaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRare cases of myocarditis and pericarditis following mRNA coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) vaccination have been reported, primarily among males under 30 years of age. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity have been hypothesized as potential contributors. Using the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS), this study investigated whether these comorbidities were disproportionately reported among young adult males with myocarditis or pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination compared with vaccinated and general populations. Additionally, this study represents a novel application of disproportionality principles in spontaneous reporting databases to explore, beyond signal detection, the factors that might be associated with the occurrence of an adverse event.MethodsAn observed-versus-expected analysis was conducted using reports of myocarditis or pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination among males aged 18–30 recorded in VAERS between 2021 and 2022. The prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity among reported cases was compared with prevalence estimates derived from epidemiological studies of vaccinated populations and national population statistics. In addition, disproportionality analyses were performed using comparator groups consisting of reports associated with other vaccines and other adverse events following immunization (AEFIs) within VAERS.ResultsA total of 859 eligible reports of myocarditis/pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination were identified among males aged 18–30. The prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity was 0.35%, 0.58%, 0.81%, and 1.75%, respectively and was significantly lower than those reported in vaccinated and prepandemic general populations. In disproportionality analyses, myocarditis/pericarditis reports following mRNA vaccination showed higher prevalence of dyslipidemia and obesity, but lower prevalence of hypertension, compared with selected VAERS comparator groups. However, absolute comorbidity prevalence remained low across all groups.ConclusionsNo evidence of a modifying role for hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or obesity was observed for the increased reporting of myocarditis/pericarditis among young adult males following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination. Despite the limitations inherent to spontaneous reporting systems, this study highlights a novel application of disproportionality-based approaches within VAERS to explore potential risk modifiers of vaccine-related adverse events in a hypothesis-generating manner.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40801-026-00568-4.

Key Points

The increased reporting of myocarditis/pericarditis among males aged 18–30 following mRNA vaccination does not appear to be explained by a higher prevalence of pre-existing hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or obesity.This study highlights a novel application of disproportionality methods within a spontaneous reporting database to explore potential risk modifiers of vaccine-related adverse events in a hypothesis-generating manner.

Introduction

The mRNA coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) vaccines played a pivotal role in reducing COVID-19-related hospitalizations and mortality, but rare cases of myocarditis and pericarditis following vaccination have been reported, particularly among males under 30 years of age [1, 2]. Myocarditis and pericarditis are inflammatory conditions affecting the heart muscle and pericardium, respectively, that are often idiopathic or secondary to processes such as a viral infections and can, in rare instances, lead to heart failure or death [3, 4].

Evidence from pharmacovigilance systems, cohort studies, and systematic reviews supports an association between mRNA COVID-19 vaccines and myocarditis/pericarditis, especially in young males [5–9]. Although case reports of potential myocarditis/pericarditis following the AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine have been described, observational studies conducted across several countries, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses, have provided the strongest evidence for a causal association between the mRNA COVID-19 vaccines and myocarditis/pericarditis [8–11].

The mechanisms underlying this increased susceptibility remain incompletely understood. Those that have been proposed include inflammatory and immune-mediated pathways, molecular mimicry, cross-reactivity, elevated free spike proteins, and genetic predisposition [12–15]. Despite growing evidence regarding vaccine-associated myocarditis/pericarditis, limited research has evaluated whether underlying medical conditions may modify this risk [16, 17].

Using an observed-versus-expected (O/E) analysis within the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS), the present study investigated whether chronic comorbidities with cardiovascular burden (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity) were more prevalent among males aged 18–30 years with reports of myocarditis or pericarditis suspected to be associated with mRNA COVID-19 vaccines than in vaccinated or prepandemic general populations. In addition, a disproportionality analysis was conducted using reports associated with other vaccines and other adverse events following immunization (AEFIs).

While O/E and disproportionality analyses are widely used in pharmacovigilance for signal detection and safety monitoring of therapeutic products, including vaccines [18], they have also been applied to explore demographic factors such as race and ethnicity [19–21]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply a disproportionality-based approach within a spontaneous reporting database to investigate potential risk modifiers of vaccine-related adverse events through evaluation of pre-existing comorbidities.

By design, this study was hypothesis-generating and aimed to assess whether hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity may act as potential risk-modifying factors for myocarditis or pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination in young adult males.

Methods

Study Design

A case-coverage study incorporating observed-versus-expected (O/E) and disproportionality analyses was conducted to investigate potential associations between pre-existing comorbidities and reports of myocarditis or pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination. The target population consisted of males aged 18–30 years who received at least one dose of an mRNA COVID-19 vaccine (Pfizer-BioNTech or Moderna).

The observed prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity among VAERS reports of myocarditis or pericarditis in males aged 18–30 years following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination was compared with expected comorbidity prevalence estimates derived from epidemiologic studies of vaccinated populations and from prepandemic national population statistics for similarly aged males. In addition, a disproportionality analyses within VAERS compared the prevalence of these comorbidities among myocarditis/pericarditis reports with their prevalence among reports involving other adverse events and vaccines in males aged 18–30 years.

Data Sources

The Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) is a national surveillance system in the USA jointly managed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to monitor AEFIs [22]. VAERS functions as an early warning system for the detection of potential vaccine safety concerns by collecting reports submitted by healthcare professionals, vaccine manufacturers, and the public [7, 23].

For this study, VAERS data from 2021–2022 were used to identify reports of myocarditis and pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination in males aged 18–30 years. The years 2021–2022 were selected because they corresponded to the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, during which vaccine rollouts were underway and many myocarditis reports were being reported. The VAERS datasets were available in three relational files, which were merged using the unique VAERS identification number assigned to each report.

Outcome Definition and Measurement

VAERS reports of myocarditis or pericarditis suspected by the reporter to be associated with mRNA COVID-19 vaccination in males aged 18–30 years were included in the study. Detailed clinical adjudication of all reports was not feasible within the scope of the study. For example, three myocarditis reports documented a prior history of COVID-19 infection, which may have represented an alternative cause of myocarditis/pericarditis rather than vaccination. Nevertheless, these cases were retained because they had been reported as suspected adverse events following COVID-19 vaccination.

Individuals under 18 or above 30 years of age, as well as reports with unknown age or sex, were excluded. Additionally, patients with a documented prior history of myocarditis or pericarditis were excluded. These eligibility criteria were applied to the observed cases and the internal VAERS disproportionality analysis only, as equivalent restrictions could not be consistently applied to the expected prevalence estimates derived from vaccinated and general population literature sources.

VAERS Search Strategy

Reports of myocarditis and pericarditis were identified using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred terms recorded in the VAERSSYMPTOMS dataset. Reports containing the terms “myocarditis,” “pericarditis,” or “myopericarditis” in any of the five symptoms fields were included. VAERS reports were coded using the MedDRA version in effect during the 2021–2022 study period (versions 24.0, 24.1, 25.0, and 25.1).

Because VAERS is a spontaneous reporting system, some reports contained incomplete or inconsistent vaccine information. Reports with unknown vaccine type were excluded from the study.

Exposure Definition and Measurement

The potential risk-modifying factors evaluated were hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity. To ensure consistency in definitions, prehypertension and borderline hypertension were not classified as hypertension. Both type 1 and type 2 diabetes were included because both are associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher, while overweight status (BMI 25–29.9) was excluded. Dyslipidemia was used as an umbrella term encompassing hyperlipidemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and hypercholesterolemia because of the limited number of VAERS reports explicitly specifying individual lipid disorders. This terminology was also consistent with the epidemiologic studies used to derive expected prevalence estimates [24, 25].

Comorbidity information was extracted from the “HISTORY,” “CUR_ILL,” and “SYMPTOM_TEXT” variables within VAERS using a keyword-based search algorithm in SAS OnDemand. A broader keyword search algorithm was initially used, followed by a comparison with a more specific algorithm in subsequent analyses. Both approaches identified the same number of comorbidities in the primary study population, suggesting that the more specific algorithm was adequate for the analysis.

Most comorbidity data were initially identified in the “HISTORY” variable; however, additional review demonstrated inconsistencies in reporting across fields, prompting expansion of the search strategy to include “CUR_ILL” and “SYMPTOM_TEXT”. The “CUR_ILL” variable captures current illnesses, whereas “HISTORY” primarily reflects chronic conditions, with potential overlap between the two. The “SYMPTOM_TEXT” variable contains the narrative description of each report.

Because hypertension may occur as a clinical manifestation during myocarditis or pericarditis events, distinguishing pre-existing hypertension from incident hypertension within narrative text was challenging. Consequently, reports identified through the search algorithm as containing hypertension underwent manual review, and only those clearly documenting a prior history of hypertension were included in the analysis.

Literature Review for Expected Prevalence Estimates

To estimate the prevalence of the selected comorbidities in vaccinated and general populations of males aged 18–30 years, a targeted literature review was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar. Because VAERS data covered 2021-2022, the literature search was limited to studies published between 2016 and 2022.

Search terms included combinations of “COVID-19 vaccination”, “hypertension,” “diabetes,” “obesity,” “dyslipidemia,” “hyperlipidemia,” and “cholesterol.” US studies reporting prevalence estimates stratified by age and sex were prioritized. Because approximately 70% of the US population had completed the primary COVID-19 vaccination series by May 2023 [26], using data from the remaining 30% as the general population comparator could therefore introduce selection bias, as this group may not accurately represent the overall population due to differences in behaviors, lifestyle choices, and demographic characteristics. Hence, pre-2021 national estimates of comorbidities prevalence were sought for general population comparisons.

Data Analysis

For each comorbidity, the expected number of cases was calculated by applying prevalence estimates identified from the literature and national population statistics to the total number of myocarditis/pericarditis reports included in the study. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare observed and expected frequencies because several expected cell counts were < 5. The O/E ratios and corresponding two-sided exact Poisson 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were also calculated assuming fixed expected values using OpenEpi’s standardized mortality ratio calculator [27]. When multiple expected prevalence estimates were available, the lowest and highest estimates identified from the literature and national statistics were used to generate a range of expected values.

Additional Disproportionality Analysis Within VAERS

Initial review of VAERS reports demonstrated low overall reporting of comorbidities. To assess whether this underreporting was specific to myocarditis/pericarditis reports or to VAERS more broadly, additional disproportionality analyses were conducted within the database. The underlying premise was that if under-ascertainment of comorbidities occurred similarly across comparator groups within VAERS, disproportionality analysis could still provide useful hypothesis-generating information regarding potential risk-modifying factors.

Comorbidity prevalence in the primary study population (males aged 18–30 years with myocarditis/pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination) was compared with the following VAERS comparison groups: (1) males aged 18–30 years who received mRNA COVID-19 vaccines during the same time period (2021–2022) and had adverse event reports other than myocarditis/pericarditis; (2) males aged 18–30 years with adverse event reports following non-COVID-19 vaccination unrelated to myocarditis/pericarditis; and (3) males aged 18–30 years with Bell’s palsy reports following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination. Guillain-Barré syndrome and multiple sclerosis were considered as additional comparator outcomes but were excluded due to the extremely low number of reports.

Expected Comorbidity Rates

The targeted literature review identified seven studies reporting the prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and/or obesity in either US populations vaccinated against COVID-19 during 2021–2022 [24, 28–30] or the general US population prior to the COVID-19 pandemic [25, 31, 32]. None of the identified studies provided prevalence estimates specifically stratified for males aged 18–30 years, which was the target population for the present study, limiting direct comparability between the observed and expected groups.

Among vaccinated populations, reported prevalence estimates varied considerably across studies, partly because of differences in study populations and age distributions. In a study evaluating mRNA COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness among US veterans aged 18 and older (89.5% males), the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia was 37.93%, 25.5%, and 19.13%, respectively [24]. In another study conducted among Kaiser Permanente Southern California members aged 12 years and older vaccinated with the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine, hypertension and diabetes prevalence were 21.39%, and 12.07%, respectively [30]. A study using healthcare data from Columbia University Irving Medical Center linked with state and city immunization registries reported hypertension prevalence of 16% for both Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna recipients aged 10 years and older, diabetes prevalence ranging from 6.6% to 7.1%, and obesity prevalence ranging from 4.0% to 5.1% [28]. In a cohort of healthcare workers from Southern California receiving the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine (30.4% males; age range 20–87 years), hypertension prevalence was 15.2% and obesity prevalence was 12.2% [29].

Prepandemic estimates from the general US population were also identified. Based on 2017–2020 National Center for Health Statistics data, diagnosed diabetes among 20–39 year-old males and females was estimated at 4.4%, while obesity prevalence was 39.9% [31]. Hypertension prevalence among males aged 18–39 years was estimated at 31.2% using 2017–2018 national survey data [32]. In an analysis of 2013–2016 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data, dyslipidemia prevalence among males aged 20–79 years (mean age: 47.5 years) was estimated at 29.5% [25].

Results of the O/E analyses, including the highest and lowest expected estimates, are summarized in Tables 3 and 4. Overall, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity among VAERS reports of myocarditis/pericarditis was lower than the prevalence estimates identified in both vaccinated and prepandemic general populations. Compared with non-myocarditis/pericarditis adverse event reports following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination, myocarditis/pericarditis reports had significantly higher rates of dyslipidemia (0.81% versus 0.33%, p = 0.029) and obesity (1.75% versus 0.85%, p = 0.013), but lower prevalence of hypertension (0.35% versus 1.30% p = 0.012). Compared with adverse event reports following non-COVID-19 vaccination unrelated to myocarditis/pericarditis, myocarditis/pericarditis reports had higher obesity prevalence (1.75% versus 0.67%, p = 0.032) and lower hypertension prevalence (0.35% versus 1.43%, p = 0.02). Finally, compared with Bell’s palsy reports following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination, myocarditis/pericarditis reports had lower prevalence of hypertension (0.35% versus 5.6%, p = 0.0001).

The initial plan also included comparison of the primary study population—males aged 18–30 with myocarditis/pericarditis reports following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination—with two additional groups: males aged 18–30 with myocarditis/pericarditis reports following non-COVID-19 vaccination and males aged 18–30 with Bell’s palsy reports following non-COVID 19 vaccination. However, both comparator groups contained fewer than 10 reports, precluding meaningful analysis.

Discussion

In this O/E analysis, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity among VAERS reports of myocarditis/pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination in males aged 18–30 years was consistently lower than expected based on estimates from vaccinated and prepandemic general populations. These findings do not support the existence of an interaction between COVID-19 mRNA vaccination and these pre-existing cardiovascular comorbidities in relation to the increased reporting of myocarditis and pericarditis observed in young adult males.

Although these comorbidities are established contributors to cardiovascular burden, their prevalence was not elevated among reported myocarditis/pericarditis cases in this demographic group. One possible explanation is the generally lower burden of chronic disease in younger adults. However, prevalence estimates from epidemiologic studies and national statistics for adult populations remained substantially higher than those observed in VAERS, suggesting that age alone is unlikely to explain the findings.

The healthy vaccine effect may partially contribute to the lower prevalence of comorbidities observed among vaccinated individuals compared with the general population [33, 34]. Individuals who seek vaccination may differ in health-seeking behavior, healthcare access, and overall health status [35]. However, this explanation does not fully account for the markedly lower prevalence observed in VAERS myocarditis/pericarditis reports relative to vaccinated population estimates identified in the literature review despite differences in targeted age groups.

A more likely explanation is under-ascertainment of comorbidities within the VAERS database. As a spontaneous reporting system, VAERS is subject to incomplete reporting and missing clinical information [23, 36–38]. While myocarditis and pericarditis are generally coded in structured symptom fields, comorbidities are often inconsistently documented within free-text history and narrative sections. Internal VAERS comparisons demonstrated similarly low reporting of comorbidities across comparator groups, suggesting that under-documentation was widespread rather than specific to myocarditis/pericarditis reports. Consequently, the true prevalence of these conditions among affected individuals was likely underestimated.

Despite these limitations, internal comparisons within VAERS remained informative. Comparisons between myocarditis/pericarditis reports and other adverse event reports following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination, Bell’s palsy reports, and reports following non-COVID-19 vaccination consistently demonstrated low overall prevalence of the evaluated comorbidities across comparator groups. Although some statistically significant differences were observed for obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension in selected comparisons, absolute prevalence remained low across all groups, generally below 10% and most commonly between 0% and 2%. These findings do not support an important modifying effect of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or obesity on the risk of myocarditis/pericarditis following mRNA vaccination in young adult males, although the higher prevalence of obesity and dyslipidemia observed in some analyses may warrant further investigation.

This study also highlights important methodological challenges associated with O/E analyses. Reliable expected prevalence estimates for narrowly defined demographic groups were limited, particularly for males aged 18–30 years during the COVID-19 vaccination period. Most available studies were conducted to evaluate vaccine effectiveness or safety and often lacked age- and sex-stratified prevalence estimates. In addition, some estimates originated from selected populations, such as veterans, healthcare workers, or regional healthcare systems, which may not be representative of the broader vaccinated population. These limitations are consistent with challenges previously identified in pharmacovigilance O/E analyses [39, 40].

Several additional limitations should also be acknowledged. First, causality between vaccination and myocarditis/pericarditis cannot be established using VAERS data alone. Some reports may reflect coincidental events or alternative etiologies, including prior viral infections. Second, comprehensive clinical adjudication of all reports was not feasible because of the volume and structure of the database. Third, the keyword-based algorithm used to identify comorbidities may have missed atypical, misspelled, or inconsistently documented terminology despite manual review of selected reports. Fourth, VAERS does not collect information on race/ethnicity to protect patient privacy, limiting analyses because the prevalence of the evaluated comorbidities is known to vary across subpopulations. However, the consistently low prevalence of comorbidities across all internal comparison groups suggests that limitations in the extraction algorithm alone are unlikely to explain the findings. Finally, incomplete documentation may have resulted in failure to identify all individuals with a prior history of myocarditis or pericarditis.

Despite these limitations, this study highlights the challenges associated with obtaining sufficiently granular prevalence estimates from national statistics and published epidemiologic data, particularly for comorbidities in specific subpopulations. The findings also highlight the potential utility of disproportionality and internal comparison approaches within spontaneous reporting databases. Despite the under-documentation inherent to spontaneous reports, these methods can still be applied in a hypothesis-generating manner to explore potential risk-modifying factors associated with vaccine-related adverse events, particularly in emergency public health settings such as mass vaccination campaigns.

There may be other comorbidities involved and alternative explanations for the elevated risk in males 18–30 years old, which should be explored further. The next step would be to conduct a formal study and hypothesis-testing with healthcare databases. Future research may also examine other factors that may predispose patients to myocarditis and pericarditis following mRNA vaccination such as other pre-existing comorbidities, history of viral infection, lifestyle and behavioral risk factors, race and ethnicity, allergies, and concomitant medication usage.

Conclusions

In this observed-versus-expected and disproportionality analysis using VAERS data, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity, among 18–30 year-old males with myocarditis/pericarditis following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination was lower than expected based on estimates from vaccinated and prepandemic general populations. This study highlights the scarcity of age- and sex-stratified data on individuals receiving mRNA COVID-19 vaccines and the under-ascertainment of comorbidities in VAERS.

Despite these limitations, internal comparisons within VAERS showed similarly low reporting of comorbidities across comparator groups, supporting the overall interpretation of the findings.

Finally, this study highlights a novel application of disproportionality methods within a spontaneous reporting database—traditionally used for medicinal product safety signal detection—to explore potential modifiers of vaccine-related adverse events in a hypothesis-generating manner. Such approaches may be particularly valuable in emergency and public health settings, including mass vaccination campaigns, where timely identification of potential susceptibility factors is important.
