Association Between Sacubitril/Valsartan Use and Risk of Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events in Patients With Durable Left Ventricular Assist Devices: A Multi‐Institutional Real‐World Cohort Study
Authors: Jheng‐Yan Wu, Keng‐Wei Lee, Sheng‐Chi Huang, Yu‐Min Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDurable left ventricular assist devices improve survival in patients with advanced heart failure (HF), yet long‐term outcomes remain limited by recurrent decompensation and end‐organ dysfunction. Evidence on the prognostic association of angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) therapy after left ventricular assist device implantation remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network. Adults undergoing durable left ventricular assist device implantation between 2015 and 2025 were identified. Patients receiving ARNI therapy were propensity score matched 1:1 with patients without ARNI exposure. The primary outcome was 1‐year major adverse cardiovascular events, defined as a composite of heart failure exacerbation or all‐cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included all‐cause mortality, heart failure exacerbation, and major adverse kidney events hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs.ResultsAfter matching, 923 patients were included in each group. ARNI use was associated with significantly lower risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (28.8% versus 35.9%; HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.60–0.83; P<0.001), all‐cause mortality (6.7% versus 10.6%; HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.41–0.78; P<0.001), heart failure exacerbation (26.0% versus 30.2%; HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.64–0.91; P=0.002), and major adverse kidney events (5.1% versus 10.4%; HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.32–0.64; P<0.001). Results were consistent across prespecified subgroups and supported by negative control and E‐value analyses.ConclusionsAmong patients with durable left ventricular assist devices support, ARNI therapy was associated with lower cardiovascular and renal event risk. Given the observational design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in prospective studies.

Advanced heart failure (HF) remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality despite major advances in pharmacologic and device‐based therapies. 1 , 2 For patients with advanced, refractory HF, durable left ventricular assist devices (LVAD) have emerged as a life‐saving therapy, improving both survival and quality of life. 3 Although LVAD support improves survival and functional status, long‐term management remains challenging because recurrent heart failure exacerbation (HFE), renal dysfunction, and adverse clinical events continue to occur after implantation. 4 , 5 , 6 As post‐implant survival increases, optimization of adjunctive medical therapy has become an increasingly important component of long‐term care.

Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibition is a cornerstone of HF management, providing mortality and morbidity benefits across a wide spectrum of HF phenotypes. 7 , 8 , 9 Yet, the evidence supporting conventional RAAS blockade after LVAD implantation remains more limited. Clinical equipoise persists about whether these agents confer additional benefit once mechanical unloading has stabilized hemodynamics and ventricular wall stress. 3 Nevertheless, several observational studies have suggested that continued RAAS inhibitor use in the post‐LVAD setting may be associated with lower mortality, improved survival, and better quality of life, underscoring the potential role of neurohormonal modulation even after mechanical support. 10 , 11 , 12

Sacubitril/valsartan, an angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), offers dual blockade of the RAAS and augmentation of natriuretic peptide signaling, leading to favorable effects on cardiac remodeling, renal perfusion, and neurohormonal modulation. 13 In a large randomized trial of patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction, ARNI therapy reduced the risk of cardiovascular death and heart failure exacerbation (HFE) compared with enalapril. 14 Despite its proven efficacy in conventional HF populations, its role among patients with durable LVAD support remains unclear. Preliminary observational studies have suggested that ARNI therapy in post‐LVAD patients may improve cardiac function, lower NT‐proBNP levels, and potentially confer prognostic benefits. 15 , 16 However, these studies have been limited by small sample sizes and single‐center designs, and robust large‐scale evidence is still lacking.

To fill this gap, we leveraged the TriNetX Research Network, a large real‐world, multi‐institutional electronic health record database, to assess whether ARNI therapy after LVAD implantation is associated with improved cardiovascular and renal outcomes. By examining 30‐day to 1‐year risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), HFE, all‐cause mortality, and major adverse kidney events (MAKE), this study aims to generate real‐world evidence to inform pharmacologic optimization in the chronic post‐LVAD phase.

METHODS

The data, analytic methods, and study materials will not be made publicly available because of TriNetX data‐sharing restrictions; however, qualified researchers may request access through the TriNetX platform.

Data Source

This retrospective observational study was performed using the TriNetX Research Network, a federated electronic health record platform that aggregates data contributed by participating health care organizations. At the time of data extraction, the network contained records from approximately 178 million individuals across 158 institutions. Available data elements include demographic information, diagnoses, procedures, medication prescriptions, and selected laboratory measurements.

Because TriNetX provides investigators access only to de‐identified, aggregated data and does not permit retrieval of protected health information, this study did not involve direct patient contact or intervention. Consistent with institutional policy governing use of the TriNetX platform, the study was considered exempt from formal review by the Western Institutional Review Board. Reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations. 17

Study Design

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥18 years who underwent durable LVAD implantation between January 1, 2015 and September 30, 2025. LVAD procedures were identified using ICD‐10‐PCS codes (02HA3QZ, 02HA0QZ, and 02HA4QZ) and CPT codes (33 975 and 33 979). Information on specific device models (eg, HeartMate 3, HeartMate 2, or HVAD) was not available within TriNetX; therefore, analyses were conducted at the level of durable LVAD support.

Patients were classified according to post‐implant exposure to sacubitril/valsartan (RxNorm 1 656 328). Individuals with documented ARNI prescriptions after LVAD implantation were assigned to the exposure cohort, whereas those without ARNI prescriptions formed the comparison cohort. Because exact clinical timing relative to recovery status or stabilization could not be determined from structured records, treatment exposure was defined according to prescription documentation.

For exposed patients, cohort entry was defined by the first available ARNI prescription following LVAD implantation. For unexposed patients, cohort entry corresponded to the date of LVAD implantation. To reduce contamination between groups, patients in the comparison cohort were excluded if ARNI prescriptions were identified from 12 months before through 12 months after cohort entry.

Baseline characteristics were assessed during the 12 months preceding cohort entry. Outcome surveillance began 30 days after cohort entry to reduce potential survival and treatment‐initiation bias and continued until outcome occurrence, death, loss to follow‐up, or completion of 1 year of observation, whichever occurred first.

Medication exposure reflected prescription records captured in the database and did not allow confirmation of adherence, treatment persistence, or discontinuation. Definitions and coding algorithms for baseline variables are provided in Table S1.

Covariates and Propensity Score Matching

To account for differences in baseline characteristics between treatment groups, adjustment variables were identified before outcome analyses and extracted from records available during the 12‐month pre‐index period. Variable selection was guided by clinical relevance, prior evidence, and availability within the TriNetX environment, with the objective of capturing determinants of both treatment allocation and clinical prognosis. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Propensity scores representing the probability of receiving ARNI therapy were estimated using multivariable logistic regression. Patients were subsequently matched in a 1:1 ratio using nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement and a caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs of the logit of the propensity score. Adequate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences, with values below 0.1 considered indicative of acceptable covariate distribution. 22

Variables for propensity score matching included in the matching procedure covered demographic characteristics, comorbidity burden, medication exposure, and selected laboratory measurements. Demographic factors included age, sex, and race. Clinical characteristics included atrial fibrillation and flutter, chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes and related complications, nicotine dependence, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Medication variables included beta‐blockers, renin–angiotensin system agents, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (including empagliflozin and dapagliflozin), and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists. Laboratory and physiologic variables included systolic blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and hemoglobin A1c. Complete coding definitions are summarized in Table S2.

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

The primary end point was MACE, defined as a composite of HFE and all‐cause mortality. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Secondary end points included each individual component of the composite as well as MAKE. HFE was identified using records indicating acute pulmonary edema or administration of injectable diuretics, whereas MAKE included hemodialysis, end‐stage renal disease, or chronic kidney disease stage 5 events. 27 , 28 As an additional assessment of residual bias, a prespecified negative control outcome analysis was performed using hernia. Because no biologically plausible relationship between ARNI exposure and hernia occurrence is expected, this end point was used to evaluate the possibility of systematic confounding.

Patients entered outcome assessment beginning 30 days after cohort entry. Follow‐up continued until the earliest occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, last available health care encounter, or completion of 1 year of observation. Definitions and coding algorithms for all study end points are summarized in Table S3.

Subgroup Analysis

For the primary outcome, subgroup analyses were performed according to the overall study population, sex (female or male), and age group (18–64 years or 65 years and older). Additional subgroup analyses were conducted based on the presence or absence of chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes, and coronary artery disease, as well as according to baseline use of beta‐blockers and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as means with SDs for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Comparative analyses were conducted after propensity score matching to improve exchangeability between treatment groups.

Associations between ARNI exposure and time‐to‐event outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression and reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% CIs. Assessment of proportional hazards assumptions was performed using diagnostic procedures available within the TriNetX analytical environment. Event‐free survival was visualized using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared using log‐rank testing. To estimate the magnitude of unmeasured confounding necessary to explain observed associations, E‐values were calculated. 29 All analyses were conducted within the TriNetX platform using embedded analytic tools.

Sensitivity Analysis

Several prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the stability of the primary findings. First, because clinical practice patterns and LVAD technology evolved during the study period, analyses were repeated after stratification according to treatment era (2015–2019 and 2020–2025). This approach was intended to explore whether observed associations were influenced by temporal differences in device utilization, background pharmacotherapy, or ARNI adoption. Second, an additional analysis restricted inclusion to patients without documented hypotension during the baseline period to reduce potential confounding related to treatment eligibility and hemodynamic instability. Third, additional exploratory end points were evaluated to characterize health care utilization and treatment safety, including all‐cause hospitalization, hypotension‐related events, and worsening renal function.

Finally, landmark analyses were conducted to examine temporal consistency of associations across prespecified follow‐up windows (1–3 months, 1–6 months, 3 months–1 year, and 6 months–1 year).

RESULTS

Study Cohort

Among the 178 033 729 patients included in the TriNetX network as of October 27, 2025, a total of 109 196 318 individuals had at least 2 clinical encounters between January 1, 2015, and September 30, 2025. After excluding patients younger than 18 years and those without LVAD implantation, 7167 eligible patients with LVAD were identified. Among them, 1432 patients received ARNI therapy, and 5735 did not. Following 1:1 propensity score matching to minimize potential confounding, 2 balanced cohorts were established, each consisting of 923 patients in the ARNI and control groups, respectively (Figure 1).

Characteristics of Study Subjects

Before matching, patients in the ARNI group were slightly younger (mean age 54.3 years versus 55.8 years) and had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including atrial fibrillation and flutter, chronic kidney disease, and type 2 diabetes (Table 1). The ARNI group also showed higher usage of cardiovascular medications, such as beta‐blockers, agents acting on the renin–angiotensin system, aldosterone antagonists, and SGLT2is. Differences between groups were reflected by SMDs greater than 0.1 across several variables, particularly in medication use and comorbidity profiles. After matching, 923 patients were included in each group, and all baseline characteristics were well balanced, with SMDs less than 0.1 for all variables. Demographic factors, comorbidities, medication use, and laboratory parameters, including systolic blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and hemoglobin A1c, were comparable between the 2 groups, indicating adequate matching and covariate balance prior to outcome analyses (Table 1).

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Table 2 summarizes the incidence and HRs of the primary and secondary outcomes between the ARNI and control groups after propensity score matching. For the primary outcome, MACE occurred in 266 patients (28.8%) in the ARNI group and 331 patients (35.9%) in the control group, with a HR of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.60–0.83; P<0.001; E‐value, 2.2). Kaplan–Meier curves consistently demonstrated a higher event‐free probability in the ARNI group than the control group (log‐rank P<0.001, Figure 2). The proportionality test yielded a P value of 0.012.

Regarding secondary outcomes, HFE was observed in 240 patients (26.0%) in the ARNI group and 279 patients (30.2%) in the control group (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.64–0.91; P=0.002; E‐value, 2.0). All‐cause mortality occurred in 62 patients (6.7%) and 98 patients (10.6%) in the ARNI and control groups, respectively (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.41–0.78; P<0.001; E‐value, 2.9). MAKE was identified in 47 patients (5.1%) in the ARNI group and 96 patients (10.4%) in the control group (HR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.32–0.64; P<0.001; E‐value, 3.9). No significant association was observed between ARNI use and the risk of hernia (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.45–1.19; P=0.209, Table S4).

Subgroup Analysis

Figure 3 presents the results of subgroup analyses examining the association between ARNI use and the primary outcome. The overall findings were statistically significant (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.60–0.83; P<0.001). Similar patterns of significance were observed across most subgroups defined by sex, age, comorbidities, and medication use. In sex‐specific analyses, significant associations were observed in both females (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.46–0.91; P=0.010) and males (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.61–0.88; P<0.001). In the age‐stratified analysis, the association was significant among patients aged 18–64 years (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.56–0.86; P<0.001) but not among those aged 65 years or older (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.62–1.04; P=0.101). For comorbidity subgroups, significant findings were noted in patients with or without chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes, and coronary artery disease. Among medication‐related subgroups, significant results were observed regardless of baseline use of beta‐blockers and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. These subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory and may be underpowered, particularly for smaller subgroups.

Sensitivity Analysis

In sensitivity analyses stratified by treatment era, the association between ARNI use and reduced risk of the primary outcome remained consistent across both time periods. During the earlier era (2015–2019), ARNI therapy was associated with a significantly lower risk of MACE (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.55–0.80; P<0.001). Similarly, in the later era (2020–2025), ARNI use remained significantly associated with reduced MACE risk (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.60–0.82; P<0.001) (Table S5). The comparable effect estimates across eras suggest that the observed association was unlikely to be solely driven by temporal changes in LVAD technology or evolving prescribing practices. In this hemodynamically more stable subgroup, ARNI use remained significantly associated with a lower risk of MACE compared with the control group (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.51–0.84; P<0.001; Table S6). ARNI use was associated with a lower risk of all‐cause hospitalization compared with the control group (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.75–0.93; P<0.001; Table S7). No significant difference was observed for ARNI tolerability‐related events (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.70–1.01; P=0.053; Table S7). In contrast, ARNI use was associated with a lower risk of worsening renal function (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.67–0.88; P<0.001; Table S7). In landmark analyses, the association between ARNI use and reduced risk of the primary outcome remained consistent across different time windows.

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter observational study of patients receiving durable LVAD support, ARNI exposure was associated with lower risks of the composite cardiovascular end point during follow‐up after implantation. In addition to the primary analysis, reductions were also observed across individual outcome components, including HFE, all‐cause mortality, and MAKE. These findings remained directionally consistent across subgroup analyses and multiple sensitivity analyses.

Our findings contribute to a growing body of literature evaluating optimization of medical therapy after LVAD implantation. Although mechanical circulatory support substantially modifies loading conditions and systemic perfusion, residual neurohormonal activation and progressive end‐organ dysfunction remain clinically relevant in the chronic post‐implant phase. The present results suggest that ARNI therapy may continue to provide incremental benefit beyond mechanical unloading; however, the observational nature of this analysis precludes conclusions about causality.

Prior studies have highlighted the prognostic value of maintaining neurohormonal blockade after LVAD implantation. In a multicenter cohort of 12 144 LVAD recipients, patients receiving triple neurohormonal therapy demonstrated a 4‐year survival rate of 56.0% compared with 43.9% among those not receiving such therapy (HR 0.34; 95% CI, 0.28–0.41), along with superior quality‐of‐life scores and longer 6‐minute walking distances. 10 Moreover, both randomized controlled trials and real‐world studies in patients with HF have consistently shown that ARNI therapy confers greater prognostic benefits compared with angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or Angiotensin II receptor blockers. 30 , 31 However, whether these cardioprotective effects extend to patients with durable LVAD support remains uncertain, as current evidence in this specific population is limited.

Several observational and early clinical studies have explored the impact of ARNI therapy in patients with durable LVAD support, though the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. In a multicenter cohort of 257 patients, no significant difference in conditional 1‐year survival was observed between those receiving ARNI and those not treated (97% [95% CI, 94–100%] versus 88% [95% CI, 80–96%]; P=0.06). 32 Conversely, a retrospective analysis of 73 patients reported that preoperative ARNI use was associated with a markedly lower 30‐day mortality compared with no treatment (HR 0.11; 95% CI 0.02–0.55; P=0.009). 16 Another single‐center study of 30 patients demonstrated significant reductions in NT‐proBNP concentrations, from a median of 1265 pg/mL at initiation to 750 pg/mL at 3 months and 764 pg/mL at 6 months (P=0.01). 33 A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis including seven studies comprising 249 LVAD recipients found that ARNI therapy improved several cardiac structural and hemodynamic parameters, including New York Heart Association class, B‐type natriuretic peptide levels, diuretic dose requirements, and left ventricular ejection fraction. 34 Furthermore, preliminary results from a randomized controlled trial enrolling 60 patients suggested a favorable trend toward improved outcomes with ARNI compared with standard care for the composite end point of death, renal deterioration, hyperkalemia, or symptomatic hypotension leading to drug discontinuation at 12 months (HR 0.42; 95% CI 0.08–2.18; P=0.30). 35

Collectively, these findings suggest that ARNI therapy may confer clinical and functional benefits in post‐LVAD patients. However, the small sample sizes, variable study designs, and substantial heterogeneity across existing studies underscore the need for larger, multicenter analyses to confirm these potential prognostic advantages. Against this background, our study provides large‐scale multicenter observational evidence examining ARNI use in patients with durable LVAD support. The observed associations with lower risks of cardiovascular and kidney outcomes extend existing hypothesis‐generating data and support further prospective evaluation in this population.

Several pathophysiologic pathways may help explain the observed associations between ARNI use and improved outcomes following durable LVAD implantation. Although mechanical support substantially unloads the left ventricle, persistent neurohormonal activation and incomplete recovery of cardiorenal function remain common during long‐term support.

By combining neprilysin inhibition with angiotensin receptor blockade, sacubitril/valsartan may provide additional modulation of pathways not fully corrected through mechanical unloading alone. 13 Enhancement of endogenous natriuretic peptide activity may promote natriuresis, improve vascular compliance, and reduce filling pressures, whereas inhibition of angiotensin II signaling may attenuate maladaptive remodeling and ongoing myocardial stress. 36 , 37 Potential renal effects may also contribute to the observed findings. Previous studies have suggested that ARNI therapy may favorably influence renal perfusion and intraglomerular hemodynamics, potentially supporting preservation of kidney function in vulnerable populations. 38 , 39 , 40 Although mechanistic confirmation is beyond the scope of this study, these pathways provide biologic context for the associations observed in our analyses.

These findings may have implications for long‐term management after durable LVAD implantation. As outcomes after implantation continue to improve, attention has increasingly shifted from short‐term survival toward optimization of chronic care and preservation of end‐organ function. 41 , 42 The observed associations suggest that pharmacologic modulation may remain clinically relevant even in the setting of LVAD support. Although these findings do not support routine ARNI initiation in all patients with LVADs, they provide supportive evidence that ARNI therapy may represent a reasonable consideration in selected patients who are hemodynamically suitable and receiving contemporary guideline‐directed therapy. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify patient selection and treatment timing.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective observational design using routinely collected electronic health records introduces the possibility of residual confounding, selection bias, and incomplete capture of clinical variables. Second, identification of exposures, covariates, and outcomes relied on structured coding and prescription documentation, which may result in misclassification. Although negative control analyses did not suggest major systematic bias, such limitations cannot be fully excluded. Third, detailed treatment information, including ARNI dose, duration, adherence, treatment discontinuation, and timing related to clinical stabilization after LVAD implantation, was unavailable. Therefore, dose–response relationships and treatment persistence could not be evaluated. Fourth, important measures of heart failure severity and device status, including INTERMACS profile, hemodynamic parameters, right ventricular function, and LVAD model information, were not captured in the database and may contribute to residual confounding.

Fifth, during the study period (2015–2025), clinical practice evolved substantially, including the transition toward newer‐generation LVAD devices (e.g., HeartMate 3), increasing off‐label ARNI use, and broader adoption of SGLT2is. Because newer devices such as HeartMate 3 have improved hemocompatibility and survival compared with earlier systems, temporal changes in device use could influence outcomes. To address potential temporal bias, we performed era‐restricted sensitivity analyses stratified by index year, which demonstrated consistent associations across treatment periods. Nevertheless, residual confounding related to evolving practice patterns, device generation, and learning‐curve effects cannot be entirely excluded. Sixth, given the long study period spanning a decade, substantial changes in LVAD technology, heart failure management, and the introduction of newer therapies such as ARNI and SGLT2 inhibitors may have influenced patient outcomes. Although era‐stratified analyses showed consistent results, residual confounding related to evolving clinical practice and unmeasured factors cannot be fully excluded, and important clinical uncertainties remain.

Seventh, the use of different cohort entry definitions between groups may introduce bias related to treatment timing and survivor effects despite implementation of delayed outcome assessment and landmark analyses. Eighth, the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied (proportionality test, P=0.012). Therefore, the estimated hazard ratios should be interpreted as average relative effects over follow‐up rather than constant hazards across time. Finally, cause‐specific mortality was unavailable, preventing distinction between cardiovascular and noncardiovascular causes of death.

Conclusions

Among patients receiving durable LVAD support, post‐implant ARNI exposure was associated with lower risks of adverse cardiovascular and kidney outcomes during follow‐up. These findings suggest that neurohormonal modulation may remain relevant despite mechanical circulatory support and highlight the potential role of pharmacologic optimization in the chronic post‐LVAD phase. However, because of the observational design and the possibility of residual confounding, these results should be considered hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in prospective studies.
