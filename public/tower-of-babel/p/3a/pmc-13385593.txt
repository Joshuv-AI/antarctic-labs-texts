Virtual vs. In-Person Training for MinMed™ 780G Initiation: Comparable Effectiveness and Safety in Adults with Type 1 Diabetes
Authors: Ana Maria Gómez, Diana Henao-Carrillo, Oscar Muñoz-Velandia, Miguel Angel Mina, Paula Andrea Bermont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA knowledge gap persists regarding whether virtual training differs from in-person training in terms of metabolic control and clinical safety among adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) initiating the MiniMed™ 780G advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) system.MethodsObservational study comparing two retrospective cohorts of adult patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) treated with the Medtronic MiniMed™780G hybrid closed-loop insulin automation system: one cohort trained in-person and a second cohort trained virtually at the Hospital Universitario San Ignacio, Bogotá (Colombia). Metabolic control and safety parameters were evaluated 12 weeks after training initiation. A logistic regression model was developed to identify factors associated with meeting glycemic target (Time in range 70–180 mg/dL ≥ 70%) at 12 weeks.Results191 patients were analyzed; (49.7% virtual 50.3% in-person training). After 12 weeks, no statistically significant differences were observed in TIR between the cohorts (75% vs. 77.5%, p = 0.51). Severe hypoglycemia (<54 mg/dL) decreased significantly in both groups (p<0.02), without differences between cohorts. The type of training was not significantly associated with achieving the goals of TIR (adjusted OR 0.78; 95% CI:0.16–3.82; p=0.76). Younger age (<45 years) was independently associated with improved metabolic control (adjusted OR 0.08; 95% CI:0.01–0.75; p=0.02). Higher socioeconomic status showed a trend toward better outcomes (adjusted OR 35.04; 95% CI:0.94–1260.6; p=0.05).ConclusionsBoth virtual and in-person training are effective and safe strategies for initiating the MiniMed™ 780G AHCL system in adults with T1D, with no significant differences between training modalities.Keywords: advanced hybrid close-loop systems, continuous glucose monitoring, diabetes education, type 1 diabetes, time in range

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) is a chronic condition affecting more than 8 million people worldwide. 1 Adequate glycemic control reduces the risk of microvascular and macrovascular complications by up to 76%.2,3 The development of diabetes technologies such as the MiniMed™ 780G advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) system has transformed T1D management by automating insulin delivery and achieving time in range (TIR) levels above 70%, increasing time in normoglycemic range (TIRN) between 70 and 140 mg/dl while minimizing hypoglycemia. 4 However, the success of these technologies depends heavily on patient education and training. 5

Current evidence demonstrates that both virtual and in-person training programs can improve adherence, self-care, and glycemic control.6,7 Virtual training gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic and showed comparable clinical results with higher patient satisfaction and reduced travel burden. 8 Studies in patients using automated insulin delivery (AID) systems (such as MiniMed 640G and MiniMed 670G) reported increases in time in range (TIR) to an average of 81.6% and reductions in hypoglycemia to levels below 2% following virtual training programs, 9 which were comparable to the values reported with in person training.

Unlike other AID systems, the AHCL, such as MiniMed™ 780G, includes a new feature called SmartGuard. This feature is characterized by the following 1 : automatic adjustment of basal insulin infusion based on sensor readings 2 ; a programmable basal glucose target of 100, 110, or 120 mg/dL 3 ; automatic self-correction of hyperglycemia with microboluses of insulin administered every five minutes 4 ; prediction of hypoglycemia; and 5 meal detection and automatic adjustments for corrections. However, a knowledge gap remains regarding whether the same type of training can be applied to patients using AHCL — to improve metabolic control and device safety.

The aim of this study is to evaluate whether virtual training, compared with in-person training, is associated with achieving the recommended metabolic control goals TIR above 70% in patients with T1D initiating therapy with the MiniMed™ 780G, after controlling for multiple confounding factors. This study is based on the experience of a diabetes technology center of excellence in Latin America.

Methods

An observational study was conducted, comparing two retrospective cohorts of adult patients older than 18 years with diagnosis of T1D treated with AHCL system (Minimed ™ 780G, Medtronic, Northridge, California): one cohort trained in person and a second cohort trained virtually between 2022 and 2024 at the San Ignacio University Hospital in Bogotá, Colombia. Patients with severe cognitive impairment that prevented device management or participation in the training process were excluded. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitario San Ignacio and Pontificia Universidad Javeriana (approval code FM-CIE-0017-24). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to initiation of insulin pump therapy and data collection.

Data were collected from the database in which all patients undergoing training at the Hospital Universitario San Ignacio are registered; at the time of each evaluation, baseline patient data are systematically requested as the primary information source. The training program consisted of three structured modules of four hours each, covering key diabetes concepts: pathophysiology, glycemic control targets, the impact of glycemic variability, and the importance of self-monitoring. The second module focused on carbohydrate counting training, and the third module deepened the use of continuous glucose monitoring and the SmartGuard.

Both educational modalities, training was delivered by the same multidisciplinary diabetes education team, composed of a certified diabetes educator and a nutritionist. The curriculum, instructional modules, and total duration of the sessions were identical for the in-person and virtual modalities. In the in-person format, training was conducted on-site at the hospital. In the virtual format, training was delivered through the hospital’s virtual diabetes clinic using the Zoom® platform, the same educational content and sequence of modules were provided. Continuous glucose monitoring data were obtained from the CareLink™ Pro 4.0 system. Information was collected from the 14 days prior to device initiation and from the same number of days, 12 weeks after the training had been completed. For the analysis of factors associated with good metabolic control, we considered the goals recommended in international guidelines, it means a TIR between 70–180 mg/dL ≥ 70%. 10 Additional continuous glucose monitoring metrics were also considered, including TIRN (70-140 mg/dL) ≥ 50%, time above range (TAR) > 180 mg/dL and 250mg/dL), time below range (TBR <70 mg/dL and < 54 mg/dL), coefficient of variation, and optimal settings (active insulin time: 2-hours, glycemic target:100 mg/dL), in accordance with the 2023 Advanced Technologies & Treatments for Diabetes (ATTD) recommendations. 11

Sample size was calculated to ensure at least 10 outcomes (patients with TIR 70–180 mg/dL ≥ 70%) for each of the 10 confounding variables of interest (sex, age, BMI, socioeconomic status, education level, time in SmartGuard, sensor wear time, duration of T1D, previously used technology, and glomerular filtration rate). Assuming a 70% probability of achieving the outcome of interest, 100 outcomes would be required, translating into a minimum of 143 patients.

Continuous variables were described using mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, depending on whether the assumption of normal distribution was met, evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The in-person cohort was compared with the virtual cohort using t-tests, Wilcoxon tests, Chi-square tests, or Fisher’s exact tests, depending on variable characteristics. Baseline time-in-range values were compared with final values using a paired t-test for repeated measures. A logistic regression model was developed to identify factors associated with meeting glycemic targets, beginning with a univariate model and subsequently a multivariate model that included all variables significant in the univariate model, as well as variables identified a priori as potentially important based on previous studies. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 19® (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

A total of 204 patients who initiated therapy with the AHCL system MiniMed™ 780G were initially identified. Thirteen patients were excluded due to failure to complete the institutional 3-month follow-up. A final sample of 191 patients was analyzed; 95 (49.7%) received virtual training, and 96 (50.3%) received in-person training. Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age was 39 years, and 59.2% were women. The only significant difference between groups was the indication for AHCL: poor metabolic control was the predominant indication in the virtual group (40%), whereas hypoglycemia was the most frequent indication in the in-person group (37.5%) (p = 0.01). Overall, the cohorts exhibited similar baseline characteristics.

At 12 weeks of follow-up, a significant improvement in glycemic control was observed in both groups, with consistent increases in TIR and reductions in hypoglycemia and %CV (Figure 1). However, the difference between groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.51). TINR (70–140 mg/dL) was similar between modalities: 54.5% in the virtual group and 53.6% in the in-person group. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 5), the type of training was not significantly associated with achieving the goals of TIR ≥ 70% at 12 weeks (adjusted OR 0.78; 95% CI: 0.16–3.82; p = 0.76). However, several variables were independently associated with better metabolic control. An age between 45 and 65 years compared with patients under 45 years had a lower probability of achieving the TIR goal (adjusted OR 0.08; 95% CI: 0.01–0.75; p = 0.02). Additionally, belonging to a higher socioeconomic stratum showed a positive trend toward improved outcomes (p = 0.05). Sensor wear time was also relevant in the univariate model (p = 0.02), although it lost significance after multivariate adjustment.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether differences exist in the effectiveness of metabolic control between virtual and in-person training among patients with T1D initiating therapy with the AHCL system Minimed ™ 780G. These findings suggest that the training modality is not a primary determinant of metabolic control after three months. Metabolic outcomes may be more strongly associated with patient age and socioeconomic status.

This study found that the type of training did not significantly impact the likelihood of achieving adequate TIR or TINR. Notably, both parameters increased significantly by the end of the follow-up period. This suggests that the structure and quality of the training program may be more important than the delivery method itself. Both groups received a structured program that covered technical device aspects and comprehensive diabetes education, indicating that standardization of the training process may mitigate differences between virtual and in-person formats. Furthermore, the high adherence rates and sensor wear times above 90% in both cohorts reflect successful technology adoption, likely facilitated by the intuitive interface of the 780G system and continuous remote support.

Evidence demonstrating that virtual training is not inferior to in-person training has been reported with other AID systems. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, virtual training for users of the MiniMed 670G system achieved TIR values above 75%, comparable to in-person results. 8 Similarly, Vigersky et al. reported equivalent outcomes between modalities, highlighting the effectiveness and safety of virtual diabetes education. 9 However, our study is the first to demonstrate these findings in a more advanced system such as the MiniMed 780G.

A second notable finding was the indication for initiating AHCL system. The virtual training group had a higher proportion of patients with poor pre-training metabolic control, whereas the in-person group showed a predominance of hypoglycemia as the indication. This suggests that virtual-training patients began at a disadvantage regarding baseline control, which may have influenced their training response. Nonetheless, at three months both groups achieved similar TIR levels, supporting the effectiveness of virtual training even among patients with poorer initial metabolic profiles.

The multivariate model indicated that patients younger than 45 years were more likely to achieve TIR ≥70%. This finding aligns with prior studies showing that younger patients tend to achieve higher time in SmartGuard and higher sensor utilization, translating into better metabolic outcomes.12,13However, it contrasts with previous real-world studies that have reported comparable or even superior glycemic outcomes among older adults using automated insulin delivery systems, 14 our findings differed in this regard. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. Older patients in our cohort may have faced greater challenges adapting to advanced diabetes technologies, particularly in virtual educational settings, due to differences in digital engagement, familiarity with technological tools, or previous exposure to diabetes devices. Additionally, many prior studies were conducted in highly selected populations from high-income healthcare systems with broad access to diabetes technology and structured support programs, which may differ from the sociodemographic and healthcare context of our population. Finally, the relatively limited number of older adults included in our study may have affected the precision of these estimates.

Additionally, in our study, socioeconomic status was associated with improved metabolic outcomes, with patients from middle and higher socioeconomic strata showing a tendency to better control. Interestingly, previous real-world studies from the United States have demonstrated minimal clinically meaningful differences in glycemic outcomes across socioeconomic strata among users of advanced hybrid closed-loop systems.6,7 Several factors may explain these differences, including variations in healthcare system organization, reimbursement policies, access to diabetes technologies, digital literacy, and continuity of structured educational support. Additionally, social determinants of health may have a greater impact on technology utilization and diabetes self-management in middle-income settings such as Latin America.

Although educational level and socioeconomic status were not significantly different between the virtual and in-person training groups, most participants in both cohorts belonged to middle socioeconomic and intermediate educational strata. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within this context. The applicability of virtual onboarding strategies may differ in populations with lower digital literacy, limited internet access, or reduced familiarity with diabetes technologies. Consequently, extrapolation of these results to more socially disadvantaged populations should be made with caution. Future prospective studies should specifically evaluate the implementation and effectiveness of virtual training programs in underserved and technologically limited settings.

This study is the first to evaluate the effectiveness of virtual versus in-person training specifically for MiniMed™ 780G users. Furthermore, it highlights a high adherence to sensor use, showing that more than 60% of patients configured SmartGuard to the ROS, independent of the training modality. However, there are limitations that should be recognised. The observational and retrospective design, which relied on clinical record documentation, could be associated with a loss of information. However, missing data were minimal (less than 5%), which reduces the likelihood of bias. Another limitation was the slight differences in the reasons for initiating AHCL system, which could potentially affect TIR outcomes. Nevertheless, despite these initial differences — particularly the poorer baseline control in the virtual group — final metabolic outcomes were similar between training modalities.

Conclusion

Both virtual and in-person training are effective and safe strategies for initiating MiniMed™ 780G system in patients with T1D. Virtual training represents a feasible alternative that expands access to diabetes education, particularly in settings with logistical or geographic constraints. Future studies should explore the long-term sustainability of these training strategies.
