L1CAM Promotes the Infiltrative Properties of Patient‐Derived Glioblastoma Cells
Authors: Asako Katsuma, Daisuke Kanematsu, Yukako Handa, Tomoko Shofuda, Atsuyo Yamamoto, Kayo Nishitani, Zhize Xiao, Daisuke Kawauchi, Kentarou Baba, Naoyuki Inagaki, Yonehiro Kanemura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTL1CAM is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily involved in cell adhesion, migration, and neural development. While several studies have shown an association between L1CAM and glioblastoma (GBM) cell motility, its mechanobiological function in patient‐derived primary cultured GBM cells remains insufficiently understood. This study focuses on L1CAM‐dependent motility mechanisms in patient‐derived GBM cells at the single‐cell level. Patient‐derived GBM cells were established from GBM tissues using the neurosphere method. L1CAM expression was evaluated by qPCR, western blotting, flow cytometry, and immunocytochemistry. Cell motility was assessed by single‐cell tracking. Functional assays included forced expression and antibody‐mediated inhibition of L1CAM. Adhesion‐clutch behavior and retrograde F‐actin flow were analyzed. For in vivo assessment, doxycycline‐inducible L1CAM‐overexpressing GBM cells were transplanted into mouse brains. L1CAM‐high GBM cells showed increased motility on laminin compared to L1CAM‐low cells. Anti‐L1CAM antibody suppressed migration of L1CAM‐high GBM cells, whereas ectopic expression enhanced migration in L1CAM‐low GBM cells. L1CAM‐positive GBM cells preferentially migrated on laminin rather than fibronectin, while L1CAM‐low cells showed no such difference, indicating L1CAM‐dependent haptotaxis. Forced L1CAM expression reduced retrograde actin flow velocity and promoted adhesion‐clutch formation. In vivo, GBM cells with L1CAM overexpression displayed greater infiltrative capacity 12 weeks posttransplantation. L1CAM expression on GBM cells' surface regulates cell motility through adhesion‐clutch mechanisms and substrate sensing. These mechanobiological features suggest that targeting L1CAM may serve as a promising strategy to suppress GBM cells infiltration and improve GBM treatment outcomes.Keywords: adhesion‐clutch, cell motility, glioblastoma, haptotaxis, L1CAM

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive neuroepithelial tumor characterized by heterogeneous malignant cells with diffuse infiltration. Despite surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, prognosis remains poor [1, 2, 3]. GBM's hallmark invasiveness arises from enhanced tumor cell migration, and although therapies such as the integrin inhibitor cilengitide have shown partial efficacy, no effective treatment suppresses this trait [4]. Therefore, novel strategies targeting GBM cell motility are needed.

L1CAM (L1, CD171), a neural cell adhesion molecule of the immunoglobulin superfamily, mediates neuronal adhesion critical for axon growth, guidance, and glial process formation [5, 6, 7, 8]. Pathogenic X‐linked L1CAM variants cause L1 syndrome, manifesting in hydrocephalus, corpus callosum agenesis, spastic paraplegia, and intellectual disability [9, 10, 11, 12]. Cancer cells often exploit developmental adhesion and migration pathways mediated by molecules like L1CAM [13, 14]. L1CAM is aberrantly expressed in many cancers, correlating with poor prognosis and enhanced motility and metastasis, making it a promising therapeutic target [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

Some GBM patient samples show high L1CAM expression [22]. In glioma cell lines (rat C6, 9L; human U‐87), L1CAM knockdown or antibody blockade reduces cell motility, suggesting its involvement in migration [23, 24]. GBM cells derived under serum‐free conditions form neurospheres, self‐renew, and exhibit high proliferation [25]. They contribute to tumor initiation, heterogeneity, progression, relapse, and resistance [26]. CD133(+) GBM express higher L1CAM levels than normal progenitors. L1CAM knockdown in CD133(+) GBM impairs neurosphere formation, induces apoptosis, inhibits growth in vitro, suppresses invasion, and prolongs survival in vivo, indicating its essential role in GBM invasion and survival [27].

Previous studies have examined GBM cell motility using human glioma cell lines, such as T98G or U‐87, and nonhuman models, such as rat C6 glioma cells [23, 24, 27, 28]. However, these cell lines were generated and maintained under conventional serum‐containing and adherent monolayer culture conditions, which do not preserve the cellular heterogeneity, stemness, or invasive potential observed in patient tumors [29]. Consequently, they do not accurately recapitulate the infiltrative behavior of GBM in vivo. Hence, although L1CAM has been implicated in tumor cell motility, our understanding of its role in GBM migration, especially with regard to mechanobiological properties at the single‐cell level, is limited.

In this study, we addressed these limitations by employing patient‐derived GBM cells cultured under neurosphere‐forming, serum‐free conditions that better preserve GBM cell characteristics. We investigated the role of L1CAM in cell migration at the single‐cell level in vitro and evaluated the invasive behavior in vivo. By focusing on clinically relevant GBM models and employing single‐cell tracking approaches, we aimed to clarify the contribution of L1CAM to GBM cell motility in a physiologically relevant manner. Importantly, our study highlights the mechanobiological aspects of GBM cell migration, revealing that L1CAM modulates actin dynamics and cell motility through its extracellular domain, suggesting that its role in invasion is driven by cell surface interactions. This extracellular localization makes L1CAM an accessible and actionable therapeutic target, potentially allowing the development of antibody‐based strategies to block its function and suppress GBM invasiveness.

Material and Methods

Materials and Methods Detailed experimental procedures for most assays are provided in the Supporting Information (Data S1).

Cell Culture

Human glioma‐derived cell lines (GDC40, GDC543, KNBTG‐8, KNBTG‐545) [30, 31] established using the neurosphere method were used in this study.

Reverse Transcription‐Quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR)

The cells were lysed in QIAzol Lysis Reagent, and RNA was extracted using the chloroform and isopropanol precipitation method. qPCR analysis was performed using gene‐specific primers (Table S1) [32].

Western Blot Analyses

The cells were lysed with cell lysis buffer. Next, 10 μg of protein from each sample was separated using SDS‐PAGE and transferred electrophoretically onto PVDF membranes. Quantification was performed based on previously established methods [33].

Flow Cytometric (FCM) Analyses

The cells were stained with primary and secondary antibodies (Table S2).

Immunocytochemical (ICC) Analysis

The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, and permeabilized and blocked with blocking buffer containing PBS, 0.1% Triton X‐100, and 10% normal goat serum. Following incubation, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies (Table S2) [32].

DNA Constructs

Full‐length and mutant L1CAM ORFs were cloned into IRES‐hrGFP or Tet‐One inducible vectors using standard PCR and Infusion Cloning methods (Figure S1A,B). These constructs are referred to as Full, Truncate, Deletion, and EXP.

DNA Transfection

Cells were dissociated using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA, and then subjected to electroporation using the super electroporator NEPA 21. The procedures were performed as described previously.

Single‐Cell Tracking Analysis

A 96‐well plate or glass‐bottom dish was coated with laminin at 2 μg/cm2 or fibronectin. These procedures were based on previously established methods [34], and cultured in the IncuCyte ZOOM live imaging system at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator (Figure S1C) [35, 36].

Antibody Blocking Assay

An antibody‐mediated L1CAM‐blocking assay (Table S2) was employed as a proof‐of‐concept approach to evaluate the role of L1CAM in substrate‐dependent cell motility.

Cell Cycle Analysis

The cells were cultured for 24 h and stained with DAPI. Cell cycle analysis, based on the quantification of DNA content in each cell, was performed using the companion program of the high‐content screening system (ArrayScan XTI HCA Reader, Thermo Fisher Scientific). This analysis was performed using a built‐in cell cycle assay protocol of the HCS Studio software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell Proliferation Analysis

The total ATP content in viable cells was evaluated using an Intracellular ATP Assay Kit ver.2 (TOYO B‐Net, Tokyo, Japan), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Luminescence was measured using an ARVO X5 Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantification was performed using previously established methods [32].

Quantification of F‐Actin Flow Velocity

F‐actin flow velocity was quantified as previously described [34, 37, 38]. Timelapse images were acquired every 5 s using a confocal microscope with a 100× objective lens.

Intracranial Orthotopic Transplantation of GDCs

Intracranial orthotopic transplantation of GDCs was performed in adult NSG mice (Charles River, Wilmington, Massachusetts, USA) under isoflurane anesthesia. DOX‐responsive L1CAM‐expressing GDC543 (EXP) cells were stereotactically injected into the right hemisphere, and DOX was administered in drinking water throughout the 12‐week posttransplantation period. Mice were euthanized, and brains were processed for histopathological analysis.

Histopathological Analysis

After 12 weeks posttransplantation, histopathological analyses were performed using H&E staining and immunohistochemistry. The sliced sections were deparaffinized and stained with an antihuman cytoplasmic marker antibody (Table S2) using a Leica Bond‐Max Autostainer. The samples were examined using a Nanozoomer S60 [32].

Genome‐Wide DNA Methylation and CNV Analysis

Patient‐derived cells and tissues were analyzed for DNA methylation using the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylationEPIC BeadChip. For molecular classification and copy number variation (CNV) analysis, the HEIDELBERG EPIGNOSTIX classifier (v12.8) was applied [39].

RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics

Total RNA was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform.

Clinical Survival Analysis

The prognostic impact of L1CAM was evaluated using TCGA‐GBM datasets via the TCGAbiolinks package [40].

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) [41]. Significant differences between the groups were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis, and t‐tests, and the Steel–Dwass test was used for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. To evaluate the clinical significance of L1CAM expression.

Results

L1CAM Is Selectively Expressed on the Surface of Patient‐Derived GBM Cells

To assess L1CAM expression in GBM, we first examined its expression in GBM cells from two patients, GDC40 and GDC543, which were established using the neurosphere culture method. We evaluated L1CAM gene expression in two GDC cell lines and their corresponding parental brain tumor tissues. In GDC40 and its original tumor tissue, L1CAM was highly expressed without significant differences, indicating that the expression characteristics of L1CAM were preserved between the tissue and the derived cell line. Conversely, the parental tissue of GDC543 exhibited significantly higher L1CAM expression than the GDC543 cell line; however, its overall expression remained notably lower than that of either the GDC40 tissue or cell line. GDC543 cells showed lower L1CAM expression than their parental tissue, which was lower than GDC40 tissue and cells (Figure 1A).

Consistent with the L1CAM transcript expression pattern, western blot analysis showed that L1CAM protein expression levels were significantly higher in GDC40 but lower in GDC543 (Figure 1B,C). FCM analysis showed that 95.7% of GDC40 expressed L1CAM on their cell surface, whereas < 1% of GDC543 expressed L1CAM (Figure 1D).

To confirm L1CAM expression in 2D adherent culture, we cultured the two GDCs for 3 days on laminin‐coated glass, an extracellular matrix (ECM) component to which L1CAM binds, and evaluated L1CAM expression using ICC staining. Similar to sphere‐forming cells, L1CAM was expressed in 87.6% ± 3.55% of GDC40, and no GDC543 cells (mean ± SD; Figure 1E,F). These three adherent cell types also highly expressed SOX2, a neural stem cell marker.

These findings showed that L1CAM is expressed on the cell surface of some SOX2‐expressing patient‐derived GBM cells. L1CAM expression was preserved in both anchorage‐independent (sphere‐forming cells) and anchorage‐dependent (2D‐adherent cells) culture conditions (Figure 1C,F).

L1CAM Expression Determines GBM Cell Migration on Laminin

Cell motility can be directed by extracellular substrate‐bound chemicals, and the association between the cell surface molecules and ECMs influences GBM cells' mobility [37]. We cultured GBM cells on two ECMs: laminin, which binds L1CAM, and fibronectin, a high‐molecular‐weight ECM glycoprotein that binds integrins, and examined GBM cells' movement at the single‐cell level. GDC40 (L1CAM‐high) cells exhibited a significantly higher migration velocity and distance on laminin than on fibronectin (Figure 2). Conversely, GDC543 (L1CAM‐low) cells migrated on both matrixes with equivalent mobility (Figure 2). On laminin, GDC40 showed higher activity than GDC543 (Figure 2A,B), while no significant difference was observed on fibronectin (Figure 2A,B). These data suggest that the migration properties of GBM cells depend on L1CAM expression and the presence of its adhesive ligand, laminin.

These findings suggest that L1CAM is associated with enhanced GBM cells migration (Figure 3A,B). Quantitative analysis showed that both migration velocity and farthest distance were significantly higher in GDC40 than in GDC543, suggesting a positive correlation between mobility on laminin and L1CAM levels. These results were consistently observed under different experimental conditions: in Figure 3A,B, 10 cells were tracked for 24 h after seeding at 1 × 105 cells/mL, whereas in Figure 3C,D, 20 cells were tracked for 24 h after seeding at 5 × 104 cells/mL. We performed a blocking assay using an anti‐L1CAM antibody to clarify L1CAM dependency of tumor cell migration (Figure 3C,D and Figure S1D). GDC40 cell mobility was suppressed by the antibody, and both migration velocity and farthest distance were significantly decreased by > 1 μg/mL of anti‐L1CAM antibody. The antibody had no effect on the movement of GDC543 cells.

To further substantiate the robustness of our findings, we expanded our analysis to include an additional pair of patient‐derived GBM cell lines: KNBTG‐8 and KNBTG‐545. Differential L1CAM expression was confirmed by RT‐PCR (Figure S2A) and western blotting (Figure S2B,C). Flow cytometry exhibited high L1CAM positivity in KNBTG‐8 (30.1%) and low KNBTG‐545 (0.34%; Figure S2D). Immunocytochemistry under adherent conditions confirmed consistent L1CAM expression and stem‐like characteristics (SOX2 positivity exceeding 80%; Figure S2E,F). Consistent with the observations from GDC 40 and GDC543 lines, single‐cell tracking analysis demonstrated greater motility in L1CAM‐high KNBTG‐8 than in L1CAM‐low KNBTG‐545 (Figure S3A–D).

L1CAM Overexpression Enhances GBM Cell Motility

To examine L1CAM's influence on GBM cells, we overexpressed full‐length wild‐type L1CAM in GDC543 (L1CAM‐low) cells and assessed its impact using single‐cell tracking analysis. With ~10% transduction efficiency, only GFP‐positive cells were analyzed by FCM. Tracking was performed exclusively on GFP‐positive single cells. After transduction (Figure 4A), L1CAM was expressed in the infected cells and detected on the cell surface (Figure 4A,B, Figure S4A,B), and cell mobility—migration velocity and farthest distance—significantly increased (Figure 4C). This enhancement was suppressed by an anti‐L1CAM antibody, as in GDC40, reverting migration to control levels (Figure 4D). To further evaluate wild‐type L1CAM's role in cell–substrate adhesion and migration, we transfected two loss‐of‐function L1CAM variants identified in patients with X‐linked severe hydrocephalus [11, 12]: a deletion variant (c.817‐819delACC, p.273delT; Figure 4A) that reduces L1CAM extracellular domain (ECD) interaction with laminin [42], and a pathogenic splice‐site variant (c.694 + 5 G>A, p.T232fs26: Figure 4A) [12] that causes aberrant RNA splicing of the L1CAM transcript, resulting in a truncating‐mutation (Figure S1A). Immunohistochemical and FCM analyses showed that the 273delT deletion form was expressed on the GDC543 cell surface, whereas the splice‐site truncating‐mutation was not expressed (Figure 4B). Single‐cell tracking analysis revealed that transduction of these loss‐of‐function mutations did not increase GDC543 cell migration (Figure 3C,D), confirming the requirement of the L1CAM–laminin interaction for enhanced cell motility.

Consistent with the results observed in the GDC lines, subsequent single‐cell tracking analysis showed that the L1CAM‐high line (KNBTG‐8) exhibited significantly greater motility than the L1CAM‐low line (KNBTG‐545) (Figure S3A–D).

L1CAM expression on GBM cells was associated with stemness and cell proliferation maintenance [27]. Proliferation assays in GDC40 and GDC543 demonstrated consistent growth rates, regardless of L1CAM antibody exposure (Figure 4E). To assess whether L1CAM influences stemness or proliferation, SOX2+ GBM cell numbers were examined post‐transduction in GDC543, showing no change with wild‐type or loss‐of‐function L1CAM (Figure 4F). Cell cycle analysis revealed comparable G0/G1, S, and G2/M distributions across all L1CAM forms (Figure 4G). These results indicate that surface‐expressed wild‐type L1CAM enhances GBM cell motility without affecting stemness or proliferation, validating its membrane‐associated role independent of endogenous regulation.

Forced L1CAM Expression Enhances Adhesion‐Clutch

We recently reported that L1CAM on a human glioma‐derived cell line, KNBTG‐8, binds to the ECM (e.g., laminin) outside the cell and connects it to the F‐actin retrograde flow inside the cell via a clutch molecule (Figure S4C) [37]. This process is called adhesion‐clutch [37]. The adhesion‐clutch enables L1CAM to fulfill its physiological function in cell migration, decreasing the speed of F‐actin retrograde flow and generating traction forces [43].

To evaluate the association between actin flow and L1CAM expression on GBM cells, we created a DOX‐inducible L1CAM overexpression system‐transduced GDC543 cells (EXP), labeled actin in the EXPs using HaloTag, and analyzed the actin dynamics in L1CAM expressed on GBM cells at the single‐molecule level. With DOX treatment, EXP expressed the L1CAM transcript (Figure 5A) and full‐length L1CAM on the cell surface (Figure 5B–E), significantly enhancing cell migration on laminin (Figure 5F), suggesting that DOX induced functional L1CAM on the EXP cell surface. With DOX treatment, the actin flow speed in EXP decreased significantly compared to that in cells without DOX treatment (Figure 6A,B). These findings indicate that L1CAM transduced in GDC543 (L1CAM‐low) cells forms a proper adhesion‐clutch, contributing to enhanced cell migration.

Forced L1CAM Expression Enhances GBM Cell ‐Driven In Vivo Tumorigenesis

We investigated the contribution of L1CAM expressed on GBM cells to in vivo tumorigenesis using an orthotopic transplantation mouse model (Figure S4D). After 12 weeks, L1CAM overexpression system‐transduced GDC543 cells (EXP) transplanted in the right brain hemisphere of NSG mice engrafted and formed tumor masses. Tumor masses were detected in both groups; however, tumor size differed significantly based on whether DOX was administered (Figure 7A, Figure S4E). The transfected cells expressed L1CAM in the presence of DOX, but not in its absence (Figure 7B). The tumor area detected using human‐specific STEM121 antibody was significantly larger with DOX administration than without (Figure 7C). Although L1CAM expression did not significantly affect cell proliferation in vitro, we interpreted its DOX‐induced expression as primarily contributing to enhanced cell motility and tumor engraftment in vivo. STEM121‐positive tumor cells were confined to narrow areas without DOX, whereas they were detected significantly more widely and distantly with DOX treatment (Figure 7A,C,D). These findings indicated that L1CAM promoted both in vivo cell migration and survivability of GBM cells and could be involved in tumor formation and invasion within brain tissue.

To assess the impact of L1CAM expression on tumor cell proliferation in vivo, we examined the MIB‐1 (Ki‐67) index within transplanted tumors. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated the presence of Ki‐67‐positive cells in both DOX(+) and DOX(−) groups (Figure 7E). Quantitative analysis revealed that the MIB‐1 index was consistently maintained at approximately 10% across both the L1CAM‐induced DOX(+) and control DOX(−) groups, with no statistically significant difference observed between the groups (Figure 7F).

L1CAM as an Indicator of Poor Prognosis in Patients With GBM

To evaluate the clinical significance of L1CAM expression, we examined its association with patient prognosis using publicly available data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [40]. patients were stratified into L1CAM‐high (n = 44) and L1CAM‐low (n = 224) groups, a 20.824 TPM threshold. L1CAM transcript differed markedly between the two groups (p < 2.2 × 10−16; Figure S5A). Kaplan–Meier analysis indicated that individuals in the L1CAM‐high group exhibited significantly shorter overall survival than those in the L1CAM‐low group (p = 0.00022, log‐rank test; Figure S5B).

GDC Lines Derived From Patient Tumor Tissues Retain the Biological Characteristics of Their Respective Original Tumors

To assess the biological correspondence between patient tumor tissues and GDC cell lines, we performed DNA methylation profiling and copy number alteration (CNA) analysis. Employing the molecular classification framework established by Capper et al. [39], both GDC40 and its parental tumor tissue were classified as glioblastoma, IDH‐wildtype, RTK2 subtype, and demonstrated close proximity in t‐SNE analysis (Figure 8, Figure S6). Further CNA assessment indicated that both samples exhibited highly similar profiles (Figure S6), suggesting that GDC40 retains biological features comparable to its source tissue. Conversely, the t‐SNE analysis mapped both GDC543 and its parental tumor tissue within the glioblastoma, IDH‐wildtype, mesenchymal subtype cluster (Figure 8). While the parental tissue was classified as a mesenchymal subtype, GDC543 exhibited similarities to the RTK2 subtype without fully aligning with any defined subtype. Although the parental tissue exhibited indications of CNAs, the extent was unclear, whereas GDC543 presented a more distinct CNA profile (Figure S6B).

The t‐SNE mapping showed all six model cell lines clustered within the GBM cell cluster, yet none corresponded to a specific subtype, and their CNA patterns appeared distinct from those observed in patient‐derived GBM samples.

GDC Lines Exhibit Distinct Gene Expression Profiles Based on L1CAM Expression

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying differences in invasive behavior, we performed integrated transcriptomic analyses of four patient‐derived GDC cell lines (GDC40, GDC543, KNBTG‐8, and KNBTG‐545). Genes associated with the gene ontology (GO) terms “cell migration” and “cell adhesion” were selected, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between L1CAM‐high and L1CAM‐low groups were identified. Hierarchical clustering (Figure 8B) and GO enrichment analyses (Figure 8C,D) revealed two distinct molecular profiles that delineate the invasive capacities of GBM cells. The L1CAM‐high group (GDC40, KNBTG‐8) exhibited pronounced enrichment of neurotropic invasion‐related genes (Set 1: L1CAM, NCAM2, BCAN, NRXN2, and HES5), suggesting these cells utilize the laminin‐rich brain parenchyma and neural networks as scaffolds. Conversely, the L1CAM‐low group (GDC543, KNBTG‐545) exhibited upregulation of integrin family members and adhesion‐related molecules (Set 2: CD44, ITGA1, ITGB5, POSTN, COL4A6, and VCL). The activation of the integrin–FAK signaling pathway supports the hypothesis that these cells adopt a vasculotropic invasion strategy along the perivascular ECM.

Discussion

L1CAM‐Mediated Molecular Clutch as a Primary Mechanical Driver of GBM Invasion

This study demonstrates that L1CAM is selectively expressed on the surface of patient‐derived GBM cells and serves as a primary mechanical driver of migration on laminin substrates. A distinct association exists between L1CAM expression levels and cellular motility: L1CAM‐high cell lines (GDC40 and KNBTG‐8) exhibit significantly higher migration velocity and distance than L1CAM‐low cell lines (GDC543 and KNBTG‐545). The migratory capacity is markedly diminished by anti‐L1CAM antibodies and requires intact extracellular‐domain interactions, as loss‐of‐function variants (273delT and splice‐site mutations) do not enhance motility. Mechanistic investigations provide novel physiological evidence that L1CAM modulates F‐actin retrograde flow velocities in GBM, establishing a functional “adhesion‐clutch” that generates the traction forces required for parenchymal invasion. In vivo orthotopic transplantation further confirms that DOX‐induced L1CAM expression results in substantial tumor expansion and distal infiltration without affecting the proliferative index (MIB‐1~10%). Transcriptomic and DNA methylation profiling indicated that L1CAM‐high cells utilize a neurotropic, L1CAM‐mediated clutch mechanism, whereas L1CAM‐low cells engage alternative integrin‐dependent perivascular pathways. Collectively, these findings, coupled with TCGA data linking elevated L1CAM expression to poor overall survival, establish the L1CAM‐mediated molecular clutch as a pivotal and clinically relevant therapeutic target for limiting GBM infiltration.

Endogenous L1CAM Promotes GBM Cell Migration via Neuron‐Like Mechanisms

In 1990s, Izumoto et al. reported that glioma cells expressed a short type of L1CAM (L1CAMcs), and that rat C6 glioma cells stimulated by soluble L1CAM and L1CAMcs released from L1CAM‐ or L1CAMcs‐transfected fibroblast cells showed migrative activity, which was significantly inhibited by antibody that recognizes the immunoglobulin C2‐like domain of L1CAM [44]. Tsuzuki et al. [22] found L1CAM in 33% of diffuse gliomas, correlating with TP53 mutation or TGF‐beta expression. Bao et al. [27] reported co‐expression of L1CAM and CD133 in gliomas, with higher L1CAM levels in CD133(+) glioma cells than in normal progenitors. Since the 2000s, the Galileo group has shown that L1CAM enhances glioma motility, suppressed by antisense vectors or ectodomain‐binding antibodies [45]. In T98G cells, ADAM10‐mediated L1CAM cleavage released ectodomains that promoted migration via integrin–FAK signaling [24]. Mohanan et al. [28] showed that L1CAM and its ectodomain act via fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs) to increase motility and proliferation, which were blocked by dual inhibition of L1CAM and FGFR. Anderson and Galileo [46] found that inhibitors of FGFR, FAK, or integrins selectively suppressed L1CAM‐mediated motility in L1CAM‐positive cells. These studies implicate L1CAM in GBM infiltration through multiple pathways.

While prior studies used nonhuman or serum‐cultured cell lines (e.g., C6, T98G, U‐87), which often lack stemness, patient‐derived cells from serum‐free cultures are considered more appropriate models [29].

We confirmed L1CAM surface expression in SOX2‐positive patient‐derived GBM cells under both sphere‐forming and adherent conditions (Figure 1). DNA methylation profiling combined with t‐SNE analysis confirmed the clinical relevance of our models, demonstrating that GDC lines consistently retained molecular characteristics of their parental tumor tissues (Figure 8). Specifically, GDC40 maintained its RTK2 (Classic) subtype profile, while GDC543 reflected the intrinsic L1CAM‐low profile observed in its original mesenchymal tumor tissue (Figure 8, Figure S6). In contrast to commonly used GBM cell lines—which frequently exhibit distinct CNA patterns and do not correspond to specific subtypes (Figure S7)—our patient‐derived cells more accurately preserve the original biological signatures. These findings support the conclusion that the L1CAM‐mediated invasion mechanisms identified in this study are consistent with those observed in patient tumor pathology.

Single‐cell tracking showed that GBM cells' motility correlated with L1CAM expression and was suppressed by anti‐L1CAM antibody (Figure 3). GBM cells with full‐length L1CAM exhibited increased mobility on laminin (Figures 2 and 4), and forced expression enhanced migration both in vitro and in vivo (Figures 5 and 6). These results, consistent with previous glioma cell line studies, suggest that surface L1CAM contributes to GBM cells' motility and GBM infiltration in vivo.

L1CAM‐high GBM cells (GDC40) showed high mobility on laminin, while L1CAM‐low GBM cells (GDC543) gained this ability upon forced expression. L1CAM mediates haptotaxis of axonal growth cones by sensing adhesive substrates [42, 47]. Similarly, GBM cells expressing L1CAM underwent haptotaxis on laminin (Figure 2). In neurons, L1CAM reduces retrograde actin flow at growth cones [37]. GBM cells expressing L1CAM also showed reduced actin flow (Figure 5), indicating functional coupling with actin, as seen in neurons. This transmembrane coupling facilitates the efficient transmission of intracellular contractile forces—arising from F‐actin polymerization and myosin motor activity—to the extracellular laminin. Through this process of mechanical transduction, retrograde flow energy is converted into effective forward protrusion, thereby driving the rapid dissemination of GBM cells within the brain parenchyma. These findings suggest that surface L1CAM in GBM cells preserves neuronal‐like migratory mechanisms and mediates haptotaxis.

In the healthy brain, laminin and fibronectin are primarily confined to the vascular basement membranes. However, within the GBM microenvironment, their expression and spatial distribution are extensively altered. Notably, fibronectin is markedly upregulated and deposited not only in the perivascular niche but also along the invasive edges of GBM tumors, acting as a key scaffold for haptotactic migration [48, 49]. This fibronectin enrichment, combined with the laminin‐rich vascular basement membranes targeted by L1CAM, forms an essential physical foundation that enables widespread tumor infiltration. Therefore, studying GBM cell motility on these ECM components is important for understanding the varied migration patterns that occur during parenchymal invasion and perivascular dispersal.

L1CAM Involvement in GBM May Be Context‐Dependent

Lentiviral shRNA targeting of L1CAM in CD133(+) glioma cells strongly disrupts neurosphere formation, induces apoptosis, and inhibits stem cell growth in vitro. In vivo, L1CAM knockdown suppresses tumor growth and prolongs survival, indicating its role in CD133(+) cell maintenance [27, 37]. However, in our analyses, L1CAM expression on GBM cells did not significantly affect stemness or proliferation (Figure 4). This discrepancy may reflect L1CAM's multifunctionality. As an immunoglobulin superfamily cell adhesion molecule, full‐length L1CAM mediates functions including haptotaxis via homophilic and heterophilic binding [8, 50]. We confirmed that full‐length L1CAM preserves cell mobility. L1CAM (200–220 kDa) is proteolytically cleaved by various proteinases, generating soluble ectodomains that contribute to functional diversity [7, 51]. These soluble forms may regulate GBM cells' maintenance and proliferation. Cheng et al. reported that L1CAM enhances GBM cells' radioresistance via its intracellular domain, promoting DNA damage checkpoint activation through NBS1 upregulation and MRE11‐RAD50‐NBS1–ATM–Chk2 signaling, highlighting the intracellular domain's role in proliferation [52, 53]. These findings suggest that L1CAM may contribute to GBM stemness through complex molecular mechanisms that warrant further study.

L1CAM‐Independent Cell Migration Also Occurs in GBM

In addition to the role of L1CAM in cell mobility, our results revealed L1CAM‐independent mobility in GBM. Both GDC40 (L1CAM‐high) and GDC543 (L1CAM‐low) cells attached to fibronectin‐coated culture flasks and migrated without significant differences (Figure 2). Anti‐L1CAM antibody suppressed GDC40 mobility; however, it could not completely block cell migration on laminin, and minor movement, as observed on fibronectin, persisted. These findings suggest that GBM mobility is controlled by at least two molecular mechanisms: L1CAM and L1CAM‐independent systems.

Other molecules are thought to be involved in this system and integrin‐mediated cell migration may be representative among them. Our RNA‐seq and GO enrichment analyses (Figure 8B–D) support this divergence in invasive strategies. In L1CAM‐high lines, the enrichment of genes such as NCAM2 and BCAN (Set 1) suggests a preference for L1CAM‐mediated neurotropic migration along the brain parenchyma. Conversely, L1CAM‐low lines are enriched with integrins and collagen‐related factors (Set 2, e.g., ITGA1, ITGB5, COL4A6), indicating an alternative integrin‐dependent perivascular invasion mode. These results demonstrate that GBM cells differentially utilize these molecular networks depending on their L1CAM status and the surrounding ECM, such as laminin versus fibronectin. The GBM mobility mechanism involves various players, necessitating approaches that target multiple pathways simultaneously in the development of future therapeutic strategies.

L1CAM Is a Viable Target for Modulating GBM Infiltration

L1CAM is overexpressed in various malignancies and is implicated in tumor invasion and metastasis [13, 20, 21, 54]. Survival analysis using the TCGA database demonstrates that high L1CAM expression correlates strongly with shortened overall survival in patients with GBM (Figure S5B). Hence, L1CAM is a promising target molecule for regulating the infiltrative or metastatic properties of tumor cells, including GBM cells. Our findings support these concepts and suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting the infiltrative properties of GBM and more differentiated GBM cells by interfering with the L1CAM‐mediated molecular pathway may represent a promising treatment approach for GBM.

The potential of anti‐L1CAM antibody therapy has been investigated in noncentral nervous system malignancies, including ovarian and endometrial cancers. Preclinical studies have demonstrated its capacity to markedly inhibit tumor proliferation and metastasis [55]. Although UJ127.11 has not yet undergone clinical testing, other anti‐L1CAM antibodies, such as the chimeric antibody chCE7, have undergone preclinical and preliminary clinical assessments in various cancers, including ovarian and endometrial carcinomas and neuroblastoma [56, 57]. Data from the Human Protein Atlas [58] confirm that L1CAM is highly expressed in normal brain tissues. Although pharmacological inhibition in adults is generally considered more tolerable than germline deficiency, which may indicate a therapeutic window [56, 59], a rigorous evaluation of this window is required for GBM. Furthermore, the limited permeability of the blood–brain barrier poses a significant challenge to the efficacy of antibody‐based therapies targeting brain tumors. To develop more effective in vivo approaches, alternative strategies targeting intracellular molecules implicated in L1CAM function may be considered. Notably, the L1CAM intracellular domain interacts with multiple molecular partners at the axonal growth cone under physiological conditions, coupling actin flow through the “molecular clutch” mechanism [34, 42, 47, 60]. These molecular systems and associated factors may be viable candidates for modulating L1CAM function and are expected to inform innovative strategies for controlling GBM tumor infiltration capacity.

Although previous research has implicated L1CAM in GBM stemness in certain contexts, our findings revealed no substantial effect on cell proliferation or stemness markers in patient‐derived models. This observation reflects the “Go or Grow” (migration–proliferation) dichotomy, wherein these GDCs, characterized by high baseline proliferative activity (MIB‐1 ~ 10%), utilize L1CAM as a specialized mechanical driver for migration rather than as a mitogenic signal. Importantly, our study provides the first mechanobiological evidence that L1CAM directly regulates F‐actin retrograde flow velocity, which accounts for approximately 50% of total migratory activity, via a functional “adhesion‐clutch.” While integrin‐mediated pathways also contribute significantly to GBM motility and tumor heterogeneity [61, 62], the L1CAM‐mediated molecular clutch delineates a distinct, major mechanical mechanism. Therapeutically targeting this infiltrative apparatus presents a promising approach for mitigating diffuse parenchymal invasion, which drives clinical recurrence.

In conclusion, we found that GBM migration was dependent on L1CAM both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, L1CAM expression reduced the actin flow rate, indicating the presence of a “molecular clutch” linking L1CAM to actin. An antibody against L1CAM blocked GBM migration. These findings suggest that antibody‐based therapy is a promising candidate for treating GBM.
