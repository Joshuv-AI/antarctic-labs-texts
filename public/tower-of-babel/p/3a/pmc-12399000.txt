Epidemiology of acute pancreatitis: the influence of age and gender in a regional population
Authors: Serdar Akca, Serkan Ocal, Sevgi Gulsen Koc, Recep Kaya, Osman Cagin Buldukoglu, Gokhan Koker, Galip Egemen Atar, Ferda Akbay Harmandar, Ayhan Hilmi Cekin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pancreatitis (AP) is a common and potentially severe inflammatory condition of the pancreas with a heterogeneous etiology influenced by geographic, demographic, and lifestyle factors. This study aimed to evaluate the distribution of AP etiologies and their associations with age and sex in a regional cohort from a tertiary center.Materials and methodsThis retrospective observational study included 445 adult patients hospitalized with a first episode of AP between January 2022 and October 2023. Data on demographics, etiology, severity (revised Atlanta classification), hospital course, and outcomes were extracted from medical records. Etiologies were classified as biliary, idiopathic, alcohol-related, drug-induced, hypertriglyceridemia (HTG), neoplasm-associated, or other miscellaneous causes. Age was grouped into five categories, and statistical analyses assessed the association between etiologies and demographic variables.ResultsThe median age was 65 years (range: 18–97), and 52.1% were female. Biliary etiology was the most frequent (66.1%), followed by idiopathic (9.4%), alcohol-related (7.0%), drug-induced (6.5%), neoplasm-associated (4.3%), and HTG-induced (3.1%). Females were significantly older than males (median 67 vs. 60 years, p < 0.001). Alcohol-related AP (AAP) was more common in males, particularly under age 55. Notably, among biliary AP (BAP) patients, males predominated in those under 55 (p = 0.02). Etiological patterns varied significantly by age. In patients aged ≥ 75 years, BAP was markedly more common (80.4% vs. 61.8%), whereas alcohol, drug, and HTG-related etiologies were rare. Severity of AP correlated with age and length of stay but not with etiology.ConclusionBiliary pancreatitis remains the predominant etiology in this region, especially among elderly patients. A novel finding of male predominance in younger BAP cases warrants further investigation. These data underscore the importance of regional age and sex specific approaches to AP prevention and management.Keywords: Acute pancreatitis, Etiology, Age, Gender, Regional

Introduction

AP represents an acute inflammatory condition of the pancreas [1]. It poses a significant global health challenge due to its rising incidence, potential for severe complications, and substantial healthcare burden [2]. AP is one of the most common gastrointestinal diseases requiring hospitalization in our center. A multitude of factors can trigger AP, including gallstones, alcohol abuse, hypertriglyceridemia, certain medications, post-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, hypercalcemia, infections, trauma, autoimmune diseases, anatomical abnormalities, neoplasms, and rare causes. When a definitive etiology cannot be identified, the condition is classified as idiopathic [3]. The distribution of these etiologies varies considerably based on geographic region and socioeconomic status, adding complexity to the understanding and management of AP [3–5].

Defining the demographics and understanding the varied etiologies across different age and sex groups, as well as the associated outcomes of AP, is paramount for developing tailored diagnosis and management policies to improve patient prognosis. Conflicting findings regarding the prevalence of diverse etiological factors underscore the need for further research in regional populations. Furthermore, the influence of age and sex on the etiology of AP remains an ongoing subject of investigation. While some studies suggest minimal impact, others report significant associations [6–10]. These discrepancies highlight the importance of regionally examining the interplay between demographics and AP etiology. This study aimed to assess the effect of age and gender on the distribution of AP etiologies and related clinical outcomes within Southwestern Turkey, which may provide insights to inform personalized diagnostic and management strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This is a retrospective, observational, hospitalized patient case series study. The study population included all patients under the care of the gastroenterology department who were diagnosed with AP either upon admission or during their hospitalization and remained in the hospital for at least 48 h. Patients under the age of 18 and those with recurrent AP were excluded from the study to ensure a focus on the adult population experiencing new-onset AP. For patients with AP who were discharged and subsequently re-hospitalized, only data from their initial hospitalization was considered to maintain the independence of data points and avoid skewing the analysis with repeated episodes from the same individuals.

The comprehensive medical records maintained by the gastroenterology department at Antalya Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary care facility equipped to handle complex medical cases with an inpatient capacity of 1,200 beds serving the city of Antalya and surrounding districts with a population of 2.6 million, served as the data source. The study period spanned from January 2022 to October 2023. AP was diagnosed based on the Atlanta criteria, which require the presence of at least two of the following three indicators: typical abdominal pain indicative of pancreatic inflammation, elevation of serum amylase or lipase levels to more than three times the normal upper limit, and characteristic imaging findings of AP [1].

Detailed medical histories, laboratory results, and imaging studies were reviewed to determine the etiology of AP. Data were initially collected by three internal medicine physicians. The etiological classification was subsequently verified by three senior gastroenterologists. All available clinical, biochemical, radiological, and endoscopic data were independently evaluated, and final etiological assignments were made by consensus.

Biliary acute pancreatitis (BAP) was diagnosed in the presence of gallstones or biliary sludge on imaging and/or elevated liver enzymes, particularly alanine aminotransferase (ALT) > 150 U/L, in line with established guidelines [11]. In cases with suspected biliary etiology but inconclusive findings on abdominal ultrasonography, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) was routinely used in our clinic to support the diagnosis. Alcohol-related acute pancreatitis (AAP) was defined based on a documented history of sustained or binge alcohol intake. HTG induced AP (HTG-AP) was diagnosed in patients with serum triglyceride levels > 1000 mg/dL in the absence of other etiologies. Drug induced AP (DIAP) was considered in patients with recent use of medications, herbals or supplements associated with AP, following exclusion of more common causes. Neoplasm associated AP was diagnosed when pancreatic or periampullary tumors were identified as the cause of ductal obstruction or inflammation. Patients in whom no etiology could be determined despite thorough evaluation were classified as having idiopathic AP (IAP). This structured classification approach aimed to enhance diagnostic accuracy and ensure consistency across the study population.

Individualized treatment plans were provided to all patients, which typically consisted of hydration with lactated Ringer’s solution to maintain fluid balance, effective pain control, interventions to address nausea, and the introduction of early oral feeding as tolerated, reflecting a comprehensive and patient-centric approach.

Age and sex of the patients and etiology of AP recorded. Additionally, we collected the revised Atlanta classification severity index (RAC) for each patient [1]. The presence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, length of stay (LOS) in hospital, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and in-hospital mortality were also recorded. Post-discharge mortality was calculated only for patients who were re-hospitalized in any ward or emergency service and died within 3 months of the index admission’s discharge.

Patients were stratified into five age groups based on categories used in the APACHE II scoring system (under 45, 45–54,55–64,65–74, equal or above 75 years old) [12].

Statistical analysis

Data were collected in Excel format and analyzed using SPSS software (version 27, IBM Corp.). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or ranges, depending on the data distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, based on distributional assumptions. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, as appropriate. Associations between ordinal or continuous variables were evaluated using Kendall’s tau-b, Spearman’s rho, or Pearson’s correlation coefficients.

Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors while adjusting for potential confounding variables. In the subgroup of patients with BAP, three multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to explore the associations of sex and age with laboratory and demographic parameters, and to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 445 patients with AP were included in the analysis. The cohort consisted of 213 males (47.9%) and 232 females (52.1%), with a median age of 65 years (range: 18–97). BAP was the most common cause of pancreatitis (66.1%), followed by IAP (9.4%), AAP (7.0%), DIAP (6.5%), neoplasms (4.3%), HTG-AP (3.1%), and other miscellaneous etiologies (3.6%). Miscellaneous cases consisted of post endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, trauma, infection, pancreatic divisum, post-operative and hypercalcemia induced AP. The median length of hospital stay was 6 days (IQR: 5). ICU admission occurred in 4.9% of cases, in-hospital mortality was 1.8%, and three-month post-discharge mortality was 6.3%.

Etiology across sex and age groups

We analyzed the relationship between age, sex, and etiology in 445 patients with AP. For each etiology, we reported the median age. To determine the significance of the difference in distribution ratio of each etiology across age categories, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze the data. To assess the trends between age and each of the seven etiological categories of AP, separate linear regression analyses were performed using age as the independent variable, and binary-coded etiology variables (1 = presence, 0 = absence) as dependent outcomes. The significance of gender difference is analyzed by chi-square tests.

BAP was the most common subtype and predominantly observed in older individuals, with a median age of 65.5 years (IQR: 54–76). The distribution of biliary etiology varied significantly across age categories (p < 0.001), with a significant linear trend indicating increasing prevalence of biliary etiology with advancing age (p = 0.002). Additionally, biliary etiology was more common in females than males (56.8% vs. 43.2%), and this sex-based difference was statistically significant (p = 0.007).

IAP had a median age of 63.5 years (IQR: 43–71). The distribution of idiopathic etiology did not significantly differ across age categories (p = 0.151), and no significant linear trend with age was observed (p = 0.161). Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference in the proportion of idiopathic etiology between males and females (p = 0.314).

AAP presented at a significantly younger age, with a median age of 52 years (IQR: 40–60), and showed a strong decreasing trend with age (p < 0.001). This group displayed marked male predominance (96.8% male; p < 0.001).

DIAP had a median age of 65 years (IQR: 55–70) and showed significant variation in age distribution (p = 0.005). However, the linear-by-linear association test did not demonstrate a significant trend across age groups, suggesting that while the distribution of DIAP varies by age, there is no clear pattern of increase or decrease with advancing age (p = 0.980). There was no significant sex predominance in this group (51.7% male; p = 0.704), indicating a relatively balanced distribution between sexes compared to other etiologies. Among the 29 patients diagnosed with DIAP, the etiology was attributed to miscellaneous herbal products or dietary supplements in 10 cases, including St. John’s Wort, Nigella sativa, stinging nettle, and Momordica charantia. The remaining causes included azathioprine (n = 6), angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors (n = 3), glucagon like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (n = 3), thiazide diuretics (n = 2), metronidazole, tetracycline, valproic acid, oral contraceptives, and illicit drug use.

HTG-AP was observed in the youngest subgroup overall, with a median age of 45 years (IQR: 38–47), showing a significant decreasing age trend (p < 0.001). Although 64.3% of these patients were male, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.211), possibly due to limited sample size or confounding metabolic factors.

Neoplasm-related AP was found predominantly in older patients, with a median age of 70 years (IQR: 66–79), and increasing age trend was significant (p = 0.008). Despite a slightly higher proportion of males (57.9%), sex differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.371).

Other miscellaneous causes, which included fewer common etiologies, showed a broad age distribution (median age 68.5, IQR: 48–81) with no significant difference across age categories (p = 0.701) or trend (p = 0.552). Although only 25% of patients in this category were male, the sex difference did not reach significance (p = 0.076), possibly due to limited sample size. Altogether the results reflect the different pathophysiology and heterogeneous nature of this group.

Subgroup analysis of patients aged ≥ 75 years

Among the 445 patients with AP, 102 (22.9%) were aged ≥ 75 years, and 343 (77.1%) were < 75 years. Female predominance was more pronounced in the elderly group (61.8% vs. 49.3%, p = 0.032). The distribution of AP etiologies differed significantly between the two age groups (p < 0.001).

BAP was significantly more common in elderly patients (80.4% vs. 61.8%, p < 0.001). In contrast, AAP was rare in this group (1.0% vs. 8.7%, p = 0.004). DIAP and HTG-AP were not observed in patients aged ≥ 75 years (0.0% vs. 8.5%, p < 0.001 and 0.0% vs. 4.1%, p = 0.047, respectively).

The frequency of IAP was similar between groups (8.8% vs. 9.6%, p = 1.000). Neoplasm-associated AP showed an increasing trend with age, peaking in the 65–74 age group. However, due to this peak occurring before age 75, the prevalence did not differ significantly between the ≥ 75 and < 75 age groups (4.9% vs. 4.1%, p = 0.780).

These findings highlight a clear shift toward biliary etiology in older adults, while alcohol-related, drug-induced, and metabolic causes are more frequently observed in younger patients.

Severity

AP was classified as mild in 314 patients (70.6%), moderate in 111 (24.9%), and severe in 20 (4.5%). There was no significant difference in AP severity between male and female patients (p = 0.549). Similarly, no significant association was found between AP severity and its etiological subtypes (p = 0.382). However, a significant difference in severity was observed across age groups (p = 0.038). Post hoc analysis using the Bonferroni-adjusted Mann–Whitney U test (adjusted α = 0.005) revealed that patients aged ≥ 75 years experienced significantly greater disease severity compared to those under 45 years of age. (p = 0.002) (Fig. 3).

Acute pancreatitis severity according to age categories

LOS in hospital increased in proportion to the severity of AP (p < 0.001). However, the etiology of pancreatitis did not appear to impact on the LOS in hospital.

In-hospital mortality

Eight patients (1.8%) died during hospitalization. The median age of the deceased patients was 73 years old (59–95). The patient died in the 4th day of hospitalization was 95 years old, and she died because of cardiovascular reasons. All other 7 patients were in moderately severe or severe AP groups, and they died of multi-organ failure (MOF) after ICU stay with a median of 30-day LOS.

In-hospital mortality was 0% in mild, 2.7% in moderately severe and 20% in severe pancreatitis cases classified according to RAC. A binary logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of in-hospital mortality among patients with APs. The model included age, etiology, and RAC as covariates. Age was independently associated with in-hospital mortality (p = 0.048), indicating that older age increases the risk of death during hospitalization. RAC was a significant predictor (p = 0.001). Compared to mild cases, patients with moderately severe and severe pancreatitis had 71.2 times higher odds of in-hospital death (OR = 71.2; 95% CI: 6.3–804.4; p < 0.001), Etiology was not a significant independent predictor (p = 0.393), and most subgroups had sparse or unstable estimates due to limited events.

Post-discharge mortality

The cumulative incidence of mortality within 90 days after discharge was 6.3% (28 patients) in our cohort. Most of these patients (78.6%) had experienced mild AP, and the median length of stay (LOS) was 6 days. The median age was 74.5 years (range: 57–97).

Among the 28 patients, 12 (42.9%) had neoplasm-associated AP, with a median age of 71.5 years (range: 66–81). Post-discharge mortality was significantly higher in the neoplasm-associated AP group compared to other etiologies (p < 0.001).

A separate logistic regression model, excluding patients who died during hospitalization, was performed to identify predictors of post-discharge mortality. Dependent variables were post discharge mortality, age, etiology and, severity were the variables included into the model. Age was independently associated with the outcome (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.06–1.17, p < 0.001), indicating that each one-year increase in age was associated with a 11.5% increase in odds. Neoplasm-associated etiology showed a strong and statistically significant association with the outcome (OR: 68.92, 95% CI: 5.56–854.35, p < 0.001). The Atlanta classification was not a statistically significant predictor in this model (OR: 0.408, 95% CI: 0.141–1.184, p = 0.104).

Among the remaining 16 patients who died post-discharge and were not in the neoplasm-associated AP group, the median age was 80 years (range: 57–97). Twelve of these patients had BAP, two had IAP, one had AAP and one had post-operative AP categorized under miscellaneous etiology. All patients had significant comorbidities. The causes of death were predominantly cardiovascular or unknown; none were directly attributed to AP recurrence or complications.

Subgroup analysis of patients with BAP

BAP was the most common etiology in both sexes. In the overall AP cohort, female participants were significantly older than male participants (median age 67 vs. 60 years, p < 0.001). To determine whether this age difference was driven solely by a higher prevalence of AAP in younger males, we conducted a subgroup analysis restricted to BAP. Among the total of 445 AP patients, 294 (66.1%) had BAP. Within this subgroup, females remained older than males (median age 67 vs. 63 years, p = 0.006).

Of the 294 BAP patients, 167 (56.8%) were female and 127 (43.2%) were male. When stratified by age, 77 patients (26.2%) were under 55 years old, and 217 patients (73.8%) were ≥ 55 years old. Among those under 55, 42 (54.5%) were male, whereas among those aged ≥ 55, 85 (39.2%) were male.

Crosstab analysis of BAP patients by age group and sex demonstrated a statistically significant difference (p = 0.023). BAP was more common in males under 55 years of age and more common in females aged ≥ 55 years, suggesting a potential age and sex related variation in the distribution of BAP within our cohort. To explore the observed male predominance among younger patients with BAP, we conducted three logistic regression models. In the first model patients were < 55 years old and dependent variable was sex. We included variables which have a p value under 0.2 in univariant analysis. Model identified smoking (OR: 6.631; 95% CI: 1.280–34.348; p = 0.024) and lower BMI (OR: 0.814; 95% CI: 0.665–0.998; p = 0.048) as independent predictors of male sex. HDL was not significantly associated with sex in this model. Second model is constructed for patients ≥ 55 years old, also used male sex as the dependent variable. In this group, male sex was independently associated with lower BMI (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.71–0.97, p = 0.020) and lower HDL levels (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.90–0.95, p < 0.001). Absence of HT was also a strong predictor of male sex (OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.17–0.70, p = 0.003). Although smoking had a positive association (OR = 2.31), it narrowly missed statistical significance (p = 0.053).

In the third model we examined predictors of being under the age of 55 years (dependent variable: age group < 55 vs. ≥55). In this binary logistic regression model, sex, BMI, HDL, smoking status, and HT were included as predictors. The results showed that male sex was independently associated with being in the younger age group, with an OR of 2.22 (95% CI: 1.09–4.54, p = 0.028), indicating that males were significantly more likely to be under 55 years of age. Additionally, higher BMI (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.13–1.47, p < 0.001) and the absence of HT (OR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.12–0.51, p < 0.001) were strongly associated with younger age. HDL levels and smoking status were not significantly associated with age category. These findings suggest that the male predominance observed in younger patients with BAP may be partially explained by demographic and clinical factors, particularly lower rates of hypertension and lower BMI in younger males.

These three complementary logistic regression models collectively suggest that the observed male predominance in younger patients with BAP may be partially explained by clinical and demographic factors, particularly lower BMI and a lower prevalence of HT in this subgroup. While smoking was a significant predictor of male sex in younger patients, it was not independently associated with age category, indicating that it does not fully account for the age related sex disparity. The influence of a distinct cardiovascular profile which is characterized by lower BMI and reduced rates of HT appears more common among younger males with BAP, also warrants further investigation.

Moreover, the male predominance in younger BAP patients may be influenced by additional unmeasured confounding factors such as dietary habits, lifestyle patterns, socioeconomic status, and other environmental or behavioral variables. These potential contributors should be explored in future studies to better understand the multifactorial nature of this demographic trend.

Discussion

In our cohort, biliary etiology was the most common cause of AP, accounting for a rate approximately six times higher than the next most common cause. This finding is consistent with prior studies from Turkey and Southern Europe, including Köksal et al., who reported a 67.1% biliary etiology rate among 2,144 Turkish patients [5, 13]. Similar trends have been observed in South America and other regions, confirming the global dominance of BAP in certain populations [2–4, 13–23].

Globally, AP etiology is influenced by sociocultural, dietary, genetic, and geographic factors [3, 16, 24, 25]. While biliary etiology predominates in some populations, AAP is the major cause in others, often associated with younger, predominantly male patients [3, 6, 16, 19–22, 25–27].

Consistent with prior literature, we found that BAP was more frequent in elderly females, likely due to the higher prevalence of gallstones in this group [16, 20, 22, 28, 29]. National data from Turkey indicate a female-to-male gallstone prevalence ratio of 1.37, closely mirrored by our BAP ratio of 1.32 [30]. However, a novel finding in our study was the male predominance in BAP among patients under 55 years of age. This pattern, not previously described in regional literature, may reflect lifestyle or referral factors specific to our tertiary center. Prior research from our institution on vitamin D status in AP showed a similar distribution, supporting the internal consistency of this finding [31]. Moreover, international studies from China, India, and Japan have also reported male predominance in BAP, indicating potential geographic variation [15, 19, 32].

Smoking may be a potential confounding factor for AP [33]. In our cohort, smoking was significantly associated with male sex in younger BAP patients, but multivariable analysis showed that it did not independently explain the age related sex disparity. Instead, our analysis suggests that the male predominance in younger BAP patients may be influenced by demographic and metabolic factors such as lower BMI and lower rates of HT. These findings, however, require confirmation in prospective studies. Due to the retrospective design, further exploration of this observation was limited. We have initiated a follow-up study focusing on the age and sex distribution of BAP. Additional factors such as biological, environmental, or healthcare access related variables may also contribute.

IAP was observed in 9% of patients, consistent with findings from other single-center and prospective studies, though lower than rates reported in national registries [3, 6]. The routine availability of MRCP, EUS, and careful clinical documentation likely contributed to the lower IAP rate. Although our diagnostic approach was thorough, we recognize that the utility of advanced diagnostics after a first AP episode must be balanced against cost-effectiveness.

AAP comprised 7% of our cases, predominantly among younger males, in line with existing literature and regional alcohol consumption patterns [27, 34]. HTG-AP was more common in patients under 55, consistent with metabolic syndrome and genetic lipid disorders [21]. Neoplasm-associated AP peaked in the 65–74 age group, reflecting the epidemiology of pancreatic malignancies in older adults [35, 36].

DIAP was observed in 6.5% of our cohort, a rate higher than those reported in the literature, which typically range between 1% and 5.3% [16, 37]. This elevated incidence may, in part, reflect the widespread use of traditional herbal remedies in our region, as well as a possible referral bias associated with our status as a tertiary care center. The retrospective design further restricted causal inference due to lack of precise temporal data and the inability to apply formal causality tools.

Striking age-related etiologic differences were observed. BAP was significantly more common in patients ≥ 75 years, while AAP, DIAP, and HTG-AP were more frequent in younger patients. These patterns emphasize the need for age stratified approaches in both diagnosis and management [9, 38, 39].

Although the primary aim was to explore etiologic trends, we also evaluated severity, LOS, and mortality. Some studies suggest AAP and HTG-AP may have more severe courses [8, 40]. In our cohort no significant association between etiology and severity was observed, likely due to the predominance of BAP and smaller size of other etiologies. Severity increased with age, particularly in patients over 75 years old. As expected, LOS correlated with AP severity [14, 38, 40].

In-hospital mortality was low (1.8%), with only 8 deaths during admission. Most of these occurred in patients with moderately severe or severe AP, often involving multi-organ failure. Our findings align with literature [14, 41, 42]. In contrast, post-discharge 90-day mortality was 6.3%, mainly among older patients due to neoplasms or cardiovascular events. Notably, many of these patients were initially classified as mild AP, highlighting the importance of monitoring post-discharge outcomes, particularly in elderly patients [43, 44].

This study is subject to several limitations. First, it represents a regional and exploratory analysis conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may introduce referral bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to broader or more diverse populations. Second, the retrospective design is inherently prone to bias due to missing, incomplete, or inconsistently recorded data. Third, the sample sizes of certain etiological subgroups (e.g., DIAP, HTG-AP, and neoplasm-associated AP) were relatively small, limiting the statistical power to detect meaningful differences between groups. Fourth, cases with multiple contributing etiologies were categorized according to the presumed dominant cause, which may have obscured the role of multifactorial pathogenesis. Lastly, post-discharge mortality was assessed only through readmissions or follow-up within our center, potentially missing deaths that occurred outside the institutional system and thus underestimating the 90-day mortality rate.

Given these limitations, we also acknowledge that the study involved numerous subgroup comparisons without correction for multiple testing, which may increase the likelihood of false positive findings. While this approach allowed for exploratory assessment of age and sex related patterns in AP etiology, the results especially those based on smaller subgroups or marginal p-values should be interpreted as hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory.

Despite its limitations, this study has several notable strengths. It presents a comprehensive analysis of 445 patients with AP, which is a relatively large sample for a single center study. The findings provide valuable regional insight into the age and sex-specific distribution of AP etiologies, including a potentially novel observation of male predominance in younger patients with BAP, as well as distinct etiological patterns among elderly patients. These results can serve as a foundation for future prospective research.

Moreover, our findings emphasize the importance of regional factors in AP prevention. Public health strategies such as promoting smoking cessation, reducing alcohol misuse (particularly among younger males), and raising awareness about the risks of drug and herbal induced AP may help lower the incidence in our population. The high prevalence of BAP in this region also highlights the need for better utilization of diagnostic resources to ensure timely and accurate identification of biliary causes. Finally, the high post discharge mortality rate highlights the need for close follow up and careful risk stratification of AP patients after hospitalization, particularly among the elderly.

Conclusion

AP etiology and demographic patterns vary widely by geographic region, age, sex and lifestyle factors. In our region, BAP is overwhelmingly dominant, with a prevalence more than six times that of other etiologies. An emerging observation of male predominance in BAP among younger patients was identified, which may reflect regional or population specific factors and warrants further investigation through prospective research and multicenter studies.

Additionally, regional properties of AP may be different. We found a distinct shift in BAP among patients aged ≥ 75 years, alcohol abuse and smoking is a problem especially in young males, DIAP is more common than expected prevalence. These findings highlight the importance of regional and age and sex specific considerations in both clinical practice and public health planning.
