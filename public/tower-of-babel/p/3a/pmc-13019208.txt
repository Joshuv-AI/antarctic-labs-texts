Enhancement of Petroleum Hydrocarbon Adsorption Performance by Attapulgite Modified Biochar: Performance and Mechanism Analysis
Authors: Yuanyuan Shao, Wenyang Wang, Na Liu, Xiaofeng Ma, Wenwen Zhang, Longmiao Yuan, Yingqin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two biochar-based adsorbents, namely, original corn cob biochar and attapulgite-biochar composite, were prepared via two-step pyrolysis at 400 and 700 °C under oxygen deficiency for petroleum hydrocarbon removal from water. Experimental results revealed that attapulgite modified the structure of biochar, increased the quantity of surface functional groups, and thereby significantly enhanced its adsorption capacity. Petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption experiments showed that adsorption kinetics was more accurately characterized by the pseudo-second-order model and isothermal adsorption was characterized by the Freundlich model, supported by R 2 and error analysis. This finding suggested that chemisorption through multimolecular layers was the predominant mechanism of adsorption. Regarding the effect of pH, the original biochar exhibited maximum adsorption capacity under weakly acidic conditions (pH 5.0), while the attapulgite-biochar composite achieved optimal adsorption performance in neutral to weakly alkaline environments (pH = 7.0–9.0), and attapulgite-biochar composite exhibited an adsorption rate 41.8% higher than that of the original biochar. In terms of salinity, it exerted a notable influence on the adsorption capacity of biochar; however, the attapulgite-biochar composite demonstrated superior adaptability over a wider salinity range (0.5% to 8.0%) and a 65.14% increase in overall adsorption efficiency compared to the original biochar. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy indicated that the adsorption mechanism primarily encompassed surface adsorption, interfacial adsorption, micropore filling, hydrogen bonding, π–π bond interactions, and chelation effects. Additionally, specific redox reactions might have occurred alongside the adsorption process. In conclusion, this low-cost, environmentally friendly, and highly efficient carbon material holds considerable promise for the removal of oil pollutants in saline-alkali environments.

Introduction

Petroleum, a critical energy source in contemporary society, plays a significant role in the national economy and serves as an important strategic resource reserve. Petroleum hydrocarbon leaks can occur during extraction, transportation, and refining processes, resulting in varying degrees of pollution to the atmosphere, water bodies, and soil environments. , Due to the high viscosity of petroleum, its components may persist in soil pores, adsorb onto the surfaces of soil minerals, or form a continuous layer on the soil surface. Petroleum in the surface soil migrates downward due to its own weight, river water erosion, or rainfall leaching, ultimately entering the groundwater. , Numerous studies have shown that petroleum hydrocarbon pollutants can cause skin and eye irritation, mucosal damage, kidney and liver complications, and adverse pregnancy outcomes in humans. , Consequently, soil and groundwater pollution caused by petroleum hydrocarbon leakage has become an urgent environmental issue, requiring immediate resolution. Currently, common remediation technologies for petroleum hydrocarbon-contaminated soil and groundwater include physical separation, chemical oxidation, and microbial degradation. − However, physical methods are inefficient for low-concentration pollutants and high in energy consumption, chemical oxidants may trigger secondary pollution, and microbial degradation is strongly dependent on environmental conditions (e.g., pH, salinity) with a long treatment cycle. , Biochar-based adsorption, essentially belonging to the category of physical remediation technologies, primarily relies on physical adsorption-dominated separation mechanisms (e.g., micropore filling, surface adsorption) while synergistically involving weak chemical interactions. In contrast to conventional physical separation methods, this technology has emerged as a promising remediation strategy due to its low cost, environmental benignity, and reusability, rendering it suitable for large-scale practical applications.

Biochar is predominantly derived from a wide range of abundant and renewable feedstocks, with agricultural and forestry wastes (e.g., crop residues, forest litter, and animal manure) being the most commonly utilized raw materials. As a high-carbon solid material with high aromaticity and chemical stability, it is synthesized via the pyrolysis of biomass under anoxic or anaerobic conditions. This carbon-rich adsorbent possesses a well-developed porous structure and surface-borne oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., −OH, −COOH, −CHO), which enable it to interact with pollutants in soil and aqueous environments, thereby facilitating pollutant sequestration and enhancing cation adsorption capacity. − Corn is a major global cereal crop, generating substantial quantities of untreated corn cob residues annually. These residues are often inappropriately discarded via open burning or landfilling, resulting in severe environmental pollution and resource wastage. Consequently, the utilization of corn cobs as a feedstock for biochar production not only improves resource utilization efficiency but also aligns with the environmental governance principle of “treating waste with waste”.

In recent years, researchers have increasingly focused on synthesizing various biochar composites; chemical and physical modification methods can enhance the properties of pristine biochar, thereby expanding its application potential. Pristine biochar’s adsorption mainly involves simple mechanisms (e.g., mineral precipitation and metal ion exchange), but its adsorption capacity is limited, largely by the nature of its adsorption sites. Therefore, composites combining biochar with natural clay minerals have been developed. Attapulgite (ATTP) is a natural hydrated magnesium silicate mineral with a moderate cation exchange capacity (CEC), a large specific surface area, and a wealth of surface-active groups. This material serves as an excellent low-cost adsorbent, attributed to its affordability, abundant natural resources, and nontoxicity. As a result, ATTP has also been extensively developed as an efficient adsorbent for removing pollutants from water and soil. Modifying biochar with ATTP may produce synergistic effects. However, few studies have focused on corn-cob-derived biochar modified by ATTP for petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption, especially under saline-alkali conditions (mimicking saline-alkali soils and aquatic environments with varying pH and salinity). High pH values can alter the surface charge of adsorbents and the chemical form of petroleum hydrocarbons, while high salinity may cause ion competition for adsorption sites, both of which can significantly reduce the adsorption efficiency of conventional adsorbents. , Thus, evaluating the performance under these conditions is critical for practical application.

Petroleum hydrocarbons accumulated in soil exhibit high toxicity and persistence, posing a prominent challenge for efficient and low-cost remediation. In this study, the original corn cob biochar (BC) and ATTP-biochar (BA) composites were fabricated via a two-step pyrolysis protocol at 400 and 700 °C; compared with conventional one-step pyrolysis, this strategy enables precise regulation of biochar’s pore structure and surface functional groups by modulating stage-specific heating rates and holding times, thereby enhancing the adsorptive performance of the composites. The specific research contents are as follows: (1) screen for optimal ATTP/BC mass ratios to obtain composites with superior petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption capacity, and systematically evaluate the adsorption behaviors (including kinetics and isothermal adsorption models) of BC and BA under varying pH and salinity conditions; (2) characterize the morphological and physicochemical properties of the materials via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and X-ray diffraction (XRD), and elucidate the underlying adsorption mechanisms by analyzing postadsorption FTIR and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) data to reveal changes in surface functional groups and petroleum hydrocarbon fractions. This work aims to develop a novel preparation strategy for high-efficiency petroleum hydrocarbon adsorbents, realize the valorization of agricultural waste with cost reduction, and provide a scientific basis for the remediation of petroleum hydrocarbon-contaminated saline-alkali environments.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials and Reagents

Corn cobs were sourced from the farmlands surrounding the Changqing Oilfield in Qingyang City, Gansu Province, China. They were dried in an oven at 65 °C until a constant weight was achieved, followed by crushing, grinding, and sieving for subsequent use. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) (analytical grade) and petroleum ether (boiling point 60–90 °C, HPLC grade) were obtained from Tianjin Damiao Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Attapulgite (ATTP, Mg5Si8O20(OH)2(OH2)4·4H2O) was sourced from Huilin Mine in Linze County, Gansu Province. It was processed via the production line of Rongwan Technology Co., Ltd. (Gansu) to remove large amounts of quartz sand and chromogenic ions for use. The primary chemical constituents of ATTP include relatively high levels of SiO2 and Al2O3, along with trace amounts of CaO. The petroleum hydrocarbon utilized in the experiment was extracted from the Changqing Oilfield and sterilized through ultraviolet irradiation for 30 min before application.

Preparation of Samples

Under a nitrogen-protected atmosphere, corn cobs were heated at a rate of 10 °C·min–1 to temperatures of 400 and 700 °C, where pyrolysis was conducted for 2 h. Following natural cooling, the material was ground to 100 mesh particle size and sieved. It was then modified with 1 mol·L–1 HCl and washed to achieve neutrality. Repeatedly rinsed with deionized water until the eluent reached neutrality, the sample was dried at 65 °C to a constant weight and stored in a desiccator. These prepared biochar samples were designated as BC400 (pyrolyzed at 400 °C) and BC700 (pyrolyzed at 700 °C).

After drying and storage, biochar designated as BC400 and BC700 were obtained. The prepared BC400 and BC700 were mixed with ATTP in mass ratios of 1:3, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1, and 3:1, stirred thoroughly for 1 h, and allowed to stand for 6 h. Subsequently, the mixtures were subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 1 h, filtered, and dried at 65 °C to a constant weight. The dried materials were then placed in a tube furnace and pyrolyzed under the same temperature conditions. After washing and drying, ten different ratios of biochar composite attapulgite materials were obtained. The preparation processes of biochar and modified biochar are illustrated in Figure .

Preparation processes of corn cob biochar and attapulgite-modified biochar composites.

Characterization of Samples

The morphology and elemental composition of biochar, ATTP, and modified biochar were detected by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 360, Germany) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The crystal structure and functional groups of the material were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’pert PRO) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, VERTEX70, Germany). The chemical valence state of the material was evaluated by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific Escalab Xi+ system, USA). The specific surface area and pore size distribution of the samples were determined via nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms (BET, ASAP 2020, Micromeritics).

GC–MS Analysis

The total sample mass and its components were analyzed before and after adsorption using an Agilent 7890N gas chromatograph (GC) coupled with a 7000B mass spectrometry detector (MSD). A KD-5MS column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm) was employed, utilizing ultrapure helium (99.999%) as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. An injection volume of 1.0 μL was used, and full scan detection was conducted in splitless mode. The GC temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 80 °C held for 1.0 min, followed by a ramp to 290 °C at a rate of 4 °C·min–1 and holding for 30 min. The quality range was set from 50 to 550 amu, and the spectral library utilized was NIST12. The petroleum hydrocarbon extracted without the addition of an adsorbent served as the control group. This study aims to elucidate the specific mechanism of biochar adsorption of petroleum hydrocarbon.

Adsorption Experiment

Removal of Petroleum Hydrocarbons by Modified Biochar in Different Proportions

To evaluate the performance of ten composite modified adsorbents (with distinct mass ratios) in treating petroleum hydrocarbon-contaminated systems, each adsorbent was added to ultrapure water at 1.0 g·L–1, followed by supplementation of petroleum hydrocarbons to an initial concentration of 2.0 g·L–1. Each biochar group was set in triplicate with one blank control, and all systems were incubated in a constant-temperature shaking incubator at 25 °C and 160 r·min–1. Subsequently, 20 mL of petroleum ether was added to each sample for extraction via a separatory funnel, and this procedure was repeated three times. The combined extracts were centrifuged, dried with anhydrous Na2SO4 to remove residual moisture, and transferred to a volumetric flask. After solvent evaporation, the extract was adjusted to 20 mL with petroleum ether for analysis. Its absorbance was determined at 225 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Yuansi-UV-6000, Shanghai, China), and the petroleum hydrocarbon removal efficiencies of the ten adsorbents were calculated from the absorbance data.

Adsorption Kinetics and Isothermal Adsorption Model

For adsorption experiments, petroleum hydrocarbon systems with an initial concentration of 2.0 g·L–1 were prepared, and a 1.0 g·L–1 biochar sample was added to each system. Each biochar group was set in triplicate and one blank controls, and all systems were incubated in a constant-temperature shaking incubator at 25 °C and 160 r·min–1. For adsorption kinetic tests, extractions were conducted following the protocol described in Section at preset time intervals (30, 60, 120, 180, 360, 480, 720, and 960 min). Adsorption kinetic curves were plotted with time (t, min) as the abscissa and equilibrium adsorption capacity (Q e, mg·g–1) as the ordinate and further fitted to kinetic models. For isothermal adsorption experiments, petroleum hydrocarbon solutions with gradient initial concentrations (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0 g·L–1), initial pH 7.0, and adsorbent dosage of 1.0 g·L–1 were prepared. All solutions were shaken at constant temperature for 6 h to reach adsorption equilibrium, followed by extraction according to the method outlined in Section . The experimental data were fitted to isothermal adsorption models with equilibrium concentration (C e, mg·L–1) as the abscissa and Q e (mg·g–1) as the ordinate.

Effects of pH and Salinity on Adsorption Performance

Batch adsorption experiments were designed to investigate the effects of pH and salinity on petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption by the biochar materials with the specific experimental settings as follows:

pH Effect Experiment

50 mL centrifuge tubes were prepared with petroleum hydrocarbon (initial concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 g·L–1) and adjusted to initial pH values of 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0, and 11.0. Biochar was added to each tube at a dosage of 1.0 g·L–1, with three parallel samples and three blank controls for each biochar group.

Salinity Effect Experiment

NaCl solutions (0.5%, 1.0%, 2.0%, 4.0%, and 8.0% (w/v)) were prepared, and petroleum hydrocarbon was added to achieve initial concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 g·L–1. The solutions were transferred to 50 mL centrifuge tubes, and biochar was added at 1.0 g·L–1, with three parallel samples and three blank controls for each biochar group.

All mixtures were thoroughly agitated at 25 °C for 6 h, following the methodology described in Section . After adsorption equilibrium, petroleum hydrocarbon concentrations were determined via a UV–visible spectrophotometer, and the adsorption capacity Q e (mg·g–1) was calculated accordingly.

Results and Discussion

Optimization of Biochar Modification Conditions

The ratio of biochar to attapulgite is a key factor influencing the adsorption performance of composite adsorbents toward petroleum hydrocarbons. Ten modified biochar-ATTP composites with varying ratios were thermally modified at two distinct temperatures (400 and 700 °C) and then were added to petroleum hydrocarbon solutions with the same initial concentrations for adsorption experiments. The results revealed a significant interaction between the biochar-ATTP ratio and pyrolysis temperature on petroleum hydrocarbon removal efficiency (Figure ). At pyrolysis temperatures of 400 and 700 °C, the highest removal rates for petroleum hydrocarbons were achieved when the mass ratios of biochar to ATTP were 2:1 and 3:1, respectively, reaching approximately 53.76% and 67.29%. This variation is presumably ascribed to differences in pore structure, surface functional group composition, and the synergistic effect between biochar and attapulgite in the composite under different pyrolysis temperature conditions. Overall, Figure illustrates that the biochar-based materials pyrolyzed at 700 °C generally exhibited higher adsorption capacity than those processed at 400 °C across most mass ratios (1:2, 1:3, 3:1), with the exception of the 2:1 ratio, where the 400 °C group performs better. As revealed by subsequent BET and SEM characterizations (Section and Table S1, Figure ), the biochar prepared at 400 °C exhibits underdeveloped porosity and a small specific surface area. When the ATTP-to-biochar ratio increases to 3:1, excessive ATTP blocks the limited pores, reduces accessible active sites, and thus leads to a significant reduction in adsorption efficiency. However, for the biochar prepared at 700 °C, a well-developed porous structure with a larger pore volume is formed. The additional ATTP does not induce pore blocking; instead, its synergistic interaction with the oxygen-containing functional groups of BC700 enhances adsorption performance. This temperature-dependent response confirms that the optimal ATTP-to-biochar ratio is determined by the intrinsic pore structure of the biochar. Therefore, in the subsequent experiments, the selected biochar types were BA400 and BA700.

Characterization of Adsorbents

As shown in Figure , the original biochar samples (BC400, BC700) exhibited unique porous morphologies with significant variations in pore diameter and abundance at different pyrolysis temperatures. With the increase of pyrolysis temperature, the pore diameters of biochar gradually expanded, while the walls of the tubular structures became progressively thinner. At 700 °C, the tubular pore structures were either broken or collapsed, resulting in the formation of numerous structurally scattered and disordered gaps (Figure a, d). The SEM images of BA biochar revealed a significant amount of ATTP present within the surface pores, exhibiting both needle-like and platelike structures (Figure b, c). These findings indicated that the ATTP-biochar composite material has been successfully synthesized. Subsequent EDS confirmed the presence of Si, Mg, Al, and Fe in both BA400 and BA700 (Figure e), verifying the successful incorporation of ATTP onto the surface of the modified biochar matrix.

BET analysis was employed to quantify the specific surface area and pore structure parameters of the biochar samples (Table S1). As illustrated in Figure a, b, the BC adsorption isotherm aligns with the characteristics of a Type I adsorption isotherm. Specifically, the N2 adsorption capacity rose rapidly in the low-pressure region and maintained a moderate upward trend in the medium-pressure region. This phenomenon suggests that pristine biochar possesses a strong N2 adsorption capacity at low pressures, which is attributed to significant micropore filling effects. As monomolecular adsorption in the voids of the biochar approaches saturation, multilayer adsorption predominates, characterized by a substantial number of mesopores and a broad size distribution. Ultimately, the sharp increase in the adsorption curve at high pressure implied the existence of macropores and microcracks in biochar, thereby enhancing N2 adsorption capacity. The primary reason for the curve not-closure may be the simultaneous presence of mesopores, macropores, and potential micropores within the biochar. During the microporous adsorption process, the increase in pressure resulted in the expansion of the interlayer distance of the layered compounds within the solid. This expansion permits nitrogen to access previously inaccessible micropores, leading to solid swelling or intercalation. Consequently, the adsorption–desorption isotherm hysteresis loop remains open. , The significant differences observed between BC400 and B700 were likely attributable to the effects of pyrolysis temperature. Variations in pyrolysis temperature resulted in distinct pore development in biochar, which was reflected in the significant differences observed in the corresponding N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms. In contrast to BC, the adsorption–desorption isothermal curves of BA for N2 in the low-pressure region largely overlap. These curves conform to Type IV isotherms, and the hysteresis loop is classified as type H3, suggesting that the pore structure of the modified biochar primarily consists of slatted pores formed by granular or flaky particles. Analysis of the average pore size distribution (Figure a, b) reveals that the pore sizes of BC400 and BC700 are predominantly <2 nm, categorizing them as micropores. In comparison, the pore size distribution of BA400 and BA700 was relatively broad, dominated by mesopores, and the pore volume of BA700 is significantly larger than that of BA400. This observation indicated that an increase in pyrolysis temperature not only enhanced the specific surface area of the material but also optimized the pore structure, thereby facilitating the transport and adsorption of substances. In summary, pyrolysis temperature exerted a significant regulatory effect on the pore structure of biobased carbon materials. Elevating the temperature promoted the transformation of micropores into mesopores and increased both the specific surface area and the pore volume.

BET-derived N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms (a, b) and pore size distributions (c, d) of BC and BA.

The crystal structures of the BC and BA samples were analyzed by using XRD (Figure a). The results indicated that BC400 and BC700 primarily consist of amorphous carbon, with weak diffraction peaks observed at 28.48° and 40.67°. This phenomenon may be attributed to the presence of crystalline carbon fiber structures within biochar or graphite-like carbon formed by aromatic compound accumulation. , In contrast to BC, the BA samples exhibited new diffraction peaks at 19.8° and 26.6°, which correspond to the (040) and (400) crystal planes of ATTP, respectively. This finding suggested that ATTP was effectively loaded onto the biochar surface and interacted during pyrolysis. , Additionally, multiple diffraction peaks of iron oxide were detected at 30.9° and 41.5°, corresponding to the crystal planes of Fe3O4 at (220) and (422), respectively. In summary, ATTP influenced the structure of modified biochar materials, particularly by promoting redox reactions involving iron elements in minerals during the adsorption process, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for subsequent experiments.

The functional groups of biochar, both before and after modification, were assessed by using FTIR results (Figure b). The peak near 3403 cm–1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of O–H. The peak at 1626 cm–1 is associated with the tensile vibrations of C=C and C=O in the aromatic ring. , The band at 1381 cm–1 indicates the stretching vibration of methyl–CH, while the stretching vibration of Si–O–Si occurs at 462 cm–1. FTIR analysis revealed that biochar contains oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups, which provide numerous active sites. In comparison to BC, both BA and ATTP demonstrated the oscillatory stretching of Si–O–Si, suggesting the successful integration of ATTP and BC during the pyrolysis process. Moreover, after the application of ATTP on the surface of biochar, the peak at 1626 cm–1 exhibited increased intensity. This enhancement may result from the influence of ATTP on the C–C or C–O adjacent groups within the aromatic ring of biochar, which intensified during the stretching vibration process, leading to a stronger absorption peak. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results revealed the chemical composition and crystalline state of biochar (Figure ). The full-spectrum XPS (Figure a) indicated that both BA and BC exhibit two primary signal peaks at 286.1 and 543.7 eV, corresponding to C 1s and O 1s, respectively. The presence of a Si 2p orbital peak in the full spectrum of BA further confirmed the successful loading of ATTP onto biochar. The XPS spectrum showed carbon atoms associated with the C 1s orbital peaks at 284.8 and 286.4 eV (Figure b, c) in the forms of O–C=O, C–C, C–O, and C–O–C. The O 1s spectrum comprises three peaks (Figure d, e), attributed to Si–O (533.1 eV), Fe–O–C (531.8 eV), and C=O (534.0 eV). Additionally, the XPS analysis of Fe 2p revealed two peaks (Figure f). The peaks at 710.3 and 715.0 eV correspond to Fe2+ and Fe3+ in the octahedral coordination of Fe3O4, respectively, while the peak at 719.3 eV is associated with Fe3+ in the tetrahedral coordination of Fe3O4. Notably, no Fe was detected in BC. These findings suggested that, in the presence of ATTP, Fe3+ and the Fe3+/Fe2+ ratio may function as cation bridges, facilitating the formation of a metal–organic complex of Fe–O–C with biochar.

Adsorption Performance

Adsorption Kinetics Analysis

Adsorption kinetics primarily examine the overall process of adsorption and its efficiency. The data were analyzed using pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models. As shown in Figure , the adsorption capacities of BC and BA for petroleum hydrocarbons at the same concentration both increased over time. Notably, the duration required for various adsorbents to reach adsorption equilibrium was approximately consistent; the adsorption capacity rose rapidly within the initial 2 h and ultimately achieved a dynamic equilibrium within 6 h. The BA composites outperformed BC in adsorption performance, with the equilibrium adsorption capacity following the order BA700 > BA400 > BC700 > BC400. Specifically, the equilibrium adsorption capacities of BA400 and BA700 reached 1075.12 mg·g–1 and 1345.77 mg·g–1, respectively, within 6 h. Supporting Information Table S2 shows that the correlation coefficients (R 2) of all materials fitted with the pseudo-second-order model were higher than those of the pseudo-first-order model, indicating a better fit to the pseudo-second-order kinetic model. This result suggested that chemical adsorption dominated the petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption process of biochar. During adsorption, petroleum hydrocarbon molecules migrate from the aqueous phase to the surface of carbon-based materials, bound to active adsorption sites, and gradually diffused into the internal layers of the adsorbents. This phenomenon can be elucidated by the intraparticle diffusion model (Figure ). The results indicated that the adsorption processes of BC and BA composites proceeded through two distinct stages: (1) surface diffusion, wherein petroleum hydrocarbon components migrated across the liquid film to reach the biochar surface, and (2) intraparticle diffusion, wherein petroleum hydrocarbons diffused into the internal pores of biochar. For petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption by biochar, the overall adsorption rate is coregulated by intraparticle diffusion rate and liquid film diffusion rate. As illustrated in Figure and Supporting Information Table S3, the fitting curve of the intraparticle diffusion model does not pass through the origin, suggesting that intraparticle diffusion is not the sole rate-limiting step, and other processes (e.g., liquid film diffusion) also participate in regulating the adsorption rate. Furthermore, as the biochar surface approaches adsorption saturation, intraparticle diffusion gradually becomes the dominant rate-limiting factor for the subsequent petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption process.

Adsorption Isotherm Analysis

Adsorption thermodynamic analyses are of paramount importance, as they elucidate the temperature-dependent evolution of adsorption capacity, thereby facilitating the elucidation of the underlying adsorption mechanisms. The Langmuir and Freundlich isothermal adsorption models were employed to analyze the isothermal adsorption process of petroleum hydrocarbon (Figure ). The fitting parameters summarized in Table S4 indicate that the correlation coefficients (R 2) for the Langmuir model exceed 0.845, demonstrating good fitting linearity. However, the theoretical saturation adsorption capacity (Q max) derived from the Langmuir model exhibited a significant discrepancy from the experimentally measured adsorption behavior. Consequently, the Langmuir model is not suitable for characterizing this adsorption process. In contrast, the R 2 values for the Freundlich isotherm model of the four biochar samples ranged from 0.927 to 0.972, with n values between 1 and 10, demonstrating a good fit with the Freundlich adsorption isotherm model. This result indicated that the adsorption of petroleum hydrocarbons by biochar occurred through a multimolecular-layer adsorption process. Notably, the multilayer adsorption behavior is predominantly governed by the oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl (−OH), carboxyl (−COOH)) on the biochar surface, as well as its carbonaceous framework. This phenomenon aligns with the adsorption behavior of graphene: π–π stacking interactions between the aromatic carbon structures of biochar form layered arrangements, which further facilitate the multilayer adsorption of petroleum hydrocarbons.

Effect of Different Conditions on Adsorption Performance

Effect of pH on the Adsorption Performance

Solution pH is a key environmental parameter governing the adsorption performance of adsorbents. This study investigated the influence of solution pH (3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0, and 11.0) on the petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption capacities of BC and BA, as illustrated in Figure . Raw biochar demonstrated a pH-dependent adsorption profile wherein capacities escalated with decreasing pH from 3.0 to 5.0. Ultimately, under weakly acidic conditions (pH = 5.0), the maximum equilibrium adsorption capacities (Q e) for different concentrations of petroleum hydrocarbons reached 303.73–1038.80 mg·g–1 (BC400) and 365.07–1440.67 mg·g–1 (BC700) under weakly acidic conditions (pH = 5.0); conversely, further elevation of pH to 11.0 resulted reduction in Q e for BC. This phenomenon may be attributed to the elevated concentration of hydrogen ions (H+) in the acidic solution, which could compete with petroleum hydrocarbon molecules for active adsorption sites on the biochar surface. whereas deprotonation of these functional groups in alkaline media induces a negative surface charge and triggers competition between OH– ions and petroleum hydrocarbon molecules for active adsorption sites, thereby diminishing adsorption efficiency. In contrast, BA attained optimal adsorption efficiency under neutral to weakly alkaline conditions (pH 7.0–9.0) with superior pH stability. At pH 7, the adsorption capacities (Q e) for different petroleum hydrocarbons reached peak values of 442.82–1521.33 mg·g–1 for BA400 and 485.72–1856.8 mg·g–1 for BA700. Even under strongly alkaline conditions (pH = 11.0), BA still exhibited approximately 46–59% higher adsorption capacities than BC. Comparative analysis of the overall adsorption capacities of BC and BA reveals that biochar pyrolyzed at 700 °C exhibits a higher adsorption capacity than that pyrolyzed at 400 °C. Additionally, the adsorption capacity of BA significantly surpasses that of BC. This trend suggested that increased pyrolysis temperature enhances porosity, thereby facilitating pollutant adsorption. The incorporation of attapulgite further amplifies nonelectrostatic interaction mechanisms, such as hydrophobic interactions and π–π interactions, between carbonaceous adsorbents. Consequently, BA demonstrates reduced sensitivity to the pH of the solution, maintaining efficient adsorption performance within a pH range of 7.0 to 9.5. This efficiency arises from the reaction of inorganic bases in the solution with organic acids and other acidic components in petroleum hydrocarbon, leading to the formation of surface activators that promote the desorption and separation of petroleum hydrocarbons in water. This characteristic offers a significant advantage for the application of BA in complex aquatic environments and saline-alkali soils.

Effect of Salinity on the Adsorption Performance

As illustrated in Figure , solution salinity is a critical environmental factor regulating the petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption performance of biochar-based adsorbents. This study systematically investigated the influence of solution salinity (0.5%, 1.0%, 2.0%, 4.0%, and 8.0% (w/v)) on the adsorption capacities of BC and BA. BC exhibited a salinity-dependent adsorption profile, wherein capacities gradually decreased with increasing salinity from 0.5% to 8.0%. Ultimately, under low salinity conditions (0.5%), the maximum equilibrium adsorption capacities (Q e) for different concentrations of petroleum hydrocarbons reached 72.85–236.57 mg·g–1 (BC400) and 142.8–297.185 mg·g–1 (BC700); conversely, further elevation of salinity to 8.0% resulted in a significant reduction in Q e for BC. At low salinity, the adsorption process was primarily governed by the salting-out effect: inorganic salt ions reduced the polarity of the aqueous phase via hydration, thereby facilitating the partitioning of nonpolar petroleum hydrocarbon fractions from the aqueous phase. This phenomenon enhanced the driving force for petroleum hydrocarbon migration and immobilization onto the hydrophobic surfaces of the biochar-based materials. In contrast, at high salinity, the elevated concentration of Na+ competed with petroleum hydrocarbon molecules for active adsorption sites on the surface of the adsorbent. Additionally, the accumulation of Na+ at the pore openings of the materials could induce pore blockage, impeding the diffusion of large petroleum hydrocarbon molecules into the internal pore structure. In contrast, BA achieved optimal adsorption efficiency under low to medium salinity conditions (0.5–4.0%) with superior salt tolerance. At 0.5% salinity, the adsorption capacities (Q e) for different petroleum hydrocarbons reached peak values of 296.85–422.82 mg·g–1 (BA400) and 347.85–465.72 mg·g–1 (BA700). Even under high salinity conditions (8.0%), BA still exhibited approximately 35.76–41.87% higher adsorption capacities than BC. This is ascribed to the synergistic enhancement effect resulting from the modification of attapulgite. The incorporation of ATTP not only enhanced the specific surface area and pore volume of the material but also provided additional adsorption sites. The stable hydrophobic structure formed by its interaction with biochar enables more effective utilization of the salting-out effect, while its well-developed mesoporous configuration exhibits increased resistance to pore blockage in high-salt conditions. In contrast to BA400, BA700 demonstrated notably higher adsorption capacity, attributed to the relationship between the pyrolysis temperature and surface area, where higher temperatures result in larger surface areas and porosities. In summary, BA showed superior structural and surface characteristics compared to BC, offering stable and efficient application potential across a broad salinity range, particularly in high-salt environments.

Adsorption Mechanism Discussion

Using BA700 modified biochar with optimal adsorption capacity as the focal point of this study, we examined the changes in petroleum hydrocarbon components before and after adsorption through GC–MS spectra (Figure a). A blank sample, BA700, was introduced to investigate whether the aromatic ring structure and alkanes present in the biochar would affect the changes in components following adsorption. Comparatively, it was observed that BA700 does not contain short-chain alkanes, but rather a minimal quantity of aromatic substances. Furthermore, after adsorption, the sample (BA700-X) exhibited a notable reduction in normal alkanes within the petroleum hydrocarbons, suggesting that the prepared composite material is effectively applicable for the removal of petroleum hydrocarbons. In the BA group, longer-chain normal alkanes may gradually decompose to yield shorter-chain alkanes, while aromatic compounds can undergo oxidation and ring-opening reactions, resulting in hydrocarbons with fewer carbon atoms. Following the removal of larger molecules, smaller molecules tend to aggregate, leading to a substantial increase in the composition of short-chain (C12–C15) alkanes. Conversely, the concentration of long-chain alkanes (C16–C31) has significantly decreased, likely due to the occurrence of various reactions, such as ring-opening and oxidation, which convert C16–C31 to short-chain alkanes with simpler structures.

GC–MS spectra of BA700-modified biochar before and after adsorption (a) and FITR spectra of BA700 and BC700 (b). Note: -X represents the sample after adsorption, Pr: basalane; Ph: Phytane; C24D50: Standard sample.

The adsorption mechanism of the BC700 and modified BA700 adsorbent on petroleum hydrocarbons was discussed and elaborated upon based on the results. The adsorption of petroleum hydrocarbons in aquatic environments is a highly complex process, making it challenging to analyze the adsorption mechanisms of organic pollutants using a single chromatographic method. Consequently, infrared spectroscopy was employed to investigate the adsorption mechanisms of BC700 and BA700 before and after modification with a focus on evaluating changes in functional groups. The results indicated (Figure b) that the prominent peak near 1626 cm–1 corresponds to the stretching vibrations of C=C and C=O. Notably, both the peak area and the intensity increased significantly after adsorption, indicating the successful adsorption of aromatic compounds from petroleum hydrocarbons. Previous studies have reported that the peak at 2925 cm–1 is characteristic of biochar and can be attributed to the stretching vibrations of saturated aliphatic C–H bonds (e.g., −CH2– and −CH3 groups), indicating the potential occurrence of chemical bond cleavage and hydrogen bond formation during the petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption process. , Moreover, after petroleum hydrocarbon adsorption, the intensities of the characteristic peaks corresponding to the O–H and aliphatic C–H bonds increased significantly. This spectral variation implies that multiple reactions may have occurred during adsorption, including cleavage of carbon–carbon single/double bonds, ring-opening of aromatic rings, and redox reactions, which collectively promoted the transformation of macromolecular petroleum hydrocarbons into smaller-molecular-weight hydrocarbon fragments.

BA composite materials have a larger specific surface area and a more developed pore structure than BC. ATTP itself is a high-efficiency adsorbent with an excellent decolorization performance. After being combined with biochar, it can react with petroleum hydrocarbons through surface adsorption and micropore capture, causing petroleum hydrocarbons to adhere to the surface of biochar or migrate and penetrate the pore structure of biochar. Petroleum hydrocarbons contain polar components (e.g., phenolic compounds, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and halogenated hydrocarbons), which can serve as either hydrogen bond donors or acceptors. The oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., −OH, −COOH, −C=O) on the surface of BA composites can form specific hydrogen bond interactions with these components, thereby significantly enhancing the selective adsorption capacity for target pollutants. During the pyrolysis process of BA composites, persistent free radicals (PFRs) are generated in situ on the material surface. Under the stimulation of external pollutants, it reacts with O2 to form ·OH. The generated ·OH possesses strong oxidizing capacity, which can induce oxidative cleavage of long-chain alkane molecules in petroleum hydrocarbons, converting them into short-chain alkanes with higher water solubility and easier desorption, thereby enhancing the overall remediation efficiency. Furthermore, previous characterization results confirmed that ATTP contains iron (Fe) species (e.g., Fe3+/Fe2+). The incorporation of Fe not only enhances the structural stability of the carbon matrix but also introduces Fenton-like catalytically active sites and additional adsorption sites for BA composites. Importantly, the Fe–O–C metal–organic complexes formed on the composite surface can chelate with nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds and polar functional groups in petroleum hydrocarbons (rather than amino components, which are negligible in petroleum hydrocarbon), thereby promoting the selective removal of recalcitrant petroleum hydrocarbon fractions.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the adsorption capacity of corn cob biochar modified with attapulgite at 400 and 700 °C toward petroleum hydrocarbons. Notably, the pyrolyzed material at 700 °C exhibited superior adsorption performance compared to that prepared at 400 °C. Compared to the BC, the BA exhibited enhanced properties due to attapulgite addition, including increased specific surface area, optimized pore structure, improved stability, and enhanced oxidation resistance. This indicated that attapulgite played a significant role in regulating the physicochemical properties of the biochar.

Adsorptive behaviors of petroleum hydrocarbons at gradient concentrations were assessed under diverse salinity and pH regimes, with both BC and BA reaching adsorption equilibrium within 6 h. In terms of pH effects, BC achieved maximum adsorption capacity under weakly acidic conditions (pH = 5) and displayed higher pH sensitivity, while BA delivered optimal performance in neutral to weakly alkaline environments (pH = 7–9) with a 41.8% higher adsorption rate than BC, alongside lower sensitivity to pH fluctuations. Furthermore, salinity exerted a notable influence on biochar adsorption capacity, but BA demonstrated superior adaptability over a wider salinity range (0.5–8.0%) and a 65.14% increase in overall adsorption efficiency compared to BC, indicating enhanced environmental robustness of the modified biochar.

Kinetics modeling indicated that both adsorbents conformed to pseudo-second-order kinetic models. Isothermal adsorption curve fitting revealed that the Freundlich model effectively described the adsorption behavior, suggesting the simultaneous occurrence of chemical adsorption and multilayer adsorption. Notably, petroleum hydrocarbon components were significantly reduced after BA adsorption. This is attributed to the provision of additional active binding sites by the attapulgite-modified biochar for petroleum hydrocarbon sequestration, which is accompanied by redox reactions. The adsorption mechanism involves the synergistic effects of surface adsorption, micropore filling, hydrogen bonding, π–π-stacking interactions, and complexation reactions. In summary, the ATTP@BC composite enables the efficient valorization of agricultural waste. It exhibits a prominent adsorptive performance and robust environmental adaptability, particularly in saline-alkali environments. This material holds great promise as a high-efficiency, reliable, and low-cost adsorbent for soil and water remediation.
