Next-generation microneedle platforms for site-specific management of diabetic neuropathy
Authors: Suresh Babu Kondaveeti, Komalpreet Kaur, Vrinda Gupta, Rajni Tanwar, Neeraj Choudhary, Dinesh Kumar, Sonia Gupta, Pooja Rani, Arunprasad VK
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetic neuropathy (DN) is a common and debilitating complication of diabetes mellitus, often presenting with chronic pain and sensory or motor deficits. Current treatment options provide only partial symptomatic relief and are frequently associated with adverse effects, underscoring the need for more effective and targeted approaches. Microneedle (MN) technology has emerged as a minimally invasive, highly efficient, transdermal-delivery strategy offering increased drug absorption, sustained drug release, and patient compliance. Different designs of MN, such as solid, coated, dissolving, and hydrogel-based, are available and provide specific strategies in DN management. The patient-specific and solid microneedles, like the lipid-cast microneedles functionalized with the lidocaine and dexamethasone, have demonstrated the possibility to provide long-term pain relief. Emulsion formulations of microneedles made of hydrogel loaded with nerve growth factor show potential in enhancing nerve repair. Biodegradable polymers such as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) can be dosed well. Still, more advanced materials, such as polylactide (PLA) and polycaprolactone (PCL), need to be engineered for scalable and cost-efficient production. It has its main obstacles that include drug stability and production, mass culturing, and complicated regulatory processes. The combination of MN platforms, nanomedicine and advanced delivery systems could provide synergistic therapeutic effects that could be the next step towards personalised, effective, and endurable DN treatment. This review identifies new developments, limitations, and areas to explore in terms of using microneedle technology as a method of targeted drug delivery for diabetic neuropathy.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Diabetic neuropathy (DN) is one of the most common and disabling complications of diabetes mellitus worldwide, and its painful form, painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy (PDPN), further degrades patient outcomes [1]. It is estimated that by 2025, there will be 589 million adults, approximately one-ninth of the worldwide population, living with diabetes, showing an increase to 850 million by 2050 [2]. DN contributes substantially to the global healthcare burden, with annual diabetes-related costs exceeding USD 760 billion. Among those affected, PDPN develops in approximately 25–50% of patients, leading to neuropathic pain, impaired sensation, and functional weakness, and is strongly associated with diminished quality of life due to persistent symptoms. Prevalence rates of DN and PDPN vary considerably across regions, ranging from 16% to 60%, depending on diagnostic criteria and population characteristics [3].

Economically, the diabetes neuropathy (DN) spending is estimated to be greater than USD 45.9 billion annually in the United States by 2025, whereby about USD 31 billion will be spent on DN-specific care. In contrast, the rest will be spent on the control of complications, including foot ulcers and amputations [4]. DN is linked to an over 46% higher use of healthcare resources in ambulatory, hospital and pharmacy environments, and comparable effects on costs and productivity are documented in Europe and emerging economies [5]. Although these are some serious clinical and societal consequences, the existing treatment approaches to diabetic neuropathy (DN) and its painful variant (PDPN) are mainly symptomatic [6]. Common pharmacological treatments that include antidepressants, anticonvulsants and opioids can bring an important reduction in pain in less than 50% of patients, and does not alter the disease, nor does it lack side effects. Currently, no treatment has been established to stimulate nerve growth or reverse the underlying neuropathology, hence the urgent need to take steps in terms of mechanism-based therapies [7].

Microneedle (MN) technology has become one of the most promising approaches to the targeted and minimally invasive delivery of therapeutic agents across the stratum corneum to increase local bioavailability. Bypassing the stratum corneum [8]New developments in MN design solid, coated, dissolving and hydrogel forming systems, make it possible to optimally deliver neuroprotective drugs, analgesics, growth factors, and regenerative molecules. MNs will overcome the significant shortcomings of traditional strategies and conform to the ideals of precision and regenerative medicine by promoting sustained and specific drug delivery to areas of nerve injury in the diagnosis of diabetes-associated neuropathy [9].

This review provides the first dedicated synthesis of microneedle (MN)-based therapies for diabetic neuropathy (DN), addressing both symptomatic relief and the emerging potential of regenerative strategies such as stem cell and gene therapies [10]. In contrast to previous reviews, it also provides a comparative analysis with standard treatment modalities provided in Table 5, highlighting the distinct benefits and limitations of MNs in this situation [11]. Based on the increasing global rates and economic cost of DN and painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy (PDPN), in addition to the insufficiency of the current symptomatic therapies, this review shows the necessity to develop innovative, patient-friendly, and disease-modifying therapies. By emphasising the translational potential of MNs and their alignment with precision and regenerative medicine, the study establishes a timely roadmap for advancing therapeutic strategies beyond 2025 [12].

Microneedles: a new approach for painless drug delivery

Microneedles (MNS), on the other hand, are a novel, emergent drug delivery mode that tries to bypass the prevailing problems in conventional routes of oral ingestion, epinephrine injections, and traditional transdermal patches [13]. It is this that allows microneedles to achieve minimally invasive delivery of drugs with no pain, and therefore improve patient compliance, ideally leading to a higher self-administration potential [14]. From tiny size with sub-pubic in diameter, high targeting specificity, they can deliver a big array of drugs; from small molecules and proteins/peptides through to vaccines or even genetic materials directly to viable epidermis [15]. Microneedle technology has become intensely active since the early 2000 s in terms of design, material choice, fabrication methods, and application fields. Researchers and developers progressively categorised microneedles into five distinct groups of structure characteristics and mechanisms of drug delivery as classified below, based on the maturity of an area: solid microneedles, coated microneedles, dissolvable microneedles, hollow microneedles, and hydrogel-forming microneedles [16].

Solid microneedles

One of the earliest types of microneedles is solid and commonly manufactured using materials like silicon or stainless steel, which are rigid and mechanically strong [17]. They are mainly used to make transient microchannels in the skin that are used for drug application later on as creams, gels, patches, etc [18]. This “poke-and-patch” technique greatly increases transdermal permeability, especially for molecules that could not diffuse into skin [19]. Solid microneedles, although easy to understand and build, consist of a two-step process: firstly, applying microneedle arrays to break the barrier, secondly, depositing the drug, which might make them less user-friendly in specific applications. Furthermore, as the drug is not embedded in the microneedle, it is also more challenging to dose and deliver efficiently topically [20].

Coated microneedles

Microneedles coated with a drug are an improvement over solid microneedles by simply having the drug directly on the surface of the needle. In the configuration considered here, the dosage formulation is wrapped as a thin layer on the microneedle, which laterally dissolves shortly after skin insertion so that there is instant drug release [21]. This category is well-suited for tiny volumes of high-dosage substances, e.g., vaccines, hormones, or biologics. Coating techniques used are dip coating, spray coating, and inkjet printing, which may provide different levels of precision and scalability [22]. At the same time, there are challenges in getting uniform coating thickness, drug stability, storage, and resistance to premature degradation or crystallisation. Despite these technical hurdles, coated microneedles have proven effective in both pre-clinical and clinical studies, especially for intradermal vaccination [23].

Dissolving microneedles

In the case of Dissolving microneedles, the therapeutic agent is not physically incorporated within the pores, but it is present in some manner within the body of the microneedles [24]. Microneedles are made of biocompatible and biodegradable polymers PVA (Polyvinyl alcohol), PVP (Polyvinylpyrrolidone), CMC (carboxymethyl cellulose), or polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) [25]. The microneedles dissolve completely within a few minutes/hours after insertion into the skin, dispensing their payload directly onto the top layers of the epidermis or upper dermis [26]. Since the needles are completely soluble, there is no risk of sharp waste, and you do not need safety needles that come with traditional needle disposal protocols. In addition, the device permits adjusting dose and release kinetics with high accuracy, and the convenient system will be ideal for self-infusion and mass vaccination programs. Such properties have made dissolving microneedles an interesting candidate for macromolecular delivery, e.g., insulin, human growth hormone, and influenza vaccines [27].

Hollow microneedles

Hollow microneedles, on the other hand, work similarly to a tiny hypodermic syringe. They are made of needles with internal bores so liquid formulations can be injected directly subcutaneously [22]. The pressure-driven or passive diffusion: Drug delivery using hollow microneedles depends on the viscosity of the formulation and device design. The microneedles in question can load a larger amount than other systems and have a controlled infusion, which allows their use in applications that require fine titration of dose (e.g., hormones or anaesthetics) [28]. Hollow microneedles are, however, more complicated to fabricate and more costly since micromachining is required to provide essential structural integrity as well as avoid clogging. Additionally, we need to be careful in preventing backflow or leakage during administration [29].

Hydrogel-forming microneedles

Hydrogel-forming microneedles are the latest innovation in the field and have a different mechanism of action. Microneedles are constructed from cross-linked hydrophilic polymers that do not dissolve in skin but swell upon contacting interstitial fluid [30]. The swelling microneedles produce aqueous channels between drug reservoirs in the patch backing and dermis upon insertion, allowing a sustained drug diffusion maintained by passive permeation [31]. The hydrogel microneedles break off after removal and can be removed in the form of residue-free waste from the body. It is well-suited to load high molecular weight compounds and drugs requiring prolonged release profiles (e.g., monoclonal antibodies or contraceptives/chemotherapeutic agents) [32]. Moreover, hydrogel-forming microneedles can be designed and functionalized to respond to mechanisms such as pH, temperature, or glucose due for this reason, that’s why they are smart drug delivery system candidates [33].

Both classes of microneedles have their advantages and disadvantages, and the choice of class depends on several parameters such as some physicochemical properties of the drug, desired kinetics for release, target tissues, and clinical setup shown in Fig. 1 [34]. Along with the continuous development in the field [4, 8], innovative multiplex microneedles such as coated-dissolving, hydrogel-hollow are also developed to advance practical performance in hybrid microneedle systems [9]. Likewise, advances in materials science, nanotechnology, and microfabrication have opened up new design possibilities for microneedle-mediated drug delivery systems, all of which are contributing to the safety, efficacy, and usability of these platforms [35]. The classification and mechanistic understanding of microneedles drives the foundation for future innovations in therapeutic development of next-generation therapeutics for a plethora of diseases and patient populations, as research moves from the proof-of-concept studies to clinical/industrial applications [36].

Materials used in microneedle fabrication

Microneedles are fabricated using various materials, each with specific advantages depending on the intended application [27].

Polymers

Polymers like Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA), Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), Chitosan, and Gelatine are widely used due to their biocompatibility, flexibility, and ease of processing. PVA and PVP, for example, are water-soluble and can dissolve upon contact with the skin, allowing for controlled drug release [50]. Chitosan, a biodegradable polymer derived from chitin, offers the added benefit of antimicrobial properties, which can be useful in preventing infections at the site of microneedle insertion. Gelatine, being biodegradable and biocompatible, is another attractive choice, especially in the development of thermally sensitive microneedles [51].

Metals

Titanium and Stainless Steel are commonly employed materials for their strength, durability, and ability to create sharp, effective microneedles [52]. Metal-based microneedles are typically used in cases requiring high mechanical strength, such as in the delivery of vaccines or other biologically active agents. Their sharpness and precision allow effective skin penetration without causing significant pain or discomfort [53].

Silicon-based microneedles

Silicon offers a versatile material for microneedle fabrication due to its ability to be processed into sharp, precise, and uniform structures [54]. Silicon-based microneedles are often used for experimental purposes and in high-precision applications. The material’s rigid nature makes it suitable for microneedles that need to penetrate deeper layers of the skin without bending or breaking [55].

Biodegradable and non-biodegradable materials

Biodegradable materials, like certain polymers, are particularly beneficial in creating microneedles that degrade after use, minimising the need for removal and reducing potential side effects [25]. Non-biodegradable materials, like metals or certain plastics, can offer greater durability for repeated use, but they may require removal after drug delivery [56].

Drug incorporation techniques

Once the microneedles are fabricated, drug incorporation is a critical step to ensure that the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) is effectively delivered to the target area in the skin [57].

Coating technologies

A drug layer is coated on the microneedles in this technique. Upon insertion into the skin, the microneedles dissolve or degrade, releasing the drug directly at the site of action [58]. Coating is commonly used for hydrophilic drugs and small molecules. The coating thickness and formulation are carefully optimised to control the release rate [59].

Encapsulation strategies

Drugs can be encapsulated within the microneedle material itself, which allows for a sustained and controlled release. Encapsulation ensures that the drug remains stable within the microneedle and is gradually released as the microneedle dissolves or degrades over time. This strategy is often used for proteins, peptides, and other large biomolecules that might be sensitive to degradation [60].

Layer-by-layer deposition

This advanced technique involves the deposition of alternating layers of materials (e.g., polymers and drugs) on the microneedles [61]. It can provide additional control over the drug release profile by varying the composition and thickness of each layer. This method is particularly useful for achieving a controlled release or multi-drug delivery from a single microneedle system [62].

Drug release kinetics and pharmacokinetics

The drug release profile and pharmacokinetics are pivotal in determining the therapeutic efficacy of microneedles. Proper control over the release kinetics ensures that the drug is delivered effectively and consistently to the desired tissue [63].

Controlled and sustained release

Microneedles can be designed to release drugs in a controlled manner, ensuring that the drug concentration remains within the therapeutic range for an extended period [64]. This is achieved through the selection of appropriate materials (e.g., biodegradable polymers) and drug delivery strategies (e.g., encapsulation, coating). Sustained-release profiles are beneficial for chronic conditions or drugs requiring long-lasting effects [65].

Influence of microneedle type on drug absorption

The microneedle used can significantly affect drug absorption. Solid microneedles, which create microchannels in the skin, typically require a separate drug reservoir or coating for drug delivery. Hollow microneedles, on the other hand, allow for real-time drug delivery during insertion [66]. Dissolving microneedles, which release drugs as they dissolve in the skin, provide a more controlled and sustained release and are often preferred for systemic drug delivery. The selection of microneedle type depends on the specific needs of the drug, the intended application, and the desired pharmacokinetic profile [67].

Risk factors for diabetic neuropathy

Diabetic neuropathy, a common and serious complication of diabetes, is influenced by several key risk factors [68]. Coronary artery disease can compromise blood flow, reducing the oxygen and nutrient supply required for healthy nerve function, thereby contributing to nerve damage [69]. Poorly controlled blood sugar is the most significant risk factor, as persistently elevated glucose levels directly injure peripheral nerves [70]. Elevated triglyceride levels further exacerbate metabolic dysfunction, worsening neuronal injury [71] while high blood pressure damages blood vessels supplying the nerves and gradually impairs their function [72]. Smoking compounds these effects by restricting circulation and promoting inflammation, which accelerates neuropathy progression. Working mechanism of microneedles in transdermal drug delivery for diabetic neuropathy

Microneedle-based drug delivery is a cutting-edge technique that facilitates the painless and targeted administration of therapeutic agents through the skin, making it especially useful for chronic conditions like diabetic neuropathy [69]. Figure 4 illustrates the sequential steps involved in the transdermal delivery of drugs using microneedles, from device application to drug absorption at the site of action.

Step 1: Microneedle device with drug solution:

The process begins with a microneedle (MN) device preloaded with a drug solution. These microneedles are designed to be short, typically less than 1 mm in length, and are made from biocompatible materials such as polymers, silicon, or metals [70]. They are engineered to be strong enough to pierce the outermost layer of the skin without reaching pain receptors, ensuring a minimally invasive and painless experience for the patient [71].

Step 2: Device insertion into the skin:

Upon application, the microneedles penetrate the stratum corneum (outer layer of the epidermis) and lodge into the viable epidermis or superficial dermis. This precise insertion bypasses the major barrier of transdermal drug delivery—the stratum corneum—without causing significant damage or discomfort. The structure of the skin, as shown in the diagram, comprises the epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous tissue, with the dermis being rich in blood vessels and nerves, key targets for neuropathy treatments [72].

Step 3: Temporary mechanical disruption of the skin:

As the microneedles penetrate the skin, they create temporary microchannels. These mechanical disruptions act as pathways that allow the encapsulated or coated drug to be delivered directly into the dermal tissue [73]. These microchannels close up naturally within a few hours, minimising the risk of infection and tissue damage. Unlike traditional injections, this method does not require needles to reach the bloodstream directly, yet still achieves systemic or local drug effects [74].

Step 4: Drug release in the dermis:

Once the microneedles are inserted, they begin releasing the drug either immediately (in the case of dissolving microneedles) or over a controlled period (in the case of coated or hydrogel-forming microneedles) [75]. In diabetic neuropathy management, this step is critical, as targeted release of neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, or analgesic agents into the dermal layer can interact directly with affected peripheral nerves or be absorbed into systemic circulation. Drugs like insulin, curcumin, nerve growth factors (NGF, BDNF), or local anaesthetics such as lidocaine can be effectively administered at this stage [76].

Step 5: Drug transport through blood vessels to the site of action:

Following dermal deposition, the drug diffuses into nearby capillaries and is transported via the bloodstream to the intended site of action. For systemic treatments (like insulin or anti-inflammatory drugs), the drug is circulated throughout the body to exert its effect [77]. For localised treatments (like pain relief in affected areas), the proximity of the dermal layer to peripheral nerves allows for direct action with minimal systemic exposure. This ensures higher efficacy, reduced side effects, and improved patient compliance compared to oral or injectable routes [78].

Microneedles in drug delivery

Microneedle-based drug delivery for diabetic neuropathy

Microneedle (MN) technology has emerged as a transformative approach to the treatment of diabetic neuropathy, a debilitating complication of diabetes mellitus. This novel delivery system provides minimally invasive, targeted, and pain-free drug administration through the skin, bypassing gastrointestinal degradation and first-pass metabolism [79]. With growing interest in improving the efficacy of neuropathy management and patient compliance, microneedle-based systems are proving to be a promising modality for systemic and localised therapies [80].

Transdermal drug delivery for neuropathy management

The skin, being the largest organ, offers a vast and accessible surface for drug delivery. However, its outermost layer, the stratum corneum, acts as a major barrier for most drugs, especially large or hydrophilic molecules [81]. Traditional transdermal systems have had limited success in effectively delivering neuroprotective and analgesic agents. Microneedles overcome this barrier by creating microscopic channels in the skin, allowing the direct delivery of therapeutic agents to the dermis or systemic circulation [82]. This is particularly beneficial for diabetic patients, reducing the need for frequent injections, minimising systemic side effects, and enabling controlled-release formulations that improve therapeutic outcomes shown in Table 2 [83].

Neuroprotective agents for diabetic neuropathy

Insulin and its analogues play a dual role in diabetic neuropathy management by regulating blood glucose and protecting nerve health, as hyperglycaemia-driven oxidative stress and inflammation are central to disease progression. Microneedle-mediated delivery of insulin offers improved pharmacokinetics, steady basal levels, and better adherence, with dissolving microneedles showing promising preclinical and early clinical results in enhancing glucose control and potentially providing neuroprotection [47, 93].

Antioxidants such as resveratrol and curcumin also hold therapeutic potential due to their potent anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties, though their poor solubility and bioavailability limit conventional use [92]. Microneedle formulations overcome these challenges by stabilising the compounds and enabling controlled dermal delivery, directly targeting inflamed and degenerating nerves [94, 95].

Growth factors like Nerve Growth Factor (NGF) and Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) are essential for neuronal repair and survival, but face limitations related to systemic degradation. Microneedle patches allow localised, sustained release of these proteins at sites of nerve injury, promoting regeneration and preventing further degeneration [37, 96].

Similarly, anti-inflammatory agents such as celecoxib and dexamethasone, which are effective in suppressing chronic inflammation, benefit from microneedle-assisted delivery that ensures targeted deposition in the dermis. This strategy minimises systemic exposure and adverse effects while providing potent local anti-inflammatory activity, offering an advantage over traditional delivery methods [97, 98].

Pain management using microneedles

Local anaesthetics such as lidocaine and bupivacaine provide temporary relief from neuropathic pain by blocking nerve conduction, and their delivery through microneedles enables rapid penetration to dermal nerve endings, offering faster onset and more sustained analgesia with fewer systemic drawbacks [93]. Similarly, cannabinoids and low-dose opioids show potential for managing chronic neuropathic pain but are often limited by systemic side effects and dependency risks. Incorporating these agents into microneedle formulations allows localised, controlled delivery that reduces systemic exposure, thereby improving safety and patient acceptability [94, 95].

Microneedles for regenerative therapy

Stem cell–based strategies hold promise for repairing damaged nerves in diabetic neuropathy through paracrine signalling and potential differentiation into neural lineages. Microneedle systems provide a minimally invasive platform for delivering stem cell–derived exosomes or encapsulated cells directly to affected sites, improving viability, precision, and ease of administration, thereby advancing regenerative therapeutic approaches [93, 94]. In parallel, gene therapy offers opportunities to correct molecular defects underlying neuropathy. Delivery of plasmids, siRNA, or CRISPR components via microneedles enables localised gene modulation while reducing systemic immune risks typically associated with viral vectors, supporting the development of personalised and durable treatment options [95, 96].

Advances in microneedles for treating diabetic neuropathy

Microneedles represent a promising strategy for managing diabetic neuropathy, a complication of diabetes that leads to progressive nerve damage. Preclinical studies in animal models such as Sprague–Dawley rats, C57BL/6 mice, and Wistar rats have reported encouraging outcomes [93]. Recent investigations from 2023 to 2025 highlight several key findings: microneedle-based delivery consistently reduced neuropathic pain [94], improved nerve function through precise local drug application [95], promoted regeneration when combined with stem cell–derived products or electrically responsive systems [96], and decreased inflammation when loaded with anti-inflammatory agents [97]. Additionally, microneedles have demonstrated benefits in accelerating wound healing, particularly in diabetic ulcers. These studies indicate that microneedles may provide an effective, minimally invasive, and patient-friendly alternative to conventional approaches. However, current evidence remains largely preclinical, and further clinical trials are essential to confirm safety, efficacy, and long-term outcomes in humans [98, 99]. Critical comparison of preclinical and clinical outcomes

Microneedle (MN) platforms for diabetic neuropathy (DN) show robust signals in preclinical models, yet translation to clinically meaningful outcomes in humans remains uneven. In the following, we summarise areas where evidence is convergent and divergent in animal studies and clinical trials. We also present limitations, gaps, and areas of controversy, which can direct future developments.

Efficacy domains: what improves preclinically vs. clinically

Efficacy domains: what improves preclinically vs. clinically

Efficacy domains: what improves preclinically vs. clinically

Nociception and pain behaviours

Rodent studies have consistently found decreased thermal/mechanical hypersensitivity of MN-mediated delivery of analgesics, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant or neurotrophic factors. In comparison, local anaesthetics or counter-irritants administered via MNs often report short-term pain relief (hours to weeks) with minimal longer-term declines in neuropathic pain scores reported, although this is less common. Such discrepancy is probably due to endpoint differences (withdrawal thresholds vs. patient-reported outcomes) and chronicity of the disease.

Neuroregeneration and function

Preclinical studies show increased nerve conduction velocities, intraepidermal nerve fibre density, and axonal endogenous markers with MN-mediated delivery of growth or pro-regenerative factors. Clinical data are still early: there is little integration of structural regeneration endpoints (e.g., corneal confocal microscopy, skin biopsy fibre counts), and the majority of studies emphasise pain scores and safety. Therefore, regenerative claims are currently more potent among animals than among humans.

Glycemic and metabolic background

Part of the preclinical MN interventions indirectly enhance neuropathy by improving glycemic parameters (e.g., through insulin/GLP-1 delivery). In clinical trials, glycemic control and neuropathy endpoints are usually separated, which potentially applies to the situation when pain is reduced, and the HbA1c stays constant (or vice versa).

2) Delivery modality and payload: generalizability of effects

2) Delivery modality and payload: generalizability of effects

Delivery modality and payload: generalizability of effects

Coated or solid microneedles have been demonstrated to be reliable in transdermal permeation and immediate action in animal experiments. In humans, they have tended to reproduce rapid but transient analgesia with small-molecule anaesthetics. Microneedles. It has been shown that dissolving or biodegradable microneedles are more effective at sustained release of peptides, antioxidants, and anti-inflammatory agents. That sustained release is better than topical creams in rodent models. Still, the ability to achieve sustained release is not fully explored, particularly with disease-modifying agents like neurotrophins, due partly to manufacturing and regulatory concerns. Hydrogel-based microneedles have high drug loading capacity and responsive release in preclinical models. Still, initial human studies of these types of needles have focused on safety and comfort over therapeutic advantage, and remain inconclusive as to their efficacy in comparison with conventional therapies. One of the controversies in the field is whether or not microneedles provide more than optimised topical patches to deliver small lipophilic drugs, as there have not been rigorous human studies head-to-head.

3) Endpoints and trial design: mismatch that hinders signal detection

3) Endpoints and trial design: mismatch that hinders signal detection

Endpoints and trial design: mismatch that hinders signal detection

A significant barrier to translation lies in the mismatch of endpoints and trial design between animal and human studies. Preclinical models are based on short-term, objective tests, including paw withdrawal or nerve conduction velocity. Still, the testing on human subjects largely relies on subjective pain scales, which are affected by variability at baseline and placebo effects. Quantitative sensory testing, nerve fibre density, or corneal nerve imaging objective biomarkers are uncommonly used in clinical practice, restricting their comparability to animals. In addition, the majority of animal studies simulate acute or sub-acute neuropathy.

In contrast, diabetic neuropathy in subjects is chronic and multifactorial, highlighting the importance of extended trials (12 or more weeks) to identify disease-modifying effects. Lastly, although preclinical trials tend to compare MNs to either no treatment or topical preparations, clinical trials do not consistently incorporate rigorous comparisons, e.g. sham MNs, active comparators, or double-dummy. Thus, it is challenging to determine the actual effect sizes.

4) Safety and tolerability: alignment but context matters

4) Safety and tolerability: alignment but context matters

Safety and tolerability: alignment but context matters

Microneedles are also generally safe and tolerable, and local reactions include erythema, itching, or pinpoint bleeding (both in animals and humans), and infection risk is low when single-use designs and hygiene are ensured. Nonetheless, systemic delivery is greater than topical creams, a positive aspect of animal regenerative agents, but an increase in dosing and off-target safety in humans, especially steroids, neurotrophins and biologics. Further, patient-specific characteristics, including thickened or dry skin, microangiopathy, and impaired healing in diabetes, could influence microneedle penetration and drug depot formation but are rarely modeled in preclinical models.

5) Practicality and adoption: from bench feasibility to bedside use

5) Practicality and adoption: from bench feasibility to bedside use

Practicality and adoption: from bench feasibility to bedside use

Micro needles exhibit high feasibility in preclinical trials, but practical issues impede clinical use. The transition of small-scale experiments to GMP-scale manufacturing offers variability in the geometry of needles, dissolution, and drug distribution, which can diminish the strength of clinical effects. In addition, patient-related factors like force during use, wear time, and site rotation are unique to the patient and directly influence adherence, but are not represented in the animal studies. Lastly, health-system integration is complicated by uncertainties in reimbursement and regulatory classification (e.g., whether device, drug-device combination, or medicinal product), and postpones large, late-phase clinical trials.

6) Sources of bias and overestimation in animal data

6) Sources of bias and overestimation in animal data

Sources of bias and overestimation in animal data

Microneedles’ preclinical evidence can be biased and overestimated by multiple sources. Popular STZ and high-fat/STZ models can recapitulate hyperglycemia and early neuropathic abnormalities but do not recapitulate chronic axonal loss, small-fibre degeneration, and comorbidities typical of protracted diabetic neuropathy. The factors of publication and performance bias also play a role, with positive results being more highly reported, whilst blinding randomisation are not always rigorously enforced, and thus the efficacy may be overstated. Further, observed improvements can be due to optimised formulations or excipients, rather than using a microneedle, which is not often disentangled in study designs.

Microneedles are effective and consistent in delivering drugs to the dermis, producing short-term pain relief that is painless. However, evidence of neuroregeneration and long-term effects is limited to animal trials. Shorter follow-up times and a mismatch of endpoint and manufacturing or regulatory challenges on complex formulations limit clinical translation. To fill these gaps in the future, objective neurobiomarkers should have been used, the duration of the study period should have been extended, rigorous sham-controlled comparisons should have been applied, and patient-specific factors (skin properties, disease severity, and usability) should be considered to understand the therapeutic potential of microneedles in diabetic neuropathy.

Improving data quality in microneedle-based treatments

Microneedle-based approaches to diabetic neuropathy have been promising, but existing clinical data are limited and more of an exploration. Initial clinical trials using drug-impregnated microneedles (exenatide, insulin, lidocaine, fentanyl) show potential advantages of reduced pain relief, better glycaemic control and less dependence on opioids as opposed to traditional oral agents [105, 107]. Although other reports suggest quicker analgesia onset and fewer systemic side effects, these are premature and must be supported by larger, well-controlled trials.

Moreover, nanotechnology-enhanced microneedles with multifunctional functionality are under preclinical investigation. Examples are curcumin- or vitamin B12-loaded MNs to prevent neurodamage and nerve regeneration, silver-coated MNs to avoid infection, and nanoparticle preparations (e.g., dexamethasone nanoparticles, lipid nanoparticles, hyaluronic acid-based MNs) to enhance drug stability, absorption, and nerve regeneration [108–114]. These methods describe how the microneedle systems are versatile and can be engineered to incorporate pharmacological, regenerative, and antimicrobial properties.

In general, the findings of animal models and initial studies in humans are promising. Still, warnings must be raised about the potential of long-term effects and the effectiveness of these systems as opposed to conventional care. To determine the real clinical worth of the microneedle-based therapies in diabetic neuropathy, larger randomised controlled trials with a more extended follow-up period are necessary.

Challenges of microneedle technology

Despite its strong potential, several barriers limit the translation of microneedle-based therapies into routine clinical use. Drug stability remains a concern, particularly for sensitive neuroprotective agents such as NGF and BDNF, which are vulnerable to temperature fluctuations [105]. Dosing precision can also be inconsistent, as variations in polymer dissolution rates may alter drug release and absorption dynamics [106]. From a manufacturing perspective, scalable and cost-effective production using micro-moulding or 3D printing techniques remains challenging, with issues in reproducibility and quality control [107]. Regulatory approval poses another hurdle, as MNs must be evaluated as drug-device combinations, often extending approval timelines [108]. Finally, inter-patient variability—including skin thickness, hydration, and sensitivity differences—may affect penetration depth, drug absorption, and overall therapeutic outcomes [57].

Comparison with traditional methods

When compared to conventional delivery routes, microneedles provide unique advantages in diabetic neuropathy management [9]. Unlike oral agents (e.g., gabapentin, duloxetine) that suffer from systemic side effects and variable bioavailability, or injectables (e.g., insulin, lidocaine) that require invasive administration, microneedles enable localised, minimally invasive, and patient-friendly drug delivery [105]. They also offer advantages over transdermal patches, with superior penetration through the stratum corneum and the potential for sustained and controlled release. Microneedles are becoming a popular method for delivering medications through the skin. They provide a good balance between being effective and easy to use for patients who do not like traditional injections. [111]. Despite their benefits, getting approval and manufacturing microneedles can be difficult. In the U.S., the FDA oversees its approval; in Europe, it falls under the EMA. Both regions have strict regulations because microneedles are considered medical devices and medicines [112].

FDA approval process for microneedles

The approval of microneedle-based technologies varies depending on their design and function. In the United States, the FDA classifies solid microneedles—used only to pierce the skin—as simple devices, generally requiring a more straightforward approval process [112]. In contrast, coated and dissolving microneedles, which actively deliver therapeutic agents, undergo a more rigorous evaluation than new drug approvals [113]. Hollow microneedles, designed for liquid injection, face even stricter scrutiny due to their complexity and higher safety risks [114]. Examples of FDA-cleared products include the Soluvia™ microinjection system for influenza vaccines [115] and the MicroCor® microneedle patch for migraine relief [104].

In Europe, microneedles fall under the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) framework. Devices delivering drugs require device certification and additional marketing authorisation for the pharmacological component [113]. For instance, the NanoPass MicronJet® microneedle system has been approved for vaccine delivery under EMA guidelines [104]. These distinctions highlight how microneedles, as drug–device combination products, must navigate regulatory pathways that are more complex than either drugs or devices alone.

Production challenges and cost-effectiveness

The large-scale production of microneedles remains complex, with techniques such as micromolding and 3D printing serving as the primary manufacturing approaches, each offering distinct advantages and limitations [112]. Although the per-unit cost of microneedles is currently higher than that of conventional oral pills, they often reduce downstream expenses by eliminating the need for refrigerated storage and lowering distribution costs [67]. Importantly, microneedle systems deliver high bioavailability, ensuring that a greater proportion of the drug reaches its target, and can provide sustained drug release, reducing dosing frequency. Their minimally invasive and painless application enhances patient compliance, particularly beneficial in chronic conditions such as diabetic neuropathy [113]. Future innovations—including integration with digital health platforms and innovative drug delivery systems—could accelerate clinical adoption of microneedles, especially in skin-based therapies and vaccination programs [114].

Recent advancements and future prospects

In recent years (2022–2025), there has been a breakthrough in the application of microneedles in diabetic neuropathy. Applying dissolving and hydrogel-forming microneedles loaded with antioxidants, growth factors, and stem-cell-derived exosomes in animal studies has demonstrated increased nerve regeneration and decreased inflammation [85]. In preliminary clinical trials of lidocaine- and insulin-laden microneedle patches, the patches show faster pain reduction and enhanced glycemic control as compared to traditional approaches [114]. The engineering of nanomaterials has made it possible to make sensitive biomolecules such as NGF and BDNF more stable and bioavailable. Also, smart-release, antibacterial-coated, and multifunctional microneedles are becoming the dual-action platforms in managing neuropathy symptoms and comorbidities like diabetic foot ulcers [115]. Microneedle (MN) technology will have a revolutionary impact on managing diabetic neuropathy through the integration with the next-generation drug delivery and diagnostic systems. Biosensors and smart microneedles may be used to monitor glucose, inflammatory biomarkers, and drug release in real time and offer the potential to personalise therapy [116]. The confluence between microneedles and nanomedicine, exosomes produced by stem cells, and gene editing technologies has provided an opportunity, such as never before, to not only treat pain but also control disease progression mechanisms via nerve regeneration. Design enabled by artificial intelligence and 3D bioprinting could further simplify manufacturing, cut expenses, and expedite regulatory clearance. As clinical-grade biodegradable polymers progress and GMPs can be produced at scale, microneedle platforms will move beyond preclinical flashes of excitement to become the standard of clinical practice in the coming decade [117].

Limitations of the study

Dependency on preclinical evidence, few large-scale clinical experiments, and translational disparities between animal and human systems limit approval of microneedles in diabetic neuropathy. Variability in manufacturing, biologic stability challenges and complicated regulatory pathways further compound progress, and individual differences in skin qualities present patients with additional obstacles to uniform effectiveness [118].

Conclusion

Microneedles are a promising and minimally invasive method to deliver therapeutic agents to diabetic neuropathy with potential benefits in drug targeting, patient compliance, and possible disease modification. Although the recent developments show that they can provide antioxidants, growth factors, and regenerative agents in a better way, they are at an early phase of translation to clinical use. Their potential must be actualised by overcoming obstacles to large-scale clinical validation, production, and regulatory approval. This review highlights the concept of microneedles as a prospective treatment modality that will strike a harmonious balance between symptom management and regenerative medicine, and eventually deliver effective, patient-focused, and sustainable therapies to diabetic neuropathy.
