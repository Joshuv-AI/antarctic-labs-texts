Cardiac MRI in cardiac dystrophinopathy: recommendations on imaging
Authors: Adam McDiarmid, Daniel Augustine, Caroline Coats, Saul G Myerson, Marina Hughes, Chet Villa, Jonathan Soslow, Kadhim Kadhim, Adam Kerr, Lisa Kuhwald, Daniel M Sado, Peter Swoboda, Cathy Turner, Michela Guglieri, John Bourke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart pumping weakness occurs progressively in all patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), but symptoms only occur very late in the process. Regular imaging assessments of heart function are needed to see how the heart is ‘doing’ and how well it is responding to treatment with heart drugs. Echocardiography (echo) has been the standard type of imaging used for these assessments in the UK. It is widely available but has limitations. Cardiac MRI (CMR), although less readily available and more expensive, is better able to detect tissue changes in the heart before there is any reduction of heart pump function and retains accuracy in all measures even as DMD progresses. An expert panel of cardiologists, muscle specialists and patient representatives was set up to review evidence and provide expert guidance on how to use both echo and CMR best to the benefit of patients with DMD, Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) and females affected by DMD-gene abnormalities (‘female gene carriers’).The working group concluded that echo should remain the predominant method used in heart checks for patients with DMD in the UK. However, wider use of CMR, performed at times individualised to key decision points after about the age of 10 years, would allow earlier detection of those heart changes prompting the introduction and changes in heart therapies. Similarly, greater use of CMR could contribute to improving cardiac management of males with BMD and females with DMD-gene variations._______________________ObjectiveTo provide guidance on the use of cardiac MRI (CMR) in the UK in paediatric and adult patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) or Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) as well as recommendations for screening and monitoring females with dystrophin-gene variations. Specifically, to examine the ‘added value’ of CMR over echocardiography at selected time points in the assessment of individuals with or at risk of developing cardiac dystrophinopathy.MethodsInitiated by an expert working group of UK-based and US-based imaging cardiologists, neuromuscular clinicians and DMD-patient representatives, draft guidelines were created based on published evidence, current practice and expert opinion. After wider consultation with UK cardiologists, consensus was reached on the optimal use of CMR in clinical decision-making about therapy.Results and conclusionsEchocardiography should remain the predominant modality for cardiac surveillance and to guide therapy for patients with DMD in the UK. However, when tolerated, wider use of CMR at key stages in patients with DMD, older than age 10 years, and for males with BMD and females with DMD-gene variations would allow earlier detection of those with cardiac involvement and inform the earlier initiation of regimes of cardiac medications. In this way, greater use of CMR could contribute to improving cardiac health in each of these patient groups.Keywords: Cardiac Imaging Techniques, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Cardiomyopathies, Quality of Health Care, Rare Diseases

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe, progressive, muscle-wasting, life-limiting condition that affects just under 20 in every 100 000 live male births around the world.1 There are currently around 2500 boys and men living with the condition in the UK.2 The DMD-gene, located on the X-chromosome, encodes for the protein dystrophin. Patients with DMD do not produce dystrophin because they have out-of-frame mutations in the gene.3 Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) is caused by in-frame mutations in the same gene, which allow production of a truncated, partially functional form of dystrophin and so, BMD typically results in a less severe condition than DMD. Being X-linked, the vast majority of those diagnosed with DMD or BMD are male. However, up to 17% of females, who ‘carry’ DMD-gene variations, develop cardiomyopathy either in isolation or with significant skeletal muscle weakness and/or cognitive difficulties.4

The lack of functional dystrophin on muscle cell membranes makes them uniquely vulnerable to injury, damaging cells and causing chronic inflammation. Over time, this overcomes repair mechanisms and results in loss of myocytes and their replacement with fibrous tissue and fat. From the average age of DMD diagnosis at 4 years in the UK,5 it is skeletal muscle function that is most noticeably affected with a gradual decline in motor function and loss of ambulation usually early in the second decade of life.6 By adolescence, DMD patients usually require non-invasive ventilator support at night because of intercostal and diaphragm muscle weakness.5 Dystrophin is also expressed in cardiac myocytes and the lack of dystrophin there has the same result as in skeletal muscle with fibro-fatty replacement of ‘working’ heart muscle and, clinically, to a progressive form of dilated cardiomyopathy.7

There is currently no effective cure for DMD or BMD. A glucocorticoid-steroid is recommended routinely and initiated between ages 4 years and 6 years because conventional steroids slow the rate of decline in skeletal muscle function and prolong ambulation, probably mainly through their anti-inflammatory actions.8 The novel ‘dissociative’ steroid, vamorolone, offers steroid benefits in DMD with less adverse impact on growth and bone health.9 Givinostat, an approved first-in-class histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor for use in DMD, has been shown to slow the rate of progression of muscle weakness and fibro-fatty replacement in skeletal muscle compared with matched historical controls.10 Each of these drugs has been shown to slow the rate of skeletal muscle deterioration in clinical trials of patients with DMD and so represent the first effective therapy for the condition since conventional steroids.

Cardiomyopathy occurs in all patients with DMD, but its age of first detection is variable and critically dependent on the sensitivity of testing used in surveillance. The more sensitive the parameters used in detection, the larger the proportion of patients found to have abnormalities and at younger ages. However, heart involvement remains asymptomatic throughout much of its course, but its onset, rate of progression and response to therapies can be determined from the results of serial cardiac imaging-assessments. Glucocorticoid steroids, deployed for skeletal muscle strengthening, also delay the onset and rate of progression of DMD’s effects on the heart.11 Whether the novel ‘dissociative’ steroid, vamorolone, provides the same benefits as conventional steroids for the heart and whether the HDAC-inhibitor, givinostat, is cardio-protective are yet to be established in DMD. To further slow the trajectory of decline in heart function, the International Care Standards DMD (2018) recommended empiric initiation of ACE-inhibitor (ACEi) medication ‘no later than age 10 years’ in addition to the steroid already deployed.12 Adapting that recommendation to the UK context and to reduce variations in the cardiac care being offered in different parts of the UK, more specific guidance was published in 202213 that recommended introducing an ACEi prophylactically before the age of 10 years, as soon as families were prepared to accept it, and explained the rationale for combining that with a mineralocorticoid receptor inhibitor and beta-blocker.14–16 As left ventricular (LV) function declines, more potent cardioactive therapy combinations are being deployed increasingly in patients with DMD in line with evidence of benefit in cardiomyopathy of other aetiologies.17 The overall aim is to preserve cardiac function for as long as possible, since that is critical to achieving prolonged survival in patients with DMD.18 However, even with the best combination of cardiac medications currently available, combined with multidisciplinary care and ventilatory support, median survival is still only to about 28 years of age in the UK with most adult patients dying of cardiorespiratory causes.2 19

Limitations of echocardiography in heart assessments of patients with DMD

Echocardiography (echo) has been the standard and almost the only imaging modality used in cardiac assessment of patients with DMD in the UK until relatively recently. Its wide availability, ease of deployment in a range of healthcare environments, lack of exposure to ionising radiation, acceptance by patients and relatively low cost meant that it could readily provide commonly used measures of cardiac function such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF%) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS%). Prior to the publication of the International Care Standards DMD 2018, heart therapy had usually only been introduced when LVEF% fell below the normal range.20 21 However, the two most consistently reported measures, LVEF% and LVFS%, are insensitive to early changes in cardiac dystrophinopathy, which first manifests with segmental LV dysfunction, affecting posterolateral and basal segments preferentially at a time when both of these measures of global LV function are still within the normal range.22 A comprehensive set of measures of heart performance, including ventricular dimensions and volumes and more sensitive measures of ventricular function, can usually be obtained in younger, ambulant patients with DMD. However, as skeletal muscle weakness progresses, the echo-window on the heart becomes increasingly restricted, limiting the range of measures obtainable.23 The reproducibility of quantitative measures of LV function also declines due to the poorer acoustic windows, increasing their variability between assessments and detracting further from their value in clinical decision-making.24 By late adolescence, echo in most patients can usually only provide semiqualitative impressions of LV function and dimensions.23 Although use of contrast can compensate for the deterioration in echo-image quality, in practice it is rarely used—because of difficulties in obtaining venous access in many of these patients.25 However, despite its limitations, serial echo-assessments can often provide enough information about LV function, such that many UK cardiologists rely exclusively on it to inform their management and monitor patient progress.

In this UK-context, the aim of convening an expert multidisciplinary group was to recommend how cardiac MRI (CMR) can best be used to complement echo in the surveillance and management of patients with or at risk of developing cardiac dystrophinopathy (ie, DMD, BMD and female carriers of DMD-gene variations) in the UK.22 A separate expert group, working in parallel, has developed guidance on the best use of echo in these patients.

Guideline development process

This is a consensus summary from an expert multidisciplinary panel of UK and USA paediatric and adult imaging-expert cardiologists, neuromuscular specialists and patient representatives from across the UK’s North Star Network of specialist neuromuscular centres (https://www.musculardystrophyuk.org/research/current-projects/northstar-programme/). Patient representatives were invited to join through Duchenne UK’s networks. At the start, a literature search was conducted on PubMed to identify publications on the assessment of LV function by CMR in patients with DMD, using medical subject headings-related keywords such as “Duchenne muscular dystrophy” AND “cardiac MR-imaging” OR “MRI*” AND “left ventricle” OR “left ventric*’. Publications on the use of CMR in other forms of cardiomyopathy were also sought and some additional articles were identified from reference listings. Only English language articles published in peer-reviewed journals in the period 1995–2024 were included to reflect up-to-date CMR practice. The published evidence identified was assembled by two members of the expert panel and circulated to panel members to inform panel discussions. They also drafted the initial version of this document. Through a series of working group meetings, held virtually, final consensus was reached, and penultimate versions were circulated more widely to paediatric and adult cardiologists, cardiac radiographers, muscle specialists and patients/patient representatives for comment. This guidance is intended to advise on the use of CMR at all stages of cardiac involvement in dystrophinopathy and much of it can be extrapolated to performing CMR in patients with other forms of muscular dystrophy. The overarching aim of the guidance is to indicate where CMR provides ‘added value’ to echo assessments, and how that can contribute to more individualised cardiac management at key stages in an individual patients DMD journey.

CMR has a particular role in the surveillance of patients with BMD, only about half of whom develop progressive LV dysfunction,26–28 and in the surveillance of females with DMD-gene variations (‘gene carrying females’), who are all at risk of developing cardiomyopathy.29 30 The final document was written to provide up-to-date guidance for treating physicians, CMR experts and patient groups and advocates.

CMR capabilities and the information it provides

CMR can provide a comprehensive assessment of cardiac anatomy and ventricular function and has additional unique tissue-characterising capability, being able to detect areas of fibrosis, as well as inflammation (table 1).31–36 In patients with cardiac dystrophinopathy, these changes are identifiable by CMR before the earliest signs of either segmental or global ventricular dysfunction are evident on echo assessment and confirm an evolving cardiomyopathy.26 28 29 31 32 The ability of CMR to identify pathological processes occurring in the ventricular wall has moved the focus for earlier and combination cardiac medication usage in DMD and made decisions less exclusively reliant on detection of ventricular dysfunction (figure 1).34 Detection of cardiac involvement at an earlier stage affords the opportunity to individualise the treatment of patients with DMD more accurately than on the basis of age alone in following the empiric international care-standard recommendations.12

At more advanced stages of DMD and unlike for echo, CMR measures of LV-function and volume retain their sensitivity to change and accuracy. When combined with assessment of the extent and pattern of myocardial fibrosis, they can also be of value in predicting prognosis such as the onset of heart failure symptoms and risk of sustained arrhythmias in a similar manner to that in patients with other forms of dilated cardiomyopathy19 35–38 (figure 2). CMR of older patients with DMD now has a contributory role in aiding decisions about a patient’s need for more potent combinations of heart failure medications,17 39 such as sacubitril-valsartan40 and/or sodium-glucose transporter protein-inhibitors,41 defibrillator implantation42–45 or consideration of suitability for advanced heart failure management,46–48 such as LV assist device implantation49 50 or heart transplantation.51 52

Optimal CMR of patients with dystrophinopathies

Table 1 summarises the advantages of CMR, but CMR also has disadvantages, depending on the stage of the patient’s condition (table 2).53–56 It is a less deployable, more expensive modality of imaging and remains a scarce resource in most hospitals in the UK. It is also a more arduous and thus sometimes less acceptable test for patients, particularly if to be used repeatedly. Image quality can be degraded by motion artefacts due to inability to breath-hold at key stages or movement resulting from discomfort and/or long study duration. Use of abbreviated imaging protocols could make CMR more acceptable for patients and more readily available.57 58 However, some patients will not tolerate the test at all because of factors such as claustrophobia or cognitive difficulties (eg, autism spectrum disorder or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder).

CMR in BMD and females with DMD-gene variations

Much less has been published to date on the sequence of changes in the myocardium of patients with BMD.26–28 The age of onset of heart involvement is even more variable and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and/or LV dysfunction occur at an older age than in DMD.59 There need not be any correlation between the severity of cardiac and skeletal muscle involvement and, indeed, cardiomyopathy can be the initial manifestation of BMD. It has not been demonstrated definitively that LGE always precedes LV dysfunction, nor has it been shown conclusively that earlier therapy improves longer-term cardiac outcome. Furthermore, and unlike in DMD, in which all patients develop cardiomyopathy, only about 60% of patients with BMD will ever do so. There is little consensus currently on when to initiate heart medications—on first detection of LV dysfunction by echo or CMR or, prophylactically, on identifying changes in myocardial tissue composition (ie, focal or diffuse fibrosis) before LV dysfunction on CMR.59–61 However, it is generally assumed that the underlying pathological processes in BMD are the same as in DMD and there is agreement on the potential benefits of earlier initiation of therapies once abnormalities are detected to improve cardiac prognosis.60 61 Therefore, CMR assessments every few years seems reasonable and a reliable way to detect early evidence of cardiac changes that may indicate the need to start cardioactive therapies in patients with BMD. In patients with advanced cardiomyopathy, CMR can also be used to identify those likely to benefit from non-pharmacological therapies such as cardioverter-defibrillator implantation or those suitable for advanced heart failure management (ie, LV assist devices or cardiac transplantation).51 62 63

Similarly, although all females with DMD-gene variations are at risk of developing cardiomyopathy, only about 17% of them will ever have detectable cardiac abnormalities and so, as for males with BMD, empiric initiation of heart medications is not justified.4 11 20 21 55 56 (figure 3).

When cardiomyopathy occurs, it usually develops in isolation, without skeletal muscle weakness or cognitive difficulties, and symptoms only emerge when there is already severe and irreversible LV dysfunction. Therefore, a schedule of cardiac-imaging surveillance is advisable for those confirmed to ‘carry’ a DMD-gene abnormality.10 11 64 65 CMR-surveillance of these females also allows earlier identification and therapy of those developing heart involvement, based on the same rationale as stated for patients with BMD. Because of its greater sensitivity and tissue characterising capabilities, CMR would allow for less frequent imaging than advisable by echo assessment and identification of those who need closer cardiac follow-up.66

Do the differences in capabilities between CMR and echo-imaging really matter clinically?

In the clinical context, it is reasonable to challenge the need for higher CMR standard of assessments in most patients with DMD on the basis that therapy can be introduced empirically based on age alone and that echo assessments provide sufficient detail for serial decision-making in the usual clinical as opposed to in research contexts. CMR can rarely be justified for as long as a comprehensive echo assessment is still possible or when it requires sedation or general anaesthesia, which is usually in boys aged about <8 years of age.67 68 The ‘added value’ of CMR-assessments depends on how cardiologists use the additional information obtained in guiding initiation and substitution of more potent drugs for individual patients over time and, also, how clinically effective and cost effective they consider such prescribing can be for DMD. Current guidance recommends starting empiric initiation of steroids from age 4 years to 6 years for its anti-inflammatory benefits and addition of an ACEi empirically no later than age 10 years in boys with DMD.10 11 Initiation of these medications in even younger patients with DMD does not depend on cardiac imaging results at all, if cardiologists are comfortable to do so, and, even earlier, in discussion with the family. However, in adolescents and adults with established LV dysfunction, echo cannot reliably distinguish between those responding satisfactorily to therapies already deployed from those whose trajectory of heart decline has not been slowed sufficiently. At this stage of cardiac dystrophinopathy heart assessment by CMR retains its accuracy and reproducibility of measures better than echo. In the later stages of cardiac dystrophinopathy, whether in patients with DMD, BMD or in females with cardiomyopathy, CMR has a unique capability to assess an individual patient’s cardiac prognosis. Wider use of CMR in the cardiac surveillance of patients with BMD and in females with DMD-gene variations would change their current assessment paradigm—lowering the threshold for initiating therapy, in those shown to have abnormal myocardium and thus predicted to progress to LV dysfunction over time, and prompting greater use of cardioactive medications in combination.36

Conclusions

For as long as acoustic windows allow sensitive measures of LV function to be obtained, echo should remain the predominant modality of imaging used in cardiac surveillance and to guide therapy for patients with DMD. This is because it is widely available, can be accessed more easily and repeated frequently. However, because of its extra capability in detecting changes in myocardial tissue composition, CMR can better inform clinical decision making about using combinations of heart medications in younger patients with DMD who are still ambulant. Later in the course of the condition, when echo assessments become more limited, CMR provides a more sensitive and accurate mode of assessment including measures that were previously obtainable by echo under ideal conditions. This supports the greater use of CMR with an individualised approach to timing of assessments in the management of adolescents and adults with DMD.

In the assessment of males with BMD and female ‘carriers’ with DMD-gene variations, more routine use of CMR in surveillance would allow earlier detection of those needing cardiac management and provide the opportunity to intervene with cardiac therapies sooner than practised currently in the UK. Greater use of CMR in these patients could contribute to improving cardiac outcomes but that awaits definitive confirmation by ongoing and future research.
