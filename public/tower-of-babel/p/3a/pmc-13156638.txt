Neonatal and Pregnancy Outcomes in Pregnant Adults With Pediatric-Onset Crohn’s Disease
Authors: Supraja Swamy, Jean-Paul Achkar, Stacey Ehrenberg, Alex Zajichek, Alex Milinovich, Ravi S. Shah, Jessica Barry, Sandra C. Kim, Jacob A. Kurowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsLow birth weight (BW) and preterm birth are recognized complications in women with active Crohn’s disease (CD). However, there is limited information on pregnancy and neonatal outcomes in patients diagnosed during childhood and adolescence. This study aims to evaluate the relationship between maternal age at CD diagnosis and pregnancy and neonatal outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, cross-sectional study of women with CD who delivered between 2012 and 2023. Gestational age (GA) at delivery and (BW) were analyzed as coprimary outcomes.ResultsA total of 262 births from 179 mothers were included, with 105 (40.1%) births from women diagnosed with CD before age 18. No significant differences were observed in GA at delivery between groups. There was a significantly higher rate of neonates born small for GA to mothers with adult-onset CD compared with pediatric-onset CD (15.9% vs 3.8%, P = .002). The odds of delivering a small-for-GA infant decreased by 21% for every 10% increase in the proportion of a woman’s life spent living with CD (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.65–0.96). A CD flare during pregnancy resulted in a 10.9% reduction in BW percentile at delivery (95% CI: −20.05 to −1.64). Antitumor necrosis factor therapy at the time of conception was associated with an 8.7% increase in BW percentiles (95% CI: 1.13–16.27).ConclusionNeonates born to women with pediatric-onset CD have excellent outcomes including GA and BW percentiles at delivery. Longer disease duration and antitumor necrosis factor therapy were positively associated with improved BW percentiles, while disease activity had a negative impact. These findings suggest that disease control has a more significant impact on neonatal outcomes than age of onset or disease duration.

Introduction

The global prevalence of Crohn’s disease (CD) continues to increase over the past 3 decades, particularly in newly industrialized countries.1, 2, 3 Additionally, approximately 25% of patients with CD are diagnosed at a pediatric age (pediatric Crohn's disease [pCD]).2 In comparison to adults, children with pCD have a higher disease burden characterized by extensive intestinal involvement, perianal disease, nutritional and growth impairments, and longer cumulative disease duration. These factors can adversely impact future fertility and pregnancy outcomes.4 Despite this, data on long-term outcomes in pCD remain limited, particularly regarding the effects of CD on young women during puberty and its relationship to future pregnancy and neonatal outcomes.

In previous studies, women with CD face a 4-fold increased risk of delivering a small-for-GA (SGA) infant, regardless of disease activity.5 In addition, women with active disease have a 2-fold higher risk of preterm birth compared to those with inactive disease, while up to 30% of patients experience a CD flare during pregnancy.6, 7, 8 Increased disease activity has been associated with increased cytokine production in the placenta, contributing to elevated rates of preterm birth and low birth weight (BW).9,10 In contrast, women with quiescent CD have infertility rates similar to healthy controls but are at a higher risk of pregnancy-related complications if the disease activity reemerges during pregnancy.11

To the best of our knowledge, pCD has not been investigated as a risk factor for pregnancy and neonatal complications. This study aims to evaluate the relationship between age of onset of CD and neonatal outcomes, with a particular focus on gestational age (GA) at delivery and BW percentile.

Methods

This is a retrospective, cross-sectional study of women with CD. The starting cohort was identified utilizing a combination of coded and uncoded data to predict a diagnosis of CD from a previously published model.12,13 The cohort was then filtered to identify women who delivered at the Cleveland Clinic between 2012 and 2023 with at least one gastroenterology or primary care visit in the Cleveland Clinic system during the year prior to delivery. Patients who sought primary inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) care at outside institutions were excluded. Demographics, CD history, and outcome data were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) and were manually verified. Clinical data included CD phenotype (inflammatory, stricturing, penetrating), perianal disease history, prior IBD-related surgical history, and CD medications at the time of conception. Mothers were classified based on the age of CD onset: pediatric-onset (diagnosis at <18 years) CD or adult-onset (diagnosis at ≥18 years) CD. The primary outcomes included GA at delivery, measured in weeks, and gestational size, defined by BW percentile. BW percentiles control for size relative to GA. The secondary outcomes were categorized into pregnancy (mode of delivery, preeclampsia, preterm premature rupture of membrane, intrauterine growth restriction, pregnancy flare, and postpartum flare) and neonatal-related (prematurity, SGA, neonatal intensive care unit [NICU] admission, antibiotic use, and congenital cardiac disease) outcomes. SGA was defined as those with a BW less than the 10th% for GA. A pregnancy flare was defined as an increase in CD activity during pregnancy requiring either a corticosteroid prescribed by a gastroenterologist or a change in therapy due to worsening disease activity.

Statistical Analysis

Patient/clinical characteristics and outcomes were analyzed at delivery. Descriptive statistics were reported for the full cohort and compared between (pediatric [<18 years] vs adult-onset CD [≥18 years] using the median (interquartile range [IQR]) for numeric variables and count (percent) for categorical variables. To estimate the average effect of age of CD onset on each outcome, 2 specifications of generalized estimating equations were used with an exchangeable correlation structure to account for within-patient correlation of observations. In all models, age of the mother at delivery was controlled for. First, age at CD onset was coded as binary: pediatric-onset CD vs adult-onset CD. Second, age at CD onset was coded as continuous, but transformed to represent the proportion of life with CD; this was done to limit collinearity, remove constraints in the parameter space, and maximize the amount of independent information contributed by each age measure. For continuous outcomes (GA at delivery, BW, and BW percentile), Gaussian error distributions were assumed, while a binomial error distribution with a logit link was assumed for all others (ie, logistic regression). Point estimates and 95% Wald confidence intervals (CIs) from robust (sandwich) variance estimates were reported for the effect of age of CD onset on all outcomes.

Demographics between the pediatric-onset and adult-onset CD groups were similar (Table 1). However, the median maternal age at delivery was statistically lower in those with pediatric-onset CD compared to adult-onset CD (30.5 vs 32.7 years, P < .001). The prevalence of perianal disease was higher among pediatric-onset CD patients compared to adult-onset CD patients, though this did not reach statistical significance (27.1% vs 18.9%, P = .142). There were no significant differences in CD phenotypes or overall rate of CD-related abdominal surgery between the 2 groups. A higher proportion of pediatric-onset CD patients were on biologic therapy at conception (49.5% vs 39.4%, P = .140) though this was not statistically significant. Rates of conception via assisted reproductive technology were comparable between the 2 groups.

Multivariate analysis (Table 3) evaluated various risk factors associated with changes in GA at delivery and BW. These included pediatric-onset CD, percent of life with CD, biologic use at time of conception (anti-tumor necrosis factor [TNF], anti-integrin, anti-IL-12/23), anti-TNF use alone, steroid use at conception, and pregnancy flare. Pediatric-onset CD was not identified as a risk factor for a decrease in GA at delivery. However, after adjusting for all other factors (Table 3), the mean BW percentile was 16.2 percentage points higher in pediatric-onset CD compared to adult-onset CD (95% CI: 8.7–23.7). Additionally, anti-TNF at conception was associated with a 0.8-week increase in GA at delivery (95% CI: 0.234–1.374) and an 8.7%-point increase in BW percentile (95% CI: 1.128–16.272) compared to those not receiving any biologic therapy. Pregnancy flare was associated with a 10.8%-point decrease in BW percentile. Furthermore, the odds of delivering an SGA infant decreased by 21% for every 10% increase in the proportion of a woman’s life spent living with CD (Table 4). Pregnancy Outcomes

A higher proportion of patients with adult-onset CD were found to have preeclampsia in pregnancy compared to pediatric-onset CD, although this did not reach statistical significance (adult-onset CD 9.6% vs pediatric-onset CD 2.9%, P = .064). After controlling for maternal age at delivery, there was still no significant difference in the effect of age at delivery and percentage of life with CD on the preeclampsia rates (Table 4). In contrast, pediatric-onset CD patients had a higher rate of cesarean section delivery (61.0% vs 47.8%, P = .049).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that patients with CD, regardless of age of onset, have infants with excellent neonatal outcomes, including normal GA at delivery and BW percentiles. Further, the rate of SGA delivery among mothers with pediatric-onset CD was close to that of the general population and lower than that reported for the broader IBD population.14 When evaluating maternal age at delivery as a continuous variable, the odds of delivering an SGA neonate decreased by 21% for every 10% increase in the proportion of a woman’s life spent living with CD, suggesting that a more recent diagnosis is associated with higher rates of fetal growth restriction (Table 4). We hypothesize this time-dependent risk factor may be attributed to CD-related complications including intestinal inflammation and malnutrition, which can impact fetal growth.10 This association warrants future investigation accounting for these variables that are limited and not able to be captured in a retrospective study.

The findings of this study may provide reassurance to patients with CD and their families regarding future neonate and pregnancy outcomes. A large Scandinavian population-based study (1994–2006) demonstrated that women with CD are at higher risk for preterm birth, SGA neonates, and cesarean section.15 In contrast, our study found that, regardless of age at onset, mothers with CD had excellent outcomes for GA at delivery and BW at delivery, with improved BW percentiles associated with longer disease duration. The multivariate analysis in our study further supports that pediatric-onset CD is not a significant risk factor for preterm delivery or fetal growth restriction. Interestingly, neonates born to women with pediatric-onset CD were shown to have a 16.2% increase in BW percentile when compared to mothers with adult-onset CD (Table 3). Additionally, a significantly higher proportion of neonates born with SGA were from mothers of adult-onset CD, compared to pediatric-onset CD. While the overall median BW percentiles compared to the general population are excellent, women with CD remain at higher risk of delivering an SGA neonate.5

Mothers with pediatric-onset CD delivered a median age of 2.2 years younger than adult-onset CD patients. While older maternal age overall trended toward smaller neonates, decreased BW was observed in those with shorter duration of disease, even after accounting for GA at delivery (Table 4). Although the difference in median age at delivery is small, in our study, the diagnosis of CD and shorter disease duration at the time of peak fertility may delay a woman’s decision to have children, potentially increasing her risk of pregnancy-related complications.16

Disease control appears more important than disease duration regarding pregnancy and neonatal outcomes. Contrary to the hypothesis that longer disease duration may result in more complications and therefore poorer control of disease, patients with longer duration of disease had lower rates of pregnancy flares.17 A limitation of this study is the retrospective nature of data collection, which may affect the accuracy of assessing disease activity or disease flares at the time of conception. When focusing on patients who were on corticosteroids at conception, no significant impact on prenatal growth or prematurity was observed. However, a pregnancy flare was associated with a 10.8% decrease in BW percentile. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating that women with active disease prior to or during pregnancy were more likely to deliver 1.5 weeks earlier and have SGA neonates.18

More mothers with pediatric-onset CD were on biologic therapy at time of conception, which may account for the lower incidence of disease exacerbations during pregnancy. While biologic usage does not equate to quiescent disease, anti-TNF therapy at conception was associated with an 8.7% increase in BW percentile and 0.8-week increase in GA at delivery. However, when evaluating additional biologics, such as anti-integrin and anti-interleukin 12/23 therapy, the statistical significance is lost, likely due to the small number of patients on these therapies. The higher rates of anti-TNF therapy use at time of conception among women with pediatric-onset CD may reflect their longer disease duration, providing more opportunities for optimized medical therapy and achieving sustained, steroid-free endoscopic remission along with prepregnancy counseling. Further, there is higher usage of anti-TNF therapy earlier in the disease course in pediatric-onset CD than adult-onset CD.13 Last, with 36.6% of patients off all therapy at conception, disease activity at delivery may have been underestimated.

Cesarean section rates were higher in women with pediatric-onset disease. This cohort also had a higher prevalence of perianal disease, which may have contributed to this finding, as perianal disease is a recognized indication for cesarean delivery in patients with CD.19 Pediatric-onset CD has a greater incidence of perianal disease compared to adult-onset CD, with estimated rates between 8% and 27% vs 5%–21%, respectively.20 In our study, even in the absence of perianal disease, cesarean section delivery rates remained higher in pediatric-onset patients (47% vs 37%). In addition, while not statistically significant, the rate of preeclampsia was 3-fold higher in adult-onset CD compared to pediatric-onset CD. We suspect the study was underpowered to detect a significant difference in the preeclampsia rates and advise caution due to the notably high prevalence compared to the general population.

Neonatal outcomes demonstrated slightly higher rates of NICU admission among patients with CD compared to the general population (pediatric-onset CD 17.1%, adult-onset CD 10.8%, general population 9.07%).21 This finding is consistent with CD-specific literature, which reports increased NICU admissions, particularly among patients with active disease.22 Notably, there was no significant difference in antibiotic use between groups, suggesting no increased risk of complicated NICU courses associated with either CD group. Rates of congenital cardiac disease were low across both groups in this study as well; however, the limited number of patients requiring NICU care or diagnosed with congenital cardiac disease limits the ability to draw meaningful conclusions.

The use of assisted reproductive technology, particularly in vitro fertilization, among patients with CD was comparable to that of the general population, although the number of patients was small.23 Limited data exist on the rates of in vitro fertilization or clomiphene usage in the IBD population, particularly in the United States.24,25

A limitation of this study is the retrospective design. Assessment of disease activity during the peripregnancy period was constrained by the availability of data in the EMR. Inconsistent documentation inflammatory markers and endoscopic findings limited concrete evaluations, and miscarriage and spontaneous abortion rates were underrepresented as a considerable proportion of these are not documented in the EMR. There were few reported cases of gestational diabetes in the cohort, none of which were insulin-dependent and whose GA and BW did not skew from the medians. Future prospective studies are needed to better evaluate perinatal outcomes in patients with pediatric-onset CD.

Conclusion

In conclusion, women with pediatric-onset CD, compared to those with adult-onset CD, do not exhibit an increased risk of delivering prematurely or having an SGA neonate. Longer disease duration was associated with a reduced risk of SGA delivery while pediatric-onset disease and anti-TNF use at conception were linked to higher BW percentiles. Overall, recent disease onset and poorer disease control appeared to have a greater impact on neonatal BW than disease duration or age of CD onset. These findings offer encouraging insights into pregnancy and neonatal outcomes for patients with pediatric-onset CD and provide valuable guidance to gastroenterologists counseling both pediatric and adult CD patients.
