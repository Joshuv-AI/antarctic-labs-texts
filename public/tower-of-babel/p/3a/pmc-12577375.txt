ACSS2 promotes proliferation and invasiveness of SKOV-3 and PA-1 ovarian cancer cell lines under hypoxia
Authors: Ola Mroweh, Louna Karam, Razane Hammoud, Jana Al Achcar, Rania Sobh, Selim M. Nasser, Joseph A. Garcia, Philippe Hussein Kobeissy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOvarian cancer is the eighth most common malignancy among women worldwide. Despite advances in treatment, chemoresistance and associated morbidity remain significant challenges. This study aims to elucidate the role of acetate-dependent acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2) in ovarian cancer progression under hypoxic conditions, identifying its potential as a therapeutic target.ResultsImmunohistochemistry revealed that ACSS2 was overexpressed and predominantly localized to the nucleus in High-Grade Serous Ovarian Carcinoma (HGSOC) tissues, with significantly higher H-scores compared to low-grade and normal tissues. Functional assays conducted on ovarian cancer cell lines (Skov-3 and PA-1) demonstrated that ACSS2 inhibition reduced proliferation, colony formation, and three-dimensional spheroid growth, with greater sensitivity observed under hypoxic conditions. Migration and invasion assays further indicated that ACSS2 inhibition significantly impaired cellular motility and invasiveness, particularly in hypoxia. Mechanistic studies, including Western blotting and RT-qPCR, showed that ACSS2 inhibition modulated the PI3K pathway and downregulated Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 2 (HIF-2) levels as well as HIF-2-responsive genes expression under hypoxic conditions. These findings highlight the role of ACSS2 in metabolic adaptation, tumor progression, and its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target in ovarian cancer.ConclusionsACSS2 is a critical regulator of ovarian cancer growth and invasiveness under hypoxic stress. Targeting ACSS2 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for managing ovarian cancer. Further studies are warranted to explore its clinical potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01815-y.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer, the fifth most common cause of death in women and the leading cause of death among all gynecological cancers, remains a significant global health burden [1]. This disease includes malignancies originating from the ovary, fallopian tubes, and peritoneum, all of which share tissue origins, clinical care, and treatment strategies [2]. Ovarian cancer is classified into epithelial, stromal, or germ cell, depending on the originating cell type. Epithelial ovarian cancer, the most aggressive and prevalent type, arises from the ovarian epithelium. Stromal class mainly derives from the stroma of the ovary whereas germ cell ovarian cancer develops from the oocytes [1, 3]. While the precise pathophysiology of ovarian cancer remains unknown, multiple hypotheses and risk factors shed light on potential mechanisms. These includes incessant ovulation, age, nulliparity, familial history of breast and ovarian cancer, genetic mutations (mainly in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes), hormonal imbalance, and chronic inflammation [4]. Current treatment for ovarian cancer typically combines surgery and chemotherapy. However, high recurrence rates and increasing drug resistance highlight the urgent need for novel therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes [1].

One potential target for treatment of ovarian cancer is Acyl-CoA Synthetase Short Chain Family Member 2 (ACSS2), a pivotal enzyme of the acetyl-CoA Synthetase (ACSS) family. ACSS2 catalyzes the conversion of acetate into acetyl-CoA in a reaction that requires ATP. ACSS2 predominantly resides in the cytosol, where it contributes to lipid biogenesis and other anabolic processes [5]. However, Acss2 also translocates to the nucleus in response to physiological stress conditions such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and injury where it exhibits significant roles in metabolic reprogramming, gene transcription, cell survival, and autophagy [6]. In the nucleus, ACSS2 increases acetyl-CoA levels, promoting acetylation of several histones and non-histones proteins including the stress-responsive transcription factor Hypoxia Inducible Factor 2 (HIF-2). The net effect of nuclear acetylation by ACSS2 is to modulate cellular processes including metabolic flux [7].

ACSS2 plays a critical role in cellular adaptation to the tumor microenvironment (TME), underscoring its importance in multiple cancer types, including pancreatic, lung, breast, and glioblastoma. Studies indicate that cancer cells, particularly under the metabolic stress of the TME, upregulate ACSS2 to survive [8, 9]. The expression of ACSS2 in these different types of cancer has been associated with higher-grade tumors and poor survival in comparison to cancers with low ACSS2 expression, likely due to its regulatory impact on proliferation, lipid synthesis, invasion, and migration in various cancers [8, 10].

Although ACSS2 has been studied extensively across different cancers, its expression in ovarian cancer and its role in ovarian cancer cell biology, especially under hypoxic conditions, is less well understood. In this report, we examine ACSS2 expression in the nuclei of human high grade ovarian cancer samples compared to low grade or border line serous carcinoma, as well as in normal ovarian epithelium and fallopian tube. We also assess the effect of ACSS2 inhibition on proliferation, colony and spheroid formation, migration, invasion, PI3K activation, and HIF-responsive genes expression of Skov3 and PA-1 ovarian cancer cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. The results of these studies provide important clinical insights into ACSS2 expression in ovarian cancers that may be leveraged in staging and highlight key functional roles for ACSS2 in ovarian cancer cell biology, thereby identifying a potentially key therapeutic target for this deadly and refractory cancer.

Materials and methods

Immunohistochemistry of human ovarian cancer samples

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on 4 μm thick sections of 38 de-identified, pre-collected formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) ovarian tumor tissues, which included low-grade, borderline, and high-grade serous carcinoma as well as normal ovarian and fallopian tube tissues served as a control. A rabbit monoclonal anti-ACSS2 primary antibody (D19C6, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was used for staining. The primary antibody was diluted at a ratio of 1:150. Staining was carried out using the Benchmark Ultra System (Ventana Medical Systems, USA), and antibody detection was performed with the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (760 − 700, Ventana Medical Systems, USA).

Immunohistochemistry scoring

ACSS2 staining in tissue samples was assessed using the H-score method in immunohistochemistry (IHC). Nuclear staining intensity was categorized on a scale from 0 to 3 (0 = no staining, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong), and the percentage of cells at each intensity level was estimated. The H-score was then calculated using the formula: H-score=∑(Intensity level×Percentage of cells at that intensity). The H-score was applied across all samples to enable quantitative comparison of ACSS2 nuclear expression.

Cell culture and treatment

Two ovarian cancer lines, Skov-3 (HTB-77, ATCC, USA) and PA-1 (CRL-1572, ATCC, USA), were used. Skov-3 is derived from the ovary of a 64-year-old female with adenocarcinoma and PA-1 is isolated from a 12-year-old Caucasian female with teratocarcinoma. Cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (D6429, Sigma, Germany) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (F9665, Sigma, Germany) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Hypoxia was induced using hypoxia chamber (27310, Stem Cell Technologies, Canada) following the manufacturer’s instruction. ACSS2 was inhibited using ACSS2 chemical inhibitor (HY-104032, MedChemExpress, USA) resuspended in DMSO (D2650, Sigma, Germany). The chamber was purged for 15 min with mixed gas (1% oxygen, 5% CO2, 94% nitrogen) to fill the chamber and the cells were incubated in the chamber for the indicated time points.

Proliferation assay

Cytotoxicity of the ACSS2 inhibitor was determined using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide assay (MTT) (M5655, Sigma, Germany). Skov-3 (5000 cells/well) and PA-1 (6000 cells/well) cells were seeded in 96-well plates in triplicates, and then treated with increasing concentrations of ACSS2 inhibitor. Control cells were treated with the vehicle DMSO. At each time point (24, 48, 72 h post treatment), MTT solution was added to each well at the final concentration of 5 mg/mL. The formed formazan crystals were solubilized 3 h later by adding 0.1 g/mL sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (L5750, Sigma, Germany) in 0.83µ/1mL 37% HCL. Blank wells with media alone were used as blanks. Optical absorption was measured using Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 570 nm. Data were plotted as percentage of cell viability relative to control (DMSO).

Colony formation assay (CFA)

Skov-3 and PA-1 were seeded in 6-well plates in complete medium at a low cell density (500 and 1000 cells/well for Skov-3 and PA-1, respectively). For optimal colony formation, cells were treated after 48 h with different concentrations of ACSS2 inhibitor. Control cells were treated with the vehicle, DMSO (D2650, Sigma, Germany). After 9 days, colonies were fixed with cold methanol for 10 min, then stained with 0.5% crystal violet (0.005 g/mL crystal violet in 25% methanol) to identify individual colonies. Cells were then imaged using Olympus CKX53 trinocular inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) and colonies were counted using ImageJ software. Data were reported as the percentage of formed colonies relative to vehicle (DMSO).

Spheroid assay

For the three-dimensional spheroid formation, we used the hanging-drop technique as previously described [11]. 20µL of cell suspension (7.5k cells/20µL) in complete media with increasing concentration of ACSS2 inhibitor or the vehicle (DMSO, D2650, Sigma, Germany) were cultured on an inverted low attachment Petri’s dish lid. Spheroids were imaged at 48 h post treatment using Olympus CKX53 trinocular inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). ImageJ software was used to measure the surface area of each formed spheroid. Data were reported as percentage of increase (Skov-3) or decrease (PA-1) in the surface area with respect to control (DMSO).

Migration and invasion assays

For cell migration, 8 × 104 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a 24-well Transwell inserts (353096, Corning, USA) in media with 1% FBS (F9665, Sigma, Germany). Complete media with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. For invasion, cells were starved using incomplete media (1% FBS) overnight, and inserts were coated with 45µL of Matrigel (354230, Corning, USA) prior to seeding. After 24 h, cells that migrated/invaded through the transwell were fixed on the membrane using 4% paraformaldehyde (15714, EMS, USA) applied for 10 min at room temperature and then were stained using crystal violet. Crystal violet was then dissolved using 10% SDS solution, and optical absorption was measured using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 590 nm. Data were plotted as percentage of cellular migration/invasion with respect to control (DMSO).

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (20–188, Merck, Germany) and protease inhibitor cocktail (S8830-20TAB, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Protein concentration was determined using DC protein assay kit (5000116, Bio-Rad, USA). 10 µg of proteins were loaded, separated onto a 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel, subjected to electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes (1620177, Bio-Rad, USA). After blocking the membranes in 5% skim milk, the blots were incubated with PI3K primary antibody (20584-1-AP, Proteintech, Germany), HIF-2 primary antibody (26422-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), or β-actin primary antibody (13E5, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) overnight at 4 °C. This was followed by an incubation for 2 h with HRP-conjugated anti rabbit secondary antibody (7074 S, Cell signaling technology, USA). The protein bands were then developed using clarity ECL substrate (1705061, Bio-Rad, USA), imaged using ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (12003153, Bio-rad, USA), and quantified using ImageJ software. Band intensities were normalized to β-actin, and the data were presented as relative intensity ratios with respect to control (DMSO).

RTqPCR

RNA was extracted using RNeasy kit (74134, Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This was followed by cDNA synthesis from 2 µg of total RNA using iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (1708891, Bio-Rad, USA) as per manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting cDNA was amplified with iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (1725121, Bio-Rad, USA) and CFX96 RT-PCR machine (Bio-rad, USA) using primers for HIF-responsive genes: VEGFa, PAI1, PGK1 (IDT, USA), and MMP9 (TIB MOLBIOL, Germany) (Table S1). Expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH (TIB MOLBIOL, Germany) (Table S1), and the results analyzed using the ΔΔCq method. Data were normalized to GAPDH expression levels and reported as relative fold change with respect to the control (DMSO).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 5 with t-test (Mann Whitney test), one sample t-test, Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA proceeded by Dunn’s multiple comparison test, and Bonferroni post-test preceded by two-way ANOVA. Data are represented as mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). P-values < 0.05 were considered significant, represented as follows: * (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.01), and *** (p < 0.001).

Results

ACSS2 is overexpressed and predominantly localized to the nucleus in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma

ACSS2 localizes to the nucleus under stress conditions and in various cancer types, where it presumably plays a critical role in metabolic adaptation and tumor progression [6, 12]. To document expression and localization of ACSS2 in ovarian cancer, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was performed on preexisting paraffin-embedded tissue sections (Fig. 1). The samples included 13 high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma tissues, 11 borderline/low-grade serous ovarian tumor tissues, 8 normal ovarian epithelium samples, and 9 normal fallopian tube samples. IHC staining was assessed to evaluate the intensity and localization of ACSS2 in both the cytoplasm and nucleus. Nuclear localization was quantitatively analyzed using the H-score method, which combines the intensity of staining and the percentage of positive nuclei. Our results revealed a significant overexpression of ACSS2 in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma tissues compared to borderline/low-grade ovarian tumor, normal ovarian epithelium, and normal fallopian tubes (Fig. 1A, B, C and D). Notably, nuclear staining of ACSS2 was markedly higher in high-grade carcinoma samples than in the other groups (Fig. 1E).

ACSS2 inhibition impedes Skov-3 and PA-1 proliferation under hypoxic conditions

In response to chronic hypoxia exposure, cancer cells rely on ACSS2 to drive survival and proliferation [8]. However, the role of ACSS2 in ovarian cancer proliferation during hypoxia remains poorly understood. To address this, we assessed the effect of ACSS2 inhibition on the viability of Skov-3 and PA-1 ovarian cancer cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions using the MTT assay (Fig. 2). Skov-3 and PA-1 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of an ACSS2 inhibitor for 24, 48, and 72 h. ACSS2 inhibition significantly reduced cellular proliferation in both cell lines in a concentration-dependent manner at 48 h compared to DMSO-treated controls (Fig. 2A and C). This inhibitory effect was not time-dependent, as similar proliferation inhibition levels were observed at all time points (Figure S1A-D). Notably, PA-1 cells were more sensitive to ACSS2 inhibition than Skov-3 cells, exhibiting greater proliferation inhibition at lower ACSS2 inhibitor concentrations. Interestingly, both cell lines demonstrated increased sensitivity to ACSS2 inhibition under hypoxic conditions. Proliferation of Skov-3 and PA-1 cells cultured in hypoxia for 48 h was significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner compared to normoxic conditions. The IC50 for Skov-3 decreased from 28.9 µM under normoxia to 10 µM under hypoxia, while the IC50 for PA-1 decreased from 16.8 µM under normoxia to 5 µM under hypoxia (Fig. 2B and D).

ACSS2 inhibition impairs ovarian cancer colony formation and 3D spheroid growth under hypoxic conditions

Given the observed effects of ACSS2 inhibition on cell proliferation, we next aimed to investigate the role of ACSS2 in tumor cell growth properties of Skov-3 and PA-1 cells using colony formation and three-dimensional (3D) spheroid models (Fig. 3). ACSS2 inhibition significantly impaired the long-term colony-forming ability of both ovarian cancer cell lines, with a notable reduction in the percentage of colonies formed in Skov-3 cells treated with 10 µM and PA-1 cells treated with 2.5 µM of the ACSS2 inhibitor (Fig. 3A-D). ACSS2 has also been implicated in regulating tumor spheroid formation [13, 14]. However, its role in ovarian cancer spheroid formation under hypoxic conditions remains unexamined. Skov-3 cells formed loosely cohesive spheroids, and treatment with increasing ACSS2 inhibitor concentrations over 48 h disrupted spheroid integrity, as evident by an increase in spheroid surface area (Fig. 3E and F). Conversely, PA-1 cells formed compact spheroids, and treatment with the inhibitor significantly reduced the surface area of these compact spheroids (Fig. 3G and H). Interestingly, both ovarian cancer cell lines exhibited enhanced sensitivity to ACSS2 inhibition under hypoxic conditions. Lower concentrations of the inhibitor were sufficient to inhibit spheroid formation compared to normoxic conditions in both cell lines (Fig. 3I-L).

ACSS2 inhibition hinders cellular migration and invasion under hypoxic Conditions

ACSS2 has been shown to regulate cancer cell migration and invasion in various cancer types under stress conditions [12, 15, 16]. To investigate the role of ACSS2 in ovarian cancer epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) during hypoxia, transwell migration and invasion assays were performed with Skov-3 and PA-1 cells (Fig. 4). Under normoxic conditions, ACSS2 inhibition significantly reduced migration and invasion of both Skov-3 and PA-1 cells. Notably, under hypoxic conditions, this inhibitory effect was further enhanced consistent with our findings described above. Skov-3 cells treated with 10 µM and 15 µM ACSS2 inhibitor and PA-1 cells treated with 5 µM and 10 µM exhibited a pronounced reduction in migration compared to normoxic conditions (Fig. 4A, B, E and F). Similarly, the invasion potential of both cell lines was significantly diminished following ACSS2 inhibition under hypoxia, demonstrating a higher sensitivity to the inhibitor compared to normoxic conditions (Fig. 4C, D, G and H).

ACSS2 inhibition modulates PI3K signaling in ovarian cancer

To explore the role of ACSS2 in key signaling pathways regulated by this enzyme, we examined the effect of ACSS2 inhibition on PI3K signaling in Skov-3 and PA-1 cells (Fig. 5A-D). The PI3K pathway is critical for regulating cancer cell metabolism, survival, and metastasis [17]. Western blot analysis showed that PI3K protein levels in Skov-3 cells remained unchanged under hypoxia in the absence of ACSS2 inhibition. Treatment with 10 or 15 µM ACSS2 inhibitor for 24 h did not significantly reduced PI3K expression under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. In contrast, PA-1 cells displayed elevated PI3K expression in response to hypoxia; however, treatment with 10 µM ACSS2 inhibitor decreased PI3K levels under both oxygen conditions.

ACSS2 inhibition reduces HIF-2α stability and downstream gene expression in ovarian cancer cells

To assess whether ACSS2 inhibition affects HIF-2α protein stability, we performed Western blot analysis in Skov-3 and PA-1 cells treated with ACSS2 inhibitor under normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6A-D). In Skov-3 cells, HIF-2α protein levels were elevated under hypoxia compared to normoxia. Treatment with 15 µM and 20 µM ACSS2 inhibitor for 24 h led to a dose-dependent reduction in HIF-2α levels under both oxygen conditions. Similarly, PA-1 cells showed increased HIF-2α expression under hypoxia, which was diminished following treatment with 5 µM and 10 µM ACSS2 inhibitor. To evaluate the downstream effects of this reduction, we measured the expression of HIF-responsive genes VEGFa, MMP9, PAI1, and PGK1 using RT-qPCR. PGK1 is a selective target of HIF-1, while VEGFa, MMP9, and PAI1 are either HIF-2 selective targets or induced by both HIF isoforms [18]. As expected, ACSS2 inhibition led to a marked decrease in the expression of all four genes in both cell lines under hypoxia, compared to DMSO-treated cells, highlighting a heightened sensitivity of HIF target genes to ACSS2 inhibition during hypoxia.

Discussion

The tumor microenvironment presents a highly stressful context for cancer cells, requiring them to undergo metabolic reprogramming and cell cycle adaptations for survival. ACSS2 has emerged as a critical player in this process. In response to stressors such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and injury, ACSS2 translocate into the nucleus, where it increases the nuclear acetyl-CoA levels essential for the acetylation of histones and non-histones protein. This function positions ACSS2 as a pivotal epigenetic regulator, with a well-established role in tumorigenesis. ACSS2 overexpression has been observed in various cancers, including liver, breast, renal, cervical, bladder, and non-small-cell lung carcinomas. This increase in ACSS2 expression was correlated with advanced disease stages, aggressiveness, and poor prognosis in several cancer types [8]. Recently, ACSS2 overexpression was also documented in ovarian cancer tissue specimens compared to normal fallopian tube tissues [14].

In this study, we confirm and expand upon these earlier reports. Our immunohistochemistry analysis of ovarian carcinoma specimens corroborates the earlier findings of ACSS2 overexpression in ovarian cancer, while also establishing a differential localization pattern of ACSS2. We demonstrate that nuclear ACSS2 expression levels are elevated in high-grade versus low-grade ovarian carcinoma tissues with the latter exhibiting a lower and more diffused expression pattern indicating a possibly important role for nuclear-localized ACSS2 in advanced disease stages. Although increasing sample size would strengthen these observations, expanding these studies is limited by the challenges of collecting adequate samples of low-grade and serous borderline cases, as ovarian cancer is often diagnosed at advanced stages. Nevertheless, our findings highlight a potential use of ACSS2 immunohistochemistry as an informative staging marker for ovarian cancers.

While ACSS2 has been extensively studied in several tumor types, its functional role in ovarian cancer has only recently been elucidated. ACSS2 upregulation induces quiescence in ovarian cancer cells, underscoring a potential role in ovarian cancer cell survival [18]. Consistent with this observation, ACSS2 inhibition under normoxic conditions promotes autophagy, reduces malignancy, and impedes chemoresistance of ovarian cancer cells [14]. However, the role of ACSS2 during hypoxia and a mechanistic understanding of ACSS2 in ovarian cancer remains poorly understood. Hypoxia is a hallmark of the tumor microenvironment, significantly influencing cancer progression. ACSS2 plays a critical role in development and survival of various cancer types under hypoxia including breast and prostate cancer cells [13]. In this study, we demonstrate the importance of ACSS2 for cellular proliferation of Skov-3 and PA-1 ovarian cancer cells under normoxic conditions [14]. We also show that hypoxia increased sensitivity of both cell lines to ACSS2 inhibition compared to normoxic conditions. Moreover, we also show that ACSS2 inhibition significantly impedes colony formation of not just Skov-3 [] cells, but also PA-1 cells. These findings in summary indicate that tumor cell properties of Skov-3 and PA-1 cells are more dependent on ACSS2 for proliferation under hypoxic conditions.

We also show for the first time that spheroid formation, which more accurately mimic the in vivo tumor environment [19], differs for Skov-3 and PA-1 cells. PA-1 cells form more compact spheres compared to Skov-3, consistent with earlier studies in ovarian cancer where more epithelial ovarian cancer cell lines like Skov-3 form more loosely cohesive spheroids compared to less epithelial and more fibroblast-like ovarian cell lines like PA-1 [20]. Hypoxia further amplifies the effects of ACSS2 inhibition on spheroid formation. Ovarian cancer spheroid cells modulate their metabolism as an adaptive response to hypoxic conditions, hence promoting tumor progression and chemoresistance [21]. These findings align with previous studies in prostate cancer where ACSS2 knockdown disrupted spheroid formation, particularly under cellular stress accompanying low-serum conditions [13]. Our data suggest that Skov-3 and PA-1 ovarian cancer cells also rely heavily on ACSS2 for survival and 3D spheroid formation, with its impact being more pronounced under hypoxic conditions.

Metastasis, the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, necessitates the identification of novel targets to impede this process [22]. The role of ACSS2 in metastasis is context-dependent, promoting migration and invasion in some cancers, such as colon and melanoma, while inhibiting these processes in others, such as hepatocellular carcinoma [12, 16, 23]. In ovarian cancer, ACSS2 has been shown to promote metastasis under normoxic conditions, but its role during hypoxia was not reported [14]. Hypoxia, a critical factor in the tumor microenvironment, influences metastasis through transcriptional and translational rewiring [24, 25]. Our migration and invasion assays revealed that ACSS2 plays a pro-metastatic role in ovarian cancer, particularly under hypoxic conditions. This shift highlights the tumor’s reliance on ACSS2 during hypoxic stress to facilitate dissemination. Given ACSS2’s established role as an epigenetic regulator conferring stress resistance and survival, it is plausible that its activity during hypoxia underpins the enhanced metastatic potential of ovarian cancer cells. These findings underscore the importance of targeting ACSS2 to mitigate ovarian cancer progression, particularly in hypoxia-driven microenvironments.

We lastly sought to study the effect of ACSS2 inhibition on two key cellular signaling pathways, PI3K and HIF2, both well-established drivers of ovarian cancer progression. Our findings support a role for ACSS2 in sustaining these pathways under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, suggesting that its enzymatic function is not merely supportive of baseline metabolism but is actively involved in stress adaptation. Deregulated PI3K activity has been observed in various cancers, including breast, colon, and hematological malignancies [26]. In ovarian cancer, ACSS2 is implicated in modulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, with ACSS2 downregulation inhibiting Akt/mTOR signaling under normoxia without significantly affecting autophagy and apoptosis [14]. However, the impact of ACSS2 inhibition on PI3K during hypoxia has not been reported. Our data demonstrate that the effect of ACSS2 inhibition on PI3K signaling persisted under hypoxia in PA-1 cells, despite the known activation of PI3K as a compensatory response to metabolic stress. This highlights a potential therapeutic advantage of ACSS2 targeting in hypoxic tumors, which are often resistant to conventional PI3K inhibitors due to pathway redundancy and adaptation [27]. For example, in pancreatic cancer, hypoxia-driven activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway confers resistance to chemotherapeutics [28]. The ability of ACSS2 inhibition to suppress PI3K under both oxygen tensions suggests that acetate metabolism might act as a parallel nutrient-sensing cue, independent of oxygen, to regulate this oncogenic axis. In contrast, PI3K expression was not upregulated in Skov-3 cells under hypoxia, and no significant changes were observed upon ACSS2 inhibition. This suggests that the effect of ACSS2 inhibition on the proliferation and invasiveness of Skov-3 cells in hypoxia is likely PI3K-independent, indicating that distinct downstream pathways may mediate the ACSS2-driven phenotype in these cells.

In contrast to PI3K signaling, hypoxia significantly influenced the response of the HIF pathway to ACSS2 inhibition in both Skov-3 and PA-1 cells. HIF-1 and HIF-2, key regulators of cellular adaptation to low oxygen levels, are strongly associated with tumor progression across multiple cancer types [29]. Our data show that ACSS2 inhibition downregulates HIF-2α level and its downstream transcriptional program further extends its relevance as a therapeutic target in hypoxic tumors. Prior studies have shown that ACSS2 provides nuclear acetyl-CoA for HIF-2α acetylation, which is critical for its transcriptional activity [30]. Our data are consistent with these reports and reinforce the model in which ACSS2 supports the stability and function of HIF-2 under hypoxia. While ACSS2 is a regulator of the HIF-2 signaling axis, its role in HIF-1 activation remains unclear and may differ in a cell-type specific manner [12, 31]. Our data reveal that ACSS2 inhibition downregulated HIF-2 target gene expression under hypoxic conditions. Interestingly, we also observed a significant reduction in PGK1, generally considered a more HIF-1 selective target gene. The downregulation of both HIF-2 and HIF-1 target genes suggests that ACSS2 may play a broader regulatory role across the HIF axis, potentially through shared acetylation co-factors or chromatin remodeling mechanisms.

Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that this regulatory relationship may be bidirectional. A recent study in pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms demonstrated that HIF-1α directly binds the promoter of ACSS2, upregulating its expression during hypoxia [32]. This observation introduces a compelling feed-forward loop, wherein hypoxia-induced HIF activity enhances ACSS2 expression, which in turn stabilizes and amplifies HIF signaling. This loop could reinforce transcriptional responses to hypoxia and sustain metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells. Consistent with this model, recent studies showed that lipid metabolism via ACSS2 contributes to stress adaptation and chromatin accessibility, further implicating ACSS2 in broader epigenetic regulation under metabolic stress [33, 34]. Further studies are needed to investigate whether a similar regulatory mechanism exists for HIF-2α, particularly in the context of ovarian cancer. In summary, our findings align with prior reports linking HIF signaling to DNA repair, EMT, angiogenesis, and metastasis [35–38].

One implication of this study is that ACSS2 expression and nuclear localization signal a poor prognostic marker for ovarian cancer. From a mechanistic standpoint, by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion when experiencing the hypoxic and other stressors associated with the tumor microenvironment, ACSS2 may rescue ovarian cancer cells from an otherwise lethal demise in solid tumors. Consistent with this prediction, ACSS2 inhibition impedes ovarian tumor cell properties in cell-based and spheroid formation assays under both normoxic as well as hypoxic conditions. Our findings further suggest that ACSS2 drives ovarian cancer progression and survival under hypoxic conditions by simultaneously activating PI3K and HIF signaling pathways. The combined downregulation of these pathways in response to ACSS2 inhibition may account for the reported heightened sensitivity of Skov-3 and PA-1 cells during hypoxia. This dual regulatory role of ACSS2 underscores its potential as a therapeutic target for ovarian cancer. Further investigations are required to define the precise molecular interactions and signaling pathways regulated by ACSS2, and to better characterize the regulatory relationship between HIF signaling and ACSS2 function in ovarian cancer. In parallel, additional studies are needed to confirm the in vivo role of ACSS2 and to assess the therapeutic utility of ACSS2 inhibition in ovarian cancer progression and treatment, respectively.
