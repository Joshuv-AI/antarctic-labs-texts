Comparing video-based and four-step simulation training in paediatric basic life support: a pilot randomised controlled study
Authors: Farah Thabet, Habib Besbes, Houda Ajmi, Seyfeddine Zayani, Jalel Chemli, Slaheddine Chouchane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe conducted a pilot randomised controlled trial in two teaching hospitals in Tunisia to compare video-based instruction and four-step low-fidelity simulation for paediatric Basic Life Support (BLS) training among interns and nurses. Knowledge was assessed using a 15-item multiple-choice questionnaire adapted from European Resuscitation Council course materials, practical skills were evaluated through a standardised SimBaby paediatric cardiac arrest scenario and learner satisfaction was measured using a 5-point Likert scale. 60 participants were enrolled (33 interns, 27 nurses). Both training modalities resulted in significant immediate improvements in knowledge and skills (p<0.001). No statistically significant differences were detected between video-based and simulation-based training immediately after training. At 3 months, retention assessment demonstrated a significant decline in knowledge and skills across both groups. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested higher skill scores among nurses trained with simulation compared with video-based instruction. All participants reported high satisfaction, with no differences between groups. Both video-based instruction and low-fidelity simulation improved paediatric BLS knowledge and skills in this pilot study; subgroup findings were exploratory. Larger multicentre studies are warranted.

Basic Life Support (BLS) is a core competency for healthcare providers. Inadequate proficiency contributes to poor outcomes in cardiac arrest, whereas structured training improves survival and neurological recovery.1 The European Resuscitation Council (ERC)2 and the American Heart Association recommend standardised BLS training.3 Traditional instructor-led simulation remains the cornerstone of BLS education; however, it is resource-intensive and difficult to implement at scale in low-income and middle-income settings. Video-based instruction has emerged as a flexible alternative, allowing standardised content delivery with reduced instructor dependence; it was endorsed by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation as an alternative or adjunct to instructor-led training. Comparative evidence in paediatric BLS training remains limited. This study aims to compare video-based instruction and four-step low-fidelity simulation for paediatric BLS training among interns and nurses.

We conducted a pilot randomised controlled educational trial (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 compliant (online supplemental material) between January and March 2024 in two Tunisian teaching hospitals. Eligible participants were paediatric interns and nurses without formal paediatric BLS certification within the preceding 2 years. Nurses were early-career staff with limited exposure to paediatric resuscitation (mean professional experience 2.0±1.0 years). Participants were excluded if absent on the training day or post-call.

Participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to video-based or simulation-based training using a computer-generated random sequence. Allocation concealment was not formally implemented, and outcome assessors were not blinded.

The video group received standardised paediatric BLS instruction based on ERC course videos, with instructor commentary. The simulation group received instructor-led training using Peyton’s four-step approach. Both interventions covered identical ERC 2021 paediatric BLS content, including airway management, chest compressions, ventilation and use of an automated external defibrillator.

Knowledge was assessed using a 15-item multiple-choice questionnaire adapted from ERC course materials, administered before training, immediately after training and at 3 months. As no validated thresholds exist for BLS competency or proficiency, knowledge scores were analysed as continuous variables. Practical skills were evaluated using a standardised SimBaby paediatric cardiac arrest scenario and scored with a 10-item checklist, each item rated on a 0–2 scale. Performance was assessed by two trained assessors. Learner satisfaction was evaluated post-training using a 5-point Likert scale. As this was a pilot study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed and analyses were exploratory.

60 participants were enrolled (33 interns, 27 nurses). 31 were allocated to video-based training and 29 to simulation-based training (interns: 17 video, 16 simulation, nurses: 14 video, 13 simulation). Baseline knowledge scores were higher among interns than nurses (66.7% vs 40.0%, p<0.001).

Median knowledge scores at three time points—pre-test and immediate post-test (total group n=60; simulation group n=29; video group n=31), and 3-month retention (total group n=55; simulation n=25; video n=30).

Both video-based instruction and low-fidelity simulation improved paediatric BLS knowledge and skills in this pilot study. No statistically significant differences were detected between training modalities overall. Simulation showed a potential advantage for nurses, although this finding is consistent with evidence that novice providers may benefit more from hands-on practice and feedback,4 5 it should be interpreted as exploratory given the pilot design and limited sample size of our study. The observed decline in knowledge and skills at 3 months highlights the need for regular refresher training.6 The strengths of this study include the randomised design and ERC-based content while limitations include the pilot nature, single-country setting and absence of long-term clinical outcome assessment. Larger multicentre studies are warranted.
