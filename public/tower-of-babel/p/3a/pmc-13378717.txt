Ecological restructuring of the nonbacterial fecal microbiome in obesity across human cohorts
Authors: Laura Gallardo-Nuell, Marisel Rosell-Díaz, Josep Garre-Olmo, Josep Puig, Rafael Ramos, Javier Pons, Vicente Pérez-Brocal, Andrés Moya, Jordi Mayneris-Perxachs, José Manuel Fernández-Real
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObesity is a complex metabolic disorder increasingly linked to alterations in the gut microbiome. While most research has focused on bacterial communities, the contribution of nonbacterial components including viruses, archaea, and eukaryotic microorganisms remains insufficiently characterized. Here, we performed a multicohort analysis to investigate the role of the nonbacterial gut microbiome in obesity across three independent human cohorts. Using compositional analyses adjusted for key covariates and network based approaches, we identified consistent multikingdom alterations associated with obesity. Individuals without obesity showed a reproducible enrichment of methanogenic archaea, particularly Methanobrevibacter smithii and Methanobrevibacter millerae, whereas individuals with obesity were characterized by increased abundance of bacteriophages from the class Caudoviricetes. In an elderly cohort, eukaryotic taxa such as Blastocystis spp. were additionally associated with the without obesity group. These patterns were largely consistent across cohorts and robust to sex stratification. Beyond taxonomic differences, ecological network analyses revealed substantial reorganization of microbial interactions in obesity. The identity and composition of hub taxa differed significantly between obesity and without obesity networks across all cohorts, indicating a shift in the taxa occupying central ecological roles. Notably, these differences were observed even when similar microbial kingdoms were represented, underscoring the importance of species-level resolution. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that obesity is associated with coordinated compositional and ecological alterations across the nonbacterial gut microbiome. This multikingdom perspective expands current understanding of microbiome dysbiosis in metabolic disease and highlights the archaeome and virome as potential contributors to host metabolic health.

Introduction

Obesity is a multifactorial metabolic disorder characterized by excessive accumulation of body fat and is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, and numerous other chronic conditions. 1 , 2 Over the past two decades, research on the gut microbiome has revealed strong links between microbial composition and metabolic health, suggesting that gut microbes play a critical role in nutrient processing, host energy balance, and inflammation. 3 , 4 Recent integrative metagenomics work has further shown that obesity is accompanied by coordinated taxonomic and functional shifts across the gut microbiome, including both bacterial and viral components. 5 However, most studies to date have focused almost exclusively on bacterial communities, leaving the broader non‑bacterial microbiome (including viruses, archaea, and eukaryotic microorganisms) comparatively underexplored in the context of obesity. 6-8 A recent comprehensive review on the gut non‑bacterial microbiome in obesity underscores this gap, highlighting that virome, mycobiome, and archaeome alterations are repeatedly observed but remain mechanistically unresolved. 9

Emerging evidence suggests that these non‑bacterial components of the gut ecosystem contribute significantly to microbial ecology and host physiology. 5 , 9 Archaea, particularly methanogens such as Methanobrevibacter smithii, consume hydrogen produced by bacterial fermentation, thereby facilitating efficient energy extraction and stabilizing gut fermentation networks. 10 , 11 In a large meta‑analysis of fecal metagenomes, M. smithii has been identified as a key species differing between individuals with obesity and those without, and as a contributor to metabolically healthier microbial profiles 5 . Meanwhile, the gut virome, dominated by bacteriophages from classes like Caudoviricetes, plays an essential role in shaping bacterial community composition through predation, lysogeny, and horizontal gene transfer. 5 , 12 The gut eukaryome, represented by protozoa and fungi such as Blastocystis spp., has also been linked to microbial diversity and healthy gut function and is increasingly recognized as part of a broader, multi‑kingdom dysbiosis in obesity. 9 , 13 , 14 Yet, integrative studies simultaneously assessing viral, archaeal, and eukaryotic communities within the same individuals, and relating them to ecological network structure, remain scarce. 5 , 9

In this study, the nonbacterial gut microbiome in relation to obesity was investigated across three independent human cohorts. Specifically, viral, archaeal and eukaryotic communities were analyzed using complementary compositional and network-based approaches to characterize differences in microbial abundance, prevalence, and ecological connectivity between obesity groups. Given previous reports implicating nonbacterial members of the gut microbiome in metabolic regulation and microbial ecosystem stability, we hypothesized that obesity would be associated with alterations across these microbial kingdoms and their interaction networks. By integrating multi-cohort metagenomic profiling with ecological network analyses, this work aims to expand current understanding of obesity-associated dysbiosis beyond bacterial communities and to provide a broader view of multi-kingdom gut microbial organization in metabolic disease.

These three cohorts were selected because they provide independent human datasets with available shotgun metagenomic sequencing and detailed clinical phenotyping. The IRONMET and METASUCC cohorts are specifically enriched for obesity-related metabolic characterization, whereas the Aging Imageomics cohort represents a broader aging population. The inclusion of these independent cohorts enabled cross-cohort evaluation of nonbacterial microbiome alterations associated with obesity and assessment of the reproducibility of microbial associations across distinct study populations.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance using appropriate statistical tests. Data are presented as counts and percentages for categorical variables, mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables, and median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Fecal DNA extraction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

IRONMET and METASUCC cohorts

Total genomic DNA was extracted from frozen stool samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Stool Kit (QIAGEN). DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each sample, 1 ng of DNA (0.2 ng/μl) was used for shotgun metagenomic library preparation with the Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads at the FISABIO Sequencing and Bioinformatics Service (Valencia, Spain).

Aging Imageomics cohort

DNA extraction was performed using the PowerSoil DNA Extraction Kit (MO BIO Laboratories). Library preparation was carried out using 400 to 500 ng of DNA with the Illumina DNA Prep kit. Library quality was assessed using the TapeStation High Sensitivity DNA kit (Agilent Technologies), and DNA concentration was measured with a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen). Libraries passing quality control were pooled in equimolar concentrations and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq2000 platform using paired-end 150 cycle chemistry. Raw reads were filtered for quality (QV > 30) using an in-house Python script.

Bioinformatic processing

FASTQ files from all cohorts were preprocessed using fastp for quality control, adapter trimming, and filtering. 16 Filtered reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using Bowtie2 to remove host-derived sequences. Unmapped reads were processed using SqueezeMeta v1.3.1 in co assembly mode. 17 Contigs were assembled with MEGAHIT, 18 and reads were mapped back to contigs using Bowtie2. 19 Open reading frames were predicted with Prodigal, 20 and taxonomic annotation was performed using Kaiju v1.6.2. 21

Diversity analyses

Alpha diversity of the nonbacterial microbiome was assessed at the species level using the Observed richness, ACE richness estimator, and Fisher diversity index implemented in the phyloseq R package. 22 Differences in alpha diversity between obesity groups were evaluated independently for each cohort using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Statistical significance was defined as #p < 0.1, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

To evaluate overall differences in microbial community composition, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on centered log-ratio (CLR)-transformed abundance data. Differences in community composition between obesity groups were assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Euclidean distances. Pairwise group comparisons were performed using the pairwise.adonis function.

Metagenomic and network analysis

Before downstream microbiome analyses, low-abundance and low-prevalence species were filtered to reduce sparsity and improve statistical robustness. For each cohort, species were retained if they reached a minimum abundance corresponding to at least 10 counts in at least 10% of samples, based on counts-per-million normalization. In addition, species detected in fewer than 20% of samples were excluded from subsequent analyses. The same filtering criteria were consistently applied across all three cohorts prior to differential abundance and network analszes.

Associations between microbial abundance and obesity status were evaluated using ANCOM-BC, which accounts for compositionality and sampling bias through a log linear regression framework with sample-specific offsets. 23 Models including the entire cohort were adjusted for age and sex, whereas sex-stratified analyses were adjusted for age only.

To correct for multiple testing, P values were adjusted using the sequential goodness of fit method implemented in the SGoF R package. 24 Compared with conventional FDR-based approaches, SGoF maintains statistical power as the number of tests increases, making it particularly suitable for high-dimensional metagenomic datasets with large numbers of features and relatively limited sample sizes. Statistical significance was set at adjusted P < 0.1.

Differentially abundant species identified by ANCOM-BC were visualized using heatmaps of log fold-change values and Venn diagrams showing overlap between significant taxa across cohorts/analyses.

To assess reproducibility and distinguish generalizable from cohort-specific findings, we performed a fixed-effects meta-analysis of logFC estimates from ANCOM-BC differential abundance analysis. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, with findings classified as: generalizable (I2 < 25%, n = 119), intermediate (25% ≤ I2 < 75%, n = 50), or cohort-specific (I2 ≥ 75%, n = 9).

Prior to network inference, microbial profiles from all three cohorts were processed consistently at the genus level. Taxa were agglomerated from species to genus level using taxonomic aggregation implemented in the phyloseq package. To avoid ambiguity arising from duplicated genus names across different taxonomic lineages, genus labels were standardized and renamed to generate unique taxonomic identifiers. Samples were subsequently stratified according to obesity status prior to network construction.

Microbial association networks were inferred separately for obesity and without obesity groups using the NetCoMi R package. 25 Networks were constructed at the genus level using the SPIEC EASI method, which estimates sparse conditional associations and is suitable for compositional microbiome data. Zero counts were handled by pseudo-count replacement prior to network inference.

Network topology metrics, including degree, betweenness, closeness, and eigenvector centrality were computed using the netAnalyze function. Hub taxa were defined as nodes with eigenvector centrality values above the 95th percentile within each network.

Differences between obesity and without obesity networks were assessed using the netCompare function. Network similarity was quantified using the Jaccard index based on highly central nodes, allowing comparison of hub taxa between obesity and non-obesity microbial association networks.

In contrast, PCA performed on CLR-transformed nonbacterial species abundance data revealed significant differences in microbial community composition between obesity groups across all cohorts, as assessed by PERMANOVA based on Euclidean distances. Significant differences were observed in the IRONMET cohort (p = 0.001), the METASUCC cohort (p = 0.018), and the Aging Imageomics cohort (p = 0.003) (Figure 4d–f).

Gut microbial species were identified using the ANCOM-BC methodology controlling for age and sex. In the IRONMET cohort, individuals with obesity had lower levels of several taxa from the genus Methanobrevibacter and, with higher levels of bacteriophages from the class Caudoviricetes (Figure 1b , Sup. Table 1). Those results were replicated when splitting analysis by sex, where several species from the genus Methanobrevibacter such as Methanobrevibacter smithii were associated with the without obesity group in women and species from the class Caudoviricetes such as Siphoviridae_sp._ctdmY20 were positively associated with the obesity group (Figure 1c , Sup. Table 2). A similar pattern was observed in men, with species such as Methanobrevibacter millerae associated with the without obesity group and Siphoviridae_sp._ctdmY20 associated with the obesity group (Figure 1d , Sup. Table 3).

To further explore microbiota differences between individuals with and without obesity, we analyzed microbial network connectivity using the NetCoMi package. This approach evaluates co-occurrence relationships among taxa, allowing the identification of highly connected nodes (hub taxa) that may play central roles in the microbial ecosystem. Again, the analysis was restricted to genera belonging to the viral, eukaryotic, and archaeal components of the microbiota.

In the IRONMET cohort, the network structure differed between obesity groups. In individuals without obesity, the most influential hubs were predominantly taxa from the Viral kingdom (Figure 1e , left panel). In contrast, in individuals with obesity, the most central nodes were mainly composed of genera from the Archaea kingdom (Figure 1e , right panel). Hub taxa were defined as nodes with the highest eigenvector centrality (top 5% of the distribution), reflecting genera connected to other highly connected nodes within the network.

To quantify the similarity between the hub taxa identified in each network, we calculated the Jaccard index, which measures the overlap in hub nodes between the two groups. This analysis evaluates whether the same taxa occupy key positions within both microbial networks. The results revealed a significant difference in hub composition between the obesity and without obesity networks (Jaccard test, p = 0.0385), indicating that different taxa occupied central positions within the inferred microbial association networks.

In the METASUCC cohort, we conducted the same analysis as in the IRONMET cohort (Figure 2a) and found similar results. Species belonging to the genus Methanobrevibacter were found to be negatively associated with the obesity group and species belonging to the class Caudoviricetes were mostly found to be positively associated with the obesity group, in both the whole cohort (Figure 2b , Sup. Table 4) and in women (Figure 2c , Sup. Table 5). In men, fewer significant associations were found, probably because of the smaller sample size (Figure 2d , Sup. Table 6). When analyzing microbial network connectivity in this cohort, taxa from the Archaea and Virus kingdoms occupied central positions in both the obesity and without obesity networks (Figure 2e). Despite this apparent similarity in kingdom-level representation, the composition of hub taxa differed significantly between groups, as indicated by the Jaccard index (p = 0.006). This result suggests that, although hubs from the same kingdoms are present in both networks, the specific taxa acting as highly connected nodes—and their interactions with other community taxa—vary between obesity and without obesity groups.

The Imageomics cohort is composed of an elderly population. When analyzing the differential microbiota composition between the without obesity and obesity groups, species belonging to the Blastocystis genus were associated with the without obesity group. In contrast, members of the class Caudoviricetes and other viruses were mainly associated with the obesity group (Figure 3b , Sup. Table 7). A similar pattern was observed when the analysis was restricted to male individuals (Figure 3c , Sup. Table 8). Given the advanced age of the cohort members, we additionally stratified the analysis by age. In men aged 61–65 y, species from the genus Methanobrevibacter were enriched in the without obesity group (Sup. Figure 1, Sup. Table 9).

Network analysis further revealed distinct ecological structures between groups. In the obesity-associated network, viral taxa occupied the central hub nodes, whereas in the without obesity network, hub taxa were primarily composed of members of the Archaea kingdom. Consistent with these observations, the Jaccard index indicated significant dissimilarities in network hub composition within this cohort (p = 0.008).

To account for potential confounding due to lipid-lowering medication use and the differences in microbial abundance patterns observed between the with and without obesity groups, we performed the same exploratory analysis with additional adjustment for dyslipidemia treatment, alongside age and sex in the full cohort analyses, and for age in the sex-stratified analyses, using ANCOM-BC methodology (Sup. Figure 2a –i; Sup. Tables 10–18). These results showed a highly similar pattern to those obtained without adjusting for lipid-lowering medication use.

Although the number of species identified across all cohorts was limited (Figure 4h-i), those that were shared exhibited concordant directions of effect, as reflected in consistent log-fold change (Figure 4g). To further assess the reproducibility of these ANCOM-BC-derived associations across cohorts, we performed a meta-analysis of 178 taxa detected in at least two out of the three cohorts. This identified 119 (67%) with highly consistent associations across cohorts (I2 < 25%, mean I2 = 1%). These generalizable findings comprised 74 enriched and 45 depleted taxa. An additional 50 taxa (28%) showed intermediate heterogeneity (I2 = 52%), while only 9 taxa (5%) exhibited cohort-specific effects (I2 = 83%) (Figure 4j). Importantly, the taxa identified as consistently associated across cohorts generally exhibited high prevalence despite low relative abundances, supporting the robustness and reproducibility of these associations. Across cohorts, relative prevalence values for these taxa ranged from approximately 58% to 100%, whereas relative abundances were typically low, most commonly on the order of 10−5–10−4 (Supplementary Table 19).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of the nonbacterial gut microbiome, focusing on viral, archaeal, and eukaryotic communities, in the context of obesity across three independent cohorts. Our findings consistently highlight that obesity was associated not only with compositional shifts in these understudied microbial kingdoms, 5 , 9 but also with differences in inferred microbial association network structure.

A key and highly reproducible observation across cohorts was the inverse association between members of the genus Methanobrevibacter and obesity. Species such as Methanobrevibacter smithii and Methanobrevibacter millerae were consistently enriched in subjects without obesity. These archaea are well-known methanogens involved in hydrogen consumption and fermentation efficiency in the gut. Their depletion in obesity may reflect a disruption in metabolic cross-feeding interactions, potentially leading to altered energy harvest or substrate utilization. While previous studies have reported conflicting roles of methanogens in host energy balance, 5 our results support an association between methanogen abundance and microbial configurations previously linked to metabolic health.

In contrast, taxa from the class Caudoviricetes were consistently enriched in subjects with obesity across all cohorts. As bacteriophages, Caudoviricetes members can modulate bacterial populations through lytic or lysogenic cycles, thereby indirectly shaping microbial community composition and function. Their increased abundance in obesity may be linked to shifts in bacterial host composition or altered phage-host ecological relationships. Importantly, this pattern was robust across sex-stratified analyses, suggesting that viral signatures associated with obesity are relatively stable across biological contexts.

The inclusion of the Imageomics cohort, composed of older individuals, provided an opportunity to evaluate these associations in an aging population. In this cohort, Blastocystis species were enriched in subjects without obesity, consistent with previous reports linking this eukaryotic microorganism to higher microbial diversity and microbiome configurations linked with metabolic health. 26 In contrast, species from the class Caudoviricetes remained enriched in subjects with obesity, suggesting that some virome-associated patterns may persist across age groups. Interestingly, Methanobrevibacter enrichment was also observed in the younger subgroup of the Aging Imageomics cohort despite the overall advanced age of the population. Together, these findings suggest that certain nonbacterial microbiome associations with obesity may extend into older populations, although further studies will be needed to better disentangle the effects of aging, cohort composition, and obesity status.

In addition to taxonomic differences, analyses of overall community structure demonstrated significant differences in nonbacterial microbiome composition between obesity groups across all three cohorts. Although ordination analyses based on CLR-transformed abundance profiles revealed only partial separation between groups, PERMANOVA analyses consistently identified significant compositional differences. Together, these findings suggest that obesity-associated alterations within the nonbacterial microbiome are reproducible across cohorts but may involve relatively subtle, distributed shifts in community structure rather than complete restructuring of the microbial ecosystem. Beyond taxonomic differences, our network-based analyses revealed notable differences in microbial association network organization between subjects with obesity and subjects without obesity. Across cohorts, the identity and kingdom-level composition of hub taxa, defined as highly connected nodes within the networks, differed markedly between conditions. Notably, in the IRONMET cohort, subjects without obesity exhibited networks dominated by viral hubs, whereas subjects with obesity exhibited a higher proportion of archaeal hub taxa. Although this pattern was not entirely consistent across all cohorts, the Jaccard index consistently demonstrated significant differences in hub composition, indicating that distinct taxa occupied central positions within the networks.

These findings suggest that obesity may be linked with altered patterns of microbial co-occurrence or conditional dependence within the nonbacterial gut microbiome. However, while ANCOM-BC and network-based approaches provide statistical frameworks for compositional microbiome data, the inferred associations do not necessarily reflect direct biological or functional interactions.

Because network inference was based on compositional microbiome data and included low prevalence taxa, some inferred associations may be sensitive to sparsity and cohort-specific detection thresholds. Importantly, while some kingdom-level patterns were shared between groups (e.g., presence of viral and archaeal hubs), the specific taxa involved differed significantly. This highlights the limitation of broad taxonomic summaries and underscores the importance of species- or strain-level resolution when studying microbiome association networks.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the observational nature of the study precludes causal inference regarding the role of nonbacterial taxa in obesity. Second, differences in cohort characteristics, including age distribution and sample size, may have influenced the detection of associations, particularly in sex-stratified analyses. However, despite variations in cohort characteristics, several consistent patterns emerged across cohorts. Third, while ANCOM-BC and network-based approaches provide robust statistical frameworks, they rely on compositional microbiome data and inferred associations, which may not fully capture functional interactions.

Interpretation of cross-cohort comparisons should also consider important sources of cohort heterogeneity. The three cohorts differed in age distribution, recruitment context, sample size, and technical procedures, including DNA extraction and library preparation methods, all of which may influence taxonomic detection and relative abundance estimates. In particular, the Aging Imageomics cohort was substantially larger and older than the other cohorts, which may have increased statistical power and contributed to cohort-specific findings independent of biological aging effects.

Despite the application of abundance and prevalence filtering prior to differential abundance and network analyses, inferred associations involving lower-prevalence taxa should still be interpreted cautiously, particularly in cross-cohort comparisons.

Although consistent associations were observed across these three independent cohorts, additional studies in geographically and ethnically diverse populations will be important to assess the broader reproducibility and generalizability of these findings.

In conclusion, current findings provide convergent evidence that obesity is associated with substantial compositional and network-level alterations within the nonbacterial gut microbiome. Our results highlight the importance of adopting a multi-kingdom perspective to better understand host-microbiome interactions and suggest that the archaeome and virome may represent additional dimensions of microbiome variation relevant to obesity. Together, these findings support continued investigation of nonbacterial microbial communities as potentially important contributors to metabolic dysfunction and host metabolic heterogeneity.
