The role of economic evaluation in public health emergency management of disease outbreaks in Africa: a qualitative research
Authors: Elias Asfaw Zegeye, Josue Mbonigaba, Mahlet Kifle Habtemariam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrent disease outbreaks impose severe health and economic burdens on Africa, exposing critical gaps in public health emergency management (PHEM). Despite growing recognition of its value, economic evaluation (EE) remains largely absent from PHEM practice on the continent. This study aims to explore the role of EE in public health emergency management and assess its potential to strengthen health security in Africa, with a focus on the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC).MethodsA qualitative research design was employed, using in-person Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) with 12 purposively selected Africa CDC division heads and senior technical advisors between 15 March and 15 April 2023. Thematic analysis was conducted following the framework of Braun and Clarke (2006), with themes derived inductively from the interview data. A logic modelling framework was applied as a complementary tool to visualise EE’s causal chain across the four PHEM phases. Supplementary review of Africa CDC strategic documents, policy frameworks, and programme guidelines was undertaken to contextualise and triangulate the findings.ResultsFour themes emerged inductively from the analysis. First, key informants unanimously recognised EE as a critical but underutilised tool across the full PHEM cycle. Second, participants identified a concrete EE research agenda including return-on-investment analyses for preparedness, cost-effectiveness of diagnostic infrastructure, and health budget compliance monitoring. Third, significant barriers to EE integration were identified — principally a shortage of trained health economists, limited financial resources, and inadequate familiarity with health economics among decision-makers — alongside strategic opportunities presented by Africa CDC’s institutional positioning. Fourth, key informants framed EE as both a technical decision-support tool and a political communication instrument for influencing high-level policymakers.ConclusionsMainstreaming EE into PHEM — through investment in health economics capacity, standardised methodologies, and institutionalised evidence use — can optimise the allocation of limited resources across preparedness, response, and recovery. Africa CDC is strategically positioned to lead this agenda continentally.

Background

Public health emergency management (PHEM) involves anticipating, preventing, preparing for, detecting, responding to, and recovering from public health threats in order to minimise their health and economic impacts. It refers to the capacity of public health systems, healthcare providers, communities, and individuals to protect against, rapidly respond to, and recover from health emergencies — particularly those that could overwhelm routine capabilities due to their scale, timing, and unpredictability [1]. In Africa, the burden of recurrent disease outbreaks is disproportionately high. The continent accounts for a significant share of the world’s emerging and re-emerging infectious disease events, including Ebola virus disease, Marburg virus, cholera, meningitis, yellow fever, and, most recently, COVID-19 and Mpox [2, 3]. These outbreaks impose severe and often long-lasting economic consequences, compounding the existing vulnerabilities of health systems that are chronically underfunded and under-resourced.

The COVID-19 pandemic brought this reality into sharp focus. Beyond its devastating health toll, COVID-19 caused an estimated 2.1% contraction in sub-Saharan Africa’s GDP in 2020 — the region’s first recession in 25 years — reversing hard-won development gains and exposing the acute cost of inadequate preparedness [4]. The recent Mpox outbreak further demonstrated that the continent remains highly vulnerable to health emergencies that can escalate rapidly and generate substantial economic and social disruption [5]. These experiences have renewed calls for a more systematic, evidence-informed approach to PHEM — one in which economic evidence is treated not as a supplementary consideration, but as a core input into preparedness and response decision-making.

Economic evaluation (EE) is a systematic analytical framework for identifying, measuring, valuing, and comparing the costs and consequences of alternative courses of action to inform public and programmatic decision-making [6]. The primary forms of EE applied in health settings include cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), cost-benefit analysis (CBA), cost-utility analysis (CUA), and cost-minimisation analysis (CMA), each of which offers a different analytical lens for assessing value for money across competing health interventions [7]. While EE has become a cornerstone of health technology assessment and routine health policy decision-making in high-income countries, its application in emergency and outbreak settings — particularly in low- and middle-income country contexts — remains limited and inconsistent [8].

Economic evaluation in health emergencies: an emerging but underdeveloped field

The application of EE to health emergencies has gained increasing attention in the academic and policy literature, driven largely by the COVID-19 pandemic. Clarke was among the first to systematically highlight the growing importance of EE in health emergencies, demonstrating that economic evaluations help assess both the direct health impacts of emergencies and their broader livelihood consequences — an insight particularly relevant in settings where health and economic vulnerabilities are deeply intertwined [9]. Building on this, Lorgelly and Adler argued that health technology assessment and economic modelling must be systematically applied to pandemic preparedness decisions if governments are to avoid repeating the chronic underinvestment patterns exposed by COVID-19 [10].

Despite this growing recognition, a foundational challenge persists: the fast-paced, high-uncertainty nature of outbreak and pandemic management creates significant methodological and practical barriers to conducting rigorous EE in real time. Pérez Velasco et al. conducted a systematic review of economic evaluations of preparedness strategies for influenza pandemics and found that such evaluations remain rare, methodologically inconsistent, and difficult to compare across settings [11]. The WHO has long emphasised the importance of economic evidence in PHEM. Its guidance on the economics of immunisation demonstrated that systematic application of EE to vaccination programmes can transform both the efficiency of delivery and the political visibility of funding needs [12]. More recently, the WHO’s Health Emergency Preparedness, Response and Resilience (HEPR) framework explicitly calls for the integration of economic analysis into preparedness planning and response evaluation [13]. Similarly, the WHO’s International Health Regulations (IHR 2005) provide a normative framework within which EE can play a critical role in guiding member states’ investments in core health system capacities [14].

EE in humanitarian and low-resource emergency settings

A distinct but related body of literature addresses the application of EE in humanitarian and low-resource emergency settings. Makhani et al. conducted a systematic review of economic evaluations in health-related humanitarian programmes and found that economic evidence is rarely integrated into humanitarian and emergency response planning in low- and middle-income countries, despite clear potential for EE to improve resource allocation efficiency [15]. Marseille et al. similarly demonstrated that cost-effectiveness analyses in global health programmes have historically been concentrated in stable, well-resourced settings, leaving a significant evidence gap in fragile, conflict-affected, and emergency-prone contexts [16].

Organisations such as Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) and the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) have grappled directly with these challenges in their field operations. MSF’s operational research programme has sought to generate economic evidence on the cost and cost-effectiveness of emergency interventions — including therapeutic feeding programmes, cholera treatment, and Ebola response — but has consistently noted the difficulties of conducting rigorous EE under field conditions [17]. The ICRC has similarly called for greater integration of economic analysis into humanitarian health programming, noting that decisions about which interventions to prioritise and how to allocate scarce resources are inevitably economic decisions, whether or not they are framed as such [18].

The African context adds further complexity. Kapiriri et al. developed a conceptual framework for integrating priority-setting across the four phases of the PHEM cycle — pre-epidemic, alert, control, and post-epidemic evaluation — and demonstrated that economic criteria are currently absent from most African countries’ priority-setting processes, which tend to rely on epidemiological burden data alone [19]. This absence is particularly consequential given that Africa faces a disproportionate burden of concurrent disease outbreaks, where the challenge is not only how to respond to a single emergency but how to allocate limited resources across multiple simultaneous crises.

Africa CDC and the case for EE integration

Continental organisations like the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) have played an increasingly essential role in managing public health emergencies across the continent. Africa CDC is a continental autonomous technical health agency of the African Union (AU), established in January 2016 and launched in January 2017. Its mission is to support public health initiatives across all 55 AU member states and to strengthen their capacity to detect, prevent, control, and respond to disease threats [20]. Despite being a relatively young institution, Africa CDC has played a critical role in outbreak response — including leading Africa’s continental COVID-19 response — demonstrating both its growing technical authority and its potential as a platform for advancing evidence-based PHEM [21].

Africa CDC’s mandate encompasses a broad range of PHEM functions: assisting regional and national laboratory networks in adopting advanced molecular technologies; supporting the development of national laboratory strategies; establishing functional public health emergency operation centres (EOCs) within national public health institutes; developing multi-sectoral preparedness and response plans; managing national and regional emergency stockpiles; and promoting integrated surveillance systems for early warning, risk assessment, and response [22, 23]. Each of these functions involves significant resource allocation decisions that could be better informed by economic evidence.

The case for integrating EE into Africa CDC’s mandate is further supported by the broader literature on health financing in Africa. The Abuja Declaration of 2001 committed AU member states to allocating at least 15% of their national government expenditure (national budget) to health — a target that the majority of member states have consistently failed to meet [24]. The importance of health economics capacity on the African continent has been highlighted by the Resilient and Sustainable Systems for Health (RSSH) initiative and the work of the African Health Economics and Policy Association (AfHEA), both of which have called for systematic investment in health economics training, data systems, and institutional capacity [25, 26]. The Global Financing Facility (GFF) and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) have similarly documented the fiscal consequences of inadequate health system investment in Africa, noting that the economic costs of disease outbreaks far exceed the costs of preparedness investment [27, 28].

Priority-setting in PHEM: the missing economic dimension

Priority-setting in health systems involves making decisions about which interventions to fund and how to allocate scarce resources when not all needs can be met simultaneously. In PHEM contexts, priority-setting tends to be driven by urgency and epidemiological data rather than by systematic economic analysis, creating the risk of both inefficiency and inequity in resource allocation [29]. Daniels and Sabin’s accountability for reasonableness (A4R) framework provides a widely used normative foundation for fair priority-setting in health systems, emphasising transparency, relevance, revisability, and enforcement as core conditions for legitimate resource allocation decisions [30]. EE provides a practical tool for operationalising some of these principles in PHEM contexts.

The Global Health Security Agenda (GHSA), launched in 2014, has further underscored the importance of systematic investment in health security capacities, including surveillance, laboratory systems, emergency response, and workforce development [31]. While the GHSA has generated significant political momentum, its implementation has been criticised for being insufficiently grounded in economic evidence — a gap that EE is well-positioned to address [32].

In line with Africa CDC’s mandate in outbreak response and management, and building on the broader literature reviewed above, this paper seeks to explore the role of economic evaluation in public health emergency management in Africa, and to assess its potential impact on strengthening public health security across the continent.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative research design to explore the role of economic evaluation (EE) in public health emergency management (PHEM) in Africa. A qualitative approach was deemed appropriate given the exploratory nature of the research question and the need to elicit in-depth perspectives from individuals with specialised institutional knowledge of continental public health emergency management.

Sampling and participants

A purposive sampling strategy was used to identify key informants with relevant expertise and experience. A total of 12 key informants were recruited from Africa CDC, comprising division heads and senior technical advisors. Participants were selected based on their direct experience in managing disease outbreaks at the continental level, their institutional knowledge of Africa CDC’s mandate and operations, and their familiarity with public health emergency preparedness and response frameworks. Purposive sampling was considered appropriate as it allowed the research team to target information-rich cases most relevant to the study’s objectives.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-person Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) conducted between 15 March and 15 April 2023. Each interview lasted between 45 and 60 min and was conducted individually in a face-to-face format at participants’ offices at Africa CDC headquarters, Addis Ababa. The lead researcher, a health economist with extensive experience in qualitative research methods and a thorough familiarity with Africa CDC’s institutional context, conducted all interviews. Prior to data collection, the lead researcher undertook a structured preparation process including review of relevant Africa CDC policy documents and pilot testing of the interview guide with a colleague not included in the study sample. All 12 eligible key informants approached agreed to participate; there were no refusals or non-participation. A structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study, organised around four thematic areas: the relevance and application of EE in PHEM; potential research questions addressable through EE; barriers and opportunities for EE integration; and the role of EE in institutional decision-making. The KIIs were conducted as part of a broader institutional process — the development of Africa CDC’s Health Economics Roadmap and Strategic Plan — which provided a natural and contextually rich setting for data collection. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim. Supplementary review of Africa CDC’s strategic documents, policy frameworks, and programme guidelines was undertaken to contextualise and triangulate the interview findings.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was employed as the primary analytical approach, following the framework outlined by Braun and Clarke [33]. This involved a systematic process of familiarisation with the data, generating initial codes, searching for and reviewing themes, and producing the final thematic framework. Themes were derived inductively from the interview data, emerging from participants’ own accounts rather than being predetermined by the research team. Inter-coder agreement was established through independent parallel coding of two randomly selected transcripts, after which discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussion and consensus before proceeding to full-dataset analysis. Triangulation was achieved by systematically cross-referencing interview-derived themes with relevant passages from Africa CDC strategic documents, policy frameworks, and programme guidelines reviewed as part of the documentary analysis. A logic modelling framework was subsequently applied as a complementary analytical tool — not as a standalone method — to map and visualise the causal chain between EE inputs, activities, outcomes, and impacts within the PHEM cycle. This approach helped to illustrate how EE can be systematically integrated across the four phases of a disease outbreak: pre-epidemic, alert, control, and post-epidemic evaluation, as described by Kapiriri et al. Trustworthiness

Several strategies were employed to enhance the trustworthiness of the findings. Credibility was strengthened through prolonged engagement with the data and triangulation of interview findings with documentary sources. Preliminary findings were shared with a subset of participants for member checking, to verify that interpretations accurately reflected their views. Transferability was supported through thick description of the research context and participant characteristics. The lead researcher maintained a reflexivity journal throughout data collection and analysis to acknowledge and manage potential biases arising from their institutional affiliation with Africa CDC and familiarity with the subject matter.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles governing research involving human participants, including the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The interviews were conducted as an integral component of Africa CDC’s institutional Health Economics Roadmap and Strategic Plan development process — an internally commissioned organisational planning initiative. Prior to each interview, all key informants were fully informed of the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, their right to withdraw at any time without consequence, and the intended use of the data — including its potential use in academic publications. All participants provided verbal informed consent before the interview commenced. Participants were assured of confidentiality and are identified solely through anonymised participant codes (e.g., Participant 01, Participant 02) in this publication.

Given that this study involved interviews conducted with professional staff in their institutional capacity, as part of an ongoing organisational planning process, collected no sensitive personal data, and posed no foreseeable risk of harm, formal ethics committee review was not required. This position is consistent with established guidance on low-risk qualitative research conducted within professional institutional settings.

Results

Twelve key informant interviews were conducted with Africa CDC division heads and senior technical advisors between March and April 2023. Following systematic thematic analysis, four main themes emerged inductively from the data: (1) the role of EE in the public health emergency management cycle; (2) research questions addressable through EE in the context of emergency preparedness and response; (3) barriers and opportunities for integrating EE into PHEM; and (4) the role of EE in institutional decision-making. While these themes are analytically distinct, they are interconnected: understanding what EE can contribute (Theme 1) informs what questions it should address (Theme 2), which in turn requires overcoming specific institutional barriers (Theme 3) before EE evidence can meaningfully influence decisions (Theme 4). Together, the four themes paint a coherent picture of EE as a valued but currently underutilised tool within Africa CDC’s PHEM architecture.

Theme 1: the role of EE in the public health emergency management cycle

Key informants unanimously recognised EE as a critical and largely untapped resource within the PHEM cycle. Their responses clustered around three distinct functions that EE can serve: assessing the value-for-money of emergency investments, generating evidence to guide resource allocation, and providing technical guidance to AU member states on cost-effective interventions.

A consistent finding across interviews was that EE is relevant not only during active outbreak response, but across the full temporal arc of emergency management — from pre-epidemic preparedness planning through to post-disaster impact assessment. This temporal breadth was explicitly articulated by a representative view from one of the participants:“EE will play two important roles in public health emergency management. That is before the outbreak occurrence for the initial emergency outbreak decisions and secondly during the post-disaster impact assessment.” [Participant 02]

“EE will play two important roles in public health emergency management. That is before the outbreak occurrence for the initial emergency outbreak decisions and secondly during the post-disaster impact assessment.” [Participant 02]

This framing is significant because it positions EE not merely as a retrospective accounting tool, but as a prospective planning instrument that can inform investment decisions before a crisis escalates. This is particularly relevant in resource-constrained African settings, where the cost of inaction during the preparedness phase can far exceed the cost of early investment. The potential of EE to address concrete investment decisions was further illustrated by another participant:“It helps to address research questions like, is it worthwhile investing in mobile laboratory technology (500,000 USD) for emergency management?” [Participant 01]

“It helps to address research questions like, is it worthwhile investing in mobile laboratory technology (500,000 USD) for emergency management?” [Participant 01]

Beyond investment appraisal, key informants emphasised EE’s role in improving operational efficiency within health systems that routinely face competing demands on limited resources:“It has a great and important role, especially with the costing and efficiency questions, especially working within the limited resource settings.” [Participant 03]

“It has a great and important role, especially with the costing and efficiency questions, especially working within the limited resource settings.” [Participant 03]

This finding underscores a broader pattern in the data: key informants perceived EE not as an abstract academic exercise, but as a practical decision-support tool with direct operational relevance. Several participants also highlighted EE’s guidance function at the member state level:“Apart from supporting the planning function, it has an important role in guiding and advising member states on cost-effectiveness choices and related pieces of evidence.” [Participant 05]

“Apart from supporting the planning function, it has an important role in guiding and advising member states on cost-effectiveness choices and related pieces of evidence.” [Participant 05]

Taken together, these findings suggest that key informants viewed EE as serving a dual function: an internal function of improving Africa CDC’s own resource allocation decisions, and an external function of strengthening the evidence base available to AU member states. This dual mandate aligns with Africa CDC’s broader institutional role as both a technical implementing agency and a continental knowledge hub.

Theme 2: research questions addressable through EE in emergency preparedness and response

Beyond articulating EE’s general relevance, key informants identified a rich array of specific policy and programmatic research questions that EE could address. These fell into four broad categories: comparative effectiveness of alternative interventions; national health financing and compliance with continental commitments; preparedness investment and return; and laboratory and health system cost optimisation.

On comparative effectiveness, participants expressed a clear need for evidence comparing different strategic options, particularly in relation to strengthening national public health institutes:“To analyse different options (Option A versus Option B) — especially to have a reputable national public health institute at member states.” [Participants 04, 05]

“To analyse different options (Option A versus Option B) — especially to have a reputable national public health institute at member states.” [Participants 04, 05]

Regarding health financing, informants highlighted the need for EE to track and assess member states’ budgetary commitment to the Abuja Declaration target of allocating 15% of national government expenditure (national budget) to health:“How much of the GDP is being committed for health as compared to the Abuja Declaration (15% of national budget) by member states?” [Participant 06]

“How much of the GDP is being committed for health as compared to the Abuja Declaration (15% of national budget) by member states?” [Participant 06]

On preparedness investment, the data revealed a strong appetite among participants for return-on-investment analysis — quantifying the economic gains of investing in preparedness relative to the costs of reactive outbreak response:“If you invest in preparedness, this will save a certain amount of dollars. If you invest in a given intervention, what is the estimated benefit, gain, and cost?” [Participants 09, 12]

“If you invest in preparedness, this will save a certain amount of dollars. If you invest in a given intervention, what is the estimated benefit, gain, and cost?” [Participants 09, 12]

This finding reflects a growing recognition among Africa CDC leadership that the economic case for preparedness investment needs to be made explicitly and quantitatively — not simply asserted — if it is to influence high-level budget decisions. Finally, participants identified several operational cost-efficiency questions specific to laboratory systems:

“Is having reference diagnostic centres across the continent cost-effective?” [Participant 03]

“Is having reference diagnostic centres across the continent cost-effective?” [Participant 03]

“What is the cost-effectiveness of having a pooled recruitment process?” [Participant 03]

“What is the cost-effectiveness of having a pooled recruitment process?” [Participant 03]

These operational questions point to an important and underexplored dimension of EE in PHEM: its potential to improve the efficiency of the institutional infrastructure that underpins outbreak response, rather than focusing exclusively on the response itself. The breadth of research questions identified by participants suggests both a strong demand for EE evidence and a significant unmet need.

Theme 3: barriers and opportunities for integrating EE into PHEM

Key informants acknowledged that while the case for EE integration is compelling, significant structural and institutional barriers currently limit its application in African PHEM settings. At the same time, they identified a set of strategic opportunities that, if leveraged, could accelerate EE’s institutionalisation within Africa CDC and across member states.

Barriers

The most consistently cited barrier was the critical shortage of health economists across the continent. Participants expressed concern not only about the absolute scarcity of trained personnel, but about the sustainability of any EE capacity that might be developed within a young institution like Africa CDC:

“How many health economists do we have on the continent? How do you ensure the continuing applicability of EE in young continental organisations like Africa CDC?” [Participants 04, 05]

“How many health economists do we have on the continent? How do you ensure the continuing applicability of EE in young continental organisations like Africa CDC?” [Participants 04, 05]

“Lack of human resources and limited financial resources could be a critical bottleneck to executing EE studies.” [Participant 01]

“Lack of human resources and limited financial resources could be a critical bottleneck to executing EE studies.” [Participant 01]

This human resource constraint is compounded by a limited understanding of health economics and its relevance to public health decision-making among many African policymakers and programme managers. Inadequate and unpredictable financial resources were identified as a further constraint, particularly given that EE studies require dedicated investment in data systems, analytical capacity, and skilled personnel — resources that are often deprioritised in the face of immediate outbreak response pressures.

Opportunities

Despite these barriers, participants were notably optimistic about the opportunities available to Africa CDC. Its cross-sectoral reach, spanning humanitarian, disaster management, and bilateral partnerships, was specifically highlighted as a strategic asset:

“There are a wide range of opportunities in terms of reaching stakeholders — humanitarian, disaster management — and there exist good bilateral organisations.” [Participant 01]

“There are a wide range of opportunities in terms of reaching stakeholders — humanitarian, disaster management — and there exist good bilateral organisations.” [Participant 01]

“Vertical and horizontal advantages with other divisions and agencies… can make use of the advantage of working with the bilateral departments within the AU.” [Participant 01]

“Vertical and horizontal advantages with other divisions and agencies… can make use of the advantage of working with the bilateral departments within the AU.” [Participant 01]

Participants emphasised Africa CDC’s identity as a technical, evidence-based institution as both a driver and a justification for EE integration:

“As a technical public health agency, this institution should be evidence-based and data-driven.” [Participant 05]

“As a technical public health agency, this institution should be evidence-based and data-driven.” [Participant 05]

“As a technical organisation within the political space, having EE is very important, especially to inform decisions.” [Participant 06]

“As a technical organisation within the political space, having EE is very important, especially to inform decisions.” [Participant 06]

Perhaps most strikingly, several participants framed EE integration not as an optional enhancement but as an institutional imperative aligned with Africa’s long-term development vision:“In line with the AU 2063 aspiration, whether we like it or not, there is a need to frame our thinking based on economic cases.” [Participant 08].

“In line with the AU 2063 aspiration, whether we like it or not, there is a need to frame our thinking based on economic cases.” [Participant 08].

This finding suggests that the appetite for EE among Africa CDC leadership is not merely technical but normative — rooted in a broader vision of Africa as a continent that governs its health systems through rigorous, evidence-informed decision-making.

Theme 4: the role of EE in institutional decision-making

The fourth theme addresses the specific ways in which EE can inform and strengthen decision-making within Africa CDC’s PHEM mandate. A cross-cutting finding was that key informants drew a clear distinction between EE as a technical analytical tool and EE as a political communication instrument. Several participants emphasised that economic modelling and investment cases are essential for persuading high-level policymakers and political actors to act on public health evidence:

“Having economic modelling will provide a comprehensive view to inform decisions.” [Participant 07]

“Having economic modelling will provide a comprehensive view to inform decisions.” [Participant 07]

“The highest priority is to generate economic cases linked to the different policy decision-making aligned to outbreak management.” [Participant 08]

“The highest priority is to generate economic cases linked to the different policy decision-making aligned to outbreak management.” [Participant 08]

“To push policies into practice and to a higher level, making economic cases through economic evaluation at every decision-making stage is critical.” [Participant 08]

“To push policies into practice and to a higher level, making economic cases through economic evaluation at every decision-making stage is critical.” [Participant 08]

This emphasis on EE as a tool for policy translation — bridging the gap between technical evidence and political action — is a notable and underappreciated dimension of EE’s potential in the African context. Participants also highlighted EE’s role in harmonising and standardising costs across programmes and member states. The HIV/AIDS programme was cited as a model from which PHEM can learn:

One key informant emphasised the need to learn from programmes such as HIV/AIDS, which has successfully integrated economics into programme management and implementation. The variation in laboratory unit costs — from 50 USD per test to 9 USD per test — illustrates precisely the kind of cost inefficiency that EE could help to identify, harmonise, and standardise across Africa CDC’s operational portfolio.

The breadth of decision-making domains in which participants saw EE as relevant was striking, encompassing: comparative assessment of laboratory technologies; investment decisions related to Electronic Medical Records; cost-effectiveness of reference diagnostic laboratory centres; standardised costing for pooled procurement; return on investment analysis for preparedness; and monitoring and evaluation across programme areas.

Logic chain analysis

Drawing on the thematic findings above, and using a logic modelling framework as a visualisation tool, key informants identified four core activities necessary for meaningfully integrating EE into PHEM: (1) enhancing the application of EE across all phases of disease outbreak management; (2) strengthening technical skills and human capital for conducting EE studies at the continental and national levels; (3) designing emergency response plans that explicitly incorporate priority-setting under resource constraints; and (4) integrating economic-epidemiological modelling to guide strategic decision-making.

These activities are interconnected and mutually reinforcing. The expected immediate outcomes include more effective use of EE evidence in PHEM planning, improved resource efficiency, enhanced programme effectiveness, and strengthened technical capacity both at Africa CDC and within AU member states. Over the longer term, sustained EE integration is expected to improve the overall efficiency and impact of PHEM efforts continentally, ultimately contributing to evidence-based policies and a safer, healthier Africa (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study explored the role of economic evaluation in public health emergency management in Africa, drawing on key informant interviews with senior Africa CDC officials. The findings reveal a clear and consistent recognition among institutional leaders that EE is an essential but largely underutilised tool in the PHEM cycle. This discussion situates these findings within the broader literature on health economics in emergency settings, reflects on what the findings add to existing knowledge, and considers their implications for policy and practice at both the continental and national levels.

EE in PHEM: confirming an emerging but underdeveloped field

The finding that key informants unanimously recognised EE’s value across the full PHEM cycle — from pre-epidemic preparedness through to post-epidemic evaluation — is consistent with, and reinforces, a growing body of literature calling for greater integration of economic evidence into emergency health management. Clarke highlighted the growing importance of EE in health emergencies, demonstrating that economic evaluations help assess both the direct health impacts of emergencies and their broader livelihood consequences [9]. Our findings extend this argument beyond COVID-19 to the broader landscape of recurrent disease outbreaks in Africa.

The finding that most PHEM decisions at Africa CDC are currently driven by epidemiological data alone is consistent with Makhani et al., whose systematic review found that economic evidence is rarely integrated into humanitarian and emergency response planning in low- and middle-income countries [15]. Our study adds important nuance: the absence of EE is not due to a lack of recognition of its value. The gap is structural — a shortage of trained health economists, inadequate financial resources, and the absence of institutionalised mechanisms for generating and using EE evidence within the PHEM cycle.

The preparedness investment gap: making the economic case

One of the most analytically significant findings is participants’ emphasis on EE as a tool for making the economic case for preparedness investment. Lorgelly and Adler noted that the COVID-19 pandemic exposed a chronic underinvestment in health system preparedness globally, and argued that health technology assessment and economic modelling should be systematically applied to preparedness decisions to prevent a repeat of this pattern [10]. Our participants articulated precisely this argument, expressing a clear need for return-on-investment analyses that can demonstrate the economic gains of investing in preparedness.

This has particular resonance in the African context, where health budgets are constrained and the political case for preparedness spending must be made explicitly and repeatedly. The Abuja Declaration committed AU member states to allocating 15% of national government expenditure (national budget) to health — a target that few countries have consistently met. EE has an important role to play here: by quantifying the economic returns of health investment, it can strengthen the advocacy case for budget compliance and help shift political incentives towards sustained investment in health system resilience.

EE as a political communication tool: a neglected dimension

A particularly novel finding from this study is the framing of EE not merely as a technical analytical tool, but as a political communication instrument. Participants consistently described economic cases and investment analyses as essential for convincing high-level policymakers and political actors — not just technical staff — to act on public health evidence. This finding goes beyond the existing literature, which tends to focus on EE’s role in improving the technical quality of decisions, and highlights a distinct function: translating complex public health evidence into economic terms that resonate within political decision-making environments.

This distinction matters practically. It suggests that building EE capacity within Africa CDC and member states must encompass not only technical analytical skills but also the ability to communicate economic evidence compellingly to non-technical audiences. This is consistent with Kapiriri et al.‘s framework, which emphasises that decision-making in outbreak contexts is inherently political as well as technical [19].

Institutionalising EE within Africa CDC: a phased approach

The logic chain analysis developed from participants’ responses points towards a coherent, phased approach to institutionalising EE within Africa CDC’s PHEM mandate. The four core activities identified — enhancing EE application, strengthening human capital, designing resource-constrained response plans, and integrating economic-epidemiological modelling — are mutually reinforcing and need to be pursued in parallel rather than sequentially.

The HIV/AIDS programme, cited by participants as a model, offers a compelling precedent: over two decades, the systematic application of economic analysis to HIV programme decisions transformed both the efficiency of programme delivery and the political visibility of HIV funding needs. The variation in laboratory unit costs highlighted by participants (from USD 50 to USD 9 per test) illustrates precisely the kind of inefficiency that institutionalised EE could identify and address. Africa CDC’s mandate to support all 55 AU member states also gives it a unique platform to set continental standards for how economic evidence is generated and used in PHEM — a catalytic role analogous to that played by the WHO in normalising EE within immunisation programmes [12].

What this study adds and what remains to be done

This study makes several contributions to the emerging literature on EE in PHEM. It provides the first qualitative exploration of EE’s role within a continental African public health institution, generates a specific and institutionally grounded research agenda for EE in PHEM, and develops a logic model illustrating the causal pathway through which EE can strengthen PHEM outcomes. It also identifies the political communication function of EE as a distinct and underappreciated dimension of its institutional value.

However, the study also highlights the scale of the work that remains. EE is currently absent from most PHEM decisions in Africa, the human resource base for conducting EE studies is critically thin, and the institutional mechanisms for generating and using EE evidence are not yet in place. Future research — including studies that extend beyond Africa CDC to encompass a broader range of institutional actors, and studies that evaluate the actual use and impact of EE evidence in specific outbreak contexts — will be essential for building the evidence base needed to drive systemic change.

Conclusion

This study set out to explore the role of economic evaluation in public health emergency management in Africa, with a focus on Africa CDC as the continent’s primary technical public health institution. Drawing on qualitative evidence from 12 senior key informants, the study addressed four interconnected questions: what role can EE play in the PHEM cycle; what specific research questions can EE address; what barriers and opportunities exist for EE integration; and how can EE strengthen institutional decision-making.

This study confirms that economic evaluation is relevant and applicable across all four phases of disease outbreak management, serves a concrete institutional research agenda — spanning return-on-investment analyses, cost-effectiveness of diagnostic infrastructure, and health budget compliance monitoring — and functions both as a technical decision-support tool and a political communication instrument. The principal barriers to EE integration are a continent-wide shortage of trained health economists, limited financial resources, and the absence of institutionalised mechanisms for evidence generation and use; Africa CDC’s unique positioning within the AU constitutes a strategic platform for overcoming these constraints.

Mainstreaming EE into Africa CDC’s PHEM mandate will require coordinated action across multiple fronts: investing in health economics human capital; developing standardised EE methodologies suited to outbreak contexts; integrating EE requirements into PHEM planning processes and guidelines; and building political will to prioritise economic evidence even under the time pressures of active outbreak response. The COVID-19 pandemic and the recent Mpox outbreak have demonstrated, at enormous cost, the price of inadequate preparedness and the consequences of resource allocation decisions made without robust economic evidence. By investing now in the capacity to conduct and use economic evaluations within the PHEM cycle, Africa CDC and its member states can build a more resilient, efficient, and evidence-informed public health emergency management system — one that is equipped not only to respond to the next outbreak, but to get ahead of it.

Limitations

This study makes an important contribution to the emerging literature on EE in PHEM in Africa, but several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting its findings.

Single-organisation sampling

The most significant limitation of this study is that all 12 key informants were drawn from a single institution — Africa CDC. While Africa CDC occupies a unique and strategically important position in continental PHEM, its staff represent one institutional perspective that may not fully capture the breadth of views across the wider African public health emergency management landscape. The perspectives of WHO country and regional offices, national public health institutes, ministries of health, humanitarian organisations such as MSF and the ICRC, bilateral development partners, and academic institutions were not represented. Future research should deliberately seek to include this broader range of institutional actors.

Sample size

A total of 12 key informants were interviewed, which, while consistent with purposive sampling approaches in qualitative research, nonetheless limits the depth and breadth of perspectives captured. A larger and more diverse sample drawn from multiple organisations and member states would have strengthened the transferability of the findings.

Potential response and reflexivity bias

The interviews were conducted as part of an internal institutional process, which may have introduced social desirability bias — participants may have been more inclined to express views aligned with the institutional direction of travel. The lead researcher’s own institutional affiliation with Africa CDC adds a further layer of potential reflexivity bias, which was managed through a reflexivity journal but cannot be entirely eliminated.

Temporal and contextual limitations

The interviews were conducted between March and April 2023 — prior to the re-declaration of Mpox as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) in August 2024. It should be noted that WHO first declared Mpox a PHEIC in July 2022, a declaration that was subsequently terminated in May 2023; a new PHEIC was declared in August 2024 following the emergence of a new clade in Central and Eastern Africa. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the more recent experiences of PHEM practitioners in the context of Mpox preparedness and response. Future research should revisit these questions in light of recent outbreak events.

Methodological limitations of qualitative design

As a qualitative study, the findings are not intended to be statistically generalisable to all PHEM contexts across Africa. Rather, they offer analytical generalisability in a field where qualitative evidence is currently scarce. Complementary quantitative and mixed-methods studies are needed to assess the actual effectiveness of EE integration in PHEM practice.

Limited formal ethics review

As noted in the methods section, formal ethics committee review was not sought for this study. While this position is consistent with established guidance for low-risk qualitative research in professional institutional settings, the absence of formal ethics approval is a limitation that future studies should address by seeking prospective institutional ethics clearance.
