Improved survival after primary tumor resection in distant metastasis medullary thyroid carcinoma: a population-based cohort study with propensity score matching
Authors: Zixia Tao, Xianzhao Deng, Zheng Ding, Bomin Guo, Youben Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Few studies have investigated the impact of primary tumor resection (PTR) on patients with distant metastasis medullary thyroid carcinoma (DMMTC). This population-based study aims to assess the application of PTR in DMMTC patients, ascertain its benefits, and identify optimal surgical indications. DMMTC Patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2020 were included through the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program. Logistic regression analysis identified driving factors of surgical decision-making. Propensity score matching (PSM), Kaplan–Meier method, and Cox regression were utilized to compare overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) between surgical and non-surgical groups. Subgroup analyses were performed to determine optimal surgical indications. Of 238 DMMTC patients included, 122 (51.3%) patients underwent PTR. Extrathyroidal extension and N1 stage emerged as independent factors promoting the surgical decision. PSM-adjusted survival analyses revealed significant advantages in both OS and DSS for the surgical group. Moreover, subgroup analyses indicated that except for patients aged ≥ 65 years, tumors ≤ 20 mm, or with multiple metastasized sites (> 1), the others significantly benefit from PTR. PTR significantly improves prognosis in selected DMMTC patients. The decision to undergo PTR in other patients should be based on a comprehensive assessment of the disease, surgeon’s experience, and family discussions for potential survival benefits.

Introduction

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) originates from parafollicular cells of the thyroid gland. Unlike differentiated thyroid carcinoma, endocrine suppressive therapy and iodine-131 treatment are ineffective against it, and surgery is the only potentially curative treatment1. MTC tends to metastasize distantly, with 10% of patients presenting with distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, primarily in bones, liver, and lungs2–4. The presence of distant metastasis, indicative of incurable late-stage disease (stage IVC), reduces the 5-year survival rate from 89 to 51%, often leading surgeons and patients to forgo surgery5,6.

Expert groups, including the American Thyroid Association (ATA), National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), recommended that only patients with distant metastasis medullary thyroid carcinoma (DMMTC) exhibiting symptoms of local invasion should undergo palliative primary tumor surgery aiming for function preservation1,7,8. Routine primary tumor resection is not universally recommended for all DMMTC patients.

A growing body of research highlights the favorable prognostic impact of primary tumor resection in various cancers, including metastatic colorectal, breast, cervical carcinoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm, major salivary gland carcinoma and head neck squamous cell carcinoma9–15. However, to our knowledge, the impact of primary tumor resection on medullary thyroid carcinoma, a neuroendocrine neoplasm, remains understudied. Additionally, further subgroup analyses are necessary to understand the effect of primary tumor resection on patients with comparatively poor survival, such as older individuals, those with locally advanced disease, and patients with multiple organ metastases1,4,5,16.

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, a public database, encompasses detailed demographic, clinicopathological characteristics, treatment information, and survival status of cancer patients from major cancer registries in the United States. Numerous large-sample cohort studies have utilized the SEER database. Propensity score matching represents a commonly used statistical method in observational studies. By evenly distributing confounding factors and biases between the treatment and observation cohorts, it enhances comparability and can yield results akin to those from randomized controlled trials17.

This population-based study, obtaining data on DMMTC patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2020 through the SEER database, aimed to assess the application of primary tumor resection in DMMTC patients, calculate its benefits, and further explore the optimal indications for resection via subgroup analysis.

Methods

Data source

Clinicopathological data of DMMTC patients in this study were sourced from the SEER-17 Registry database of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program (2000–2021), published in April 2024, encompassing 17 high-quality, population-based registries, covering 28% of the US population. The SEER program updates follow-up data annually, at minimum monthly intervals. In order to achieve a sufficient follow-up period, our study identified DMMTC patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2020, and the follow-up end point was December 2021. Given that all data were derived from a public database, this study was exempted by the institution's ethics review board. This study adhered to the STROCSS reporting guidelines18.

The inclusion criteria for this study are as follows: (1) ‘Primary site label’ variable was set to C73.9: thyroid. (2) Pathological types were identified using the ‘ICD3-O-3’ variable: medullary thyroid carcinoma (8345, 8510–8513). (3) Distant metastasis was ascertained using variables: “CS mets at dx(2004–2015)”, “Derived SEER Combined M(2016–2017)”, “EOD METS(2018 +)”. (4) The “Diagnostic Confirmation” variable was used to include only microscopically confirmed cases. The exclusion criteria for this study are as follows: (1) Cases of Autopsy and Death certificates only were excluded by the “Type of reporting source” variable. (2) Patients with no information about whether the primary tumor surgery were performed. (3) Patients who were diagnosed before January 2010 or after December 2020 (Due to the year limit of the specific metastatic sites variables and consideration of mature follow-up period).

Cohort characteristics comprised demographic features (sex, race, age at diagnosis, marital status, and household median income), clinicopathological characteristics (tumor size, invasion, N stage, and metastasis site), therapeutic approaches (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy) and survival status. Demographic variables such as ‘Sex’, ‘Age recode with single ages and 85 + ’, ‘Race recode (W, B, AI, API)’, ‘Marital status at diagnosis’, ‘Median household income’ were employed to determine sex, age, race, marital status and household median income. Age was divided into categorical variables: 0–44, 45–64, ≥ 65 years, corresponding to young, middle-aged, and old patients, respectively. Clinicopathological characteristics were primarily identified and categorized using ‘CS Tumor/lymph node (2004–2015)’, ‘Derived SEER Combined T/N (2016–2017)’, and ‘EOD Primary tumor/lymph node (2018 +)’. Surgery data were extracted from the ‘Rx Summ-Surg Prim site’ variable and reason for non-surgical treatment were extracted from ‘Reason no cancer-directed surgery’. Previous tumor history was identified from ‘First malignant primary indicator’. The specific metastatic sites were identified from ‘SEER Combined Mets at DX-bone/brain/liver/lung’. Distant lymph nodes metastasis was extracted from ‘CS mets at dx (2004–2015)’ and ‘Mets at DX-Distant LN (2016 +)’. Referring to clinical practice, metastasized sites registered as unknown in SEER database were defined as no or no evidence. Outcome data were gathered from “Survival months”, “Vital status recode” and “SEER case-specific death classification” variables. Ultimately, 238 patients were included in the study cohort. The flowchart of this study is depicted in Fig.S1.

Statistical analyses

Considering some variables with missing data have potential impact on prognosis and surgical decision-making, and directly deleting these missing values or defining them simply as unknown (about 58 cases) might greatly affect the efficacy of the statistical test, the ‘Mice’ package was employed for multiple imputation of missing data, after consulting statistical expert. Besides, the ‘Matchit’ package was applied for propensity score matching (PSM) in the study cohort to reduce the baseline difference. PSM utilized nearest neighbor caliper matching, set at 1:1 matching, without replacement, with a caliper value of 0.2. Demographic variables (age, sex, race, marital status, income) were included in PSM formula in order to reduce potential inter-group bias in underlying diseases. Besides, clinicopathological variables (tumor size, invasion, N stage, number of metastasized sites), which had potential impact on prognosis, were also included in PSM formula. Fisher's precision probability test was used to calculate the baseline difference between the two groups, before and after PSM. Logistic regression analysis predicted the likelihood of surgical decision-making. The Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test compared the overall survival (OS) and the disease-specific survival (DSS). Cox regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for death. Variables with p < 0.15 in univariate analysis or with potential prognostic impact on outcomes were incorporated into multivariate analysis, and stepwise regression using the backward method identified the optimal model. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1) and SPSS (version 25, IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). All tests were two-tailed with α defined as 0.05.

Application of primary tumor resection in DMMTC patients

Between 2010 and 2020, the surgical rate for DMMTC remained steady, fluctuating around 50%, despite the advancement in diagnostic years. The predominant reason cited for not undergoing surgery in 93.9% of non-surgical patients was “not recommended by surgeon”. Among DMMTC patients who underwent primary tumor resection, 92.6% received total thyroidectomy, and 90.2% underwent lymph node dissection. (Fig.S2).

After PSM, 154 patients from surgical and non-surgical groups were included in the survival analysis. For the non-surgical group, the DSS rates were significantly lower at 54.8%, 40.0%, and 29.9%, respectively. Discussion

In a retrospective cohort study of 238 Distant Metastasis Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma (DMMTC) cases, we explored the implementation of primary tumor resection and the factors driving surgical decision. We assessed the effects of primary tumor resection versus non-surgical treatment on DMMTC patients’ prognosis and delved into the optimal indications for primary tumor resection. This study is the first to use PSM to highlight and validate the positive prognostic influence of primary tumor resection in selected DMMTC patients, augmenting the detailed indications and optimal age cutoff of surgical benefit.

The management of primary tumors in DMMTC patients remains controversial, as, unlike differentiated thyroid cancer with distant metastasis, DMMTC does not benefit from conventional post-surgical iodine-131 treatment and endocrine suppression therapy19–22. Expert groups from ATA, NCCN, and ESMO recommend that only patients with DMMTC exhibiting symptoms of local invasion should undergo palliative primary tumor surgery, aiming for function preservation. Though the protective impact of primary tumor resection on prognosis has been highlighted in many solid tumors, like metastatic colorectal, breast, cervical, and major salivary gland carcinoma, few studies focus on the prognostic impact of primary tumor resection on DMMTC patients. Recently, Liu et al. utilized the SEER database to highlight the protective impact of PTR on the prognosis of DMMTC patients and thoughtfully evaluated the optimal candidates23. While the study's methodology is commendable, it is essential to consider the potential for bias due to unadjusted potential underlying disease status, insufficient follow-up period in newly diagnosed patients in recent 3 years and a small cohort size resulting from stringent exclusion criteria. Additionally, the omission of patients with incomplete data in specific subgroup analyses could skew the determination of the most suitable candidates.

Our study is the first to use PSM to identify primary tumor resection as an independent protective factor against death in DMMTC patients, significantly enhancing survival outcomes. The 1-year DSS rate for surgical patients after PSM is 81.3%, the 3-year DSS rate is 60.0%, and the 5-year DSS rate is 51.3%. In contrast, for non-surgical patients, these rates are 59.7%, 41.7%, and 33.4%, respectively. Moreover, the median disease-specific survival time notably improved from 15 months in the non-surgical group to 60 months in the surgical group. It is worth mentioning that after PSM corrected the potential bias of underlying disease status and grouping imbalance, the median disease-specific survival decreased from 85 to 60 months in the surgery group, while the median disease-specific survival did not change significantly in the non-surgery group.

Our study revealed that besides non-surgical treatment, significant independent risk factors for mortality in DMMTC patients are old age, male and multiple metastasized sites. This aligns with the findings of previous studies on overall MTC patients5,16,24. Furthermore, unlike overall MTC patient mortality factors, extrathyroidal extension, and lymph node metastasis do not significantly correlate with DMMTC patient mortality, implying that the death causes in DMMTC patients may be less associated with symptom caused by local progression (like dyspnea, dysphagia), and more possible to relate with distant site progression or cachexia4,5.

Our study observed that the implementation rate of primary tumor resection remained stable between 2010 and 2020, fluctuating around 50%, lower than the overall level of distant metastasis thyroid cancer (74.2%)19. The predominant reason for patients undergoing non-surgical treatment was lack of surgical recommendation by doctors (93.9%). Logistic regression analysis identified extrathyroidal extension and lymph node metastases as the driving factors for undergoing surgery, while multiple metastasized sites is the sole independent promoting factor for undergoing non-surgical treatment. This might imply that clinicians are inclined to recommend primary tumor resection for patients with severe local invasion symptoms, while being more cautious with patients showing severe distant metastasis symptoms. This practice aligns with current guideline recommendations1,25. However, our subgroup analysis challenges this conventional recognition. Our results showed that apart from patients aged ≥ 65 years, with multiple metastasized sites or with tumors ≤ 20 mm, primary tumor resection enhanced the DSS in nearly all other subgroups, which means that the recommended indication for primary tumor resection should be relaxed rather than limited to palliative surgery for local progression.

We conducted an in-depth exploration of the optimal age cutoff for benefit from surgical treatment. The protective effect of surgery on DSS was significant in patients aged 55–64 years (p = 0.01), began to be lost in patients aged 65–74 years (p = 0.28), and disappeared completely in patients aged ≥ 75 years (p = 0.86). OS showed a similar trend. Similarly, a study on overall MTC patients revealed that the disease-specific mortality of patients over 65 years old and over 80 years old were 2.9 times and 6.7 times higher, compared with patients under 65 years old16. This suggests that for patients over 65 years old, particularly those over 75 years old, who have worse prognosis, the protective effect of surgical treatment remains uncertain.

It is worth noting that subgroup analysis showed that patients with tumor sizes ≤ 20 mm did not benefit from surgery (p = 0.09). This is the opposite of the prognostic factor in overall MTC patients (smaller tumor size meaning better prognosis)5. Considering that the sample size of patients in this subgroup was relatively small (N = 56), and the survival curves showed that the prognosis of the surgery group was obviously better, with a p-value close to 0.05, we speculated that the small sample size might lead to statistical insignificance. However, it is considered that there are DMMTC patients with small primary tumors but serious metastases in clinical practice. Prospective cohort studies or clinical trials in the future are needed to further determine the benefit of surgery in this subgroup of patients.

In summary, our study advocates for primary tumor resection in DMMTC patients aged < 64, with tumor size > 20 mm, and with single metastasized site to achieve significant survival benefits. For patients aged 65–74 years, tumor size ≤ 20 mm, or with double metastasized sites, clinicopathological characteristics and overall health should be considered comprehensively to decide whether to perform primary tumor resection for potential survival benefit. For patients over 75, or with multiple metastasized sites (> 2), primary tumor resection may have no benefit.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, due to the low incidence of brain metastasis and distant lymph node metastasis, even in the large population sample of the SEER database, it is impossible to calculate the benefits of primary tumor resection for these patients. Patients with these metastases necessitate careful consideration by surgeons and family members regarding the advisability of primary tumor resection. Secondly, the absence of patients’ underlying disease information in the SEER database led us to use PSM to minimize the inter-group bias of factors affecting underlying diseases, such as age, sex, race, marital status, and income. We also used the DSS as the outcome, in order to reduce the influence of underlying diseases on surgical selection and death. However, as with previous studies, there remains a contention that bias has not been completely eliminated9–14. In order to handle such bias, follow-up large-sample prospective cohort studies with underlying disease information are still needed. Thirdly, due to the small size of the cohort (N = 238), there was over-stratification in subgroup analyses, especially in small sample subgroups (age ≤ 44 years, tumor ≤ 20 mm, multiple metastasized sites, etc.). These subgroups should be further studied in future clinical trials and prospective cohorts to obtain more definitive conclusions. Finally, with the release of multi-kinase inhibitors and selective RET inhibitors, the prognosis of advanced MTC has been significantly improved26–29. Recent retrospective cohort study revealed the protective impact of neoadjuvant targeted therapy (targeted therapy first and then surgery) and adjuvant targeted therapy (surgery first and then targeted therapy) on prognosis in advanced BRAF V600E mutant anaplastic thyroid cancer30. We speculated that the combination of primary tumor resection and targeted therapy may further enhance the prognostic impact of primary tumor resection on DMMTC patients. However, neoadjuvant targeted therapy or even primary tumor resection for DMMTC patients is rarely mentioned in published trials. This needs to be further carried out in future randomized clinical trials or prospective cohort studies, especially in several groups where primary tumor resection cannot effectively improve prognosis, in order to find the optimal treatment for DMMTC patients.

Conclusion

The implementation rate of primary tumor resection in DMMTC patients remained stable between 2010 and 2020, fluctuating around 50%. Extrathyroidal extension and lymph node metastases are the driving factors for undergoing surgery, while multiple metastasized sites (> 1) is the sole independent promoting factor for undergoing non-surgical treatment. PSM-adjusted survival analyses indicate that primary tumor resection significantly improved the OS and DSS in DMMTC patients. Further subgroup analyses advocate for primary tumor resection in DMMTC patients aged < 64, with tumor size > 20 mm, and with single metastasized site to achieve significant survival benefits. For patients aged 65–74 years, tumor size ≤ 20 mm, or with double metastasized sites, clinicopathological characteristics and overall health should be considered comprehensively to decide whether to perform primary tumor resection for potential survival benefits. For patients over 75, or with multiple metastasized sites (> 2), primary tumor resection may have no benefit.
