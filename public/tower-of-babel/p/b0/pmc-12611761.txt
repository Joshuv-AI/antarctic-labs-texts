Designing protein-based artificial kinetochores as decoys to prevent meiotic errors in oocytes
Authors: Yuanzhuo Zhou, Kohei Asai, Hirohisa Kyogoku, Tomoya S Kitajima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromosome mis-segregation during meiosis in oocytes causes miscarriages and congenital diseases. Ageing-associated premature chromosome separation is a major cause of mis-segregation. Effective prevention of premature chromosome separation has not yet been achieved. Here we design protein-based artificial kinetochores that act as decoys to prevent premature chromosome separation. Designed artificial kinetochore-like decoys are submicroscale clusters of NDC80-NUF2-tethered protein particles that can establish a biorientation-like state by competing with chromosomal kinetochores for HURP-decorated microtubules. This competition reduces excessive bipolar microtubule pulling forces exerted on chromosomes, thereby effectively preventing premature chromosome separation during meiosis I and II in aged mouse oocytes. These effects suppress egg aneuploidy. This study provides a decoy strategy with biocompatible artificial kinetochores to prevent ageing-associated meiotic errors in oocytes.Subject terms: Chromosome segregation, Kinetochores, Synthetic biology

Main

Chromosome mis-segregation during meiosis in oocytes is the leading cause of miscarriage and congenital diseases, such as Down’s syndrome. The frequency of mis-segregation increases with maternal age1,2. In mouse oocytes, ageing-associated increase in premature chromosome separation is the primary cause of chromosome mis-segregation in both meiotic divisions, meiosis I and II3,4. Similarly, in human oocytes, premature chromosome separation is a major cause of ageing-associated chromosome mis-segregation, although additional defects also have a role3,5,6. Effective prevention of premature chromosome separation during meiotic divisions in oocytes remains an unattained goal.

Most premature chromosome separation occurs during the M phase of meiotic division3,4. At the onset of the M phase of meiosis I, each meiotic chromosome consists of two maternal and two paternal chromatids, held together by cohesin, a protein complex that mediates chromosome cohesion1,2. During the M phase, spindle microtubules attach to the pair of kinetochores on chromosomes, pulling them towards opposite spindle poles. These bipolar microtubule forces are counteracted by chromosome cohesion, generating tension between the pair of kinetochores. The tension stabilizes microtubule attachments, establishing chromosome biorientation. This allows chromosome alignment at the spindle equator, forming the metaphase plate, a prerequisite for proper chromosome segregation7. However, with advancing age, chromosome cohesion weakens8,9. Owing to this, in aged oocytes, chromosomes often fail to resist microtubule pulling forces, leading to premature separation of maternal and paternal (homologous) chromosomes during metaphase of meiosis I3,5,10. These prematurely separated chromosomes result in mis-segregation at anaphase I, including homologous chromosome non-disjunction (4:0 segregation), unbalanced segregation of sister chromatids (3:1 segregation, also known as unbalanced predivision) and balanced segregation of sister chromatids (2:2 equational-like sister segregation, also known as balanced predivision)3,11. Ageing-associated weakening of cohesion also compromises meiosis II, where centromeric cohesion between sister chromatids is critical for faithful chromosome segregation4,12,13. In naturally aged mouse models, premature separation of sister chromatids during metaphase II is more prevalent than premature chromosome separation during metaphase I, leading to segregation errors at anaphase II3,4,12,13. Given that premature chromosome separation is facilitated by microtubule pulling forces3,10, modulating their magnitude may offer a strategy to prevent premature chromosome separation during meiotic divisions.

Our recent findings demonstrated that microscale beads tethering the outer kinetochore heterodimer NDC80-NUF214,15 (hereafter referred to as NDC80 beads) can displace chromosomes from the inner region of the spindle equator, where microtubule pulling forces are stronger, and reduce the incidence of premature chromosome separation in aged mouse oocytes10,16. However, whether NDC80 beads reduced premature chromosome separation by displacing chromosomes or by weakening microtubule pulling forces remains unclear. Moreover, NDC80 beads only slightly reduced premature chromosome separation, and the effect was observed only in a non-physiological condition where metaphase I was pharmacologically prolonged10. In addition, strategies using NDC80 beads raise at least two major concerns that limit their practical use. First, as non-biodegradable materials, the use of beads raises concerns about biocompatibility. Second, the displacement of chromosomes by NDC80 beads may have adverse effects on subsequent processes such as chromosome segregation. These limitations have prompted us to develop biocompatible, protein-based artificial kinetochores that effectively prevent premature chromosome separation while not affecting chromosome positioning during unperturbed meiotic divisions.

In this Report, we show a design of protein-based artificial kinetochores that act as decoys. They can establish a biorientation-like state by competing with chromosomal kinetochores for spindle microtubules, eliminating excessive microtubule pulling forces exerted on chromosomes without affecting chromosome positioning. This effect substantially prevents premature chromosome separation during both meiosis I and II in aged mouse oocytes, suppressing the incidence of egg aneuploidy. These results provide a decoy strategy using biocompatible artificial kinetochores to prevent ageing-associated meiotic errors in oocytes.

Results

Designing protein-based kinetochore-like particles

To create protein-based functional kinetochore-like particles, we used the design concept of NDC80 beads as a guide. NDC80 beads are ~2-μm-diameter spherical beads that carry NDC80-NUF2 on their surface, capable of establishing a biorientation-like state independently of recruiting SPC24–SPC2516, a heterodimer that provides a link to inner kinetochore proteins17,18. Owing to the unavailability of microscale protein-based particles, we used genetically encoded fluorescent multimeric (GEM) nanoparticles with a diameter of ~40 nm (ref. 19) as a protein-based scaffold. We genetically fused a fluorescent protein with an FK506 binding protein (FKBP)–rapamycin binding domain (FRB)20 to the surface of the GEM particles (Fig. 1a). In addition, FKBP was fused to the C terminus of NDC80ΔSPC (Fig. 1a), an NDC80 construct lacking the SPC24–SPC25-binding domain16. We microinjected RNAs encoding these engineered proteins, together with that encoding NUF2, into the cytoplasm of prophase I-arrested mouse oocytes. Oocytes were induced to synchronously enter the M phase of meiosis I in the presence of rapamycin, which triggers FKBP–FRB binding20, thereby bridging the GEM to NDC80ΔSPC (the resulting form is hereafter referred to as NDC80ΔSPC-GEM) (Fig. 1a). To assess microtubule-independent self-assembly of particles in the cytoplasm, we additionally treated oocytes with nocodazole (Extended Data Fig. 1a), a drug that inhibits microtubule polymerization. Using confocal microscopy, we observed many fluorescent foci distributed throughout the cytoplasm (Fig. 1b,c, ‘NDC80ΔSPC-WT-GEM’). These foci contained NUF2 (Extended Data Fig. 1b). The diameter of the individual foci was ~500 nm (Fig. 1d,e), much larger than that of a single GEM particle (40 nm)19, suggesting that they are a cluster of multiple NDC80ΔSPC-GEM particles. By contrast, when GEMs were expressed without NDC80ΔSPC-FKBP, they did not show such fluorescent foci, but diffuse, uniform signals throughout the cytoplasm (Fig. 1b,c, ‘GEM’), consistent with the formation of freely diffusing 40-nm particles that were not resolved by confocal microscopy. Mutations at the loop domain of NDC80, which promotes NDC80–NDC80 interaction21, significantly reduced the formation of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM fluorescent foci (Fig. 1b,c, ‘NDC80ΔSPC-DFAA-GEM’). The NDC80ΔSPC-DFAA-GEM foci were significantly smaller in diameter (Fig. 1d,e) and had weaker fluorescence than NDC80ΔSPC-GEM foci (Extended Data Fig. 1c). These results suggest that the NDC80 loop facilitates the clustering of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM particles, resulting in the formation of submicroscale NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters.

Designing the clusters of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM particles

These observations prompted us to test whether we could obtain even larger NDC80-GEM clusters by facilitating GEM–GEM interaction. To this end, we additionally expressed a linker protein carrying FKBP domains at both ends (FKBP–FKBP, hereafter referred to as ‘Linker’) (Fig. 1f). Co-expression of Linker resulted in the formation of significantly larger NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters (Fig. 1g,h; hereafter referred to as ‘NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker’), accompanied by increased fluorescence intensity (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Thus, Linker can serve as a tool to enlarge NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters in the submicroscale range.

Size-correlated alignment of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters

We then investigated the capacity of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters to align at the spindle equator, like NDC80 beads16. Live imaging throughout the M phase of meiosis I in nocodazole-free oocytes, followed by three-dimensional (3D) analysis, revealed that, without Linker, the vast majority of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters did not align at the spindle equator but localized to spindle poles (Fig. 2a–d, Extended Data Fig. 2a and Supplementary Video 1), a behaviour similar to CENP-T-tethered ~25-nm particles in somatic cells22. By contrast, with Linker, a significantly increased fraction of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters were positioned within 5 μm from the spindle equator at metaphase (Fig. 2a–d, Extended Data Fig. 2a and Supplementary Video 1), indicating the acquisition of alignment capacity, although the spindle pole-localizing fraction was still substantial (Fig. 2a–d). Tracking of the aligned NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters showed that ~80% of them maintained their position near the spindle equator for over 60 min until anaphase onset (Fig. 2e,f). The aligned NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters often exhibited an elongated shape along the spindle axis by late metaphase (Fig. 2a,g,h), suggesting the possibility that they were pulled towards the opposite spindle poles in a balanced manner by microtubules. Consistent with this idea, these clusters remained at the spindle midzone even after chromosomes were separated at anaphase (Fig. 2a). These results suggest that submicroscale NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters can stably align in a size-correlated manner, although their capacity to align is substantially lower compared with chromosomes.

SPC24–SPC25-binding domain improves the alignment of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM clusters

Next, we tested whether the alignment capacity of NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters is enhanced by allowing the recruitment of additional kinetochore proteins from the endogenous pool. To do this, we used full-length NDC80 (NDC80FL), which contains the C-terminal domain that interacts with SPC24–SPC25, a subcomplex that links NDC80-NUF2 to the outer kinetochore Mis12 complex and the inner kinetochore protein CENP-T17,18 (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Unlike NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker, NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters carried CENP-T (Extended Data Fig. 3b), but not the Mis12 complex component DSN1 (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Notably, NDC80FL-GEM-Linker self-assembled into slightly but significantly larger clusters than NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker in nocodazole-treated oocytes (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3d). Moreover, unlike NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters, NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters often exhibited an elongated shape (Fig. 3a), suggesting that the C-terminal domain of NDC80 facilitated microtubule-independent directional assembly through unknown mechanisms. Live imaging through the M phase of meiosis I in nocodazole-free oocytes showed that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters aligned more efficiently than NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters, although a substantial but relatively small fraction of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters remained at the spindle poles (Fig. 3c–e, Extended Data Fig. 3e and Supplementary Video 2). The majority of aligned NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters maintained their positions near the metaphase plate over 60 min until anaphase onset, but their alignment was not as stable as chromosome alignment (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 3f). Similar to NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters, aligned NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters exhibited an elongated shape along the spindle axis (Fig. 3c). The degree of the elongation was rapidly reduced upon treatment with monastrol (Extended Data Fig. 3g,h), a KIF11 (also known as Eg5) inhibitor that inhibits bipolar microtubule pulling forces23,24, consistent with the idea that aligned NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters are stretched by these forces. These results suggest that the SPC24–SPC25-binding domain of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker facilitates their particle clustering and efficient alignment, although their alignment capacity is still limited compared with chromosomes.

NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters can establish a biorientation-like state

The alignment of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters suggests their capacity to establish a biorientation-like state with bipolar microtubule attachment. To investigate the status of microtubule attachment, we used HURP as a marker for kinetochore microtubules25. Immunostaining at metaphase I showed that HURP-decorated fibres were attached to >80% of chromosomal kinetochores (Extended Data Fig. 4a) and overlapped with cold-stable microtubules (Extended Data Fig. 4b), as previously reported10,16, confirming that HURP serves as a marker for kinetochore microtubules. We found that a substantial but limited fraction of aligned NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters exhibited bipolar attachment of HURP-positive microtubule bundles at metaphase I (Fig. 3g,h and Extended Data Fig. 4c). By contrast, a smaller fraction of aligned NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker clusters showed bipolar attachment (Fig. 3g,h). Thus, the alignment capacity of NDC80-GEM-Linker clusters correlates with the efficiency of bipolar HURP-positive microtubule attachment. Notably, when metaphase I was prolonged by treatment with the anaphase inhibitor proTAME26, bipolar attachment of HURP-positive, cold-stable microtubules was observed on the vast majority of both aligned NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker and NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters (Fig. 3i,j, Extended Data Fig. 4d,e and Supplementary Video 3). These results suggest that both NDC80ΔSPC-GEM-Linker and NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters can establish a biorientation-like state and that the establishment efficiency is enhanced by the SPC24–SPC25-binding domain of NDC80.

NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters do not preferentially position in the inner region of the metaphase plate

The detectable but limited capacity of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters to establish a biorientation-like state motivated us to compare them with NDC80 beads, which establish a biorientation-like state as efficiently and robustly as chromosomes16. The biorientation-like state of NDC80 microbeads is accompanied by their preferential positioning in the inner region of the metaphase plate, displacing chromosomes from the inner region10,16. By contrast, NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters did not preferentially locate in the inner region of the metaphase plate nor prevent internal chromosome positioning (Fig. 4a–d). We hypothesized that the microscale size of NDC80 beads, but not the submicroscale size of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters, promotes their preferential internal positioning. Supporting this hypothesis, NDC80 beads with a diameter of ~1 μm did not preferentially position internally, unlike those with a diameter of ~3 μm (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b), while both exhibited the capacity to align at the metaphase plate (Extended Data Fig. 5c,d). Mean fluorescence intensity did not significantly differ between the beads of different sizes (Extended Data Fig. 5e), indicating that the density of NDC80 on the bead surface was comparable. These results support the idea that the submicroscale size of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters allows a biorientation-like state without preferential internal positioning.

NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters compete with kinetochores for HURP-decorated microtubule fibres

These findings allowed us to investigate whether artificial kinetochores can affect the status of chromosome biorientation independently of affecting chromosome positioning. Careful assessment of immunofluorescence signals showed that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters significantly reduced the thickness of HURP-decorated microtubule bundles that were attached to chromosomal kinetochores (Fig. 4e,f). Nevertheless, NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters did not increase chromosome misalignment during metaphase I (Fig. 4g) or the aneuploidy rate of metaphase II eggs after the completion of meiosis I (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). These results suggest that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters decrease the number of kinetochore-attached microtubules within each bundle, while having little adverse effect on the fidelity of chromosome segregation during meiosis I.

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that artificial kinetochores compete with chromosomal kinetochores for finite components of HURP-decorated microtubule fibres. If this hypothesis holds true, increasing the microtubule-binding activity of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters should reduce the number of HURP-decorated microtubules that are attached to kinetochores. To test this possibility, we used NDC80-9A, a phospho-deficient mutant form that has a higher microtubule-binding affinity27,28. Consistent with the hypothesis, NDC80FL-9A-GEM-Linker clusters tended to reduce the thickness of HURP-decorated microtubule bundles attached to chromosomal kinetochores more than NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters (Fig. 4e,f). Furthermore, NDC80FL-9A-GEM-Linker clusters significantly increased misaligned chromosomes (Fig. 4g). These results suggest that artificial kinetochores compete with chromosomal kinetochores for finite components of HURP-decorated microtubules in the spindle.

NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters prevent premature chromosome separation during prolonged metaphase I in aged oocytes

These observations prompted us to test whether NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters could suppress premature chromosome separation in aged oocytes, a defect that occurs predominantly in the inner region of the metaphase plate where HURP-decorated microtubule fibres are enriched10. We used aged mouse oocytes arrested at metaphase I by treatment with proTAME, a model for a sensitive assay to detect effects on premature chromosome separation10. In this model, premature chromosome separation depended on bipolar microtubule pulling forces, as treatment of monastrol significantly reduced premature chromosome separation (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). Notably, rapamycin-induced NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters significantly reduced the frequency of premature chromosome separation (Fig. 5a,b), while not significantly increasing the misalignment of intact chromosomes (Fig. 5c). By contrast, rapamycin treatment in the absence of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker components did not significantly affect the frequency of premature chromosome separation (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d). Furthermore, consistent with the idea that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters suppressed premature chromosome separation by reducing HURP-decorated microtubule fibres attached to chromosomal kinetochores, HURP overexpression significantly increased premature chromosome separation during prolonged metaphase I in aged oocytes (Fig. 5d,e). These results demonstrate that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters effectively suppress premature chromosome separation, probably by competing with chromosomal kinetochores for HURP-decorated microtubule fibres, without affecting chromosome positioning or alignment during prolonged metaphase I in aged oocytes.

Effects of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters suppress chromosome mis-segregation during unperturbed meiosis I in aged oocytes

These findings raise the intriguing possibility that these effects of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters suppress ageing-associated egg aneuploidy. To test this possibility, we immunostained aged metaphase II oocytes carrying NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters after the completion of unperturbed meiosis I. Counting the number of kinetochores in three dimensions showed that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters significantly reduced the frequency of oocytes with an abnormal number of kinetochores (that is, greater or less than 40) at metaphase II (Fig. 5f,g). These results suggest that the effects of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters suppress chromosome mis-segregation during unperturbed meiosis I in aged oocytes, thereby reducing the incidence of egg aneuploidy.

NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters prevent premature sister chromatid separation during unperturbed metaphase II in aged oocytes

We additionally examined whether NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters prevent premature sister chromatid separation during metaphase II, at which oocytes are naturally arrested until fertilization. Premature sister chromatid separation during metaphase II is more prevalent than premature chromosome separation during metaphase I in aged mouse oocytes3,4,12,13. Therefore, most of prematurely separated chromatids observed at metaphase II in aged oocytes carrying the normal number of kinetochores (that is, equal to 40) are attributed to chromatid separation after entry into meiosis II, rather than to balanced segregation of sister chromatids during meiosis I. Remarkably, rapamycin-induced NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters significantly reduced the frequency of oocytes with prematurely separated chromatids at metaphase II in aged oocytes (Fig. 6a,b). By contrast, rapamycin treatment in the absence of NDC80FL-GEM-Linker components did not significantly affect the fraction of oocytes with prematurely separated chromatids (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b). Consistent with the idea that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters attenuated bipolar microtubule pulling forces on chromosomal kinetochores at metaphase II, the distance between paired sister kinetochores was significantly reduced (Fig. 6c,d). Nevertheless, NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters did not significantly increase the misalignment of paired sister chromatids at metaphase II (Fig. 6e), suggesting that the level of bipolar forces required for chromosome alignment is maintained. We suggest that the moderate attenuation of bipolar forces on chromosomal kinetochores by NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters prevents premature separation of sister chromatids while maintaining their alignment at metaphase II in aged oocytes. Even with these attenuated forces, the distance between sister kinetochores in aged oocytes carrying NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters was greater than in normal, young oocytes (Fig. 6d), probably due to the weakening of centromeric cohesion associated with age4,8,9,12. Experiments using more severely aged (83–84 weeks old) mouse oocytes yielded consistent results (Extended Data Fig. 9a–e). These results demonstrate that NDC80FL-GEM-Linker clusters effectively prevent premature sister chromatid separation without affecting chromosome alignment during unperturbed metaphase II in aged oocytes.

Discussion

This study has demonstrated a design of protein-based artificial kinetochore clusters that establish a biorientation-like state in the spindle and effectively prevent premature chromosome separation during both meiotic divisions, meiosis I and II. Artificial kinetochore clusters function as decoys, competing with chromosomal kinetochores for a limited number of components for HURP-decorated microtubule fibres. Consequently, artificial kinetochore clusters reduce microtubule-mediated pulling forces exerted on chromosomes, thereby preventing premature chromosome separation during both meiosis I and II in aged oocytes. These effects suppress the incidence of egg aneuploidy (Fig. 6f).

The protein-based artificial kinetochore clusters designed in this study appear as submicroscale fluorescent foci, smaller than the previously reported NDC80 beads of ~2 μm in diameter16. Individual submicroscale artificial kinetochore clusters are less efficient at establishing a biorientation-like state compared to the larger microscale NDC80 beads, consistent with the notion that a larger scaffold enhances NDC80-NUF2-mediated biorientation16. In addition, protein-based artificial kinetochores do not preferentially position in the inner region of the metaphase plate, unlike NDC80 beads. Despite the limited ability of individual submicroscale clusters of artificial kinetochores to establish a biorientation-like state and localize to the inner region of the metaphase plate, their in cellulo self-assembly design enabled the production of large numbers of such clusters in oocytes. This abundance allowed them to compete effectively with chromosomal kinetochores for microtubules without displacing chromosomes to the outer region of the metaphase plate, thereby effectively preventing premature chromosome separation.

Although the strength of microtubule pulling forces may have evolved to be optimal for chromosome segregation in young oocytes, the force strength may be suboptimal in aged oocytes. As chromosome cohesion weakens with age, chromosomes become more prone to premature separation under microtubule pulling forces3,5,10. In aged oocytes, a pulling force weaker than that optimal for young oocytes may be more suitable to minimize the incidence of chromosome segregation errors. This study presents a strategy that uses artificial kinetochores as decoys to reduce microtubule-mediated pulling forces on chromosomes. By carefully controlling the strength of kinetochore-like decoys without disturbing chromosome alignment, we demonstrated the prevention of premature chromosome separation during meiosis I and meiosis II in aged mouse oocytes.

This study has several limitations. First, this study focused on evaluating the effectiveness of the decoy strategy under the in vitro maturation (IVM) culture of oocytes. Future studies will be needed to optimize the decoy strategy for IVM followed by in vitro fertilization, to facilitate the production of euploid zygotes. Such optimization will probably include refining the strength and dosage of the decoy components and potentially introducing temporal control of decoy assembly and disassembly by adding and washing out rapamycin. Second, this study did not evaluate the effectiveness of this strategy in preventing premature chromosome separation in human oocytes. Instead, we demonstrated that the decoy strategy prevents premature chromosome separation during pharmacologically prolonged metaphase I in aged mouse oocytes and suppresses the incidence of egg aneuploidy during unperturbed meiosis. Given that human oocytes require approximately 17 h to complete meiosis I29, much longer than mouse oocytes, it is possible that the decoy strategy can also prevent premature chromosome separation in unperturbed meiosis I of aged human oocytes and reduce the incidence of egg aneuploidy.

A recent study using an in vitro reconstituted follicle growth culture from the secondary stage to the antral stage showed that aged oocytes grown in young follicles maintain chromosome cohesion better than those grown in aged follicles30. This follicle culture strategy is unlikely to restore chromosome cohesion weakened during primordial and primary stages, which span most of the oocyte lifespan, because the establishment of chromosome cohesion by cohesin requires DNA replication and, thus, is limited to foetal oocyte development30,31. Our decoy strategy is similar to their follicle strategy in that it does not restore weakened chromosome cohesion. Rather than restoring chromosome cohesion, our strategy compensates for weakened chromosome cohesion by reducing excessive microtubule-mediated forces that promote premature chromosome separation. A substantial difference between our strategy and their follicle strategy is the culture duration. In contrast to their long-term follicle culture strategy (~7 days), our strategy uses a short-duration IVM culture (<1 day). Unlike follicle culture, IVM culture is already widely used in clinical settings, currently mainly for patients with polycystic ovarian syndrome32–34. The decoy strategy using protein-based artificial kinetochores may pave the way for developing biocompatible and temporally controllable tools to prevent ageing-associated egg aneuploidy for assisted reproductive technology.

Methods

Mice

All animal experiments are approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at RIKEN Kobe Branch (IACUC). B6D2F1 (C57BL/6 × DBA/2) female mice (Japan SLC) were used to obtain oocytes. Mice aged 60–95 weeks were used to obtain aged oocytes. For the other experiments, mice at 8–12 weeks of age were used.

Mouse oocyte culture

Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 0.2 ml (10 IU) of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin or 0.1 ml of CARD HyperOva (KYUDO). Fully grown germinal vesicle oocytes were collected 48 h after injection of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin. All samples were randomly allocated into different experimental groups. Oocytes were incubated in the M2 medium containing 200 µM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX, Sigma) at 37 °C. Meiotic resumption was induced by IBMX washout. When indicated, 10 µM nocodazole (Sigma), 100 µM monastrol (Sigma), 2 µM proTAME (Bio-Techne R&D Systems) or 500 nM rapamycin (Funakoshi) was added to the medium immediately after IBMX washout. In Extended Data Fig. 3g, 20 µM monastrol (Sigma) was added to the medium at 5 h after IBMX washout.

RNA microinjection

mRNAs were transcribed and purified using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mRNAs were microinjected into fully grown germinal vesicle oocytes, followed by 2-h incubation at 37 °C until IBMX washout. 0.3 pg of NDC80(ΔSPC-WT/ΔSPC-DFAA/FL-WT/FL-9A)-FKBP, 0.3 pg of NUF2, 0.3 pg of NUF2-HA, 0.45 pg of H2B-mCherry, 3.5 pg of hAG-Pfv-Sapphire-GS-FRB, 0.15 pg of FKBP-GS-FKBP, 1 pg of CENP-T-mCherry, 1 pg of mCherry and 3 pg of EGFP-HURP were injected. For experiments with microbeads, microinjections were performed with 1 pg of NDC80-GFP, 1 pg of NUF2 and 1.5 pg of H2B-mCherry16.

The hAG-Pfv-Sapphire-GS-FRB construct was made from pCDNA3.1-pCMV-PfV-GS-Sapphire (a gift from L. Holt; Addgene plasmid 116933; http://n2t.net/addgene:116933; RRID:Addgene 116933)19 and phAG (humanized Azami-Green for Fluoppi, MBL, AM-VS0801M). The constructs with FRB and FKBP were made from pEGFP-FRB (a gift from K. Hahn; Addgene plasmid 25919; http://n2t.net/addgene:25919; RRID:Addgene 25919)35 and YFP-FKBP (YF) (a gift from T. Meyer; Addgene plasmid 20175; http://n2t.net/addgene:20175; RRID:Addgene 20175)36, respectively. The other constructs were previously described16,37.

A step-by-step protocol for the construction of protein-based artificial kinetochores designed in this study is available via protocols.io at 10.17504/protocols.io.e6nvw41k9lmk/v1 (ref. 38).

Bead microinjection

Rat anti-GFP antibody (D153-3, MBL; 1:30) was immobilized to protein G-conjugated beads (MonoMag Protein G Beads, 1 µm in diameter MPG1000 or 3 µm in diameter MPG3000, Ocean NanoTech; 1:20) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 2.5 h at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were washed and stored in PBS. The anti-GFP-bound beads were introduced into mRNA-injected germinal vesicle-stage oocytes through microinjection with a piezo pulse. Four beads with a diameter of 3 µm, or 4–11 beads with a diameter of 1 µm, were microinjected into each oocyte. The microinjected oocytes were cultured at 37 °C for 1 h before meiotic resumption.

Live imaging

An LSM900 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss) equipped with a 40× C-Apochromat 1.2 numerical aperture (NA) water immersion objective lens (Carl Zeiss) was controlled using Zen software with a custom ImageJ macro implementing the previously reported autofocus method39. For experiments with nocodazole treatment, we recorded 81 z-confocal sections (every 0.5 µm) of 512 × 512 pixel xy images, which covered a total volume of 79.9 µm × 79.9 µm × 40 µm, at 30-min time intervals. For experiments with monastrol treatment, we recorded 29 z-confocal sections (every 1.25 µm) of 512 × 512 pixel xy images, which covered a total volume of 39.93 µm × 39.93 µm × 35 µm, at 3-min time intervals. For the other experiments, we recorded z-confocal sections (every 1 µm or 1.5 µm) of 512 × 512 pixel xy images, at 10-min time intervals. For experiments with microbeads, an LSM780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss) equipped with a 40× C-Apochromat 1.2 NA water immersion objective lens (Carl Zeiss) was controlled using Zen software with the multiposition autofocus macro PipelineConstructor40. For imaging of chromosomes and beads, we recorded 25 z-confocal sections (every 1.25 μm) of 512 × 512 pixel xy images, which covered a total volume of 38.65 µm × 38.65 µm × 30 µm, at 5-min time intervals for at least 12 h after the induction of maturation. See also Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence

Oocytes were fixed with a fixation buffer (1.6% formaldehyde in 100 mM PIPES (pH7.0), 1 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% Triton X-100) at room temperature for 30 min. After washing four times, oocytes were incubated in PBT (PBS + 0.1% Triton X-100) at 4 °C overnight. Oocytes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA)-PBT at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies in 3% BSA-PBT at 4 °C overnight. Oocytes were washed four times in 3% BSA-PBT and incubated with secondary antibodies and 5 µg ml−1 Hoechst33342 (Invitrogen) in 3% BSA-PBT at room temperature for 2 h or at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used were mouse anti-HA (BioLegend, MMS-101R; 1:500), rabbit anti-DSN1 (a gift from H. Shibuya; 1:500), rabbit anti-CENP-C (a gift from Y. Watanabe; 1:500), mouse anti-mCherry (Clontech, 632543; 1:500), human anti-centromere antibodies (ACA, Antibodies Incorporated, 15–234; 1:500), rabbit anti-HURP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-98809; 1:500) and rat anti-α tubulin (YL1/2) (Bio-Rad, MCA77G; 1:1500). The secondary antibody used was Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-Human IgG (H + L) (A11013), Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-rat lgG (H + L) (A21434), goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (A21424), goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (A21429), Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-Human IgG (H + L) (A21445), goat anti-rat IgG (H + L) (A21247) and donkey anti-rabbit lgG (H + L) (A31573) (1:500, Molecular Probes). Oocytes were imaged under an LSM880 with Airyscan confocal microscope equipped with a 40× C-Apochromat 1.2 NA water immersion objective lens (Carl Zeiss). For experiments with nocodazole treatment, we recorded z-confocal sections (every 0.5 µm) of 512 × 512 pixel xy images. For the other experiments, we recorded z-confocal sections (every 0.2 µm) of 512 × 512 pixel xy images. See also Supplementary Table 1.

4D analysis

Chromosomes and NDC80-GEM foci were reconstructed and segmented in three dimensions using Imaris software (Bitplane) with an automatic thresholding, followed by manual validation. The spindle axis was manually estimated based on chromosome distribution at each timepoint, and the spindle centre was defined as the centre of mass of chromosomes. For the timepoints before the spindle axis was established (for example, 2 h after nuclear envelope breakdown (NEBD)), the same spindle axis at 3 h after NEBD was applied10. In the alignment analysis, NDC80-GEM clusters located outside of the spindle were manually excluded. The volume of NDC80-GEM clusters was calculated by the algorithm equipped in Imaris. To generate the kymograph of chromosomes and NDC80-GEMs, signals were projected to the spindle equator for each timepoint with an in-house macro in Fiji41.

Signal intensity quantification

All measurements of fluorescence signals were performed in Fiji41. For the full width at half maximum (FWHM) analysis, a line selection was made through the centre of the NDC80-GEM cluster or perpendicular to the microtubule bundle at a distance of 0.5 µm from the microtubule tip. The intensity plot profile along the line was obtained and fitted to a Gaussian distribution, which was used to calculate the FWHM. To measure the signal intensity of NDC80-GEM clusters and kinetochores, ten randomly selected rectangular areas (four pixels each) were measured for each group. The cytoplasmic background intensity was then subtracted. To measure the signal intensity on NDC80 beads, the mean fluorescence intensity on each bead was measured and subtracted by the mean intensity in an adjacent cytoplasmic region. For classification of microtubule attachments using immunofluorescence images, the HURP intensity plot profile along a line over the NDC80-GEM cluster was obtained and background-subtracted. On the line profile, the peak HURP intensities at the two sides of the NDC80-GEM cluster were defined as Int_A and Int_C, respectively, while the minimum HURP intensity around the middle of the cluster was defined as Int_B. If Int_B was less than half of both Int_A and Int_C, it was categorized into ‘bipolar attachment’.

Statistical analysis

All graphs and statistical analysis were performed in R. Box plots were generated using geom boxplot in R. The central line represents the median, the box shows the IQR (spanning from the 25th to the 75th percentile) and the whiskers extend to 1.5 times the IQR. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. All experiments were performed independently at least three times. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments.
