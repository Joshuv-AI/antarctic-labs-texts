Phase 1/2 study of IMC-C103C, a T cell receptor bispecific (MAGE-A4×CD3) ImmTAC targeting MAGE-A4-expressing malignancies

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIMC-C103C is an ImmTAC bispecific (MAGE-A4×CD3) T cell engager targeting a peptide from the cancer-testis antigen MAGE-A4 presented by HLA-A*02:01. This phase 1/2 study in advanced solid tumors (IMC-C103C-101; NCT03973333) investigated the safety and preliminary anti-tumor activity of IMC-C103C.MethodsHLA-A*02:01+ patients with previously treated advanced solid tumors received IMC-C103C by weekly intravenous infusion, with step-up dosing introduced at higher dose levels. Dose-escalation decisions were guided by the mTPI-2 method. Key objectives included evaluating safety and preliminary anti-tumor activity; pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and biomarkers were also assessed.Results68 patients were enrolled in 10 dose-escalation cohorts (n=56; 64% ovarian carcinoma (OC)) and one OC expansion cohort (n=12). In dose escalation, patients received doses ranging from 0.5 to 240 µg. The dose-limiting toxicity rate at the two highest dose levels (1/6 and 2/10) did not exceed the maximum tolerated dose. A lower-dose regimen of 15–45-140 µg, with clinical activity, and a more favorable tolerability profile, was selected as the expansion dose. There were no treatment-related discontinuations or deaths. The most common treatment-related adverse events were cytokine-mediated, including grade 1/2 cytokine release syndrome and associated symptoms. While MAGE-A4 expression was observed in most patient’s tumors (71% positive), the level of expression was low (median H-score=16). Initial clinical and pharmacodynamic activity was observed at doses of 15 µg, with more consistent activity observed at doses ≥90 µg. At doses ≥90 µg, patients with MAGE-A4+ OC had deeper reductions in tumor size and circulating tumor DNA and trended to have longer overall survival than patients with MAGE-A4– OC.ConclusionsIMC-C103C was well tolerated at doses up to 140 µg, induced dose-dependent pharmacodynamic changes, and demonstrated clinical activity in multiple patients with MAGE-A4+ solid tumors. Clinical activity was dependent on MAGE-A4 expression; however, expression was especially low in the higher dosing cohorts, limiting adequate efficacy assessment.Trial registration numberNCT03973333.Keywords: Solid tumor, T cell Receptor - TCR, Immunotherapy, Ovarian Cancer

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This first-in-human phase 1/2 study showed that the MAGE-A4-targeting ImmTAC IMC-C103C had a manageable safety profile, induced dose-dependent pharmacodynamic activity, and showed preliminary antitumor activity that was largely restricted to patients with detectable tumor MAGE-A4 expression.

Introduction

Cancer testis antigens (CTAs) are a subgroup of tumor-associated antigens that are expressed by a wide variety of tumors, yet in healthy tissues, their expression is restricted to immune-privileged sites such as the testis or placenta.1 2 Melanoma-associated antigen A4 (MAGE-A4) is a member of the MAGE family of CTAs. Many CTAs are known to be processed into antigenic peptides that can be presented to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells by HLA Class I (HLA-I) molecules,3,5 making them attractive targets for anti-tumor immunotherapy.6 7

Expression of MAGE-A4 has been detected to varying degrees in several cancers, with high expression in urothelial, lung, ovarian, esophageal, gastric, and head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC).8,13 Expression of MAGE proteins is often associated with more aggressive tumors and correlates with a poorer prognosis.9,1114 Testicular and placental cells can express MAGE-A4, but they do not express HLA-I molecules and thus are not targeted by MAGE-A4-specific cytotoxic T cells. Although the biological function of MAGE-A4 in normal tissues and tumors is still poorly understood, it may provide a unique marker to selectively target tumor cells over normal tissue, minimizing the likelihood of on-target, off-tumor effects associated with MAGE-A4-targeting therapies. Given the ability of the immune system to recognize these tumor-specific proteins, multiple attempts have been made to target MAGE proteins using immune therapy, including afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel), the recently approved TCR-T cell therapy targeting MAGE-A4.17,19

ImmTAC (Immune mobilizing monoclonal T cell receptor Against Cancer) molecules are soluble T cell redirecting bispecific fusion proteins that use an affinity-enhanced T cell receptor (TCR) to target any protein, including intracellular antigens, that is processed and presented as peptide-HLA complexes on the target cell surface.20 21 IMC-C103C is an investigational ImmTAC bispecific comprising an affinity-enhanced HLA-A*02:01-restricted T cell receptor, specific for a peptide derived from MAGE-A4, fused to an anti-CD3 single chain Fv effector domain. on binding to its intended peptide-HLA complex on the tumor cell surface, the anti-CD3 effector domain can recruit and activate any T cell, regardless of its native specificity, to produce effector cytokines, trigger an anti-tumor immune response, and lyse the target-bearing tumor cell.

Proof of concept for the approach of using a TCR bispecific to redirect polyclonal T cells against tumor cells was shown with tebentafusp (gp100×CD3), which demonstrated an overall survival (OS) benefit of HR 0.51, and is now approved for the treatment of HLA-A*02:01-positive adult patients with unresectable or metastatic uveal melanoma (mUM).22 23 The overall response rate (ORR) for tebentafusp was modest at 11% and significantly underestimated the OS HR 0.51. In contrast to ORR, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) molecular response (0.5 log (68%) reduction in ctDNA levels from baseline) by week 9 was observed in a majority of patients and was strongly associated with OS.24 25 Similar observations were seen in the phase 1/2 study of brenetafusp (PRAME×CD3; formerly IMC-F106C), an ImmTAC targeting the CTA PRAME, where the rate of disease control and ctDNA molecular response may be better early endpoints of clinical benefit than Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) ORR (NCT04262466).26

This study investigated the safety and efficacy of IMC-C103C as monotherapy in HLA-A*02:01-positive patients with advanced solid tumors.

Methods

Study design

IMC-C103C-101 was an open-label, multicenter, phase 1/2 first-in-human trial of IMC-C103C as monotherapy in HLA-A*02:01-positive patients with advanced solid tumors (NCT03973333). The primary objective of dose escalation was to identify the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) or recommended dose of IMC-C103C. The primary objective of the expansion cohort was to assess the preliminary anti-tumor activity of IMC-C103C monotherapy in patients with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (OC). Additional objectives included safety and tolerability of IMC-C103C, pharmacokinetics, anti-drug antibody (ADA) formation, and pharmacodynamic changes.

The combination of IMC-C103C with atezolimab was initially planned for non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) and urothelial carcinoma based on contemporary labeled indications. However, following emerging data and low enrollment of patients with these tumor types in monotherapy dose escalation, further investigation of the combination was not pursued.

Patients

Patients were eligible if they were HLA-A*02:01-positive by the central laboratory assay, aged ≥18 years, had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1, had a life expectancy of at least 3 months, had unresectable or metastatic NSCLC, HNSCC, esophageal carcinoma, gastric carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, OC, or synovial sarcoma that had relapsed after, was refractory to, or who were intolerant of standard treatment regimens. There was no limit to the number of prior therapies, and patients previously treated with T cell therapies were permitted to enroll. Key exclusion criteria were untreated or symptomatic CNS metastases, clinically significant pulmonary disease or impaired lung function, clinically significant cardiac disease or impaired cardiac function, active autoimmune disease requiring treatment, or out-of-range protocol-defined laboratory parameters.

The expansion cohort enrolled patients with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) who had progressed during or within 6 months of completing a platinum-based chemotherapy regimen, and if eligible, had received a prior PARP inhibitor. Participants in the expansion cohort were required to have measurable disease at baseline per RECIST V.1.1.

Prospective testing for tumor-specific MAGE-A4 protein expression was required for indications documented to have low MAGE-A4 prevalence (gastric and urothelial carcinoma); all other indications (NSCLC-SCC, esophageal-SCC, HGSOC, synovial sarcoma) were tested retrospectively on freshly obtained or archival tissue biopsies.

Treatment

Study participants received IMC-C103C by weekly intravenous infusion until unacceptable toxicity, disease progression, or a decision to withdraw by the investigator or patient. Each treatment cycle was defined as 21 days. Treatment beyond initial radiographic progression per RECIST V.1.1 was allowed in the absence of clinically significant symptomatic progression (eg, decline in performance status, threat to vital organs).

Dosing began at 0.5 µg, which was predicted to provide a maximum serum concentration equal to the minimum anticipated biological effect level (figure 1). The first two dose cohorts enrolled two patients. All other dose-escalation cohorts initially enrolled three to six patients and could be expanded to better characterize safety and activity. During dose escalation, the study safety team, comprising the study investigators, sponsor medical monitor, and sponsor statistician, implemented multiple approaches to mitigate the risk of cytokine-mediated adverse events (AEs). The dosing regimen was adjusted from a single fixed dose to step-up dosing, initially with a single step dose (15–45 µg) and then two step doses (15–45-90 µg). The initial step doses were held fixed while the target dose (given weekly starting Day 15) was further escalated, to a maximum dose of 240 µg. A standard premedication regimen (antipyretic, antihistamine, and antiemetic) was added at the 15–45 µg dose level and corticosteroid premedication (dexamethasone 4–6 mg) was also added during evaluation of the 15–45-90 µg dose level. Patients in the expansion cohort received two step-up doses of 15 and 45 µg prior to the weekly target dose of 140 µg (15−45−140 µg).

Dose escalation schema. One participant was initially enrolled in cohort 15−45−90 µg (duration 2.1 months) and re-enrolled in cohort 15−45−180 µg. Above 1.5 µg, all dose escalation decisions (indicated by arrows) were made after assessing safety in at least 3–6 participants, including at least three participants with confirmed MAGE-A4 positive tumor expression.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary objective of dose escalation was to identify the MTD and/or recommended dose of IMC-C103C as intravenous monotherapy. Dose escalation decisions were made following review of all available safety, pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and efficacy data, and were guided by the mTPI-2 method using a target toxicity rate of 30% and a target toxicity interval of (25% to 32%). Dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) included any grade ≥3 AE during the 21-day DLT-evaluation period for which a relationship to the study drug could not be ruled out, with certain modifications.

AEs were assessed and graded by the investigator according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events V.5.0, except for cytokine release syndrome (CRS), which was graded based on the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy 2019 consensus grading system.27

Treatment efficacy was assessed by the investigators using RECIST V.1.1. Tumor assessments were conducted every 9 weeks. OS was measured from the start of treatment to the time of death. Patients were censored on the last date they were known to be alive.

Pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity

Serum concentrations of IMC-C103C were determined at multiple timepoints over 7 days (168 hours) following the first three doses using an electrochemiluminescent immunoassay (ECLIA); the lower limit of quantification was 10 pg/mL. Non-compartmental analysis was performed on IMC-C103C concentration-time data using Phoenix WinNonlin (V.8.1, Certara, Princeton, New Jersey, USA). Serum samples were collected at baseline and periodically during treatment for ADA testing. Antibodies to IMC-C103C were detected using a bridging format ECLIA. Samples testing positive in the screening assay were further evaluated in confirmatory and titer assays.

MAGE-A4 expression on tumor biopsy samples

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor samples were collected prior to treatment. Baseline or archival tumor samples were sectioned and stained by immunohistochemistry (IHC) using a monoclonal antibody to MAGE-A4 (clone OTI1F9, OriGene) with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine chromogen and counterstained with hematoxylin on the Dako PT Link autostainer. Digital images were evaluated by a board-certified pathologist and reported as H-score and tumor cell positivity.

A tumor was classified as positive if specific nuclear staining was seen at any intensity in ≥1% of viable tumor cells. A tumor was determined as unknown for MAGE-A4 positivity if the sample was unevaluable due to lack of viable tumor.

Pharmacodynamics and biomarker assessments

IHC analysis was performed on available baseline and on-treatment (post second dose; C1D8-14) tumor biopsy samples. T cells were enumerated using primary antibodies against CD3 (clone 2GV6) and CD8A (clone SP57), performed on the Ventana Discovery Ultra autostainer (Roche Diagnostics). Positive cells were visualized with a purple chromogen (Roche Diagnostics), counterstained with hematoxylin, and enumerated from digital images using HALO software (Indica Labs).

Changes in absolute lymphocyte counts (ALCs) were assessed predose and 24 hours postdose, for the first 2–3 doses, locally at study sites using routine hematology analysis. Changes in serum cytokine concentrations (including IL-2, IL-6, and IFNγ) were assessed before and 4, 8, and 24 hours after the third dose using Luminex Bead-based multiplexing kits and acquired with xPONENT software V.4.2 SECURITY on a MAGPIX instrument.

ctDNA levels were assessed in plasma samples collected at baseline and at week 7 (cycle 3 day 1) on-treatment using the next-generation sequencing Guardant 360 assay (Guardant Health). This assay enables detection of up to 74 gene alterations covering point mutations and deletion variants. On-treatment changes in ctDNA levels were evaluated for all patients with OC who received target doses ≥90 µg with evaluable samples at both time points. Variants with allele frequencies≤0.3% at baseline were excluded from the analysis. Molecular response was defined as a 0.5 log (68%) reduction in ctDNA levels from baseline.

Statistical assessments

Safety was assessed in all patients treated with IMC-C103C. ORR and DCR were assessed in the full analysis set, which included patients with measurable disease at baseline who received ≥1 dose of study treatment. Efficacy was also assessed in patients with OC who received IMC-C103C target doses of ≥90 µg.

Time-to-event estimates of OS were calculated via Kaplan-Meier methods. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software V.9.4 or R V.4.1.0 for ctDNA analyses. The clinical data cut-off date was May 12, 2023.

Results

Patient population

From November 26, 2019 to March 1, 2023, 68 patients were enrolled in the study including 56 patients in ten dose escalation cohorts and 12 patients in an expansion cohort (online supplemental figure 1). The median age was 58 years, and all patients had a baseline ECOG performance score of 0 or 1 (table 1). Dose escalation included 36 patients with OC (64%), eight patients with synovial sarcoma (14%), four each with esophageal carcinoma and NSCLC (7% each), two with HNSCC (4%), and one each of urothelial and gastric carcinoma. Expansion was restricted to HGSOC.

Patient demographics and disease characteristics

HNSCC, head and neck squamous cell carcinomas; NSCLC, non-small cell lung carcinoma.

Most participants (71%) had OC (n=48; table 1), mostly HGSOC (90%), and had received a median of four prior lines of therapy; all had received prior platinum and taxane-based chemotherapy, 83% (n=40) had received prior bevacizumab, and 65% (n=31) had received a prior PARP inhibitor. Of the eight patients with synovial sarcoma, none had received prior TCR-T therapy.

Tumor MAGE-A4 expression

Overall, tumor expression of MAGE-A4 was low with a median H-score of 16 (range, 0–300; table 1). MAGE-A4 expression was not detectable in tumor biopsies from 15 patients (22%), low (2+or 3+staining in <10% of cells) in 23 patients (34%), and high (2+ or 3+ staining ≥ 10% of cells) in 25 patients (37%). MAGE-A4 expression in OC was consistent with results from the overall study population (25% negative, 40% low, 25% high) with a median H-score of 8 (range 0–298).

Exposure and disposition

The median duration of treatment in dose escalation was 2.1 months (range, 0.5–17.7) and 2.1 months (range 0.9–5.7) in the expansion cohort. 38 patients (56%) missed at least 1 weekly dose due to an emergent AE, with neutropenia (n=10), abdominal pain, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) increase, COVID-19, CRS, and dyspnea (n=3 each) being the most common events. Ten patients had a delay in step-up dosing due to an AE and nine patients, all in dose escalation, reduced from the target dose due to an AE. One-third of patients (n=20; 33%) were treated beyond initial radiographic progression with a median duration of 31 days (range, 8–54).

At data cut-off, most patients (94%) had discontinued treatment due to disease progression (online supplemental figure 1). One patient continued to receive treatment in a single-site rollover study until January 2025. One patient withdrew consent after nearly a year of treatment due to difficulties with travel, and one patient discontinued treatment due to participant/investigator decision with suspected disease progression. The primary reason for discontinuing the study was patient death (68%), followed by the Sponsor’s decision to end the study (19%), and five patients withdrew consent for follow-up.

Safety

The most common treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) were CRS, observed in 46% of patients, and CRS-related symptoms of fever (60%), chills (54%), headache (38%), and nausea (35%) (table 2, online supplemental table 1). The most common grade 3 and grade 4 TRAEs were neutropenia (19%), lymphopenia (15%), and transaminase increase (ALT increased 10%, AST increased 9%). 22 patients (32%) missed at least 1 weekly dose due to a TRAE (most commonly neutropenia (n=10), ALT increase, and CRS (n=3 each)). No TRAE led to treatment discontinuation or death.

CRS was grade 1 or 2 in severity (with one exception) and typically resolved within 1–2 days of onset with supportive care. The incidence of CRS and associated symptoms was generally dose-dependent below 90 µg, with few cases at doses of 0.5–4.5 µg and higher frequency at higher doses, plateauing at 90–240 µg (table 2). In the highest dose cohorts (180–240 µg), TRAEs of hypotension, vomiting, and neutropenia were also reported in over one-third of patients. The one event of Grade 3 CRS occurred at 240 µg (DLT; online supplemental table 2).

Most events of CRS occurred in the first 3–4 weeks of treatment and attenuated with repeated dosing (figure 2). The incidence and severity of CRS after week 3 was dose-dependent (online supplemental table 3), with few cases below 140 µg.

Incidence of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) over time.

Neutropenia was most frequent at 90 µg (71% all grade and 57% grade 3/4; table 2). At this dose level, neutrophil counts typically showed a transient increase 1 day after the first three doses, with a later onset of neutropenia, typically after 4–6 weeks of treatment. Neutropenia was reversible with treatment interruption and/or growth factor support. Neutropenia was not associated with fever or opportunistic infection, and patients were able to successfully resume treatment after neutrophil recovery, so neutropenia was not considered a DLT.

Transient lymphocyte count decreases, observed 1 day after the first three doses, were attributed to lymphocyte trafficking.

Seven patients had grade 3 or 4 ALT and/or AST elevations, mainly in the two highest dose cohorts (n=5, 31%), typically within 1–2 days after reaching the target dose. These increases were mostly asymptomatic and reversible, with no cases meeting potential Hy’s law criteria due to concurrent bilirubin elevation.

Four patients at three dose levels experienced AEs that met the criteria for a DLT (online supplemental table 2): grade 3 worsening pleural effusion (following an initial dose of 15 µg), grade 4 ALT/AST increased (180 µg), grade 3 AST increased, and grade 3 CRS (both at 240 µg). All DLTs resolved, and all participants subsequently continued study treatment (one with a dose reduction). Based on the overall DLT rate, the MTD was not exceeded at any dose level (step 1 dose of 15 µg: 1/7; target dose of 180 µg: 1/6; target dose of 240 µg: 2/10), though the overall safety profile at 180 and 240 µg was less favorable. Therefore, the study safety team decided not to escalate further, and a regimen of 15–45-140 µg was selected for expansion.

Pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity

Serum levels of IMC-C103C increased with dose in a nearly proportional manner over the dosing range (online supplemental figure 2) and did not accumulate further after participants were dose escalated from day 1 to day 15. For the two-step dose escalation cohorts with target doses of 64 µg to 240 µg, the half-life was 13.44 to 21.36 hours and clearance was 5.23 to 6.79 L/day.

ADAs were induced in 10 of 68 (15%) patients assessed (8/56 dose escalation; 2/12 expansion). Among these ADA-positive patients, a measurable drop in maximum plasma concentration was observed in 8 of the 12 patients after ADA onset. The impact of ADA on pharmacodynamics could not be assessed given the timing of PD assessments was before the onset of ADA.

Pharmacodynamics

Aside from one patient with the highest possible MAGE-A4 expression (H-score 300) treated at 4.5 µg, no pharmacodynamic activity was observed at target doses below 15 µg (figure 3A,B). Initial pharmacodynamic activity was observed at doses of 15 µg or higher, as evidenced by increases in the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 and transient decreases in blood lymphocytes, with stronger and more consistent cytokine induction and drops in ALCs at doses of 90 µg and higher. Pro-inflammatory cytokine levels typically peaked at 4 hours postinfusion and returned to baseline at 24 hours postinfusion.

Importantly, pharmacodynamic activity was dependent on MAGE-A4 expression; IFNγ induction occurred mainly in patients with MAGE-A4-positive tumors, who also showed a higher percentage>fourfold fold increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNγ, IL-2, and IL-6) (figure 3C). Similarly, of the nine patients assessed by IHC following the second dose (C1D8-14; 6 MAGE-A4+, 3 MAGE-A4−), all patients with a ≥1.5-fold infiltration of CD3+ T cells into tumors (n=4/9; median 2-fold) were MAGE-A4 positive (figure 3D). Likewise, all three patients who exhibited a ≥1.5-fold increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration into tumors (n=3/7; median 3-fold) were MAGE-A4 positive. T cell infiltration was seen within both the tumor mass and surrounding peritumoral stroma.

Antitumor activity

The ORR in patients with OC was 4% with two partial responses at 15 and 90 µg lasting 8.3 and 12.7 months. Both responses occurred in patients positive for MAGE-A4 tumor expression (online supplemental table 4). Eight patients achieved stable disease as their best response, while 36 patients had a best response of progressive disease. Of note, there was one patient with HNSCC with a partial response at 240 µg lasting 4.2 months in the non-OC group for an ORR of 5%. This patient also had very high MAGE-A4 tumor expression (H-score=285).

While two-thirds (65%) of patients with OC were positive for MAGE-A4 tumor expression, protein expression levels were generally low (median H-score 19). Furthermore, at the pharmacodynamically active doses of ≥90 µg, one-third of patients with IHC-evaluable tumors were MAGE-A4-negative (33%, 10/30), and those with detectable MAGE-A4 had a median H-score of 29; only two patients at these doses had high tumor MAGE-A4 expression (H-score >200; online supplemental figure 3).

At doses ≥90 µg, one-fifth (20%) of patients with MAGE-A4+ tumors demonstrated a radiographic reduction in tumor size, with three showing over 30% reduction (figure 4). Minor reductions (1%–29%) were also observed in two of five patients with unknown MAGE-A4 expression and one of 10 patients with MAGE-A4-negative tumors. A trend to longer OS was observed in patients with MAGE-A4+ tumors with a 6-month OS rate of 89% (95% CI 61% to 97%) vs 58% (95% CI 23% to 82%) in patients without detectable MAGE-A4 expression (figure 5A).

Of the 14 evaluable patients, eight (57%) had any reduction, including three who achieved a ctDNA molecular response (≥ 0.5 log (68%) reduction) (figure 5B).

Discussion

IMC-C103C is an ImmTAC bispecific T cell engager targeting tumors expressing the CTA MAGE-A4. In this phase 1/2 study primarily enrolling patients with OC, IMC-C103C was well tolerated with no treatment-related discontinuations or deaths and an overall safety profile that was consistent with other ImmTAC molecules. As observed in trials of both tebentafusp and IMCnyeso,28 29 IMC-C103C induced dose-dependent cytokine release and transient decreases in peripheral lymphocytes at doses at or above 15 µg, consistent with the mechanism of T cell activation. However, the overall magnitude of the cytokine response was less than expected based on prior studies of ImmTAC molecules, and IFNγ induction was only consistently observed at doses ≥90 µg in MAGE-A4-positive tumors, with pharmacodynamic activity linked to MAGE-A4 expression.

The safety profile in this study consisted of mostly grade 1–2 TRAEs that were clinically manageable, with no cumulative toxicity and no related event leading to discontinuation or death. The most frequent TRAEs, consistent with the mechanism of T cell engagement, were low-grade CRS and its related symptoms (eg, fever, chills, hypotension), occurred primarily following the first few doses, and attenuated with repeat dosing, and were more prevalent in the higher, pharmacodynamically active dosing cohorts. Only one grade 3 CRS event, also a DLT, occurred at the highest dose of 240 µg. Most grade 3–4 events were laboratory abnormalities, including transient lymphopenia consistent with the mechanism of T cell activation and redirection. Neutropenia, which was also observed following treatment with IMCnyeso,28 proved reversible with either treatment interruption alone or with G-CSF treatment.

Using established approaches to mitigate CRS, the present study of IMC-C103C incorporated primary corticosteroid prophylaxis at higher doses. This allowed investigation of higher target doses, differing from previous tebentafusp trials in which corticosteroid prophylaxis was not permitted.29 30 Three of the four DLTs observed occurred at the highest doses of 180 and 240 µg, where the incidence and severity of CRS after week 3 were also highest. Although an MTD was not formally identified, based on the emerging safety profile, a step dosing regimen of 15−45−140 µg was selected as the expansion dose.

The level of MAGE-A4 expression in positive tumors in this clinical study was lower than expected, with a median H score of only 16 out of a possible 300. Furthermore, at the active doses of 90 µg and above, over one-third of patients with OC were negative for MAGE-A4. Similarly, among the five patients with synovial sarcoma who received target doses of ≥45 µg, only two had a MAGE-A4 H score greater than 10. Enrollment did not require prospective confirmation of tumor MAGE-A4 expression because no threshold for MAGE-A4 expression had been established, and preclinical studies demonstrated that ImmTAC bispecifics could mediate the killing of target cancer cells with as few as 10 peptide-HLA (pHLA) complexes per cell.31 Furthermore, prior data from tebentafusp showed that gp100 expression was not associated with OS.32 Thus, it was hypothesized that tumors with little or no MAGE-A4 detectable by IHC may nonetheless have sufficient target peptide presentation to respond to IMC-C103C. However, in contrast to these assumptions, both pharmacodynamic and clinical activity in the present study were largely restricted to patients with detectable tumor MAGE-A4 expression by IHC. This discrepancy may reflect differences in antigen processing between lineage and CTAs, or a requirement for higher target density to overcome reduced T cell fitness in this heavily pre-treated population. While IHC negativity may not equal a complete absence of functional antigen, these results suggest that IHC-based enrichment may be a pragmatic strategy for future studies.

The pharmacodynamic activity of IMC-C103C depended on MAGE-A4 tumor expression, and the anti-tumor activity, including three partial responses (one HNSCC and two OC), was also limited to patients with tumors positive for MAGE-A4. Although the RECIST V.1.1 ORR was only 4% in MAGE-A4+OC, there was numerically longer OS in this patient population compared with those without MAGE-A4 expression (6-month OS rate 89% vs 58%). This is similar to observations in previous studies of tebentafusp and more recent analyses of brenetafusp, where RECIST radiographic responses underestimated the drug-induced disease control and OS.22 25 Furthermore, as observed in other ImmTAC studies, early reductions in ctDNA during treatment may be a more sensitive early marker of IMC-C103C activity than radiographic tumor size measurements.24,26 More than half of evaluable patients with MAGE-A4+ OC showed ctDNA reduction, including three patients who achieved a ctDNA molecular response (≥68% reduction) compared with only a fifth who showed a radiographic reduction in tumor size. While these findings align with the early molecular activity seen with other ImmTAC molecules, the small sample size and lack of longitudinal data preclude definitive conclusions regarding an association with OS.

The main limitations of this study were the small population size, single-arm design of this phase 1/2 study, and the low enrollment, in the active dosing cohorts, of patients with MAGE-A4 tumor expression, which appears associated with clinical benefit for this target. Given that the half-life of IMC-C103C is less than 24 hours, it remains unclear whether prolonged exposure would enhance clinical activity or if other resistance mechanisms (such as loss of antigen presentation machinery) contributed to reduced clinical efficacy in this study. Tebentafusp, which has a similar half-life, demonstrated an OS benefit in mUM despite a low objective response rate, and this benefit was not associated with gp100 expression. Furthermore, recent analyses of the tebentafusp and brenetafusp studies identified that T cell fitness, defined by a three-gene (TESPA1, CD28, GPR183) expression signature, accurately stratified clinical benefit. Although IMC-C103C-exposed patients were not included in this evaluation, it is hypothesized that poor T cell fitness may have also impacted outcomes in this study, independent of MAGE-A4 expression. Focused biomarker studies should be incorporated into future development of MAGE-A4 TCR-based clinical studies.

Conclusions

IMC-C103C at doses of ≥90 µg demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and evidence of clinical activity, with no MTD identified and an expansion dose selected (15–45-140 µg). This off-the-shelf bispecific TCR therapy was tolerated across a broad patient population, providing important insights into the application of TCR-based approaches. Nonetheless, the clinical utility of IMC-C103C may be limited due to low target antigen presentation, particularly in OC. Within the broader therapeutic landscape, a MAGE-A4-directed TCR-T cell product has received regulatory approval for synovial sarcoma, while a related ImmTAC molecule directed against the more broadly expressed CTA PRAME has shown durable responses in early-phase studies. Collectively, these findings contribute to the evolving understanding of TCR-based therapies and inform the future development of targeted immunotherapeutics.
