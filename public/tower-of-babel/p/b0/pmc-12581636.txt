Outcomes After Definitive Knee Resection Arthroplasty for Recurrent Periprosthetic Joint Infection
Authors: Kiran Kittur, Josue G. Layuno-Matos, Matthew Nester, Connor C. Diaz, Michael Miranda, Thomas Bernasek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee resection arthroplasty (KRA) is performed as definitive treatment for recurrent periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). This study aims to evaluate infection eradication and functional outcomes following KRA as a definitive treatment of PJI.MethodsWe reviewed all KRAs performed between 2002 and 2022. We identified 28 knees (27 patients) treated with definitive KRA for recurrent PJI. Primary outcomes included infection eradication, ambulatory status, and ambulatory aid use. Failure to eradicate infection was defined as any reoperation for infection. Ambulation was defined as any level of weight-bearing mobility using assistive devices, such as a walker.ResultsThe mean age at KRA was 72 years and average follow-up was 22 months. Patients had on average 3 knee operations prior to KRA. Of the 28 infections, 79% were eradicated by KRA. Of the remaining 6 persistently infected knees, 4 required an average of 4 irrigation and debridement to eventually control infection, resulting in 93% of infections eradicated overall. At latest visit, 33% of patients were on chronic antibiotic suppression. A total of 67% of patients could ambulate. Most patients (85%) required knee–ankle–foot orthoses for instability. Subsequent knee fusion was chosen by 3 patients. A patient with bilateral KRAs died within 90 days of surgery. The overall reoperation rate for KRA patients was 29%, and the overall mortality rate was 15%.ConclusionsDefinitive treatment with KRA demonstrated effectiveness in eradicating infection, although with moderate rate of reoperation. Postoperatively, most patients could ambulate with assistive devices. The KRA is an effective alternative for knee salvage when managing chronic, recalcitrant PJI.Level of evidenceIV, case series.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is one of the most common orthopaedic procedures and has a projected increase of 139% by 2040 [1]. A corresponding increase in complications, particularly periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) and increased morbidity and mortality is anticipated [2]. The current standard of care for TKA PJI includes 2-stage revision arthroplasty with a high-dose antibiotic spacer followed by subsequent reconstruction [3]. This treatment strategy is often complicated by substantial bone loss preventing implant and antibiotic spacer stability. When recurrent PJI is refractory to debridement and revision TKA, knee salvage is often indicated. Knee salvage strategies for persistent infections include knee fusion, above-knee amputation (AKA), and knee resection arthroplasty (KRA). In patients with significant bone deficiency, knee resection arthroplasty as a salvage procedure retains the patient’s leg and hopefully allows greater ambulatory function and reconstruction potential compared to an AKA [4] (Figs. 1A and B, 2A and B). Although less popular than AKA, past literature on KRA has shown relatively positive outcomes regarding infection eradication and recovery of function given its bone and limb preservation [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. However, KRA creates significant instability, and the use of bracing and ambulatory aids becomes mandatory [10]. Due to the limited number of studies evaluating definitive KRA for chronic PJI, including recent work by Goldman et al., this study aims to contribute to the growing body of literature by reporting our institution’s experience managing a similarly complex patient cohort [6]. This study aims to evaluate number of procedures performed before KRA, infection eradication, ambulation status, and functional outcomes of patients that underwent KRA for the definitive treatment of persistent PJI. We hypothesize that KRA would offer effective infection control, while also enabling modest ambulatory capacity with gait aids.Figure 1Preoperative radiographs. Material and methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to the commencement of this study. This is a retrospective study evaluating all patients treated with KRA as definitive treatment of recurrent PJI by a single surgeon (T.B.) at a single institution. The electronic medical record was queried for KRA procedures from January 2002 to June 2022 using Current Procedural Terminology code 27488. Patients were included if they were over the age of 18, had a history of failed surgical treatment of PJI following TKA, and were treated with KRA as definitive treatment. Patients selected for definitive KRA typically had multiple failed prior revision attempts (mean 3 procedures) and were deemed poor candidates for reimplantation due to bone loss, soft tissue compromise, or systemic comorbidity. All included patients were considered by the operating surgeon to have limited reconstructive options. Patients were excluded if they underwent staged resection or reimplantation. Patients undergoing alternative salvage procedures including AKA and knee fusion for chronic PJI were also excluded. Patients who later underwent elective fusion were included in the cohort, as KRA was performed with the original clinical intent of definitive treatment in these cases. Conversion to fusion occurred postoperatively based on evolving patient preference and symptom management, not as part of a preplanned staged protocol.

For patients meeting the inclusion criteria, patient demographics, comorbidities, complications, and postoperative outcomes were recorded. Primary outcomes included infection eradication, ambulatory status, visual analog scale (VAS) pain score, and gait aid use. Ambulation was defined as any level of weight-bearing mobility using assistive devices such as a walker, crutches, or cane, including patients requiring supervision or physical assistance. Patients were considered infection-free following KRA if they did not display signs of persistent infection at subsequent follow-up visits and did not require further surgical treatment for infection. The need for chronic antibiotic suppression was determined in consultation with the infectious disease service, typically based on clinical risk factors including polymicrobial infection, host immunocompromise, or virulence of organisms. Infection eradication was defined clinically by absence of wound complications, pain, drainage, and reoperation for infection.

Surgical technique

The operation involved removal of prosthetic hardware and a thorough debridement of the infected joint. A medial parapatellar arthrotomy was most commonly utilized. Initial debridement included a complete synovectomy and removal of all infected soft tissue. Tissue specimens were collected and sent for frozen and permanent section analysis and cultures (aerobes, anaerobes, fungal and tuberculosis). Following thorough irrigation and debridement, a standard layered closure was performed using dissolvable monofilament sutures. None of the patients required soft tissue flap coverage at the time of KRA. Documentation regarding preoperative draining sinuses was inconsistent; however, surgical debridement was performed in all cases to address any suspected or potential soft tissue compromise.

Postoperatively the knee was splinted in extension for 6 to 8 weeks to facilitate soft tissue healing (Figs. 3A, B and 4A, B). No specific attempts were made to mold knee alignment during this period. During this time, patients were maintained on non–weight-bearing precautions. Weight-bearing was gradually resumed after the 6 to 8 week period based on individual tolerance and clinical progress. Patients were placed on a 6 week intravenous antibiotics regimen postoperatively. Further chronic antibiotic suppression was managed by infectious disease.Figure 3Postoperative radiographs. Data analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS statistics (IBM, Armonk, NY). Data analysis included descriptive statistics reporting means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. A repeated analysis of variance test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative VAS scores.

Results

Our initial query identified 146 patients. After application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 28 knees (27 patients) treated with KRA as definitive treatment for recurrent PJI were identified and included in analysis. A patient who had bilateral KRAs died within 90 days of surgery. The 27 patients had a mean of 3 knee operations prior to definitive treatment with KRA (range, 1-8). The mean age at the time of KRA was 71.9 ± 7.9 years (range, 53-84) and the average follow-up was 21.6 ± 21.2 months (range, 1-106 months). The wide standard deviation reflects several patients with short follow-up, including one who died within 90 days postoperatively, as well as 1 patient with extended follow-up of 106 months. When excluding patients with less than 3 months of follow-up, the mean was 26.1 months with a standard deviation of 20.9 months. The mean Charlson Comorbidity Index was 4.1 ± 1.7.

Of the 28 knees with PJIs, 22 (78.6%) were infection-free following treatment with KRA alone. The 6 knees that remained infected after KRA required a mean of 4 irrigation and debridement procedures to eradicate an additional 4 infections. Of note, 1 of these 4 knees required a distal femoral resection in addition to irrigation and debridement to eventually control infection. Overall, infection was controlled in 26 (92.9%) knees after KRA. At latest follow-up, 9 patients (33.3%) were on chronic antibiotic suppression, including the remaining 2 persistently infected knees. Of these 2 remaining infections, 1 patient died within 1 year of their surgery. The other electively converted to knee fusion 7 months postoperatively due to pain.

Following resection arthroplasty, 66.7% (18 of 27) of patients were ambulatory, all of whom utilized gait assistance with either a walker, crutches, or cane. The majority of patients (85%, 23 of 27) utilized KAFOs for joint instability postoperatively. The average VAS pain score decreased significantly from 5.8 ± 3.0 preoperatively to 4.1 ± 2.8 following KRA (P = .0496). Following KRA, 2 (7.4%) patients who were infection-free electively converted to knee fusion for chronic pain and ambulatory capacity, respectively.

The overall reoperation rate was 28.6% (8 of 28 knees). The overall mortality rate was 14.8% (4 of 27), with an average time to death of 20.5 months (range, 2 to 62).

Discussion

This single institution retrospective study found that KRA can be an effective salvage for the definitive treatment of chronic recurrent PJI with good infection control and significant pain reduction. Our study population was characterized by high comorbidity and advanced age, which reflects the complexity inherent to cases typically considered for treatment with KRA. Despite these challenges, KRA alone was successful in eradicating infection in 78.6% of the 28 chronically infected knees treated. Overall, treatment with KRA controlled infection in 92.9% of knees.

While these infection control rates are encouraging, it is important to highlight the necessity of repeated debridement and chronic antibiotic suppression to control infection observed in our cohort. The 6 knees that remained infected following KRA required an average of 4 irrigation and debridement procedures to eventually control 4 additional infections. Furthermore, 9 patients (33.3%) required chronic antibiotic suppression. These results underscore the difficulty controlling infections, indicating that aggressive medical management after KRA is often necessary for optimal outcomes.

Although there remains a paucity of recent studies analyzing the effectiveness of KRA, the findings of this investigation are consistent with the sparse literature surrounding infection eradication [5,6,[8], [9], [10]]. Most recently, Goldman et al. retrospectively analyzed the outcomes of 25 knees in 23 patients treated with resection arthroplasty for definitive management of chronic PJI [6]. Comparable to our study, the authors found that 84% of knees treated were infection-free at the latest follow-up. Of these successfully treated patients, 2 remained on antibiotic suppression. In a retrospective study of resection arthroplasties performed on 10 knees in 9 patients, Mine et. al. observed a 100% rate of infection eradication without any instances of recurrence [10]. Lettin et al. similarly reported infection eradication in their series of 15 resection arthroplasties [8]. Additionally, Falahee et al. evaluated 28 resection arthroplasties performed on 26 patients and recorded an 89% infection eradication rate [5]. These studies, in concordance with our findings, support the broader application of KRA as an effective salvage option for chronic PJI.

The rates of infection eradication and outcomes observed with KRA are comparable to the more commonly performed knee salvage modalities of knee arthrodesis and AKA. In a retrospective analysis of 51 failed TKAs due to PJI treated with knee arthrodesis, Yeung et al. experienced an infection clearance rate of 94.1% [11]. In a comparably sized study evaluating 41 patients treated with knee arthrodesis for complex infected TKAs, Chalmers et al. reported infection eradication rate of 85% at 5 years [12]. Similar knee arthrodesis infection clearance rates have been reproduced in the literature [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Of note, patients treated with knee arthrodesis experienced reoperation rates similar to our experience with KRA at 32% and 50%, with revision often indicated during the early postoperative period for nonunion or delayed union [11,12]. In comparison, while AKA typically achieves near-complete infection eradication, reoperation rates are similarly high [15,16,18,19]. Schnetz et al. found that of the 47 patients treated with AKA for chronic PJI included in their study, 37% required revision surgery [15]. Despite the lower risk of persistent infection with AKA, the need for additional surgeries and morbidity of limb loss remains a significant consideration when selecting between salvage options.

Patient mobility and functional recovery are also critical considerations when evaluating knee salvage options. In our cohort, 66.7% of patients preserved some level of ambulatory function, although all required gait aids and majority were prescribed KAFOs due to expected postoperative knee instability. In comparison to KRA, studies evaluating functional outcomes of knee arthrodesis report similar ambulatory capacity [11,17]. Razii et al. reported a cohort of 12 patients where 83% were ambulatory without pain post knee arthrodesis for PJI [17]. Similarly, 41 patients (85.4%) in the cohort reported by Yeung et al. remained ambulatory after fusion [11]. Notably, 22% of these patients were ambulatory without the need for an assistive device. Ambulation outcomes after AKA are comparable to KRA; however, AKA prostheses are often not functional in debilitated elderly patients. Abouei et al. retrospectively reviewed 21 patients who underwent AKA for unresolved infected TKA and observed postoperative ambulation in 12 patients (57.1%) [18]. 17 of these patients were fitted with prostheses, of which only 5 were able to ambulate without limitation. Juryn et al. similarly observed ambulatory capacity in 12 of their 14 patients (85.7%) treated with AKA for PJI [20]. Of note, 7 of these 12 patients did not ambulate outside of home. Overall, our study’s findings suggest KRA may result in comparable functional outcomes to knee fusion and AKA. However, unlike AKA, KRA manages to preserve the limb and allows for immediate mobility and better long-term function, particularly with bracing. Additionally, unlike knee fusion, KRA maintains some degree of joint movement, which can facilitate wheelchair use and improve overall mobility.

In our cohort, KRA was associated with significant pain reduction, an important consideration when comparing resection to alternative procedures such as knee fusion and AKA. Our findings are in line with the recent study by Goldman et al., in which most patients reported no pain at all post resection aside from instability [6]. These results are comparable to reports of knee fusion, which have shown similar pain relief postoperatively [11,21]. In a systematic review of 23 studies analyzing knee arthrodesis outcomes for PJI, Mercurio et al. found a significant reduction in average VAS score postoperatively from 5.1 to 2.7 [21]. These findings have not been entirely universal; however, evident by Röhner et al.’s study of 26 knee arthrodeses, in which 73% reported persistent pain postoperatively [22]. Persistent pain has also been associated with AKA for PJI as reported by Schnetz et al.’s cohort, in which no significant reduction in pain was seen and 40.4% reported neuropathic pain post AKA [15]. Although KRA may allow for significant pain relief, it is essential to acknowledge that one patient in our cohort electively opted for knee fusion post resection due to persistent pain. Pain management post knee salvage for PJI therefore remains a challenging issue and it is difficult to draw conclusions on which modality may provide the most benefit.

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective study with a limited sample size, although the latter can be attributed to the relative infrequency KRA is performed. Additionally, while small, this study’s cohort is one of the largest of KRA reported in the literature to our knowledge. Our cohort is also limited by follow-up, which makes it difficult to assess the long-term outcomes and durability of KRA. The variability in follow-up duration may limit the generalizability of infection control outcomes, particularly in patients with short or unusually long follow-up periods. Additionally, formal patient-reported outcome measures were not consistently available due to the retrospective nature of this study, limiting our ability to assess functional outcomes from the patient perspective. Likewise, detailed gait analysis was not feasible due to inconsistent documentation across the retrospective time frame. Moreover, since all surgeries were performed by a single surgeon at a single institution, the results observed may lack generalizability to other practices or surgeons with varying expertise. This study is also limited by the lack of direct comparative groups of AKAs and knee fusions to more accurately demonstrate differences in knee salvage options.

Conclusions

KRA as definitive treatment showed effective infection eradication and should be considered as a reliable option for knee salvage in chronic, recalcitrant PJI. While infection clearance is high, aggressive antimicrobial management is often necessary for optimal outcomes. Clinical and functional results following KRA are comparable to knee arthrodesis and AKA, but KRA offers advantages in immediate postoperative mobility and long-term function. Bracing with KAFO and ambulatory aids are usually required. However, pain relief outcomes are mixed and remain challenging postoperatively. Careful consideration of patient expectations of functional ability and pain relief in combination with shared decision making is warranted when selecting among knee salvage approaches.
