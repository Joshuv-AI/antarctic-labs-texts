Exploiting DprE2 pathway redundancy to enhance potency and overcome resistance to pretomanid in Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Kamel Djaout, Aurore Dreneau, Léo Faion, Marion Prieri, Maxime Neuville, Alice Boulloy, Alexandre Biela, Rosangela Frita, Marie Devaere, Stephanie Slupek, Rudy Antoine, Ruben C Hartkoorn, Alexandre Vandeputte, Catherine Piveteau, Florence Leroux, Julie Dumont-Ryckembusch, Adrien Herledan, Adrian Pál, Monika Záhorszká, Katarína Mikušová, Jana Korduláková, Christophe Guilhot, Benoît Déprez, Marion Flipo, Alain Baulard, Nicolas Willand

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB), the deadliest infectious disease globally, still poses an enormous public health challenge exacerbated by the rise of multi-drug resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) M. tuberculosis strains. The bicyclic nitroimidazoles pretomanid (PTM) and delamanid (DLM) represent the most recent class of anti-tubercular compounds to achieve regulatory approval and clinical implementation in TB chemotherapy regimens. Both are prodrugs whose activity relies on the deazaflavin-dependent nitroreductase Ddn. High-throughput screening on a clinically relevant DdnL49P mutant reveals molecules capable of restoring PTM activity. Optimisation through rational medicinal chemistry leads to highly potent compounds capable of drastically reducing the MIC of PTM while improving its bactericidal activity. Mechanistic studies using liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), transposon sequencing and thin-layer chromatography of radiolabeled extracted lipids show that these molecules do not trigger bioactivation pathways, but instead target a compensatory pathway involving Rv2073c, an enzyme that is redundant with DprE2 for bacterial cell wall biosynthesis. The concomitant perturbation of both the DprE2- and Rv2073c-dependent steps by the PTM-NAD adduct and optimised norbornene derivatives leads to a collapse in arabinan synthesis correlating with bacterial death. This study thus highlights Rv2073c as a promising vulnerability that can be exploited to potentiate the efficacy of nitroimidazole anti-tuberculosis drugs.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), the deadliest infectious disease globally, poses an enormous public health challenge exacerbated by the rise of multi-drug resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) M. tuberculosis strains. The bicyclic nitroimidazoles pretomanid (PTM) and delamanid (DLM) represent the most recent class of anti-tubercular compounds to achieve regulatory approval and clinical implementation in TB chemotherapy regimens. The BPaL regimen (bedaquiline-pretomanid-linezolid) and its BPaLM variant (with moxifloxacin) represent a major advance against drug-resistant TB, now endorsed by WHO guidelines. Nevertheless, the emergence of resistance to one or more components of these regimens, including PTM, threatens their long-term clinical impact.

PTM and DLM are nitro-dihydroimidazo-oxazine and -oxazole derivatives, respectively, exhibiting bactericidal activity against both replicating and non-replicating Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) populations1–4. Recent years have substantially advanced our mechanistic understanding of anti-TB bicyclic nitroimidazoles mode of action. Both PTM and DLM require bioactivation by the deazaflavin-dependent nitroreductase Ddn (Rv3547). In PTM-treated M. tuberculosis, the major metabolite is the desnitro derivative, formed concurrently with the release of intrabacterial nitric oxide (NO)2 PTM bioactivation also results in the formation of a NAD-desnitro-PTM adduct, similar to those observed with DLM, ethionamide and isoniazid3,5,6. This adduct has been proposed to inhibit DprE2 in M. bovis BCG, which, together with DprE1, catalyzes the synthesis of decaprenyl-phosporyl-D-arabinose, an essential precursor for the arabinogalactan layer of the mycobacterial cell wall7. The concomitant release of nitric oxide, combined with cell-wall synthesis inhibition, may therefore account for the dual bactericidal mechanism of PTM8.

Resistance to PTM and DLM has been reported across in vitro, animal models and clinical settings, prominently attributed to mutations disrupting the F420-dependent bioactivation pathway. Mutations in ddn reduce or abolish the nitroreductase activity essential for prodrug activation, while mutations in the F420 biosynthetic genes (fbiA, fbiB, fbiC, fbiD) impair cofactor availability by disrupting the sequential enzymatic steps of deazaflavin synthesis. Additionally, mutations in fgd1 compromise the regeneration of the reduced F420H2 cofactor from its oxidized form3,9–12. Naturally occurring ddn single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) have been widely reported, particularly, though not exclusively, in lineage 2 (Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex) strains. Clinical evidence further demonstrates that loss of ddn function incurs no significant fitness cost13,14. These findings emphasize the critical need to monitor and mitigate resistance thereby preserving the long-term efficacy of PTM and DLM in clinical practice.

Adjuvant therapies are increasingly emerging as promising strategies to combat antimicrobial resistance. Historically, β-lactamase inhibitors have effectively restored the activity of penicillin and cephalosporin antibiotics by inactivating β-lactamase enzymes15. In tuberculosis, Alpibectir and C10 have recently been shown to revert ethionamide and isoniazid resistance, respectively16. Alpibectir, a small molecule modulator of the transcriptional regulator VirS, has been developed to awaken a cryptic bioactivation pathway of ethionamide and boost its potency. This innovative approach offers a compelling strategy to overcome bacterial resistance mechanisms and holds clear clinical potential17–20. The combination of ethionamide and alpibectir is currently under evaluation in a Phase 2a clinical trial (NCT06748937).

The present work was initiated as an extension of this paradigm. Actinobacteria carry a formidable arsenal of nitro-reductases21 and it is possible that a subset could replace Ddn for the bioactivation of PTM and DLM. We reasoned that if it were possible to solicit such an enzyme by acting at the transcriptional level for example, then we could revert PTM and DLM resistance as we have done for ethionamide. We have initiated a systematic screening campaign to identify small molecules capable of circumventing PTM resistance. Herein, we report the discovery and characterization of a novel series of norbornene derivatives that demonstrate dual functionality: (i) partial restoration of pretomanid susceptibility in Ddn-deficient Mtb strains, where the F420H2-dependent nitroreductase is non-functional, and (ii) potentiation of pretomanid efficacy against wild-type Mtb strains. These compounds represent promising chemosensitizers that may overcome the primary resistance mechanism mediated by the inability of ddn mutants to bioactivate nitroimidazoles. Concurrently, this study sheds new light on the incompletely characterized mechanistic pathways underlying PTM and DLM activity.

Results

Rationale and experimental design for identifying PTM resistance modulators

To identify molecules capable of restoring PTM sensitivity in resistant Mtb strains, we designed a screening strategy targeting the primary resistance mechanism: loss of Ddn-dependent bioactivation. This approach required first generating PTM-resistant mutants harbouring clinically relevant mutations in ddn, followed by screening for compounds that could bypass or complement the defective nitroreductase activity.

Generation and characterization of PTM-resistant M. tuberculosis mutants

Spontaneous PTM-resistant mutants were selected by plating up to 108 H37Rv-gfp bacilli on solid media containing 2 mg.L−1 of PTM, which corresponds to 20-fold the MIC. Resistant colonies appeared at the expected frequency of 10−610. Whole-genome sequencing of a selection of individual clones revealed mutations in key components of the PTM bioactivation pathway: the nitroreductase ddn gene itself and a series of genes essential for the biosynthesis of the deazaflavin cofactor F420. Two representative mutants were selected for detailed characterization: H37Rv_DdnL49P and H37Rv_fbiC_fs* which carries a 4-nucleotide deletion (1427tccg), resulting in I476T, followed by a frameshift and stop codon. Liquid culture MIC determination revealed differential resistance levels: DdnL49P conferred a 300-fold increase in MIC (30 mg.L−1), while the fbiC frameshift resulted in complete resistance (MIC > 50 mg.L−1) (Fig. 1).

Growth was calculated as percentage of GFP fluorescence. Dose–response curves were drawn using the mean ± standard deviation of three biological replicates.

The DdnL49P variant was selected as our primary screening strain because this naturally occurring polymorphism is frequent in Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex lineage 2 strains and is associated with efficient transmission without fitness costs, making it a clinically relevant target13,14.

High-throughput screening identified norbornene derivatives that overcome PTM resistance

We screened 25,600 compounds from our chemical library against the H37Rv_DdnL49P strain using a dual-readout approach: each compound was tested alone (30 µM) and in combination with a sub-inhibitory concentration of PTM (5 mg.L−1) (Fig. 2A). This design enabled us to specifically identify compounds that lack intrinsic antimycobacterial activity but restore PTM efficacy against the DdnL49P mutant.

Analysis of the screening data revealed a distinct activity profile when plotting individual versus combination effects. The majority of compounds clustered along the diagonal, indicating no interaction with PTM (Fig. 2A).

However, a distinct subset emerged that displayed minimal activity alone yet substantially inhibited growth of the PTM-resistant DdnL49P mutant when combined with 5 mg.L−1 of PTM.

Hit compounds were selected based on stringent criteria: (i) no or limited growth inhibition when the compound was used alone (GIalone < 20%), (ii) robust activity when the compound was combined with PTM (GIcombined > 30% and GIcombined > GIalone + 30%). The 141 initial hits meeting these criteria (highlighted in blue in Fig. 2A) were retested in dose response in the presence and absence of PTM. Compounds were ranked by determining their bC50 (DdnLP)—the concentration enabling 5 mg.L−1 of PTM to achieve 50% growth inhibition of the DdnL49P mutant. Nineteen compounds, whose structures were confirmed by LCMS, showed bC50 (DdnLP) below 40 µM and were selected for further characterization. Remarkably, structural analysis revealed that six of the validated hits contained a norbornene core motif (red points in Fig. 2A; structures in Fig. 2C, including one structurally related compound), with BDM5700 emerging as the most potent derivative (bC50(DdnLP) = 1.3 µM) (Fig. 2B). This observation prompted us to initiate structure-activity relationship studies to define the pharmacophore requirements for partially restoring PTM activity, as well as mechanistic studies aimed at understanding their mode of action.

Structure-activity relationships of norbornene derivatives

To elucidate the minimal pharmacophore required for the partial restoration of PTM activity against the DdnL49P mutant, we synthesized a focused library of BDM5700 analogues. Each compound was evaluated for its ability to potentiate a sub-inhibitory PTM concentration (5 mg.L−1) against the resistant DdnL49P mutant.

The methoxy analogue (BDM78040) proved equipotent to BDM5700 against the resistant mutant with a bC50 (DdnLP) = 1.3 µM. Intriguingly, BDM78040 not only partially restored PTM susceptibility in the resistant DdnL49P mutant but also enhanced PTM potency against wild-type H37Rv with intact Ddn function, reducing the MIC by 9-fold (Fig. 3).

To assess the PTM-potentiating activity on H37Rv, all compounds were also evaluated against the wild-type strain with 20 µg.L−1 of PTM. BDM78040 boosted PTM activity against wild-type bacteria in the same range of concentrations as seen against the ddn-mutated H37Rv strain (bC50 (wt) = 2.1 µM) (Table 1). The endo isomer (BDM78039), bearing a methoxy group in position 6, was less potent against both the wild-type strain (bC50(wt) = 2.5 µM) and the DdnL49P mutant (bC50 (DdnLP) = 7.9 µM). Replacement of the methoxy group in position 6 of the benzothiazole ring with other substituents showed that an electron donating group like CH3 (BDM77278, bC50(wt) = 1.0 µM) was better tolerated than electron-withdrawing groups like a chlorine atom (BDM77280, bC50(wt) = 8.9 µM). BDM77278 was confirmed to allow PTM at 5 mg.L−1 to inhibit the growth of the DdnL49P mutant at a bC50 (DdnLP) of 0.7 µM. Removal of the methoxy group (BDM77281) decreased potency, particularly against the DdnL49P mutant. Finally, the isomers in the exo series (BDM78097), and endo/exo isomers in the succinimide series (BDM78058 and BDM78096) were less potent against both strains.

Selection of resistant mutants to the PTM-BDM77278 combination

To gain insight into the mechanism of action of norbornene-PTM combination, spontaneously resistant clones were selected under dual exposure to PTM (5 mg.L−1) and BDM77278 (10 µM) (Table 4). Whole-genome sequencing of resistant clones revealed mutations in genes of the F420 biosynthetic pathway, including fbiB, fbiC, and fbiD. Critically, the PTM-BDM77278 combination was completely inactive against an fbiC frameshift mutant (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that the F420-dependent metabolism is essential for the activity of the combination.

Norbornene derivatives do not improve the bioactivation of PTM

PTM bioactivation by the F420-dependent nitroreductase Ddn generates several stable, metabolites, including a desnitro derivative, as well as reactive nitrogen species such as nitric oxide. To determine whether the BDM77278-mediated boosting activity involves an increase of the metabolization of PTM, we quantified PTM-derived metabolites by LC-MS/MS in whole Mtb cells. In our analysis, we monitored the two major metabolites of PTM, des-nitro-PTM (m/z 315.25) and the oxazin-2-amine derivative (m/z 291.23)22, in both the supernatant and pellet fractions of M. tuberculosis H37Rv, H37Rv DdnL49P, H37Rv fbiC-STOP, CDC1551, and CDC1551_ddn::tn (MT3651) strains (the latter included as a control to rule out residual Ddn activity in the mutant DdnL49P) (Fig. 4). These measurements were performed in a kinetic framework to capture metabolite turnover. In parallel, we assessed the abundance of reactive nitrogen species by quantifying the azo dye generated in the Griess reaction from culture supernatants of the same strains treated with either PTM alone or PTM combined with BDM77278. Across all strains and conditions, neither the kinetics of metabolite formation nor the levels of nitric oxide production were different between these two conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). The rate of PTM disappearance upon activation was also equivalent.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that PTM potentiation by norbornene compounds does not involve induction of an alternative F420-dependent bioactivation pathway.

In vitro and ex vivo activity of PTM-BDM88927 combination

In a checkerboard assay, we measured PTM MIC in the presence of increasing concentrations of the optimized norbornene BDM88927 against both H37Rv_DdnL49P and the wild-type strain. In the DdnL49P mutant background, BDM88927 shifted the PTM dose–response curve in a concentration-dependent manner, lowering the MIC of PTM by up to 10-fold (from 30 mg/L−1 to 3 mg/L−1) (Fig. 5A, B). A similar effect was observed in the wild-type strain where a 25-fold increase in PTM potency resulted in a reduced MIC of 5–10 µg.L−1 (Fig. 5C, D).

Consistent with these results, killing assays revealed the bactericidal impact of the combination. Against wild-type H37Rv, 1000 µg.L−1 of PTM treatment (10× MIC) resulted in 1 log decrease in CFU over 4 days of treatment. This activity was statistically equivalent to that of 5 µg.L−1 of PTM in combination with 10 µM of BDM88927 (Fig. 6A). In combination with BDM88927, as little as 10 and 50 µg.L−1 of PTM (1/10× and ½× MIC, respectively) were able to clear over 2-log CFU in the same time frame (Fig. 6A). As a comparison, 50 µg.L−1 of PTM (½× MIC) was fully inactive on wild-type H37Rv (Fig. 6A).

Similarly, PTM alone at 10 mg.L−1 (one-third of the MIC) was fully inactive against H37Rv_DdnL49P, with CFU counts comparable to the DMSO control after 4 days of treatment (Fig. 6B). In contrast, the combination of PTM at 10 mg.L−1 with BDM88927 resulted in a 2–3 log10 reduction in CFU counts over the same period, demonstrating that BDM88927 restores significant bactericidal activity to an otherwise ineffective PTM concentration. Notably, even at 2 mg.L−1 of PTM (1/15× MIC), addition of BDM88927 achieved a bacteriostatic effect, as bacterial counts did not differ significantly from the day 0 inoculum (Fig. 6B).

Efficacy of the combination PTM-BDM88927 was also evaluated against intracellular Mtb. Survival of THP-1 monocyte derived macrophages was measured after 6 days of infection with wild-type H37Rv at a multiplicity of infection of 10:1 (bacteria:cells) and the potency of PTM to protect the cells was measured in the presence of increasing concentrations of BDM88927 (Fig. 7). Although the PTM MIC could be decreased 25-fold, as observed for axenic cultures, the dose of booster required to achieve this effect was increased 30-fold, suggesting possible cellular penetration or stability issues of BDM88927 (Fig. 7).

Target identification via transposon sequencing (Tn-Seq)

Since the norbornenes display no antibacterial activity on their own, we hypothesized that their target is non-essential in the absence of PTM, and that mutants in the corresponding gene could therefore be represented in a transposon library. Transposon-sequencing was performed with a sub-inhibitory concentration of PTM (50 µg.L−1) in the presence and absence of BDM88927 (10 µM). High-dose PTM (1 mg.L−1) and the booster alone were included as controls (Supplementary File 1). The drift in the transposon library was assessed from the onset of cultivation to the conclusion of the experiment, and the few mutants that were differentially represented were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3). When treated with BDM88927 alone, no difference in the relative abundance of mutants was observed, in line with the lack of activity of the booster in the absence of PTM (Supplementary Fig. 3).

High-dose PTM treatment mainly selected mutants in the genes involved in PTM bioactivation (ddn, fbiA, fbiB, fbiC, fgd1, but not fbiD for which no insertion mutant is present in our library). Notably, under both high and low dose PTM treatment, two mutants exhibited hypersensitivity to the drug: cinA and rv2073c (Fig. 8). CinA has been recently described for its pyrophosphatase activity5. CinA may cleave the NAD moiety from all anti-tuberculous prodrugs that are active through NAD-drug adducts including PTM, and this results in drug tolerance. In the presence of PTM and BDM88927, cinA mutants were slightly overrepresented (3-fold increase in relative abundance) suggesting that loss of function in cinA could result in tolerance or resistance to the combination. Retesting of a cinA mutant and its complemented strain in isogenic backgrounds using checkerboard assays confirmed the previously reported hypersensitivity to PTM. The growth of the cinA-deleted strain was slowed when treated with the PTM-BDM88927 combination, but not inhibited, as suggested by the Transposon-sequencing results (Supplementary Fig. 4).

rv2073c mutants were remarkably under-represented in the presence of sub-inhibitory concentration of PTM (> 100-fold decrease in relative abundance). However, mutants in rv2073c were not differentially represented when the Tn library was treated with the BDM88927-PTM combination. Taken together, our data suggest that Rv2073c loss of function results in hypersensitivity to PTM similar to the boosting effect of BDM88927.

To investigate the role of Rv2073c in the mechanism of action of BDM88927, the gene was deleted using recombineering23 and replaced with a hygromycin resistance cassette. The rv2073c knockout strain showed marked hypersensitivity to PTM, as recently shown by Mei-Yi Yan and co-workers24, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 5 µg.L−1, complete loss of sensitivity to BDM88927, and rapid killing at low PTM concentrations (Fig. 9). Ectopic overexpression of rv2073c on pMV261 plasmid was used to complement the deletion of rv2073c. Complementation decreased PTM sensitivity, increasing the MIC to 19.5 µg.L−1. This partial complementation is possibly due to non-physiological expression levels of rv2073c, but nevertheless excludes any polar effects in the phenotype observed with the rv2073c deleted strain. Moreover, when the rv2073c mutation was complemented, BDM88927 could potentiate PTM potency, and PTM-induced rapid killing could only be observed when BDM88927 was used at very high concentrations (20 µM), equivalent to the observed killing with 0.6 µM of BDM88927 on wild-type H37Rv (Fig. 9).

Rv2073c is a short-chain dehydrogenase of unknown function, but it shows sequence and structural similarity to DprE225. DprE1 and DprE2 are responsible for the epimerization of decaprenylphosphoryl-D-ribose (DPR) to decaprenylphosphoryl-D-arabinose (DPA), the sole arabinose donor in mycobacteria. Inhibition of DprE1 by PBTZ169 results in the absence of the arabinan acceptor required for mycolic acid attachment, causing mycolic acids to accumulate in the form of glycolipids such as trehalose monomycolates (TMM) and trehalose dimycolates (TDM)26. Therefore, we investigated the effect of PTM-BDM88927 on the mycobacterial cell wall glycolipid biosynthesis.

Treatment of wild-type H37Rv or H37Rv_DdnL49P with PTM alone did not result in accumulation of TMM and TDM as would be expected for a DprE2 targeting drug. However, combination of PTM and BDM88927 resulted in strong accumulation of these glycolipids (Fig. 10), and this effect correlated accurately with the active concentrations of boosted PTM. This suggested that in the absence of BDM88927, PTM inhibition of DprE2 is not sufficient to result in the accumulation of these species as observed for inhibition of DprE1, and that some DPA could probably still be produced. This observation supports the idea that Rv2073c may act as an alternative to DprE2 under certain conditions. However, its non-essential nature suggests it can only partially compensate for DprE2 function. This partial redundancy becomes apparent when DprE2 is inhibited by PTM, under which condition Rv2073c appears to become conditionally essential. Notably, rv2073c is deleted in M. africanum (lineage 5 and 6 of the MTBC) and M. bovis strains such as BCG 1173P2, as part of RD927. BCG 1173P2 is coherently insensitive to BDM88927, further supporting its role in the activity of the PTM-norbornene combination (Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, PTM treatment in BCG results in accumulation of TMM and TDM in the absence of BDM88927 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Contrary to what was shown in BCG7, overexpression of DprE2 does not result in resistance to PTM in Mtb (Supplementary Fig. 7) but instead abolishes the boosting effect of BDM88927.

Discussion

The introduction of the BPaL/M all oral regimen represents a clinical turning point for the treatment of drug-resistant TB cases. However, the durability of this regimen relies on monitoring the emergence of resistance to these drugs and implementation of programmatic ways to address such cases. Of particular concern, bicyclic nitroimidazoles exhibit a frequency of resistance similar to that of INH, in the range of 10−6 in vitro. Resistance to PTM and DLM occurs via loss of Ddn bioactivating activity, or loss of the reduced F420H2 cofactor. Because of their recent introduction, data availability about resistance in the field is still scarce and current data suggest that 66% of resistant strains carry mutations in ddn that either abolish its promiscuous bioactivating activity, often maintaining its innate menaquinone reducing catalytic capability, or result in loss of functional Ddn altogether. The ddnL49P allele, a naturally occurring sequence polymorphism in Beijing strains (Lineage 2) represents alone 13% of DLM resistant strains in the Cryptic consortium dataset released in 2021. Strains carrying this mutation carry no fitness cost in their ability to infect and disseminate in humans as evidenced by outbreaks in Vietnam, the Netherlands and Mexico13.

Our original screening approach has led to the identification of potent norbornene derivatives able to improve PTM activity against the clinically relevant DdnL49P mutant, while also potentiating its activity in wild-type Mtb. Among the optimized analogues, BDM88927 emerged as one of the most potent compounds, displaying robust potentiation in vitro, bactericidal synergy in killing assays, and significant boosting of PTM activity in infected macrophages. Mechanistic investigations revealed that norbornenes act independently of alternative nitro-reductase induction and instead exploit the conditional essentiality of Rv2073c, a short-chain dehydrogenase with probable functional overlap with DprE2 in decaprenyl-phosphoryl-arabinose synthesis. Notably, Rv2073c was identified in a recent CRISPRi/CRISPR-KO chemical genetic screen as a gene whose deletion increased susceptibility to PTM24. The authors suggest that Rv2073c allows Mtb to resist killing by PTM.

Norbornenes, when combined with PTM, resulted in the accumulation of cell wall precursors, providing a mechanistic explanation for the observed synergy. The limited bactericidal activity of PTM alone further supports this model. Inhibition of DprE2 by the NAD-PTM adduct reduces flux through the DPA synthesis pathway, but residual activity can be maintained by Rv2073c, allowing cell wall biosynthesis to proceed at a sufficient level to prevent glycolipid accumulation and promotes cell survival. Our data suggest that norbornenes block this compensatory route, collapsing flux through the pathway and thereby converting PTM inhibition into a bactericidal outcome. Overexpression of DprE2 likely restores flux capacity which explains the loss of boosting under these conditions.

As suggested by Tn-sequencing results, loss of cinA results in resistance to BDM88927, a yet unexplained observation in the mechanism of action of the BDM88927-PTM combination. CinA catalyzes the conversion of the PTM-NAD adduct into a ribose-nicotinamide-PTM derivative. Our results suggest that it is likely this processed adduct that engages most efficiently with the booster. Furthermore, the natural deletion of rv2073c in M. africanum (lineage 5 and 6) may contribute to their reported hypersensitivity to PTM, an observation that aligns with the idea that absence of this compensatory enzyme removes the tolerance mechanism and thereby enhances nitroimidazole killing28.

Together, these findings support a model in which PTM generates a NAD adduct that is further processed by CinA into a ribose-nicotinamide-PTM derivative; norbornenes synergize with this species by disrupting the Rv2073c-mediated compensatory flux in DPA synthesis, leading to accumulation of cell wall glycolipids, which correlates with increased PTM potency.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations constrain the applicability of this approach. First, the current norbornene derivatives suffer from limited cellular penetration or stability, as evidenced by the higher concentrations required in macrophages compared to axenic cultures. Second, while the boosting effect restores PTM activity in ddn mutants, it is ineffective against strains carrying mutations in F420 biosynthesis or recycling genes, which represent a substantial fraction of clinically observed resistance. Third, the molecular interaction between norbornene compounds and Rv2073c remains undefined, and further mechanistic and biochemical studies will be required to fully elucidate their mode of action.

Future work should therefore aim to optimize the physicochemical properties of the norbornene scaffold to improve solubility and intracellular activity, while clarifying the structural basis of Rv2073c inhibition. Establishing the in vivo efficacy of these combinations in relevant infection models will be critical to evaluate their therapeutic potential. In parallel, mapping the prevalence and diversity of rv2073c polymorphisms across clinical isolates will help assess the breadth of applicability of this strategy.

Taken together, this work demonstrates the feasibility of chemical boosting as a strategy to enhance the efficacy of bicyclic nitroimidazoles and to overcome specific resistance mechanisms and opens new avenues for extending the therapeutic lifespan of this important drug class.

Methods

Bacterial strains

All strains were cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 medium (BD, Difco) supplemented with Tween-80 (0.05%), glycerol (0.2%) and OADC (BD Difco). Except for deletion/complementation experiments, H37Rv wild type carried an integrative plasmid expressing GFP to ease the assessment of growth, and was cultures in the presence of 50 µg/mL of Hygromycin B (Euromedex). All agar plates were performed with Middlebrook 7H11 medium supplemented with glycerol (0.5%) and OADC (BD Difco). E. coli DH5α was cultures in standard LB medium in the presence of ampicillin 150 µg/mL or kanamycin 50 µg/mL.

High throughput screening

Compound library (150 nl) were transferred to black Greiner 384-well clear bottom polystyrene plates (Greiner, no. 781091) using an Echo 550 Liquid Handler (Labcyte) and each plate was prepared in duplicate. DMSO (Merck D8418) and rifampicin (0.1 mg.L−1) were included in each assay plate as controls. Exponentially growing H37Rv_DdnL49P_GFP was diluted to 2 × 106 CFU/ml. PTM (Cliniscience A11401-10) at a final concentration of 5 mg.L−1 or 0.05% DMSO was added to the bacterial suspension and each were distributed to one of duplicate compound plates using a ViaFill Dispenser (Integra), 50 µl per well. The final DMSO concentration was 0.35% for all wells. The plates were sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 5 days after which GFP fluorescence was measured on Victor Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer) at Ex/Em = 485/535 nm. Z’ factor was calculated for each plate and plates were validated if the Z factor was higher than 0.4. Growth inhibition was normalized to the positive (100%) and negative (0%) controls in each plate.

Compound handling and dispensing

Compounds (10 mM in DMSO) were transferred to a 384-well low-dead-volume polypropylene plate (LP-0200, labcyte), which was used to prepare assay plates. Concentrations ranged from 50 µM to 20 nM distributed among ten 3-fold serial dilution points and were distributed to black Greiner 384-well clear bottom polystyrene plates (Greiner, no. 781091) using an Echo 550 liquid Handler (Labcyte). The DMSO volume was adjusted so that the final concentration of DMSO in all wells was 0.7%, accounting for added PTM volume.

MIC measurement

Mycobacterial strains were grown to mid-log phase in 7H9 (BD) medium supplemented with 0.05% tween-80 (Sigma T8761), 0.2% glycerol (Invitrogen 50405) and 10% OADC (BD, Difco) until mid-log phase (OD 0.4–0.6). On the day of the experiment, the bacterial cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.02 and 50 µL of the bacterial suspension was added to each assay well. Assay plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 5 days. The fluorescence intensity of the GFP was measured using a Victor Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer) at Ex/Em = 485/535 nm or EnSight Multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer) at Ex/Em = 488/510 nm or OD600 as a surrogate for growth. The growth was normalized to DMSO (100%) and rifampicin 0.1 mg.L−1 (0%) as negative and positive controls, respectively. Data points were then analysed using GraphPad Prism to calculate MIC values by fitting them to the equation [Y = Bottom + (Top-Bottom)/(1 + (X^HillSlope)/(IC50^HillSlope)].

For the Δrv20773c mutant and its complemented strain, 384-well plates were inoculated at an initial density of approximately 104 CFU/ml and incubated 14 days before growth measurement by OD600. The complemented strain exhibited slower growth in the presence of sub-inhibitory PTM concentrations, requiring an extended incubation time to be detectable by OD600 readout. This lower inoculum and extended incubation period were chosen to minimize the probability of spontaneous resistance emergence to PTM during treatment.

Metabolization

M. tuberculosis was cultivated in 7H9 (BD) supplemented with 0.2% glycerol, 0.05% tween 80 and 0.2% dextrose to avoid slow albumin precipitation in acetonitrile. Bacterial cultures were treated in round flasks (Thermo Scientific 4115-0.125) with PTM and booster. DMSO was adjusted to 0.2% in all flasks. At each time point, 5 ml of bacterial culture was centrifuged at 3000 × g, 5 min at room temperature. 500 µl of supernatant was mixed with 500 µl of acetonitrile (Sigma, 34851). Pellets were resuspended in 500 µl acetonitrile similarly, and transferred to Lysing matrix B tubes (MP biomedicals). All samples were heat inactivated (20 min at 80 °C). Pellet samples were broken on Fastprep 24 (MP Biomedicals) at 6.5 m/s, 40 s twice with 5 min on ice between runs. All samples were further centrifuged 10 min at 11,000 × g at 4 °C and the supernatant stored at −20 °C until LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS

Samples were analysed on a UPLC system Acquity I-Class (Waters) coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer Xevo TQD (Waters), under MRM detection. The column, a Waters Acquity BEH C18 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm – Waters), was placed at 40 °C, the flow rate was 600 µL/min and the injection volume 1 μL; the mobile phase was H2O 5 mM Ammonium formate pH 3.8 (A) and acetonitrile, 5 mM ammonium formate pH 3.8, 5% aqueous (B). The gradient was initiated at 2% B, maintained for 10 s then increased linearly to 98% B in 1′50 and maintained at 98% B for 30 s before returning to initial conditions. The MRM parameters (capillary voltage, cone voltage and collision energy) were optimized for each monitored compound.

Selection of spontaneous resistance mutants

The M. tuberculosis H37Rv_DdnL49P strain was grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 0.05% Tween-80, 0.2% glycerol and OADC (BD Difco) until reaching an OD600 of 1. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (5 min, room temperature), resuspended in fresh medium to concentrate the culture, and plated on 7H11 agar supplemented with glycerol, OADC, PTM 5 µg/mL and BDM77278 10 µM at inocula of approximately 107 and 108 cells per plate. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4 weeks until colonies appeared. Individual colonies were restreaked on identical selective medium and incubated for 3 weeks to exclude mixed populations. Isolated were grown in liquid medium, and resistance was confirmed by determination of minimum inhibitory concentration.

For genomic DNA extraction, 10 mL of mid-log phase culture were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in 500 µL of nuclease-free water, and mixed with 500 µL of phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, Invitrogen). The suspension was transferred to a Lysing Matrix B tube and disrupted using a FastPrep FP120 cell disruptor (Thermo) at 6.5 m/s for 40 s. Samples were incubated 5 min at room temperature and centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 × g at 4 °C. Approximately 400 µL of the aqueous phase were transferred to a new tube, mixed wth 40 µL of 4 M sodium acetate and 800 µL of 100% ethanol, and incubated overnight at −20 °C. DNA was pelleted by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C), washed twice with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 50 µL of nuclease-free water. Genomic DNA concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and samples were submitted to Genoscreen (Lille, France) for library preparation and whole-genome sequencing.

Whole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq®2500 platform, generating 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw genomics reads were subjected to quality filtering using Prinseq29 on the basis of the criterion of a minimum read length of 50 and a sequencing error rate lower than 0.1% (Q30). Reads were aligned to the M. tuberculosis H37Rv reference genome using the BWA-mem algorithm30. SNPS were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller with default settings31. SnpEff was used to annotate the variants32. Reported variants were visually inspected using QIAGEN CLC Genomics Workbench 23.0.

Killing assays

Bacterial cultures were grown to mid-log phase and diluted to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.05, corresponding to approximately 5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL. Cultures (10 mL) were incubated in T25 flasks at 37 °C with the indicated compound concentrations. The final DMSO concentration was maintained at 0.2% in all conditions. After 4 days of incubation, bacterial suspensions were serially diluted, and 10 µL of each dilution were plated onto Middlebrook 7H11 agar plates. Plates were incubated for three weeks at 37 °C, after which colonies were enumerated to determine the remaining viable bacterial counts.

THP-1 survival assays

THP-1 cells were cultures at a density of 2 × 105–106 cells/mL in RPMI medium (ThermoFisher Scientific, 61870036) supplemented with 10% Fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco 10500) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Three days before infection, THP-1 cells were differentiated with 40 ng/mL of phorbol 12-myristate-13-acteate (Sigma P1585). On the day of infection, an exponentially growing H37Rv culture was washed and resuspended in RPMI-FBS. Clumps were removed passing through a 0.8 mm diameter syringe 5 times followed by centrifugation at 100 × g, and bacterial density was estimated by OD600. Bacterial cells were added to the cell monolayer at multiplicity of infection of 10 bacteria per cells and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cell monolayer was washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular bacteria, and incubated for 1 h in RPMI-FBS in the presence of 50 µg/mL of amikacin. After washing, the cells were incubated with Versene (Gibco 15040-066) for 20 min. Cells were then detached using a cell scraper, centrifuged at 100 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in RPMI-FBS. 96-well plates carrying the compounds were distributed with 100 µL of the cell suspension at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 6 days. After incubation, resazurin was diluted in RPMI-FBS at a concentration of 0.05% and 10 µl were added to each well before plates were placed back to incubation for an additional day. On day 7, THP-1 survival was measured by resorufin fluorescence on a Victor Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer) at Ex/Em = 530/580 nm.

Tn-sequencing

A pooled transposon mutant library of Mtb H37Rv, constructed by transduction with the MycoMar T7 phage33,34 and containing more than 20,000 mutants, was cultured in 25 mL of 7H9 medium supplemented with Tween 80 0.05%, ADC 10% (BD Difco) and kanamycin (40 µg/mL) for 6 days at 37 °C. The culture was centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min) and the pellet was resuspended in 125 mL of fresh medium without kanamycin and cultured for 2 days at 37 °C, until an OD600nm of approximately 0.3 was reached. Part of the culture was used to inoculate 25 flasks at an OD600nm of 0.025 in 25 mL of medium. Another part was centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min) and the pellet was stored at −20 °C before DNA extraction. Flasks were separated into 5 groups of 5, and each group was treated with either pretomanid at 50 ng/ml or 1 µg/mL, BDM88927 at 10 µM, or BDM88927 at 10 µM and pretomanid at 50 ng/ml, or left untreated. Three of the non-treated cultures were also plated on 7H11 medium supplemented with OADC 10% (BD Difco), in order to determine the killing rate during the treatment. After 3 days of treatment at 37 °C, the cultures treated with pretomanid 50 ng/ml, BDM88927 alone and neither were diluted 1/100 in 25 mL of fresh medium, and then incubated 7 days at 37 °C. Since there was more killing in the cultures treated with pretomanid 50 ng/ml and BDM88927 10 µM, these cultures were not diluted, but the pellet was washed with PBS 1X supplemented with Tween 80 0.05%, and resuspended in 25 mL of fresh medium for a regrowth step of 7 days at 37 °C. For each condition, 2 cultures were also plated on 7H11 medium with OADC 10% after the 3 days of treatment, to estimate the killing rate. At the end of the regrowth phase, while the OD600nm of the cultures were between 0.3 and 1, they were centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min) and the pellets were stored at −20 °C until DNA extraction.

Genomic DNA was extracted (Quick-DNATM Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit, Zymo Research), and transposon-genome junctions were amplified by PCR as previously described33. An index specific to each sample was added during this PCR, allowing all samples to be pooled to create the Tn-seq library (TruSeqMC DNA Nano). This library was sequenced by Illumina paired-end sequencing to determine the composition of the transposon mutant library in each sample. Resulting reads were mapped onto the Mtb H37Rv reference genome. For each sample, a table was generated containing the number of read for each TA insertion site33. Data were processed using the TRANSIT pipeline35.

Deletion and complementation of Rv2073c

The rv2073c gene was deleted from M. tuberculosis H37Rv using recombineering. Briefly, DNA fragments corresponding to the upstream and downstream flanking regions of rv2073c were amplified by PCR using primers listed in Supplementary Table 1. The upstream fragment was digested with NdeI and HindIII, and the downstream fragment with XbaI and PstI, before sequential cloning into pUC-Hygro vector using Quick Ligation kit (New England Biolabs) and chemically competent E. coli DH5α, yielding pUC-Hygro-Rv2073c. For allelic exchange, pUC-Hygro-Rv2073c was digested with XbaI and NdeI, and the resulting fragments were purified using PCR cleanup kit (Macherey-Nagel) and electroporated into H37Rv carrying pJV53 plasmid. Transformants were selected on 7H11 agar plates containing 50 µg/ml hygromycin B (Euromedex) and incubated for three weeks.

To obtain a strain curated of pJV53, recombinant clones were cultured for 7 days without kanamycin (25 µg/ml) and then plated on 7H11 agar. Colonies were repicked on 7H11 with and without kanamycin to identify clones that had lost the recombineering plasmid. For complementation, the rv2073c coding sequence was amplified using primers listed in Supplementary Table 1, digested with HindIII and DraI, and ligated into pMV261 previously digested with HindIII and MscI, generating pMV261-Rv2073c. The construct was validated by restriction digestion and Sanger sequencing before transformation into Δrv2073c strain.

Lipid labelling and TLC

M. tuberculosis H37Rv and DdnL49P strains were grown in 7H9 medium supplemented with 10% ADC and 0.05% Tween 80, shaking at 120 RPM, until they reached an O.D.600 of 0.2–0.5. Culture aliquots (96 μL) were transferred to Eppendorf tubes containing different concentrations of PTM alone or in combination with 10 μM BDM88927 (2 μL each, added from DMSO stock solutions). After 48 h of static incubation at 37 °C, 14C-acetate (ARC; specific activity 110 mCi/mmol) at final concentration 0.5 µCi/mL was added, and the cultures were incubated statically at 37 °C for an additional 24 h.

Lipids were extracted from 100 µL culture aliquots using 1.5 mL CHCl3/CH3OH (2:1) at 65 °C for 3 h. After adding 150 µL H2O, samples were mixed, centrifuged (3000 × g, 3 min), and the organic phase was collected and dried under nitrogen. Extracts were washed with CHCl3/CH3OH/H2O (4:2:1), dried under nitrogen, resuspended in 30 µL CHCl3/CH3OH (2:1), and 5 µL were loaded on silica gel 60 F254 plates (Merck). Phospholipids, trehalose monomycolates and trehalose dimycolates were separated using CHCl3/CH3OH/H2O (20:4:0.5). Autoradiograms were obtained by exposing plates to Kodak BIOMAX MR films at −70 °C for 4–12 days.
