Nonimaging optical design breaking the conventional symmetry of headlamp optics by side lighting with white LEDs
Authors: Chi-Shou Wu, Yu-Chi Chen, Chun-Yi Huang, Tsung-Hsun Yang, Yeh-Wei Yu, Ching-Cherng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work proposes a novel design in which an LED is mounted on the side wall of a multi-segment reflector, breaking the conventional symmetry of headlamp optics for the application of vehicle such as bicycle where the headlamp is always requested compact and light. Monte Carlo ray tracing was used to optimize the reflector, which was divided into 16 segments to generate a hot spot for the cut-off line. A cylindrical lens array (CLA) was then introduced to laterally spread and homogenize the pattern, ensuring symmetry and uniformity. A CNC-machined aluminum mockup with 80% reflectivity was fabricated and tested using an automated measurement system at 10 m. The measured illumination patterns of both low and high beams were symmetrical and met all regulatory checkpoints. The results demonstrate the feasibility of side-mounted LEDs with segmented reflectors and CLAs for compact bicycle headlamp designs.Subject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Bicycles have long been regarded as one of the best short-range transportation tools1. In addition, cycling is not only a competitive sport but also a popular healthy activity accessible to everyone2–6. A bicycle headlamp is essential for ensuring high visibility during nighttime riding. Since cycling is physically demanding, all associated equipment, including headlamps, must be compact and lightweight. This requirement makes the design of bicycle headlamps distinct from other types of headlamps7–11, with volume size becoming a critical factor.

Although bicycle headlamps must remain small and compact, they are still subject to regulations that restrict their illumination patterns12–14. The most important regulation StVZO TA 23 is the so-called K-mark regulation12, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Since K-mark regulation does not touch high beam for a bicycle, we introduce the regulation ISO 6742 for high-beam requirements14, as shown in Fig. 1b. For the low beam, sufficient short-range illumination must be provided without causing glare to oncoming pedestrians or vehicles. To achieve this, the regulation mandates a high-contrast cut-off line—one of the most challenging aspects of optical design, due to the relatively large étendue of light sources (e.g., white LEDs) compared with the small size of the headlamp15–17.

The check points in the k-mark regulation for (a) low beam by the K-mark regulation, and (b) high beam by the regulation of ISO 6742, where VH points are the main reference points of the illumination pattern. The HV point of K-mark low beam is located at the same vertical level as HV point of the high-beam regulation.

To satisfy the demands of lightweight, compact construction, various optical approaches have been applied in bicycle headlamp design18, including upward/downward multi-segment reflectors19–21, total internal reflection (TIR) lenses22, slanted light tubes11, and image projectors23. Image projectors have traditionally been used with high-pressure discharge lamps and white LEDs, but to minimize headlamp size, multi-segment reflectors and TIR lenses are generally more practical. Nevertheless, users continue to demand even smaller headlamps. One extreme idea is to integrate the headlamp into the bicycle’s crossbar24. While the crossbar provides significant length, its limited cross-section severely restricts headlamp size unless the optics extend along the crossbar.

Compared with the high beam, low-beam design is far more difficult due to the requirement of a sharp cut-off line. Given the geometrical constraints of the crossbar, one possible way to enlarge the optics is to place the light source on the side wall, rather than the top, bottom, or back wall20,25. However, this approach is atypical, since it breaks the traditional symmetry plane formed by the centers of the illumination pattern and the optical element, as shown in Fig. 2. To date, no commercial headlamp has adopted such a symmetry-breaking design.

Schematic diagram of a general geometry of a LED head lamp, where the plane formed by the dash lines is called symmetry plane. In general, the light source is located at this plane.

In this paper, we propose a new optical design based on a multi-segment reflector with an LED light source placed at the side wall, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The challenge lies in producing a symmetric illumination pattern despite the off-axis placement of the LED, which naturally leads to an asymmetric reflector geometry. For the optical design of a reflector producing a horizontally symmetric light pattern, the downward-lighting reflector is compared with the side-emitting reflective proposed in this work, as shown in Fig. 4. Figure 4a shows a symmetric reflector design, while Fig. 4b shows the proposed side-emitting reflective structure. From the analysis of the reflector curvature, as shown in Figs. 4c, d, the symmetric reflector uses the light source as the central axis and has a left-right symmetric curvature distribution on the reflective surface. This makes it easier for designers to generate a symmetric light pattern, and the superimposed light pattern can readily produce a uniform illuminance distribution. In contrast, for the side-emitting reflector, Fig. 4d shows that the radius-of-curvature distribution differs for each segment of the reflective surface. This design is intended to modulate the light pattern generated by each reflecting segment and, after superposition, to shape the bright region of the overall light pattern as close as possible to the cutoff-line region. To overcome this challenge in non-imaging optics, we developed a special design rule to ensure regulatory compliance. The novel headlamp geometry

The geometry of the proposed headlamp is illustrated in Fig. 5. The system consists of two identical reflectors: one functions as the low beam, and the other serve as the high beam, where the orientation of the reflector is mirror image of the former one. In both cases, a white LED light source is positioned at the side wall of the reflector. The reflector design is identical for both beams, with one illumination pattern satisfying the low-beam regulation and the inverted version meeting the high-beam regulation.

The top-view structure of the low/high beams: (a) two LEDs are back-to-back attached on the same heat sink, (b) two LEDs are attached on different side walls. CLA: cylindrical lens array.

Because both the lateral and vertical dimensions are constrained by the crossbar, each reflector segment collects emission over a larger solid angle, due to the low spatial coherence and large étendue of the LED source. As a result, the achievable contrast of the cut-off line is inherently limited. With careful optical design, however, the reduced contrast remains acceptable for the low beam and does not compromise the high beam. The design principle, therefore, is to optimize the low-beam reflector to achieve a well-defined cut-off line with the maximum attainable contrast.

Since the LED source is offset to one side, the illumination pattern may suffer from asymmetry in the reflector geometry. To address this, the design first directs the emission toward the forward (normal) direction to generate a central hot spot. A cylindrical lens array (CLA) is then introduced to laterally spread and smooth the distribution, resulting in a symmetric and uniform illumination pattern26,27.

Optical design and verification

The design flowchart is presented in Fig. 6. The first step is to determine the reflector dimensions: width (x) > 20.0 mm, height (y) = 21.0 mm, and thickness (z) = 25.3 mm. The height and thickness are constrained by the diameter of the bicycle crossbar. For such a compact reflector, a white LED with low étendue and high exitance is preferred28. Accordingly, we selected the Osram HWQP as the light source28, which delivers a luminous flux of 280 lm at 3 W injection power29. The Osram HWQP is well known for its relatively small étendue and high exitance, making it an ideal choice for this design. In our previous work, we reported the development of a precise optical model for this LED30–33, which has been successfully applied to a variety of headlamp designs20,26,27. The area of the white LED called HWQP by Osram is 1.03 × 1.03 mm2 as shown in Fig. 7(a). The luminous intensity distribution is shown in Fig. 7(b) when the output flux of the white LED is 280 lm.

Following the flowchart, the first-order optical design begins by calculating the reflector curvature without segmentation. At this stage, a spherical mirror directs light from the source—approximated by a point source in place of the actual LED—toward the illumination field along the z-axis. Using Monte Carlo ray tracing in ASAP34, the mirror curvature is iteratively adjusted at different positions, after which segmentation is applied. where n is the refractive index, θ is the viewing angle from each segment, Aₛ is the area of the light source, and Ω is the solid angle subtended by the segment with respect to the light source. A segment located farther from the light source receives less luminous flux, but it also has a smaller étendue. Such segments can therefore be used, together with the CLA, to form a high-contrast boundary for the cut-off line. The étendue of the emitting area of the HWQP LED is calculated to be 3.177 mm²·sr, whereas the receiving étendue of the reflector is only 2.303 mm²·sr. This indicates that a portion of the emitted light does not reach the reflector, resulting in a geometrical loss in the design. With respect to the K-mark regulation, the étendue associated with the cut-off-line region can be estimated to be approximately 1.529 mm²·sr, which is smaller than the étendue accepted by the reflector. Therefore, the curvatures of the selected segments located farther from the light source are key design factors for achieving the required cut-off-line performance. Accordingly, the segments 3, 6, 9, and 12, with the total etendue of 0.349 mm2sr are selected, which are primarily responsible for generating a hot spot that contributes to the cut-off line after passing through the CLA. Figure 9 illustrates both the sub-illumination patterns from individual segments and the combined pattern from the full reflector. Prior to passing through the CLA, the illumination pattern does not satisfy regulatory requirements. However, at this step, the hot spot’s upper boundary must already exhibit a strong contrast relative to the dark region above. To achieve the required distribution, a properly designed CLA is then introduced to laterally spread and smooth the illumination pattern, as shown in Fig. 10.

The main advantage of using a CLA is its ability to laterally spread the illumination pattern within a controlled range and to improve lateral uniformity. The CLA design depends on the radius of curvature (R) and the conic constant (K) of the cylindrical lens. For the low beam, the CLA is designed with R = 2.76 mm and K = − 2, resulting in a spreading angle of 6° for normally incident plane waves. For the high beam, the CLA is designed with R = 2.2 mm and K = − 2, producing a spreading angle of 4.5° under normal incidence27. A larger spreading angle increases the lateral coverage of the illumination pattern but reduces overall illuminance. According to regulations, the minimum illuminance must reach 20 lx at the hot spot for the low beam and 50 lx for the high beam. Therefore, the spreading angle of the CLA for the high beam is chosen narrower than that of the low beam to maintain sufficient illuminance. Figure 12 illustrates the regulatory checkpoints for low- and high-beam performance, along with the corresponding simulated illumination patterns. Note that the A3 point is located at the HV point of the low beam regulation, so the illuminance at A3 point will be larger than that in HV point by the low beam. The results in Figs. 11 and 12 confirm that the illumination patterns are symmetrical and that all regulatory checkpoints are satisfied when the HWQP LED was injected by 1000 mA and resulted in 283 lm flux emission. These findings demonstrate the success of the proposed optical design with a side-mounted light source.

Following the flowchart in Fig. 6, a successful design must be validated by measuring the illumination pattern of a fabricated mockup sample. The mockup was produced by CNC machining a bulk aluminum block, after which the reflector surface was polished to remove machining marks and achieve a reflectivity of approximately 80%, consistent with the value assumed in the simulation. In the experiment, the low-beam reflector and LED were mounted on a three-dimensional translation stage, allowing precise adjustment of their relative positions. An automated measurement system developed by our group was then employed to evaluate all regulatory checkpoints, as illustrated in Fig. 13. Since only one mockup mirror was made, we needed to use the reflector to perform low beam and high beam, respectively. The illumination patterns by the low beam and high beam are shown in Fig. 14, where the illuminance pattern by the manufacturing reflector is wider than that in simulation due to manufacturing errors. However, measurement of all the check points of the manufacturing sample meet the requirements of the regulations. If any measured illuminance at the checkpoints fails to meet regulatory requirements, two possible failure modes must be considered. Condition 1 corresponds to fabrication error, most often caused by insufficient surface polishing of the reflector. In such cases, the mockup is returned to the factory for re-machining. Condition 2 corresponds to design failure, typically due to neglecting the edge effects of individual reflector segments. Stray light generated at these edges can brighten the dark zone excessively, causing non-compliance. In this case, the development process must revert to the optical design stage, where all segment edges are carefully reviewed and, if necessary, the number of segments is reduced to suppress strong stray light. Fortunately, in our experiment, the measured illuminance at all checkpoints for both low and high beams satisfied the regulatory requirements, confirming the effectiveness of the proposed design.

Summary

Bicycle headlamps must be compact and lightweight while meeting strict regulatory requirements, such as the K-mark regulation, which specifies illumination patterns for both low and high beams. The most critical challenge lies in achieving the high-contrast cut-off line required for the low beam, despite the relatively large étendue of LED light sources and the limited dimensions imposed by bicycle crossbars. In this work, we propose a novel optical design for a bicycle headlamp that integrates a multi-segment reflector with an LED light source positioned on the side wall—an unconventional placement that breaks traditional symmetry. The reflector was optimized through Monte Carlo ray tracing and divided into 16 segments, with selected segments forming a hot spot for the cut-off line. A cylindrical lens array (CLA) was then employed to laterally spread and homogenize the illumination pattern, ensuring symmetry and uniformity. The optical design was verified experimentally using a CNC-machined aluminum mockup reflector with 80% reflectivity. Illumination measurements were conducted with an automated system at a distance of 10 m. Although the lateral illumination pattern of the fabricated sample is wider than that predicted by simulation, the measurements show that both low and high beams meet all regulatory checkpoints. In addition, the illumination is symmetric, indicating that the inherent asymmetry introduced by side lighting has been effectively compensated by the CLA. These findings demonstrate that a side-mounted LED combined with a segmented reflector and CLA can achieve compact, lightweight bicycle headlamps that comply with strict illumination regulations, offering a promising direction for future headlamp designs.
