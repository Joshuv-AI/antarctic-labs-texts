Meta-Analysis: The Effectiveness of Universal School-Based Mental Health Programs for Children and Adolescents
Authors: Kimberley C Tsujimoto, Ross D Neville, Sheri L Madigan, Olivia Carere, Jasmine Amini, Anett Schumacher, Sherinne Clarissa, Daphne J Korczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the effectiveness of school-based mental health programs on internalizing and externalizing symptoms and examine how these effects vary by program characteristics.MethodMEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, ERIC, and Scopus were searched for studies on the effects of universal intervention or prevention programs for internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Programs were implemented during the school day with students as the primary recipients. Random-effects meta-analyses estimated percent difference in post-program mean symptoms, comparing treatment and control groups, adjusting for baseline differences and informant.ResultsA total of 21,679 non-duplicate records were retrieved, yielding 59 relevant studies (232 sample estimates, N = 83,092, 53.8% female) across 22 countries. Results indicated marginal reductions for internalizing (standardized mean difference = –0.12, 95% CI = [–0.19,–0.06]) and externalizing symptoms (–0.08, [–0.14, –0.02]). Moderator analyses revealed stronger program effects for children with higher baseline internalizing symptoms (–0.17, [–0.26, –0.08]), but less so for externalizing symptoms (–0.10, [–0.20, –0.01]). Effects on internalizing symptoms were greatest for cognitive–behavioral therapy programs (–0.25, [–0.35, –0.15]). Results showed evidence of program effects when facilitated by mental health practitioners (–0.18, [–0.26, –0.10]). For externalizing symptoms, programs delivered in elementary (–0.10, [–0.18, –0.01]) and middle (–0.11, [–0.22, –0.002]) school were associated with marginal reductions, whereas effects for high school were inconclusive (0.03, [–0.18, 0.24]). Marginal reductions were observed in samples with higher socioeconomic status (SES) (–0.11, [–0.19, –0.03]), with slightly stronger effects in higher-SES elementary samples (–0.14, [–0.28, –0.01]).ConclusionSchool-based mental health programs demonstrate some effectiveness as universal interventions for internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Current programs may be more effective for children with elevated internalizing symptoms, suggesting potential benefits as targeted initiatives.Study registration informationSchool-based mental health interventions: A meta-analysis of the characteristics and effectiveness of interventions across school-aged children; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024528751.Key words: mental health, children and adolescents, school, intervention, prevention

Mental health problems are highly prevalent among children and youth, with up to 1 in 4 children meeting clinically significant thresholds.1,2 Since the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers have reported a general worsening of mental health symptoms among young people.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 As the demand for mental health services has increased, so too has interest in community-based, universal mental health prevention programs.

Schools may be an ideal setting for delivering preventive and supportive mental health programs, given their wide reach and accessibility.8, 9, 10 Universal approaches, which aim to provide access to supports for all children and youth irrespective of individual risk differences, are often first line when considering preventive interventions.8,9,11 A meta-analysis of studies conducted prior to the pandemic identified 118 unique trials of school-based interventions targeting internalizing mental health symptoms among children and youth,8 finding that school-based psychological interventions had a small effect on reducing internalizing (ie, depression and anxiety) symptoms.

Controlled trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of school-based programs for certain behavioral outcomes, including universal classroom strategies for behavior management.12,13 Whole-school systemic approaches that implement changes in the school environment, curriculum, and community level may also be an effective Tier 1 approach, but findings are inconclusive.14 A meta-analysis of 28 studies reporting on whole-school interventions found evidence of some effectiveness for peer and health behaviors (ie, cyber-bullying, cyber-aggression, smoking); however, effects on mental health outcomes were non-significant.14 In contrast to whole-school approaches, instructional approaches that can be integrated into the school day may be a feasible way to offer supplemental mental health support to a significant number of children and youth. These include programs that aim to help children and youth to identify and articulate their feelings or to learn strategies to manage negative emotions when they arise. Some research has reported that curriculum-based intervention and prevention efforts that are implemented at the classroom level may support child and youth mental health in the school setting.8,9,11,15 However, previous meta-analyses on universal instructional approaches to mental health intervention and prevention have almost exclusively focused on internalizing symptoms.8,9 This includes a recent meta-analysis by Hayes et al., who found some evidence of program effectiveness for depression and anxiety but did not evaluate program effects for externalizing outcomes.11 Given increasing concerns about externalizing problems among school-aged children and youth, examination of program effects on behaviors related to symptoms of irritability, inattention, and hyperactivity is needed. In addition, previous estimates may not accurately reflect current effectiveness of universal programs, which may be influenced by the increasing prevalence of child and youth mental health problems among students in the classroom. Experts in children’s health have identified schools as critical spaces to access support; however, despite growing interest in schools as centers for supplemental mental supports, funding for these programs is a challenge.16 Rigorous evaluations that are based on the most current evidence of the impact of these programs are needed to inform the decisions of policymakers in children’s health and education.

Although the meta-analytic results are promising, there are several reasons why an updated review is necessary. First, recent evidence of worsening child and youth mental health3, 4, 5, 6, 7 highlights the urgent need to update current evidence of the effectiveness of school-based programs. Second, a trend that was catalyzed by the online transition of many services and daily activities, including school programming, during the pandemic was an increase in the number of digital mental health school programs. The effectiveness of these digital interventions compared to traditional in-person programs has remained mostly unexamined meta-analytically.8,17 Third, recent reports have raised concern for increasing externalizing symptoms among children and youth,18,19 yet meta-analyses of school-based mental health programs have focused primarily on internalizing symptoms.8,9,11,15 Finally, previous meta-analyses have been limited in their ability to examine how baseline mental health symptoms influence universal program effectiveness. Rigorous evaluations that examine how preexisting symptom severity affects intervention outcomes is crucial for tailoring school-based mental health programs.

A comprehensive synthesis of extant literature—one that considers both developmental stage and program characteristics—is needed to inform best practices in the design and implementation of mental health interventions in schools. For instance, the factors that contribute to making a program successful for elementary school–aged children may differ from those for youth in middle school or high school, who have different cognitive, social, and emotional needs. Moreover, research on program type (eg, cognitive–behavioral therapy [CBT], mindfulness) and facilitator characteristics (eg, teacher-led vs practitioner-led) are mixed.8,9 As school-based mental health programs have become of increasing interest to school boards and educators, a closer examination of the specific program characteristics that contribute to effective programs for all children and youth is warranted.

Our primary objective was to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of the published literature on universal school-based mental health programs that aimed to either support mental health or reduce mental illness and to quantify their effectiveness. We focused on universal, or Tier 1, interventions, given their potential for supporting the mental health and/or prevention of mental illness at the school-wide or classroom level. Specifically, curriculum-based programs, with children and youth as the primary recipients, and those implementing instructional strategies or activities during the school day were considered. We examined their effectiveness across school ages (elementary, middle, and high school) on broad mental health symptom domains, including internalizing (depression, anxiety) and externalizing (irritability, inattention, hyperactivity) symptoms. Our secondary objective was to identify program characteristics that may be associated with effectiveness. Specifically, baseline mental health symptoms, program type, facilitator, school stage, socioeconomic status (SES), and study quality were examined as moderators of program effectiveness.

Method

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

This review followed the methods and guidance of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Reviewer’s Manual and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, and was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024528751). Systematic searches were constructed in the following databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, APA PsycINFO, ERIC, and Scopus. An initial search was conducted up until March 26, 2024 and was then updated to include studies up until July 2, 2025. The search strategy included 4 blocks of terms: (1) school-based interventions, (2) mental health, (3) population, and (4) method. The full search strategy for MEDLINE is provided in Table S1, available online; searches of other databases were similar to that for MEDLINE). Additional studies were identified during full-text review and manually retrieved. This included relevant studies cited by articles that were captured by search strategies. Retrieved studies were stored using EndNote reference software and imported into Covidence software for title and abstract review.

Studies were included in the meta-analysis if they reported on (1) universal (ie, Tier 1) intervention or prevention programs aiming either to support mental health or to reduce mental illness and included (2) internalizing and/or externalizing mental health symptoms as outcomes; (3) school-aged samples (ie, kindergarten to grade 12, mean age 4-19 years), (4) a comparator group, and (5) intervention or prevention programs with a component that was instructional and that engaged students in program activities that were delivered as part of the school day. Multi-component interventions were considered if the psychological and psychoeducational mental health component constitutes >75% of the program content. To ensure that the review captured a full range of universal mental health programs being implemented in school settings, inclusion criteria did not limit eligible studies by program type or framework (eg, cognitive–behavioral therapy, mindfulness).

Studies were excluded if programs (1) comprised selective or targeted interventions, (2) targeted parents or teachers as the primary recipients, or (3) focused on drug and alcohol use, physical activity, diet and nutrition, or a pharmacological curriculum.

Titles and abstracts were reviewed for inclusion eligibility in duplicate by independent coders (KT, OC, AS1, SC, JM) with 87% to 99% agreement. Full-text articles were reviewed for inclusion eligibility by the first author and one other independent coder (OC, AS1, SC, JM, AS2) with 85% to 98% agreement. Discrepancies were resolved via consensus between KT and DK. Quality assessment was conducted by 2 independent coders using the JBI appraisal checklists for RCTs and quasi-experimental studies. The average among appraisal checklist items, rated as 0 = not evident or 1 = evident, was used as a total continuous score for study quality.20,21 Discrepancies were discussed and resolved via consensus between raters (KT, OC, JA, AS1, SC).

Data Extraction and Preparation

Summary data were extracted from each study: author, publication year, study characteristics (eg, location), program characteristics (eg, program type, facilitator), sample characteristics (eg, sample mean age, school stage, race and ethnicity, SES), modality (eg, in-person, virtual, hybrid), type of comparator, and mental health outcomes for internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Program type was extracted for all studies and then reviewed to determine the categories available for moderation analyses, which included CBT, Mindfulness, Social–Emotional Learning (SEL), and Multicomponent. Other program types that were too few to be categorized for moderation analysis are described below. Program type was coded based on the theoretical or conceptual frameworks of an intervention as described by the study authors. The Multicomponent category included programs that combined multiple strategies. For facilitator, categories included teacher and clinical/mental health practitioner. School stage categories included elementary school (kindergarten–grade 5), middle school (grades 6-8), and high school (grades 9-12/13). Socioeconomic status was categorized as lower or higher at the sample level using SES-related indicators reported in each primary study. Samples were coded as lower SES when studies reported markers of socioeconomic disadvantage, such as lower household income, eligibility for free or reduced-price lunches, lower-income school catchment areas, or higher parental unemployment.

Pre- and post-intervention means, standard deviations, and sample sizes were extracted for treatment and control groups. Minimum and maximum possible scale values for all outcomes were extracted to facilitate estimation of post-program mean differences between groups. For one study, the post-intervention mean score was calculated using the raw pre-intervention symptom mean score and reported mean change. For studies that did not report post SDs, the SD was imputed from studies with complete data and the same outcome (ie, internalizing or externalizing symptoms). A total of 17 studies had missing data and were excluded after unsuccessful attempts to retrieve data from study authors. All data were extracted independently by 2 coders. Discrepancies were resolved via consensus between coders.

Data Analysis

Random-effects meta-analyses were used to estimate post-program mean differences in internalizing and externalizing symptoms between treatment and control groups using the mixed-model procedure (Proc Mixed) in the Statistical Analysis System (SAS OnDemand for Academics version 9.4). In each meta-analysis, a single fixed effect represented the 2 treatment conditions (ie, treatment and control) interacted with informant (ie, child, parent, teacher). An additional fixed effect accounted for pre-program baseline differences in internalizing and externalizing symptoms between the treatment conditions and informants.

Random effects accounted for study identity, estimate identity within studies (ie, treatment or control), and estimate informant identity (ie, child, parent, teacher reported data). These 3 random effects were nested and estimated as variances.22 The square root of the sum of these between- and within-study variances provided the tau statistic as an estimate of heterogeneity, which has been shown to be preferable to the I2 statistic.23,24

For subsequent moderator analyses, modified random-effects models incorporated an additional fixed effect to estimate the treatment effect at different levels of the moderator for both continuous (ie, study quality) and categorical (ie, facilitator, program type, school stage, SES, continent) moderators. The meta-analyzed effects are reported as the percent difference in post-program means for internalizing and externalizing symptoms between treatment conditions, with corresponding uncertainties expressed as 95% confidence intervals. To aid interpretation, we expressed the magnitude of program effects and moderating effects in standardized units by dividing meta-analyzed effects and their 95% CIs by a common reference SD. To avoid artifactually inflating the meta-analyzed effects because of study-level variation in pre-intervention SDs, we conducted this standardization after the meta-analyses rather than before, as recommended by Hopkins and Rowlands.25 The reference SD for standardizing was the sample-size–weighted mean of the baseline SDs for internalizing and externalizing symptoms across all studies. The corresponding thresholds for interpreting program effects sizes were <0.2 (marginal), 0.2 to <0.5 (small), 0.5 to <1.0 (medium), and ≥1.0 (large).26 The corresponding thresholds for interpreting tau (the between-study SD of true effects) were set at half of these values; that is, <0.10 (marginal), 0.10 to <0.25 (small), 0.25 to <0.50 (medium), and ≥0.50 (large).27

Random-effect solutions from meta-analysis models were used to generate sample-estimate residual values and their corresponding p values. Residual values were considered outliers if their p value, divided by the degrees of freedom (df), was <.05.

Internalizing Symptoms

Characteristics of 50 studies reporting program effects on internalizing symptoms are displayed in Table 1 (also Table S2, available online). These studies yielded 128 sample estimates from 43,332 school-aged children and youth (54% female) across 20 countries. Of these, 30 studies (60%) were conducted in Europe, 9 (18%) in Oceania, 5 (10%) in North America, 4 (8%) in Asia, and 1 study each (4%) in Africa and South America.

CBT was the most common program type (30%), followed by mindfulness (16%), social–emotional learning (14%), and multicomponent programs (16%), most of which integrated clinically informed methods (eg, CBT) with other cognitive, social, or behavioral strategies. Other, less common, program types included interventions focused on mental health literacy, problem solving, coping skills, emotion regulation, psychosocial education, nature-based, life skills and communication, extracurriculars, peer support, behavior management, and transdiagnostic approaches. Most program facilitators were classroom teachers (58%). One in 5 programs was facilitated by clinicians or mental health practitioners (20%). The remaining studies (22%) involved researchers as facilitators, program-specific instructors, or a combination of facilitators. Most studies sampled from elementary schools (kindergarten–grade 5, 36%), followed by middle school (grades 6-8, 18%) and high school (grades 9-12, 18%). Among studies that reported SES, more included higher-SES samples (32%) than lower-SES samples (18%). SES data were not reported in half of the included studies.

The pooled effect of universal school-based mental health programs indicated a marginal reduction in internalizing symptoms (standardized mean difference = –0.12, 95% CI = –0.19 to –0.06, p = .0003) (Figure 1, Table 3). Program effects were slightly more pronounced (ie, of marginal to small magnitude) in samples with higher baseline internalizing symptoms (–0.17, 95% CI = –0.26 to –0.08, p = .0002). Estimated between-study heterogeneity (tau = 0.18, 95% CI = 0.15 to 0.21) was consistent with a small spread in true treatment effects for internalizing symptoms across studies. A sensitivity analysis for post-SD imputation is provided in Table S3, available online.

Moderation analyses indicated that CBT-based programs produced larger reductions in internalizing symptoms than mindfulness-based (standardized difference in program effects between program types of 0.22, 95% CI = 0.05 to 0.40, p = .0133) and social–emotional learning-based programs (0.32, 95% CI = 0.16 to 0.49, p = .0002). Evidence that CBT-based programs outperformed multicomponent programs was weaker, with a smaller standardized difference and a confidence interval that included zero (0.13, 95% CI = –0.03 to 0.29, p = .1029).

The effect of programs facilitated by clinicians or mental health practitioners showed a marginal reduction in internalizing symptoms (–0.18, 95% CI = –0.26 to –0.10, p < .0001). There was also evidence that practitioner-led programs produced larger reductions in internalizing symptoms than teacher-led programs, although the difference was marginal in magnitude (–0.12, 95% CI = –0.18 to –0.05, p = .0004).

A marginal effect of publication year was observed (0.14, 95% CI = 0.07 to 0.21, p = .0013), indicating that more recent studies reported smaller average reductions in internalizing symptoms. We found limited evidence that program effects on internalizing symptoms differed substantially by school stage, sample SES, or study quality (Table 3).

Externalizing Symptoms

Characteristics of 42 studies reporting program effects on externalizing symptoms are summarized in Table 2 (also Table S2, available online). These studies yielded 104 sample estimates, encompassing 39,760 school-aged children and youth (53% female) across 19 countries. Of these, 26 studies (62%) reported data from Europe, 7 (17%) from Oceania, 4 (10%) from North America, 4 (10%) from Asia, and 1 study (2%) from South America.

The most common program type was CBT (33%), followed by multi-component (19%), mindfulness (17%), and social–emotional learning (14%) programs. Other, less common, program types included interventions focused on coping skills, nature-based, extracurriculars, peer support, wellbeing therapy, and behavior management approaches. Facilitators were most often classroom teachers (57%), followed by clinicians or mental health practitioners (21%). Most studies sampled from elementary schools (45%), followed by middle schools (14%) and high schools (14%). Among studies that reported SES, more included higher-SES samples (29%) than lower-SES samples (21%). Half of studies did not report SES data.

The effect of universal school-based mental health programs also indicated a marginal reduction in externalizing symptoms (standardized mean difference = –0.08, 95% CI = –0.14 to –0.02, p = .0075) (Figure 2, Table 3). Estimated between-study heterogeneity (tau = 0.08, 95% CI = 0.05 to 0.10) was consistent with a marginal-to-small spread in true treatment effects for externalizing symptoms across studies. Moderation analyses indicated that marginal reductions in externalizing symptoms were observed for CBT-based (–0.12, 95% CI = –0.18 to –0.05, p = .0018) and multicomponent programs (–0.11, 95% CI = –0.19 to –0.04, p = .0051). Mindfulness-based programs showed slightly weaker evidence of a marginal reduction (–0.10, 95% CI = –0.20 to 0.001, p = .0528), and there was limited conclusive evidence of reductions following social–emotional learning–based programs (0.01, 95% CI = –0.07, 0.08, p = .8916).

Programs delivered to elementary (0.10, 95% CI = –0.18, –0.01, p = .0234) and middle (–0.11, 95% CI = –0.22, –0.002, p = .0479) school students were associated with a marginal reduction in externalizing symptoms, whereas effects for programs delivered to high school students were inconclusive (0.03, 95% CI = –0.18, 0.24, p = .7809), with 95% CIs approaching a range that would encompass both small negative and small positive effects.

In addition, marginal reductions in externalizing symptoms were observed in higher-SES samples (–0.11; 95% CI = –0.19 to –0.03, p = .0110), with slightly stronger effects in higher-SES samples receiving programs in elementary school settings (–0.14; 95% CI = –0.28 to –0.01, p = .0444).

We found limited evidence that program effects on externalizing symptoms differed substantially across levels of other moderators, such as program facilitator, study quality, or study year (Table 3).

Discussion

The objective of this meta-analysis was to review the literature on universal school-based mental health programs delivered directly to children and youth, and to evaluate program effectiveness for internalizing and externalizing mental health symptoms. We found evidence of some effectiveness of universal school-based mental health intervention programs in reducing internalizing symptoms. Overall, effect sizes were marginal. Moderators, including baseline symptom level, program type, program facilitator, school stage, and SES, indicated the circumstances under which some programs may be more effective.

Our findings differ from those found by Hayes et al.,11 who reported no significant effects of universal mental health programs on broad internalizing symptoms. This may, in part, be due to the larger number of studies (50 vs 15) in the current meta-analysis that examined effects on internalizing symptoms, which may have strengthened our ability to detect small effects. In addition, our analytical approach allowed for adjustment for pre-program symptom differences, which enabled an evaluation of program effectiveness while considering children with and without elevated mental health symptoms at baseline.

Greater evidence of program effectiveness was found for children with higher baseline internalizing symptoms prior to receiving the intervention. These findings may, in part, be explained by the types of programs implemented as universal mental health interventions in schools. Specifically, programs mostly comprised CBT approaches, either wholly or in part, as many of the mental health programs included clinically informed frameworks that incorporated some CBT strategies (Tables 1 and 2). As CBT was developed as a psychotherapeutic intervention to treat clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms, school-based universal mental health programs grounded in CBT may be more tailored to the needs of targeted populations, specifically children and youth with elevated internalizing symptoms.8

In addition to greater program effects for CBT interventions, multicomponent programs were also comparatively more effective. Multicomponent programs typically used a variety of social or emotional strategies (eg, mindfulness, emotion regulation); however, most also incorporated those core to CBT. The range in the types of programs that have been implemented as mental health intervention and/or prevention programs in schools is noteworthy. In addition to CBT, social–emotional and mindfulness programs were also common program types. These programs often target skill development in social, emotional, and behavioral domains, all of which are relevant to mental health. As schools continue to seek out best practices to support student mental health, comparing effects across program types is critical. The wide range of programs and conceptual overlap highlight the importance of evaluating the utility of different frameworks for mental health intervention or prevention in schools. For instance, our results and prior research provide some support for the effectiveness of school-based CBT for depression and anxiety,9 particularly in targeted approaches.15 Although we found evidence of reduced treatment effects among more recently published studies, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the distribution of study years varied by program type. Some program types contributed data across almost the full 2005 to 2025 range, whereas others were represented only within a much narrower range of years.

Finally, stronger evidence of effectiveness was found for programs that were led by clinical or mental health practitioners, which may reflect their increased familiarity with CBT concepts and their expertise in introducing its core skills and strategies. In contrast, programs led by teachers were not effective. Previous meta-analyses have presented mixed program effects for teacher and clinical program facilitators.8,9 However, Zhang et al. also found that programs were more effective when led by clinicians.9 Our findings similarly highlight the importance of involving experts in school-based mental health programs that target internalizing symptoms. Taken together, findings suggest that current programs, albeit posited as universal prevention programs, appear to perform as modified clinical programs and, as such, may be more appropriately implemented as targeted (Tier II) programs.

Previous reviews and meta-analyses have focused predominantly on internalizing symptoms as intervention outcomes for universal school-based programs.8,9 This has led to a gap in understanding regarding their potential for universal school-based interventions to reduce externalizing mental health symptoms, despite growing concerns for behavior problems at school.18,19 Our meta-analysis found limited evidence that these programs effectively reduce externalizing symptoms such as aggression, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. This finding aligns with previous research underscoring that addressing environmental factors (eg, calm and consistent structure and routine, engagement in positive activities, positive reinforcement),85,86 may play a more significant role in managing disruptive behaviors than approaches focused primarily on internal individual factors (eg, cognitive restructuring).87,88

The current study focused on programs in which students were directly engaged in activities, rather than interventions operating at the environment level. Universal programs that target the school or classroom environment,85 such as those that promote structure and consistency and that provide feedback about positive and pro-social behaviors, may be better suited to addressing externalizing symptoms than curriculum-based interventions focused on individual factors. Although some programs may implement classroom activities targeting behavior management strategies, there were too few to evaluate their effectiveness compared to other instructional programs in the current study. Rather, the majority of programs reviewed consisted of CBT, mindfulness, or social–emotional learning approaches. Relatedly, there was some evidence that program effects for externalizing symptoms were greater for elementary and middle school samples. This may, in part, be because of the greater consistency in structure and routines in school settings during earlier school stages, as children generally remain in the same classroom with the same teacher.

The results of the current study suggest that broader consideration of school-based influences to improve children’s mental health may be required, given the marginal effects of current curriculum-based programs. For example, school-based programs designed to address risk factors known to influence mental health symptoms, or those aimed at increasing mental health knowledge and reducing stigma (eg, mental health literacy or awareness programs10), may be more effective as universal school-based programs than those that focus on solely on reducing mental health symptoms. Programs that reduce risk factors for poor mental health, such as excessive screen use,89 bullying,90 and food insecurity, and that increase factors associated with improved mental health, such as engagement in school extracurricular clubs and sports,91 academic support, and community building,92 may yield better results and may be more aligned with approaches within educators’ expertise. Alternatively, programs aimed at increasing mental health literacy may lead to improved self-awareness and help-seeking by reducing mental health stigma. However, meta-analytic evaluations of the effectiveness of universal school-based mental health literacy programs in decreasing stigma and facilitating the early identification of symptoms are needed.10

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, to address limitations of previous meta-analyses, the present study estimated intervention effectiveness as post-mean differences using percentage of maximum possible estimated effects. This method provides a more robust estimation of program effects that (1) accounts for biases in heterogeneity, which are often inflated with common standardized mean differences such as Hedges g25; (2) considers the effects of baseline symptom levels in program effectiveness; and (3) accounts for the presence of baseline differences identified during quality assessment Relatedly, studies varied with respect to whether mental health outcomes were reported by youth, parents, or teachers. Youth self-reports are more reliable for measurement of internalizing symptoms, compared with parent or teacher report, as youth are better informants of their internal emotional state.93,94 In contrast, teacher and parent reports may be more reliable for observable externalizing behaviors.95,96 Although informant biases are a challenge to address within studies, the analytical approach used allowed us to model and examine variation associated with informant type while accounting for dependency among informant-specific estimates. However, this method also requires greater information about the scales used for study outcomes compared with Hedges g estimates. This resulted in the exclusion of 17 studies, potentially limiting the statistical power to detect significant effects. Despite this limitation, rigorous methods that estimate program effects with the greatest possible precision are critical to determine the impact of school-based mental health programs.

Second, limited data were available to assess differences in program effects for potentially meaningful moderators: modality, child race/ethnicity, sex, and SES. Relatedly, the types of programs being implemented were highly variable, and comparisons across all types of programs were not possible because of the low frequency for some (eg, peer support, life skills). Too few virtual or hybrid programs (n = 6) were included to evaluate differences compared to programs implemented in-person. Virtual or hybrid interventions were variable with respect to content and delivery. Two programs were implemented as video games designed to teach emotional resilience or social–emotional skills. Another 2 programs were offered as single-session interventions using scenarios or vignettes to teach coping and problem-solving skills. One program used a hybrid format that was similar to multi-session in-person CBT interventions, but with an online component. One program was designed as an in-person mindfulness program, but had to be offered virtually during the COVID-19 pandemic. As elevated screen use persists post pandemic, including online learning, future research should still consider differences between in-person and virtual programs. This includes programs that are offered in school settings but that are primarily delivered via an electronic device rather than a human facilitator such as a teacher or mental health practitioner.

Similarly, few studies reported race and ethnicity of study samples (n = 16), and there was limited variability in sex because of the nature of universally-designed interventions, which are typically clustered at the school or classroom level. Thus, the potential effects of sex and race could not be evaluated. Finally, few studies included samples of higher social risk, and many from Europe and Oceania (Australia), limiting the generalizability of study findings with respect to lower- and middle-income countries and settings. It is possible that demographic differences in program effects exist, but we were unable to assess these in the current study.

In conclusion, there is some evidence to suggest that universal school-based mental health programs may be effective for reducing internalizing and externalizing symptoms. However, the benefits for internalizing symptoms appear to be strongest for children with higher internalizing symptoms at baseline, rather than for all students. Future research synthesizing current knowledge of the effectiveness of school-wide approaches that aim to reduce risk factors (eg, screen time, food insecurity), to bolster protective factors (eg, school engagement, academic support), and to increase mental health literacy among students and educators is needed. By identifying the most effective strategies, research can help to ensure that schools allocate their limited time, resources, and efforts to interventions that maximize impact and provide the greatest benefit for the mental health of all children.
