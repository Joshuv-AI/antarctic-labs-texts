Risk factor assessment and phased management for post-stroke dysphagia in hospitalized patients: a retrospective evidence-based study
Authors: Luwen Zhang, Anqi Wang, Tianyao Yue, Jingjing Liang, Yan Zhan, Qianqian Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke dysphagia (PSD) is a common functional impairment after stroke and is associated with aspiration, pneumonia, malnutrition, and reduced rehabilitation safety. Existing studies have mainly examined PSD risk factors at a single stage of recovery, with limited evidence on phase-specific risk assessment in inpatient rehabilitation settings. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of PSD and its associated risk factors across different phases of the disease course in hospitalized stroke patients and to inform phase-based management strategies.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted among 5,363 stroke patients admitted to the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Xiangyang Central Hospital, Hubei, China, between January 2021 and December 2023. PSD was screened using the Chinese version of the Eating Assessment Tool-10 (EAT-10). The disease course was classified as acute (within 2 weeks post-stroke), subacute (2 weeks to 6 months), and chronic phases (beyond 6 months). Group differences were examined using the chi-square test, and multi-variate logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with PSD overall and within each phase.ResultsAmong the 5,363 patients, 2,306 had PSD, yielding an overall prevalence of 43.0%. In the overall analysis, intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH, OR 1.79, 95% CI 1.54–2.09), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH, OR 2.22, 95% CI 1.58–3.14), brainstem lesion (OR 1.79, 95% CI 1.43–2.24), cerebellar lesion (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.28–2.31), advanced age (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.82–2.38), and previous surgery of stroke (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.17–1.51) were independently associated with higher PSD risk. Subcortical lesion (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.85) and higher education status (OR 0.55, 95% CI 0.43–0.70) were protective factors. Phase-specific analyses showed that SAH and cortical lesion were the main risk factors in the acute phase, ICH and brainstem lesion in the subacute phase, and advanced age and ICH in the chronic phase.ConclusionPSD is common among hospitalized stroke patients undergoing rehabilitation, and its risk profile varies across disease phases. Phase-based screening and management strategies may improve early identification, guide individualized rehabilitation, and reduce dysphagia-related complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05126-7.Keywords: Post-stroke dysphagia, Rehabilitation, Risk factors, Phased management, Inpatient rehabilitation

Introduction

Stroke represents a significant disease burden in China and is a major public health concern. As of 2024, China has 14.94 million stroke patients, ranking first in the world [1]. Post-stroke dysphagia (PSD) is one of the most common functional disorders following a stroke, with an incidence rate ranging from 30% to 80% [2]. Research on PSD has always been a key focus in stroke studies.

PSD arises from brain lesions, dysfunction of the swallowing control centers, or failure of neural network compensation, reorganization, or repair, resulting in impaired safe and effective transport of food to the stomach [3, 4]. Clinically, PSD is characterized by deficits in two fundamental swallowing processes: mastication and bolus formation. Patients may also present with transient respiratory pauses during swallowing, drooling, and other related manifestations [5]. This dysfunction predisposes patients to serious complications such as aspiration and pneumonia, and is associated with adverse outcomes including malnutrition, dehydration, and diminished quality of life [6]. The risk of PSD is influenced by factors including the location and severity of brain lesions, stroke subtype, presence of multiple or bilateral infarcts, advanced age, and comorbid conditions [7, 8].

PSD typically manifests early during inpatient rehabilitation, often coinciding with the initiation of rehabilitative interventions, and its course is frequently protracted [9]. PSD not only compromises the safety of rehabilitation but also markedly elevates mortality risk. The post-stroke recovery trajectory is conventionally divided into acute (within 2 weeks post-stroke), subacute (2 weeks to 6 months), and chronic phases (beyond 6 months). Evidence indicates that over half of stroke survivors develop PSD during the acute phase [10]. In a subset of patients, dysphagia persists into the subacute and even chronic phases, substantially extending the rehabilitation timeline [11]. In China, inpatient rehabilitation remains the predominant modality for stroke recovery. Multiple studies have demonstrated that inpatient rehabilitation yields superior improvements in functional outcomes and quality of life compared to alternative rehabilitation settings [12]. Nevertheless, the high prevalence and persistence of PSD not only prolong inpatient rehabilitation duration but also present significant obstacles to the safety and efficacy of the rehabilitative process [13].

Identifying risk factors for PSD across different phases is essential for developing more precise and individualized rehabilitation strategies. Such an approach can mitigate complication rates, enhance rehabilitation outcomes, and safeguard patient safety throughout the recovery process [14]. However, the majority of existing studies have focused on risk factors within a single phase. For instance, Gu and colleagues conducted a meta-analysis exclusively examining risk factors during the acute phase, highlighting variables such as advanced age, hypertension, diabetes, prior stroke, and atrial fibrillation [15]. Mao’s research encompassed PSD patients across various rehabilitation stages but did not stratify risk factors by phase [16]. Similarly, Song emphasized that current literature predominantly addresses risk factors at isolated time points or phases, lacking a comprehensive, systematic evaluation of their dynamic evolution throughout the entire disease course and across multiple phases [17].

Currently, there is a paucity of studies investigating risk factors and formulating corresponding rehabilitation strategies from the perspective of distinct phases of stroke progression. Unlike studies that primarily focus on a single time point or report overall prevalence estimates, the present study specifically focuses on hospitalized stroke patients undergoing inpatient rehabilitation and aims to characterize the phase-specific distribution of PSD, identify stage-specific risk factors, and provide clinical evidence for phased management and rehabilitation resource allocation. Accordingly, this study aimed to analyze risk factors across different phases of inpatient rehabilitation and propose more targeted and refined management approaches to optimize clinical rehabilitation outcomes.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This was a retrospective observational study conducted in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Xiangyang Central Hospital, Xiangyang, Hubei Province, China. The department is a provincially designated key clinical specialty and a national training base, serving as a technical leader and regional medical center for northwestern Hubei. The study population comprised stroke patients who received inpatient rehabilitation in this department between January 2021 and December 2023. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of PSD among hospitalized stroke patients at different stages of rehabilitation and to identify stage-specific risk factors, thereby providing evidence to inform phased management strategies.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years old; and (2) a confirmed diagnosis of stroke based on cranial CT or MRI findings in accordance with relevant clinical criteria. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of dysphagia or primary oropharyngeal disease; (2) a history of other severe neurological disorders; and (3) inability to complete the swallowing assessment because of coma, marked disturbance of consciousness, severe cognitive impairment, aphasia, or other related conditions.

A total of 11,574 patients were admitted to the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine during the study period. After excluding 5,160 non-stroke patients, 6,414 stroke patients remained. A further 1,051 patients were excluded (14 aged under 18 years, and 1,037 unable to complete the swallowing assessment), leaving 5,363 patients in the final analysis. Among the 1,037 excluded patients, 32 (3.1%) were in the acute phase, and the primary reason for exclusion was coma. Data collection

Demographic and primary diagnostic information were extracted from the case summary pages of the hospital information system (HIS). Imaging data, including cranial CT and MRI findings, were obtained from the radiology and examination information system. Swallowing assessment results were retrieved from the rehabilitation assessment system. These three data sources were linked using each patient’s unique identification number and subsequently merged into a single dataset, after which all records were anonymized. To ensure data accuracy, the integrated dataset was subjected to secondary verification by rehabilitation physicians and therapists, and two physicians independently cross-checked all entries.

Dependent variable

The outcome variable in this study was the presence or absence of PSD. PSD was screened using the Chinese version of the Eating Assessment Tool-10 (EAT-10), which was originally developed by Belafsky, translated into Chinese by an expert panel in 2013, and subsequently validated for its reliability and validity [18]. The scale consists of 10 items, each scored from 0 to 4, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating more severe swallowing difficulty. In this study, an EAT-10 total score greater than 3 was defined as indicative of PSD, whereas a score of 3 or less was considered to indicate absence of PSD.

Independent variables

The independent variables in this study were grouped into the following domains: stroke phase, stroke subtype, brain lesion characteristics, clinical features, comorbidities, and demographic factors. The time interval from stroke onset to hospital admission was used to classify the disease course into acute, subacute, and chronic. Stroke subtypes included ischemic stroke (IS) and hemorrhagic stroke, with the latter comprising intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Brain lesion characteristics were determined based on MRI findings and comprised cerebral hemisphere involvement (left, right, bilateral, or none), cortical lesion, subcortical lesion, cerebellar lesion, and brainstem lesion, with the latter four coded as binary variables. Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, and previous surgery of stroke. Demographic variables included sex (male, female), age (18–59 years old, ≥ 60 years old), educational status (primary school or below, junior/senior high school, college or above), marital status (married, single/divorced/widowed), smoking history (yes, no), and drinking history (yes, no).

Statistical methods

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test. A Sankey diagram was constructed to illustrate the distribution of PSD across different stroke phases and stroke subtypes. To identify independent risk factors associated with PSD, multi-variate logistic regression analysis was performed. First, potential risk factors from different domains were added stepwise to assess the robustness of the overall model; subsequently, separate multi-variate logistic regression models were fitted for each of the three disease stages to determine stage-specific risk factors for PSD. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence and clinical characteristics of PSD

Among the 5,363 patients included in the analysis, 2,306 (43.0%) developed PSD. Of the 2,306 patients with PSD, 1,577 (68.4%) were in the subacute phase, 366 (15.9%) in the acute phase, and 363 (15.7%) in the chronic phase (Supplementary Table S2-S4). This reflects the case-mix of the department rather than a higher per-patient risk: the within-phase prevalence of PSD was similar in the acute (44.7%) and subacute (46.2%) phases and lower in the chronic phase (32.2%). Within the PSD cohort, 50.0% had experienced IS, 70.9% were experiencing their first stroke, and 50.8% were undergoing their initial rehabilitation. Regarding brain lesions, 67.5% presented with cerebral hemisphere lesion, 49.8% with subcortical lesion, 7.3% with cerebellar lesion, and 15.2% with brainstem lesion. Comorbid conditions included hypertension (81.9%), coronary heart disease (14.5%), dyslipidemia (5.2%), and previous surgery of stroke (51.2%). The majority of patients were male (63.5%) and had completed junior or senior high school (59.1%). All aforementioned variables demonstrated statistically significant differences between patients with and without PSD (P < 0.05), as summarized in Table 1.

Prevalence of PSD across disease phases and stroke subtypes

The distribution of PSD across phases and subtypes is shown in Fig. 2A, and the within-phase prevalence by subtype in Fig. 2B. The subacute phase accounted for the majority of PSD cases in every subtype (IS 721, ICH 760, SAH 96), which is a consequence of the predominantly subacute case-mix of the department. The within-phase prevalence, however, was comparable between the acute and subacute phases for each subtype (IS 43.2% vs. 44.0%; ICH 49.7% vs. 47.1%; SAH 71.4% vs. 57.8%, the acute SAH estimate based on only 7 patients), and was consistently lower in the chronic phase (IS 30.0%, ICH 32.9%, SAH 47.6%). Thus the burden of PSD during inpatient rehabilitation is concentrated in the subacute phase, whereas the per-patient risk is broadly stable from the acute to the subacute phase before declining in the chronic phase.

Overall and phase-specific risk factors for PSD

Multi-variate logistic regression analysis identified several independent risk factors for PSD. Patients with ICH (OR 1.79, 95% CI 1.54–2.09) and SAH (OR 2.22, 95% CI 1.58–3.14) exhibited a significantly higher risk of PSD compared with those with IS. The risk of PSD was also elevated in the subacute phase (OR 1.18, 95% CI 0.98–1.43) relative to the acute phase, while patients in the chronic phase demonstrated a reduced risk (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.95). Regarding brain lesion characteristics, brainstem lesion (OR 1.79, 95% CI 1.43–2.24) and cerebellar lesion (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.28–2.31) were associated with a markedly increased risk of PSD. In contrast, subcortical lesions (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.85) and higher education status (college or above: OR 0.55, 95% CI 0.43–0.70) appeared to be protective factors. Advanced age (≥ 60 years) was a strong risk factor (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.82–2.38). Comorbid dyslipidemia (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.54–0.90) and previous surgery of stroke (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.17–1.51) were also independently associated with PSD risk. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of PSD risk, highlighting the importance of stroke subtype, lesion location, age, and education level in clinical risk stratification (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analyses of all included variables (Fig. 4) demonstrated that during the acute phase, patients with SAH exhibited a markedly increased risk of PSD compared with those with IS (OR 6.35, 95% CI 1.21–47.93). Additionally, patients with cortical lesion had a significantly higher risk of PSD than those without cortical lesion (OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.27–2.90).

In the subacute and chronic phases, ICH was a strong risk factor for PSD, with odds ratios of 1.76 (95% CI 1.47–2.12) and 2.37 (95% CI 1.61–3.51), respectively. The subacute phase was characterized by an increased risk of PSD associated with brainstem lesion (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.46–2.61), cerebellar lesion (OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.31–2.81), and advanced age (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.93–2.69).

Discussion

In the current study, the overall prevalence of PSD among the 5,363 hospitalized stroke patients undergoing rehabilitation was 43.0%. This finding is highly consistent with the estimates reported in a previous meta-analysis (46.6%) and a large-scale epidemiological survey (38.7%) [17, 19]. These results underscore that PSD is not merely one of the most frequent functional impairments following a stroke, but also a critical clinical management issue that can compromise rehabilitation safety, delay the therapeutic process, and adversely affect long-term prognosis.

With respect to the disease course, the subacute phase contained the largest number of PSD cases (1,577 of 2,306; 68.4%). This pattern is likely related to the admission characteristics of the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, which primarily receives stroke patients who are relatively clinically stable and require ongoing rehabilitation interventions; consequently, inpatient rehabilitation mainly takes place during the subacute phase. When recalculated within each disease phase, the phase-specific prevalence of PSD was 44.7% in the acute phase and 46.2% in the subacute phase, suggesting broadly comparable prevalence between these two phases, while the prevalence in the chronic phase was relatively lower, which was generally in line with previous findings [15, 16]. From a clinical management perspective, because the number of patients was greater in the subacute phase, rehabilitation wards still bear a greater practical burden in PSD screening, assessment, and ongoing intervention. These findings suggest that dysphagia management should not be limited to acute stroke units, but should also be strengthened in rehabilitation wards through standardized screening, swallowing assessment, and continuity of care.

In the overall multi-variate analysis, hemorrhagic stroke, brainstem lesion, cerebellar lesion, advanced age, and previous surgery of stroke were independently associated with an increased risk of PSD, whereas higher education status and subcortical lesion acted as protective factors. From a pathophysiological perspective, the brainstem serves as a critical control center for initiating and temporally coordinating the swallowing reflex [20], while the cerebellum is involved in the modulation and coordination of swallowing-related motor activities [21, 22]. Therefore, it is biologically plausible that damage to these neurological structures significantly increases the risk of PSD. From a clinical management perspective, given that inpatient rehabilitation is a phase requiring continuous and long-term monitoring, these findings suggest that while clinical teams address biological risk factors, they must simultaneously strengthen health education for patients with lower educational levels and limited health literacy. Enhancing caregiver training is equally crucial to improve the early recognition and standardized management of PSD.

In the acute phase, SAH [23] and cortical lesion [24, 25] emerged as the primary risk factors for PSD. This finding aligns with the general consensus of previous studies, suggesting that the elevated prevalence of dysphagia during this period is largely driven by widespread neural suppression secondary to the primary brain lesions, particularly in the context of SAH-related complications such as hydrocephalus [26, 27]. Concurrently, the impact of cortical lesion during the acute phase likely manifests as impaired swallowing initiation and disrupted oropharyngeal motor programming [24, 28], thereby increasing the risk of early-onset dysphagia. Accordingly, acute-phase management must prioritize treatment safety rather than prematurely pursuing high-intensity functional training. For high-risk individuals, particularly those with SAH or cortical lesion, standardized bedside swallowing screening should be conducted as early as possible upon admission. Management protocols should primarily focus on airway protection, the prevention of aspiration pneumonia, and nutritional support to mitigate the risk of early complications.

In the subacute phase, ICH and brainstem lesion emerged as the principal risk factors for PSD. This shift suggests that as patients’ systemic conditions gradually stabilize, the primary determinants of swallowing recovery transition from short-term physiological fluctuations to structural damage within neural pathways. Specifically, brainstem lesion directly compromises the central nuclei and conduction pathways integral to the swallowing reflex, leading to more persistent deficits in pharyngeal-phase function [20, 29]. Conversely, patients with ICH may exhibit a sustained high risk during the subacute phase owing to mass effects, secondary brain lesions, and a characteristically protracted recovery trajectory [17, 30].

Given that 68.4% of the PSD cases in this cohort clustered in the subacute phase, this period must be recognized as a critical window for inpatient rehabilitation management. In terms of clinical strategy, it is imperative to strengthen multidisciplinary team (MDT) collaboration and formulate individualized intervention protocols tailored to specific lesion locations and functional profiles [14, 31].For patients with predominant brainstem involvement, rehabilitation interventions targeting pharyngeal-phase deficits, such as catheter balloon dilatation, neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), and specialized functional training, should be prioritized [32–34]. Concurrently, the frequency of clinical evaluations should be increased to dynamically monitor changes in swallowing function, thereby facilitating timely adjustments to the therapeutic regimen.

In the chronic phase, advanced age and ICH remained significant risk factors for PSD. This finding suggests that as the disease progresses, the primary drivers of PSD shift further from acute post-stroke injuries toward depleted long-term functional reserves and constrained recovery capacities. For older patients, reduced neuroplasticity, sarcopenia, and diminished oropharyngeal sensory and motor functions may collectively contribute to the persistence and chronicity of dysphagia. Meanwhile, the sustained impact of ICH indicates that a subset of patients continues to suffer from pronounced structural sequelae well into the chronic phase, leaving limited potential for further improvement in swallowing function.

Consequently, the primary objective of chronic-phase management should pivot from functional restoration to long-term care and the prevention of complications. Specifically, patient and caregiver education must be reinforced to provide standardized guidance on food texture modification and safe feeding practices, combined with compensatory swallowing postures when appropriate, to minimize adverse outcomes such as aspiration and malnutrition [35]. Furthermore, establishing a continuous care model that seamlessly bridges in-hospital rehabilitation with community-based follow-up is imperative. Providing ongoing monitoring of swallowing function, nutritional assessments, and caregiving support post-discharge can substantially mitigate the long-term risks of aspiration, malnutrition, and hospital readmission [36–38].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the study sample was obtained from a single medical institution. Future research could involve multi-center collaboration to improve the generalizability of the results. Secondly, since the rehabilitation department mainly admits patients in the subacute phase and the patients at different disease phases were not derived from the same cohort with continuous follow-up, this study may have certain selection biases. However, this clinical reality itself holds significant research value, and the empirical evidence based on actual distribution can provide better assistance in clinical practice. Third, standardized measures of stroke severity or functional status, such as the NIHSS, mRS, and FIM, were not available in the present study. This may have limited our ability to fully account for stroke severity as a potential confounder and may therefore have affected the interpretation of the findings. In future studies, we will further improve the continuous collection and standardized documentation of indicators related to stroke severity and functional status and incorporate these variables as important covariates into the analytical models, in order to improve the interpretability of the findings and to examine the stability of PSD risk factors across different disease phases. Fourth, treatment-related variables, including the type and frequency of swallowing rehabilitation interventions (e.g., NMES, balloon dilatation, and dietary texture modifications), were not systematically recorded and therefore could not be adjusted for in the analysis. These factors may influence the occurrence, persistence, and recovery of PSD. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized documentation of rehabilitation protocols to allow for more robust adjustment.

Conclusion

This study examined the prevalence and risk factors of PSD across different phases of the disease course among hospitalized stroke patients undergoing rehabilitation and found that, although the subacute phase accounted for the largest share of PSD cases, the within-phase prevalence of PSD was similar in the acute and subacute phases and lower in the chronic phase. These findings highlight that the determinants and burden of PSD are phase-specific rather than static over time. Future research should further refine and validate phase-based risk stratification models and develop personalized rehabilitation protocols tailored to the risk profiles of different stroke phases. Clinically, greater emphasis should be placed on early identification of high-risk patients, with timely bedside screening and close monitoring of swallowing function in the acute phase, intensive functional rehabilitation and targeted medical interventions in the subacute phase, and long-term chronic disease management and nutritional support in the chronic phase. Implementing such phase-specific strategies may help optimize swallowing outcomes, reduce dysphagia-related complications, and ultimately improve overall prognosis in stroke survivors.
