The value of an open biopsy in the diagnosis of periprosthetic joint infection
Authors: Adrik Z. Da Silva, Michael A. Moverman, Silvia M. Soule, Christopher D. Joyce, Robert Z. Tashjian, Peter N. Chalmers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDetermining the presence of bacteria in the shoulder prior to shoulder arthroplasty can be challenging especially in the case of revision arthroplasty. An open biopsy provides an opportunity to obtain tissue samples with minimal patient morbidity. The purpose of this study was to characterize the diagnostic utility of an open shoulder biopsy.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed at an academic medical center. All patients that underwent an open shoulder biopsy using a small proximal deltopectoral incision between 2008 and 2021 were included. Demographics, surgical history, culture results, and development of subsequent infection were recorded. Subsequent infection was defined as the development of a sinus tract, purulent drainage, or revision surgery with greater than or equal to two tissues specimens with growth of the same bacterial species. Sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV) of an open biopsy were calculated based on the development of subsequent infection. As culture positive patients were treated for their infection, positive predictive value and specificity could not be determined.ResultsWe identified 55 patients that underwent 75 open biopsies. Most patients had a shoulder arthroplasty in place at the time of biopsy (69.1%), while 23.6% had an antibiotic spacer, and 7.3% had a native shoulder. Patients with a history of infection were more likely to have a spacer in place at the time of biopsy (65% vs. 0%; P < .001). The sensitivity of an open biopsy was 60% and the NPV was 83% among patients with a shoulder arthroplasty with no history of infection to predict infection after revision arthroplasty. The sensitivity was 63% and NPV was 75% among patients with any history of shoulder infection. The sensitivity was 67% and the NPV was 83% among patients with an arthroplasty in place at the time of biopsy independent of prior infection. The sensitivity was 62% and the NPV was 75% among patients with an antibiotic spacer at the time of biopsy. Mean operative time was 32.2 ± 10.5 minutes.DiscussionThis diagnostic utility of an open shoulder biopsy is not influenced by whether there is a history of infection or whether there is currently a spacer or an arthroplasty in place, with a sensitivity between 60% and 67% and a NPV between 75% and 83%.

The incidence of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) after shoulder arthroplasty ranges between 0.7% and 7% and is influenced by both patient and surgical factors.2,5,15 Given the morbidity and mortality associated with PJI, increasing efforts have been placed on accurately identifying the presence of bacteria.1,3,9 However, reliable diagnosis of infection is difficult. Low virulence bacteria, in particular Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes), can lead to shoulder pain without any other overt signs of infection.7,10,11,13,20 Common inflammatory labs have been found to have poor sensitivity and specificity in identifying shoulder infections especially due to low virulent bacteria.12 Furthermore, studies have questioned the utility of a preoperative synovial fluid aspiration, given a discordance of 44% when compared to intraoperative biopsy in detecting shoulder PJI.19 Additionally, techniques such as intraoperative frozen section have been investigated, but have similarly been found to have a 50% sensitivity in detecting C. acnes infections.8

Given these concerns, arthroscopic shoulder biopsy has become increasingly utilized, given its previously reported sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value (NPV) to detect bacteria.4,17 However, arthroscopic biopsy is imperfect. First, the joint is irrigated as part of the arthroscopy, which could wash away bacteria. Indeed, irrigation is part of the treatment for infection. Second, surgeons often only send limited tissue samples because of the limited arthroscopic access. Third, visualization can be challenging, leading to a more extended procedure, with potentially more patient morbidity. To avoid these issues, one of the authors developed an operative technique for an open biopsy. It has been our experience that an open biopsy provides high quality tissue samples including bone samples (in the setting of a native shoulder), with relative operative ease, and minimal patient morbidity. The purpose of this study was to characterize the diagnostic utility of open shoulder biopsy in multiple settings including patients with and without a history of infection and those with and without an arthroplasty or an antibiotic spacer. We hypothesized that an open biopsy would provide adequate sensitivity (>60%) and NPV (>70%) to guide decision making in determining the presence of PJI regardless of the clinical setting.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was performed at a tertiary academic medical center. Patients that underwent an open shoulder biopsy between 2008 and 2023 by two fellowship trained shoulder and elbow surgeons were included. Patients with less than 3 months follow-up from time of biopsy, and those without recorded culture data were excluded. All shoulder biopsies were held for culture for 14 days. Results of the biopsy were compared to the development of subsequent clinical infection. Infection was defined as either the development of a sinus tract, purulent drainage, or revision surgery with greater than or equal to two tissue specimens with growth of the same bacterial species. This definition was utilized due to its generalizability and clinical ease in determining PJI, as well as its success in determining PJI in a prior study.18 In our practice, patients with positive biopsies underwent two-stage revision with an antibiotic spacer. Patients were categorized into groups based on a history of prior infection (infection vs. no infection) as well as implant at the time of biopsy (arthroplasty/antibiotic spacer/native shoulder). In cases when a patient underwent multiple shoulder biopsies, each biopsy was counted as an individual event.

Surgical technique

The patient was placed in the beach chair position and draped in standard fashion. A 3-centimeter incision was made through the proximal deltopectoral interval, centered over the coracoid (Fig. 1). Skin and soft tissue were dissected and the deltopectoral interval was dissected with electrocautery (Fig. 2). The deltoid was then retracted laterally while the pectoralis major was retracted medially, exposing the coracoid (Fig. 3). A triangle of tissue was then resected from the rotator interval. This triangle has sides of the upper rolled border of the subscapularis, the leading edge of the supraspinatus, and the coracoid lateral edge. In cases in which the rotator cuff was deficient, this triangle was between the coracoacromial ligament, deltoid, and coracoid/strap muscles. This was sent for culture and provided an arthrotomy into glenohumeral joint. Using a rongeur, biopsies were then taken from the superior capsule and anterior capsule. A sample was then taken from the anterosuperior glenoid using a rongeur. The humerus was then internally rotated and abducted, exposing the tuberosity, and a small sample was taken from the tuberosity (Fig. 4). All cultures were sent for aerobic and anaerobic cultures and held for 14 days. The wound was then thoroughly irrigated, and hemostasis was obtained. The deltopectoral interval was closed with #0 monofilament absorbable suture (Fig. 5). The skin was then closed using absorbable suture in two layers. Statistics

Descriptive statistics were calculated. Data was assumed to be normal if the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was insignificant. Parametric and nonparametric statistics were used as appropriate based on the results. P values less than .05 were considered significant. All analyses were conducted in Excel X (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and SPSS 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). NPV was calculated as the true negative count divided by the total true negative plus false negative count for each subgroup. In our case, the true negative was a biopsy that was culture negative and there was no recurrence of infection. The sum of this number was then divided by the total of culture negative samples in its respective subgroup. The sensitivity was calculated by dividing the true positive rate by the total of true positive plus false negatives. In our case, a true positive was considered as a positive culture that then met criteria for subsequent infection. This was divided by the total of culture positive samples in its respective subgroup. Due to the fact that almost all culture positive patients underwent treatment for their infection, positive predictive value and specificity were unable to be calculated.

Results

We identified 55 patients that underwent 75 open biopsies. Overall, 35 patients had a history of prior infection, while 20 patients had no history of infection at the time of biopsy. Most patients (38/55) had a shoulder arthroplasty in place at the time of biopsy (69.1%), while 23.6% (16/55) had a spacer, and 7.3% (4/55) had a native shoulder. Patients with a history of infection were more likely to have a spacer in place at the time of biopsy (65% vs. 0%; P < .001). Discussion

Predicting infection after revision shoulder arthroplasty in the setting of a failed arthroplasty or prior infection is challenging. Our study demonstrates that an open shoulder biopsy is an efficient and safe procedure with an adequate, if imperfect, sensitivity and NPV regardless of whether there is a history of prior infection or currently an implant or antibiotic spacer in place. While these results are lower than our previously reported results, our prior study was evaluating only painful shoulder arthroplasties without a history of prior infection. Overall, open biopsy is a reasonable procedure to provide further information regarding the likelihood of infection in patients with history of infection and native shoulder, patients with a history of infection and antibiotic spacer in placer, or revision shoulder arthroplasty after a failed shoulder arthroplasty with or without prior infection.

As the utilization of shoulder arthroplasty grows, techniques to reliably identify the presence of PJI will become increasingly important. While inflammatory markers and a synovial fluid aspiration are helpful in diagnosing PJI, reliance on these techniques alone may mis-categorize or incorrectly diagnose certain patients.6,12,19 As a result, arthroscopic biopsy has gained increasing attention and utilization, especially in cases that are not clinically clearly infected. While arthroscopic biopsy is reasonable,4 an open biopsy provides excellent tissue samples with relative operative ease, and minimal patient morbidity. Specifically, we have found that open biopsy allows us to obtain samples of bone (humeral and glenoid), as well as soft tissue samples that would be too large to fit through an arthroscopic portal, without first washing the samples with arthroscopic fluid, and thus potentially cleansing them of bacteria – which may reduce the sensitivity of the biopsy. Additionally, we have found that open biopsy is technically easier than an arthroscopic procedure – as in our series the mean operative time was 32 minutes. It also avoids the need for the use of all the arthroscopic equipment, and thus could be less expensive.

In our cohort we found that among patients with no history of infection, an open biopsy provides a sensitivity of 60% and NPV of 83%. For those with a history of prior infection, sensitivity was 63% and NPV was lower at 75%, likely because these patients tended to have an antibiotic spacer in place. Previously published sensitivity and NPV values of a prerevision biopsy are mixed and may be confounded by varying definitions of PJI that are utilized. For example, some studies have reported a prerevision open biopsy NPV rates ranging from 66.7% to 90.0%.4,16 Conversely, Dilisio et al reported 100% sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and NPV among a cohort of 19 patients that underwent arthroscopic biopsy to determine the presence of a bacteria at the time of revision arthroplasty.4 We previously reported the sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV of a biopsy of a noninfected shoulder arthroplasty to predict infection after revision arthroplasty of 0.9, 0.86, 0.9, and 0.86, respectively.16 Of note, all of these studies were smaller than the current study. Based upon these numbers, some surgeons may not feel that an open procedure is indicated and our hope is this data will be useful to future surgeons as they decide whether to biopsy patients undergoing a revision arthroplasty.

When comparing open biopsy to arthroscopic biopsy, Prujin et al reported a mini open biopsy sensitivity of 66.7% and NPV of 50% (25 patients) compared to sensitivity 60% and NPV of 75.0% for arthroscopic biopsy.14 Our data, which is to our knowledge the largest series of open shoulder biopsies, demonstrates a similar sensitivity, but an improved NPV compared to the results published by Prujin et al. Given the high NPV in our series (75%-83% depending on a history of prior infection), an open biopsy provides reasonable confidence to rule out infection. This is particularly important since oftentimes, in a shoulder that has undergone multiple surgeries, arthroscopic visualization is difficult due to scar tissue formation. However, an open biopsy also has risks. Many patients in whom an open biopsy would be considered have poor superficial tissues and/or a wound that has been reopened multiple times. Reopening this wound for an open biopsy may increase the risk for wound healing complications, wound drainage, or super-infection. In the author’s experience, this procedure does require delicate handling of the soft tissues and a tight wound closure to avoid these potential complications.

An additional finding of our study is that an open biopsy performs similarly well as a diagnostic tool when performed in the setting of an existing arthroplasty or antibiotic spacer. We found that the sensitivity was 67% and the NPV was 83% when a biopsy was performed in the setting of an arthroplasty. Likewise, the sensitivity was 62% and the NPV was 75% when an antibiotic spacer was in place. No prior studies have compared the diagnostic accuracy of a shoulder biopsy between patients with antibiotic spacers with known prior infection vs. arthroplasty. Our data suggests that surgeons can be equally confident when interpreting the results of a biopsy, irrespective of the presence of an arthroplasty or antibiotic spacer.

The primary strengths of our study are the large patient cohort and the variety of implants (antibiotic spacer/arthroplasty) studied as well as patients with and without a history of prior infection. However, our study is not without limitations. First, this study is a retrospective study. Second, the varying definitions of infection and reinfection in the literature vary, making it difficult to compare diagnostic utility across studies. Third, the 15-year enrollment window is large; however, the surgical technique did not change greatly during this time. Fourth, although the sample sizes in the subgroup analyses are small, the NPV and sensitivity are comparable to other reported values. Fifth, we did not include culture data which could influence antibiotic choice and reinfection rates. Sixth, the heterogenous settings in which the biopsies were performed is a limitation; however, this reflects the varied use of this procedure and maximizes the generalizability of our outcomes. Further limitations are the lack of a power analysis and an independent aspiration on every patient. Lastly, we only included short-term follow-up, and thus it is possible patients may have developed a subsequent infection after three months.

Conclusion

This diagnostic utility of an open shoulder biopsy is not influenced by whether there is a history of infection or whether there is currently a spacer or an arthroplasty in place, with a sensitivity between 60% and 67% and a NPV between 75% and 83%.
