Prevalence and Association of Autoimmune Comorbidities Among Adults with Vitiligo: A Systematic Literature Review and Meta-analysis of USA-Based Studies
Authors: Jinan Liu, Sudheer Matangi, Yogitha Malempati, Adnan Nasir, David Rosmarin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVitiligo is an autoimmune, depigmenting skin disease affecting 0.1–1.6% of adults in the USA. Despite observed correlations between vitiligo and other autoimmune conditions in global patient populations, a focused review of comorbidities in US patients with vitiligo is lacking. This systematic literature review and meta-analysis evaluated the prevalence of autoimmune comorbidities in adult patients with vitiligo in the USA.MethodsA systematic literature search of the PubMed and Embase electronic databases (January 1, 2012–November 30, 2022) selected observational studies reporting data on prevalence of autoimmune diseases in US patients with vitiligo. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach for prognosis was applied to assess certainty of evidence.ResultsEight studies were eligible for inclusion, encompassing 10,246 patients with vitiligo. Thyroid diseases (14.2%), psoriasis (5.1%), rheumatoid arthritis (3.2%), and alopecia areata (2.7%) were among the most common comorbidities by pooled prevalence rates, with the highest certainty of evidence for associations with thyroid disease and alopecia areata. In addition, there was high or moderate certainty of evidence that greater vitiligo extent is associated with increasing prevalence of thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and pernicious anemia.ConclusionsThere is clear evidence of correlations between vitiligo and select autoimmune comorbidities in US adults despite the limitations of this study, including the small number of available high-quality studies. The findings presented here demonstrate the importance of future longitudinal studies to identify causal links between vitiligo and comorbidities, and to evaluate potential benefits of screening and early management for thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and other autoimmune comorbidities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-025-01506-y.Keywords: Autoimmune, Comorbidity, Meta-analysis, Skin disease, Vitiligo

Key Summary Points

Vitiligo is correlated with autoimmune comorbidities in various geographic regions worldwide.This systematic literature review identified eight observational studies evaluating prevalence of autoimmune comorbidities among adults with vitiligo in the USA.Vitiligo is associated with several autoimmune comorbidities, with the highest certainty of evidence for associations with thyroid disease and alopecia areata.Vitiligo extent is associated with an increased prevalence of autoimmune comorbidities, including thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes mellitus, rheumatoid arthritis, and pernicious anemia.

Introduction

Vitiligo is a chronic autoimmune skin disease affecting 0.1–1.6% of US adults, regardless of ethnicity, sex, or skin type [1–4]. The disease presents with progressively depigmenting skin lesions that form as a result of autoimmune targeting and destruction of melanocytes [5]. Patients with vitiligo may experience significant quality-of-life impairment and are at increased risk for various comorbidities that can further increase the burden of the disease [6–10]. In particular, patients with vitiligo are at risk for psychiatric (including depression and anxiety) [8–10] and other autoimmune diseases, with a recent systematic literature review and meta-analysis of global patient populations identifying thyroid diseases, alopecia areata, rheumatoid arthritis, and pernicious anemia among the most common and of highest risk for patients with vitiligo [6, 11]. However, although studies have reported correlations between vitiligo and autoimmune conditions in global patient populations [6, 12–14], a contemporary and comprehensive systematic review of comorbidities in US patients with vitiligo is lacking. This report summarizes findings from a systematic literature review and meta-analysis of observational studies to determine prevalence of autoimmune diseases in US adults with vitiligo and characterize associations between vitiligo and autoimmune comorbidities.

Methods

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Search Strategy

A systematic literature search of PubMed and Embase was conducted for articles published between January 1, 2012 and November 30, 2022. Relevant studies were identified with a combination of National Library of Medicine’s medical subject headings and other keywords specific to the comorbidities of interest. Searches were limited to articles published in the English language. Complete search strings for PubMed and Embase are available in Supplementary Table S1. To supplement the PubMed and Embase literature searches, bibliographic hand searching of systematic reviews that assessed vitiligo comorbidities was also performed. The study design and analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines.

Study Eligibility Criteria and Selection

Observational studies (including cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies) providing data on the incidence or prevalence of autoimmune diseases in US adults (≥ 18 years old) with vitiligo of any extent were included. Comparative studies were eligible for inclusion, with acceptable comparators including the general population, healthy controls, and adults without vitiligo.

Data Extraction

The extraction of data from all included studies was done using structured data tables. Data extraction was initially performed by an independent methodologist, with subsequent quality control performed by additional independent reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the original data extractor and the independent reviewer.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The included studies were assessed for bias using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS; cohort and case–control studies) [15] or a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (mNOS; cross-sectional studies) [16]. The risk of bias assessment was completed by an independent methodologist, with subsequent quality control by independent reviewers. The NOS and mNOS assess individual studies across three broad categories and award stars per the scoring system: selection (maximum score, 4 stars for NOS and 5 for mNOS), comparability (maximum score, 2 stars for NOS and mNOS), and exposure (maximum score, 3 stars for NOS) or outcomes (maximum score, 3 stars for mNOS). A total score of 7 to 9 indicates a low risk of bias (i.e., high quality), a score of 4 to 6 indicates a high risk of bias, and a score of 1 to 3 indicates a very high risk of bias (i.e., low quality). Use of the NOS and mNOS, considered the industry standard for assessing risk of bias in systematic literature reviews (SLRs), allowed a consistent set of assessment criteria and a common scoring interpretation to be applied to the cohort and cross-sectional studies identified in this review.

Evidence Synthesis and Statistics

Meta-analysis was conducted using Open Meta-Analyst software (Brown University, Providence, RI, USA). Crude prevalence data were pooled from studies that listed the number of patients with vitiligo and the number with vitiligo and a comorbid autoimmune disease. In addition, comparator studies were required to report the total number of reference individuals and the number of reference individuals with a comorbid autoimmune disease. Individual estimates were pooled using a random-effects model and the method of DerSimonian and Laird [17]. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Higgins I2 value and the chi-square test. A Higgins I2 value ≥ 50% and P values < 0.05 were considered to represent significant heterogeneity.

Assessment of Certainty of Evidence

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach for prognosis was used to assess the overall certainty of the evidence for each outcome [18, 19] and to formulate evidence-based statements on the association between vitiligo and autoimmune comorbidities. The GRADEPro Guideline Development Tool (Evidence Prime, Hamilton, ON, Canada) was used to create evidence profiles that categorized certainty evidence into one of four categories: high, moderate, low, or very low.

Formulating Statements of Association

Statements regarding the association between vitiligo and comorbid conditions were drafted based on the evidence profiles, the strength of the estimated association between vitiligo and the selected comorbid condition, and the overall certainty of the evidence of association. The drafted statements were then reviewed by a separate team member to reach a consensus on the statements of association.

Results

Identified Studies

The literature search identified 5783 publications through PubMed, 1668 records through Embase, and 4 publications through manual search (Fig. 1). After removal of duplicates and studies with publication dates before January 2012, 3104 records were eligible for abstract and title screening, of which 226 full-text articles were assessed further for eligibility. Most publications excluded at abstract and title screening were because the study outcomes or study population were not of interest and therefore could not provide meaningful data toward the predefined objectives of this literature review. A total of 15 publications met the full eligibility criteria for quantitative synthesis. Of these, seven were excluded from this analysis for not reporting data on associations between vitiligo and autoimmune comorbidities. The remaining eight publications met all selection criteria and included 10,246 unique patients [9, 20–26]. Six records were cross-sectional studies [9, 20–23, 25], and two were cohort studies [24, 26] (Table 1). Overall, four studies were rated as high quality [20, 21, 23, 24], three as moderate [9, 25, 26], and one as low quality [22] (Table 1, Supplementary Tables S2 and S3); however, no study was excluded on the basis of the quality assessment.

Seven studies examined autoimmune comorbidities in patients with vitiligo [9, 20–25], and the remaining study examined vitiligo as a comorbid disease in patients with alopecia areata [26]. The most common comorbidities across all studies by pooled prevalence estimates (occurring in > 3% of patients) were thyroid diseases (14.2%), psoriasis (5.1%), and rheumatoid arthritis (3.2%; Table 2). On the basis of GRADE assessment, there is a high certainty of evidence that greater vitiligo extent is associated with increasing prevalence of hypothyroidism and type 1 diabetes (Table 3). There is also moderate certainty of evidence that vitiligo in adults is associated with alopecia areata and hyperthyroidism, and that greater vitiligo extent is associated with increasing prevalence of thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and pernicious anemia.

Thyroid Diseases

The pooled prevalence of thyroid disease in adults with vitiligo was 13.6% (95% CI 8.9–19.2) but with substantial heterogeneity across studies (n = 7; I2 = 98.1%) [9, 20–25]. The individual pooled prevalences of hypothyroidism (including Hashimoto’s thyroiditis) and hyperthyroidism (including Graves’ disease) were 9.9% (9.1–10.7%; n = 4; I2 = 0%) and 2.1% (1.6–2.6%; n = 2; I2 = 0%), respectively [20, 21, 23, 25]. In addition to reporting crude prevalence values, one included study calculated the risk of developing thyroid disease [25]. This cross-sectional study showed that adults with vitiligo are associated with significantly increased odds of developing hypothyroidism compared with the general population (odds ratio [95% CI] 4.02 [3.29–4.88]; P < 0.05).

Three included studies also reported data indicating that thyroid disease prevalence may vary across patient subgroups. A cross-sectional study reported that hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism were associated with older age and more extensive vitiligo (measured by affected body surface area, number of body parts involved, and bilaterality), disease duration, and vitiligo-associated itching and burning [22]. In addition, subgroup analyses from two additional cross-sectional studies revealed that thyroid disease was more prevalent in female versus male patients with vitiligo (hypothyroidism, 88% vs 25% [20]; thyroid disease, 18.5% vs 5.1% [P < 0.001] [21]).

Psoriasis

The pooled prevalence of psoriasis in adults with vitiligo was 5.1% (95% CI 2.3–7.9), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (n = 4; I2 = 97.0%) [21–24]. Furthermore, associations between vitiligo and psoriasis may depend on patient race. One included cross-sectional study reported that psoriasis occurred significantly less frequently in Black patients with vitiligo compared with patients of White or other races (P = 0.021) [21].

Rheumatoid Arthritis

The pooled prevalence of rheumatoid arthritis among adults with vitiligo was 3.2% (95% CI 1.7–4.6), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (n = 6; I2 = 95.8%) [9, 20–24]. Rheumatoid arthritis prevalence may also differ across patient subgroups on the basis of evidence reported by two included cross-sectional studies. The first study reported that rheumatoid arthritis was observed in a higher percentage of female patients with vitiligo (2.1%) than male (0.9%) [20]. The second study found that rheumatoid arthritis was associated with older age and more extensive, long-standing, or active vitiligo (based on affected body surface area, disease duration, and vitiligo-associated itching and burning) [22].

Alopecia Areata

The pooled prevalence of alopecia areata among adults with vitiligo was 2.7% (95% CI 2.3–3.1), with low heterogeneity (n = 5; I2 = 13.2%) [9, 21–24]. In addition to reporting crude prevalence values, one included study calculated the risk of developing alopecia areata [26]. The prospective cohort study showed that adults with vitiligo have significantly increased risk of developing alopecia areata compared with patients who have no history of autoimmune disease (multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio [HR] [95% CI] 3.13 [1.08–9.10]; P < 0.05). However, alopecia areata was not shown to be associated with vitiligo extent [22]. The association between vitiligo and alopecia areata is also dependent on patient race, with one included cross-sectional study indicating that alopecia areata occurred less frequently in White patients with vitiligo compared with patients of Black or other races (P = 0.024) [21].

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

The pooled prevalence of type 1 diabetes mellitus in adults with vitiligo was 1.8% (95% CI 0.9–2.8), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (n = 5; I2 = 92.8%) [9, 21–24]. No association was identified between vitiligo and type 1 diabetes mellitus on the basis of specific patient or disease characteristics [9, 21–24].

Pernicious Anemia

The pooled prevalence of anemia or pernicious anemia in adults with vitiligo is 1.7% (95% CI 0.6–3.3), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (n = 5; I2 = 94.9%) [9, 20–22, 24]. In addition, one included study assessed the effects of vitiligo extent (as measured by affected body surface area, number of body parts involved, and bilaterality), reporting that pernicious anemia was associated with more extensive vitiligo lesions [22].

Chronic Urticaria

The pooled prevalence of chronic urticaria in adult patients with vitiligo is 1.6% (95% CI 0.7–2.5), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (n = 2; I2 = 80.6%) [9, 22]. No association was identified between vitiligo and chronic urticaria on the basis of specific patient or disease characteristics [9, 22].

Inflammatory Bowel Disease

The pooled prevalence of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in vitiligo was 1.4% (95% CI 0.8–2.1), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (n = 6; I2 = 85.3%) [9, 20–24]. In addition to heterogeneity among included studies, a cohort study that did not meet the meta-analysis selection criteria reported no association between vitiligo and an increased risk of IBD, including ulcerative colitis (HR [95% CI] 1.14 [0.77–1.68]) and Crohn’s disease (HR [95% CI] 1.45 [0.87–2.41]) [27].

Other Autoimmune Associations

Vitiligo is also associated with other comorbidities with a pooled prevalence of less than 1.5%, including systemic lupus erythematosus (1.2% [126/9750]), Sjögren’s syndrome (1.1% [67/5822]), celiac disease (0.9% [≤ 43/4724]), Addison’s disease (0.8% [38/4724]), multiple sclerosis (0.3% [8/2585]), and myasthenia gravis (0.2% [6/2585]) [9, 20–24]. In a cross-sectional study, systemic lupus erythematosus was only observed in Black patients; however, statistical significance was not reached versus other races [21]. In a prospective cross-sectional survey, Sjögren’s syndrome was associated with more extensive vitiligo (based on affected body surface area) and abdominal lesions [22]. No further associations based on patient or disease characteristics were identified between vitiligo and these other autoimmune conditions [9, 20–24].

Discussion

This systematic literature review identified several autoimmune comorbidities associated with vitiligo or vitiligo extent in US patient populations, with the highest level of certainty for thyroid disorders and type 1 diabetes mellitus. The autoimmune comorbidity profile identified here was generally consistent with that described for broader geographic regions in a global meta-analysis [6]. This association between vitiligo and other autoimmune diseases may arise from overlap in genetic risk factors. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified at least 50 genetic loci that contribute to vitiligo risk [28], of which at least 19 are risk factors for other autoimmune diseases. In a recent Mendelian randomization study, genetic predisposition to vitiligo (based on these 50 loci) was associated with increased risk for autoimmune hypothyroidism, psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, pernicious anemia, and alopecia areata, with disruption of multiple metabolic pathways potentially accounting for these associations [29]. These findings are consistent with the autoimmune comorbidity profile identified in the meta-analysis reported here.

Thyroid disease was the most common autoimmune comorbidity identified in the included studies, with a prevalence rate nearly three times greater than in the general US adult population (5.1%) [30]. Notably, hypothyroid disorders (which also included Hashimoto’s thyroiditis) were reported in a tenth of patients with vitiligo in the current meta-analysis, whereas overt hypothyroidism is relatively rare in the general US population across all age groups (0.3%) [31]. The association between hypothyroidism and vitiligo is supported by a second recent Mendelian randomization analysis of GWAS data, which found a positive causal relationship between genetically increased risk for vitiligo and autoimmune thyroiditis, autoimmune hyperthyroidism, and Graves’ disease [25, 32]. The studies included in this meta-analysis also indicate a relatively high prevalence of hyperthyroidism in vitiligo, 10 times higher than the US prevalence of overt hyperthyroidism across all age groups (0.2%) [31]. The two included studies in this meta-analysis reported all cases of hyperthyroidism, regardless of whether the disease had autoimmune etiology (e.g., Graves’ disease) or not [21, 23]. Nevertheless, the identified association between vitiligo and hyperthyroidism is consistent with results of a global meta-analysis, which found a similar association between vitiligo and hyperthyroidism [6]. Both thyroid disease and vitiligo are linked to aberrant interferon-γ activity [33, 34], which may represent a pathophysiological mechanism underlying the association between these conditions. Taken together, it is recommended that patients with vitiligo, especially those with extensive disease, be screened for thyroid disease [20, 22]. This recommendation is consistent with guidelines for the management of people with vitiligo [35].

The prevalence of rheumatoid arthritis determined in this meta-analysis was 10 times higher than that found in the general US population (0.3%) [36]. Vitiligo and rheumatoid arthritis have 12 immune-related genetic susceptibility loci in common [28], and a third recent Mendelian randomization analysis found a causal relationship between genetically predicted vitiligo and a higher risk for rheumatoid arthritis [37]. Thus, it may be prudent to examine older patients with extensive vitiligo of longer duration for signs of comorbid rheumatoid arthritis [22].

The prevalence of pernicious anemia determined in this analysis is at least 15 times higher than prevalence in the general population of the USA (0.1%) [38], consistent with the association between vitiligo and pernicious anemia identified in a previous global meta-analysis [6]. A GWAS found four loci associated with both pernicious anemia and vitiligo, including two associated with hypothyroidism and one with autoimmune thyroid conditions [39]. Patients with vitiligo who present with anemia and those with extensive vitiligo of longer duration should be screened for deficiencies in folic acid and vitamin B12 as well as presence of antigastric parietal cell antibodies [22, 40].

In this meta-analysis, prevalence of alopecia areata was 13 times higher than in the US adult population (0.2%) [41], and vitiligo is associated with increased risk for developing alopecia areata [26]. The higher prevalence of alopecia areata among patients with vitiligo supports a commonality in the underlying pathogenesis of these two diseases [21]. Vitiligo and alopecia areata both result from increased reactive oxygen species and a high cellular stress level, with interferon–γ-mediated immune responses driving pathogenesis [42].

The prevalence of psoriasis was two-thirds higher than that reported for the general US adult population (3.0%) [43]. This finding is consistent with a previous global meta-analysis that found higher prevalence of psoriasis among patients with vitiligo; however, a Mendelian randomization analysis did not find any evidence of a causal relationship [6, 44]. Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease with clinical phenotypes that range on a spectrum from generalized pustular psoriasis (autoinflammatory) to plaque psoriasis (autoimmune) [45]. Taking into consideration the results of the previous global meta-analysis and the heterogeneity of the disease and data, it cannot be stated with certainty whether an association between vitiligo and psoriasis exists.

The prevalence of type 1 diabetes mellitus in this meta-analysis was nearly four times higher than in the general US adult population (0.5%) [46], consistent with findings from the previous global meta-analysis [6]. The pathogeneses of vitiligo and type 1 diabetes mellitus similarly involve both oxidative stress and cytotoxic T cell–mediated destruction [47, 48]. In addition, 10 immune-related genetic loci are known to be associated with both vitiligo and type 1 diabetes mellitus, contributing to a significant association between the two conditions in a Mendelian randomization analysis [28, 29].

There is mixed evidence to support an association between vitiligo and IBD in the literature [49, 50]. Prevalence of IBD in this meta-analysis was two-thirds higher than the overall US adult self-reported prevalence (1.2%) [51]. However, the global meta-analysis did not identify an association between vitiligo and IBD [6], and a recent cohort study based on US insurance claims data did not identify a significant association between vitiligo and either ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease [27]. Thus, we cannot state with certainty whether IBD is associated with vitiligo, and routine screening for IBD is not recommended for patients with vitiligo unless suggestive symptomatology is present [22].

Chronic urticaria prevalence was eight times higher than the US general population prevalence of 0.2% [52]. This finding is consistent with a previous global meta-analysis, which found that patients with vitiligo had increased risk for chronic urticaria [6]. However, only two studies included in the meta-analysis reported here identified chronic urticaria as a comorbidity, and the results were highly heterogeneous. Thus, although there is evidence of an association between vitiligo and chronic urticaria, it cannot be stated for certain that such an association is present.

Despite substantial research on vitiligo-associated autoimmune comorbidities through epidemiologic studies described above, there is currently a lack of conclusive evidence demonstrating that screening for autoimmune comorbidities and early management of these comorbidities improve patient outcomes. Therefore, autoimmune comorbidities should be considered on a case-by-case basis when managing vitiligo. With that goal in mind, dermatologists should discuss with patients how various autoimmune comorbidities relate to vitiligo to help patients better understand their skin condition and general health, and they should support their contribution to shared decision-making, including decisions around screening for comorbidities. Dermatologists should encourage patients to consult with primary care physicians about autoimmune comorbidities that are outside of the dermatologist’s expertise. By doing so, dermatologists can actively contribute to the general well-being and health-related quality of life of patients with vitiligo. Moreover, dermatologists should take a holistic approach to treating patients with vitiligo and additional comorbidities. For example, oral Janus kinase inhibitors may provide benefit in treating vitiligo and other autoimmune comorbidities of an inflammatory nature [53, 54].

Interpretation of this analysis is limited by the number of eligible studies, impacting the precision of the pooled prevalence analysis. Despite the autoimmune nature of vitiligo, the systematic literature review uncovered few US studies that examined associations between vitiligo and other autoimmune comorbidities. Furthermore, the small number of high-quality studies (i.e., those with a low risk of bias) limits the robustness and reliability of the findings presented here. However, the risk of bias assessment does suggest most of the identified studies include a representative target population, suggesting the findings are generalizable to the wider US population of people with vitiligo. The heterogeneity of study design, comorbidity definitions, and comorbidity reporting, as well as significant heterogeneity of comorbidity prevalence across studies, make comparisons across studies challenging. There may also be a small-study effect regarding comorbidities reported in an insufficient number of studies, with wide variation of sample size. The small-study effect describes the occurrence of different, often larger, treatment effects being reported in smaller compared to larger studies, which can negatively affect the validity of systematic reviews [55].

Conclusion

There is substantial evidence that vitiligo is associated with multiple other autoimmune diseases in the US patient population, emphasizing the autoimmune-pathogenic nature of vitiligo. On the basis of the findings presented here, we recommend that healthcare providers offer screening for thyroid disease in patients with vitiligo, particularly those with extensive disease.

Given the economic burden of autoimmune comorbidities associated with vitiligo, a deeper understanding of these correlations is crucial for more efficient management of vitiligo and associated comorbidities, for reducing associated costs, and for minimizing the overall disease burden for patients. Future longitudinal studies are needed to identify causal links between vitiligo and other autoimmune comorbidities including underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, as well as to assess the impact of screening and early management of autoimmune comorbidities in patients with vitiligo.
