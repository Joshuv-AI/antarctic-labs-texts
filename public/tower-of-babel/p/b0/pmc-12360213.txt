Proteins that recognize unique features of U7 snRNA and may substitute for Gemin5 in the assembly of U7-specific Sm ring
Authors: Xiao-cui Yang, Zbigniew Dominski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractU7 snRNA is a 60 nucleotide component of U7 snRNP, a multisubunit endonuclease that cleaves precursors of metazoan replication-dependent histone mRNAs at the 3′ end, hence generating mature histone mRNAs. The Sm site in U7 snRNA differs from the Sm site in spliceosomal snRNAs and promotes the assembly of a unique Sm ring containing Lsm10 and Lsm11 instead of the spliceosomal SmD1 and SmD2 proteins. While the spliceosomal-type Sm site is recognized by Gemin5, a subunit of the SMN complex, the identity of the protein that recognizes the unusual Sm site of U7 snRNA resulting in the incorporation of Lsm10 and Lsm11 has not been determined. Here, we looked for proteins in mammalian extracts that interact with U7 snRNA and identified polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1) and insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3) as two major proteins with this characteristic. The binding of PTBP1 and IGF2BP3 to U7 snRNA depends on its unique Sm site and on the upstream CUCUUU motif that base-pairs with histone pre-mRNAs and defines substrate specificity of U7 snRNP. Among proteins that bind U7 snRNA, we also identified hnRNP A1. We show that hnRNP A1 interacts with the SMN protein of the SMN complex, a likely prerequisite for the protein that substitutes for Gemin5 in the assembly of U7-specific Sm ring. Our results also suggest a mechanism that explains why Gemin5 does not bind the Sm site of U7 snRNA.Keywords: U7 snRNP, SMN, Lsm10/11, Gemin5, PTBP1, hnRNP A1

INTRODUCTION

In metazoans, 3′ end processing of replication-dependent histone pre-mRNAs does not follow the two-step pathway of cleavage coupled to polyadenylation and instead involves a single-step cleavage, yielding mature histone mRNAs terminated with a highly conserved stem–loop structure (Dominski and Marzluff 2007). The cleavage reaction is conducted by U7 snRNP, a multicomponent endonuclease consisting of U7 snRNA and several proteins, including symplekin, CPSF100, and CPSF73, which is the catalytic component (Dominski et al. 2005; Sun et al. 2020; Dominski and Tong 2021).

The substrate specificity of U7 snRNP is provided by U7 snRNA, an ∼60 nucleotide RNA whose 5′ region base-pairs with a purine-rich region in histone pre-mRNAs known as histone downstream element (HDE) (Schaufele et al. 1986; Bond et al. 1991). Centrally located within U7 snRNA is the Sm binding site of 11 nucleotides with the AAUUUGUCUAG consensus sequence that nucleates the assembly of a ring composed of seven proteins: Lsm10, Lsm11, SmE, SmF, SmG, SmB, and SmD3 (Schumperli and Pillai 2004; Khusial et al. 2005). This sequence differs from the 9 nucleotide spliceosomal consensus, AAUUUUGG, which promotes the assembly of the canonical Sm ring containing Sm proteins D1 and D2 instead of Lsm10 and Lsm11. Lsm11, the largest protein of the Sm/Lsm family, interacts with FLASH, a protein of 220 kDa, and these two components act together to recruit symplekin, CPSF100, and CPSF73 to U7 snRNP, specifying its function as an endonuclease (Yang et al. 2011, 2013).

The assembly of the canonical heptameric ring on spliceosomal snRNPs occurs through a multistep process controlled by the SMN complex. The SMN complex consists of nine proteins: SMN, Gemin2-8, and Unrip (Meister and Fischer 2002; Battle et al. 2006a; Neuenkirchen et al. 2008; Li et al. 2014; Gruss et al. 2017). Within the complex, Gemin5 recognizes the Sm site in spliceosomal snRNAs (Battle et al. 2006b; Lau et al. 2009; Yong et al. 2010), whereas the SMN protein binds Sm subunits and facilitates their orderly transfer onto the Sm site (Friesen et al. 2001; Selenko et al. 2001; Cote and Richard 2005; Tripsianes et al. 2011). How exactly these steps occur and what role is played by the remaining subunits of the SMN complex is unknown.

The assembly of the unique Sm ring of U7 snRNP is also controlled by the SMN protein (Pillai et al. 2003; Paknia et al. 2016). RNAi-mediated downregulation of this component of the SMN complex results in a gradual disappearance of mature U7 snRNP, accumulation of a longer U7 snRNA precursor and a defect in 3′ end processing of histone pre-mRNAs (Tisdale et al. 2013, 2022). However, the distinct sequence of the Sm site in U7 snRNA is not recognized by Gemin5 (Battle et al. 2006b). Thus, in the assembly of U7 snRNP, this component of the SMN complex is likely replaced by another RNA binding protein, which may in turn mediate the recruitment of Lsm10 and Lsm11 instead of SmD1 and SmD2.

Here, we conducted biochemical experiments to identify proteins in mammalian cell extracts that bind wild-type U7 snRNA but fail to bind mutant U7 snRNA whose Sm site was converted to the spliceosomal consensus. Our studies identified polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1) (Garcia-Blanco et al. 1989; Ghetti et al. 1992) and insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3) (Nielsen et al. 2002) as proteins displaying this binding specificity. Importantly, the interaction of these two proteins with U7 snRNA was abolished not only by changing the Sm site, but also by mutations within the highly conserved pyrimidine motif that base-pairs with histone pre-mRNAs and facilitates the recruitment of these substrates to U7 snRNP. Thus, PTBP1 and IGF2BP3 can simultaneously recognize the two U7 snRNA features that are critically important for the function of the U7 snRNP in 3′end processing. In addition to PTBP1 and IGF2BP3, U7 snRNA interacts with hnRNP A1 (Mayeda et al. 1994; Jean-Philippe et al. 2013). This protein recognizes the UAG trinucleotide (Kashima and Manley 2003; Beusch et al. 2017), a different characteristic feature of the U7-specific Sm site conspicuously absent from the spliceosomal Sm sites. We show that hnRNP A1 interacts with SMN, providing a potential link to other subunits of the SMN complex and the Sm ring assembly machinery.

RESULTS

Proteins that bind U7 snRNA

Mature human U7 snRNA consists of 60–63 nucleotides and three functional/structural regions. The sequence of nucleotides 12–17 is highly conserved among distant metazoan species and contains the CUCUUU core, referred here to as the pyrimidine (Py) site (Fig. 1A). The Py site forms a duplex with histone downstream element (HDE), a purine-rich sequence in histone pre-mRNA located downstream from the site cleaved by the U7 snRNP (Cotten et al. 1988; Bond et al. 1991). The formation of this duplex is the primary mechanism that defines substrate specificity of U7 snRNP. Nucleotides 21–31 encompass the Sm site, which mediates the assembly of the U7-specific Sm ring consisting of Lsm10, Lsm11, and the five proteins shared with the spliceosomal snRNPs: SmB, SmD3, SmE, SmF, and SmG (Schumperli and Pillai 2004). The 3′ half of U7 snRNA folds into an extended stem–loop structure. U7 snRNA is synthesized by RNA polymerase II as a longer transcript, which is cleaved cotranscriptionally by the integrator complex (Baillat et al. 2005; Egloff et al. 2008), yielding U7 snRNA precursor (pre-snRNA) extended at the 3′ end by ∼35 nucleotides (Tisdale et al. 2013). This precursor serves as the substrate for the assembly of the Sm ring, with the extended tail being exonucleolytically removed prior to importing the U7 snRNP to the nucleus (Matera et al. 2007).

We carried out experiments to address one of the most outstanding questions in the biogenesis of U7 snRNP: how the specific Sm site of U7 snRNA is recognized, ultimately resulting in the incorporation of Lsm10/11 heterodimer and preventing an erroneous incorporation of the more abundant SmD1/D2 heterodimer that occupies the same position in the spliceosomal ring. To identify proteins that recognize the U7-specific Sm site and may act as the functional counterpart of Gemin5, we incubated human cell extracts with mouse U7 snRNA precursor containing a 35 nucleotide 3′ tail (mU7/WT pre-snRNA). This RNA was generated by T7 transcription and modified at the 5′ end by a cap structure to mimic in vivo generated U7 snRNA assembly substrate. At the 3′ end, mU7/WT pre-snRNA was terminated with an adapter sequence complementary to a 22-mer containing a photocleavable linker and biotin at the 5′ end (Supplemental Fig. S1A). Proteins bound to the duplex RNA formed by annealing mU7/WT pre-snRNA and 22mer were immobilized on streptavidin beads, eluted with UV and analyzed by silver staining, western blotting, and/or mass spectrometry, as previously described (Skrajna et al. 2018, 2019). As a control to the mU7/WT pre-snRNA, we used mU7/Sm mutant RNA in which the U7-specific Sm site was changed to match that in U4 snRNA (Supplemental Fig. S1A).

As determined by mass spectrometry, mU7/Sm pre-snRNA incubated with a HeLa cell lysate bound to Gemin5 (UniProt ID Q8TEQ6), which migrated as a strong silver-stained band just below the 185 kDa size marker. In addition, global mass spectrometry analysis of the entire eluted material identified Gemin4 (UniProt ID P57678) and Gemin3 (also known as DDX20, UniProt ID Q9UHI6) (Supplemental Fig. S1B). In other experiments carried out with the use of different batches of HeLa extracts, the proteome bound to mU7/Sm pre-snRNA also contained SMN and Gemin2 (not shown). However, in most experiments, Gemin5 was the only subunit of the SMN complex detected by silver staining and mass spectrometry. Altogether, our results confirmed previous studies using less direct approaches that RNAs containing a spliceosomal-type Sm site interact with Gemin5 and that this interaction does not require any other subunits of the SMN complex (Battle et al. 2006b).

Importantly, mU7/WT pre-snRNA failed to interact with Gemin5 (Supplemental Fig. S1C,D), consistent with previous results that the unique Sm site in U7 snRNA has no affinity for Gemin5 (Battle et al. 2006b). To our knowledge, this is the first direct demonstration of endogenous Gemin5 discriminating between the two types of snRNAs and a clear indication that the recognition of the Sm site in U7 snRNA is mediated by a protein different than Gemin5. Instead of Gemin5, mU7/WT pre-snRNA interacted with cap binding protein of 80 kDa (CBP80, UniProt ID Q09161) and cap binding protein of 20 kDa (CBP20, UniProt ID P52298), the two subunits of the cap binding complex (Supplemental Fig. S1C,D). These two proteins were detected in trace amounts in the material bound to mU7/Sm pre-snRNA, likely due to the competition with Gemin5, which uses its different domain to bind the 5′ cap (Bradrick and Gromeier 2009; Jin et al. 2016; Wahl and Fischer 2016). Besides CBP20 and CBP80, no other major RNA binding protein was detected in the mU7/WT pre-snRNA proteome that could substitute for the function of Gemin5. One possibility was that the formation of a duplex structure by the 22mer oligonucleotide at the 3′ end of each pre-snRNA affected their overall conformation, precluding some protein interactions.

We tested other approaches, varying the method of RNA synthesis, the length of RNA and the position of biotin attached to the RNA. The most consentient results were obtained with chemically synthesized 69 nucleotide human U7 snRNA precursor containing biotin at the 3′ end (Fig. 1A). Compared to the mature U7 snRNA of 63 nucleotides found in U7 snRNP (Tisdale et al. 2013; Sun et al. 2020), this precursor contains six additional nucleotides at the 3′ end (Fig. 1A), partially resembling the in vivo assembly substrate extended by 35 nucleotides. In parallel to this 69 nucleotide RNA referred to as 69/WT, we used 69/Sm RNA (Fig. 1A) (see Table 1) whose Sm site was converted to the AAUUUuUggAG spliceosomal consensus (three nucleotide substitutions are indicated with small letters), as previously described (Grimm et al. 1993; Stefanovic et al. 1995). 69/WT and 69/Sm RNAs were incubated at 4°C with HeLa cytoplasmic extracts and bound proteins were immobilized on streptavidin (SA) beads via the 3′ biotin tag and separated by SDS/PAGE. To select high affinity binders, we initially used a limited amount of RNA (750 ng) and 750 µL of a cytoplasmic extract (∼1.5 mg total protein). Only background proteins were detected by silver staining in the negative sample prepared in the absence of RNA (Fig. 1B, left panel, lane 1). Importantly, three major protein bands were visualized in the material bound to 69/WT RNA, but not to 69/Sm RNA (Fig. 1B, left panel, lanes 2 and 3, respectively). Two of these bands migrating at ∼60 kDa were identified by mass spectrometry as the long (L) and short (S) splicing isoforms of polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1, UniProt ID P26599) (Garcia-Blanco et al. 1989; Ghetti et al. 1992). Both isoforms contain four RNA recognition motifs (RRMs), with the long isoform containing an additional 26-residue segment that separates RRM2 and RRM3 (Sawicka et al. 2008). The third unique protein band migrating above the 70 kDa size marker was identified as insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3, UniProt ID O00425). The identity of PTBP1 and IGF2BP3 was confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 1B, right panel, lane 2).

The 69/Sm mutant RNA, while failing to bind PTBP1 and IGF2BP3, interacted with a unique protein migrating at ∼185 kDa. This protein was identified by mass spectrometry and western blotting as Gemin5 (Fig. 1B, left and right panels, lane 3). In addition to Gemin5, the material purified by 69/Sm RNA contained other components of the SMN complex, including Gemin3, Gemin4 and SMN (Fig. 1B, left and right panels, lane 3). We note that Gemin3 and Gemin4 were detected in only some extracts, in agreement with the above experiments using mU7/Sm pre-snRNA (Supplemental Fig. S1B–D). All these proteins were conspicuously absent from the material bound by 69/WT RNA (Fig. 1B, left and right panels, lane 2). Among other proteins identified by mass spectrometry, the material purified via 69/WT RNA contained Matrin3 (Matr3, UniProt ID P43243), a known binding partner of PTBP1, and SFRS14 (Fig. 1B, right panel, lane 2) also known as SURP and G-patch domain-containing protein 2 (SUGP2, UniProt ID Q8IX01), a splicing factor that likely copurifies with Matrin3 (Coelho et al. 2015). Both RNAs interacted with three highly abundant proteins, hnRNP U (UniProt ID Q00839), hnRNP U-like 1 (UniProt ID Q9BUJ2), and hnRNP U-like 2 (UniProt ID Q1KMD3). These three proteins co-migrate with Matrin3 and in silver-stained gels mask the enrichment of Matrin3 by 69/WT RNA. In addition to hnRNP U and U-like 1-2, 69/WT and 69/Sm RNAs also interacted with hnRNP A1 (UniProt ID P09651) and its two paralogs, hnRNP A2 (UniProt ID P22626) and hnRNP A3 (UniProt ID P51991).

To confirm these results by a different method and to demonstrate that PTBP1 directly binds 69/WT RNA, we carried out electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA). 69/WT and 69/Sm RNAs were labeled at the 5′ end with 32P, mixed with a cytoplasmic extract, and RNA/protein complexes were separated in a 6% native polyacrylamide gel. The assay revealed a striking difference in the ability of each RNA to form RNP complexes (Fig. 1C). When incubated with a HeLa cytoplasmic extract, the 69/WT probe displayed a reduced electrophoretic mobility in a native polyacrylamide gel due to forming a larger complex (Fig. 1C, compare lanes 1 and 2). The complex was not eliminated by a 500-fold molar excess of 69/Sm unlabeled RNA (Fig. 1C, lane 3). Thus, the protein that binds to 69/WT has a much lower affinity for the U7 snRNA mutant containing a spliceosomal-type Sm site. To determine whether the complex contains PTBP1, IGF2BP3, SFRS14, or Matrin3, we used specific antibodies against each of these proteins. Only an anti-PTBP1 antibody super-shifted a small fraction of the complex to a higher position on the gel (data not shown), providing an initial clue that the major band observed in the native gel might be due to binding PTBP1 to 69/WT probe.

When 5′-labeled 69/Sm RNA was used as a probe, the characteristic shift seen for 69/WT RNA was not observed, and instead a different complex migrating near the top of the gel was formed (Fig. 1C, compare lanes 4 and 5). This complex was quantitatively super-shifted by an antibody against Gemin5 (Fig. 1C, lane 7, arrow), identifying the bound protein as Gemin5, consistent with the results of pull-down experiments. A 500-fold molar excess of 69/WT cold RNA abolished this interaction (Fig. 1C, lane 6), suggesting that the U7-specific Sm site has a detectable affinity for Gemin5.

PTBP1 is known to tightly interact with RNAs containing CU-rich regions (Singh et al. 1995; Perez et al. 1997a; Oberstrass et al. 2005; Auweter and Allain 2008). In the presence of the CU oligonucleotide (5′ CUCUCUCUCUUUCUUU 3′) containing multiple PTBP1 binding sites, the formation of the main 69/WT complex was inhibited (Fig. 1D, lanes 4–6), supporting the notion that the interacting protein is indeed PTBP1. Note that the use of this oligonucleotide facilitated the identification of a minor complex that migrates just below the PTBP1 complex and contains a protein resistant to the excess of the CU competitor (asterisk). We also used a different oligonucleotide with the sequence identical to the first 20 nucleotides of U7 snRNA (5′ U7), containing the CUCUUU site (Fig. 1A). This oligonucleotide only partially inhibited the formation of the PTBP1 complex (Fig. 1D, lane 3), suggesting that it has a lower affinity for PTBP1. Both oligonucleotides, while inhibiting the PTBP1 complex with 69/WT RNA, also promoted the formation of a weak complex with Gemin5 that is detectable on longer film exposures (Fig. 1D, arrow).

PTBP1 requires at least three sequence elements in U7 pre-snRNA for high affinity binding

We used a rabbit reticulocyte lysate (Promega), 35S-methionine, and appropriate cDNAs to synthesize radioactively labeled short (S) and long (L) isoforms of PTBP1 (Fig. 2A, lanes 2 and 3). As expected, a negative control prepared in the absence of exogenous cDNA yielded no radioactively labeled protein (Fig. 2A, lane 1). When tested by the mobility shift assay, the negative control lacking in vitro synthesized PTBP1 formed only a minor complex with the 69/WT probe (Fig. 2B, lane 3). This complex co-migrated with the complex formed in HeLa cytoplasmic extracts (Fig. 2B, compare lanes 1 and 3), suggesting that it contains endogenous PTBP1 from the rabbit reticulocyte lysate. A much more pronounced complex of similar mobility was formed with the 69/WT probe by the TnT sample containing the short PTBP1 isoform (Fig. 2B, compare lanes 3 and 4). The same ability to form a complex with the 69/WT probe was observed for the TnT sample containing PTBP1 (L) (Fig. 2C, lane 3). Based on these results, we conclude that the two splicing isoforms of PTBP1 bind 69/WT RNA, forming complexes with electrophoretic mobilities similar to that of the major cytoplasmic complex. Importantly, in vitro generated PTBP1 (L) (Fig. 2C, lane 6) and PTBP1 (S) (not shown) failed to interact with 69/Sm, thus mimicking the behavior of endogenous PTBP1 from HeLa cytoplasmic extracts. No complex formation with this probe was also observed for the negative TnT sample (no cDNA added) (Fig. 2C, lane 5), supporting the notion that the minor complex formed by this sample with the 69/WT probe was due to binding endogenous PTBP1 present in the rabbit reticulocyte lysate.

In addition to PTBP1, we also tested in vitro generated PTBP2 (Boutz et al. 2007; Keppetipola et al. 2012; Vuong et al. 2016). This neuronal-specific paralog shares 75% sequence identity with PTBP1 and displays the same binding properties, forming a complex with 69/WT but not with 69/Sm (not shown). We conclude that PTBP1 and PTBP2 bind 69 nucleotide U7 pre-snRNA with the wild-type sequence but not its mutant containing a spliceosomal-type Sm site.

Upstream of the Sm site, all known U7 snRNAs contain a CUCU sequence, which is part of a larger pyrimidine-rich substrate recognition site that base-pairs with the purine-rich HDE in histone pre-mRNAs and plays a key role in recruiting substrates for U7 snRNP (Schaufele et al. 1986; Bond et al. 1991). We generated 69/Py mutant RNA by changing CUCU to GAGA (Fig. 1A) and analyzed its ability to bind PTBP1 using EMSA. This mutant RNA also failed to form a complex with both the cytoplasmic (Fig. 2D, lane 6) and in vitro generated PTBP1 (not shown). 69/WT RNA tested in parallel readily formed a complex with PTBP1 from the same extract (Fig. 2D, lane 2), whereas 69/Sm RNA mainly interacted with Gemin5 instead (Fig. 2D, lane 4). One additional CUCU motif that may act as a PTBP1-binding site is located near the 3′ end in 69/WT RNA (Fig. 1A). Due to its terminal location, this motif is either partially or entirely deleted in 63 nucleotides long mature U7 snRNA (Fig. 1A). When tested in a mobility shift assay, this shorter RNA (63/WT) formed with HeLa cytoplasmic extracts only a barely detectable PTBP1 complex, contrasting with 69/WT RNA, and as expected, formation of this complex was inhibited by the CU RNA competitor (Fig. 2E, lanes 2 and 3). Thus, PTBP1 may simultaneously recognize at least three independent CU-containing sequence elements in U7 snRNA.

We next investigated the effect of base-pairing of an antisense oligonucleotide to the 5′ region of U7 snRNA (nucleotides 2–17) that includes the CUCU recognized by PTBP1 (Table 1). Consistent with the data shown above, the 69/WT probe interacted with PTBP1 from a HeLa cytoplasmic extract, generating a stable complex migrating near the middle of the gel (Fig. 2F, lane 2). In the presence of a molar excess of the CU oligonucleotide, the PTBP1 complex was eliminated, and the complex containing Gemin5 became more apparent (Fig. 2F, lane 3, arrow), suggesting that the inability of Gemin5 to bind 69/WT at least in part results from the competition with PTBP1. The presence of the CU competitor again confirmed that what appeared to be solely the PTBP1 complex consists in fact of two closely migrating complexes (Fig. 2F, lane 2), with only the top complex containing PTBP1 (arrow), and the bottom one containing a different protein insensitive to the CU oligonucleotide (asterisk). As expected, given the importance of the CUCU element, 69/WT RNA duplexed with the antisense oligonucleotide only weakly interacted with PTBP1 (Fig. 2F, lane 5, arrow). Note that the duplex formation shifts each probe and its complexes to higher positions on the gel. Again, the Gemin5-containing complex was formed, and the intensity of this complex increased in the presence of the CU competitor (Fig. 2F, lane 6). 69/Sm RNA retained some ability to bind cytoplasmic PTBP1 due to the presence of other binding sites in this mutant, and this residual binding was abolished by CU oligonucleotide (Fig. 2F, lanes 8 and 9).

The switch from an almost complete inability of Gemin5 to recognize U7-specific Sm site to an efficient binding was even more obvious with mouse U7 snRNA annealed to the antisense oligonucleotide (Supplemental Fig. S2). One interpretation of these results is that the complementary oligonucleotide, by forming a duplex with the 5′ end region of U7 snRNA, blocks the upstream PTBP1 binding site and also changes the overall folding of U7 snRNA, with the two effects independently contributing to converting 69/WT RNA from a preferable target for PTBP1 to a target that detectably interacts with Gemin5.

Recombinant PTBP1 binds U7 snRNA as a dimer

The above experiments confirmed that PTBP1 recognizes at least three sequence elements in U7 pre-snRNA: Py site, Sm site, and the 3′ extension. We next generated a double RNA mutant, 69/2x, in which the Py and Sm mutations were combined (Fig. 1A) and tested its binding to PTBP1 in a mobility shift assay using a HeLa cytoplasmic extract. As expected, most of the 69/WT probe bound to cytoplasmic PTBP1, whereas the 69/2x probe failed to form this complex but instead interacted with Gemin5 (Fig. 3A, lanes 2 and 4). The double mutant RNA additionally formed a major complex (question mark) that migrated closer to the bottom of the gel (Fig. 3A, lane 4). This complex is barely detectable in the case of the 69/WT probe and likely contains hnRNP A1, which readily interacts with mutant U7 pre-snRNAs unable to bind PTBP1 (see below).

Binding of recombinant PTBP1 to U7 snRNA. (A–D) EMSA with indicated probes and either a HeLa cytoplasmic extract or recombinant proteins N-terminally tagged with 5xHistidine (5xH) or GST. The question mark in A indicates a complex that likely contains hnRNP A1. A monomer of 5xH-PTBP1 bound to 69/WT RNA is indicated with an arrow (B).

We bacterially expressed PTBP1 (longer form) N-terminally tagged with either five histidines (5xH) or glutathione S-transferase (GST). The two recombinant proteins were tested for their ability to bind the 69/WT probe in EMSA. Surprisingly, the 5xH-tagged PTBP1 despite having a similar size to endogenous PTBP1 formed with the 69/WT probe a complex migrated significantly higher than the cytoplasmic complex (Fig. 3B, compare lanes 2 and 3). One possibility was that the recombinant proteins form dimers due to their large molar excess over the probe. This possibility was supported by the presence of a small amount of a 5xH-PTBP1 complex that migrated with a similar mobility as the cytoplasmic complex (Fig. 3B, lane 3, arrow), and by UV cross-linking experiments (Supplemental Fig. S3). Moreover, 63/WT RNA, which lacks most of its 3′ terminal CU motif and only weakly interacts with cytoplasmic PTBP1, forms two major complexes with recombinant 5xH-PTBP1, likely corresponding to PTBP1 monomer and dimer (Fig. 3C, lane 2). Thus, the formation of the PTBP1 dimer requires a high concentration of protein and is facilitated by the presence of multiple CU binding sites in 69/WT RNA.

PTBP1 was previously shown to dimerize under nonreducing conditions via cysteine 23 located in the N-terminal region preceding RRM1 (Monie et al. 2005; Clerte and Hall 2006). The presence of a high concentration of DTT did not significantly reduce the amount of the large PTBP1 complex (data not shown), suggesting that the two recombinant proteins may multimerize upon binding U7 snRNA (Monie et al. 2005; Clerte and Hall 2006), with the large molar excess of each protein over the probe enhancing PTBP1 dimerization. Our attempts to lower the amount of recombinant PTBP1 resulted in its precipitation. To further analyze the mechanism of dimerization, we bacterially expressed GST-Δ53n, a truncated version of PTBP1 lacking the entire unstructured N terminus (amino acids 1–53). The deleted region includes cysteine 23 that mediates intermolecular disulfide bond formation and PTBP1 dimerization in a nonreducing environment (Monie et al. 2005). The complex of 69/WT RNA and this protein had a similar mobility to the complex containing GST-tagged full length PTBP1 (Fig. 3D, lanes 3 and 4), suggesting that PTBP1 dimerization is triggered by U7 snRNA rather than formation of a disulfide bond.

In addition to GST-PTBP1 and GST-Δ53n, we also bacterially expressed GST-PTBP2. When tested by EMSA, these three proteins bound not only 69/WT (Fig. 3D, lanes 3–5) but also the 69/Sm and 69/Py mutant probes (not shown), and this clearly resulted from using an excess of recombinant proteins and the fact that each mutant RNA contains multiple PTBP1 recognition sites. Importantly, neither of the three recombinant PTBP proteins bound the 69/2x double mutant probe in which the Py and Sm mutations were combined, hence leaving only the 3′ terminal PTBP binding site (Fig. 3D, lanes 8–10).

Proteins interacting with U7 pre-snRNA in extracts from other cell lines

Having established that recombinant PTBP1 is virtually unable to interact with 69/2x RNA even in the presence of high protein concentrations, we used this RNA as a negative control in several affinity purification experiments with various mammalian extracts. Compared to the initial experiments, we increased the amount of RNA 10-fold (7.5 µg of RNA for 750 µL of extract or ∼1.5 mg total protein) to purify proteins having both high and low affinities for U7 pre-snRNA. Consistent with the above experiments, in a HeLa cytoplasmic extract only PTBP1 and IGF2BP3 showed reduced binding to U7 snRNA when Py and Sm sites were mutated individually (Fig. 4A, left and right, lanes 3 and 4), and virtually no binding when the two mutations were combined (Fig. 4A, both panels, lane 5). The two RNAs with a spliceosomal-type Sm site, 69/Sm and 69/2x, interacted with cytoplasmic Gemin5, confirming the data from EMSA. Other HeLa cytoplasmic proteins, including hnRNP U, hnRNP U-like 1 and hnRNP A1, bound each of the three mutant RNAs with the same or higher efficiency than did 69/WT. As determined by western blotting, the binding of Matrin3, which is largely recruited to U7 snRNA via PTBP1, was severely reduced by mutating the two sites simultaneously (Fig. 4A, right, lane 5). We conclude that PTBP1 and IGF2BP3 are the only major HeLa proteins that bind to the wild-type U7 snRNA precursor but not to its mutant simultaneously altered within the Py and Sm sites.

We used 69/WT and 69/Sm RNAs to test whether the same binding pattern can be observed with a nuclear extract prepared from the same batch of HeLa cells. In contrast to the cytoplasmic extract, the nuclear extract contained a significantly lower concentration of Gemin5 (not shown), and IGF2BP3 was undetectable (Fig. 4A, right, NE input), consistent with the cytoplasmic localization of this protein (Yaniv and Yisraeli 2002; Yisraeli 2005; Bell et al. 2013). Based on silver staining, 69/Sm RNA was less efficient than 69/WT RNA in binding nuclear PTBP1 (Fig. 4, left, compare lanes 7 and 8), although western blotting failed to detect this difference due to the strong signal produced by the antibody (Fig. 4A, right, lanes 7 and 8).

We used human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma (Kovalevich et al. 2021) and mouse C2C12 skeletal muscle cells to determine whether binding of PTBP1 and IGF2BP3 to 69/WT is conserved in cell lines of different origin. We prepared whole cell lysates from these cell lines to simultaneously look for U7 snRNA binders in both the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions and used 69/WT and 69/2x RNAs that represent drastically different binding patterns. In cell lysates from both SH-SY5Y and C2C12, PTBP1 and its paralog PTBP2 were identified as the only proteins that specifically interacted with the wild-type RNA, with 69/2x RNA displaying only a residual ability to bind either paralog (Fig. 4B,C). In the two cell lines, IGF2BP3 was poorly expressed and its binding to 69/WT RNA was not detected by western blotting (not shown).

As determined by western blotting using two anti-SMN antibodies, the material from SH-SY5Y and C2C12 lysates bound to the increased amount of 69/WT RNA also contained the SMN protein (Fig. 4B, right, lane 2, and not shown). This was surprising since no SMN was detected in the experiments carried out with a HeLa cytoplasmic extract and a much smaller amount of the same RNA (Fig. 1B, right panel, lane 2). Expectedly, SMN was significantly more abundant in the material purified by 69/2x RNA, consistent with this mutant RNA interacting with Gemin5, which in turn recruits other subunits of the SMN complex, including the SMN protein.

Binding of hnRNP A1 to U7 snRNA

hnRNP A1 and its two paralogs, hnRNP A2 and A3, were among the most abundant proteins purified by 69/WT pre-snRNA. The three mutations that eliminated PTBP binding sites (Sm, Py, and 2x) acted in the opposite manner on hnRNP A1 binding, having a stimulatory effect. Thus, PTBP1 and hnRNP A1 may compete for the same or overlapping binding sites. Previous structural and biochemical studies demonstrated that hnRNP A1 recognizes sequences containing a UAG trinucleotide (Beusch et al. 2017). Two UAG motifs are present in U7 snRNA, one separating the Py and Sm sites and the other being located further downstream, at the 3′ end of the Sm site (Fig. 1A). This downstream UAG motif is the hallmark of the Sm site and overlaps with the preceding CU that is required for binding PTBP1. The two UAG trinucleotides are universally conserved in all known vertebrate U7 snRNA sequences but are absent from the Drosophila U7 snRNA (Fig. 5A).

To map sequences of U7 snRNA that are essential for binding hnRNP A1, we compared proteins of HeLa cytoplasmic extract that interact with 69/WT, 69/Py and Drosophila (Dm U7) U7 snRNAs. Human and Drosophila U7 snRNAs are partially conserved only within the Py and Sm sites (Marz et al. 2007). The two UAG motifs found in vertebrate U7 snRNA are replaced in Drosophila U7 snRNA with UGA and UUG (Fig. 5A). Beyond the Py and Sm sites, human and Drosophila U7 snRNAs have no recognizable homology. As shown above, 69/WT RNA in contrast to 69/Py RNA readily bound PTBP1 but was less efficient in binding hnRNP A1 (Fig. 5B, lanes 2 and 3). Drosophila U7 snRNA, despite its vastly different sequence than 69/WT RNA, bound even more PTBP1 but failed to interact with IGF2BP3 and hnRNP A1 (Fig. 5B, lane 4). Dm U7 snRNA also weakly interacted with Gemin5 (Fig. 5B, lane 4), possibly as a result of alleviated competition from hnRNP A1 (see below). Again, 69/WT and 69/Py RNAs, while failing to bind Gemin5, were both associated with the SMN protein, with 69/Py binding more of this protein, in a good correlation with the increased amount of hnRNP A1 (Fig. 5B bottom, compare lanes 2 and 3). On the other hand, the material bound to Dm U7 snRNA contained a smaller amount of SMN, likely recruited by Gemin5 (Fig. 5B, lane 4). These results suggest that the SMN protein can be recruited by two independent mechanisms, via Gemin5 or hnRNP A1.

To test the possibility that hnRNP A1 recruits SMN to human U7 snRNA, we sequestered hnRNP A1 in a HeLa cytoplasmic extract (750 µL, ∼1.5 mg total protein) with excess (15 µg) of an AG oligonucleotide previously selected as a high affinity target for hnRNP A1 (Burd and Dreyfuss 1994). In parallel, we used the CU oligonucleotide to sequester PTBP1. The extracts containing either oligonucleotide and an untreated extract were next incubated with 69/WT RNA, and bound proteins were immobilized on streptavidin beads and analyzed by silver staining (not shown) and western blotting (Fig. 5C). As expected, the AG oligonucleotide inhibited the interaction of 69/WT RNA with hnRNP A1 but had no effect on PTBP1 binding (Fig. 5C, lane 2), whereas the CU oligonucleotide acted in the opposite manner, eliminating PTBP1 but not hnRNP A1 (Fig. 5C, lane 3). Importantly, the AG oligonucleotide also prevented the binding of SMN to 69/WT RNA (Fig. 5C, right panel, lane 2), consistent with the possibility that this subunit of the SMN complex is recruited to U7 snRNA via hnRNP A1.

In human U7 snRNA (Fig. 1A), the two UAG high affinity targets for hnRNP A1 flank the key nucleotides of the Sm site that bind Gemin5 (Battle et al. 2006b; Jin et al. 2016; Tang et al. 2016; Wahl and Fischer 2016; Xu et al. 2016). We considered the possibility that Gemin5 and hnRNP A1 may compete with each other for binding an overlapping site in human U7 snRNA, resembling the competition between Gemin5 and PTBP1. This possibility is supported by the binding behavior of Drosophila U7 snRNA (Fig. 5B, lane 4). We carried out a binding experiment with the AG competitor using 69/2x RNA instead of 69/WT RNA (Fig. 5D). Consistent with the data shown above, this mutant RNA is unable to bind PTBP1 but efficiently binds hnRNP A1, and due to the presence of a spliceosomal-type Sm site, directly interacts with Gemin5, which in turn brings other subunits of the SMN complex, including Gemin3, Gemin4 and the SMN protein (Fig. 5D, lane 1). When binding was carried out with HeLa extract preincubated with the AG competitor, hnRNP A1 was missing in the bound material, and the amount of Gemin3, 4 and 5, as judged by both silver staining (not shown) and western blotting (Fig. 5D, compare lanes 1 and 2), clearly increased. In addition to eliminating hnRNP A1, the AG oligonucleotide drastically reduced the amount of hnRNP U bound to 69/2x RNA. Thus, binding of Gemin5 to the Sm site of U7 snRNA might be at least in part limited by the competition with hnRNP A1 and/or hnRNP U.

To provide more direct evidence that the binding of hnRNP A1 to human U7 snRNA depends on the two UAG motifs located in the proximity of the Sm site, we altered their sequence to UGA (Fig. 1A). The resultant 69/AG mutant RNA when incubated with a HeLa cytoplasmic extract, only weakly bound hnRNP A1, and this change was accompanied by a strong reduction in the amount of SMN (Fig. 6A, compare lanes 2 and 3). At the same time, no quantitative change of hnRNP U in the bound material was detected. These results confirm that hnRNP A1 recognizes either one or two UAG motifs located near the U7-specific Sm site and suggest that this protein recruits the SMN protein to U7 snRNA.

We investigated whether the interaction between hnRNP A1 and U7 snRNA can be detected by EMSA. As shown above, the 69/WT probe mixed with a HeLa cytoplasmic extract yields a major complex containing PTBP1, and a few minor complexes of unknown origin (Fig. 6B, lane 2). To determine whether any of these complexes contain hnRNP A1, the 69/WT probe was preincubated with the cytoplasmic extract containing a 500-fold molar excess of the CU and AG oligonucleotides to simultaneously sequester PTBP1 and hnRNP A1. Only two complexes were eliminated (Fig. 6B, lane 3): the major complex containing PTBP1 and a barely detectable complex migrating below, likely containing hnRNP A1 (Fig. 6B, compare lanes 2 and 3, arrow). While an antibody against hnRNP A1 failed to super-shift this complex, the conclusion that it contains hnRNP A1 was supported by using 69/2x RNA in the mobility shift assay. This mutant RNA is unable to interact with PTBP1 but is more efficient in forming the lower complex that is sensitive to the competitors (Fig. 6B, lane 5, arrow, see also Fig. 3A, lane 4, arrow). Altogether, these results support the notion that hnRNP A1 and PTBP1 compete with each other for binding their overlapping target sites in U7 snRNA.

To get additional insight into the competition between the two proteins, we incubated a HeLa nuclear extract with recombinant GST-PTBP1 alone or bound to 69/WT RNA, and the associated proteins were immobilized on glutathione (GSH) beads and analyzed by silver staining (Fig. 6C). Three major proteins were detected when GSH beads were incubated with the nuclear extract containing GST-PTBP1 but not 69/WT RNA (Fig. 6C, lane 2): GST-PTBP1 itself and two background proteins detected also in the absence of GST-PTBP1 (Fig. 6C, lane 1). In addition, multiple less intense silver-stained bands were visible, representing both degradation products and potential binding partners of recombinant PTBP1 (see below). A complex of GST-PTBP1 with 69/WT RNA bound a specific HeLa protein(s) that migrated at ∼38 kDa and was absent from the two remaining samples (Fig. 6C, compare lane 3 with lanes 1 and 2). Surprisingly, both mass spectrometry and western blotting (not shown) determined that this band contains hnRNP A1 and its closely related paralogs, hnRNP A2 and A3. Thus, PTBP1 and hnRNP A1, despite competing for binding their respective target sites can also form a ternary complex with U7 snRNA. Additional experiments (data not shown) demonstrated that only a small fraction of cytoplasmic hnRNP A1 binds U7 snRNA together with GST-PTBP1, suggesting that the formation of this ternary complex is inefficient in vitro and may result from using excessive amounts of recombinant GST-PTBP1. Importantly, the material purified by the GST-PTBP1/RNA complex, but not by GST-PTBP1 itself, also contained detectable amounts of SMN (Fig. 6C, bottom, lane 3), further supporting the notion that hnRNP A1 and SMN directly interact with each other.

We bacterially expressed GST-tagged hnRNP A1 (GST-A1) and used EMSA to compare its ability to bind 69/WT with that of 5xH-tagged PTBP1. 69/WT RNA formed with GST-A1 a single complex that migrated close to the PTBP1 dimer (Fig. 6D, lanes 2 and 3). Based on these relative mobilities, we tentatively conclude that GST-tagged hnRNP A1 forms a dimer. As expected, 69/AG only weakly interacted with GST-hnRNP A1 but efficiently formed a complex with PTBP1 (Fig. 6D, lanes 5 and 6). We conclude that hnRNP A1 directly interacts with human U7 snRNA, and for binding requires two highly conserved UAG motifs flanking the Sm site.

hnRNP A1 directly interacts with the SMN protein

The above results raised the possibility that hnRNP A1 directly interacts with SMN. To test this possibility, GST alone (negative control) or GST fused to hnRNP A1 were incubated with in vitro synthesized 35S-labeled SMN and immobilized on glutathione beads. Human SMN is a 324-amino acid protein and contains three functionally characterized regions (Fig. 7A): Gemin2-interacting domains (amino acids 23–55), Tudor domain (amino acids 92–142) and YG box (amino acids 258–286). All these regions, as well as a proline-rich region preceding the YG box (amino acids 240–249), are highly conserved among distant vertebrates (Supplemental Fig. S4).

Recombinant proteins were visualized by staining with Coomassie Blue (bottom), and 35S-labeled SMN variants were detected by autoradiography (top). While GST alone failed to detectably interact with 35S-labeled SMN (Fig. 7B, lane 2, Fig. 7C, lane 2), as much as 15%–20% of the labeled protein used in the binding assay was recovered via GST-tagged full length hnRNP A1 of 320 amino acids (Fig. 7B, lane 3, Fig. 7C, lane 4). To better monitor the specificity of the interaction, we also tested GST fused to the first 250 amino acids of human Sin3a protein. As GST alone, this negative control failed to detectably interact with SMN (Fig. 7C, lane 3). In addition to the most common splice variant of 320 amino acids, hnRNP A1 also exists as hnRNP A1B, a minor splice variant extended by 52 amino acids within the C-terminal glycine-rich region (Mayeda et al. 1994; Gagne et al. 2021). This longer variant fused to GST (GST-A1B) bound SMN with the efficiency comparable to that of the major hnRNP A1 isoform (Fig. 7C, compare lanes 4 and 5). To map the region(s) of SMN that interacts with hnRNP A1, we expressed only the N-terminal half of SMN containing the Gemin2-interacting domain and Tudor domain. This 35S-labeled 146-amino acid fragment (SMN/146N) failed to interact with GST-tagged hnRNP A1 (Fig. 7C, lanes 6–10), suggesting that the interacting domain is located within the C-terminal half of the protein that contains a YG box and mediates SMN multimerization.

We next tested which region of hnRNP A1 is responsible for its interaction with SMN. The N-terminal half of hnRNP A1 (amino acids 1–184) contains two RNA recognition motifs, RRM1 and RRM2, whereas the C-terminal half contains an RGG box (repeats of Arg-Gly-Gly) that mediates RNA binding (Kiledjian and Dreyfuss 1992; Mayeda et al. 1994). We expressed the N-terminal region of hnRNP A1 (amino acids 1–189) fused with GST (GST-A1/N) and determined that this fusion protein interacts with 35S-labeled full length SMN (Fig. 7D, lane 4). These initial experiments mapped the interacting region to the N-terminal half of hnRNPA1 that encompasses two RRMs and to the C-terminal half of SMN that contains the YG box. When only these fragments were used in the binding assay, a significant interaction was detected (Fig. 7E, lane 6).

The YG box of ∼30 amino acids is the most conserved region of the C-terminal half of SMN, being almost identical in human and Danio rerio orthologs (Supplemental Fig. S4). A clear conservation is also observed for a short track of prolines upstream of the YG box (amino acids 240–249). The remaining parts of the C-terminal half are evolutionary more variable. To get initial clues on what part of the C-terminal region of SMN mediates the interaction, we tested whether GST-A1/N (amino acids 1–189) binds 35S-labeled D. rerio SMN (DrSMN). A strong and specific interaction was observed in the GST pull-down assay (Fig. 7E, lanes 7–9), suggesting that SMN uses its conserved YG box and/or the immediately upstream proline-rich region to form a complex with the N-terminal half of hnRNP A1. We expressed two shorter C-terminal fragments of human SMN, encompassing the last 101 residues (SMN/101C, amino acids 194–294) or 65 residues (SMN/65C, amino acids 230–294). Both SMN/101C (Fig. 7F, lanes 1–3) and SMN/65C (Fig. 7F, lanes 4–6) interacted with GST-tagged hnRNP A1, further supporting the notion that the interaction is mediated by the YG box and/or the proline-rich track.

U7 snRNA stabilizes complexes of PTBP1 with its binding partners containing PRI motifs

One interesting aspect of PTBP1 is that it forms complexes with proteins containing a single or multiple copies of PTBP RRM2 interacting (PRI) motif with the (S/G)(I/L)LGxxP consensus. This motif interacts with the helical face of the RRM2 that does not participate in RNA recognition, allowing formation of ternary complexes in which PTBP1 is bound to both its RNA target and a PRI-containing protein. These proteins include Matrin3, Raver1, Raver2, CCAR1, and CCAR2 (Kleinhenz et al. 2005; Henneberg et al. 2010; Joshi et al. 2011; Coelho et al. 2015, 2016).

We used cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts from HeLa cells to determine how U7 pre-snRNA affects the binding of PRI-containing proteins to PTBP1. Briefly, 69/WT RNA or its mutants were incubated either alone or prebound to GST-PTBP1 with various HeLa extracts and associated, proteins were immobilized on streptavidin (SA) beads via biotin attached to the RNA, or on glutathione (GSH) beads via GST tag attached to PTBP1. Initially, 69/WT RNA alone or in a complex with GST-PTBP1 was incubated with a HeLa nuclear extract and immobilized on SA beads. To determine which protein bands in silver-stained gels correspond to the recombinant protein, the complex was bound to SA beads in the presence of a buffer (Fig. 8A, top, lane 4). In agreement with the results shown above, the material bound to 69/WT RNA contained long and short isoforms of endogenous PTBP1 and its interacting partner with a PRI motif, Matrin3 (Fig. 8A, top, lane 2). Preincubation of 69/WT RNA with GST-PTBP1 to form their complex did not change the amount of endogenous PTBP1 immobilized on SA beads, indicative of excess of RNA used in the experiment (Fig. 8A, top, lane 3). Surprisingly, the material bound by this complex contained a protein of 130 kDa, which was missing in the lane containing material bound to 69/WT RNA alone (Fig. 8A, top and bottom, compare lanes 2 and 3, arrow). This band was identified by mass spectrometry and western blotting as a different PRI motif-containing protein, CCAR2. Only a small amount of this protein was detected by western blotting in the sample lacking GST-PTBP1, suggesting that CCAR2 preferentially interacts with recombinant PTBP1, hence behaves differently than Matrin3, which copurifies with endogenous PTBP1.

To confirm this different behavior of Matrin3 and CCAR2, we carried out an additional experiment using a HeLa cytoplasmic extract and both 69/WT and 69/2x RNAs that drastically differ in their ability to bind PTBP1. As determined by analyzing the material immobilized on SA beads, 69/WT RNA, but not 69/2x RNA, readily interacted with endogenous PTBP1 and Matrin3 (Fig. 8B, top and bottom, compare lanes 2 and 4). Note that the difference in the amount of Matrin3 bound to 69/WT and 69/2x RNAs can be visualized by western blotting (bottom), but not by silver staining (top), as Matrin3 co-migrates with hnRNP U, U-like 1 and U-like 2 that interact with both RNAs. As expected, 69/2x RNA bound endogenous Gemin5 (Fig. 8B, top and bottom, lane 4). The material bound by a complex of 69/WT RNA and GST-PTBP1 contained readily detectable amounts of CCAR2 (Fig. 8B, top and bottom, compare lanes 2 and 3, arrow). Only a residual amount of this protein was detected by western blotting in the material purified by the complex of 69/2x and GST-PTBP1, in a perfect correlation with the small amount of GST-PTBP1 (Fig. 8B, top and bottom, lane 5, arrow).

The second half of each sample was incubated with GSH beads rather than SA beads, allowing for the selection of proteins that bind recombinant PTBP1 either alone or in complex with U7 pre-snRNA. In all samples containing GST-tagged PTBP1, regardless of whether the RNA was added or not, CCAR2 was readily detectable by silver staining and western blotting (Fig. 8B, top and bottom, lanes 6, 8, and 10). This result demonstrates that CCAR2 binds recombinant PTBP1 in an RNA-independent manner, although 69/WT RNA, but not 69/2x RNA, has a clear stimulatory effect on this interaction, resulting in more CCAR2 purified on GSH beads (Fig. 8B, top and bottom, compare lane 8 with lanes 6 and 10). Both silver staining and western blotting failed to detect Matrin3 in the material immobilized on GSH beads when just GST-PTBP1 was used, although a small amount of this protein was detected in the presence of 69/WT RNA (Fig. 8B, top and bottom, lane 8, arrow). 69/2x RNA, which does not bind PTBP1, had no effect (Fig. 8B, lane 10). We conclude that in contrast to Matrin3, which is bound to endogenous PTBP1, CCAR2 exists in HeLa cytoplasmic extracts predominantly as a free protein, available to form a complex with recombinant PTBP1.

To confirm that 69 nucleotide U7 snRNA precursor stimulates the binding of recombinant PTBP1 to Matrin3 and CCAR2, we repeated this experiment and increased the intensity of silver staining to detect less abundant proteins immobilized on GSH beads. As determined by analyzing the material immobilized on GSH beads, GST-PTBP1 in the absence of 69/WT RNA again interacted with only a small amount of Matrin3 (Fig. 8C, lane 1), consistent with this protein being primarily associated with endogenous PTBP1. In the presence of 69/WT RNA, this amount clearly increased, as determined by both silver staining and western blotting (Fig. 8C, top and bottom, lane 2), confirming that U7 snRNA stimulates formation of the complex between recombinant PTBP1 and Matrin3. The same stimulatory effect of U7 snRNA was also observed on binding CCAR2. Interestingly, the U7 snRNA-mediated increase in the amount of Matrin3 and CCAR2 bound to recombinant PTBP1 was accompanied by a disappearance of multiple bands that were purified in the absence of U7 pre-snRNA (Fig. 8C, lane 1, arrows). Mass spectrometry identified these bands as ribosomal proteins of the large and small subunits (not shown).

We tested whether the opposing effects of U7 snRNA on the ability of recombinant PTBP1 to interact with ribosomal proteins and the two PRI proteins, Matrin3 and CCAR2, can be reproduced using any RNA that is recognized by PTBP1. In this experiment, GST-PTBP1 was prebound to either 69/WT RNA or CU oligonucleotide, and the resultant complexes were immobilized on GSH beads and incubated with a HeLa cytoplasmic extract. Strikingly, prebinding of CU oligonucleotide to GST-PTBP1 reduced the amount of not only ribosomal proteins but also Matrin3 and CCAR2 (Fig. 8D, lane 3). In contrast, prebinding of 69/WT RNA to GST-PTBP1, while eliminating ribosomal proteins, dramatically stimulated the interaction of GST-PTBP1 with Matrin3 (Fig. 8D, lane 4). The stimulatory effect of U7 snRNA on the interaction between CCAR2 and recombinant PTBP1 was less evident in this experiment, largely due to relatively strong silver staining of CCAR2 in lane 2.

Only a limited number of HeLa nuclear extracts and whole cell lysates allowed for a successful copurification of Raver1, another PTBP1 binding partner containing a PRI motif. Binding of also this protein, largely or exclusively of nuclear localization, to recombinant PTBP1 was clearly stimulated by U7 snRNA, thus mimicking the behavior of Matrin3 and CCRA2 (Fig. 8E, top and bottom, compare lanes 2 and 3).

Matrin3, CCAR2, Raver1, and other proteins containing PRI motifs interact with the helical face of RRM2, with the key role being played by tyrosine 247, and its substitution with glutamine is sufficient to disrupt the interaction (Joshi et al. 2011). We expressed Δ53n variant of PTBP1 (see Fig. 3D) containing this mutation and tested its effect on the interaction with PRI motif-containing proteins in the presence of 69/WT RNA. As expected, while GST-PTBP1 interacted with easily detectable amounts of Matrin3 and CCAR2 (Fig. 8F, top and bottom, lane 1), GST-tagged Y247Q mutant was fully impaired in binding these two proteins (Fig. 8F, top and bottom, lane 2).

DISCUSSION

Animal cells contain U7 snRNP, a relative of the spliceosomal snRNPs that has no role in splicing and instead functions as an RNA-guided endonuclease and 5′-3′ exonuclease in 3′ end processing of replication-dependent histone pre-mRNAs (Sun et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2020; Dominski and Tong 2021). The Sm site in U7 snRNA with the consensus of AAUUUGUCUAG departs from the spliceosomal consensus and nucleates the assembly of a unique Sm ring that lacks the spliceosomal SmD1 and D2 proteins, containing instead Lsm10 and Lsm11 (Schumperli and Pillai 2004; Dominski and Marzluff 2007; Gruss et al. 2017). The unusual Sm site in U7 snRNA is not recognized by Gemin5 (Battle et al. 2006b), although the SMN protein and likely some other components of the SMN complex are required for more downstream steps in the assembly of the U7-specific Sm ring (Schumperli and Pillai 2004; Tisdale et al. 2013, 2022). The identity of the protein(s) that recognizes distinct features of the Sm site in U7 snRNA and substitutes for Gemin5, hence promoting the incorporation of Lsm10 and Lsm11 into the Sm ring of U7 snRNP, has not been determined. In this study, we used RNA affinity chromatography and extracts from mammalian cells to identify proteins that interact with a 69 nucleotide precursor to human U7 snRNA in a manner that depends on the wild-type sequence of its Sm site. We identified three proteins that meet this criterion: PTBP1, IGF2BP3, and hnRNP A1.

Binding of PTBP1 to U7 pre-snRNA

A 69 nucleotide precursor to human U7 snRNA (pre-snRNA) with the wild-type sequence of its Sm site forms a tight complex with endogenous PTBP1 and with both recombinant and in vitro generated PTBP1. The binding of PTBP1 to U7 snRNA is severely inhibited by changing its Sm site to the spliceosomal consensus. In cytoplasmic extracts, this mutant RNA, while failing to interact with PTBP1, forms a complex with Gemin5, in agreement with previous in vivo studies where the same U7 snRNA mutant promoted the assembly of an Sm ring containing SmD1 and SmD2 (Grimm et al. 1993; Stefanovic et al. 1995). Binding of PTBP1 to U7 pre-snRNA also requires the upstream CUCUU sequence (Py site) that base-pairs with histone pre-mRNA, defining the substrate specificity of U7 snRNP. Thus, PTBP1 simultaneously recognizes two functionally essential and highly conserved sequences in U7 snRNA. Our observation that the Py site in U7 snRNA is required not only for base-pairing with histone pre-mRNAs but also for binding PTBP1 may explain the usual evolutionary conservation of this site, which is virtually invariable in metazoans (Marz et al. 2007).

The interaction of PTBP1 with the Py and Sm sites is consistent with the known preference of PTBP1 for CU dinucleotides (Perez et al. 1997b; Oberstrass et al. 2005), which are the hallmark of the two sites in U7 snRNA (Marz et al. 2007). Near the 3′ end, the human U7 snRNA contains an additional CU-containing element (nucleotides 62–66) that contributes to the interaction between PTBP1 and U7 pre-snRNA. This region is removed exonucleolytically from U7 snRNA after the completion of the Sm ring assembly and cap trimethylation.

PTBP1 is a versatile and abundant RNA binding protein, regulating a plethora of processes, including alternative splicing, cap-independent translation, 3′ end processing, mRNA degradation, and mRNA localization (Wagner and Garcia-Blanco 2002; Spellman et al. 2005; Sawicka et al. 2008; Dai et al. 2022). In human cells, PTBP1 has two paralogs, PTBP2 and PTBP3. These three highly related proteins are believed to fulfill both specific and redundant functions (Spellman et al. 2007), and our studies demonstrated that they all bind U7 pre-snRNA. PTBP1 and its paralogs contain four RRMs. The two N-terminal domains act independently of each other and bind relatively short CU stretches (Oberstrass et al. 2005; Clerte and Hall 2009). In contrast, RRM3 and RRM4 form a single functional unit capable of binding two CU motifs positioned in an antiparallel orientation and separated by ∼15 nucleotides, resulting in RNA looping (Oberstrass et al. 2005; Lamichhane et al. 2010). The spacer between the CUCU motif in the Py site and UCU motif in the Sm site consists of 11 nucleotides and is likely sufficiently long to be looped out following the recognition of these sites by the RRM3/4 tandem. RRM1 and RRM2 may in turn interact with the two remaining CU-containing motifs, one near the 3′ end and the other one in the 3′ terminal loop. This may result in a very tight RNA/protein complex, with both components adopting a unique conformation and spatial arrangement.

PTBP1 uses the helical face of the RRM2 that does not participate in RNA recognition to interact with a group of proteins containing the PTBP RRM2 interacting (PRI) motif(s) with the (S/G)(I/L)LGxxP consensus (Kleinhenz et al. 2005; Henneberg et al. 2010; Joshi et al. 2011; Coelho et al. 2015, 2016). These proteins include Matrin3, Raver1, its paralog Raver2, CCAR1, and its paralog CCAR2. Matrin3, Raver1, and likely other proteins containing PRI motifs assist PTBP1 in regulating alternative splicing of multiple transcripts (Gromak et al. 2003; Spellman et al. 2005; Rideau et al. 2006; Coelho et al. 2015). The close partnership between PTBP1 and the PRI proteins may also serve other purposes, including the recognition of internal ribosome entry segments (IRES) in viral and cellular mRNAs (Kafasla et al. 2012) and 3′ end processing (Coelho et al. 2016).

Our binding experiments resulted in copurification of all five PTBP interacting proteins. Matrin3 was almost exclusively associated with endogenous PTBP1, contrasting with both Raver1–2 and CCAR1–2, which likely exist in a free state available for binding to recombinant PTBP. Interestingly, U7 snRNA precursor stabilizes the interaction of PTBP1 with the PRI-containing proteins, whereas a different PTBP1 RNA target consisting of multiple CU dinucleotides has a destabilizing effect. This observation suggests that the interaction of U7 snRNA with PTBP proteins may be biologically relevant, although further studies are required to determine what processes could be regulated by this interaction.

Binding of IGF2BP3 to U7 pre-snRNA

In addition to PTBP1, our biochemical assays identified IGF2BP3 as a protein that interacts with U7 pre-snRNA in a manner that requires its unique Sm site. IGF2BP3 also requires the Py site that base-pairs with histone pre-mRNA, thus resembling the binding specificity of PTBP1. Surprisingly, the binding of IGF2BP3 to U7 pre-snRNA was not inhibited by excess of the CU oligonucleotide, strongly contrasting with PTBP1. It is possible that IGF2BP3 recognizes certain structural features within the 69 nucleotide U7 snRNA precursor that depend on the wild-type sequences of the Py and Sm sites (Chao et al. 2010).

In contrast to IGF2BP3 from mammalian cytoplasmic extracts, in vitro generated IGF2BP3 failed to bind to U7 snRNA, precluding more detailed analysis. It is possible that IGF2BP3 may require other cytoplasmic protein(s) or a post-translational modification to recognize U7 pre-snRNA. IGF2BP3 and its two paralogs, IGF2BP1–2, also known as the zipcode-binding proteins 1–3 (ZBP1–3) or IMP1–3, are thought to function as general regulators of multiple RNAs, affecting various aspects of their metabolism, including intracellular localization, stability, binding to other proteins and efficiency of translation (Yisraeli 2005).

Binding of hnRNP A1 to U7 pre-snRNA

hnRNP A1 was identified as another protein that interacts with human U7 snRNA. hnRNP A1 has a strong specificity for a UAG motif (Burd and Dreyfuss 1994; Kashima and Manley 2003; Beusch et al. 2017), and two UAGs are found in U7 snRNA, with one of them separating the Py and Sm sites and the other one being an integral part of the Sm site (Marz et al. 2007). Both these sequences are universally conserved in vertebrate U7 snRNAs but absent from the Drosophila U7 snRNA. hnRNP A1 contains two RRMs and they also interact with each other, forming a single binding platform capable of interacting with bipartite binding sites in RNAs (Shamoo et al. 1997; Beusch et al. 2017).

In contrast to PTBP1 and IGF2BP3, hnRNP A1 readily interacted with the U7 snRNA mutant in which the Sm site was mutated from AAUUUGUCUAG to the spliceosomal consensus AAUUUUUGGAG (changed nucleotides are underlined), initially disqualifying this protein as a potential counterpart of Gemin5. While hnRNP A1 preferentially binds UAG motifs, it makes key contacts with the AG dinucleotide (Oberstrass et al. 2005; Beusch et al. 2017), which was left unchanged in the Sm site mutant. Substituting the two AG dinucleotides with GA abolished hnRNP A1 binding.

Interestingly, our biochemical studies demonstrated that bacterially expressed PTBP1 and endogenous hnRNP A1 from cytoplasmic or nuclear extracts form with U7 pre-snRNA a ternary complex. This was a surprising observation given the proximity of the binding sites for the two proteins. While the efficiency of binding of PTBP1 and hnRNP A1 to the same U7 pre-snRNA molecule in vitro is relatively low, this process may be much more efficient in vivo.

What prevents the recognition of the U7-specific Sm site by Gemin5

The binding of Gemin5 to the spliceosomal AAUUUUGG consensus sequence (Battle et al. 2006b; Lau et al. 2009; Yong et al. 2010) critically depends on the recognition of the second adenosine and the first and third uridines (Battle et al. 2006b; Jin et al. 2016; Tang et al. 2016; Wahl and Fischer 2016; Xu et al. 2016). Despite containing the same nucleotides in the Sm site, U7 snRNA failed to interact with Gemin5 in our affinity purification and mobility shift assays, confirming previous in vivo studies by others (Battle et al. 2006b). Interestingly, we observed a visible increase in the efficiency of Gemin5 binding to U7 snRNA in the absence of free PTBP1 or hnRNP A1. Thus, PTBP1, hnRNP A1 and potentially other abundant RNA binding proteins may compete with Gemin5, preventing its binding to the U7-specific Sm site.

We also observed an improved binding of Gemin5 to U7 snRNA in which the first 16 nucleotides, including the Py site but not the Sm site, were base-paired to a complementary oligonucleotide. By forming an RNA duplex, this oligonucleotide eliminated the upstream site for PTBP1 located within the Py site, but also likely affected the overall structure of U7 pre-snRNA. In the spliceosomal snRNAs, the Sm site is located between two stem–loop structures, and this arrangement may expose the Sm site for efficient Gemin5 binding. The U7 snRNA contains only one stem-loop structure flanking the Sm site at the 3′ side, with the 5′ region containing the Py and Sm sites likely remaining unstructured. One attractive possibility is that these two highly conserved and functionally critical sequences may adopt a unique conformation that prevents binding of Gemin5 to U7 snRNA, facilitating instead the recruitment of a different RNA binding protein and the assembly of the U7-specific Sm ring. Interestingly, structural studies on fully recombinant and functionally active U7 snRNP revealed that the C and G nucleotides within the UCUAG sequence at the 3′ end of the Sm site in U7 snRNA form an intramolecular base pair (Sun et al. 2020). This CG base pair may function as one of these unique structural determinants that in vivo ultimately result in the incorporation of the Lsm10/11 rather than SmD1/D2 to U7 snRNP.

While our direct biochemical assays confirmed previous in vivo studies that Gemin5 does not bind U7 snRNA, they were unable to identify its counterpart in the assembly of U7 snRNP. What favors PTBP1 and/or hnRNP A1 in this process is that they recognize the highly conserved UCUAG sequence in the Sm site of U7 snRNA (Marz et al. 2007) that is essential for the formation of U7 snRNP in vivo (Kolev and Steitz 2006). More importantly, hnRNP A1 through its two N-terminal RRMs directly interacts with the C-terminal YG region of SMN, providing a potential link to other subunits of the SMN complex and the assembly machinery. The SMN protein through its Tudor domain was also shown to directly interact with IGF2BP1 (Fallini et al. 2014), adding this protein and its close paralog IGF2BP3 to the list of potential counterparts of Gemin5 in the assembly of U7 snRNP.

Clearly, more targeted studies are required to determine whether the assembly of the unique Sm ring in U7 snRNP involves any of the three proteins that in vitro recognize the wild-type Sm site of U7 pre-snRNA. Or perhaps the interaction of PTBP1, IGF2BP3, or hnRNP A1 with U7 pre-snRNA contributes to other processes that are unrelated to the U7 snRNP assembly and 3′ end processing of histone pre-mRNAs. In terminally differentiated cells that permanently exited cell cycle, U7 snRNP is likely dispensable for 3' end processing of replication-dependent histone pre-mRNAs, raising the possibility that in these cells U7 snRNA may become repurposed for a more specialized function mediated by PTBP1, IGF2BP3 or hnRNP A1.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Antibodies

The following commercial antibodies were used in this study: anti-Matrin3 (A300-591A, BETHYL), anti-CCAR2 (22638-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-IGF2BP3 (PA5-46704, Invitrogen), anti-PTBP1 (12582-1-AP, Invitrogen), anti-PTBP2 (55186-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Raver1 (A303-939A, BETHYL), anti-hnRNP A1 (11176-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-hnRNP U1 (MA5-35471, Invitrogen), anti-hnRNP U-like 1 (10578-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-hnRNP A2 (sc-374053, Santa Cruz), anti-SMN (60154-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Gemin2 (2E17, Sigma), anti-Gemin3 (12H12, sc-57007, Santa Cruz), anti-Gemin4 (17D10, sc-136199, Santa Cruz), anti-Gemin5 (A301-325A, BETHYL), and anti-Gemin5 (10G11, Santa Cruz).

Cell culture and preparation of cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts

HeLa cells were grown by Cell Culture Company (Minneapolis, MN), shipped on dry ice as a frozen pellet and used after thawing. Nuclear extracts were prepared as described previously (Dominski et al. 1995; Skrajna et al. 2018; Sun et al. 2021). The crude cytoplasm generated after collecting HeLa nuclei was supplemented with NP40 to a final concentration ranging from 0.025% to 0.1% and used to prepare cytoplasmic extracts, as described previously (Mayeda and Krainer 1999), with the ultracentrifugation and dialysis steps being omitted.

Preparation of whole cell lysates

Pellets of HeLa, C2C12, and SH-SY5Y cells were resuspended in 0.5% NP40 lysis buffer (40 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5% NP40), using 5 mL of buffer per 1 mL of cell pellet. The cell suspensions were rotated in a cold room for 30 min and spun for 30 min at 30,000g in a refrigerated centrifuge.

Isolation and identification of mammalian proteins interacting with U7 pre-snRNA

Proteins that bind 69/WT RNA and its mutants were purified essentially as previously described (Sabath et al. 2013; Skrajna et al. 2018, 2019). Briefly, a HeLa cytoplasmic extract (750 µL or ∼1.5 mg total protein) was mixed with 69/WT RNA or its mutants (0.75–7.5 µg, depending on the experiment) and rotated in a cold room for 60 min. The sample was spun in a cold room for 10 min at 13,500g and the supernatant loaded over 25 µL of streptavidin beads (Sigma) and rotated in a cold room for 1 h. The beads were gently spun, rinsed twice and rotated for 60 min with a buffer containing 40 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM DTT and 0.025% NP40. A fraction of each sample was separated by electrophoresis in a 4%–12% SDS/polyacrylamide gel (SDS/PAGE), and the bound proteins visualized by silver staining and identified by mass spectrometry in the Laboratory of Mass Spectrometry at the Institute of Biochemistry and Biophysics (Warsaw, Poland), as previously described (Yang et al. 2013; Skrajna et al. 2018). Protein identities were confirmed by western blotting using specific antibodies. Whole cell lysates and nuclear extracts were diluted by mixing with the same volume of a cytoplasmic buffer (40 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.5 mM DTT), and used in the RNA binding assay, as described above.

Protein expression

cDNAs encoding full-length PTBP1 (short and long form), PTBP2, and hnRNP A1 were cloned into pET42a or pET28a vectors (Novagen) and the encoded proteins expressed in Escherichia coli and purified on Ni-NTA agarose beads (QIAGEN) using standard protocols. In vitro protein synthesis by the coupled transcription and translation (TnT) system (Promega) was carried out in the presence of 35S-methionine in either wheat germ or rabbit reticulocyte lysates, as suggested by the manufacturer.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The formation of RNA/protein complexes was monitored by electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) using RNA probes labeled at the 5′ end with 32P, as described previously (Yang et al. 2006). Typically, each sample (total volume of 10 µL) contained 5 µL of EMSA buffer (40 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 µg/µL of yeast tRNA, and 5% glycerol) and 5 µL of a TnT lysate, cytoplasmic extract, or recombinant protein (total 0.5–1 µg). The samples were incubated for 15 min on ice and resolved in a 6% native polyacrylamide gel. The radioactive RNA probe and its complexes were detected by autoradiography.
