Revealing the burden of chronic kidney disease in Mexican women, 1990–2021
Authors: Claudio A Dávila-Cervantes, Marcela Agudelo-Botero
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a concerning public health issue, affecting people regardless of their sex, age, or socioeconomic status. We aimed to analyze the burden of female CKD in Mexico between 1990 and 2021, expressed in terms of years lived with disability (YLDs), mortality, years of life lost (YLLs), and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs). Additionally, we evaluated the relationship between DALYs and the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) and the Healthcare Access and Quality Index (HAQI).MethodsSecondary data analysis from the Global Burden of Disease, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021. We used mortality, years of life lost due to premature mortality (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs to analyze CKD in women between 1990 and 2021. We used a log-linear segmented regression model to analyze trends in female CKD DALY rates over time. Age-standardized rates (ASR) were used.ResultsBetween 1990 and 2021, the ASR mortality and ASR-DALYs due to CKD increased significantly at the national level. The DALYs are almost entirely explained by YLLs, indicating that a large proportion of women with CKD in Mexico die prematurely. Disparities in the burden of this disease were observed across different states and age groups within the country. Background

Within the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) framework, achieving gender equality and empowering women have been established as integral components of each of the 17 established goals [1]. This effort includes protecting women from economic crises, violence, and discrimination and addressing their health needs, which extend beyond sexual and reproductive health [1]. Like men, women are exposed to risk factors that lead to various diseases, including non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and health issues across different stages of the life cycle.

Globally, 74% of deaths each year are attributed to NCDs [2], and it is projected that this type of death will continue to rise in the coming years, particularly among women [3]. In 2021, 55% of incident NCDs cases were in women [4]. Although the profile of NCDs causes is increasingly similar between the sexes [5, 6], the burden of these diseases often differs due to biological factors that intersect with social and gender constructs, increasing risks in certain social groups [7–10].

In Mexico, the leading causes of mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and years of life lost (YLLs) among women in 2019 were due to NCDs [11]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was a significant contributor to these indicators, ranking as the second highest cause in standardized DALYs rates that year, second only to diabetes mellitus [11]. Kidney disease has become a concerning public health issue, affecting people regardless of their sex, age, or socioeconomic status [12–14].

Between 1990 and 2021, the incidence rate of CKD (per 100,000 people) increased significantly for men (188.1%) and among women (153.1%) in Mexico. However, by 2021, this rate was 417.6 among men and 527.5 for women [15]. In the same year, the prevalence rate was 9 400.2 for men and 8 838.1 among women, with a similar increase in both sexes (69.4%) [15].

Overall, the epidemiological analysis of CKD considers sex as a variable that interacts with other clinical and social variables. However, CKD manifests differently in men and women and is mainly influenced by physiological, social, and structural factors that affect the onset and progression of the disease [7–10]. Therefore, more research is needed on the burden of CKD among women, including its impact on morbidity and mortality, to generate evidence that supports the design of ad hoc public policies.

Health disparities are not solely attributable to biological factors, as ample evidence shows how the social and cultural fabric shapes how men and women perceive and interpret diseases, influences behaviors and determines the search for and access to healthcare [6,7,916]. Gender, a socially constructed category, refers to the activities, expectations, and opportunities deemed appropriate for individuals within a given context and the relationships and distribution of power [16].

Inequalities based on sex and gender tend to be more pronounced among individuals with greater socioeconomic vulnerability, leading to inequities. Such is the case with CKD, which has been documented as a disease strongly associated with factors that perpetuate and exacerbate these inequities, particularly among women [7]. Gender affects women’s access to healthcare in various ways. For instance, compared to men, women are more likely not to receive care or to rely on private outpatient services, resulting in catastrophic expenses for themselves and their families [17, 18]. Other gender-related factors affecting women include a healthcare approach centered on sexual and reproductive functions, the socially assigned role of caregivers, lower participation in the formal labor market, and lower social security coverage rates [17, 18].

We aimed to analyze the burden of female CKD in Mexico between 1990 and 2021, expressed in terms of mortality, YLDs, YLLs, and DALYs. Additionally, we evaluated the relationship between DALYs and the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) and the Healthcare Access and Quality Index (HAQI).

Methods

Data source and CKD definition

Secondary data analysis from the Global Burden of Disease, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021 [4]. The methodological approach utilized by the GBD-2021 has been previously published [19, 20]. This Study employed a standardized methodology to produce estimates for each epidemiological quantity of interest for 371 diseases and injuries by age-sex-location-year for 25 age groups from birth to 95 years and older, for males, females, and both sexes combined, in 204 countries and territories grouped into 21 regions and seven super-regions, and for every year from 1990 to 2021 [19, 20].

The GBD used data from vital registration systems, verbal autopsies, censuses, household surveys, disease-specific registries, and health service contact data. The collected data was then corrected for known bias by redistributing deaths from unspecified codes to more specific disease categories [19, 20].

In the GBD, CKD is defined as a permanent loss of renal function, as indicated by an estimated glomerular filtration rate of (eGFR) < 60 ml/min/1.73m2 or albumin to creatinine ratio (ACR) > 30 mg/g [19, 21]. CKD is further categorized into that caused by type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D), arterial hypertension (AH), glomerulonephritis (GN), and all other/unknown causes (OTH) [21].

To create the CKD category, GBD uses codes based on the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD) in its ninth and tenth versions. In the ICD-9, the codes used were 403–404.9, 581–583.9, 585–585.9, 589–589.9, 753–753.3; and in the ICD-10 were D63.1, E10-E11.9, I12-I13.9, N00-N08.8, N15.0, N18-N18.9, P70.2, Q61-Q62.8 [15, 17]. More details on the specific methodological procedures for estimating the burden of CKD can be found in other publications [19–21].

GBD measures and data analysis

For this study, we use the main metrics that make up the description of the burden of the disease, among which are mortality, years of life lost due to premature mortality (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), a combination of the latter two [22]. YLLs measure the gap between the age at death and an ideal life expectancy, assuming minimal risk of death. YLDs quantify the impact of disease by considering its prevalence, complication frequency, and the health loss associated with resulting health states [22].

We report information about mortality, YLLs, YLDs, and DALYs in female Mexicans due to CKD from 1990 to 2021. The information was disaggregated by age subgroups and sub-causes at the national level and for the 32 states. Absolute numbers, age-specific rates, and age-standardized rates (ASR) were used. Standardization was done using the direct method, applying the GBD global-age structure [23].

We used a log-linear segmented regression model to analyze trends in female ASR-DALYs due to CKD over time. This model identifies significant changes in trends, beginning with a single trend line and including up to four joinpoints, creating a maximum of five distinct periods [21]. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) determined the optimal number of joinpoints. We presented the Annual Percent Change (APC) and the Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC), a weighted mean of the APC [24]. The analysis used Joinpoint Regression Version 5.2.0 [25].

Joinpoint regression has been widely applied across various fields to analyze changes in time series data, such as mortality rates, DALYs rates, and disease risk. It is recognized as a powerful tool for detecting trend shifts in epidemiological research and other domains, making it particularly valuable for identifying significant changes in long-term data patterns [25].

We also analyze the relationship between DALYs with the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) and the Healthcare Access and Quality Index (HAQI) [26, 27]. Both indicators were used as a proxy for socioeconomic and health inequalities. The SDI is derived from the average rankings for per capita income, average educational level, and fertility rates in a specific country and year, with a scale from 0 (lowest development level) to 1 (highest development level) [26]. The HAQI is based on preventable deaths that should not occur with timely and effective healthcare, ranging from 0 (worst level) to 100 (best level) [27].

Correlation and regression analysis were applied to the data to define and quantify the relationship between the ASR-DALYs with SDI and HAQI. Correlation analysis estimates the relationship strength between two variables using the correlation coefficient (r), which ranges from − 1 to + 1. A value of − 1 indicates a perfect negative relationship, while + 1 indicates a perfect positive relationship [28]. Linear regression analysis produces an equation (Y = mX + b) that describes the line of best fit, allowing for the prediction of Y values based on X [25]. The coefficient of determination (r²) indicates the degree of variability in Y explained by X [28].

The data were obtained through the GBD Results Tool (GBD Results) and organized in Excel 365® sheets, while the processing was done using the software Stata®18 [29]. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. The 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) are presented for all measurements, represented by the 2·5th and 97·5th percentile values across the draws. The UI analyzes the variability and possible biases in the data and models; therefore, if these do not overlap, the differences between the estimates are considered statistically significant [22]. The analysis was based on the GBD Protocol, which follows the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) [22, 30].

Results

National overview of female CKD burden

In 2021, 33 529 (95%UI 27 769 − 41 395) female deaths were estimated in Mexico due to CKD. Between 1990 and 2021, the number of deaths from CKD women significantly increased by 348% (95% UI 277–437) from 7 484 (95%UI 7 170-7 886) in 1990. The leading causes of female CKD deaths in 2021 were OTH and T2D, each accounting for over 8 000 deaths. Additionally, the number of deaths rose significantly across all CKD sub-causes. The highest growth was seen in T1D-related CKD (395%; 95%UI 280–579), followed by AH (366%; 95%UI 298–454), T2D (351%; 95%UI 286–426), OTH (337%; 95%UI 257–442) and GN (331%; 95%UI 244–449) (data not shown).

The ASR mortality, ASR-DALYs, ASR-YLLs, and ASR-YLDs due to CKD in women are also shown in Table 1. The female ASR mortality due to CKD (per 100 000 inhabitants) increased from 40.4 deaths (95%UI 38.2–43.0) in 1990 to 50.9 (95%UI 42.4–62.3) in 2021 (a significant change of 26.1%; 95%UI 7.2–48.4). Female ASR mortality due to CKD increased for GN, OTH, and T1D between 1990 and 2021. T1D had the highest relative increase (82.2%; 95%UI 40.9-145.4), followed by GN (46.8%; 95%UI 18.8–86.1). In 2021, the highest ASR mortality was found in T2D (12.5; 95%UI 9.8–16), OTH (12.4; 95%UI 9.2–16.5) and AH (12.3; 95%UI 9.4–15.6), with the lowest rate due to T1D (2.4; 95%UI 1.5–3.7). ASR-DALYs due to CKD per 100 000 inhabitants increased 44.7%, from 937.9 (95%UI 893.7–988.0) in 1990 to 1357.1 (95%UI 1112.5, 1708.5) in 2021. Between 1990 and 2021, the female ASR-DALYs due to CKD increased significantly for all sub-causes, especially for T1D, with a rise of 75.5%. In 2021, OTH and GN accounted for 27.5% and 26.3% of the total ASR-DALYs, respectively, while T1D contributed 6.5% to the total ASR-DALYs among women. In 2021, 91% of the female ASR-DALYs due to CKD were attributable to YLLs, while YLDs accounted for only 9%. There was a significant increase of 50.3% in the female ASR-YLLs due to CKD between 1990 and 2021, from 820.7 (95% UI 792-855.7) to 1233.6 (95% UI 1001, 1580.3), respectively. The ASR-YLDs due to CKD had a non-significant increase of 5.4% between 1990 and 2021, from 117.2 (95% UI 83.6–152.0) to 123.5 (95% UI 87.7, 160.1). There was a statistically significant increase in the ASR-YLLs across all CKD sub-causes, while GN was the only CKD sub-cause with a considerable increase in the ASR-YLDs.

Age groups overview of female CKD burden

The age-specific DALYs rates for females due to CKD were highest for individuals aged 85 years or older in both 1990 and 2021 (Fig. 1, A and B). Between these years, the CKD burden for females significantly decreased among those under 15 years old and those 85 years or older. Among females aged 20 to 84 years, age-specific DALYs rates due to CKD saw a significant increase, with rates more than doubling for the 50 to 59 age group between 1990 and 2021.

By sub-cause, the CKD burden in females under 15 years was primarily due to OTH. For those aged 15 to 59, GN was the leading sub-cause of CKD DALYs. In females aged 65 to 84, T2D was the main sub-cause of CKD, and AH was the leading cause for those over 85 years. The age-specific YLDs rates increased significantly among women aged 45–49 and those 85 years or older, while these rates decreased in females under 10. The age-specific YLLs rates followed the same pattern as the DALYs rates, with increases and decreases observed in the same age groups. Premature mortality (YLLs) had the highest percentage of the CKD burden in females between 50 and 74 years of age, while disability (YLDs) was highest in those aged 5–9 and 30 to 44 years of age (Fig. 1, C and D).

Over that period, a statistically significant increase in the female ASR-DALYs due to CKD for women in Mexican states in 1990 and 2021 was observed across 28 states. Chiapas, Tabasco, Veracruz and Tamaulipas experienced a surge of over 70% in the ASR-DALYs due CKD. On the other hand, the ASR-DALYs experienced a non-significant change in Mexico City, Querétaro, Colima, Jalisco, and Guerrero. In 2021, Tabasco and Chiapas (southern Mexico) had the highest female ASR-DALYs due to CKD. In contrast, Sonora, Baja California Sur, Nayarit, and Sinaloa (northwestern region) recorded the lowest ASR-DALYs (Fig. 2, A and B).

In 2021, across all states, sub-causes AH, GN, OTH, and T2D each accounted for a similar percentage (between 18.2% and 28.8%) of the total female ASR-DALYs (with GN and OTH representing more than 50%), while T1D contributed between 5.6% and 7.2% of the ASR-DALYs. These percentages were analogous in 1990 (Fig. 2, C and D).

Trend analysis of female CKD burden

Nationally, the ASR-DALYs due to CKD for women significantly increased between 1990 and 2021 (AAPC of 1.0) (Table 2). In 29 states, a significant increase in the ASR-DALYs was observed, with AAPC ranging from 0.4 in Sonora to 2.2 in Tabasco. Guerrero was the only state that experienced a significant decrease (with an AAPC of -0.3). The national trend of the ASR-DALYs was segmented into four periods, whereas the subnational trends varied, ranging from four to a maximum of five. Nationally, there were several statistically significant periods showing changes in ASR-DALYs: an increase between 1996 and 1999 with an APC of 12.4%, a further rise between 1999 and 2009 with an APC of 2.5%, and a decrease from 2009 to 2021 with an APC of -2.4%. Most states experienced a significant rise in ASR-DALYs during the second period of the trend (around 1996–2000), with APCs ranging from 7.2% in Tamaulipas to 20.1% in Tabasco. Additionally, half of Mexico’s states saw another increase in the third segment of the trend, ending around 2009, with APCs ranging from 0.8% in Mexico City to 4.5% in Veracruz. Finally, in 2010, 24 states experienced a significant decrease in female ASR-DALYs due to CKD, with changes ranging from − 1.9% in Zacatecas to -5.6% in Quintana Roo.

Relationship between SDI and HAQI with female CKD burden

The SDI and the HAQI increased in all states between 1990 and 2021 (Fig. 3). Nationally, we identified a significant positive correlation (0.64) between ASR-DALYs and the SDI. Additionally, 29 states showed a significant positive correlation between these measures. Sonora, Jalisco, and Guerrero were the states where the correlation was non-significant. These results suggest a linear association between sociodemographic development and the CKD burden among females in most regions of Mexico. Concerning the HAQI, we found a positive correlation (0.76) between this index and the female CKD burden. Almost all states, except for Guerrero, had a positive significant correlation between the HAQI and the ASR-DALYs. Moreover, the regression analysis showed that, at the national level and in most states, the ASR-DALYs due to CKD were significantly associated with the SDI and HAQI (Table 3).

Discussion

This is the first study conducted in Mexico that focuses on the specific analysis of the burden of CKD in women. Between 1990 and 2021, the ASR mortality and ASR-DALYs due to CKD increased significantly at the national level. The DALYs are almost entirely explained by YLLs, indicating that a large proportion of women with CKD in Mexico die prematurely. Disparities in the burden of this disease were observed across different regions and age groups. The SDI and HAQI were associated with DALYs due to CKD in most areas.

This study adds to the existing literature on the burden of CKD in women and the disparities in health outcomes. The results can serve as a foundation for further research into the underlying factors contributing to these regional differences and their connection to socioeconomic conditions and access to healthcare. Additionally, the findings provide accurate information that can be used to design and implement evidence-based interventions aimed at reducing the observed disparities in the burden faced by women living with CKD.

Analysis of the connections between CKD and gender is emerging as a relevant topic within nephrology, though it has been scarcely explored so far [7, 31]. Some authors suggest that sex and gender significantly influence CKD and disease progression [7, 8, 31, 32]. It has been found that, compared to men, women are 29% more likely to have CKD, while men are 47% more likely to receive a kidney transplant [8]. Although CKD progresses more slowly in women, their likelihood of dying before receiving any renal replacement therapy is higher than that of men [9, 10, 32]. Similarly, delays in referral for renal replacement therapy and late initiation of dialysis are more common among women [31, 32].

Overall, women live longer but with worse health conditions. For example, in Mexico, women spend 11.8 years of life with a disability or some functional limitation, while men spend 8.8 years under alike circumstances [33]. CKD is one of the leading contributors to the loss of healthy years, as it ranks among the top causes of YLLs and YLDs for women of all ages [11]. Among NCDs, CKD has experienced the highest increase as a cause of YLLs and DALYs in women from 1990 to 2019 [11]. Additionally, Mexican women had the highest ASR mortality due to CKD in Latin America in 2019 [34].

The overview for CKD among Mexican women is not optimistic. National data show that in recent years, the prevalence of NCDs, many of which are associated with CKD, has been steadily increasing. This prevalence tends to be higher among women than in men. For instance, obesity among people aged 20 and older increased from 34.6% (95% confidence interval [CI] 33.5–35.8) in 2006 to 41% (95% CI 38.7–43.3) in 2022, representing an 18.5% change [35]. The prevalence of diabetes in 2022 for this same age group was 20.1% (95% CI 17.1–23.5) compared to 16.3% (95% CI 13.0-20.3) in men [36]. The prevalence of pre-diabetes was 20.9% (95% CI 20.6–29.7), which is 6% points higher than in men [36]. Although hypertension is more prevalent in adult men than in women, it rose to 42.4% (95% CI 40.2–44.7) among women [37].

It has been observed that the prevalence of diseases such as type 2 diabetes (DT2) and CKD is increasing at younger ages [13, 38]. Specifically, the burden of DALYs due to CKD among Mexican women aged 15 to 39 increased by 47.8% (95% UI 28.5–70.8) between 1990 and 2019. Women also experience multimorbidity (the presence of two or more diseases) at a higher rate. A study using data from 2018 to 19 found that the prevalence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity is notably higher among women (30.7%) than among men (23.5%) in Mexico [39]. These differences persist across all age groups but are more pronounced among older adults [39].

The detected contrasts in these indicators must be understood in the context of Mexico’s aging population, which overlaps with the epidemiological and nutritional transitions [5]. The health profile of the Mexican population, and women in particular, has been rapidly changing over the past 30 years [11]. This has led to the coexistence of NCDs, communicable diseases (CDs), and injuries [11]. These diseases include persistent and emerging conditions [11], creating significant pressure on the health systems, which have had to adapt and respond quickly at a high economic cost [40]. The dramatic rise in NCDs has been associated with increased consumption of ultra-processed foods (high in energy, low in nutrients) and sugary drinks and a decline in physical activity among the Mexican population [41–43].

Additionally, the health system is segmented and fragmented [44], with uneven distribution of infrastructure and healthcare resources across the country and heterogeneous quality standards [45, 46]. Segmentation creates different opportunities for various populations, with a more unfavorable impact on women, as social security depends on formal employment [17]. As of early 2024, 55.6% of the economically active female population was employed in the informal sector [47]. Fragmentation, conversely, results in a lack of coordination between healthcare subsystems and care levels, undermining access to and the quality of health services. In this context, women have less social capital (education, support networks, etc.) and fewer economic resources to overcome the barriers that prevent them from fully exercising their right to health [17].

In Mexico, there is no comprehensive renal health policy, and existing health programs lack a gender perspective that considers sex differences in the burden of chronic diseases [18, 43]. However, it has been noted that in lower-middle-income settings, women receive limited and lower-quality medical services [48]. In 2022, it was estimated that 37.5% of women in the country, or 25.1 million, lacked access to healthcare services, rising to 57.2% (2.2 million) among women who speak an indigenous language [49]. The literature indicates that women often have limited or no information and resources to lead a healthier lifestyle, including regular physical activity and a sufficient, balanced diet [48], which also makes them vulnerable to receiving late diagnoses and treatments, reducing their survival [32, 48, 50]. A qualitative study with nephrologists found that women with CKD face more barriers than men in receiving care due to social norms, medical stereotypes, caregiving responsibilities, low empowerment, lack of support, and multiple social and economic disadvantages [51]. It is noteworthy that only 38.0% (95% CI 25.0–50.0) of nephrology professionals in Latin America are women [52], and women are more likely to donate kidneys for transplantation than to receive them [53].

Although this is a valuable analysis of the burden of CKD in women, due to its granularity, future studies must delve into the various intersectional relationships between gender and factors such as social class, age, ethnicity, race, sexual orientation, migration status, and more [18, 54, 55]. This would provide a better understanding of these interactions and how they affect the onset and progression of CKD, access to and quality of medical care, survival, well-being, and quality of life, among other aspects. An intersectional and gender-based framework would support the advancement of a more equitable agenda for women who are at higher risk of kidney disease or who are already living with this disease.

Public health policies on renal health for women, from a gender perspective, must ensure equitable access to medical care, the development of targeted educational campaigns, and training for healthcare personnel on this issue. However, much remains to be addressed, and further research should be promoted that combines an understanding of the biological and social factors influencing the burden of renal disease in women at different life cycle stages.

Among the most recognized limitations of using GBD data are the scarcity and variability of data, especially at the subnational level. This is an ecological study, so the individual characteristics of each person with CKD are unknown, which can also be considered a limitation. However, the GBD employs the principle of continuous assessment of apparent validity by identifying results that may appear nonsensical based on the knowledge of global and local experts [20]. The primary limitation in estimating CKD is adjusting for errors arising from inappropriate coding of underlying causes [18]. To address this, the GBD uses algorithms to reclassify and redistribute what are known as “garbage codes” to appropriate codes for each specific cause of CKD [21]. The GBD is a crucial resource in countries where data on CKD are scarce or insufficient (such as Mexico), and it is considered much more helpful to have these estimates than none at all [20, 21]. This study provides timely, valid, and relevant assessments based on a rigorous review of data and advanced statistical modeling, ensuring the best quality of available data and indicating the level of uncertainty for each measurement [20].

Conclusion

Mexican women experience a significant burden due to CKD, reflected in premature deaths and years lived with disability, while disparities between states need to be addressed to reduce inequities. Over the past 32 years, improvements in socioeconomic indicators and the quality and access to healthcare have not contributed to reducing the DALYs rate due to CKD, indicating a need to redirect policies to impact women’s well-being and health positively. The country´s public health agenda must incorporate a sex and gender perspective in the epidemiological approach to CKD, as well as in actions for risk factor prevention, medical access, treatment, and follow-up of this condition. These are essential steps to achieving the long-awaited justice in renal health.

Addressing CKD in women requires understanding both the physiological aspects and the underlying social factors that influence the burden of the disease and contribute to inequities. Therefore, it is essential to implement comprehensive actions that ensure women’s overall well-being, involving collaboration between the health sector and areas such as education, economy, and social protection. This approach would improve women’s health conditions while enhancing the efficiency and quality of healthcare services.
