Advance Care Planning Among People With Dementia in the Netherlands: Insights From an Exploratory Survey of Health Care Professional Perspectives
Authors: Lise Lutgarde Vandaele, Denise Abbel, D. G. Burghoorn, M. M. H. Strating, M. E. A. van Eersel, P. G. de Graeff, K. M. Vermeulen, B. C. van Munster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdvance care planning (ACP) for patients with dementia has gained increasing attention in recent years, recognizing the importance of aligning future care with the values and preferences of patients with progressive cognitive decline. Health care professionals (HCPs) play an important role in facilitating ACP. However, little is known about the day‐to‐day implementation of ACP in this group.AimThis study therefore aims to gain insights into how HCPs experience discussing frequency, importance, and difficulty of ACP topics with patients with dementia. Additionally, we investigated the determinants of frequency, importance, and difficulty of discussing ACP topics.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey study, where HCPs involved in dementia care in the Netherlands were invited for an online survey. A survey covering 11 topics related to ACP, in which HCPs indicate the importance, frequency, and difficulty of each topic. The survey invitation was sent to hospitals, nursing homes, and dementia care networks by email, flyers at care facilities with QR code, and social media. Data were collected between May 2022 and July 2022.ResultsIn total, 189 HCPs competed the surveys (median 48 years, range: 19–70). The majority of respondents were female (91.0%), had a high educational level (63.5%), and worked parttime (68.3%). Older HCPs experienced more difficulty discussing ACP topics. HCPs scored almost all ACP topics as high importance. Preferred place of death and organ donation were the least discussed topics and were also considered less important than other topics (e.g., quality of life factors). The mean scores of perceived importance of all topics were higher than perceived frequency.ConclusionsHCPs assigned a high level of importance to almost all ACP topics and a low level of difficulty. Training for older HCPs and the utilization of tools/intervention in standard care could improve care or experiences of ACP.

Introduction

Patients with dementia experience increasing difficulties in communication and decision‐making due to cognitive decline [1]. Consequently, self‐advocacy about care becomes more challenging once the disease progresses [2]. Over time, patients with dementia are thus faced with a growing need for psychosocial support, increased reliance on informal caregivers, and greater assistance in the decision‐making process [2, 3].

Advance Care Planning (ACP) empowers patients to plan their care in anticipation of future cognitive decline. This to ensure that when decisions need to be made, values and preferences are already well documented [4, 5]. ACP is associated with increased care satisfaction in patients and reduced stress and anxiety in informal caregivers (ICs) [6]. ACP can be conceptualized as a relationship‐centered definitional framework that emphasizes the involvement of people with dementia and their family. It adopts a holistic perspective in which physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains may be addressed [7]. Implementation of ACP could lead to care that is better aligned with patients' preferences and reduces possible inappropriate hospital admissions [8]. Despite this, implementing ACP in practice is limited, given that ACP is rarely documented in health records [9, 10].

Implementing ACP depends, among other factors, on the attitudes of the healthcare professionals (HCPs) toward ACP [11]. These attitudes are shaped by HCP characteristics such as professional role, age, and gender, which may affect their ability to build the trusting relationships necessary to initiate ACP [12]. The perceived importance of ACP, along with its perceived difficulty, influences the degree to which ACP is implemented in patients with dementia [13, 14]. Whereas literature focuses on the theoretical framework about ACP implementation, little is known about the day‐to‐day implementation of ACP in patients with dementia in the Dutch context. Some authors reported associations between HCPs' characteristics and the attitude and practice regarding ACP [15]. Therefore, this study will investigate the current implementation, perceived importance, and difficulty of ACP in patients with dementia, according to HCPs in the Netherlands and the associated HCPs' characteristics.

Methods

Participants

HCPs were invited to participate in the digital self‐reported survey if they met the following inclusion criteria: (i) they were currently employed in a Dutch healthcare organization that provides care for patients with dementia; (ii) they were aged above 18; and (iii) they were proficient in the Dutch language. All survey participants provided digital informed consent to participate in this study. Under Dutch law, approval from METC was not required due to the scope of the study. The participation in this study was anonymous. Participants were excluded if the demographics did not match the inclusion criteria or if none of the ACP questions were answered.

Survey

Participants were asked to provide information on demographics (age, gender, education level, marital status), their occupational role and weekly working hours (fulltime ≥ 36 h or parttime < 36 h), the type of healthcare organization they work for and their involvement in ACP. The selection of ACP topics for the survey was informed by national guidelines, namely the guideline for advance care planning in the palliative phase. In this guideline, the national standard for recording advance care planning and a conversation card with topics are included [16]. In addition, the Dutch Toolkit Advance Care Planning (LAEGO) [17] was used as a reference framework. Based on these sources, the following ACP topics were incorporated: having a legal representative, resuscitation policy, future hospital admissions, further diagnostics when suspecting serious disease, treatment with antibiotics or other life‐lengthening medication, admission to the intensive care, quality of life factors, perceived loss of meaning of life, preferences in end‐of‐life care (including euthanasia), organ donation, and preferred place of death [14]. The complete Dutch survey is provided in Appendix 1. Participants rated the answers of perceived frequency of ACP topics on a 7‐point Likert scale (strongly agree (++) to strongly disagree (− −)) and the answers of importance and difficulty of ACP topics on a 6‐point Likert scale (strongly agree (++) to strongly disagree (− −)). In addition, there was an option for each question on frequency and importance to indicate “I don't know” or “Not applicable”.

Development and Pilot Testing

Prior to distributing the survey through Qualtrics, the draft was tested in a small subsample of HCPs to ensure acceptability, feasibility, and specificity of the survey. The Cronbach's alpha of all study concepts represents high internal consistency: 0.89 for frequency, 0.96 for importance, and 0.89 for difficulty [18].

Distribution Survey

To include different health care professionals, we aimed for diversity across Dutch geographical regions. In order to reach as many HCPs as possible by means of convenience sampling, invitations for participating in the survey were distributed in three different ways. First, the survey was sent by email to healthcare organizations who are involved in care delivery to patients with dementia. These healthcare organizations included hospital departments, nursing homes, and dementia care networks. Second, the survey was also distributed through social media. One could participate in the survey by scanning a QR code that led to the survey. Third, flyers with the same QR code were distributed among hospital departments who work with patients with dementia and nursing homes. The data were collected between May 2022 and July 2022.

Data Analyses

For data analysis, SPSS version 28.0 was used [19]. Response indications “I don't know” and “Not applicable” were imputed into the neutral category [20, 21]. Total scores for each ACP topic regarding frequency, importance and difficulty were summarized into participants' scores for each topic and mean values with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were presented. Age was categorized into the following age categories: 19–44 years, 45–64 years and 65 years and older [22]. The encountered diversity in occupational roles enabled a comparative analysis per role. To investigate whether physicians and non‐physicians differed in their total scores, the categorical variable ‘occupational role’ was transformed into a binary variable. The three levels of education were categorized into two groups: low/middle level and high level. The reason that low and middle levels were combined was the small size of HCP with a low level of education (n = 2). To ensure the consistency of the different outcomes, the perceived frequency, importance, and difficulty of ACP topics were all rescaled to a 5‐point Likert scale (strongly agree (++) to strongly disagree (− −)). The topics were rescaled to a 5‐point scale to make it possible to develop a radar plot. To compare total scores on frequency, importance and difficulty across different participant characteristics, Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney tests were used.

Data Presentation

Mean scores for each ACP topic are presented in a radar plot, an intuitive descriptive tool for multivariate data [23]. Subsequently, to present a radar plot, mean scores for each topic were calculated by dividing the total sum scores by the number of participants. For establishing the radar plot, R and packages readxl, fmsb, and scales were used [24, 25, 26, 27].

Results

In total, 352 participants started the survey. After excluding participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria (n = 85) and removing participants with missing data (n = 78), 189 (54%) HCPs remained.

Characteristics of Participants

The median age of participants was 48 years (range: 19–70 years), and 91% (n = 172) of the participants were female (Table 1). The majority of participants had a high education level (63.5%). Common places of employment were hospitals (30.2%) and high‐intensity care homes (38.1%). Carers or nurses formed the largest occupational group that responded (42.4%), followed by dementia case managers (18.5%). A larger proportion of the participants worked part‐time (68.3%).

ACP—Perceived Importance

Participants indicated that all topics were of importance (Figure 1b). Topics of great perceived importance were having a resuscitation policy (83.1% indicated ‘very important’), quality of life factors (85.7% indicated ‘very important’), and experienced loss of meaning of life (75.7% indicated ‘very important’).

ACP—Perceived Difficulty

Participants indicated that they had little difficulty discussing all topics (not surpassing mean neutral score +/−; see Figure 1c). Compared to other topics, the discussed preferences in end‐of‐life care seemed slightly more difficult (6.9% indicated ‘very difficult’).

ACP —Relative Comparison Between Frequency, Importance and Difficulty of Discussing ACP Topics

Whereas participants scored perceived importance of all topics as high and the perceived difficulty of topics as low, scores for perceived frequency of topic discussion remain in between (see Figure 1d). Mapping the mean scores for each ACP topic in the radar plot gives the impression that mean scores of perceived importance are larger than those of perceived frequency. Except for organ donation, the perceived difficulty was higher than the frequency. The perceived frequency and importance of quality of life factors were the highest.

Determinants of Frequency, Importance and Difficulty of Discussing ACP Topics

The total mean sum scores (tmss) of difficulty differed between age groups (p = 0.035) (Table 2). Older HCPs had higher tmss of difficulty (33.67 [95% CI, −4.84−72.18]), compared to younger (27.38 [95% CI, 25.34–29.41]) and middle‐aged adults (24.41 [95% CI, 22.60–26.21]). The tmss for importance appeared to differ between males and females (p = 0.006). In female participants, a higher mean tmss for importance was observed (48.92 [95% CI, 48.19 to 49.65]) compared to male participants (44.71 [95% CI, 41.14–28.48]). Lastly, tmss for frequency differed across employment status (p = 0.030). Participants who worked full time exhibited a higher mean tmss for frequency (42.73 [95% CI, 40.96–44.50]) in comparison to those who worked part time (40.79 [95% CI, 39.60–41.98]). Notably, the other characteristics showed no significant differences (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to provide insights into the current implementation of ACP in patients with dementia according to a heterogenous group of Dutch HCPs. Overall, the importance of most topics was high, whereas the perceived difficulty of talking about ACP topics was low. Despite this, not every topic of ACP is frequently discussed (preferred place of death and organ donation). Characteristics of HCP, such as age, gender, and employment status, were associated with the perceived frequency, importance, and difficulty.

Most frequently discussed topics according to HCPs were resuscitation policy, loss of meaning of life, and factors for optimizing quality of life. However, preferred place of death and organ donation were the least discussed topics of ACP. We hypothesize that the observed difference in ACP topics is due to the topics' applicability in particular healthcare settings. For example, the preferred place of death may be less relevant for nursing home residents as there is a high likelihood that the place of death of the residents will be in a nursing home. Given that comorbidity is common in nursing home residents, the large share of residents will not be eligible for organ donation [28]. 38.1% of our HCPs worked in a nursing home; further research involving a larger sample size is recommended.

In particular, a difference has been found between different age groups and difficulty. For instance, older HCPs tend to have higher difficulty scores, which may seem counterintuitive given their greater experience in communicating with patients with dementia. One possible explanation of why older HCPs may find discussing ACP topics more difficult may relate to the generational difference regarding emphasis on ACP in education. Over the past decade, ACP gained momentum in the Dutch medical training [29]. As a result, younger HCPs may be more comfortable with discussing ACP context as part of shared decision making. Another characteristic that seemed to play a role in the perceived importance of ACP was gender. Specifically, in our study, female participants reported higher levels of importance for ACP, which is noteworthy given that 91% of the sample consisted of female participants. Additionally, it is important to highlight that most HCPs working in Dutch dementia care are female [30]. HCPs who work full time discuss ACP more frequently than those who work part time, probably because they have greater opportunities to build extensive patient relations compared to parttime employees and, as such, are more likely to engage in discussions about ACP. The degree to which HCPs can form extensive patient relations is a known facilitator of the ACP initiation process [14]. Reported differences across HCP characteristics and ACP perception should be interpreted with caution as it is unclear whether these findings represent the entire population.

All in all, this study illustrates the multifaceted nature of discussing ACP topics in daily practice. Several HCP characteristics play a role in the HCP's perception about ACP implementation in daily practice. Not all topics are always prioritized or applicable to each person with dementia. As such, outcomes of this study illustrate that ACP should always be adjusted to the patient. Improvement in frequency could be achieved through the utilization of tools or standard care, ensuring that all topics relevant to ACP are addressed. Although ACP must be adapted to the specific context, education and training can enhance the awareness of all its topics.

This is the first study to provide insights into ACP's current implementation, perceived importance, and difficulty with patients with dementia in Dutch clinical practice. This contribution had several strengths. First, the survey was distributed through different channels to ensure inclusivity across all healthcare settings. Second, the participation was anonymous, which minimized socially desirable responses. However, readers should also be aware of several limitations of this study. First, although the dataset was quite substantial, some of the subgroups were small (e.g., HCP's who were male or had a low educational level). The representation of gender in our study is not proportional to the gender distribution in the overall workforce, in 2020, approximately 1 in 5 HCPs were male (26). Second, we cannot rule out the possibility of response bias as HCPs with more affinity with ACP may have been more likely to participate in the survey. Heterogeneity could not be avoided in this study as ACP implementation was explored across multiple professional groups who inherently differ in training, responsibilities, and clinical contexts. Such variability is intrinsic to multidisciplinary dementia care and reflects real‐world practice. The variability between HCPs may influence the outcomes, which require careful interpretation. Differences in professional roles and settings can introduce confounding, reduce the representativeness, and obscure subgroup‐specific patterns. A third limitation is that respondents likely answered based on their own clinical experience, which may vary depending on the stage of dementia they most frequently encounter. This variability could have influenced the diversity of responses. Despite the heterogeneity and variability in clinical experience, the findings of this study provide insights into the current ACP implementation. The study offers clinically relevant insights that may inform policy development and educational initiatives for integrating ACP more consistently into routine dementia care. Lastly, due to cultural and political differences, our study results including solely Dutch HCPs might not be generalizable to HCPs in other countries.

The importance and frequency of ACP with people with dementia, according to HCPs, were already relatively high. This exploratory study underscores the need for further research involving larger and more diverse samples across care settings to enable reliable comparisons between different groups of HCPs. Other factors for successful ACP implementation, including HCPs' skills, the timing of ACP, and patients' preferences and experiences, were not measured in this study. Future research about ACP implementation in daily practice should also involve different perspectives, such as patients and their informal caregivers. It would be important to study whether and how HCPs' perspectives on ACP practice compare to the views of patients and their informal caregivers.

Conclusion

The findings of this exploratory survey demonstrate the multifaceted nature of discussing ACP topics in daily practice. Not all topics are always prioritized or applicable to each person with dementia, which illustrates the person‐centered approach. HCPs assigned a high level of importance to almost all ACP topics and a low level of difficulty, yet these topics are not discussed as frequently as their perceived importance suggests. Although these findings provide valuable insights, they should be interpreted with caution due to the small and heterogeneous sample. Nevertheless, these exploratory results provide a useful starting point for future efforts to integrate ACP more consistently into routine dementia care. Future research should include larger and more diverse samples of HCPs to enable robust subgroup analyses.
