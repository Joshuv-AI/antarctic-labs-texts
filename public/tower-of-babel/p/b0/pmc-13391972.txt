Association of Serum Adiponectin, Sleep Quality, and Health-promoting Lifestyle with Metabolic Syndrome in Patients with Schizophrenia
Authors: Smit Ratilal Makwana, Naresh Nebhinani, Pranshu Singh, Dharamveer Yadav, Akshaya B Raj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Metabolic syndrome (MS) significantly contributes to premature mortality in schizophrenia, which is driven by multiple biological and behavioral factors. While individual risk factors are known, comprehensive studies examining the combined influence of biological markers (adiponectin) and behavioral factors (sleep, lifestyle) in the Indian context are limited.Methods:In this cross-sectional, observational study, 130 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia were assessed for MS using modified National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III criteria. We evaluated symptom severity using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale, Sleep quality using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and lifestyle behaviors using the Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile-II. Blood samples were analyzed for serum adiponectin and metabolic profiles to diagnose MS.Results:The MS rate was 24.6%. Bivariate analyses revealed that patients with MS had a significantly longer time spent in exacerbation (p = .03), a longer treatment duration (p = .01), lower serum adiponectin levels (p = .02), poorer sleep quality (p = .001), and lower physical activity and nutritional habit scores (p = .01). In the multivariable logistic regression model, poorer sleep quality (OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.06–1.30, p = .002) and a lower physical activity and nutritional habit score (OR = 0.92, 95% CI = 0.86–0.98, p = .01) emerged as significant predictors of MS.Conclusions:One-fourth of the patients with schizophrenia had MS in this cohort. Notably, modifiable behavioral factors—specifically sleep quality and lifestyle—were stronger predictors of metabolic risk. This signifies the vital need to integrate routine screening for cardiometabolic risk factors and to apply therapeutic strategies into the standard of care for schizophrenia.Keywords: Adiponectin, lifestyle, metabolic syndrome, schizophrenia, sleep

Schizophrenia is a severe mental disorder associated with high premature mortality, primarily due to cardiovascular comorbidities. 1 This mortality gap is due to physical comorbidities, with cardiovascular diseases accounting for approximately 60% of the excess mortality. A primary reason for cardiovascular risk is metabolic syndrome (MS), a cluster of cardiometabolic risk factors including abdominal obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, elevated blood pressure, and impaired fasting glucose.2–4 The prevalence of MS in patients with schizophrenia is two to four times greater than in the general population.3,5 As per the meta-analyses, it is reported to be around 33.4% globally, 6 26.3% in Indian studies, 6 and 29.83% in another meta-analysis of Indian research. 7

The high prevalence of MS in schizophrenia arises from a complex interplay of the illness itself, genetic predispositions, lifestyle challenges, and the metabolic side effects of essential antipsychotic medications. Current evidence indicates that multiple biological and behavioral factors are critically involved in this association. 8

Serum adiponectin, a protective adipokine, enhances insulin sensitivity and reduces inflammation. 9 Schizophrenia patients with MS exhibit significantly lower adiponectin, higher leptin, and an increased leptin-to-adiponectin (L/A) ratio.10,11 Though antipsychotics independently influence adiponectin,12,13 its reduction fundamentally correlates with accumulating MS components, demonstrating positive correlations with HDL-C and negative correlations with diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, and triglycerides. 14 This mechanism is vital for the Asian “thin-fat” demographic, which is characterized by higher metabolic risks at lower body mass indices. 7 While Indian cohorts corroborate this inverse association between adiponectin and MS,15,16 specific research within the Indian schizophrenia population remains sparse.

Concurrently, behavioral factors such as poor sleep quality and unhealthy lifestyle habits (e.g., low physical activity, poor nutrition) are critical, modifiable risk factors for metabolic dysregulation in this population.5,17–19 Systematic reviews have demonstrated that non-pharmacological interventions targeting these lifestyle factors are effective in reducing weight and cardiometabolic risk in schizophrenia. 20

Rationale for the Study

Till now, very few Indian studies have explored the role of serum adiponectin or other biological markers in predicting MS among patients with schizophrenia. Furthermore, the study employs a comprehensive approach by simultaneously assessing this biomarker alongside critical modifiable behavioral factors, including sleep quality and lifestyle, within a single cohort. This comprehensive assessment is a major methodological advantage, in contrast to isolated studies that examine these variables separately. Our approach enables the identification of true, independent predictors of MS by accounting for the complex, interconnected nature of these factors.

Hypothesis

The poorer sleep quality, less health-promoting lifestyle, and lower serum adiponectin levels will be significantly associated with a higher risk of MS in Indian patients with schizophrenia.

Aims

To investigate the combined influence of serum adiponectin, sleep quality, and health-promoting lifestyle behaviors on MS in patients with schizophrenia to inform holistic preventive strategies.

Methods

This cross-sectional, observational study was conducted between October 2023 and December 2024 in the Psychiatry outpatient and inpatient settings of a multispecialty tertiary care center in India.

Ethical Considerations

The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their enrollment in the study. For any participant whose illness severity impaired their capacity to consent, written informed consent was obtained from their Legally Authorized Representative or Guardian. As this study enrolled only adults aged 18–60 years, assent for minors was not required.

After obtaining written informed consent, 130 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia according to the criteria of the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11), 21 who were aged 18–60 and able to understand English or Hindi, were enrolled via purposive sampling. The diagnosis of schizophrenia was established through a comprehensive clinical interview conducted by a psychiatrist, supplemented by medical records. Individuals with other major psychiatric disorders (e.g., active substance use disorders, except tobacco, severe intellectual disability) or chronic medical conditions that could independently confound the metabolic or behavioral assessments (e.g., uncontrolled thyroid dysfunction, chronic inflammatory diseases, Cushing’s syndrome, polycystic ovary syndrome, chronic kidney or liver disease, active systemic infections, or physical disabilities limiting mobility) were excluded.

The sample size was determined based on a time-bound, purposive sampling strategy. All eligible patients who presented to the psychiatry outpatient and inpatient settings during the predefined study period (October 2023 to December 2024) and met the inclusion criteria were approached for the study. This comprehensive recruitment approach yielded a final, feasible sample of 130 participants. This sample size was deemed statistically adequate to power the primary multivariable logistic regression analysis and to detect significant associations between the targeted biological and behavioral predictors.

Any individuals identified with MS or other medical abnormalities during the study were appropriately counseled, referred to the relevant medical outpatient departments, and initiated on treatment as required.

This manuscript was drafted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for observational studies, and the completed STROBE reporting checklist has been uploaded as Supplementary online material.22,23

Variables

The primary outcome variable in this study was the presence of MS, diagnosed according to the modified National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) criteria adapted for South Asian populations. This diagnosis required the presence of at least three of the following five cardiometabolic abnormalities: central obesity (waist circumference ≥90 cm in males or ≥80 cm in females), hypertriglyceridemia (≥150 mg/dL or on specific lipid-lowering medication), reduced HDL cholesterol (<40 mg/dL in males or <50 mg/dL in females), elevated blood pressure (≥130/85 mmHg or on antihypertensive treatment), and elevated fasting blood glucose (≥100 mg/dL or on antidiabetic treatment). 24

The primary predictor variables (exposures) assessed for their independent association with the outcome included the biomarker serum adiponectin, along with behavioral factors such as sleep quality and lifestyle profile. To isolate the true predictive value of these variables, relevant sociodemographic and clinical parameters were considered as potential confounders.

Tools

Comprehensive sociodemographic details and clinical information were collected using a semi-structured proforma. Anthropometric data essential for the MS diagnostic criteria, specifically waist circumference and resting blood pressure, were measured utilizing a standard non-stretchable measuring tape and a calibrated sphygmomanometer, respectively.

The following scales were administered:

Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)

An 18-item scale used by clinicians to measure the severity of psychiatric symptoms based on patient interview and observation over the previous 2–3 days. Each item is rated on a 7-point scale from “not present” to “extremely severe,” with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.65–0.87). 25 The scale has demonstrated high inter-rater reliability and validity in the Indian setting, where it has been successfully used to differentiate between symptom subtypes in psychiatric populations. 26

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

A 19-item self-rated questionnaire assessing sleep quality and disturbances over the past month. It yields seven component scores, which are summed to produce a global score. A global score greater than five indicates poor sleep quality, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.74 to 0.75. 27 The scale has been validated for use in the Indian population, demonstrating good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.736). 28

Health-promoting Lifestyle Profile-II (HPLP-II)

A self-rated scale measuring health-promoting behavior. For this study, the subscales for physical activity (8 items) and nutritional habits (9 items) were utilized to assess these specific lifestyle domains. A composite score of the sum of both of the above was calculated as the physical activity and nutritional habit score (higher scores indicate a better lifestyle profile, whereas lower scores reflect poorer health-promoting behaviors), with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.89 to 0.94. 29

Biochemical Analysis

Fasting venous blood samples were collected in the morning after overnight fasting and analyzed at our National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories (NABL) accredited Clinical Biochemistry laboratory for metabolic profiles and adiponectin. Serum was stored at −80°C for analysis of adiponectin levels, which were measured using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To minimize potential assessment bias, standardized instructions were provided to all participants before administering the self-report questionnaires (PSQI and HPLP-II), and all biochemical analyses were conducted by laboratory personnel blinded to the patients’ clinical and psychiatric status.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). 30 Due to non-normal data distribution confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test, non-parametric tests were used. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for bivariate comparisons of continuous variables, and the Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used for categorical variables. Simple binary logistic regression was performed to estimate unadjusted odds ratios for the variables included in the final model. Finally, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the independent predictors of MS. Variables that demonstrated statistical significance (p < .05) in the bivariate analyses were entered into the regression model to control for potential confounding. Assumptions for logistic regression, including absence of multicollinearity and linearity of the logit, were verified before analysis. The statistical protocol specified that any missing data would be handled using listwise deletion for the multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Profile

During the recruitment period, 155 patients were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 15 did not meet the inclusion criteria (e.g., due to comorbid chronic medical conditions or being outside the age range), and 10 declined to participate (primarily citing a lack of time), resulting in a final sample of 130 patients. The flow of participants through the study is detailed in Figure 1. The study sample comprised 130 patients with a mean age of 33.94 years (SD ± 12.60 years). Complete data were available for all included participants, with no missing values for the primary biological or behavioral variables of interest. The majority of participants were male (65.4%), married (56.2%), and resided in urban localities (51.5%) (Table 1). The most common diagnostic subtype was “Schizophrenia, continuous, currently symptomatic” (57.7%). The mean duration of illness was 94.81 months, and the mean duration of treatment was 54.48 months.

Rate of MS, Biological, and Behavioral Factors

The rate of MS in the cohort was 24.6% (n = 32). The most frequent metabolic abnormalities were reduced HDL cholesterol (50.8%) and elevated waist circumference (43.8%). The mean serum adiponectin level was 42.58 µg/mL. The majority of participants (82.3%) had a Global PSQI score greater than 5, indicating poor sleep quality.

Biomarker and Behavioral Factors

Serum Adiponectin

Lower serum adiponectin levels were significantly associated with the presence of MS (mean rank 51.63 for the MS group vs. 70.03 for the non-MS group; p = .02).

Sleep Quality

Patients with MS had significantly higher Global PSQI scores, indicating poorer sleep quality (mean rank 84.31 vs. 59.36; p = .001).

Health-promoting Lifestyle

Lower physical activity and nutritional habit scores were significantly associated with MS (p = .01), suggesting that a less health-promoting lifestyle is a risk factor (Table 3).

Global PSQI Score

Poorer sleep quality remained a significant predictor of MS (OR = 1.17; 95% CI = 1.06–1.30; p = .002). For each one-point increase in the Global PSQI score, the odds of having MS increased by 17.4%.

Physical Activity and Nutritional Habit Score

A less health-promoting lifestyle also remained a significant predictor (OR = 0.92; 95% CI = 0.86–0.98; p = .01). For each one-point increase in the health-promoting lifestyle score, the odds of having MS decreased by 8.3%.

Discussion

This study focused on a comprehensive investigation into the associations between MS, serum adiponectin, and key behavioral factors in Indian patients with schizophrenia. The findings not only confirm the significant health burden in this population but also highlight specific, modifiable targets for clinical intervention.

The present study identified MS in 24.6% of patients with schizophrenia, a rate significantly higher than the general Indian adult population estimates of 15%–16%.31,32 Our findings align with prevalence rates reported in previous literature, including a pooled prevalence of 26.3% in Indian studies, as reported by Vancampfort et al. 6 and 29.86% in the Indian population, as reported by Ganesh et al. 7 The variances in prevalence across different studies may be because of multiple factors including differences in the specific diagnostic criteria used for MS (e.g., Adult Treatment Panel III vs. International Diabetes Federation), variations in socioeconomic conditions, healthcare systems across regions, diverse methodologies employed in the studies, characteristics of the patient populations (e.g., age, ethnicity, medication status—our study included patients on various antipsychotics), duration of illness and severity of illness (e.g., illness severity profile in our sample—predominantly mildly ill 63.8%). 33

In the index study, no statistically significant differences were found between schizophrenia patients with and without MS concerning age, monthly family income, sex, marital status, locality (urban/rural), occupation, family type, religion, education years, or current tobacco use. This suggests that within this specific population, these common demographic factors may not be the primary distinguishing features, and illness-related variables might play a more dominant role.

We observed that patients with MS had a significantly longer period spent in exacerbation and a significantly longer duration of cumulative treatment compared to those without MS. This finding is consistent with the understanding that prolonged illness exacerbation and longer exposure to antipsychotic medications, many of which have known metabolic side effects, increase the risk of developing MS.2,5 The increased duration of exacerbations might also reflect poorer illness control or more severe underlying illness, which could contribute to lifestyle disruptions or neurobiological changes leading to MS. 34

The significant association between lower serum adiponectin levels and MS in our cohort corroborates international studies that establish low adiponectin as a reliable biomarker of metabolic dysfunction.9,10,35 This is further supported by studies in the general Indian population, which also confirmed a negative association between adiponectin and MS.15,16 This is particularly relevant in the Indian context, where adiponectin dysregulation is strongly linked to the “thin-fat” phenotype and insulin resistance even at lower body mass indices.

Our study revealed a high prevalence of poor sleep quality (82.3%) among participants with schizophrenia. This is consistent with reviews by Pritchett et al. 17 and Kaskie et al., 36 which indicate that sleep and circadian disruptions affect 30%–80% of individuals with this illness, often preceding the diagnosis. Crucially, we found that patients with MS had significantly higher (worse) Global PSQI scores compared to those without MS. This aligns with findings by Balcioglu et al., 37 who reported a 64% prevalence of poor sleep in schizophrenia and directly linked it to elevated total cholesterol, LDL levels, and higher mean blood pressure driven by an evening chronotype. Given that the link between poor sleep and MS is well-established in both the general population and among patients with schizophrenia, 38 our findings further confirm that sleep disturbances act as active, independent drivers of cardiometabolic risk, rather than secondary symptoms of the psychiatric illness.

Our study found that a less health-promoting lifestyle was significantly associated with MS. Patients with MS had significantly lower scores on the Physical activity and Nutritional habit score, which is consistent with previous Indian research by Malhotra et al. 19

Our study found that sleep quality and a health-promoting lifestyle were significant predictors of MS in the given population, even after controlling for other factors. That is consistent with global research by Okubo et al. 39 and Yan et al., 18 who found subjective sleep quality and sleep latency as independent predictors of MS in patients with schizophrenia. This aligns with evidence linking sleep disturbances to disrupting the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in altered cortisol rhythms and increased systemic inflammation. These neuroendocrine changes impair glucose metabolism and dysregulate appetite-controlling hormones (leptin and ghrelin), directly promoting central adiposity and insulin resistance.38,40–42 A recent study by Dinesh et al., 40 did not find a significant association between MS and poor sleep in their cohort. The difference in our findings is likely due to our larger sample size (130 patients vs. 60 in the earlier study), which provided greater statistical power to detect this relationship.

This study confirmed the protective role of a health-promoting lifestyle, which correlates with recent research from China by Fan et al., 43 and with Indian research by Malhotra et al. 19 and Grover et al. 44 Furthermore, a study by Şanlıer et al. 45 showed that patients consumed more daily energy and fat and had lower physical activity levels than controls. The potential for positive change is supported by trials such as that of Fernández-Abascal et al., 46 which demonstrated that combined exercise and psychoeducation interventions were effective in improving health outcomes. This can be explained by the fact that patients with schizophrenia face substantial barriers to maintaining healthy behaviors, including the negative symptoms of the illness (e.g., avolition, anhedonia), cognitive deficits, side effects of many antipsychotic medications leading to obesity, and socioeconomic disadvantages. Furthermore, recent research suggests that patients often have less “personal control” over their health behaviors compared to healthy individuals and lack specific motivators to promote lifestyle changes. 47

Despite the pronounced difference in mean adiponectin levels between the two groups in the initial analysis, serum adiponectin was not retained as a significant predictor in the regression model for MS. This suggests that behavioral factors like poor sleep quality and an unhealthy lifestyle may exert a more direct and potent influence on the development of MS than the circulating levels of adiponectin alone. It is plausible that the effects of these lifestyle factors are, in part, mediated through their influence on adiponectin and other adipokines. Essentially, poor sleep and sedentary behavior may drive the reduction in adiponectin; thus, targeting these modifiable behaviors could yield greater clinical benefits than focusing solely on biological markers.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Mitchell et al. 48 identified older age, longer illness duration, and specific antipsychotics (e.g., clozapine) as key predictors for MS. While our initial bivariate analyses showed a significant association of MS with duration of treatment and total period spent in exacerbation, their effects were attenuated in the final multivariable model. This suggests that their influence on MS risk may be mediated through their impact on sleep and lifestyle. For instance, the sedative or activating effects of medications and exacerbation of illness can disrupt sleep patterns, and medication-induced weight gain can discourage physical activity, making sleep quality and lifestyle the more proximal determinants of metabolic outcomes. This aligns with evidence suggesting that, regardless of the specific antipsychotic regimen, metabolic abnormalities are prevalent and that lifestyle interventions remain a safe and effective strategy for management.

Implementing these findings in busy psychiatric outpatient settings requires practical, low-resource strategies. First, routine preconsultation metabolic screening (e.g., tracking waist circumference and blood pressure) must be systematized. Second, sleep quality should be managed via targeted psychoeducation, native-language sleep hygiene pamphlets, and timing antipsychotic medications to align with the patient’s natural sleep–wake cycle. Third, lifestyle interventions must actively include family caregivers, who are the cornerstone of Indian healthcare, by prescribing specific, measurable behavioral targets (e.g., 30 minutes of supervised brisk walking daily) rather than vague advice. Where feasible, establishing dedicated, multidisciplinary “metabolic clinics” within psychiatry departments will provide optimal, structured support for sustained behavioral change.

Strengths

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published study in the Indian context that evaluates the biological marker serum adiponectin specifically among patients with schizophrenia. Furthermore, the study employs a comprehensive approach by simultaneously assessing this biomarker alongside critical modifiable behavioral factors, including sleep quality and lifestyle, within a single cohort. Additionally, the larger sample size provided greater statistical power to detect significant metabolic and behavioral relationships than previous regional studies.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study precludes establishing causal relationships; it demonstrates association, not causation. The reliance on self-report measures for sleep (PSQI) and lifestyle (HPLP-II) could be subject to recall bias. This was a single-center hospital-based study; the findings may have limited generalizability to other regions or clinical settings in India. Additionally, while we accounted for total treatment duration, the inability to control for recent medication changes, specific antipsychotic metabolic profiles, and smoking status remains a limitation that future longitudinal studies should address.

Conclusions

This study confirms a high rate of MS among Indian patients with schizophrenia. The presence of MS was significantly associated with lower serum adiponectin levels and could be predicted by poor sleep quality and lifestyle factors (lesser physical activity and nutritional habits). These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated care models that prioritize physical health alongside psychiatric management. Clinicians should implement routine screening for MS and its behavioral risk factors, with particular emphasis on developing and implementing non-pharmacological interventions to improve sleep hygiene and promote healthy physical activity and nutritional habits. Efforts are already underway in India to develop such multicomponent lifestyle modification packages tailored specifically for persons with schizophrenia, and our findings further emphasize the necessity of such integrated care models promptly. 49
