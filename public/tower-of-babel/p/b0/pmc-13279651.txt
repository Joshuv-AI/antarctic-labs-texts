Two-port laparoscopic placement of a peritoneal dialysis catheter using guideline-based trans-rectus insertion, rectus sheath tunneling, and a dedicated exit site: a technical note
Authors: Ichiro Tamaki, Tatsuki Kusuhara, Yusuke Fukui, Yoshikuni Yonenaga, Takashi Nitta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We describe a simple modification of the two-port laparoscopic technique for peritoneal dialysis (PD) catheter placement incorporating guideline-recommended trans-rectus insertion and dedicated exit-site design. A 5-mm trocar is inserted through a small trans-rectus incision using an open (Hasson) technique and subsequently serves as the final catheter insertion site. After placement of the first port, a second 5-mm port is safely inserted at the planned superficial cuff site under laparoscopic guidance and subsequently serves as the camera port for the remainder of the procedure. After laparoscopic inspection, the PD catheter is introduced along an oblique trans-rectus pathway to enhance catheter stability, and the superficial cuff is secured at the port site. The catheter is then directed through a subcutaneous tunnel and exteriorized through a small exit site. This modification provides a simple and reproducible option for PD catheter placement.

INTRODUCTION

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is an established renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease, and its long-term success largely depends on reliable peritoneal access [1]. Various techniques for PD catheter insertion have been described, including open surgical, percutaneous, and laparoscopic approaches [2]. Among these, laparoscopic techniques allow direct visualization of the peritoneal cavity and facilitate adjunctive procedures such as adhesiolysis or omentopexy, which may improve catheter function [3,4].

In the early 2000s, two-port laparoscopic techniques were introduced as a modification of conventional three-port methods, allowing catheter placement under direct vision with fewer ports. However, some previously reported two-port techniques involve placement of the first port through the midline and utilize the accessory port site as the catheter exit site [5,6].

The 2019 guidelines for optimal PD access published by the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis recommend avoiding midline insertion in favor of a paramedian approach and discourage the use of laparoscopic port sites as catheter exit sites [7]. Therefore, certain aspects of previously described techniques may not fully align with current recommendations for optimal PD access.

To address these issues, we developed a modified laparoscopic PD catheter insertion technique designed to comply with the principles described in the 2019 guidelines. This technique avoids midline access and allows independent planning of the catheter exit site while maintaining a minimally invasive two-port approach.

In this report, we describe the surgical technique and discuss its potential advantages for safe and guideline-concordant PD catheter placement.

TECHNIQUE

The key steps of the surgical procedure are illustrated in Fig. 1. The procedure is performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. A dual-cuff peritoneal dialysis catheter with a preformed bend is used. Before the procedure, the planned positions of the deep (first) cuff and superficial (second) cuff are marked on the skin to guide port placement.

A 3-cm vertical skin incision is made at the planned deep cuff site using a trans-rectus approach. The incision site is selected to direct the catheter toward the pelvis and facilitate appropriate pelvic positioning of the catheter tip. The anterior and posterior sheaths of the rectus abdominis muscle are incised to expose the peritoneum. A 3-0 Vicryl suture (Ethicon) is placed in the peritoneum as a stay suture, after which the peritoneum is opened. A 5-mm port is inserted using an open (Hasson) technique [8]. The abdominal cavity is then inspected laparoscopically to assess for intra-abdominal adhesions. A second 5-mm port is safely inserted at the planned superficial cuff site under laparoscopic guidance. This port subsequently serves as the camera port for the remainder of the procedure, providing stable visualization to facilitate accurate positioning of the PD catheter within the pelvis. Under laparoscopic visualization from the second port, the initial trocar at the deep cuff site is removed and exchanged for the PD catheter. Under direct laparoscopic visualization, a 3-0 Vicryl purse-string suture is placed proximal to the cuff, followed by circumferential fixation of the posterior rectus sheath and peritoneum to the cuff with four interrupted sutures. Endoscopic guidance ensures that the cuff remains extraperitoneal and prevents inadvertent intraperitoneal exposure, thereby reducing the potential risk of future visceral adhesions. Absence of leakage is confirmed, and if an obvious air leak is detected, additional sutures are placed externally under laparoscopic observation.

Subsequently, a stylet is advanced within the catheter under laparoscopic visualization to guide the tip into the pouch of Douglas. Correct positioning is confirmed directly. In most cases, catheter tip positioning can be achieved with a stylet alone without additional laparoscopic manipulation.

After confirmation of the final catheter tip position, the anterior rectus sheath is closed while guiding the catheter through the rectus muscle in an oblique craniocaudal direction. This maneuver directs the catheter toward the pelvis and creates an oblique trans-rectus tunnel before the catheter is advanced into the subcutaneous layer. This configuration is consistent with the principles described in the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD) guidelines, which recommend oblique passage of the catheter through the rectus sheath to encourage pelvic orientation of the catheter tip.

The scope port site corresponds to the fixation site of the superficial (second) cuff. After removal of the scope port, the fascial defect is closed with 2-0 Vicryl, and using the same suture, the superficial cuff is simultaneously secured to the fascia. The catheter is then tunneled subcutaneously to the exit site. The exit site is created by a small skin puncture using a sharp introducer, allowing the catheter to be brought out with a snug fit and minimal skin–catheter gap.

DISCUSSION

This technique has been applied in nine consecutive patients since December 2023, with all procedures completed laparoscopically without intraoperative complications. The median follow-up period was 412 days (range, 31–884 days). No catheter migration or dialysate leakage was observed during the period. One patient receiving antiplatelet therapy developed postoperative bleeding requiring surgical hemostasis. One patient developed an exit-site infection 6 months after surgery.

Laparoscopic placement of peritoneal dialysis (PD) catheters has become widely accepted because it allows direct visualization of the peritoneal cavity and accurate positioning of the catheter tip. In addition, laparoscopy facilitates the identification of intra-abdominal adhesions and enables adjunctive procedures when necessary [3,9,10]. Various laparoscopic techniques have been reported, most commonly using two or three ports [4].

Several authors have described two-port laparoscopic techniques for PD catheter insertion since the early 2000s, demonstrating that the procedure can be safely performed with fewer access ports while maintaining adequate visualization of the abdominal cavity. For example, Kun Moo Choi and colleagues reported a case series of two-port laparoscopic PD catheter placement with favorable outcomes in 2014 [5]. These reports suggest that reducing the number of ports may decrease surgical invasiveness without compromising procedural safety [5,6].

Another important consideration in PD access surgery is adherence to the principles recommended in the 2019 ISPD Peritoneal Dialysis Access Guideline. In addition to laparoscopic visualization, the guideline emphasizes surgical principles traditionally described in open surgical dissection techniques. These include trans-rectus catheter insertion and the creation of an intramuscular or fascial tunnel, which help stabilize the catheter, reduce the risk of catheter migration, and prevent dialysate leakage [7].

In the present technique, the catheter is introduced through a trans-rectus pathway with a fascial tunnel created within the abdominal wall. This configuration is intended to provide mechanical stability of the catheter and to reduce the likelihood of postoperative catheter displacement or pericatheter dialysate leakage. Thus, although the procedure is performed laparoscopically with only two ports, the catheter fixation concept follows the established surgical principles recommended for PD access creation.

Another feature of the present technique is the method used to create the catheter exit site. The catheter is tunneled subcutaneously and exteriorized through a small, dedicated skin puncture rather than through a laparoscopic port wound. Creating a narrow skin opening around the catheter helps minimize the gap between the catheter and surrounding tissue, which may contribute to the prevention of exit-site infection and subcutaneous tunnel infection [7].

While previously reported two-port laparoscopic techniques have demonstrated the feasibility of reduced-port surgery, the detailed methods for exit-site creation vary among reports and are not always clearly described. In the present approach, particular attention was paid to designing the exit site independently from the laparoscopic port wounds while maintaining a minimally invasive two-port configuration.

From a practical perspective, the present technique integrates three important elements: minimal laparoscopic access using two ports, adherence to guideline-recommended catheter fixation principles such as trans-rectus insertion and fascial tunneling, and a dedicated exit-site puncture designed to minimize the catheter–skin gap. Combining these features may provide a practical and reproducible approach to PD catheter placement. In the present series, catheter placement was successfully achieved in all patients without major early catheter-related complications.

In patients with suspected intra-abdominal adhesions, laparoscopic observation following open port insertion can be particularly helpful. Direct visualization of the peritoneal cavity enables identification of adhesions and allows the catheter to be placed while avoiding adherent areas.

The present technique has several limitations. First, this procedure requires pneumoperitoneum and general anesthesia, which may limit its applicability in frail patients. In such patients, percutaneous catheter insertion techniques under local anesthesia, including the Seldinger approach, may remain preferable options. Therefore, appropriate patient selection is important when considering the present technique. Second, opening the fascia and peritoneum may theoretically increase the risk of incisional hernia or postoperative adhesions. Careful fascial closure and minimally invasive handling may help reduce these risks; however, further evaluation with long-term follow-up is necessary. Finally, this study included a limited number of patients. Additional studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are required to validate the long-term safety and durability of this technique.

The present technique represents a practical modification of two-port laparoscopic PD catheter placement that incorporates guideline-recommended catheter fixation principles, including trans-rectus insertion and a dedicated exit-site design. This approach preserves the advantages of laparoscopic visualization while maintaining minimal surgical invasiveness and may provide a simple and reproducible option for PD catheter placement.
