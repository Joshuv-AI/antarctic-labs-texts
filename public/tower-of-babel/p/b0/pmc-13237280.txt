Fingerprint profiling human gut microbiota through boronic acid-based sensor array and its application on colorectal cancer classification
Authors: Kuicheng Zhao, Xiaohua Zhu, Feng Liu, Jun Lu, Ying Long, Bo Zhou, Cuiyan Wu, Meiling Liu, Peng Yin, Haitao Li, Youyu Zhang, Bo Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interplay between gut microbiota and host plays a crucial role in human health and disease pathogenesis. However, current profiling methods for gut microbiota are costly, time-intensive and require specialized instrumentation. Herein, we develop a boronic acid-based sensor array, GUT-SCAN (Gut Unique Typing via Spectral and Chemical Analysis using Boronic Acid Nanosensor), for rapid profiling of human gut microbiota. The sensor array consists of six pH-responsive boronic acid derivatives and three pH environments (6.0, 7.4, and 8.2), forming an 18-channel sensor array. This array successfully distinguishes gut-derived bacteria with 100% accuracy. Additionally, it enables quantification of bacterial species associated with colorectal cancer (CRC) and identification of distinct bacterial compositions at varying mixing ratios. Notably, this sensor array is also capable of discriminating CRC at different stages. The simplicity, rapidity and affordability of this platform highlight its potential for microbiota analysis and its applicability in disease diagnostics, particularly for CRC.

Introduction

The gut microbiota is integral to human health, influencing immune regulation, metabolism and neurological functions1,2. As a complex microbial ecosystem, it facilitates nutrient digestion and provides a critical defense against pathogenic invasion3. However, disruption of the gut microbiota composition, termed dysbiosis, has been implicated in various diseases, including metabolic disorders4, inflammatory diseases5, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)6 and colorectal cancer (CRC)7,8. Among these, CRC is the third most prevalent malignancy worldwide and constitutes the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality9. Early detection and precise screening are essential for improving CRC prognosis and clinical outcomes.

Emerging evidence indicates that gut microbiota contributes to CRC pathogenesis by modulating inflammation, inducing DNA damage and regulating apoptosis10,11. Specifically, the alterations in microbial composition can exacerbate intestinal inflammation, promote carcinogen production and facilitate tumorigenesis. These findings highlight the potential of gut microbiota as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for CRC12,13. Therefore, comprehensive profiling and dynamic monitoring of gut microbiota are of significant interest, offering promising avenues for CRC prevention, early diagnosis and treatment through microbiota-based therapeutics and precision medicine.

Despite its clinical potential, gut microbiota detection remains challenging for two primary reasons. Firstly, the gut microbiota constitutes an intricate and highly diverse ecosystem, necessitating robust and reliable detection methods14,15. Current profiling techniques, such as metagenomic sequencing (e.g., 16S rRNA analysis), rely on next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms and require specialized bioinformatics expertise16. However, these methods are expensive, labor-intensive and time-consuming, limiting their feasibility for routine clinical application. Secondly, bacterial species exhibit substantial morphological and structural diversity, further complicating detection17,18. Molecular methods, including multiplex PCR19, CRISPR-based diagnostics (CRISPR-Dx)20 and DNA microarray21, while highly specific, are similarly constrained by high costs, prolonged processing times, and the need for trained operators. These constraints significantly underscore the need for a rapid, high-throughput and cost-efficient sensing platform for gut microbiota detection.

Fluorescence-based methods have gained significant attention due to their high sensitivity, specificity and operational simplicity. Traditional fluorescence-based probes often employ a fluorophore-ligand conjugate22,23, enabling selective bacterial recognition (Fig. 1a). For instance, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) has been used to analyze the spatial organization of gut microbiota24. However, these approaches generally rely on a “lock-and-key” strategy, which lacks versatility in complex environments with diverse bacterial populations. In contrast, chemical sensor arrays inspired by the mammalian olfactory system offer a versatile platform for bacterial detection25–28. These arrays leverage cross-reactive interactions among various probes and bacterial species to generate distinct response patterns. While widely applied for microbial discrimination, current sensor arrays primarily rely on the electrostatic interaction and/or hydrophobic/hydrophilic interaction (Fig. 1b), which can be affected by environmental conditions, potentially compromising detection accuracy, especially in complex biological matrices such as the gut microbiota. Boronic acid derivatives are an ideal candidate for this task due to their unique capacity for selective binding to cis-diol-containing molecules, which forms the fundamental principle of our sensing platform. This capability is particularly advantageous for bacterial profiling, as bacterial surfaces are rich in carbohydrate moieties including cis-diols29, and boronic acid-based sensors provide a promising approach for bacterial identification30,31. The binding is characteristically pH-dependent, which is modulated by their molecular structure and the pH of the medium32. When the surrounding pH is higher than the pKa value of boronic acids, the boronic acid readily forms five- or six-membered cyclic esters with cis-diols, resulting in the binding affinity between boronic acid derivative and cis-diol structures. Fine-tuning the electronic properties of boronic acid derivatives through structural modifications enables optimization of their pKa and binding properties33,34. Moreover, the structural variations in bacterial surface oligosaccharides and polysaccharides offer an opportunity for differential binding, facilitating species-specific detection. To exploit this, we construct a sensor array composed of structurally diverse boronic acid probes. This system effectively leverages the intrinsic variability of surface glycans in conjunction with the boronic acid probes’ key attributes—pH-dependent binding affinity and structural tailorability—to produce complex, multidimensional response patterns. Thus, the construction of a boronic acid-based sensor array holds a highly promising strategy for accurate gut microbiota profiling in a complex biological environment.

Herein, we introduce GUT-SCAN (Gut Unique Typing via Spectral and Chemical Analysis using Boronic Acid Nanosensor), a boronic acid-based sensor array for rapid and robust profiling of human gut microbiota. The GUT-SCAN system consists of six engineered boronic acid derivatives (BP1–BP6) with varying affinities for cis-diol structures, exhibiting pH-dependent fluorescence responses. By incorporating three distinct pH environments (6.0, 7.4, and 8.2), we generate an 18-channel detection platform, significantly enhancing detection resolution. Pyrene (Py), a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon with an extended conjugated system, is employed as the fluorescent signal reporter. Upon exposure to gut-derived bacteria, the fluorescence intensity increases due to the adhesion of boronic acid probes on the bacterial surfaces, generating unique fluorescence “fingerprints” for different bacterial species (Fig. 1c). This 18-channel sensor array achieves 100% classification accuracy and 98% correct identification of unknown samples. In addition, the proposed GUT-SCAN system successfully detects and differentiates human gut microbiota from human fecal samples and demonstrates robust discrimination of CRC patients at different disease stages. These findings establish GUT-SCAN as a powerful, cost-effective tool for gut microbiota analysis, with potential for advancing microbiota-based diagnostics and CRC detection.

Results

Design and synthesis of the probe library

Boronic acids can form reversible covalent bonds with cis-diols and have been extensively utilized for detecting cis-diol-containing molecules such as saccharides29, nucleosides35, glycoproteins36, and so forth37,38. The binding affinity between boronic acids and cis-diols is strongly influenced by the pKa of the boronic acid (Supplementary Fig. 1). The pKa of boronic acid can be precisely tuned by modifying the microenvironment of the boron atom39. In this work, the core design principle is to precisely tune the electron density of the boron center via phenyl substituents to optimize probe performance. For example, phenylboronic acids with electron-withdrawing groups possess increased acidity and a lowered pKa value40. On the contrary, the presence of the electron-donating group may increase the pKa value41. Hence, we aimed to regulate the microenvironment of boron atoms in boronic acids, adjusting their pKa and thus influencing their binding affinity with cis-diol structures. The boronic acid derivatives are expected to interact with the cis-diols structures present in bacterial cell walls. Each probe is endowed with a different binding affinity and is expected to produce a distinct binding affinity to cis-diols on the surface of gut-derived bacteria. Due to the signatures of the surface characteristics, size and morphology of the gut-derived bacteria, leading to differential fluorescence signals from the sensor array. Employing machine-learning algorithms to analyze fingerprints, effective classification of gut microbiota samples can be achieved.

The sensor array of the present study relies on the use of six different probes, BP1–BP6, each probe incorporates a Py and a modified boronic acid unit (Fig. 2a). Among them, the boronic acid unit exhibits varying binding affinities for cis-diols groups, which are expected to produce distinct binding affinities to cis-diols on the surface of gut-derived bacteria42. The Py, a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon with a large conjugated structure, exhibits excellent optical properties and exerts an electron-withdrawing effect43. The six probes were synthesized via straightforward synthetic routes with satisfactory yields (Supplementary Fig. 2). The chemical structures of the probes were fully characterized by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Supplementary Figs. 3–15).

To further understand the electronic effects of these modifications, electrostatic energy calculations were performed on the molecular structures. The calculations demonstrated that the electron cloud density distribution was significantly altered by the functional group modifications (Fig. 2b), further corroborating the ability to fine-tune pKa through structural modifications. Additionally, the hydrophobicity of the probes was also influenced by these modifications, which could further impact their solubility and interaction with biological environments. To experimentally validate these effects, we detected the pKa values and ClogP of BP1–BP6 shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 16. The results indicated that the boronic acid derivatives with the electron-withdrawing groups possess increased acidity and lowered pKa value, while the introduction of the electron-donating group slightly increases the pKa value. Moreover, structural modifications also influenced the overall hydrophobicity of the probes, which could affect the binding affinity toward the target biomolecules. Additionally, the binding affinities between boronic acid derivatives and cis-diol-containing molecules also show pH dependence (Supplementary Fig. 1). To validate the pH responsiveness of binding between BP1 and BP6 probes and cis-diol-containing saccharides, we test the binding constants between a series of saccharides and BP1–BP6 at different surrounding pH values (see details in the Supporting Information). Herein, we selected five representative monosaccharides (Fig. 2d) as target analytes because of their biological relevance, especially their presence on the surface of bacterial cell surfaces44,45. As expected, the binding affinities (i.e., Keq) between the BP1–BP6 and different monosaccharides were found to range from 12.1 to 3985.5 M−1 (Supplementary Figs. 17–34 and Supplementary Table 1). The value of Keq is affected by the surrounding pH. At lower pH levels, boronic acids are less ionized, and binding affinities tend to decrease. In contrast, at higher pH levels, boronic acids are more fully ionized and capable of forming boronate esters, and the binding constants increase (Fig. 2e). The binding affinities of boronic acid derivatives can be effectively modulated by adjusting the pKa through structural modifications. Additionally, pH serves as an important environmental variable that further fine-tunes these interactions. Together, these strategies offer valuable insights for the design of a boronic acid-based sensor array for pattern recognition of gut-derived bacterial strains.

Fluorescence response of gut-derived bacterium

With the BP1–BP6 probes in hand, the photophysical properties of the probes were investigated. UV-vis absorption peaks centered at 320–350 nm were observed for all BP probes (Supplementary Fig. 35), indicating characteristics of Py compounds46. A peak at ~400 nm was observed in the fluorescence spectra of all BP probes (Supplementary Fig. 36), serving as the signal reporting units. The bacterial cell walls consist of amounts of peptidoglycans and glycopolymers, containing a lot of cis-diols structures47,48. We hypothesize that the interaction between our BP probes and gut-derived bacterium can be driven through binding to the cis-diols structure of the bacterial cell wall. Thus, we tested the effect of bacteria on the fluorescence emission intensity of BP1–BP6. A typical gut-derived probiotic Lactobacillus plantarum (L. pl)49 was used as target analyte (Fig. 3a). Increasing concentrations of L. pl (OD600 = 0.4–1.0) to the solutions of the probes, a gradual increase in the fluorescence intensity was observed in all cases (Supplementary Figs. 37–39). To facilitate the study of pH-binding relationships, we compared the fluorescence response of L. pl at various concentrations and different surrounding pH values (pH 6.0, 7.4, and 8.2). As shown in Fig. 3b, a gradual increase in fluorescence response of L. pl compared to additional concentration at different pH was observed. The fluorescence response of L. pl in different BP probes shows different response intensity at various pH values. Specifically, the probes BP1–BP3 show a greater response at pH 6.0, while the probe BP4–BP6 shows a greater response at pH 7.4, which can be explained by the modification of the boron atom, directly binding to the cis-diol. These findings provide compelling evidence that the modification of the boron atom in boronic acid can mediate its interactions with cis-diol and that pH serves as an important environmental variable that further fine-tunes these interactions. Then, we investigated the effect of pH to bacterial physiology, subsequent growth in standard medium was identical across groups (pH 6.0, 7.4, and 8.2), confirming that short-term pH exposure does not affect bacterial viability or metabolic state under our experimental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 40).

To validate the binding of BP probes on the surface of bacteria, the diverse surface properties among L. pl were investigated through testing the surface hydrophilicity and fluorescence imaging. Accordingly, bacterial adherence to hydrocarbons (BATH) were used to assess the changes of bacterial surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 3c). The results indicated that a significant increase in BATH levels of L. pl were observed following incubation with the BP probes, suggesting the successful adhesion of probes to the bacterial surface50. The enhanced hydrophobicity should be due to the interaction between the boronic acid and cis-diol-containing compounds on the surface of the bacterial cell, facilitating stronger probe adhesion. Consequently, heat-inactivated or fixed bacteria is used to effectively distinguish the specific surface binding of the BP probes from active biological responses of bacteria. Our results reveal that the BP probes primarily bind to surface cis-diol structures independently of bacterial viability or metabolic activity. (Supplementary Fig. 41). To further validate the adhesion mechanism between bacteria and BP probes, L. pl was treated with tunicamycin (which degrades the glycosyl)51 and lysozyme (which degrades the cell wall)52 and the fluorescence intensity showed varying degrees of decrease following these treatments (Supplementary Fig. 42). In addition, after treatment with concanavalin A (binds to glycoproteins in the cell wall)53 and urea (disrupts hydrophobic interactions)54, the fluorescence intensity of L. pl was more negative than that without (Supplementary Figs. 43 and 44), suggesting the fluorescence features of the boronic acid-based probes are mainly dependent on the interaction with the cis-diol-containing bacteria. Furthermore, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to further confirm the binding of the probes to the bacterial surface. As shown in Fig. 3d, the BP probes not only can efficiently stain the L. pl but also show diverse response signals. Diverse fluorescence responses were presented when one strain was incubated with different probes (Fig. 3e), which is a prerequisite for the construction of a sensor array for gut microbiota discrimination.

Application of sensor arrays for gut-derived bacterial strains

Due to the differences in surface glycan composition of various bacterial species, we hypothesize that the interaction between the BP probes and bacteria varies. We developed a BP probe-based array that distinguishes gut-derived bacteria. The array was deliberately designed with 18 channels constituting an orthogonal design combination of six boronic acid probes (BP1–BP6) and three pH conditions (6.0, 7.4, and 8.2) (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 2). According to the features of gut microbiota, six representational bacteria, including four probiotics (B. longum, B. breve, L. a, and L. pl), two commensal bacteria (E. coli and E. cloacae), and four pathogenic bacteria (F. nucleatum, B. fragilis, S. aureus, and C. k), were selected as the target analyte (Supplementary Table 3). Among them, F. nucleatum, B. fragilis and E. coli linked to tumorigenesis through inflammation, DNA damage and immune modulation. The BP probes were orthogonally incubated with the gut-derived bacteria in a 96-well microplate, and the resulting fluorescence response of each well was recorded. As shown in Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 45, after incubation with the BP probes, the gut-derived bacteria showed an enhanced trend in fluorescence intensity, and fluorescence response patterns were produced. Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) of the fluorescence responses indicated that each gut-derived bacterium exhibited well-separated clusters, suggesting the differences among the patterns of the ten bacterial strains evaluated are statistically significant (Fig. 4b). The fluorescence responses were analyzed through a supervised pattern recognition algorithm, linear discriminant analysis (LDA). As shown in Fig. 4c, all ten bacteria are well spatially separated in the 2D space. Notably, all Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria formed distinct clusters on both sides of the LDA plot, with no regions of overlap. Intriguingly, all probiotics can cluster well together, which can distinguish the other pathogenic bacteria from opposite ends. In addition, our BP probe-based array can also discriminate the gut-derived bacteria at different concentrations (OD600 = 0.4 and OD600 = 0.6) (Supplementary Fig. 46). Further, we compared the prediction performance of machine learning (ML) algorithms for classifying the fluorescence response. The ML algorithms include GPC (Gaussian process classifier), KNN (K-nearest neighbor), LDA (linear discriminant analysis), SVM (support vector machine), DT (decision tree), and LR (logistic regression). LDA, GPC, and LR exhibited the highest model accuracy (100%) (Fig. 4d). Meanwhile, Jackknife analysis (Fig. 4e) on the training data set (6 probes × 3 pH values × 10 bacterial strains × 5 replicates) revealed 100% between-group cross-validation accuracy, indicating the LDA method as a robust statistical tool for this system. Furthermore, to evaluate the BP sensor array’s reproducibility, 42 unknown samples underwent further validation. The reproducibility assessment indicated that the accuracy of only one pH value was 83% (pH 6.0), 76% (pH 7.4), and 81% (pH 8.2), respectively (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 47, and Supplementary Data 1). However, combining the three pH values, 41 blind samples were predicted with 98% correct unknown identification by the 18-channel sensor array (Supplementary Fig. 47 and Supplementary Data 1). The result showed that our BP probe-based array accurately and reliably distinguished gut-derived bacteria.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the imbalance between probiotics and pathogenic bacteria directly disrupts the microbial environment and mediates the CRC process55,56. Compared to healthy controls, CRC patients exhibit significantly increased E. coli abundance and reduced probiotic populations57. Among probiotics, the most frequently used probiotic species is L. pl58. Thus, the L. pl and E. coli were chosen to validate the quantitative analysis capabilities of the BP sensor array. As illustrated in Fig. 4g and Supplementary Figs. 48 and 49, the 2D LDA scatterplots depicted a significant variation between bacteria at different concentrations (OD600 ranging from 0.4 to 1.0). Moreover, the Factor 1 values exhibited a linear relationship with the concentrations of L. pl and E. coli (Supplementary Figs. 48 and 49). The results indicate that the proposed sensor array can effectively quantify individual gut-derived bacteria.

Moreover, to mimic the gut microbiota composition of CRC patients, accurately identifying the mixed ratios of probiotics to pathogenic bacteria is crucial. Herein, L. pl and E. coli. were selected as the models of gut microbiota to verify the accuracy of the sensor array. The model samples were prepared by mixing L. pl and E. coli in different ratios (9:1, 8:2, 7:3, 6:4, 5:5, 4:6, 3:7, 2:8, 1:9), designed to span microbial profiles from CRC-affected to healthy populations59. The fluorescence response of these nine binary mixtures (the total concentration of bacteria is OD600 = 0.5) was recorded (Supplementary Fig. 50). LDA analysis revealed a clear clustering of all nine combinations in the score plot (Fig. 4h). In addition, using E. coli, S. aureus and L. pl blends in varying ratios as a ternary mixture, the BP sensor array successfully resolved nine distinct clusters in 2D LDA space (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 51). These results demonstrate our BP probe array’s ability to differentiate bacterial consortia with subtle compositional differences, highlighting its potential for analyzing complex gut microbiota in clinical samples.

To further verify the practicability of the proposed sensor array, an analysis of gut-derived bacteria in real samples (human urine, fetal bovine serum samples, intestinal extract, and simulated intestinal fluid) was performed. As a result, urine and serum produce unique response patterns, and the same type of bacteria aggregate into seven non-overlapping groups, meanwhile, intestinal extract and simulated intestinal fluid produce unique response patterns, and the same type of bacteria aggregate into eleven non-overlapping groups (Supplementary Fig. 52). The result indicated that the BP sensor array has excellent selectivity for gut-derived bacteria, demonstrating the sensor array’s discrimination capability in real samples and its potential for practical clinical applications.

Application for the CRC diagnosis

Recent studies show that CRC patients have a unique microbial composition compared to healthy individuals, with significant variations in microbiota diversity at different stages of disease progression60,61. Assessing gut microbiota differences presents a promising strategy for CRC diagnosis and staging62. Therefore, to explore the proposed sensor array’s diagnostic potential, fecal microbiota from healthy volunteers and CRC patients at different pathological stages were analyzed (Fig. 5a). The identification focused on the whole gut microbiome communities rather than individual bacterial species. The feces samples of CRC patients were stratified into two groups according to the tumor node metastasis (TNM) staging system: early-stage CRC (I, II) and advanced-stage CRC (III, IV)63. Histopathological analysis, the gold standard diagnostic method for CRC staging, confirmed the clinical classification of samples (Fig. 5b). Microbiota suspensions were prepared according to the detailed protocols described in the Supporting Information. Subsequently, the sensor array assessed gut microbiome samples at the same concentration (OD600 = 0.5). The fluorescence response of 15 samples (volunteer information in Supplementary Table 4) could effectively generate a fluorescent fingerprint (Supplementary Fig. 53). After being analyzed through LDA algorithms, healthy volunteer, early and advanced CRC patients were separated in a 2D score plot (Fig. 5c). Moreover, the score of Factor 1 in the LDA plot significantly differs among healthy volunteers, early and advanced CRC patients (Fig. 5d). The results suggest that our BP probe-based array may be effective for clinical CRC diagnosis.

To further verify the BP probes-based array method, the gut microbiome composition of healthy volunteers and CRC patients at different stages was investigated by 16S rRNA sequencing technology. As shown in Fig. 5e, both the bacterial richness and diversity of microbiota in the feces of CRC patients were drastically reduced. Moreover, there were noticeable changes in the relative abundance of microbiota between the early and advanced-stage CRC fecal samples. The results showed variations in microbiota diversity across different stages of CRC development. At the phylum level, the CRC group significantly decreased the relative abundance of Firmicutes while increasing the relative abundance of Proteobacteria (Fig. 5f), indicating dysbiosis in CRC patients. Additionally, the distinct clustering observed in the partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) plots (Fig. 5g) indicated significant alterations in the microbiota composition between the early and advanced-stage CRC fecal samples, allowing us to identify distinct microbial patterns associated with different stages of CRC. Orthogonal validation via lectin microarray analysis64 with FITC-ConA confirmed significant differences in the surface glycome among the samples (Supplementary Fig. 54). All the results support the hypothesis that specific microbiota variations correspond to CRC progression, reinforcing the potential of microbial composition as a biomarker for CRC diagnosis and staging and highlighting both the consistency and reliability of microbiota-based differentiation in CRC classification using our sensor array.

Our research aimed to create a portable, low-cost and noninvasive method for identifying healthy individuals and CRC patients at various stages. To assess the clinical reliability and diagnostic accuracy of the BP probe-based array, 147 blind samples (40 advanced-stage CRC (Adv.), 50 early-stage CRC (Ear.), 30 healthy controls (Hea.), 12 inflammatory and 15 adenoma controls; details in Supplementary Table 5) were analyzed. As a result, the BP sensor array generated unique fluorescent fingerprints for each sample (Supplementary Fig. 55 and Supplementary Data 2). LDA analysis reveals that individual samples are identifiable in the 2D score plot with minimal overlap, while an initial separation is observed between the CRC samples and the inflammatory/adenoma controls (Fig. 5h). Although some minor overlaps existed between CRC stages, their distributions showed statistically significant separation as anticipated. Factor 1, explaining over 60% of the variance in the LDA model, enabled effective differentiation between early and advanced CRC samples (Fig. 5i). To further validate diagnostic performance, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis65 was conducted on three constructed models: (Model 1: Hea. VS. Adv., Model 2: Hea. VS. Ear., and Model 3: Ear. VS. Adv.). As illustrated in Fig. 5j, these models demonstrated exceptional discriminatory power with area under the curve (AUC) values of 1.00, 0.99, and 0.92, respectively—well above the clinical significance threshold of 0.8066. These results establish the BP sensor array as a promising tool for noninvasive early CRC detection and staging.

Discussion

In this study, we developed GUT-SCAN, a boronic acid-based sensor array that enables rapid, cost-effective gut microbiota profiling and colorectal cancer (CRC) classification. By leveraging pH-responsive boronic acid–cis-diol interactions, we successfully generated fluorescence “fingerprints” that differentiate probiotics, commensals, and pathogenic bacteria with 100% accuracy and classify unknown bacterial mixtures with 98% accuracy. More significantly, GUT-SCAN identifies CRC-associated microbial signatures and distinguishes different CRC stages, demonstrating its potential as a noninvasive diagnostic tool. These results highlight the advantages of chemically encoded fluorescence-based detection as an alternative to current sequencing-dependent microbiota profiling techniques.

Fluorescence-based detection methods have been widely used for bacterial classification due to their high sensitivity and ease of implementation. Among them, fluorescent molecular probes, such as boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY)67, cyanine dyes68, and quantum dots (QDs)69, have been employed for bacterial labeling and imaging69,70. While these approaches offer bright fluorescence signals and tunable spectral properties, their detection mechanisms often rely on electrostatic interactions, membrane permeability or metabolic uptake, which can be influenced by bacterial physiology, growth conditions and sample heterogeneity. For example, BODIPY-based probes selectively stain Gram-negative bacteria due to differences in cell membrane structure, but they struggle to distinguish bacterial species within the same Gram classification67. Similarly, QDs offer strong and tunable fluorescence but require functionalization with bacterial-specific ligands, increasing complexity and cost71. Compared to these conventional fluorescent probes, GUT-SCAN’s boronic acid-based detection strategy provides a more versatile and chemically selective approach. Instead of relying solely on charge-based or hydrophobic interactions, boronic acid derivatives bind to cis-diol-containing carbohydrates on bacterial surfaces, enabling species-specific detection without the need for genetic modification or metabolic uptake. Additionally, our pH-tunable sensor array introduces an additional layer of selectivity, which significantly enhances bacterial classification accuracy. While most fluorescence-based bacterial detection methods provide binary “on-off” signals, GUT-SCAN generates distinct, multidimensional fluorescence patterns across different pH environments, allowing for the simultaneous detection of multiple bacterial species in complex microbiota samples.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS), including 16S rRNA sequencing and shotgun metagenomics, remains the gold standard for microbiota profiling. These methods provide high taxonomic resolution and allow for the identification of both culturable and unculturable bacteria. However, they suffer from high costs, long processing times and the need for bioinformatics expertise. Moreover, sequencing approaches typically analyze static microbial composition, failing to capture the functional and biochemical state of bacteria in real-time. In contrast, GUT-SCAN directly assesses bacterial surface chemistry, providing an instantaneous phenotypic readout that reflects microbial metabolic states and structural differences, which are often critical in disease pathogenesis. Metagenomic sequencing also requires careful sample handling, DNA extraction, and library preparation, all of which introduce biases and may lead to loss of rare bacterial populations. On the other hand, GUT-SCAN is a label-free, amplification-free platform that preserves the native state of microbiota while allowing for rapid, high-throughput analysis. This makes it particularly attractive for clinical applications, where speed and ease of use are paramount. Unlike sequencing-based methods, which often require batch processing of samples, GUT-SCAN provides near-instant fluorescence responses, making it suitable for real-time diagnostics and point-of-care applications.

One of the most significant findings of this study is the ability of GUT-SCAN to distinguish CRC-associated microbial signatures and classify different CRC stages. Gut microbiota composition is increasingly recognized as a biomarker for CRC risk, with specific bacterial taxa such as F. nucleatum, B. fragilis and E. coli linked to tumorigenesis through inflammation, DNA damage and immune modulation. While previous studies have employed 16S rRNA sequencing and qPCR to assess CRC-associated bacterial populations, these methods require lengthy workflows and specialized equipment, limiting their potential for large-scale screening.

Compared to conventional CRC screening techniques, such as colonoscopy and fecal occult blood tests (FOBTs), GUT-SCAN offers a rapid and noninvasive alternative. Colonoscopy, while highly effective, is invasive, expensive and often associated with low compliance rates. FOBTs, on the other hand, suffer from low sensitivity, particularly for early-stage CRC. By detecting CRC-associated microbial shifts in stool samples, GUT-SCAN has the potential to serve as an early-warning system for CRC, enabling population-wide microbiota-based screening with minimal patient burden.

Despite its strong performance, GUT-SCAN has several limitations that warrant further investigation. One challenge is taxonomic resolution—while the sensor array effectively classifies bacteria at the genus level, its ability to differentiate closely related strains remains limited. Expanding the boronic acid probe library and incorporating additional pH conditions could improve strain-level discrimination. Another factor to consider is sample variability, as gut microbiota composition is influenced by diet, medication and host genetics. Future studies should include larger, more diverse clinical cohorts to validate GUT-SCAN’s robustness across populations. The other is that detailed individual dietary information was not collected. However, all volunteers were recruited from a single geographic region (Hunan Province, China), which may minimize significant variability arising from regional dietary differences. Nevertheless, individual dietary habits remain a potential confounding factor. Future prospective studies should systematically collect such data for a more comprehensive analysis.

Quantitative accuracy is another area for improvement. While GUT-SCAN can semi-quantitatively assess bacterial abundance, refining the signal-to-bacteria correlation will enhance absolute microbial load estimation. The integration of ML algorithms for fluorescence pattern analysis could further improve classification accuracy and enable predictive modeling of microbiota-related diseases. These findings offer avenues for leveraging gut microbiota knowledge in clinical applications, including microbiota analysis as biomarkers for screening, prognosis, or prediction, and microbiota modulation to prevent cancer, enhance therapies, and mitigate treatment side effects. Additionally, miniaturizing the sensor array for portable or point-of-care applications will be a key step toward clinical translation. Beyond CRC detection, GUT-SCAN has broad potential applications in microbiome research, infectious disease diagnostics, and personalized medicine. The ability to rapidly characterize microbiota could aid in diagnosing IBD, metabolic disorders and neurodegenerative diseases, where gut dysbiosis is increasingly implicated. Furthermore, real-time microbiota monitoring could be used to assess treatment responses in probiotic therapies, antibiotics, and dietary interventions.

In addition to its diagnostic potential, the boronic acid-based sensing mechanism could be adapted for engineered bacterial biosensors, environmental microbiota analysis and synthetic biology applications. By designing boronic acid derivatives with tailored pKa values, it may be possible to develop highly specific bacterial sensors for targeted therapeutics and precision medicine approaches.

This study establishes GUT-SCAN as a rapid, cost-effective and clinically relevant tool for microbiota-based diagnostics and CRC screening. By integrating pH-responsive boronic acid–cis-diol interactions with fluorescence sensing, GUT-SCAN outperforms traditional fluorescence-based bacterial probes and sequencing-based methods in speed, accessibility and real-time classification. The successful detection of CRC-associated microbial shifts further underscores its potential as a noninvasive cancer screening tool. While challenges remain, ongoing advancements in sensor design, AI-driven analysis, and clinical validation will pave the way for its translation into routine diagnostic applications.

Determination of apparent ARS-boronic acid association constants (KARS)

To spectroscopically determine the association constant of ARS with a boronic acid72,73, each boronic acid was titrated into solutions of constant ARS concentration. A stock solution of ARS was prepared in DI water (9.0 × 10−5 M), along with stock sodium phosphate buffers (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 6.0, 7.4, or 8.2). Stock solutions were prepared in DMSO for each boronic acid (2.0 × 10−2 M). From these solutions, two separate solutions were prepared in DI water, one containing a boronic acid (2.0 × 10−3 M), ARS (9.0 × 10−6 M), and PBS (0.1 M), with the second solution containing the same. These solutions were used to perform titrations of constant ARS concentration and decreasing boronic acid concentration. A non-fluorescent ARS standard was prepared as a solution of ARS and PBS in the absence of boronic acid and was used as a fluorescence blank. Fluorescence measurements were acquired with the F-7100 spectrometer, with the samples being excited at 468 nm and emission being measured at 570 nm. A full presentation of component concentrations for every measurement can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Determination of apparent diol-boronic acid association constants (Keq)

To determine the diol-boronic acid association constant of each diol and boronic acid72, three solutions were prepared from the previously mentioned stock solutions. The first solution contained the boronic acid (2.0 × 10−3 M), ARS (9.0 × 10−6 M), and PBS (0.1 M), while the second solution also contained the diol in question. The concentration of the diol varied with each boronic acid-diol combination and at each pH. A third ARS standard solution was prepared containing only ARS (9.0 × 10−6 M) and PBS (0.1 M). The first solution was then titrated into the second to prepare solutions of constant ARS and boronic acid concentrations but varying diol concentration. The ARS-only solution was used as a fluorescence blank. Fluorescence measurements were acquired with the F-7100 spectrometer, with the samples being excited at 468 nm and emission being measured at 570 nm.

Machine-learning algorithms

Linear discriminant analysis

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was performed using the linear discriminant analysis class from the scikit-learn library (version 1.0.2) in Python 3.8 (Anaconda distribution). All variables were included in the model (full model) with a tolerance of 0.001. The fluorescence response patterns were transformed into canonical variables. Mahalanobis distances of each sample to the centroid of each group in the LDA-transformed space were calculated, and classification was based on the shortest distance.

Decision tree classifier

Decision tree, a supervised learning algorithm, employs a tree structure to facilitate decision-making. Within this structure, every node corresponded to a feature, while the edges symbolized decision rules. The terminal nodes, or leaves, represented the output results. In our work, the decision tree utilized “entropy” as a criterion, enabling each tree to make decisions regarding feature values. This criterion guided the decision-making process, ultimately assigning decision labels as the results. Furthermore, it allocated scores based on the accuracy of classification.

Support vector machine

Support vector machine (SVM) serves as a supervised learning algorithm used for classification and regression. It functioned by finding the hyperplane that maximized the margin between the two classes. Upon data ingestion, the SVM is trained using a polynomial kernel with the support vector classifier (SVC), enabling it to identify the appropriate hyperplane for each classification.

Logistic regression

Logistic regression, a statistical method used for binary classification problems, estimates the probability of an event occurring. In this context, the logistic regression model was trained using default parameters.

K-neighbors classifier

The k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm is a simple instance-based learning method. It predicted the category of a new data point by selecting the k closest training samples to that point.

Gaussian process classifier

The Gaussian process classifier uses Gaussian processes for probabilistic classification. A Gaussian process is an infinite-dimensional stochastic process, used for non-parametric Bayesian inference of functions. In classification tasks, the Gaussian process is utilized to predict the probability of a data point belonging to a specific category. It offered a flexible, kernel-driven approach, allowing the model to automatically adapt to the complexity of the data. The code for the analysis is available as Supplementary Note 1.

16S rRNA gene sequencing of fecal samples

Fecal sample collection and processing

Fresh fecal samples (0.25–0.5 g) were placed into 2 mL sterile centrifuge tubes; 200 μL was used for liquid samples. Then 500 μL buffer SA, 100 μL buffer SC, and 0.25 g grinding beads were added. For RNA removal, 10 μL RNase A (TIANGEN, RT405-02) was added (Supplementary Data 3). The mixture was homogenized, heated at 70 °C for 15 min, centrifuged at 13,400 × g for 1 min, and ~500 μL supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Unprocessed samples were stored at −80 °C to avoid repeated freeze-thaw.

Total fecal DNA extraction

Total DNA was extracted using TGuide S96 Magnetic Universal DNA Kit (Tiangen, DP812) with minor modifications: 200 μL buffer SH was added to the supernatant, vortexed for 5 s, and incubated at 4 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation at 13,400×g for 3 min, the supernatant was mixed with 500 μL buffer GFA (confirmed isopropanol added). Then 10 μL magnetic bead suspension G was added, shaken for 5 min, and the magnetic beads were adsorbed on a magnetic rack to remove liquid. After washing with 700 μL proteinase K buffer RD (ethanol checked) and twice with 700 μL wash buffer PWD (ethanol checked), beads were air-dried for 5–10 min. Finally, 50–100 μL elution buffer TB was added, incubated at 56 °C for 5 min (shaken three times), and DNA was collected. DNA quality (A260/A280 = 1.8–2.0) and integrity were detected by ELISA reader (GeneCompany, Synergy HTX) and 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively, and stored at −20 °C.

PCR amplification of 16S rRNA gene

The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was PCR-amplified using purified DNA as template with primers 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The 10 μL system contained 2.5–4 ng genomic DNA, 0.3 μL each of 10 μM VnF/VnR, 5 μL KOD FX Neo Buffer, 2 μL dNTP (2 mM each), 0.2 μL KOD FX Neo (TOYOBO), and ddH₂O. Each sample had three replicates and a blank control. PCR conditions: 95 °C pre-denaturation for 5 min; 25 cycles of 95 °C (30 s), 50 °C (30 s), 72 °C (40 s); 72 °C final extension for 7 min; 4 °C hold. PCR products were verified by 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis (target band ~468 bp).

PCR product purification

PCR products from the same sample were pooled and purified with VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme) at a 10:8 (product:beads) ratio. After 5 min incubation at room temperature, beads were adsorbed, supernatant removed, washed twice with 200 μL 80% ethanol, air-dried for 3 min, and eluted with 8–10 μL ddH₂O. Purified products were quantified by ImageJ and stored at −20 °C.

Library construction and sequencing

Libraries were constructed from purified PCR products via end repair, 3’-A tailing, and adapter ligation using KOD FX Neo (TOYOBO), Phusion HF MM (Biolink), and VnF/VnR primers (SYNBIO). Target fragments (500–600 bp) were selected by 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified with Monarch DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Hongyue). After Solexa PCR enrichment, library quality was detected, and sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE 250).

Sequencing data processing and analysis

Raw sequencing data were filtered to remove low-quality reads (Q < 20 > 50%), adapters, and N-rich reads to get clean reads. Paired-end reads were spliced by FLASH (unsuccessful ones discarded). Sequences with ≥97% similarity were clustered into OTUs (singletons removed). Species annotation was done via the Silva database alignment. α-diversity (Shannon, Chao1) and β-diversity (PCoA, NMDS) were calculated to analyze microbial community composition.

Statistics and reproducibility

Sample size was determined by power analysis (G*Power, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, effect size d = 0.6), requiring ≥30 samples per group. No data were excluded. All key experiments were independently replicated at least three times with consistent results. Random assignment and blinding were applied during data collection and analysis. Data analysis and illustrations were performed using Origin 2021. The fluorescence intensities were quantified using ImageJ software for the region of interest analysis. Statistical comparisons between two groups were determined by a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. P < 0.05 was considered significant, and the P values are provided as follows: ****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, ns not significant.
