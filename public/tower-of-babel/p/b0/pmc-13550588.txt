Prognostic significance of the endothelial activation and stress index in acute respiratory distress syndrome: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mingkun Yang, Zhiying Zhou, Yanwen Lu, Yijia Lin, Shenghui Miao, Zhouxin Yang, Jing Yan, Weihang Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endothelial cells play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). The Endothelial Activation and Stress Index (EASIX) is regarded as a reliable biomarker of endothelial dysfunction. The aim of this study was to evaluate the prognostic value of baseline and dynamic EASIX trajectories in ARDS patients. The data of this study were sourced from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) 2.2 database. The study population was divided into three groups according to the tertiles of the EASIX index. The primary outcome was 28-day ICU mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, multivariate Cox regression, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) were used to assess the correlation between the baseline EASIX and mortality. Furthermore, Latent Class Mixed Models (LCMM) were employed to identify dynamic EASIX trajectories within the first 72 h. The association between these identified trajectories and clinical prognosis was subsequently evaluated. Finally, the Boruta algorithm was applied to screen for key predictive features, and seven machine learning (ML) algorithms were developed to predict 28-day mortality. According to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1044 ARDS patients were ultimately included in this study. Kaplan-Meier curves showed that patients in the high baseline EASIX group had higher 28-day mortality. Multivariate Cox regression revealed that higher EASIX was associated with increased 28-day mortality (HR = 1.07; 95% CI 1.01–1.14, P = 0.03) and remained significant at 60 and 180 days. The RCS curves indicated a linear relationship (P-non-linear > 0.05). In the dynamic analysis, LCMM identified three distinct trajectories: Trajectory 1 (Persistently Low), Trajectory 2 (Persistently Increasing), and Trajectory 3 (Rise-and-Fall). Notably, Trajectory 2 exhibited the poorest prognosis (HR = 7.56; 95% CI 3.45–16.57,P < 0.001). Feature selection via Boruta algorithm consistently identified EASIX as a key predictor. Among the seven ML models evaluated, the Random Forest algorithm demonstrated superior performance, achieving an AUC of 0.826. Both baseline EASIX and dynamic EASIX trajectories are independent predictors of mortality in ARDS patients, suggesting that EASIX has great potential as a reliable prognostic indicator for ARDS patients. Introduction

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) is an acute respiratory failure caused by diffuse pulmonary inflammation and edema1, with a mortality rate consistently around 30–40%2. The onset of ARDS is typically triggered by either intra-pulmonary or extra-pulmonary factors, leading to damage of the alveolar epithelial-mesenchymal-vascular endothelial cell barrier3. Pulmonary endothelial cells (ECs) play a crucial role in this pathological process. Endothelial dysfunction is characterized by a pro-inflammatory state, impaired vasodilation, and an increased tendency for endothelial thrombosis4,5. When activated, ECs release inflammatory mediators and recruit leukocytes, enhancing their adhesion to the vascular endothelium and promoting their infiltration into the pulmonary parenchyma6. This exacerbates immune dysregulation, particularly when neutrophils and monocytes enter the lungs. The consequent excessive immune response leads to cytokine activation, which further compromises both vascular and pulmonary tissues7,8. Eventually, this leads to severe complications, including capillary thrombosis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pulmonary edema, and hemorrhage9.

In recent years, the Endothelial Activation and Stress Index (EASIX) has come to the fore as a novel prognostic indicator for gauging the extent of endothelial dysfunction. The EASIX score is computed based on the following formula: serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level (U/L) multiplied by the creatinine level (mg/dL), and then divided by the platelet count (109/L)10. Research findings have indicated that EASIX not only mirrors the degree of endothelial damage subsequent to allogeneic stem cell transplantation11–13 but also exhibits correlations with several endothelial activation markers, such as interleukin-18, chemokine-X-C-ligand 8, insulin-like growth factor-1, and suppressor of tumorigenicity12,14. Furthermore, EASIX has manifested a robust predictive capacity for mortality risk in diseases linked to endothelial dysfunction, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma15, graft-versus-host disease11, severe COVID-1916 and sepsis17. In view of the strong correlation between the initiation and advancement of ARDS and endothelial cell damage, recent studies have begun to explore the prognostic value of baseline EASIX in ARDS18. However, current evidence remains largely confined to static, single-time-point assessments. Because endothelial activation in ARDS is a highly dynamic and rapidly evolving process, isolated baseline measurements fail to capture the full prognostic picture.

Building upon the exploration of baseline EASIX levels, this study further investigated the dynamic trajectories of EASIX in patients with ARDS and evaluated their prognostic value. Furthermore, we sought to integrate EASIX with machine learning algorithms to construct a robust 28-day mortality prediction model, providing a valuable reference for individualized risk stratification in critically ill ARDS patients.

Materials and methods

Data source

Patient data were obtained from version 2.2 of the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV v2.2), an open-access clinical database encompassing patients admitted to the intensive care units (ICUs) or the emergency department between 2008 and 2019. MIMIC-IV is a comprehensive and freely available resource comprising a wide range of information, including demographic characteristics, vital sign monitoring, laboratory and microbiological test results, imaging studies, intake and output records, medication usage, length of stay, survival data, and documentation of discharge or mortality19. To access the relevant data, our team successfully completed the Protection of Human Research Participants Test and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) training course, along with their respective examinations(Certificate Code: 11528383). Since this study does not involve interventions affecting clinical care and all protected health information has been deidentified, ethical approval and informed consent were not required by the institutional review board (IRB) at MIT.

Study population

Our study screened all patients in the database to identify those diagnosed with ARDS according to the Berlin definition20. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) ICU stay of less than 24 h; (b) age under 18 years; (c) missing data on serum creatinine, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), or platelets within the first 24 h of ICU admission. For patients with multiple ICU admissions during the same hospitalization or multiple hospitalizations, only data from the first ICU admission of the first hospitalization were included. From this primary cohort, a sub-cohort was established for dynamic trajectory analysis. Patients were included in the dynamic analysis cohort if they had at least three EASIX records within any 12-hour interval within the first 72 h of ICU admission. Patients lacking sufficient serial measurements to support trajectory modeling were excluded from this sub-cohort(as shown in Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Patient data during ICU stays were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database using Structured Query Language (SQL) with PostgreSQL (version 9.6). The variables that were extracted encompassed demographic features such as age, sex, weight, and ethnicity; vital signs namely body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, mean arterial pressure (MBP), and saturation of peripheral oxygen (SpO2); as well as laboratory findings including white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin, platelet, sodium, potassium, blood glucose, creatinine, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2) and serum lactate. Comorbidities considered included myocardial injury, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), liver disease, kidney disease, cancer, and diabetes. Disease severity was assessed using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPSII), Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS). Vital signs and laboratory test results were defined as the mean values of all available measurements recorded for each patient during the first 24 h following admission to the ICU. Treatments administered within the first 24 h included renal replacement therapy (RRT) and vasoactive drug support. Survival outcomes measured were 28-day, 60-day, and 180-day mortality, as well as the length of ICU and hospital stays, with 28-day mortality designated as the primary outcome. The EASIX score was calculated using the formula: LDH [U/L] × creatinine [mg/dL]/platelet count [109/L]. Given the skewed distribution of EASIX, a log transformation was applied prior to analysis, as described in previous studies14,17.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized in the form of medians along with interquartile ranges(IQR), and comparisons among them were carried out using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and the Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were employed for making comparisons.

The study population that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria was partitioned into three groups (designated as high, medium, and low) based on the tertiles of log2-EASIX. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted to plot the survival curves for the 28-day, 60-day, and 180-day periods for these groups, and log-rank tests were utilized for the purpose of comparison. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to evaluate the association between log2-EASIX and the 28-day, 60-day, and 180-day mortalities among the patients. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, with adjustments made for potential confounders across different models. Specifically, the unadjusted model did not involve any variable adjustments. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, race, and body weight. Model 2 incorporated further adjustments for comorbidities such as myocardial injury, cerebrovascular disease, COPD, liver disease, renal disease, cancer, and diabetes. Model 3 was built upon Model 2, with additional adjustments for laboratory and clinical parameters, including WBC, hemoglobin, sodium, potassium, glucose, PT, APTT, lactate, PaCO₂, PaO₂/FiO₂, SAPSII, and interventions like RRT and vasopressor use. Covariate selection was based on clinical relevance and evidence from previous literature21,22. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a value < 10 indicating the absence of severe multicollinearity. Restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to investigate potential nonlinear relationships between log₂-EASIX and survival outcomes. Subgroup analyses were carried out to assess the robustness of the association between log2-EASIX and prognosis in patients with ARDS and to evaluate potential interaction effects. The subgroups were defined based on age, gender, race, weight, lactate, SOFA score, PaO₂/FiO₂, diabetes, COPD, cerebrovascular disease, myocardial injury, renal disease, and whether vasopressors or RRT were used on the first day. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the results by excluding patients with non-pulmonary sepsis.

To investigate the dynamic changes of EASIX, we applied Latent Class Mixed Models (LCMM) using the lcmm package in R software, based on repeated measurement data collected within the first 72 h of ICU admission. Models with 2 to 6 latent classes were compared based on statistical criteria, including maximum log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and average posterior probability (AvePP). An AvePP greater than 0.70 was considered indicative of adequate classification. Furthermore, to ensure representativeness, each class was required to comprise at least 5% of the total sample size. Finally, we evaluated the differences in prognosis among ARDS patients across the identified trajectories.

We employed machine learning (ML) approaches to construct predictive models for 28-day mortality, integrating EASIX with a comprehensive set of clinical parameters. To optimize variable selection, the Boruta algorithm, a random forest-based wrapper method comparing attributes with randomized permuted copies, was used to identify the top 10 predictive features. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and an independent validation set (30%). To rigorously prevent overfitting and optimize hyperparameters, a 10-fold cross-validation strategy was implemented internally within the training set. Seven algorithms were implemented using the scikit-learn, xgboost, and lightgbm libraries in Python: Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), and LightGBM. Performance was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), and calibration curves. Finally, feature importance for the best-performing model was interpreted using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values.

All statistical analyses were executed using R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation) and Python (version 3.10.4). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In this study, we identified 1,044 patients with ARDS from the MIMIC-IV database, based on established inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The median age of the study population was 63.13 years, and approximately 60% of them were male. The 28-day mortality rate in the ICU was 35%.

To explore the impact of log2-EASIX on clinical outcomes, we stratified patients into three groups according to tertiles of log2-EASIX: T1 (< 0.676), T2 (0.676–2.322), and T3 (> 2.322). The baseline characteristics of the enrolled patients are presented in Table 1. Notably, patients in higher log2-EASIX tertiles demonstrated distinct laboratory and clinical characteristics. Laboratory findings showed that higher log2-EASIX levels were associated with reduced hemoglobin, platelet, pH, PaO₂, PCO2 and PaO2/FiO2. Conversely, these patients exhibited elevated levels of potassium, blood glucose, creatinine, PT, APTT, LDH, and lactate. Vital sign analysis revealed faster heart rates, lower MBP, and higher respiratory rates in patients with elevated log2-EASIX. Disease severity scores, including SOFA, SAPS II, APSIII, and OASIS, were significantly higher among patients in the higher log2-EASIX tertiles. Patients in the higher log2-EASIX groups also had increased prevalence of cardiac injury, kidney disease, and liver disease. Furthermore, the proportion of patients requiring RRT and vasoactive drugs on the first day was significantly higher in these groups. With respect to survival outcomes, an elevated log2-EASIX was correlated with higher 28-day, 60-day, 180-day, and in-hospital mortality rates (all with P-values < 0.001), as well as longer ICU and hospital stays.

The relationship between log2-EASIX and survival outcomes

Cox regression analysis revealed that the log2-EASIX index was independently correlated with an elevated risk of mortality across multiple time points. Specifically, the log2-EASIX was associated with higher risks of 28-day mortality (HR 1.19; 95% CI 1.14–1.24; P < 0.001), 60-day mortality (HR 1.18; 95% CI 1.13–1.22; P < 0.001), and 180-day mortality (HR 1.16; 95% CI 1.12–1.21; P < 0.001). Multicollinearity was ruled out, as all VIF were < 10 (Table S1). Subsequently, in the fully adjusted Model 3, the log2-EASIX maintained a significant positive correlation with the risk of 28-day mortality (HR 1.07; 95% CI 1.01–1.14; P = 0.03), 60-day mortality (HR 1.08; 95% CI 1.01–1.14; P = 0.02), and 180-day mortality (HR 1.07; 95% CI 1.01–1.13; P = 0.02) (Table 2).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the stability of the association between log2-EASIX and 28-day mortality in patients with ARDS. The effect size trends remained consistent across all subgroups (Fig. 4A), indicating a robust relationship between log2-EASIX and mortality. No significant interactions were identified between subgroups (P interaction > 0.05). Similar findings were observed in subgroup analyses for 60-day and 180-day mortality rates(Fig. 4B and C). However, a significant interaction was specifically noted within the weight subgroup for 180-day mortality. Furthermore, to assess whether the prognostic value of EASIX was driven by septic shock-associated multiorgan failure, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding patients with non-pulmonary sepsis. In this analysis, the association between log2-EASIX and 28-day mortality remained statistically significant (HR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.04–1.23; P = 0.004) (Table S2).

Identification of dynamic EASIX trajectories

We performed dynamic trajectory analysis using the LCMM algorithm on the study population with available time-series EASIX data. Based on statistical criteria, including maximum log-likelihood, AIC, BIC, and AvePP (Table S3), we evaluated and compared models with varying numbers of latent classes. The optimal model identified three distinct EASIX trajectories: Trajectory 1 (Persistently Low), Trajectory 2 (Persistently Increasing), and Trajectory 3 (Rise-and-Fall) (Fig. 5). Kaplan-Meier survival curves revealed significant differences in 28-day, 60-day, and 180-day mortality among the three groups (log-rank test, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6). Notably, Trajectory 2 exhibited the lowest survival rate, followed by Trajectory 3. Multivariate Cox regression analysis further confirmed the significant association between these trajectories and mortality. After adjusting for all potential confounders, the mortality risks for Trajectory 2 and Trajectory 3 remained significantly higher than for Trajectory 1, with HRs of 7.56 (95% CI: 3.45–16.57, P < 0.001) and 1.98 (95% CI: 1.13–3.47, P = 0.02), respectively. Consistent results were observed for 60-day and 180-day mortality outcomes (Table 3).

Development of machine learning models

During model construction, the Boruta algorithm was employed for feature selection, initially identifying 19 statistically significant variables (Fig. S1). To balance model parsimony with clinical utility, the top 10 predictors, including EASIX, were retained for the final models. The comparative performance of the seven machine learning models in predicting 28-day mortality is illustrated in Fig. 7A and Table S4. The RF model achieved the highest performance in the validation set (AUC = 0.826), followed by XGBoost (AUC = 0.819), LightGBM (AUC = 0.814), ANN (AUC = 0.789), LR (AUC = 0.789), SVM (AUC = 0.777), and Decision Tree (AUC = 0.702). Furthermore, the predictive accuracy of the RF model exceeded that of traditional disease severity scoring systems, such as SAPS II, SOFA, OASIS, and APS III(Fig. 7B). DCA and calibration curves are presented in Fig. S2A and Fig. S2B.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate a significant association between elevated baseline EASIX index values and increased 28-day mortality rates in ARDS patients. This correlation was also observed in the 60-day and 180-day mortality rates among ARDS patients, revealing a consistent and linear relationship. Moreover, subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of this correlation across different patient subsets. Beyond static measurements, our dynamic analysis identified three distinct longitudinal EASIX trajectories in ARDS patients, with a ‘persistently increasing’ pattern signaling the poorest prognosis. Furthermore, machine learning models integrating EASIX demonstrated excellent predictive value. Among the evaluated algorithms, the Random Forest model performed the best (AUC = 0.826), outperforming traditional severity scoring systems.

ARDS is a complex inflammatory response in the lungs and throughout the body, involving neutrophil activation, damage to alveolar epithelium and vascular endothelium, which leads to non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema and atelectasis23. As a crucial barrier between the circulatory system and the lung parenchyma as well as the airways, pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells play a key role in maintaining the integrity of the endothelial barrier between the microvascular lumen and the pulmonary interstitium24. A range of pro-inflammatory agonists, including interleukin-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α, vascular endothelial growth factor, angiopoietin-2, as well as bacterial toxins and reactive oxygen species, induce dynamic changes in the cytoskeletal structure. These alterations disrupt adhesion junctions and cause the detachment of vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) from the actin cytoskeleton, thereby increasing endothelial cell permeability25,26. Moreover, during the infection process, inflammatory infiltration and the production of related pro-inflammatory cytokines can lead to the death of endothelial cells, further impairing the structural integrity of the pulmonary endothelial barrier27,28. Current studies have confirmed that the levels of endothelial biomarkers, such as von Willebrand factor (vWF)29, angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2)30,31, soluble intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1)32, endothelial cell-specific molecule-1 (ESM-1)33, selectin34, and others, are closely associated with the diagnosis and prognosis of ARDS. Therefore, given the pivotal role of endothelial activation in the pathophysiology of ARDS, EASIX offers significant practical utility for risk stratification. While EASIX serves as a valuable prognostic surrogate for the systemic burden of endothelial dysfunction, its direct correlation with specific biomarkers of pulmonary endothelial injury (e.g., Ang-2, vWF) warrants further validation. We also acknowledge the lack of causal or mediation evidence linking EASIX to systemic endothelial dysfunction in ARDS patients. Therefore, although EASIX correlates with endothelial dysfunction and is a useful prognostic surrogate, it should be interpreted as a composite risk marker rather than a direct measure of endothelial damage.

The EASIX has been identified as an important indicator for the prognosis of multiple diseases. In a study exploring the correlation between EASIX and the risk of non-relapse mortality (NRM) after allogeneic stem cell transplantation, the results showed that an EASIX score of ≥ 3 was associated with more than a twofold increase in the risk of NRM (HR = 2.18, 95% CI 1.2–3.94; P = 0.01)10. Moreover, a retrospective cohort study pointed out that among patients diagnosed with sepsis in ICU, an increase in the EASIX score was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality at 28 days and 90 days17. In other diseases related to endothelial injury, such as coronary artery disease35, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma15, multiple myeloma36, SARS-CoV-2 infection16 or chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy37, the prognostic predictive effect of EASIX has also been observed. In our study, we determined that the EASIX score is an independent risk factor for the mortality of patients with ARDS at 28 days, 60 days, and 180 days. Compared with the group with a low EASIX level, the risk of death within 28 days for patients with ARDS in the high EASIX group increased by 1.5 times (HR = 1.52; 95% CI 1.10–2.11; P = 0.01). In the sensitivity analysis, we also found that there was no interaction in all subgroups, and the trend of the effect size was consistent, indicating that the association between EASIX and the mortality rate of patients with ARDS is stable.

Given the substantial heterogeneity of ARDS, relying solely on static baseline indicators often fails to fully capture the complex disease evolution38–40. Consequently, recent studies have increasingly adopted trajectory analysis to classify patients into subgroups with similar temporal patterns. For instance, Bai et al.41 utilized longitudinal PaO2/FiO2 data to identify three oxygenation phenotypes, which demonstrated superior predictive value for prognosis and PEEP response compared to static definitions. Similarly, da Cruz et al.42 successfully validated distinct COVID-19 ARDS sub-phenotypes based on the longitudinal assessment of PaO2/FiO2 ratio, respiratory compliance, and ventilatory ratio. Inspired by these findings, we applied longitudinal modeling to investigate the dynamic prognostic value of EASIX. Through longitudinal trajectory analysis of EASIX data within the initial 72 h, we identified three distinct phenotypes: Trajectory 1 (Persistently Low), Trajectory 2 (Persistently Increasing), and Trajectory 3 (Rise-and-Fall). Trajectory 1 likely represents a subpopulation with preserved systemic homeostasis and stable organ function, corresponding to the most favorable prognosis. In contrast, Trajectory 2 was associated with the highest mortality, likely reflecting a state of progressive systemic dysregulation driven by severe, unremitting endothelial stress. Trajectory 3 may reflect an initial surge in systemic stress that is subsequently resolved, indicating a positive physiological response to treatment or spontaneous recovery. These findings suggest that serial EASIX measurements offer a novel perspective for personalized risk stratification and highlight the potential of dynamic monitoring in guiding timely interventions for ARDS.

To further leverage the demonstrated prognostic value of EASIX in ARDS, we integrated it with a comprehensive panel of clinical parameters to develop a robust predictive model for 28-day mortality. To achieve this, we developed and rigorously compared seven machine learning algorithms: LR, DT, SVM, RF, XGBoost, ANN, and LightGBM. These algorithms were specifically selected to represent a broad spectrum of methodological families—spanning linear models, distance-based algorithms, tree-based ensemble methods, and neural networks—which are mature, reliable, and have been widely validated in critical care research43–45. Our analysis revealed that tree-based ensemble methods, particularly the Random Forest model, achieved superior predictive performance compared to traditional linear or distance-based models. This advantage can be attributed to the inherent capability of ensemble algorithms to capture high-dimensional, non-linear relationships and complex interactions among clinical variables. Furthermore, to address the objective of an ablation study—namely, evaluating the contribution of specific model components—we incorporated SHAP analysis into our pipeline. Unlike conventional ablation approaches that require iterative feature removal and model retraining, SHAP offers a theoretically grounded and model-agnostic framework to explicitly quantify the marginal contribution of each variable while preserving the original model’s integrity. Our SHAP summary plot clearly demonstrates that EASIX ranks among the top-contributing features, confirming its substantial influence on the optimal model’s output and reaffirming its central prognostic value.

Our study also has several limitations. Firstly, this study is a retrospective study based on the MIMIC-IV database. Although we have enhanced the robustness of the conclusions by adjusting for potential confounding factors, the possibility of confounding factor bias cannot be completely ruled out. Therefore, future studies need to adopt a multicenter, prospective design to further verify our findings. Secondly, although this study has established that EASIX, as an endothelial activation stress index, is an independent risk factor for the prognosis of ARDS, we lack direct causal or mediation evidence linking EASIX to systemic endothelial dysfunction. Its specific association with both generalized and pulmonary-specific endothelial cell injury requires further confirmation through the direct measurement of specific biomarkers, such as ICAM-1 and Ang-2.

Conclusion

Our study confirms that both elevated baseline EASIX and persistently increasing EASIX trajectories are significantly associated with mortality in ARDS patients. Consequently, EASIX represents a valuable tool for dynamic risk stratification in the ICU. However, large-scale prospective studies are still required to validate these findings and evaluate their clinical utility.
