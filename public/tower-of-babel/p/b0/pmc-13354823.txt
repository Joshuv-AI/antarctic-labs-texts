Group Differences of Multiregion Glutamate Concentration in Clinical High Risk and Schizophrenia
Authors: Shue Kit Man, M. Belen Blasco, Razieh Alemi, Samira Raminfard, Pablo M. Rusjan, Jamie Near, Deepak K. Sarpal, Romina Mizrahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlutamatergic changes are one of the characteristic features of psychotic disorders, with changes reported in their clinical high‐risk states (CHR), first episode psychosis (FEP), and schizophrenia. Despite the abundance of literature using proton magnetic resonance (1H‐MRS) to quantify glutamate, the general pattern of changes across different brain regions is not well studied. We investigated the presence of diagnosis group effects on glutamate concentrations in 99 participants in the left dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, associative striatum, and hippocampus using 1H‐MRS in individuals with CHR (n = 31), schizophrenia spectrum psychotic disorder individuals (SCZ) with minimal antipsychotic exposure (n = 30), and healthy controls (n = 38). 1H‐MRS data were normalized against white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid fractions within regions of interest (ROI) to quantify local glutamate concentration. Our results showed no significant group differences in glutamate concentration across the four ROIs. Exploratory analyses showed a significant relationship between region and positive symptom severity on glutamate concentration. Post hoc regression analyses revealed that higher positive symptom severity was significantly associated with higher hippocampal glutamate concentrations. We conclude that glutamate concentrations may be related to positive symptoms.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a chronic disorder characterized by positive symptoms (hallucinations and delusions), negative symptoms (anhedonia and avolition), and cognitive deficits (working memory and executive dysfunction) (McCutcheon et al. 2020; Howes and Murray 2014). It affects approximately 1% of the global population, causing severe impairment in daily functioning and quality of life (McCutcheon et al. 2020; Vos et al. 2020). The period of attenuated symptoms preceding frank psychosis is commonly designated as the clinical high risk (CHR) stage, leading to conversion to first episode psychosis (FEP) of ~30% in 3 years (Fusar‐Poli et al. 2020).

Glutamatergic dysfunction has been widely implicated in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia, with converging evidence pointing to abnormalities in both cortical and subcortical glutamate signaling. It is proposed that hypofunction of NMDA receptors on cortical GABAergic interneurons reduces inhibition on cortical pyramidal neurons leading to excessive glutamate release (Krystal et al. 2003; Coyle 2012). The cascade may trigger subcortical dopamine hyperactivity that subsequently correlates with psychotic symptoms (Lodge and Grace 2011).

Given this proposed glutamatergic dysregulation in emerging and established psychosis, in vivo measures of glutamate offer a valuable approach to characterizing neurochemical abnormalities across illness stages. Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) is used for noninvasive, reliable quantification of in vivo neurometabolite alterations in different brain regions. Glutamate alterations in CHR, FEP, and schizophrenia have been reported across key nodes of cortico‐striato‐limbic circuits, and 1H‐MRS enables region‐specific assessment of these neurochemical changes. Many circuitries are strongly implicated in early psychosis, and glutamatergic alterations in such regions are often either observed in early psychosis or schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Merritt et al. 2023; McCutcheon et al. 2021). The ACC, which mediates decision‐making, cognitive control, and error monitoring, has shown elevated glutamate levels in individuals with schizophrenia relative to healthy controls on 1H‐MRS, along with age‐related reductions in glutamate within schizophrenia patients (Rowland et al. 2017; Brandt et al. 2016). The DLPFC facilitates executive functions, and increased glutamate concentration was reported in schizophrenia relative to healthy controls (Merritt et al. 2023; Tebartz Van Elst et al. 2005). The hippocampus, central to memory and contextual processing, showed increased hippocampal Glx (glutamate + glutamine) and hypermetabolism, possibly predictive of later hippocampal atrophy and psychosis onset in CHR (Bossong et al. 2019; Schobel et al. 2013). Finally, the associative striatum, where cortical glutamatergic projections modulate dopaminergic signaling, has shown elevated glutamate levels in unmedicated FEP patients. Higher striatal glutamate has also been reported in CHR individuals who later convert to psychosis compared with nonconverters, suggesting its potential relevance as a biomarker of transition (Fuente‐Sandoval et al. 2011; De La Fuente‐Sandoval, León‐Ortiz, Azcárraga, Favila, et al. 2013; Plitman et al. 2016).

Several previous 1H‐MRS studies have examined glutamate levels in schizophrenia, including at least three meta‐analyses, with most targeting single brain regions. Across the schizophrenia spectrum, these studies consistently report elevated glutamatergic metabolites in the basal ganglia, the thalamus, and medial temporal lobe (including hippocampus) (Merritt et al. 2023; Merritt et al. 2016). However, these meta‐analyses also emphasized substantial methodological and clinical heterogeneity (Merritt et al. 2023), particularly in the medial frontal cortex where findings vary as a function of age, ROI localization, illness stage, symptom severity, and medication exposure (Merritt et al. 2023; Merritt et al. 2016; Merritt et al. 2021). Overall, the literature points to region‐specific glutamatergic abnormalities but remains limited by predominantly single‐ROI approaches.

Based on the circuitry most strongly implicated in early psychosis, the present study used 1H‐MRS to quantify glutamate concentrations across four ROIs, the left DLPFC, ACC, hippocampus, and associative striatum in healthy controls (HC), CHR, and schizophrenia spectrum psychotic disorder (SCZ) individuals with minimal antipsychotic exposure. We hypothesize a graded pattern of glutamatergic alterations with HC showing the lowest concentration, followed by CHR, and SCZ exhibiting the highest concentration. Moreover, we conducted exploratory analyses evaluating the effects of age, biological sex, antipsychotic dose, cannabis and tobacco use, and psychotic symptoms severity on glutamate concentration, and evaluated whether group differences persisted when the associative striatum was excluded, given its distinct neurobiological modulation relative to cortical regions.

Methods and Materials

Participants

Participants were recruited from March 2014 to March 2020, previously approved by the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health Research (CAMH) Research Ethics Board (REB), Toronto, Canada, and later approved for secondary analyses under the Clinical and Translation Sciences (CaTS) BioBank by the REB of the Centre Intégré Universitaire de Santé et de Services Sociaux (CIUSSS) de l'Ouest‐de‐l'Île‐de‐Montréal (Mental Health and Neuroscience subcommittee). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after detailed explanations of the study procedures, and the capacity for informed consent was tested in CHR and SCZ populations via the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool for Clinical Research (MacCAT) (Grisso et al. 1997).

All SCZ participants met the criteria for DSM‐IV psychotic disorder (including schizophrenia, schizophreniform, schizoaffective, delusional disorder, and psychosis not otherwise specified), determined via a structured clinical interview for DSM‐IV (SCID) (Diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders: DSM‐IV 1994) and confirmed by a trained psychiatrist. Participants in the CHR group met criteria for prodromal risk syndrome using the Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndromes (Miller et al. 2003). All patients were antipsychotic free or received less than 250 mg of chlorpromazine equivalent antipsychotic dose. Healthy controls had no history of psychiatric illnesses as determined by SCID. Drug use history was assessed during baseline interviews and current use was confirmed via urine drug screen. Exclusion criteria for participants included pregnancy or breastfeeding, medical or neurological illness, and the presence of metal implants or other contraindications to MRI.

Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) (Kay et al. 1987) as well as the Scale of Psychosis‐Risk Symptoms (SOPS) (Miller et al. 2003) were used to assess the severity of symptoms in SCZ and CHR populations, respectively. One SCZ participant had data available only for the PANSS positive subscale and was therefore excluded from analyses involving PANSS total or negative symptom scores.

1H‐MRS Data Acquisition and Processing

1H‐MRS scans were acquired at the CAMH Brain Health Imaging Centre using a 3T General Electric Discovery MR750 scanner (Milwaukee, WI, USA) equipped with an eigth‐channel head coil. Structural images were obtained using a T1‐weighted fast spoiled‐gradient‐echo (FSPGR) sequence (TE = 3.0 ms, TR = 6.7 ms, TI = 650 ms, flip angle = 8°, FOV = 28 cm, acquisition matrix 256 × 256 matrix, slice thickness = 0.9 mm).

Single voxel 1H‐MRS spectra were obtained using a standard GE Proton Brain Examination (PROBE) sequence with point‐resolved spectroscopy (PRESS) sequence (TE = 35 ms, TR = 2000 ms, bandwidth = 5000 Hz, 4096 data points used, 256 water‐suppressed and 16 water‐unsuppressed scans). 1.4 × 3.0 × 1.0 cm3 voxels were placed in the hippocampus, 2.0 × 3.0 × 1.5 cm3 voxels were placed in the ACC, 1.5 × 3.0 × 3.0 cm3 voxels were placed in the DLPFC, and 1.5 × 2.5 × 5.4 cm3 voxels were placed in the associative striatum. Details of voxel placements and region‐specific example MRS spectra are illustrated in Figures S2–S5.

Spectral quantification was performed using LCModel (Version 6.3‐1R) with the standard PRESS basis set of metabolites at a TE of 35 ms. The basis set included the following metabolites: L‐alanine, aspartate, creatine (Cr), Cr methylene, γ‐aminobutyric acid, glucose, glutamate, glutamine, glutathione, glycerophosphocholine, L‐lactate, myo‐inositol, N‐acetylaspartate (NAA), N‐acetylaspartyl‐glutamate, phosphocholine, phosphocreatine, scyllo‐inositol, and taurine, as well as lipid and macromolecule components (MM) (Lip09, Lip13a, Lip13b, Lip20, MM09, MM12, MM14, MM17, and MM20).

All spectra included in the analyses met standard LCModel quality criteria, including full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM) ≤ 0.1 ppm and signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) ≥ 10, as recommended in the LCModel manual (Provencher 2014). Scans not meeting these quality thresholds or with evidence of artefact were excluded from the further analysis.

A total of 99 participants had usable MRS data for at least one region. Of these, 74 had hippocampus data, 92 had ACC data, 96 had DLPFC data, and 77 had associative striatum data (Figure S1). Region‐specific acquisition parameters are provided in Table S1.

Voxel Tissue Heterogeneity

Voxel tissue composition was estimated by co‐registering each participant's T1‐weighted structural images to the corresponding 1H‐MRS voxel to each region. T1‐weighted images were segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using the statistical parametric mapping (SPM) (Statistical Parametric Mapping 2007). Co‐registration of the 1H‐MRS voxel to the segmented structural image, as well as the extraction of WM, GM, and CSF proportions, were performed using Gannet 3.0 (Edden et al. 2014). Absolute glutamate concentrations were calculated using the absolute quantification method as described in Dhamala et al. (Dhamala et al. 2019), which correct for intersubject variability in voxel tissue composition by accounting for T1 and T2 relaxation times, water concentration, and volume fractions of GM, WM, and CSF within the voxel, as well as T2 relaxation time of the glutamate, echo time and repetition time of the acquisition to increase interstudy comparability of glutamate concentration. (Dhamala et al. 2019).

Statistical Analysis

Group differences in demographics and clinical variables were examined using independent‐sample t‐tests or chi‐square tests, as appropriate. Linear mixed‐effects models were used to test the main hypothesis. Glutamate concentration was included as the dependent variable and ROI (ACC, DLPFC, hippocampus, and associative striatum) as a repeated within‐participant factor. Group (HC, CHR, and SCZ), ROI, and group × ROI interaction were included as fixed factors to test for diagnoses effects and their association with regional glutamate concentrations. Participants were modelled as random intercepts. Age was included as a covariate in all analyses due to the unbalanced age distribution between clinical and control groups, as well as to account for its effects on the hippocampus (Kaiser et al. 2005; Pomerleau et al. 2025).

The covariance structure was selected after comparing alternative structures (diagonal, unstructured, and toeplitz) based on AIC/BIC fit indices and model convergence parameters. Analyses both including and excluding the associative striatum were done due to the differences in functionality and relationship in glutamate across ROIs. Exploratory analyses were conducted to evaluate the effect of potential confounding variables. Because the group × ROI interaction was not significant in the primary model, this interaction term was removed for parsimony. The potential effect of sex, antipsychotic medication, tobacco use, and cannabis use was examined by adding each covariate separately to the main model.

To explore the association between symptom severity and glutamate concentrations, separate mixed‐effects models were run within SCZ and CHR groups. Total symptom severity (PANSS total in SCZ; SOPS total in CHR) and positive and negative symptom domains were tested, including their interaction with ROI. As three symptom dimensions were assessed (total, positive, and negative), multiple comparisons were controlled using an alpha of 0.05/3.

Effect sizes were calculated using sum of squares from SPSS Version 29 and were interpreted as follows: Cohen's f = 0.1, small effect; Cohen's f = 0.25, medium effect; and Cohen's f = 0.40, a large effect. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Ninety‐nine participants, including 30 SCZ, 31 CHR, and 38 healthy controls completed all required imaging procedures and were included in the analysis (including QC) (Figure S1). Across the combined CHR‐SCZ cohort (n = 61), 50 participants were antipsychotic‐free at the time of scanning, one had incomplete medication data, and the remaining participants had received less than 250 mg of chlorpromazine equivalent antipsychotic exposure. Glutamate concentration did not significantly differ across groups (F 2,91.024 = 0.022, p = 0.978) when the associative striatum was excluded from the analysis. The effects of covariates relevant to psychosis and glutamate concentration (age, sex, antipsychotic use, etc.) are detailed in Table S3. No significant differences or effects in glutamate concentration were found between groups when controlling for age, sex, antipsychotic dose, cannabis use, and tobacco use.

Symptom Severity Associated With Glutamate Concentration

There was a significant interaction effect between positive symptoms severity and ROI (F 3, 33.091 = 4.930, p = 0.006, Cohen F = 0.669), an effect that survived correction for multiple comparisons. Post hoc analysis showed a significant positive correlation between positive symptoms severity and glutamate concentrations in the hippocampus (Figure 2). Discussion

The primary aim of the current study was to examine whether glutamate concentration differed across key corticostriatal and hippocampal regions in individuals at CHR for psychosis (CHR) and patients diagnosed with schizophrenia spectrum psychotic disorder (SCZ), relative to healthy controls. Across the hippocampus, ACC, DLPFC, and associative striatum, we did not observe significant group effects on glutamate concentrations. However, we identified a significant interaction between region and symptom severity in the SCZ group: higher positive symptom severity was associated with higher hippocampal glutamate concentrations. No other ROIs showed symptom‐related effects.

These findings partially contrast with earlier reports describing higher glutamatergic metabolites, particularly Glx or glutamine, in SCZ in regions such as the hippocampus and associative striatum (Merritt et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2023). Notably, our results are more consistent with recent meta‐analytic evidence showing limited or absent group differences in glutamate in young, early‐phase, and minimally medicated SCZ (Merritt et al. 2023; Merritt et al. 2016), as well as in CHR individuals (McCutcheon et al. 2021). This pattern corresponds to our cohort characteristics, which included predominantly young individuals with minimal antipsychotic exposure. Prior work suggests that small group‐level effects observed in some studies may reflect alterations primarily in Glx rather than glutamate specifically (Merritt et al. 2016; Nakahara et al. 2022), which may also explain the absence of group differences in the present study. Similarly, in CHR populations, early studies reported increases in medial frontal glutamatergic metabolites (Merritt et al. 2016); however, a more recent meta‐analysis incorporating larger datasets and improved harmonization protocols has not found reliable group differences for glutamate or Glx in this group (McCutcheon et al. 2021). This aligns with our finding of no glutamatergic abnormalities in CHR individuals across four ROIs.

Notably, the clinical groups in the present study were relatively young (SCZ mean age = 26, CHR = 20.9) and largely antipsychotic free, whereas many earlier MRS reports have predominantly examined older, chronically ill, and medicated patients (Merritt et al. 2023; Nakahara et al. 2022; Salazar De Pablo et al. 2021). Age and medication status have emerged as key moderators of glutamatergic findings: A recent meta‐analysis demonstrated that both factors are independently associated with frontal glutamate levels, with older age and greater cumulative antipsychotic exposure linked to lower concentrations (Merritt et al. 2021). Consistent with this, longitudinal studies have reported reductions in glutamate following antipsychotic treatment (Merritt et al. 2019; Egerton et al. 2017), and meta‐analytic evidence indicates that frontal glutamate decreases are observed primarily in medicated patients (Merritt et al. 2021). These observations provide a plausible explanation for the absence of group differences in our predominantly young and minimally medicated cohort.

Furthermore, potential confounding variables, including antipsychotic equivalent dose, sex, tobacco use, and cannabis use, did not significantly influence glutamate levels in any ROIs, and their inclusion in the mixed‐effects model did not alter the null group findings. Given the distinct neurobiological characteristics of cortical and striatal glutamatergic systems, we also evaluated regional heterogeneity. Cortical glutamate primarily reflects excitatory neurotransmitter among pyramidal neurons with high NMDA receptor density (Moghaddam and Javitt 2012), whereas striatal glutamatergic activity is strongly shaped by dopaminergic modulation of corticostriatal inputs (Howes et al. 2017). To account for these mechanistic distinctions, we conducted an exploratory analysis excluding the associative striatum; however, group differences remained nonsignificant (Table S3). Taken together, these findings underscore the complexity and regional specificity of glutamatergic alterations in psychosis and suggest that large, robust case–control differences in glutamate may not be a robust feature of younger, less chronically treated cohorts.

We examined associations between glutamate and symptom severity given previous findings suggesting that clinical severity corresponds to alterations in in vivo glutamatergic measures (Merritt et al. 2021). In individuals with SCZ, we observed a significant relationship between positive symptom severity and hippocampal glutamate concentrations. This finding aligns with evidence linking greater symptom severity to elevated glutamate metabolites within the medial temporal lobe (Merritt et al. 2023) and converges with multimodal evidence implicating early hippocampal dysfunction in psychosis. For example, genetic studies have demonstrated that schizophrenia risk variants are enriched in genes highly expressed in excitatory hippocampal neurons (Trubetskoy et al. 2022), and neuroimaging studies have consistently reported hippocampal hypermetabolism and dysconnectivity in early stages of schizophrenia (Shakory et al. 2018).

Few prior 3T MRS studies have examined PANSS–glutamate associations specifically in the hippocampus (Shakory et al. 2018; Olbrich et al. 2008). Although no significant associations were reported, those studies included smaller samples, and one of them reported associations with cognitive symptoms (Shakory et al. 2018). Here, we did not observe significant associations in other symptoms or symptom domains. However, Egerton et al. reported that increased PANSS negative symptoms were associated with higher glutamate concentration in the anterior cingulate cortex in FEP cohorts, and de la Fuente‐Sandoval et al. showed a positive correlation between striatal glutamate concentrations and PANSS positive scores (Egerton et al. 2012; De La Fuente‐Sandoval, León‐Ortiz, Azcárraga, et al. 2013).

In CHR individuals, we found no significant relationship between glutamate and prodromal symptom severity, although previous work has shown elevated hippocampal GLx in CHR individuals who later transition to psychosis (Bossong et al. 2019). Together, these findings suggest that symptom dimensions may map onto regional neurochemical alterations in a more targeted and region‐specific manner than categorical diagnoses alone would predict.

These results should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, although our study defines the ACC as the ROI, the alignment of the voxel covers mostly the dorsal ACC. This should be considered when comparing our results with other studies as the dorsal ACC is functionally more involved in cognitive and motor control than emotional processing. Second, we acquired spectra using a 3T MRI scanner, which provides less spectral resolution compared to higher strengths (Smucny et al. 2021; Pradhan et al. 2015) and may lead to comparability limitations beyond clinical heterogeneity. For instance, Kim et al. reported elevated ACC glutamate concentration in SCZ individuals at 4T, whereas Wang et al. observed decreased glutamate in the same region at 7T. Third, although our cohort consisted largely of young and antipsychotic‐free individuals, features that reduce medication‐related confounding (Merritt et al. 2023), these same characteristics may also be associated with greater clinical heterogeneity and reduced effect sizes, as noted in previous early‐psychosis studies (Salazar De Pablo et al. 2021; Bromet 2005). Fourth, the number of high‐quality spectra varied across regions, with smaller effective sample sizes in the hippocampus and associative striatum, which may have reduced statistical power to detect small effects. A post hoc power analysis suggests that our study is well powered (0.8) to detect an effect size of 0.15 with an α error of 0.05. Finally, although some participants screened positive for cannabis, including this in the model did not significantly affect the main findings (Results S1).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found no significant differences between HC, CHR, and SCZ individuals in terms of glutamate concentrations across the hippocampus, ACC, left DLPFC, and associative striatum. In line with previous evidence, our findings suggest that case–control differences in glutamate are unlikely to represent a robust feature of early psychosis in young unmedicated cohorts, and that symptom‐level dimensions may offer a more sensitive index of glutamatergic dysregulation than diagnostic category alone. Future research using higher‐field‐strength scanners or dual voxels may offer an improved separation of glutamate and glutamine and investigation of hemispheric differences, which are important to rule out previous discrepancies in the literature. Longitudinal studies with high‐resolution MRS are also needed to determine whether glutamate concentrations change over the course of psychosis development.
