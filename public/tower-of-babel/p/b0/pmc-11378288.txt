Defining the Spatial Resolution of Analyte Recovery during Microperfusion-Based Sampling of Brain Parenchyma
Authors: Luke A Stangler, Su-Youne Chang, Inyong Kim, Jonghoon Choi, Abbas Z Kouzani, Kevin E Bennet, Terry C Burns, Jamie J Van Gompel, Gregory A Worrell, Charles L Howe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe unique architecture of the brain and the blood-brain barrier imposes challenges for the measurement of parenchyma-derived biomarkers that prevent sufficient understanding of transient neuropathogenic processes. One solution to this challenge is direct sampling of brain interstitial fluid via implanted microperfusion probes. Seeking to understand spatial limitations to microperfusion in the brain, we employed computational fluid dynamics modeling and empirical recovery of fluorescently labeled dextrans in an animal model. We found that dextrans were successfully recovered via microperfusion over a 6 h sampling period, especially at probes implanted 2 mm from the dextran infusion point relative to probes implanted 5 mm from the injection site. Experimental recovery was consistently around 1% of simulated, suggesting that this parameter can be used to set practical limits on the maximal tissue concentration of proteins measured in microperfusates and on the spatial domain sampled by our multimodal microperfusion probe.Keywords: microperfusion, diffusion, brain, computational fluid dynamics, biomarker, interstitial fluid

Introduction

Biomarker discovery fuels deeper understanding of pathogenic mechanisms and accelerates early detection and prognostication across essentially all human diseases. In parallel, quantitation of such biomarkers is vital to tracking disease progression and to validating therapeutic effect and efficacy. Most organ systems and most pathogenic processes occur within tissues that are readily accessible to systemic vascular perfusion and therefore molecules that are liberated during such processes are detectable within the blood at levels that linearly reflect tissue-specific concentrations. The brain, however, represents a uniquely challenging compartment for biomarker detection and quantitation. The blood-brain barrier, while not strictly impermeable to molecules either entering the central nervous system (CNS) from the blood or exiting from the CNS into circulation, introduces robust nonlinearity into the exchange of materials and imposes unpredictable limitations on the utility of using blood borne biomarkers to assess CNS pathobiology.1 Likewise, while access to the cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) may provide valuable insights into molecular events outside the vasculature, the CSF only interacts with parenchymal tissue a short distance from the ependymal lining of the brain ventricles or the leptomeningeal surface of the brain. Indeed, the average distance from any neuron to a blood microvessel is approximately 10–20 μm throughout the neuraxis2,3 but the vast majority of neurons in the brain parenchyma are millimeters or centimeters from the nearest CSF compartment.4

As we have previously discussed,5 the application of microperfusion probe-based sampling of brain interstitial fluid provides a unique mechanism for sampling dynamic changes in molecular and protein concentrations at sites within the CNS that are otherwise inaccessible to systemic compartments. While neurosurgical implantation of microperfusion probes is not something that will be implemented in the average outpatient population, within the context of specific neuropathological conditions such as drug-resistant epilepsy, quantitation of interstitial fluid molecules and macromolecules may provide valuable insights into pathogenic mechanisms and offers the possibility of assessing therapeutic efficacy at the site in which such mechanisms are active. To this end, we previously reported the construction of a multimodal probe that coupled electroencephalography (EEG) with microperfusion.6 This dual-sensing probe uses an M Dialysis 71 fluidics assembly (with microdialysis membrane removed) inserted into an Ad-Tech Spencer depth electrode customized by laser ablation of 560 μm diameter ports around the perimeter of the electrode. Fluid perfusion is controlled by a push–pull pump to achieve zero net fluid loss through the large ports. Using this device and unbiased proteomic analysis of the microperfusate, we reported recovery of high molecular weight proteins from the brain parenchyma at the site of electrical stimulation that were not detected in cerebrospinal fluid or in bulk tissue interstitial fluid collected from the same site.6 These observations prompted us to consider the spatial domain over which the sampling occurred. For example, we measured approximately 0.5 nM interleukin-6 at the site of stimulation in microperfusate collected during a 1 h epoch–did this represent sampling within only a few microns of the probe? or over tens or hundreds of microns or even millimeters? While the field has provided both experimental and theoretical guidance regarding the likely recovery space around microdialysis systems,7,8 we wanted to characterize the recovery performance of our specific dual-sensing microperfusion probe. Given that our long-term goal is to use such multimodal EEG-microperfusion probes in patients, we experimentally measured the spatial and temporal profile of analyte recovery within the brain using this probe and we compared these findings to simulations in order to identify practical limits on the spatial domain within which such sampling occurs.

Results and Discussion

Simulated Diffusion of 3 kDa Dextran

Finite element analysis was performed in ANSYS Fluent for both brain parenchyma and 0.3% agar by treating the system as an isotropic semipermeable medium. The geometry was simplified to a planar wedge extracted from a sphere with a radius of 10 mm (Figure 2A). The source was modeled as a central 100-μm diameter sphere (reduced to a central planar wedge) and the boundary surface was defined as the inlet for 330 μM dextran dissolved in water. The outer circumference of the model was specified as a zero-concentration boundary. Other system parameters included 37 °C, no gravity, and 1 atmospheric pressure. Assumptions included zero bulk flow in the tissue matrix, constant inlet concentration, and matrix isotropy. For the 3 kDa dextran in agar we used diffusion coefficient D = 2.33 × 10–6 cm2/s, as previously described.9 For the effective diffusion coefficient of 3 kDa dextran in brain, we chose to use the minor axis value measured by Xiao et al.9 (D* = 5.65 × 10–7 cm2/s) in order to offset the absence of anisotropy considerations in our simulation and the lack of knowledge regarding anisotropy in the tissue at the site of our probe placement. Parameters such as volume fraction and other barriers to diffusion are included in the diffusion coefficients. The steady state concentration at t → ∞ was solved on a 318-element mesh (Figure 2A) and plotted against distance from the central source (Figure 2B). In addition, transient concentrations at 15 min, 1 h, and 6 h time points were calculated using 0.1 s time steps (Figure 2B).

Actual Diffusion of Dextran in Agar

A cocktail of fluorescently labeled dextrans with different molecular weights (3 kDa, Alexa488; 10 kDa, Alexa647; 40 kDa, Texas Red) was introduced continuously at 30 nL/min into 0.3% agar for up to 6 h. The concentration of each dextran was 330 μM in PBS. Microperfusion was performed using our previously described dual-sensing EEG-microperfusion probe.6 Perfusate was collected at 2 μL/min via two probes located at 2 and 5 mm (centerline distance) from the dextran source (Figures 1C and 3A). Fractions (120 μL) were collected every hour from each probe and analyzed spectrophotometrically to determine the recovered concentration of each dextran species (Figure 3B,C). The actual recovery of 3 kDa dextran at 2 mm from the source was consistently around 1% of the simulated concentration at each time point (1.1% ± 0.3%, mean ±95%CI). Excluding the 1 h sample (54% of simulated), the recovery of 3 kDa dextran at 5 mm from the source was also around 1% of the simulated concentration at each time point (1.0% ± 0.6%).

Actual Diffusion of Dextran in Brain Parenchyma

The dextran cocktail was intraoperatively delivered into the brain parenchyma of a pig using the same parameters employed in the agar experiments: 330 μM dextran, 30 nL/min delivery infusion rate (Figure 4A). Likewise, microperfusion probes were stereotactically placed at 2 and 5 mm centerline distance from the point of infusion and perfusate was collected at 2 μL/min for 6 h. Fractions (120 μL) were collected every hour from each probe and analyzed spectrophotometrically to determine the recovered concentration of each dextran species (Figure 4E,F). Microscopic visualization of the labeled dextrans in post-mortem brain tissue (Figure 4A–D) confirmed the expected molecular weight dependent difference in diffusion through the brain parenchyma but also revealed anisotropic dispersion in the tissue and cellular uptake/aggregation of the low molecular weight dextran in a penumbra around the infusion site. In addition, at least for the specific animal used for microscopy, the 5 mm probe experienced lateral displacement during insertion and was positioned only 3.5 mm from the infusion site. This spatial discrepancy may explain the large variation observed between two different experiments at the 5 mm probe (Figure 4F). Unexpectedly, one animal exhibited recovery of 10 kDa dextran during the fourth and sixth 1 h epochs that approached or exceeded the level obtained for the 3 kDa molecule (Figure 4E). Finally, the recovery for both 3 kDa and 40 kDa molecules largely plateaued from the third epoch through the sixth epoch.

Simulated versus Actual Recovery in Brain Parenchyma

Based on the finite element analysis of 3 kDa dextran diffusion we expected to recover 16.7 μM dextran during the sixth one hr microperfusion epoch at the 2 mm probe location (Figure 5A). The actual recovery during this epoch was 40 nM, or less than 0.25% of expected. Across all epochs the recovery was approximately 0.6% ± 0.4% at the 2 mm distance and approximately 0.9% ± 0.8% at the 5 mm probe (excluding the first two epochs) (Figure 5B). However, another way of looking at the recovery requires following the conservation of molecular mass within the system (Table 1). For 3 kDa dextran, delivered at 30 nL/min from a 330 μM source, we infused a total of 10.7 μg (or 2.2 × 1015 molecules) into the system. At the 2 mm probe we recovered a total of 78 ng (or 1.6 × 1013 molecules), resulting in an aggregate recovery of 0.7% (compared to 0.6% actual relative to simulated). Notably, recovery reached 1% of infused by the third epoch for 3 kDa dextran (Table 1) and this recovery was maintained over the fourth and fifth epochs. For 10 kDa dextran at the 2 mm probe site, 35.6 μg (7.2 × 1015 molecules) was infused and 134 ng (2.7 × 1013 molecules) was recovered over the 6 h time frame, resulting in an aggregate recovery of 0.4%. For 3 kDa dextran at the 5 mm probe site, only 1 ng (2 × 1011 molecules) was recovered (all in the sixth hr epoch in one animal) (Figure 4F), resulting in an aggregate recovery of 0.01%. Overall, actual recovery of 3 kDa and 10 kDa dextrans at the 2 mm probe site was approximately 1% of the amount expected based on the physical parameters used in the finite element analysis simulation.

Microperfusion Sampling Implications

Local field potentials measured by each macroelectrode along a clinical stereoelectroencephalographic depth probe represent the linear superposition of postsynaptic currents originating within a volume approximately 1 mm from the surface of the electrode,10 depending upon brain region.11,12 Using the same microperfusion probe setup employed in the current study, we previously estimated recovery of ∼140 pg interleukin-6 (IL6) during a 1 h epoch following electrical stimulation of pig brain.6 IL6 was undetectable in the perfusate during the epochs prior to stimulation and was also not detected in CSF and surrounding tissue bulk ISF. Based on the constraints imposed by our empirical measurement of dextran recovery, what is the likely physiologically relevant range over which we detected this IL6 production? Several assumptions are required to tackle this question: (1) professional cytokine secreting cells such as dendritic cells release approximately 46,000 IL6 molecules per cell per hour, equivalent to about 2 fg/cell/h, following inflammatory stimulation;13 (2) stimulated macrophages exhibit a supersecretor phenotype in which some cells release over 10 fg/cell/h IL6;14 (3) IL6 is released by microglia but not astrocytes in response to electromagnetic stimulation;15 (4) the density of microglia in cortex is on the order of 8,500 cells per cubic millimeter.16

Hence, within any microliter volume of cortex, if all microglia responded maximally to stimulation, the upper limit of released IL6 would be ∼2 × 109 molecules per hr, or about 85 pg IL6. Converting back to molar concentration

Recovery of 140 pg IL6 in 45 μL perfusate in a 1 h poststimulation microperfusion epoch (equivalent to 3.4 × 109 molecules IL6) in our previous experiments therefore suggests that we collected IL6 that must have derived from 1.7 mm3 (1.7 μL) of brain tissue in that epoch.

Based on this analysis, the relationship between concentration of analyte in the perfusate and the potential distance to the source of the analyte can be considered for the case in which the target molecule is released in a region concentric to the probe cylinder. This case is likely for our published observation of IL6 release induced by electrical stimulation at an electrode surrounded by the microperfusion ports.6 We make the following additional assumptions: (1) the height of the capture space along the probe cylinder is 6000 μm (see Figure 1 in Stangler et al., 20236); (2) the distribution of cells releasing the target molecule is isotropic in the volume surrounding the probe; (3) the volume fraction for brain extracellular space (i.e., interstitial volume) is approximately 20%.17 Using the microglial density and maximal response outlined above, our recovery of 140 pg IL6 captured cytokine released by cells within only ∼50 μm from the probe edge. However, our findings in this study suggest that analyte recovery via the microperfusion probe is only on the order of 1% of the actual tissue concentration. Therefore, if we measured 140 pg IL6 in the microperfusate collected during a 1 h epoch, the actual interstitial fluid concentration would contain 14 ng of IL-6 (3.4 × 1011 molecules). Assuming maximal release of IL6 in response to electrical stimulation, the volume required to produce 14 ng IL6 is approximately 167 mm3 (167 μL) of brain tissue in that epoch.

This is equivalent to a cylindrical volume expanding from the surface of the probe for 2.2 mm. This distance is conveniently similar to the 2 mm probe distance used in our current simulations and experiments to estimate the molecular recovery from a distal source.

A number of variables impact this estimate, including the nonlinearity in our actual recovery relative to distance from a point source. For example, our findings indicate recovery of 1% of dextran when the point source is 2 mm from the microperfusion probe. But this value likely increases nonlinearly as the source moves closer to the probe. Likewise, while we capture perfusate in 1 h epochs, the effect of time on diffusion of the molecules is obviously crucial to determining the sampling volume. Indeed, in our previous study regarding IL6 recovery following electrical stimulation, we observed a 5-fold increase in IL6 in the second 1 h epoch after the stimulation relative to the first hour. Whether this was due to ongoing stimulation-induced release or was the result of diffusion through a larger volume is unknown. Further complications include the effects of tissue tortuosity, the impact of electrical stimulation and neural network activity on such tortuosity, the impact of molecular trapping by either nonspecific or receptor-mediated cellular uptake, proteolytic degradation of the molecules in transit, loss to transport across the blood-brain barrier, and inhomogeneous diffusion due to tissue anisotropy (such as shunting by nearby white matter tracts).

Conclusions

Our near-term goal is to develop a multimodal electrophysiology and microperfusion probe for investigation of focal drug-resistant epilepsy in patients undergoing depth electrode mapping to identify seizure onset zones prior to neurosurgical resection. Our longer-term goal is to create a discovery platform for human and nonhuman preclinical animal model use to simultaneously monitor electroencephalography and interstitial fluid composition in the mammalian brain during numerous pathological states in order to gain new insights into pathogenic mechanisms and to identify new therapeutic interventions. In the present study our objective was to establish parameters that define the potential tissue volume over which analytes of interest are recovered in situ. Overall, this study establishes practical limits on the volumetric space that can be associated with microperfusion sampling using our multimodal device. The findings also, however, validate operation of the device within physiologically relevant parameters. For example, if detection of ∼100 pM concentrations of cytokines in microperfusate captured during a 1 h epoch requires production at tissue concentrations of 10 nM over a volume defined by a radius of approximately 1 mm, then this is an achievable state (for example, we previously measured >1 nM IL6 in inflamed mouse hippocampus18). Moreover, our findings suggest that the spatial domain of cytokine sampling in the microperfusate (∼2 mm) is on the same order of magnitude as the EEG sampling space (∼1 mm), strengthening the possibility of correlating altered electrophysiological responses with pathophysiologically relevant inflammatory signaling in the CNS of patients with drug-resistant epilepsy undergoing monitoring with our device.

Materials and Methods

Simulations

The computational fluid dynamics simulations were conducted using finite element analysis in ANSYS Fluent (ANSYS, Inc., Canonsburg, PA, US) to mathematically partition simulated agar or brain tissue and to model diffusion from a discrete concentration inlet using Fick’s law.

In Vitro Microperfusion

A brain surrogate system was constructed using 0.3% agar prepared in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (1 mM KH2PO4, 155 mM NaCl, 3 mM Na2HPO4·7H2O, ∼ 300 mOsm/kg, pH 7.4). Wells of plastic 12-well dishes were filled with agar at 37 °C and then stored overnight at 4 °C to harden. The system was equilibrated to 37 °C prior to sampling. Microperfusion probes modified as previously described6 were aligned perpendicular to the agar surface, spaced at 2 and 5 mm from the center of the well, and then inserted to the same depth (Figures 1C and 3A). The microperfusion probe was perfused with PBS at 2 μL/min using two synchronized NE-4000 push–pull syringe pumps (New Era Pump Systems, Farmington, New York, US). A cocktail of fluorescently conjugated dextrans (3 kDa, Alexa488; 10 kDa, Alexa647; 40 kDa, Texas Red) (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, US) was prepared at 330 μM in PBS and delivered into the center of the agar plug at 30 nL/min using a QSI nanoliter syringe pump (Braintree Scientific, Braintree, Massachusetts, US). Fractions were collected every hour (∼120 μL) and analyzed on a SpectraMax M3 multimodal spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, US) to quantify fluorescence. Concentrations were derived from standard curves prepared in PBS.

In Vivo Microperfusion

All study procedures were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for Animal Research (Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals) and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Pigs were housed socially in a controlled environment (21 °C, 45% humidity, 12 h light-dark cycle) with once daily feeding and ad libitum access to water. Subjects were fasted starting the day before surgery. Two separate pigs were used for microperfusion experiments. Each animal was sedated with Telazol (tiletamine/zolazepam) and xylazine at 5 mg/kg via intramuscular injection. An endotracheal tube was placed, and an intravenous catheter was inserted and secured in an ear vein. Fentanyl was continuously infused at 10 μg/kg/h through the intravenous catheter. Surgical depth of anesthesia was maintained with 1.5–3.0% isoflurane throughout the procedure. Heart rate, indirect blood pressure, body temperature, and oxygen saturation were monitored throughout the procedure.

For placement of the microperfusion probes, the subject’s head was fixed in a stereotactic frame. A midline cutaneous incision was made in the scalp, burr holes were drilled in the skull, and the dura was opened. Four microperfusion probes were implanted into the estimated location of the primary sensory cortex. Two probes were employed for the dextran recovery experiments. These were placed in the right hemisphere to a depth of 7 mm and separated from one another by 7 mm (inner edge to inner edge). At 2 mm from the posterior and 5 mm from the anterior probe a Hamilton syringe needle was inserted into the brain to 6 mm depth. The same experimental parameters (probe design, pump system, flow rates) used for microperfusion in agar were applied to in vivo analysis in the pig brain. Microperfusates were collected hourly for 6 h and analyzed as above. At the conclusion of the sampling the probes were explanted, the animal was euthanized, and a tissue block encompassing the experimental site was resected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed tissue was oriented by visual inspection and sectioned at 100 μm by vibratome perpendicular to the plane of probe emplacement. Sections were washed in PBS, mounted on glass slides under VectaShield antifade mountant (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, US), and imaged on an Olympus Fluoview 1000 confocal microscope. Images were postprocessed in Adobe Photoshop and Fiji.19
