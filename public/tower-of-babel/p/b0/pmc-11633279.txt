Association of Abdominal Obesity and Diabetes among the Adult Tribal Population in Tamil Nadu
Authors: Kalaivanan Ramamoorthy, Logaraj Muthunarayanan, M Palanivel Sundaram, M Devivaraprasad, VV Anantharaman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Obesity and overweight are growing health issues in India. It is important to determine the quantum of the risk contribution by individual risk factor siuch as abdominal obesity.Aims:To find out the association of abdominal obesity and diabetes among the adult tribal population.Methods and Material:A cross-sectional study was conducted using multi-stage random sampling techniques. Using a pretested validated modified Stepwise questionnaire, a detailed interview with the participants aged above 30 years were performed. INTRODUCTION

India’s tribal population is the second-largest of all tribal population in the world which accounts to 8.6% of countries total population.[1] The areas inhabited by the tribal constitute a significant part of the underdeveloped areas of the country. The tribal live mostly in isolated villages or hamlets. Some of them are far away from civilization with their own traditional values, beliefs, customs, and myths intact. So identifying the risk of an individual as early as possible and also proper awareness about the lifestyle habits will make an effect on their morbidity, functional dependency, and premature mortality. An individual with obesity has abnormal or excessive body fat that has a detrimental influence on their health. Previously overweight and obesity are considered to be an issue of high-income countries but now increasingly becoming more prevalent in low-income and middle-income countries, especially in urban areas. Obesity and overweight are growing health issues in India.[2] Persons who work in agricultural or manual labor are less likely to be an overweight than people who live in cities.[2] In developing countries such as India, the increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity has coincided with the demographic and epidemiological transitions, in which mortality and fertility have declined, and lifestyle-related diseases have become more common. Compared with white people, Indians are more likely to be obese and have lower levels of muscular mass. Increased consumption of unhealthy food and lower levels of physical activity might likely have contributed to an increase in the prevalence of obesity and its comorbidities. In India, hypertension and T2DM are the major noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) leading to catastrophic complications including death. It is important to investigate the role of modifiable risk factors resulting in NCDs such as obesity, physical inactivity, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption. It is, therefore, important to determine the quantum of the risk contribution by individual risk factor such as obesity. Available evidence suggests strong associations between obesity and NCDs. However, very rare of the earlier reviews have specifically evaluated the role of abdominal obesity in the etiology of T2DM in India. The prevalence of obesity has increased significantly in India over the last few decades. As a result, the number of persons with T2DM could increase exponentially. We limit our investigation to T2DM. Recognizing the potential role of abdominal obesity and its impact on diabetes will inform future interventions to reduce the burden of these diseases. High prevalence of abdominal obesity was found in southern states of Kerala (65.4%) and Tamil Nadu (57.9%) and Northern states of Punjab (62.5%) and Delhi (59%).[3] Very few studies have evaluated on abdominal obesity and diabetes among tribal population.

This study was performed among the adult tribal population of Kalvarayan Hills, Kallakurichi district, Tamil Nadu, India. The Kalvarayan Hills are a major range of hills situated in the Eastern Ghats of the Southern Indian state of Tamil Nadu. The hills straddle a number of Tamil Nadu districts, extending Northeast from the Salem District. The range serves as a boundary between the Salem and Villupuram districts.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

A cross-sectional study was conducted from June 2021 - October 2022 in Kalvarayan Hills. Adults aged 30 years and above from both genders who were permanent residents of the study area and who gave consent were included in the study. Whereas those who are not willing to participate in the study or give consent and who are not available at the time of visits [after 3 consecutive visits] were excluded from the study.

Using the study by Parthibane Sivanantham et al.,[4] which reported an overall 46.1% of participants are obese, a sample size of 382 is obtained. With a nonresponse rate of 10%, sample size was 420. Multistage random sampling methods were used—One PHC is randomly selected out of 5 PHCs, From the selected PHC one sub-center is randomly selected. Nine villages under the sub-center have been covered through probability proportionate to size. Using simple random sampling the desired sample size is chosen in each of the villages. Single participants who satisfied the study criteria were included in the study. If more than one eligible respondent is available in the selected house then one respondent is selected using lottery method, To give equal importance to all eligible age groups. If the particular house did not satisfy the criteria, or participants refused to give the consent or if the house was locked for 3 consecutive visits, then the successive house was taken for the study. After institutional ethical committee clearance, the data collection was started. Sampling procedure was given in Supplement Flowchart 1. Each individual was interviewed face to face. The interview was conducted by single interviewer to avoid interobserver bias. A detailed interview of participants was conducted using a pretested validated modified Stepwise questionnaire[5] which included personal information, demographic details, age, gender, smoking, alcohol consumption, diet, educational status, economic status, and family history of any disease. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated for all participants based on their anthropometry and classified according to the Asian classification. <18.5 – underweight, 18.5–22.99 – normal, ≥23-overweight, and ≥25 – obesity.[2]

During the course of the interview, waist circumference was recorded by the trained field staff. Study participants were asked to raise their clothes and the midpoint of the lowest rib and the anterior superior iliac spine was marked. This point was measured using a measuring tape rounded off to the nearest 0.1 cm and noted.[6] For hip circumference, the person was made to stand erect, with both feet placed together and arms by the side. The person who was measuring would sit at the level of the maximum point from the buttocks. He would place the flexible measuring tape around at this point. The measuring tape was placed snugged against the skin. However, should not compress the soft tissue. The measurement was rounded to nearest 0.1 cm and noted as the hip circumference. Waist hip ratio for females >0.85 and males >0.9 is considered as high.[6]

Statistical analysis

Data collected were entered into Microsoft Excel 360 and analyzed using SPSS version 26. The statistical tests such as proportions, univariate and multivariate regression analysis, and chi-square test was used. P value less than 0.05 is considered to be statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This is a cross-sectional study performed in Kalvarayan Hills situated in the Kallakurichi district of Tamil Nadu among 425 adult tribal participants aged 30 years or above.

Age and sex distribution

Majority of the participant 44.7 % [190] in this study belonged to the age group 30-44 years followed by the age group of 60 years and above 28.2 %) [120] and 45-59 years 27.1 % [115]. The age of the study participants varied from 30 to 85 years. About 56.9 % were females and 43.1 % were males. The observation in this study is similar to the study reported by Sathiyanarayanan et al.[7] with 59.8 % were females and 40.2% were males.

Educational status

In this study, majority of the study population was illiterate 56.5 % [240] and 17.4 % [74] perceived their primary school education. Whereas study conducted by Sathiyanarayanan et al.[7] among the tribal population in Jawadhu Hills revealed that 82.2 % were illiterate which shows higher prevalence than our study. In study conducted by Logaraj Muthunarayanan et al.[8] on cardiovascular risk prediction in rural population revealed that 38.1% of illiterates and 18% perceived primary education.

Distribution of socio economic class

Majority of the study population was in lower 79.1 % [336] followed by lower-middle 15.1 % [64], middle class 4.2 % [18], and 1.6% [7] of them were in upper-middle socioeconomic class. These observations are also reported by earlier studies in the literature by Akila Doddamani et al.[9]

Occupational status

With respect to occupation, 65.2% [277] of the study population were unskilled, followed by 24.2% [103] were unemployed/homemaker, 7.5% [32] of them were semi-skilled, 1.7% [7] were skilled, and 1.4% [6] were professional. In study conducted by Logaraj Muthunarayanan et al.[8] revealed that 64.1 % are homemakers, 19.5 % are unskilled, 6.4 % are professional, and 4.7 % are unemployed in rural block of Tamil Nādu.

Abdominal obesity

In the current study, the prevalence of abdominal obesity was 49.6% [211] among the study participants which was similar to a study among Himalayan tribes reported 43.5% [10] and higher when compared with study performed by Priyanka Sajeev et al. [11] revealed 22.1% and Vani Kandpal et al.[12] showed 33.7%. Also the current study prevalence was much lower when compared with study performed by P S Sarma et al.[13] which revealed 60.2%. A study on tribe of Western India [14] reported 22.5% individuals being abdominally obese. On the other hand, a tribe from Eastern India revealed 11% to be affected with central obesity.[15] This variation in the prevalence may be because of participants from different cultural groups and practices among the tribes.

Association between abdominal obesity and diabetes

The association between abdominal obesity and diabetes were found to be statistically significant [χ2 - 7.624, P - 0.006]. As overweight and obese, centrally obese individuals were also at great risk of many systemic and metabolic disorders. In this study, a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus 72.7 % [24] was documented among centrally obese individuals, which is more than three-fold of individuals who were not centrally obese.

The limitation of this study is that it was only a cross-sectional design which might not show temporal relationships and thus the observed associations may not necessarily be causal. The inclusion of adults above the age of 35 years could also affect the overall prevalence. Though dietary practices among tribal population plays a commendable role in diabetes among them, this study could not explore this aspect.

CONCLUSION

The prevalence of abdominal obesity was higher in the study population. As this burden among tribal people is on the increasing trend when compared with other general populations, effective strategies to prevent this must be devised among the tribal population. Abdominal obesity showed a strong association with diabetes. Understanding the health concerns linked to abdominal obesity will require more investigation. This type of obesity is more common in women than males, which puts them at high risk for noncommunicable diseases. To combat the rising trend of abdominal obesity in India, the government and other important stakeholders should proactively consider creating targeted interventions. The Government of India’s Health and Wellness Centers, introduced as part of Ayushman Bharat, are an ideal place to start for implementing wellness initiatives. There is a need to increase awareness about the disease through appropriate information, education, and communication that will help in promoting preventive measures, early diagnosis, and control of these diseases.
