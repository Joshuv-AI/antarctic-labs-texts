Cf-PWV but not cr-PWV or cd-PWV is associated with cardiovascular mortality in patients with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Yan Wang, Hui-xin Liu, Jie-ran Xiong, Zhun Sui, Mi Wang, Li Zuo, Mei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), it is revealed that carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cf-PWV) is related to cardiovascular (CV) mortality, but few studies have investigated carotid-radial PWV (cr-PWV) and carotid-dorsalis pedis PWV (cd-PWV). This study aims at examining the relationship between cf-PWV, cr-PWV, and cd-PWV and CV mortality in CKD patients.MethodsWe enrolled non-dialysis CKD patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m². Baseline arterial stiffness was assessed through office-based measurements of cf-PWV, cr-PWV, and cd-PWV using standardized protocols. Participants were then prospectively followed for all-cause mortality and CV mortality. Cox proportional hazards model and Fine-Gray competing risk model was used to examine the association of cf-PWV, cr-PWV, and cd-PWV with all-cause and CV mortality. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the predicting performance of cf-PWV on CV mortality.ResultsA total of 233 patients were enrolled. Their average cf-PWV, cr-PWV, and cd-PWV were 12.8 ± 3.6 m/s, 9.9 ± 2.0 m/s, and 9.4 ± 3.7 m/s, respectively. During 9.0(4.0, 12.6) years of follow-up, 144 (61.8%) patients died, of which 76 (52.8%) were due to CV death. After adjustment using multivariate Cox regression analysis, only cf-PWV was an independent risk factor for CV mortality [HR 1.198,95% CI (1.097, 1.308)]. The areas under the curve of cf-PWV for 1-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year of CV death were 0.78, 0.76, 0.70, and 0.75, respectively.ConclusionsIn CKD patients, cf-PWV is associated with CV mortality, and its predictive value remains significant even after 10 years. Background

The global prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is approximately 8% to 16% [1] and has become a global public health problem. The mortality rate of CKD patients is significantly higher than that of healthy people, and the leading cause of death is cardiovascular (CV) disease [2]. Studies have shown that patients with CKD stage 3 and 4 have a 2-3-fold increase in CV mortality compared to the general population, while patients with CKD G5 have a 20-fold increase in risk [3].

Arterial stiffness is a predictor of CV events and CV death [4, 5], which can be reflected by measuring pulse wave velocity (PWV). The principle of PWV is to measure the speed at which pulse waves travel from a specific location of the artery along the wall to another specific location, thus reflecting the elasticity of the artery. As arterial elasticity decreases, pulse wave transmission speeds up [6]. Depending on the artery selected, PWV can be obtained from different sites. It is believed that carotid-femoral PWV (cf-PWV) reflects the stiffness of the aorta; carotid-radial PWV (cr-PWV) reflects the stiffness of the subclavian and brachial arteries; and carotid-dorsalis pedis PWV (cd-PWV) reflects the overall stiffness of the elastic arteries and peripheral arteries.

Arterial stiffness, specifically aortic stiffness reflected by cf-PWV, is strongly associated with CV mortality in healthy adults, hypertensive patients, diabetic patients, and dialysis-dependent patients [7–9]. However, fewer studies have been conducted in non-dialysis CKD patients. For example, a study involving patients with CKD stage 4–5 demonstrated that cf-PWV was an independent predictor of cardiovascular events. However, this study included both dialysis and non-dialysis CKD patients [10]. Another study, the Impact of Phosphate Reduction On Vascular End-points in Chronic Kidney Disease (IMPROVE-CKD), which exclusively enrolled patients with CKD stages 3b–4, reported in its baseline data that a PWV ≥ 10 m/s was associated with a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Nevertheless, this evidence remains indirect and limited [11]. In addition, only a few studies have focused on the relationship between cr-PWV, cd-PWV and CV mortality. Therefore, we hypothesize that cf-PWV is an independent predictor of CV mortality in patients with non-dialysis CKD, and that cr-PWV and cd-PWV are also associated with CV mortality in this population. To test this hypothesis, the present study was conducted to evaluate the association between cf-PWV, cr-PWV, cd-PWV and CV mortality in patients with non-dialysis CKD.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Peking University People’s Hospital, enrolling non-dialysis dependent CKD (stages G3-G5) inpatients admitted to the Department of Nephrology from March 2006 through November 2012. Inclusion criteria include: (1) age > 18 years; (2) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2; (3) not on maintenance dialysis; (4) PWV data is available. Exclusion criteria include: (1) uncontrolled malignancy; (2) other causes of life expectancy shorter than 6 months; (3) kidney transplantation planned within 6 months; (4) comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation or aortic valve stenosis that may affect the accuracy of PWV measurements; (5) acute cardiovascular events within 3 months; (6) active phase of autoimmune diseases; (7) active infections requiring systemic therapy within 3 months; (8) major surgeries within 3 months. Patients were followed up until May 2023.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (2019PHB027-01) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500099567; Registration Date: 25 Mar 2025). As this was a retrospective observational study, written informed consents were exempted by the Ethics Committee.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were obtained from medical records, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), primary disease, comorbidities, blood pressure, heart rate, and antihypertensives. Laboratory data included hemoglobin, albumin, eGFR, adjusted serum calcium, phosphate, intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH), triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and 24 h-urine total protein. Estimated glomerular filtration rate was assessed using the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) equation [12].

Measurement of PWV

PWV was measured under standardized office/clinic conditions using an automatic PWV measuring instrument (Complior SP, Artech-Medical, France), a mechano-transducer based device that provides online pulse wave recording and automatic PWV calculation as previously described [13]. The device has been validated against other widely adopted tonometry-based systems such as the SphygmoCor device, showing excellent agreement in measured PWV values [14].

PWV measurement were performed in accordance with the recommendations published by the American Heart Association (AHA) [15] and the manufacturer’s instructions. It is specified as follows: Subjects were rested for 15 min before the examination and then placed in the supine position. Arterial pulse points were identified at: (1) Carotid artery: at the point of maximal pulsation medial to the sternocleidomastoid muscle (right neck), (2) Femoral artery: at the mid-inguinal point (right groin), (3) Radial artery: at the most prominent pulsation over the radial styloid process (right forearm), and (4) Dorsalis pedis artery: at the maximal pulsation point between the malleoli (right foot). The Straight-line distance (D) between the carotid reference point and distal measurement sites were determined using a validated non-elastic tape, with special attention given to maintaining the right arm at 90° abduction during cr-PWV measurements to ensure consistency. All distance values were directly input into the Complior SP system prior to waveform acquisition. The sensors were placed in the sites mentioned above, and technicians would fine-tune the probe to make the waveform clear. The instrument automatically measured the pulse wave conduction time (T) between the two points and calculated PWV. PWV = D/T. Ten PWV values were measured continuously, and the average value was calculated and recorded. All measurements were conducted by three experienced technicians who had undergone comprehensive training and standardized competency assessment in PWV measurement protocols (see Supplementary Table 1).

Outcomes

The primary end point was death due to any cause (all-cause death). The secondary end point was adjudicated CV death, defined as death due to acute myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death, heart failure, stroke, CV procedures, CV hemorrhage, and other CV causes [16].Patients were censored at the time of kidney transplantation, or at the end of the study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were calculated using mean ± SD (normally distributed) or median and interquartile range (IQR) (non-normally distributed). For categorical variables, the frequency and proportion were used.

Missing data were imputed using multivariate imputations by chained equations (MICE), 5 imputed datasets were generated using MICE package in R version 4.4.3. Cox proportional hazard models were used to examine the association of cf-PWV, cr-PWV, and cd-PWV with all-cause death and CV death, respectively. In brief, variables were first examined by the univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, variables with p < 0.1 and those considered clinically significant (sex, dialysis, and type of antihypertensives) were then entered into multivariate Cox regression analysis. Rubin’s rules were applied to pool regression coefficients and variance estimates across imputed datasets. Hazard ratio (HR) and 95%confidence intervals (CI) are reported.

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the predicting performance of cf-PWV on CV mortality, area under the curve (AUC) at critical clinical timepoints (1, 3, 5, and 10 years) were computed, using time ROC package (R software 4.4.3).

Statistical analysis and visualization of data were performed by R software version 4.4.3 (Free Software Foundation), and GraphPad Prism Version 9(GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA), statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 445 participants were initially assessed for eligibility. Of these, 212 were excluded based on predefined criteria, as detailed in Fig. 1. Ultimately, a total of 233 non-dialysis CKD patients were enrolled. Their average age was 57.2 ± 14.5 years, and 49.8% of them were male. Of these, 30 (12.9%) patients were in CKD stage G3a, 24 (10.3%) patients were in CKD stage G3b, 38 (16.3%) were in CKD stage G4, and 141 (60.5%) were in CKD stage G5. The median urinary protein was 2.2 (1.0, 4.5) g/d, and eGFR was 11.5 (6.5, 28.0) [ml/(min∙1.73m2)]. Their mean hemoglobin was 9.8 ± 2.5 g/l, and mean albumin was 37.1 ± 6.3 g/l. The mean values of cf-PWV, cr-PWV, and cd-PWV were 12.8 ± 3.6 m/s, 9.9 ± 2.0 m/s, and 9.4 ± 3.7 m/s, respectively. The baseline characteristics of patients are shown in Table 1.

PWV with CV mortality

In the univariate Cox regression analysis, age, CVD, type 2 diabetes, urinary protein, albumin, cf-PWV, and cr-PWV were associated with CV mortality (Table 3). After adjustment in the multivariate Cox regression model, only cf-PWV, but not cr-PWV or cd-PWV, was an independent risk factor for CV mortality in CKD patients, and the HR and 95% CI was 1.198(1.097, 1.308) (Table 3; Fig. 2B).

The predictive values of cf-PWV on CV mortality

Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of cf-PWV on CV mortality. The areas under the curve (AUC) for 1-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year of CV death were 0.78, 0.76, 0.70, and 0.75, respectively (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this cohort study, we observed that cf-PWV predicted CV death for up to 10 years in CKD patients, and neither cr-PWV nor cd-PWV was associated with all-cause or CV mortality.

The concept of PWV was first proposed by Crighton Bramwell in the early 20th century as a way to understand the relationship between blood pressure and arterial wall elasticity [17]. With advances in technology, noninvasive measurement of cf-PWV has been used as a gold standard for the assessment of arterial stiffness [15]. In the 2023 European Society of Hypertension (ESH) Guidelines for the management of arterial hypertension, cf-PWV is considered the gold standard for measuring large artery stiffness, and cf-PWV >10 m/s is a CV risk factor in patients with hypertension [18]. A meta-analysis examined the global distributions of age- and sex-related arterial stiffness, revealing that PWV is influenced by age, gender, and country. The global age-standardized means for cf-PWV was 7.45 m/s (95% CI: 7.11–7.79 m/s) [19].

In CKD patients, PWV increases at an accelerated rate, and tends to increase significantly with the progression of CKD stages. The main pathogenesis of significant arterial stiffness in CKD patients is oxidative stress and chronic vascular inflammation, leading to impairment of endothelial function and calcium deposition in the arterial wall [20]. It is revealed that patients with CKD stages G1 to 2,3 to 4, and 5 had a mean PWV of 8.4 ± 2.0 m/s, 10.1 ± 2.3 m/s, 11.6 ± 3.3 m/s [21]. In our study, the mean cf-PWV was 12.8 ± 3.6 m/s, and no significant differences in PWV were observed across different stages of CKD. To explore potential reasons, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by CKD stage, with particular focus on factors known to influence PWV. The results suggested that serum calcium may be one of the contributing factors. Previous studies have reported a correlation between elevated serum calcium levels and increased cf-PWV [22]. From a pathophysiological perspective, elevated serum calcium promotes arterial stiffness through several key mechanisms. It directly induces vascular calcification by driving the deposition of calcium phosphate crystals and secondary calciprotein particles in the vascular wall, thereby reducing elasticity [23]. Concurrently, high calcium disrupts vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) function by impairing calcium-sensing receptors [24] and enhancing the mineralization potential of matrix vesicles [25]. Furthermore, chronic hypercalcemia can suppress Klotho expression [26], diminishing its vasoprotective effect and further reducing vascular compliance. Finally, high calcium levels can enhance vascular smooth muscle contractility and induce endothelial dysfunction. These mechanisms collectively increase arterial wall rigidity, thereby elevating PWV. In our cohort, patients with stage 3 CKD exhibited significantly higher serum calcium levels compared to those with more advanced stages. This may partly explain the elevated PWV observed even in the early stages of CKD.

Sex is also a known influencing factor in PWV. Among normotensive volunteers, men aged 50–64 exhibited significantly higher 24-hour PWV compared to age-matched women (P < 0.01) [27]. Similarly, in overweight/obese individuals without prior cardiovascular disease, females demonstrated lower cf-PWV than males, even after adjusting for confounders (P < 0.001) [28]. However, this sex-based discrepancy was not observed in hemodialysis patients [29]. In our study, sex-stratified analysis revealed that male patients had higher cf-PWV (13.5 ± 4.0 m/s) than females (12.1 ± 3.1 m/s; P = 0.003), while no significant differences were detected in cr-PWV or cd-PWV (Supplementary Table 2). Multivariate analyses stratified by sex revealed that higher cf-PWV was significantly associated with CV mortality in the female subgroup (adjusted HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.58; P = 0.003), but this association was not observed in the male subgroup (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Potential explanations for this inconsistency include: (1) methodological differences, as prior HD studies relied on 48-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM)-derived PWV estimates; or (2) the dominant confounding effect of dialysis itself, which may overshadow sex-related influences on arterial stiffness. Ethnic background also influences PWV. Previous research has shown Chinese individuals have lower median cf-PWV (8.9 m/s) compared to Swedish populations (10.1 m/s, P < 0.001) [30]. These differences may result from variations in body size, cardiovascular physiology, and metabolic characteristics between European and Asian groups. In CKD populations without dialysis, the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) study reported an average cf-PWV of 9.69 m/s [31]. Our study found higher values, likely due to the predominance of advanced-stage CKD patients in our cohort and potential ethnic-specific vascular responses to kidney dysfunction.

The relationship between cf-PWV and mortality has been well studied in the general population. It is observed that cf-PWV was a strong predictor of CV mortality, and all-cause mortality. A meta-analysis showed that an increase of 1 m/s in cf-PWV corresponded to an age-, sex-, and risk factor-adjusted increase in risk of 14%, 15%, and 15% for total CV events, CV mortality, and all-cause mortality, respectively [4]. The same phenomenon has also been validated in other populations, such as individuals with diabetes, hypertension, kidney transplant recipients, postmenopausal women, and even dialysis-dependent patients [32–34]. For example, a study observed that in hemodialysis patients, for every 1 m/s increase in the PWV index (measured PWV - theoretical PWV), there is a 34% (crude) and 14% (adjusted) increase in CV mortality and all-cause mortality, respectively [35].

As to non-dialysis CKD patients, A large prospective study, namely the CRIC Study, investigated the relationship between PWV and all-cause mortality through 5.4 ± 1.8 years of follow-up. It is found that cf-PWV was associated with all-cause mortality [36], but the effect of PWV on death was not adjusted. Our study showed a strong correlation between cf-PWV and all-cause mortality when unadjusted, but no association was found after adjusted for confounders. Possible reasons for this include the long follow-up period, where the effect of getting old was too significant to observe the effect of cf-PWV. Another explanation is that cf-PWV cannot accurately predict all-cause death due to the complex pathophysiological changes caused by CKD. More studies are needed to confirm the effect of cf-PWV on all-cause mortality in CKD patients.

As to the relationship between cf-PWV and CV mortality in CKD patients, previous studies mainly focused on dialysis-dependent patients [7, 37, 38]. Most studies on cf-PWV in non-dialysis CKD patients were cross-sectional [39, 40], and there are few studies with CV mortality as the endpoint. Our study showed that cf-PWV was also a strong predictor of CV mortality in non-dialysis CKD patients. The predictive potency of cf-PWV for CV death, as shown by time-dependent ROC curves, remained significant even after 10 years. This indicates that cf-PWV can be an effective predictor of long-term prognosis in CKD patients.

Unlike cf-PWV, there are few studies on the relationship between cr-PWV and cd-PWV and adverse outcomes in patients with CKD. Cr-PWV calculates the travel time of the pulse wave between the carotid artery in the neck and the radial artery in the wrist, making it predominantly influenced by the stiffness of muscular peripheral arteries in the arm. In contrast, cd-PWV measures the pulse wave travel time from the carotid artery to the dorsalis pedis artery on the top of the foot. This pathway is considerably longer, traversing both the central aorta and nearly the entire length of the muscular arteries in the leg. As a result, cd-PWV provides a measure of generalized arterial stiffness. Previous studies revealed that cr-PWV was associated with blood uric acid [41], blood homocysteine [42], copeptin level [43], and brachial and aortic pulse pressure [44]. Another study showed that cd-PWV was negatively correlated with glutathione peroxidase [45], but few studies evaluated the relationship between cr-PWV and cd-PWV and death. Our study observed that cr-PWV was not associated with either all-cause mortality or CV mortality. This may be explained by several factors. First, aortic stiffness directly increases cardiac afterload and damages end organs, while peripheral arterial stiffness has more localized effects and can be partially compensated through vascular remodeling. Second, regional PWVs are significantly influenced by multiple parameters, including individual variations in blood vessel length and multiple reflection effects on pulse waveforms [46]. Third, in CKD patients, competing risks of mortality from causes not primarily related to arterial stiffness—such as infections—may dilute the statistical association observed with peripheral PWVs. Given the additional economic burden and practical inconvenience imposed on patients from multi-site measurements, we propose that the assessment of cf-PWV alone is sufficient for predicting mortality risk in this population.

This study offers several advantages that may contribute to the existing literature. First, it focuses specifically on a non-dialysis CKD population, which remains relatively less explored in terms of arterial stiffness and CV outcomes. Second, the extended follow-up duration of up to 12.6 years offers longitudinal insight into the associations between arterial stiffness parameters and mortality. Furthermore, in addition to the well-established cf-PWV, this study also examined cr-PWV and cd-PWV, offering novel insights into the differential prognostic value of central versus peripheral arterial stiffness in predicting CV mortality in CKD patients.

The study has several limitations. First, while baseline PWV was used to define exposure categories, we did not evaluate the impact of PWV changes during follow-up. Second, as a single-center study involving only hospitalized patients, our cohort predominantly comprised individuals with moderate-to-severe renal impairment (CKD stages 3–5), excluding those with early-stage CKD (G1-2). We assumed that PWV-mortality relationships in CKD G1-2 patients would resemble those in the general population, where this association has been well-established. However, we recognize that the overrepresentation of advanced CKD (stages 4–5) in our cohort likely contributed to the overall elevated PWV values observed. Future investigations including earlier CKD stages would yield valuable insights into arterial stiffness progression across the complete CKD severity spectrum. Third, although we excluded patients with recent acute cardiovascular, infectious, or inflammatory events, the measurements were taken during hospitalizations, which may not fully represent the broader stable CKD population. Fourth, as this was a retrospective study, we did not include inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein or interleukin-6, nor did we collect data on glucose-lowering medications. Their inclusion could have provided deeper insights into the mechanisms linking arterial stiffness and cardiovascular mortality in this population.

In conclusion, this study indicates that in patients with CKD stage G3 to G5, cf-PWV is associated with CV mortality and remains predictive up to 10 years. Cr-PWV and cd-PWV are not associated with either all-cause or CV mortality, and routine monitoring is not recommended.
