Improving the detection and documentation of suspected cauda equina syndrome: a quality improvement project
Authors: Maiar Elhariry, Tahir Khaleeq, Rachna Prem, Kanthan Theivendran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCauda equina syndrome (CES) is a rare but critical neurological emergency. Prompt diagnosis and accurate documentation are essential to avoid permanent neurological impairment. This quality improvement project aimed to improve the accuracy and completeness of neurological examination documentation for patients presenting with suspected CES.MethodsUsing three Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles, a digitally prompted documentation tool ‘#CES’ was developed and implemented in the electronic patient record system. The tool was designed in alignment with the 2019 GIRFT Spine Report and 2023 GIRFT National CES Pathway. It included red flag symptoms and neurological assessment criteria from the ASIA classification. A teaching session on its use was delivered in January before the second data collection period (19 September 2024–20 October 2024), and it was later incorporated into resident doctor induction in December before the third data collection period (5 January 2025–5 February 2025).ResultsIn the initial cycle (May–August 2024), documentation was inconsistent, particularly for sensory (0%) and reflex (4.9%) examination. Following implementation and a teaching session (September 2024), documentation improved in the second cycle (September–October 2024): sensation (81.3%), reflexes (57.1%), though tone and motor documentation declined. In the third cycle (January–February 2025), full template use rose to 80% (from 25%) and documentation further improved: sensation (94.1%), motor (64.7%), reflexes (64.7%), tone (64.7%), vascular (58.8%).ConclusionsA CES-specific, digitally prompted template significantly improved documentation quality. Integration of GIRFT red flags, clinician codesign and repeated education enhanced uptake and accuracy. Further PDSA cycles will focus on sustainability, time-to-diagnosis metrics and addressing clinician workload concerns.

Background

Cauda equina syndrome (CES) can cause irreversible neurological and functional impairment if not diagnosed and treated swiftly, deeming it a medical emergency.1 This can lead to debilitating consequences, including permanent loss of bladder and bowel control, sexual dysfunction and motor weakness.2 Timely diagnosis and surgical intervention are crucial to improve patient outcomes and prevent permanent disability.3 4

The 2019 GIRFT Spine Report and the 2023 GIRFT National Suspected CES Pathway established the UK standard for CES management. They emphasise timely recognition of red flags and standardised clinical evaluation to minimise diagnostic delays. Prior to this project, no standardised proforma for CES documentation existed in our Trust. Neurological findings were entered as unstructured free text, often omitting key indicators.

Complete and accurate documentation of neurological examination performed on patients presenting with suspected CES (usually suspected in patients presenting with symptoms such as incontinence, back pain or saddle anaesthesia) is essential for timely detection and consequent management.5 Nevertheless, poor documentation is a recurring problem, particularly in environments where clinicians face a high patient and workload with limited time pressures, leading to incomplete records.6 7 In such cases of potential CES, inadequate documentation can result in delayed diagnosis and treatment, which increases the risk of permanent neurological impairment.8

Previous efforts addressing issues with CES-related documentation included checklists and educational sessions to increase clinician awareness of red flag symptoms. Educational sessions were effective in increasing knowledge, but implementing this knowledge is challenging, especially in busy settings.9 Alternatively, manual checklists’ efficacy is limited due to clinician resistance and the time taken to complete them.10 11 Moreover, manual entries are prone to human error and need continuous reinforcement through training.11 Prior to this project, no standardised proforma or structured checklist for CES documentation existed within the department. Documentation relied on free-text entries, leading to variability and frequent omissions of key red flags.

This quality improvement project (QIP) assesses the use of an automated digital documentation tool, ‘#CES’, within the electronic patient record (EPR) system, to improve the accuracy and completeness of CES documentation. Like manual checklists, ‘#CES’ serves as an aid-mémoire that guides clinicians through documenting key red flag signs for CES. By automating this, #CES minimises reliance on memory and reduces resultant inconsistencies. This project focuses on marking this shift from manual to digitally prompted documentation using structured templates.12 13

Methods

Measures

To evaluate the efficacy of the ‘#CES’ tool on the accuracy and completeness of examination records in suspected CES cases, both process and outcome measures were selected. Process Measures included the frequency of using #CES (measured as the number and proportion of suspected CES cases documented with the tool) and template completion (percentage of cases where all fields of the #CES templates were filled). These measures also highlight clinician engagement with the template, reflecting the level of adherence to documentation standards. Outcome measures looked at documentation of key neurological findings to evaluate whether clinicians noted CES-specific red flags among other neurological symptoms by ruling them in or out. This is crucial in terms of prioritisation, particularly when identifying patients that may need urgent management and ensuring it is initiated in a timely manner as per the GIRFT CES Pathway. Resultants were validated by reviewing the EPR logs of clerking documentation for suspected CES for consistency and completeness.

Data collection

An initial baseline service evaluation was conducted prior to the implementation of the #CES tool. This was followed by two full PDSA cycles. The initial service evaluation spanned May to August 2024, before the #CES template’s introduction, to assess the documentation standard before implementing the intervention. This focused on the clerking documentation of patients presenting with back pain to the Sandwell and West Birmingham Hospitals NHS Trust. After the #CES tool’s implementation and an initial teaching session on 1 September 2024, follow-up data were collected from 19 September 2024 to 20 October 2024 to track the intervention’s impact, focusing on clinician adherence and documentation of neurological findings. A second PDSA cycle was then conducted from 5 January 2025 to 5 February 25, following the template’s integration into resident doctor induction in December. This cycle aimed to reinforce template use and assess whether structured teaching at induction improved uptake and documentation completeness.

By comparing the results across all cycles, tracking direct template use and extent of completion, the impact of the intervention on documentation standards was evaluated. Variations in documentation among those who fully used the template, partially used it, or did not use it at all continued to serve as key indicators of the template’s efficacy.

Clinician engagement involved a series of focus group discussions, department-wide surveys and iterative feedback sessions. The hospital’s quality improvement (QI) team facilitated structured feedback-guided improvements, ensuring that the template was user-friendly and aligned with clinical workflows.

Design

The core intervention to improve documentation quality for suspected CES was the creation of an automated template, ‘#CES’, in the EPR system. This tool was designed to include all key neurological symptoms and CES red flags, including motor function, power, reflexes (knee, ankle, plantar), muscle wasting and bulk, tone, sensory (light touch and pin prick), perianal examination (perianal sensation and anal tone), vascular exam (capillary refill), pulses (dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial, popliteal, femoral) and bladder scan (prevoid and postvoid). It serves as both a structured prompt and a standardisation tool, reducing reliance on manual recollection and minimising documentation errors. The user types in the prompt ‘#CES’ into the documentation field of the EPR to generate the automated digital template. Design was based on the GIRFT Pathway and ASIA chart. The tool incorporated prompts and warnings for missed fields but did not include auto-fill or AI functions.14 15 The #CES tool also incorporated a checklist of red flag symptoms in line with the GIRFT National CES Pathway. Clinician engagement and education

A structured face-to-face teaching session was delivered on 1 September 2024 to 12 resident doctors rotating into the Trauma and Orthopaedics department. The session lasted approximately 30 min and included a slide presentation covering: (1) the clinical significance of CES, (2) key red flag symptoms as per the GIRFT CES pathway and (3) a live demonstration of how to access and complete the #CES template on the EPR. The session also included time for questions and a hands-on walkthrough of the template in a test environment. Although formal preteaching and post-teaching knowledge assessments were not undertaken, informal feedback was collected verbally and indicated that the session improved confidence in documentation and familiarity with the tool.

A potential issue anticipated was that clinicians might perceive the tool as time-consuming, leading to reluctance in its use. To address this, engagement with clinicians during the development phase was prioritised to ensure the tool was user-friendly and integrated smoothly into existing workflows. As part of the intervention, a targeted teaching session was delivered on 1 September 2024 to familiarise clinicians with the template, highlighting its importance and practical application. Additionally, the template was incorporated into resident doctor induction in December to reinforce engagement and ensure familiarity among incoming clinicians. To minimise confounding, the third data collection cycle (5 January 2025–5 Feberuary 2025) was strategically conducted before registrar and resident doctor turnover to allow a more accurate assessment of the intervention’s impact without the variability introduced by staff changes.

The project team included clinicians, informatics specialists and members of the hospital’s QI team, who worked together to address both technical and clinical aspects of creating and implementing the template and intervention. The informatics team ensured the template is available on the EPR, modified according to outcomes from subsequent PDSA cycles and clinicians’ advice, and ensured the tool’s technical compatibility within the EPR. The hospital’s QI team, alongside clinical supervisors, collaborated with us to schedule and deliver teaching sessions of #CES to increase awareness and encourage using the template. Strategy

Our improvement strategy followed the PDSA methodology, with each cycle focused on refining the documentation process. Documentation was assessed by reviewing EPR entries for suspected CES. Template usage and parameter completion were analysed. Informal feedback was collected on workload and usability.

Baseline: initial service evaluation and intervention

The goal was to assess the baseline accuracy of CES documentation and identify gaps. We hypothesised that inadequate documentation was impacting timely CES diagnosis and planned to assess this. From May to August 2024, we conducted a service evaluation, reviewing all patient records with suspected CES. The evaluation revealed significant gaps in documentation. For instance, only 4.9% of cases documented reflexes and 9.8% documented vascular assessments. Based on these findings, we recognised the need for a standardised documentation approach, which led to the development of the #CES template to ensure that key clinical examination findings were consistently recorded. While the evaluation confirmed documentation gaps, its impact on diagnostic delays was not directly measured. On 1 September 2024, the #CES tool was embedded in the EPR to prompt clinicians to document all essential neurological findings (figure 1).

PDSA cycle 1: implementation and initial use of #CES

After rolling out the #CES tool, we evaluated the documentation improvements from 19 September 2024 to 20 October 2024 using the same outcome measures. Although there was an increase in documentation of reflexes (57.1%) and vascular assessment (14.3%), adherence was incomplete, with only 25% of clinicians fully using the tool. This cycle aimed to increase the automated template’s use by educating resident doctors, the clinicians who tend to document most of the neurological exams conducted on patients with suspected CES, on implementing the #CES automated template for standardising documentation. Moreover, to address partial completion, we engaged the informatics team to enhance the template with prompts for frequently missed signs. This refinement aimed to encourage complete documentation in subsequent cycles.

PDSA cycle 2: reinforcement and reassessment

To assess the intervention’s impact while minimising confounding from staff turnover, a third service evaluation was conducted from 5 January 2025 to 5 February 2025. Findings demonstrated that increased familiarity with the tool led to greater adherence and documentation accuracy. Despite these improvements, template usage remained suboptimal, highlighting the need for continued reinforcement and integration into routine clinical practice. This cycle aimed to further improve clinician adherence and completeness in using the #CES template. Following the initial implementation and teaching session, we identified the need for sustained engagement to address incomplete documentation. To reinforce usage, the #CES template was incorporated into the Resident doctor induction in December 2024, ensuring that new clinicians were familiar with the tool before starting rotations. The same cohort of resident doctors who received the December induction were responsible for documenting suspected CES cases during PDSA Cycle 2 (January–February 2025). This ensured that the impact of the structured training on template use and documentation quality could be meaningfully assessed in this cycle. This was attended by all 12 doctors who will be rotating into our Trauma and Orthopaedic department next. To further promote implementation, the #CES template has become a permanent part of the resident doctor induction. Additionally, outgoing staff are now encouraged to actively demonstrate the tool’s use to incoming doctors during the shadowing period, reinforcing learning through active engagement. This approach aimed to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical implementation, as experiential learning has been shown to improve skill retention and application in clinical settings.13 By embedding hands-on training into the transition process, we aim to promote adherence further and ensure sustained integration into clinical workflows.

Results

Baseline measurements from the service evaluation revealed significant gaps in the documentation of neurological exams when clerking 44 potential CES cases. The baseline data identified 42 patients. 50% of cases had comprehensive documentation of myotome lower limb motor function, 4.9% cases documented reflexes and 9.8% documented vascular assessments. Documentation of both light touch and pinprick sensations was absent from all records, but all records had a mention of either touch or pinprick sensation. On the other hand, documentation of prevoid and postvoid bladder scans had higher compliance, as it was documented in 95% of cases. Results from this cycle highlighted the problem of incomplete documentation, supporting the need for an intervention aimed at improving CES-related documentation practices.

After developing the #CES template, assessing the documentation in 16 suspected CES cases during PDSA 1 revealed that the documentation of previously frequently missed metrics improved significantly. These improvements coincided with the formal education session delivered on 1 September 2024, which provided resident doctors with practical training on CES documentation and use of the #CES tool. The structured teaching likely contributed to the increased uptake and completeness observed in this cycle. This included the increased documentation of sensation and muscle wasting to 81.3% and 56.3% after not being previously documented. Documentation of reflexes increased to 57.1% (previously 4.9%), and vascular assessment to 14.3% (previously 9.8%). However, some of the key gaps in neurological examination did not increase as expected, with some metrics being reported less frequently than they were before the implementation. Documentation of tone decreased to 57.1% (previously 75.6%), and motor function to 28.6% (previously 48.8%). 53.3% of the documentations used the template, and 25% of them used the template fully. Notable improvements were linked to documentation in which clinicians fully utilised the #CES tool. These documents included all key signs and symptoms, both positives and negatives, confirming the tool’s potential as a reliable prompt for comprehensive documentation. Nevertheless, 75% of cases did not use or partially complete the template.

Following the induction teaching in December 2024, a second service evaluation from 5 january 2025 to 5 February 25 assessed the documentation in 17 suspected CES cases. Findings from the second PDSA cycle demonstrated further improvements. Template usage increased to 59% (previously in PDSA 1: 53.3%), and among those who used it, full completion rose to 80% (previously in PDSA 1: 25%). Overall documentation rates also improved (table 1): reflexes (65%), sensory (94%), motor myotomes (65%), muscle wasting (47%), tone (65%), vascular exam (59%) and bladder scan (94.2%). Missing data were primarily associated with partial or non-use of the template rather than tool limitations, reinforcing that full and consistent engagement is essential to maximise its effectiveness. Inconsistent and partial engagement continues to act as challenge and highlights the role of contextual factors (ie,time constraints in busy settings and tool familiarity). This variability illustrates that while the tool facilitated standardised and complete documentation when employed fully and effectively, a lack of consistent or complete utilisation by clinicians limited its overall impact. Nevertheless, the increase in its usage and completion reflects the potential for better adherence to template usage. We monitored data completeness and accuracy by looking at which fields within the #CES template were filled to the expected standard. Missing documented data were associated with partial or lack of template usage rather than tool limitations, suggesting that the tool itself was effective in meeting its aim but required consistent engagement to maximise its effectiveness.

There were several unintended outcomes, positive and negative, on implementing the #CES template. Improvement was most notable in the documentation of red flag signs and sensory findings. Partial or non-use of the template was associated with poorer documentation, and the tool provided an embedded checklist serving as a learning aid that augmented memory. However, feedback from clinicians revealed an increased workload due to templates, particularly in terms of documenting negative findings that are not considered red flags. The variation in outcomes and perceptions of the tool accessibility and ease highlights the need for future cycles that address these workflow integration issues. No additional costs were incurred preimplementation, during or post implementation. Nevertheless, future cycles will directly assess time, that is, time to MRI and surgical intervention to quantify clinical impact implications and cost implications, that is, in terms of cost saved by more timely diagnosis. These findings suggest that while the tool supports more thorough documentation, further refinement and adaptation to ensure sustainability and implementation in busy and high patient load settings is required.

Discussion

This project demonstrated the importance of utilising and refining structured documentation tools like the #CES template in standardising care. As template usage and completion rates improved over the PDSA cycles, documentation of key CES findings also improved across most parameters, indicating that engagement with the tool directly correlates with better documentation. It emphasised that introducing the template is only effective if fully used to ensure it fulfils its aim of promoting thorough documentation of key CES symptoms. Nevertheless, the inconsistent use of the tool by clinicians was a major limitation, indicating that adherence remains a challenge. Time pressures in emergency or busy settings and the need for clinicians to adapt quickly to new processes were likely contributors to these adherence issues.

The project’s strength lies in its use of an electronic documentation tool embedded in the EPR. While the #CES template provides structured prompts to guide clinicians through neurological examinations, it is not a fully automated system. Human error remains a factor, as incomplete or inaccurate entries can still occur. Engaging the informatics team early helped streamline the tool’s integration and maintain clinician access across shifts, reinforcing its potential for sustainability and adaptability to feedback and observed results from prior PDSA cycles. Furthermore, the #CES template has now become a permanent part of the resident doctor induction, ensuring that all new doctors receive structured training on its use. Outgoing staff are also encouraged to actively demonstrate the tool’s use to incoming doctors during the shadowing period, reinforcing learning through active engagement. Further modifications, such as shortening the tool or enhancing visual cues for frequently missed signs and symptoms, could support clinicians in completing all critical sections more effectively.

With the advent of artificial intelligence (AI), ambient voice-to-text (Scribe) technology could assist in filling out the #CES template within the EPR, reducing the need for manual typing.16 17 Clinicians could review and verify the AI-generated documentation before saving it, improving efficiency and reducing workload and burnout.17 Further innovation in clinical documentation could involve integrating ambient AI, such as natural language processing, into a structured open standards clinical data store like openEHR.1820 This would enable semantic interoperability (so that different medical software can understand healthcare data and talk to each other) across different EPRs, facilitating data analysis and seamless information sharing between departments and healthcare organisations.

Currently, the EPR system relies heavily on free-text entry, which limits its compatibility with other EPR platforms. This lack of standardisation can lead to inaccurate or incomplete information being transferred to the referring neurosurgical team, which may use a different system.16 18 By using openEHR, we can develop structured templates similar to #CES, ensuring that documentation is easily shared and interpreted across various healthcare systems without requiring manual copying and pasting between different electronic referral systems, leading to poor interoperability and patient safety issues.21 This shift towards standardised interoperable digital documentation using the openEHR standard would enhance data accuracy, streamline referrals and improve patient safety.22

Limitations

While delivering the implementation, several practical issues limited the tool’s efficacy assessment. These include the limited sample size and short timeframe, which may have restricted the project’s ability to yield robust, generalisable data. Additional PDSA cycles with larger sample sizes and longer time frames would help further assess its impact, mitigate biases and support continuous improvement for long-term sustainability. Moreover, factors such as clinician turnover, varied familiarity with the EPR and fluctuating patient volumes in the emergency setting introduced potential biases and confounding factors. To minimise such issues, future PDSA cycles could involve continuous practical education sessions focusing on implementation aimed at clinicians who usually document the examination of patients presenting with potential CES. Moreover, regular feedback to reinforce template use and gather clinician input could be helpful to better tailor the tool to clinicians’ preferences and hopefully increase their preference in using the tool in documentation. This could also be in the form of interviews with the project team, where clinicians are invited to give their input to help refine the tool and encourage its full use.

While the #CES tool significantly improved the documentation of neurological findings, a key limitation is that it does not fully distinguish between general neurological deficits (American Spinal Injury Association/ International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Canal Injury, ASIA/ISNCSCI) and specific red flag features for CES, as outlined in the GIRFT interactive CES pathway. This distinction is critical in clinical prioritisation and decision-making, particularly for red flags requiring urgent escalation, such as saddle anaesthesia, bilateral leg symptoms and bladder dysfunction. Although these features are included in the template, they are not currently highlighted or prioritised within the user interface. Future PDSA cycles should consider enhancing the template to prompt for GIRFT-defined red flags more explicitly, for example, using visual cues or priority labels, to improve recognition and escalation pathways.

Additionally, while this project effectively demonstrated improvements in documentation (a valuable process outcome), direct clinical outcome measures, such as time to MRI, time to neurosurgical review or time to theatre, were not evaluated. As such, although improved documentation may support better clinical care, this assumption remains unvalidated in this cycle. Future work will seek to correlate template usage with these outcome-based metrics to assess the tool’s true impact on patient care and service efficiency.

Next steps

Future iterations of the #CES tool may benefit from more explicit integration of GIRFT-defined red flags to distinguish features that require immediate escalation. For instance, incorporating visual cues, colour-coded alerts or time-sensitive prompts (eg, ‘MRI within 24 hours recommended’) could improve diagnostic prioritisation and reduce delays. In addition, to better demonstrate clinical benefit beyond documentation quality, subsequent PDSA cycles will aim to incorporate outcome-based metrics such as time to MRI, time from clerking to definitive management and appropriateness of neurosurgical referral. These will provide a clearer picture of how the tool impacts patient safety, diagnostic efficiency and care quality, thereby aligning the intervention with both national recommendations and measurable clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

This early-stage QIP demonstrates the effectiveness of a digitally prompted documentation tool in improving neurological assessment for suspected CES. We believe that alignment with the GIRFT National Pathway and incorporation of clinician feedback contributed to its success. While previous efforts like checklists and educational sessions have been helpful, this project uniquely highlights the effectiveness of an automated template as a documentation tool or augmentation. The goal of improving documentation accuracy was mostly achieved, with significant improvements in key neurological parameters. The third PDSA cycle demonstrated that structured induction training further enhanced template use and completion rates. However, inconsistent adherence to the #CES tool remained a challenge, reinforcing the need for ongoing engagement and reinforcement strategies.

While the project’s core aims remained unchanged, implementation was refined through collaboration with the informatics team, leading to better integration of the tool. The automation of the #CES template has the potential to reduce delayed diagnoses and associated risks, though further analysis is required to quantify this impact. The metrics used effectively assessed documentation quality, but additional balancing measures, such as clinician time burden, could enhance evaluation.

The #CES tool is now a permanent component of resident doctor induction, ensuring sustained use. Additionally, outgoing staff are encouraged to demonstrate its application to incoming doctors during shadowing periods, reinforcing active engagement. Its accessibility within the EPR system supports widespread adoption across different clinical settings. Further PDSA cycles are planned to monitor sustainability, measure clinical impact (eg, time to MRI), and explore refinement using user feedback. With further studies, this model has the potential to improve documentation consistency across a broader range of clinical presentations.
