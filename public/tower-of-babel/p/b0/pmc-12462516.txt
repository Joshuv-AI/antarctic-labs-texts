Chirality-driven spin dynamics and manipulation in all-inorganic chiral heterostructures
Authors: Hanyu Liu, Min Ouyang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The interplay between structural chirality and spin represents an emerging field with profound implications across multiple disciplines. As compared with organic chiral molecules and their organic-inorganic hybrids, all-inorganic chiral platforms remain underexplored, despite their potential as robust, tunable solid-state systems for the exotic chirality-entangled physics. Here we investigate chirality-driven light-matter-spin couplings in all-inorganic chiral heterostructures, comprising nanoscale chiral metal plasmonic cores and semiconductor quantum shells, without relying on extrinsic coupling to chiral molecules. We demonstrate that the structural handedness of the chiral Au core imprints chirality onto the CdS quantum shell, inducing significant spin polarization in the semiconductor. Moreover, observation of ultrafast dynamic chirality emerging in achiral CdS shells, driven by optical excitation of near-field chiral plasmons, suggests a transient chirality-driven effective magnetic field and provides a novel mechanism for ultrafast coherent spin manipulations. Our findings lay the groundwork for advancing solid-state chiral photonics and chiral spintronics, unraveling the potential of chirality-driven light-matter-spin interactions in quantum engineering.

Introduction

Chirality in matter introduces an asymmetric degree of freedom that fundamentally influences light-matter interactions, offering unique opportunities for coherent control of photonics and spintronic phenomena under new physical principles. The synergy between light, structural chirality and spin arises from the intrinsic asymmetry of chiral systems, which modulates inherent light-matter interactions, spin-orbit coupling, and quantum scattering potential1–4. These interactions hold profound implications for photonics, quantum technology, and the study of fundamental physical phenomena. While chirality has been extensively explored in biological and organic realms5–12, the study of chirality, specifically chirality-spin interplays, in purely inorganic systems remains at a nascent stage. Inorganic systems offer robust and tunable solid-state platforms that could enable deeper insights into chirality-driven spin dynamics and novel chiral photonic technologies. However, achieving well-defined all-inorganic chiral structures and probing their chirality-spin interactions have been significant challenges. To date, evidence of chirality-induced spin polarized states has been demonstrated primarily in organic-inorganic hybrid systems, where chiral organic molecules interface with inorganic semiconductors, oxide or perovskites13–24. A limited number of studies have also shown similar effects in pure inorganic chiral crystals under charge current injection, although this requires homochiral crystal structures25–28. However, the vast majority of pure inorganic solids adopt achiral space groups, and their organic-inorganic hybrids often suffer from interfacial complexities and coupling ambiguities, limiting precise control and interpretation. Recently, a new class of inorganic chiral metal nanostructures has emerged, spanning different length scales and shapes through enantioselective synthesis29–31. Unlike other inorganic chiral nanostructures that exhibit extrinsic chiroptical response through interfacial coupling with external chiral molecules, the handedness of intrinsically chiral metal nanostructures arises from morphological features with controlled exposure of chiral high-Miller index facets. This structural chirality is not constrained by crystallographic chiral space groups, enabling flexible design of chiral functionality even in materials with inherently achiral lattices and thus offering broad opportunities for tailored chiral light-matter interactions. Moreover, the resulting inorganic chirality has been demonstrated to be highly robust, making these nanostructures ideal building blocks for creating more complex chiral hetero-nanostructures through integration with other functional inorganic materials, such as core-shell metal-semiconductor heterostructures29. These provide a unique platform to explore optically controllable chirality-dependent phenomena in solid states. Notably, metal nanostructures like Au can generate highly localized intensive plasmonic fields under optical excitations that can be coupled to other fundamental entities like excitons, potentially realizing chiral light-matter interactions with emerging technology applications32–36.

We report the observation of chirality-induced spin dynamics in all-inorganic chiral heterostructures, comprising intrinsically chiral metal cores and semiconductor quantum shells, without relying on chiral molecules or crystallographic chiral space groups in the crystal lattice. Utilizing multi-color femtosecond time-resolved all-optical techniques that can circumvent challenges of electrical contact resistance and mismatch in a spin transport measurement34, we unambiguously identified a direct relationship between the observed spin polarization in nanoscale semiconductors and corresponding structural chirality, attributable to intrinsic chiral morphology. Furthermore, a new effect of dynamic chirality in nanoscale achiral semiconductor can be induced by ultrafast optical excitation of near-field chiral plasmons, offering a viable mechanism to dress quantum spin states in semiconductors, analogous to the application of a transient magnetic field with potential for innovative chirality-driven quantum control. A series of chirality-driven ultrafast coherent spin manipulations in semiconductors has been achieved.

Chirality-driven spin polarization at the absence of external magnetic field

We further compared spin excitations by using circularly polarized pumps at the same photon energy while maintaining the same probe pulse conditions. Results for (L-Au)-CdS and (D-Au)-CdS samples are presented in Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10, respectively. Both left- and right-circularly polarized pump pulses induce spin polarization in opposite directions, manifested as a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}π-phase difference in their TRFR traces. However, their TRFR amplitudes show significant differences. For the (L-Au)-CdS sample, the left-circularly polarized pump results in almost six times larger spin polarization than the right-circularly polarized pump. Interestingly, we observed the opposite tendency for the (D-Au)-CdS sample, where the right-circularly polarized pump pulses favor spin polarization. The clear and consistent dependence of spin dynamics in chiral hetero-nanostructures on their structural handedness suggests that structural chirality plays a dominant role in governing spin dynamics and can be employed to polarize spin, as shown in Figs. 1c and 2a.

We investigated the roles of QS thickness in chirality-driven spin dynamics through TRFR measurements using linearly polarized pump excitations on three control samples. Each sample featured the same chiral L-Au NC core but varied in CdS QS thickness: 3.8 nm, 6.9 nm, and 12.1 nm, with results summarized in Fig. 2b. All three samples consistently exhibit spin polarization with the same orientation under linearly polarized pump pulse excitation. However, a clear dependence on QS thickness emerges in two key aspects. This monotonic variation can be attributed to the variation of quantum confinement effect determined by the QS thickness44. Second, the normalized TRFR amplitude varies significantly with thickness, with the 3.8 nm CdS QSs showing an amplitude almost seven times larger than that from the 12.1 nm thick QSs. The significantly larger chirality-driven spin polarization effect observed in thinner CdS QS samples implies a pronounced role of the Au-CdS interface in the underlying mechanism.

Role of chiral interface in chirality-driven spin polarization

Our TRFR experiments with chiral Au-CdS hetero-nanostructures have clearly demonstrated a relationship between structural chirality and inherent spin dynamics. Several mechanisms could potentially contribute to our observations. For example, residual chiral cysteine molecules, which are used to induce and control the chirality of Au NC cores, could potentially remain in samples and influence intrinsic magnetic properties14,49–51. Several control experiments have been conducted to evaluate the possible roles of these molecules in our chirality and spin measurements: First, we performed step-by-step Raman spectroscopy at different stages of sample preparation, focusing on the characteristic vibrational features of cysteine molecules52,53. The results, presented in Supplementary Fig. 11, reveal that cysteine molecules are detectable on the surface of L-Au NCs prior to the sequential growth of Ag and CdS shells. However, their presence becomes negligible (if not entirely eliminated) after Ag and CdS shell overgrowth, as indicated by the substantial reduction in Raman peak intensities; Second, we compared the CD and absorption spectra of sequentially synthesized L-Au, (L-Au)-Ag and (L-Au)-CdS samples (Supplementary Figs. 12a and 12b). A key observation is the disappearance of CD signal in the (L-Au)-Ag sample, despite the presence of a strong plasmonic absorption peak, followed by the reappearance of a strong CD signal in the final (L-Au)-CdS structure. If the observed chirality were caused by residual L-cysteine molecules, consistent CD features would be expected across all samples. The loss of CD after Ag overgrowth is attributed instead to the electron delocalization in the Au-Ag system and the absence of chiral surface morphology, in agreement with previous report29. Importantly, this evolution of CD and absorption spectra is well reproduced by FEM simulation based on mechanism of chiral morphology and interfacial effects, without invoking chiral molecules (Supplementary Figs. 12c and 12d); Third, we highlight an important detail regarding preparation of achiral Au-CdS control sample for Supplementary Fig. 8 (also see Methods). Specifically, while chiral cysteine molecules were not used during the synthesis of achiral Au NCs, we deliberately introduced L-cysteine molecules after achiral Au NC formation but before Ag shell overgrowth under otherwise identical conditions to their chiral counterparts. This control directly tests whether any remaining chiral molecules, even if not completely absent, could contribute to spin polarization. Despite the intentional presence of L-cysteines, this control sample exhibited no detectable spin polarization in TRFR measurements. Taken together with the spectral and structural characterizations, this result allows us to safely exclude any significant contribution from chiral cysteine molecules to the observed spin polarization in our chiral Au-CdS heterostructures.

Notably, our chiral Au-CdS core-shell hetero-nanostructures are grown through a non-epitaxial growth mechanism, allowing the formation of monocrystalline semiconductor layers on intimately contacted metal surfaces without restrictions from interfacial lattice mismatch and uneven morphology37,38. This immediately suggests that the inner surface morphology of CdS QSs should precisely follow the chiral morphology of the Au NC core, as evidenced by TEM characterizations in Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 5. We evaluated theoretically the effect of morphology of CdS QSs in a chiral hetero-nanostructure by removing its chiral Au core, and computed the corresponding chiroptical responses of pure CdS QSs using FEM simulation (blue and red curves in Supplementary Fig. 13). Our results show that the inner surface morphology of CdS QSs manifests chiral signatures, resulting in a non-zero CD band coinciding with the exciton energy of the CdS QSs. CdS QSs with inner surface morphology imprinted by L- and D-Au NCs possesses opposite signs of CD peaks. The computed CD magnitude of pure CdS QS is very small compared to the plasmonic CD band (Supplementary Fig. 4), making its contribution to the total CD spectra of chiral hetero-nanostructures negligible. Nevertheless, the emerging CD from the existence of chiral geometric characteristics on the inner surface of CdS QSs suggests potential modification of its electronic and spin properties due to large spin-orbit coupling in the inorganic semiconductors.

To experimentally verify the effect of the chiral interface, we developed a new type of chiral (L/D-Au)-Ag2S-CdS hetero-nanostructures with two consecutive shells, Ag2S and CdS (Supplementary Fig. 1). The Ag2S shell acts as a structural buffer layer, preventing direct interfacial contact of CdS QS with the chiral Au NC core during non-epitaxial growth process (Supplementary Fig. 14). This results in achiral inner and outer surfaces of the CdS QSs in the hetero-nanostructures, and the corresponding FEM simulation of its isolated CdS QS shows null CD (green curve in Supplementary Fig. 13). We studied the spin dynamics of CdS QSs in both (L-Au)-Ag2S-CdS and (D-Au)-Ag2S-CdS samples, which possess the same 7.5 nm Ag2S and 3.1 nm CdS QSs but opposite handedness of the chiral Au NC core, under various pump excitation conditions (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). By comparing the results with their (L-Au)-CdS and (D-Au)-CdS counterparts, we found that both (L-Au)-Ag2S-CdS and (D-Au)-Ag2S-CdS samples showed no net spin polarization under linearly polarized pump excitation. Only circularly polarized pumps could induce spin polarization in both samples, and the left- and right-circularly polarized pump pulses resulted in nearly equal spin polarization amplitude but opposite orientations, consistent with conventional achiral II-VI semiconductors but different from Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10. This stark contrast to the (L/D-Au)-CdS samples underscores the crucial role of the chiral interface in driving spin polarization in inorganic nanostructures. For example, the imprinted chiral inner morphology of CdS QSs might create a chiral quantum scattering potential favoring a specific spin state54,55. Additionally, the spin-orbit coupling of CdS QSs can also be modified, thus lifting the degeneracy of spin levels to induce spin polarization3,12,56. More in-depth theoretical studies are necessary to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Observation of ultrafast dynamic CD in semiconductor induced by chiral plasmons

Figures 1 and 2 experimentally demonstrate that chiral morphology in inorganic semiconductor CdS QSs can generate net spin polarization without the need for an external magnetic field. This spin polarization arises from the morphological chirality imprinted onto the CdS QS through intimate contact with a chiral Au NC core in a core-shell configuration. From the perspective of the chiral Au NC core, it furthermore supports strong localized surface plasmon resonance (Supplementary Fig. 12), which significantly enhances and spatially confines the optical near-field around its surface. Our FEM simulations reveal that the chiral morphology of the Au NC governs the spatial distribution of this near-field, giving rise to non-zero optical chirality density and chiral electric field pattern under linearly polarized resonant excitation (Supplementary Fig. 17). These findings present a unique opportunity to explore chiral light-matter interactions, where the plasmonic modes from the metal and the excitonic states from the semiconductor coexist and potentially couple, and where chirality introduces an additional degree of freedom for tuning these interactions, including handedness-dependent excitonic coupling57.

We chose chiral (L/D-Au)-Ag2S-CdS hetero-nanostructures as a testbed for investigating new chiral plasmon-exciton coupling, based on several considerations. First, the addition of an intermediate Ag2S shell serves as a dielectric spacer that effectively mitigates direct morphological imprinting from the chiral Au NC core onto the inner surface of the CdS QS, rendering the CdS QS structurally achiral with null static CD (Supplementary Fig. 13), a finding corroborated by the spin dynamic measurements shown in Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16; Second, while the presence of these shells alters the local dielectric environment and red-shift the plasmon resonance energy (Supplementary Fig. 12), the intensive chiral plasmonic near-field generated from the Au NC core (Supplementary Fig. 17) enables handedness-dependent coupling to the adjacent, structurally achiral semiconductor CdS QS; Third, the spectral proximity, but not complete overlap, between the plasmonic resonance of the Au NC and the excitonic transition of the CdS QS allows independent optical control over chiral plasmons and semiconductor excitons, while still facilitating strong coherent coupling. This is particularly advantageous for probing coherent light-matter coupling phenomena, such as the resonantly enhanced (a.c.) optical Stark effect (ac-OSE) previously demonstrated in judiciously engineered core-shell hybrid metal-semiconductor nanostructures34,58. In such hybrid systems, coherent coupling between a discrete interband excitonic state in the semiconductor and a surface plasmon having a continuous spectrum in the metal can result in hybrid exciton state with sizable optical Stark shift32. Moreover, when driven by circularly polarized sub-resonant excitation, such coupling has been shown in prior studies to induce net spin splitting of carriers due to the helicity of optical field34. Building upon these insights, our chiral (L/D-Au)-Ag2S-CdS hetero-nanostructures present a compelling platform to explore the role of chiral plasmons in modulating plasmon-exciton interactions. Our simulations of the chiral near-field distribution (Supplementary Fig. 17) suggest a previously unexplored pathway for achieving momentum-dependent ac-OSE mediated by the chiral plasmonic field. To explore this potential, we conducted an experiment to evaluate the dynamic CD of achiral CdS QSs induced by the proximate chiral plasmonic fields emergent from their neighboring Au NCs. In this experiment, we tuned the energy of a linearly polarized pump pulse between 2.06 eV and 2.40 eV, encompassing the chiral plasmonic regime, while independently varying the pump-probe time delay between −2.0 ps and 2.0 ps. The dynamic CD of CdS QSs, defined as the transient difference between the left- and right-circularly polarized probe beams under ultrafast pump excitation, was monitored by using a photoelastic modulator at a fixed probe energy of 2.59 eV that match electron transition in the CdS QSs. Both (L-Au)-Ag2S-CdS and (D-Au)-Ag2S-CdS samples were investigated under identical pump-probe conditions for comparison, with results presented in Fig. 3b.

For both samples, our findings reveal that dynamic CD of CdS QSs depends intricately on both time delay and pump energy. Notably, a pronounced dynamic CD peak emerged when pump and probe pulses coincided temporally, and the pump energy matched the chiral plasmonic resonance, albeit with opposite signs for the two samples. Several important implications can be drawn from Fig. 3b. First, this new effect is pump-induced, originating from ultrafast chiral plasmonic coupling. The amplitude of dynamic CD varies with pump pulse intensity, with the sign dependent on the handedness of chiral plasmons (Supplementary Fig. 18); Second, since the chiral plasmonic energy is lower than the excitonic energy of CdS QSs, the pump pulse cannot directly excite CdS QSs. Consequently, the underlying chiral plasmon-exciton coupling governing the dynamic CD of CdS QSs is a virtual process rather than charge carrier-induced effects; Third, the opposite sign of dynamic CD from these two samples under linearly polarized pump pulse indicates plasmonic handedness dependence; Last but not least, the emergence of ultrafast dynamic CD in achiral semiconductor CdS QSs strongly suggests that time-dependent chiral plasmonic field can perturb exciton spin states, inducing spin splitting akin to the effect of an external ultrafast magnetic field. The orientation of this chiral plasmons-driven effective magnetic field (denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\boldsymbol{H}}}}}_{{{{\boldsymbol{chiral}}}}}$$\end{document}Hchiral) aligns with the incident laser direction and is uniquely defined by the handedness of chiral plasmons, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 3a. This phenomenon can be understood within a framework analogous to the ac-OSE. In conventional OSE processes, when an optical field carries angular momentum, such as circular polarization, it can break the degeneracy of excitonic spin states, resulting in helicity-dependent spin splitting34,39. In our current system, although the CdS QSs are intrinsically achiral, they are coupled to chiral Au NC cores that exhibit strong localized surface plasmon resonances with non-zero optical chirality density under linearly polarized excitation (Supplementary Fig. 17). These plasmonic near-fields act as effective ultrafast chiral perturbations, analogous to circularly polarized sub-resonant light but confined and structured by the local morphology. When coupled to the adjacent excitonic states in the CdS QSs, the chiral plasmonic field induces a momentum-dependent, handedness-specific OSE shift that lifts the spin degeneracy of the excitons, resulting in oppositely shifted energy levels for different spin states. The resulting spin-split states further leads to differential absorption for left- and right- circularly polarized light, manifesting as dynamic CD revealed in Fig. 3b. This real-time spin splitting enacted by chiral plasmon-driven OSE is further supported by the observed amplitude dependence of the dynamic CD signal on the excitation power of chiral plasmons, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, consistent with the intensity scaling of the OSE. Notably, the sign of the dynamic CD reverses with the handedness of the chiral Au NCs, providing compelling evidence that the chirality of the plasmonic near-field governs the direction of spin splitting. It is important to highlight that the emergence of ultrafast dynamic CD in an achiral CdS QS hinges on breaking both spatial inversion symmetry (parity) and time-reversal symmetry59. This contrasts with chiral morphology induced spin polarization in Figs. 1 and 2, which arises from a static geometric property and preserves time-reversal symmetry, making it a parity odd but time-reversal even effect. The chiral plasmonic field imposes spatial asymmetry (parity odd), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, while the handedness-dependent time-varying Stark perturbation breaks time-reversal symmetry (time-reversal odd). This combination of parity odd and time-reversal odd effects enables a transient, handedness-selective optical response in achiral excitonic system that is otherwise symmetric under equilibrium conditions. This emergent effect is not rooted in intrinsic magnetic properties or external magnetic field, but instead arises solely from the engineered interactions between chiral plasmonic fields and excitonic states, underscoring its fundamentally electronic and photonic origin. The significance of our experimental finding of dynamic CD indicates that ultrafast chiral plasmons can be utilized to dress quantum states of nearby semiconductor, potentially being employed for chiral quantum engineering.

Discussion

Our study provides a unique testbed for exploring the rich and emerging field of chirality-driven light-matter-spin interactions. Compared to organic chiral molecules or hybrid organic-inorganic chiral structures, all-inorganic chiral structures beyond conventional chiral crystals can provide superior structural robustness, design flexibility, and precisely tailorable optical and quantum properties. In particular, chiral plasmonic metal-semiconductor hetero-nanostructures exhibit significantly larger absorption cross-sections and dissymmetry factors, along with broad spectral tunability. These features enable strong and tunable light-matter interactions across diverse chemical environments, opening pathways to a range of applications. Moreover, we have provided unambiguous experimental evidence demonstrating that chiral morphology can give rise to spin polarization in inorganic semiconductors. This finding opens exciting avenues to engineer structural configuration and to fine-tune the chiral morphology (and thus dissymmetric factor) to control the degree of spin polarization in future studies. Particularly, in the current work we have focused on the core-shell hetero-nanostructures where the QS represents a quasi- two-dimensional system with one-dimensional confinement. Alternatively, the non-centrosymmetric architectures combining different three-dimensionally confined colloidal quantum dots would provide another platform for future exploration of tunable bandgap energy, spin-orbit coupling and Landé g-factors38. These inorganic systems inherently possess larger spin-orbit coupling than their organic counterparts, creating fertile ground for advancing our understanding and optical control of spin dynamics in chiral environments61,62. Our demonstration of dynamic chirality in semiconductors and its role in coherent spin manipulation underscores the potential of near-field chiral plasmons as a powerful platform for spin-based quantum technologies, where precise spin control is vital for quantum information processing and communication. The implications of this finding are far-reaching, with structurally chiral materials presenting novel strategies for designing quantum devices and systems with tailored quantum spin properties63. Moreover, the concept of leveraging periodic optical excitation of chiral plasmons to generate effective ultrafast magnetic fields introduces an exciting paradigm for chiral Floquet engineering64, unlocking new possibilities in quantum control with chiral photonic and plasmonic devices. Looking ahead, the integration of all-inorganic chiral nanostructures with other quantum materials offers a promising strategy to create hybrid systems with unprecedented light-spin interplay, setting the stage for transformative advancements in quantum information science and a new era of chiral photonics and quantum technologies65.

Synthesis of chiral Au NCs

The synthesis of chiral Au NCs and the corresponding notation of their handedness follows our previous work29. The Au seed NCs were synthesized by a revised seed-mediated method66. Typically, 1 ml of Au seed NCs solution was added into 3 ml of 80 mM CTAC solution. A. 30 ~ 40 µl of 70 µM L-cysteine (or D-) solution was added and the mixed solution was kept stirring for 30 mins to have the cysteine molecules adsorbed on the surface of Au NCs. The mixed solution was centrifuged at 15,583 × g for 10 mins and the supernatant was discarded to remove the extra cysteine molecules in solution. The centrifugation process was repeated twice. The precipitate was then redispersed into 1 ml of DI water, followed by the addition of 3 ml of 80 mM CTAC solution and 200 µl of 0.1 M ascorbic acid under stirring. 500 µl of 1 mM HAuCl4 solution was slowly injected into the mixture solution by syringe pump for 30 mins. Chiral Au NCs were washed out by centrifugation and were redispersed into 4 ml DI water. Typical quality of chiral Au NCs was shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Synthesis of chiral Au–Ag core-shell nanostructures

Typically, 1 ml of chiral Au NCs were added into 8.1 ml DI water and 1 ml of 0.2 M CTAC solution. The mixture was put into a 60 °C oil bath. Typically, 500 µl of 2 mM AgNO3 and 500 µl of 50 mM AA were added drop-by-drop simultaneously into the mixture with active stirring. For thickness control of the Ag shell, 250 µl of 2 mM AgNO3 and 250 µl of 50 mM AA were used for thinner shell and 750 µl of 2 mM AgNO3 and 750 µl of 50 mM AA were used for thicker shell. The reaction was left for 3 hrs in the 60 °C oil bath. Finally, the chiral Au-Ag core-shell nanostructures were collected by centrifugation and redispersion into 4 ml DI water.

Preparation of S precursor

128 mg S powder was added into 10 ml oleylamine and 20 ml oleic acid. The mixture was purged with N2 and kept under the protection of N2 for the whole process. The mixture was put in a 100 °C oil bath and kept stirring for 20 hrs. The S powder was almost dissolved completely and gave a brown-orange colored solution. Undissolved S powder was removed by centrifugation at 13,240 × g for 10 mins. The supernatant was then diluted by 30 ml toluene and kept for future use.

Synthesis of chiral Au-CdS hetero-nanostructures

1 ml chiral Au-Ag nanoparticles were centrifugated and redispersed into 1 ml DMF. Meanwhile, 0.4 ml S precursor and 10 µl of 1-decanethiol were added into 2 ml toluene while stirring. The 1 ml Au-Ag DMF solution was added into the previous mixture drop-by-drop. The reaction was left for 1 hr at room temperature and the chiral Au-Ag2S nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation and redispersion into 2 ml toluene. For synthesis of chiral Au-CdS nanoparticles, 20 µl oleylamine, 40 µl oleic acid and 500 µl of 20 mg/ml Cd(NO3)2 methanol solution were added into the 2 ml chiral Au-Ag2S toluene solution. The mixture was put in 50 °C oil bath and kept stirring. 20 µl TBP was added into the mixture to initialize the reaction. After 1 hr stirring, the mixture was taken out and put in the sonication bath for better dispersion. The full reaction was reached by 2 hrs in the 50 °C oil bath under active stirring. Finally, the chiral Au-CdS nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation with addition of 2 ml ethanol and redispersion into 2 ml toluene. For thickness control of the CdS shell, Au-Ag nanoparticles with different thickness of Ag shell were used.

For the final step, 0.4 ml S precursor was added into the 2 ml toluene solution of chiral Au-Ag-CdS nanoparticles and the mixture was kept stirring at room temperature for 1 hr. The resultant chiral Au-Ag2S-CdS nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation with addition of 2 ml ethanol and redispersion into 2 ml toluene. For preparation of thin film, the sample solution is concentrated. A typical photo of the concentrated solution is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3a.

Phase transfer of chiral Au NCs from aqueous phase to oil phase

1 ml chiral Au NCs were centrifugated and redispersed into 1 ml DMF. Meanwhile, 0.4 ml S precursor was added into 2 ml toluene while stirring. The 1 ml chiral Au NCs in DMF was added into the previous mixture drop-by-drop. The mixture was left for 1 hr stirring at room temperature and the chiral Au NCs were collected by centrifugation and redispersion into the mixture of 2 ml toluene and 100 µl oleylamine. The dispersion of chiral Au NCs in toluene was further improved by sonication in 50 °C water bath for 2 hrs and stirring overnight. The S precursor should create Au-S bonds at the surface of the chiral Au NCs and thus promote the ligand exchange. The CD of the chiral Au NCs remained after the transfer.

Preparation of thin films samples for low temperature optical study

For low temperature optical spin measurements, it is crucial to achieve optically clear thin films with reduced light scattering. Typically, a stock solution of poly(vinyl butyral-co-vinyl alcohol-co-vinyl acetate) (PVB) with Mw ~ 50,000-80,000 in toluene (1:3 volume ratio) was prepared and is kept stirring until the polymer solution becomes clear. The chiral nanostructures in toluene with concentration finely adjusted for optimum optical density of final thin film sample, and then mixed homogeneously with PVB stock solution. A few drops of mixture solution of chiral nanostructures in PVB were added into a pre-cleaned microscope slide. In order to ensure high quality and homogeneous free-standing thin films with minimum light scattering, extra cares were needed for controlling such as evaporation speed of mixture solution by adjusting environmental pressure to avoid rapid evaporation of solvent. Once the film was dry with desired optical density, it could be peeled off by adding liquid nitrogen into the backside of microscope slide, while the thin film side was protected by dry clean N2 gas to avoid water condensation during the peel-off step. It is important to note that for our current study, the density of colloidal chiral nanostructures is always kept sufficiently low to avoid inter-particle coupling or interactions. A typical optically clear thin film with chiral nanostructures is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3b.

Characterizations of chiral hetero-nanostructures

For TEM, SAED, EDS mapping and line-scan characterizations, all samples were prepared by adding one drop of solution containing desired nanostructures onto the 300 mesh copper grids with carbon support film (Ted Pella, 01820) and the 400 mesh copper grids with ultrathin carbon film on lacey carbon support film (Ted Pella, 01824). A JEM 2100 LaB6 and a JEM 2100 FEG TEM/STEM equipped with a field emission gun from Maryland Nanocenter at the University of Maryland were applied. Typical TEM imaging parameters were: 200 keV electron landing energy, 108 μA beam current, 0.5 s acquisition time and 2004 pixel by 1336 pixel digital resolution. The CD and absorption spectra of solutions samples were simultaneously acquired on a commercial JASCO J-815 spectrometer at room temperature using a cuvette with light path of 1.0 cm. All Raman spectra were acquired on a commercial RENISHAW InVia system with 514 nm Ar ion laser.

FEM simulations

All the FEM simulations in the current work were performed by using commercial COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0 software. The structural models of different nanostructures were firstly constructed based on TEM characterizations by using commercial 3D modeling software SOLIDWORKS, and then are imported to the FEM solver. The imported structure was encapsulated by a spherical shell with size of the maximum excitation wavelength as the achiral environment and its outer surface set as perfect matching layer (PML) boundary. The dielectric function of environment was always set to be that of water in order to simulate CD measurement condition (in DI water). The optical response of the nanostructure was computed by illumination of left- and right- circularly polarized plane waves with incident wave vector k. The CD was computed with the wave vector k perpendicular to the nanostructure. The dielectric function of Au, Ag2S and CdS are from literatures67–70. If the CD signal was intrinsically small (e.g., CD of pure chiral CdS shell in Supplementary Fig. 13), extra care needed to be taken to minimize discretization numerical error that originated from asymmetric mesh. Laser and optical apparatus

Supplementary Fig. 7 shows schematically lab-built setup for low-temperature all-optical spin measurements and manipulations. All optical measurements in our current work were performed at 2 K in a magneto-optical superconducting magnet system (SM4000-8) from Oxford Instruments with magnetic field up to 7 T. This temperature was achieved by fully opening the needle valve to fill the sample space with LHe and pumping the sample space to reduce vapor pressure of LHe (the stable base temperature around 2 K could be achieved after a few minutes). All samples were always prepared freshly right before loading to magneto-optical cryostat to avoid sample degradation. Under our experimental conditions in current work, we have not yet found any evidence of sample damage or degrading.

All ultrafast optical spectroscopy measurements in our current work were performed with the same laser system that was configured to meet experimental requirements for different samples and chiral-spin measurements and manipulations. The whole laser system is based on a Coherent’s regenerative amplifier laser (RegA) with two home-made optical parametric amplifiers (OPAs): A 18 W CW solid-state 532 nm laser (Verdi) acts as pumps for both Ti:S laser (MIRA 900) and RegA simultaneously to generate seed laser input for OPA. Two OPAs are used in the system operating at 256 kHz repetition rate, producing multiple 100 fs pulses of energy tunable monochromatic beam in the range of 0.55–1.15 eV (infrared OPA1) and 1.65–2.82 eV (visible OPA2), respectively. A lab-made frequency doubler/tripler is also used with OPA1 to produce laser pulse in the range of 2.06–3.01 eV. Ultrafast broadband whitelights are also simultaneously produced by both OPAs. OPA also generate whitelight output by using sapphire crystal. Single wavelength component of the whitelight pulse can be selected by using a mini-monochromator (Optometrics Corp.) and can be used as a probe pulse to detect the spin polarization.

Chirp correction in time-resolved optical spectroscopy measurements

All laser pulses were synchronized in the time domain. It is worth noting that the whitelight continuum from OPA typically exhibited a significant chirp (i.e., different wavelength components are temporally separated in whitelight pulse), and needed to be corrected if it was used as a time-resolved probe pulse. The chirp correction was carried out under second order approximation (i.e., the chirp of whitelight continuum could be described as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t\left(\lambda \right)={C}_{0}+{C}_{1}\lambda+{C}_{2}{\lambda }^{2}$$\end{document}Δtλ=C0+C1λ+C2λ2), which was found to be sufficient for our current work. We used a routine two-photon absorption measurement to determine pump-probe cross correlation for the chirp correction34,72. Briefly, the monochromatic pump and the whitelight probe were spatially overlapped in a zinc oxide sample and the time delay was scanned for each transmitted whitelight component with selected photon energy. After collecting all the time-resolved data across the whole spectral range of whitelight, a second order polynomial fit was applied to fit the data with the above function. Once the coefficient Ci (i = 0, 1, 2) were determined they could be used to add a corrected temporal offset to time-resolved measurements to correct the chirp effect.

All-optical time-resolved spin measurements and manipulations for chirality-induced spin dynamics

Our all-optical spin experiments always involved three main key components: spin initialization along the laser propagation direction by using either circularly polarized or linearly polarized ultrafast optical pulse with the selective photon energy and intensity at the zero time delay (the circularly polarized pulse was created by the achromatic zero-order quarter waveplates with retardation accuracy of ±λ/100 from Newport Corp.); spin detection by using linearly polarized probe pulse with the selective photon energy and intensity at the time delay t (the linearly polarized pulse was created by using a Glan-Thompson linear polarizer with high extinction ratio more than 100,000 from Newport Corp.). For ultrafast dynamic CD measurement, a PEM (I/FS50) with controller from the Hinds Instrument was used for the probe beam to modulate left- and right- circularly polarized states; a third ultrafast optical pulse with selective photon energy and intensity at the pre-determined time delay ttip was applied to excite chiral plasmons of Au core for some experiments in the current work. The arrangements of ultrafast laser beams depicted in Supplementary Fig. 7 were always configured and adjusted to meet specific experimental conditions set by samples and experimental purpose. Typically, monochromatic output of visible OPA2 was used for spin initialization, and the monochromatic output of infrared OPA1 was doubled/tripled to produce high energy pulses to match bandgap of CdS QSs with shell different thickness. To improve detection sensitivity, a home-made laser power stabilizer with feedback was always applied in optical path to reduce static noise level to below 0.1%. For every laser pulse a mechanical optical chopper with different modulation frequency was applied for lock-in detection. We have found that the lock-in detection at the sum frequency (that is generated from home-made circuit) and in a cascade detection scheme could efficiently isolate the effect of unexcited or partially excited quantum structures. All these considerations were crucial to achieve the data quality presented in the current study. For all the optical measurements in our current work, all pulse intensities were always adjusted and kept low enough to avoid potential Auger process and biexciton effects. In general, the number of excited electron-hole pairs was always kept one order of magnitude (or more) lower than the total number of quantum structures in the focus volume (~ 75 μm in diameter).

In a regular TRFR measurement, the pump pulse arrived at the zero-delay time, spin polarization in sample at t was measured by a linearly polarized probe pulse with a home-made circuit, whose polarization plane was rotated by an amount proportional to the spin magnetization along the laser propagation direction (e.g., Fig. 1c). The zero-delay time was always corrected for the chirp of OPA laser. When the in-plane magnetic field H0 was applied (i.e., Voigt configuration), the pump-injected spins comprised a coherent superposition of states quantized along the field direction and separated in energy by the Zeeman splitting. As the system evolved in time, the energy difference between the spin states produced a quantum beating of spin magnetization along the optical path, resulting in an oscillatory Faraday rotation of the time delayed linearly polarized probe pulse at the Larmor frequency (νL) that was field and sample dependent. Such TRFR technique has been successfully employed to study ensemble spin dynamics of colloidal nanostructures, including colloidal II-VI quantum dots and quantum shells40–48. From prior experimental and theoretical studies42,46,47, it was generally believed that the spin observed in such colloidal semiconductor structures in various optical studies could be assigned to excitons due to quantum confinement effect.
