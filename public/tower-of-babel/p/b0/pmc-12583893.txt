Experiences of financial stress on health, wellbeing and food insecurity among student dietitians on unpaid mandatory student placements
Authors: Jarrah Manning, Rebecca Heins, Kelly Lambert, Natalie Nicholls, Gabrielle O'Flynn, Karen Walton, Anne‐Therese McMahon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsUnpaid mandatory student placements are common among allied health disciplines. This qualitative study aimed to specifically explore the student dietitian experiences of and perspective on financial stress and food insecurity during unpaid mandatory student placements as well as describe any related impacts on their health and wellbeing.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews were undertaken with student dietitians living in Australia and New Zealand. Participants had self‐nominated for interviews from a larger interprofessional mixed method study on student placement poverty. Inductive thematic analysis involving coding, categorisation, theme development and refinement was used to generate themes.ResultsA total of 11 interviews were undertaken. Most students were female, studying full‐time and living in Queensland, New South Wales and New Zealand. All students had undertaken at least one unpaid placement. Thematic analysis of the student experience of placement generated five key themes and 18 subthemes. The key themes were (1) Overall Wellbeing on Placement, (2) Financial Security, (3) Social Support, (4) Income Sources and (5) Course Structure. Overall, unpaid placements had a significant impact on the ability of students to maintain their overall wellbeing whilst on placement. This included impacts on financial security. There was noted to be substantial cognitive dissonance between high levels of nutrition knowledge and the inability to maintain their own food security.ConclusionThese findings highlight that unpaid mandatory student placements (especially those requiring full‐time attendance) represent a major challenge for student dietitians, with implications for student physical and mental wellbeing and future diversity within the profession of dietetics.

INTRODUCTION

University students face financial stress at higher rates than other adults in Australia. Three out of five students reported prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic that finances were a source of worry, 1 and concerningly, the 2024 University Accord reports financial stress for students is increasing. 2 For university students, financial stress can broadly impact basic needs of daily living, from safe and secure housing to food intake. 1 One serious consequence of financial stress among students includes decreased ability to purchase adequate food. 3 , 4 This is not an unexpected outcome, as more broadly in Australia, 79% of food insecure households state the high cost of living is a major contributing factor, and 42% report that low income significantly contributed to food insecurity. 5 Notably, university students are a group more likely to fall into low‐income categories, 6 , 7 and are a known vulnerable group who are likely to experience food insecurity. 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

Food insecurity is defined as ‘physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food.’ 12 According to the Foodbank Hunger Report from 2023, 5 over a third (36%) of Australian adults can be classed as food insecure and 12% more are only marginally food secure, facing anxiety over food running out. Multiple studies have established university students are a group at higher risk of food insecurity than the general population. 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 This is attributed to a combination of factors that directly impact students' financial security, from less secure employment and lower incomes, 6 , 7 to higher likelihood of being a renter. 8 Food insecurity can have major implications, including poorer physical health outcomes, 13 higher levels of psychological distress, 10 , 14 and worsening academic performance. 6 , 7 Economic access is the main barrier faced by students in acquiring appropriate types and amounts of food. 3 , 11 This has been exacerbated by the COVID‐19 pandemic and worsening economic conditions since. 9 , 10 , 15 Subgroups of students such as international students, 10 and students with children, 14 have felt the impact of recent economic events even more acutely than the rest of the student population. Students with additional burdens which impact opportunities to work, such as unpaid placements, are likely to be affected as well.

Placements are an integral component in the current structure of university learning for students in many allied health degrees. Placements are generally unpaid but require students to attend professional sites within normal work hours, usually for 5 days per week. These placements have been shown to be a large financial burden for students, interrupting paid work commitments or making them impossible altogether. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Despite recent interest in the financial impacts of placements, and the demonstrated issue of food insecurity in university students, there is a major knowledge gap surrounding students' experiences of food insecurity whilst on unpaid placements. A 2020 study explored barriers to health behaviours in nursing students, where participants specifically connected their financial stress whilst on placement with the quality of food they were able to buy. 23 Another study focused on the financial challenges faced by nursing students on placements, finding the health and wellbeing of 61% of participants was impacted by financial issues. 20

The current structure of mandatory unpaid placements likely reinforces socioeconomic barriers within tertiary education, ensuring only those who are able to work unpaid full‐time are able to successfully gain the qualification. The workforces within many of the health disciplines that utilise unpaid placements, including dietetics, are also largely female dominated. 24 This may lead to women entering the workforce under higher financial stress or with debt levels exceeding those of men graduating at the same time, widening the gap in gender equality. 25 Other population groups who are more vulnerable (such as single parents or international students) are also more likely to experience impacts during placements.

Despite recently updated competency standards from Dietitians Australia, 26 acknowledging socioecological determinants of health and recognising marginalised populations, dietetics has a history of, and still maintains, a monolithic culture of majority Australian‐born, middle‐class women. 24 Unpaid placements contribute to furthering a discipline comprised mostly of health professionals from higher income backgrounds, resulting in a homogenous workforce that does not reflect the population of Australia. 22 , 24 For instance, some demographics of Australians including people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, people living with disability, and First Nations people experience higher rates of poverty than average. 27 These communities also face the highest burden of diet‐related diseases, 28 , 29 , 30 and therefore are often in need of dietetic care. Therefore, it would be optimal for these communities to be represented within the profession of dietetics. However, known financial stress during university study combined with unpaid placements are likely to dissuade people from marginalised demographics from pursuing a career within placement‐oriented degrees such as dietetics. This ultimately exacerbates poorer health outcomes experienced by these same communities, as they are not involved in the design or provision of their own healthcare. 31 , 32

For the student dietitians who are able to undertake placement, it is worth exploring limitations faced in managing day‐to‐day personal health. In reality, student dietitians would be arguably the best suited of any profession to manage nutritional health and avoid food insecurity within the financial constraints of placements. Student dietitians have been shown to have high levels of both health literacy 33 and food and nutrition literacy 34 , 35 due to their area of study, as well as being likely to prioritise their own nutritional health more than other disciplines. However, recent evidence by our research team 22 indicates the requirement to undertake months of unpaid placement poses a significant financial burden to student dietitians, impacting their ability to manage their own basic living needs. 22

The aim of this study is to explore student dietitians' experiences and perspectives of financial stress on health, wellbeing and food insecurity during unpaid mandatory placement. The findings may inform further support and guide the development of strategies to address the impacts of placement on the health, wellbeing and food security of student dietitians.

METHODS

This study is a retrospective qualitative analysis of semi‐structured interviews that were collected as part of the original study in 2024 on placement poverty. 22 The study is reported according to the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research guidelines for qualitative research. 36 The study was approved by the University of Wollongong Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval number: 2023/175). The original study 22 explored the financial concerns and impact of costs due to unpaid placement from current and recently completed university students from a health or education degree (medical, nursing, allied health, social work, teaching or psychology). The original study design incorporated a two‐part parallel mixed methods study using both survey and interview data collection methods. 22 Survey participants could choose to opt in to undertake a semi‐structured interview. Interview questions were based on the work of Usher et al., 20 as well as the experiences of the broader research team managing students on placement and feedback from placement supervisors. Of the 375 participants who agreed to be interviewed in the parent study, 22 purposive sampling was used to ensure a range of age groups, student types (domestic or international), geographic locations, institutions and genders were represented. Interviews were then conducted online by one of five experienced qualitative researchers unknown to the participants (four dietitians and one teacher, all female). Interviews were digitally recorded and held between August 2023 and January 2024. All students identifying as a student dietitian were included in this analysis. The research questions guiding the analysis were: ‘What is the impact of the financial implications of mandatory placements on the health and wellbeing of student dietitians?’ and ‘How has financial stress impacted the students' food security?’

Written and verbal informed consent was provided by all participants prior to interviews when completing the survey and again prior to and on the day of the interview. Interview participants were also given information on support services in the case that interviews were distressing. Participants were offered the option of reviewing the transcript. However, many were in the final weeks of study for their degree and were unreachable or not interested in this opportunity. Interview transcripts were instead authenticated with thorough transcript cleaning.

Descriptive statistics were self‐reported by all participants and collated for all interviews. Demographic information including age, gender, student type (domestic or international), geographic location and institution was gathered as part of the written survey prior to interviews being conducted. Participants' age was categorised as <26, ≥26 years or unknown, as people 26 years or older commencing postgraduate study are considered mature age students. Characteristics recorded as unknown indicate questions not completed by student dietitians in this initial survey stage.

Recorded interview data was de‐identified to ensure anonymity by a project officer on the project not involved in the analysis. Interviews were transcribed using Otter ai (AISense, 2016, Mountain View California) software, then reviewed for accuracy by the researcher (Jarrah Manning) as part of data cleaning. The cleaning of transcripts also served as preliminary familiarisation with the data as the first point of analysis within thematic analysis using Braun and Clarke's six‐step framework. 37 , 38 This method incorporates iterative analysis, allowing for the inductive generation of themes as well as reflexivity in order to acknowledge and address the influence of the analysts (Jarrah Manning, Anne‐Therese McMahon) in the analytic process to ensure the integrity of the process. 38 Initial coding of all interview transcripts was undertaken by two researchers separately and agreed by consensus to support rigour and credibility within the analysis process. 37 , 39 Reflexivity included memoing during the coding analysis process, including reflections on the beliefs and experiences of the key analyst and discussions with the broader research team. Further stages of analysis were completed through iterative analysis supported by NVIVO 14 software.

Credibility in data collection was supported through the use of multiple interviewers as well as a semi‐structured interview guide (Table 1). Credibility was also reinforced through critical reflexivity used to examine the context through which analysts' own experiences have shaped the generation and interpretation of themes. 37 , 38 The lead researcher had current lived experience of unpaid dietetic placements, and all other researchers were experienced in supporting students during placement periods.

The codes generated were categorised by the lead researcher and critically analyzed to develop themes and subthemes encompassing the student dietitians' experience of unpaid placements. Mind maps were used to explore interactions between themes and subthemes and depict the experiences that were discussed in the interviews, articulated in the quotes assigned to codes and developed into a thematic map. These themes were defined and reviewed iteratively throughout the process of data analysis as new themes were generated or others deemed insignificant, in line with Braun and Clarke's framework. 37 Analysis of the relationship between themes occurred through the lens of both food security and financial security to address the research questions and explore the overarching narrative from the data. Final themes were reached through a consensus of researchers and key quotes were used to authenticate all themes and subthemes.

RESULTS

A total of 11 student interviews were included in the study. All students were studying dietetics and had undertaken at least one clinical placement (Table 2). Most were female (73%) and one third of students (36%) were <26 years of age. The majority were studying full‐time (82%) and the most frequent geographic areas represented were Queensland (36%), New South Wales (18%) and New Zealand (18%). Interview duration was between 20 and 60 min.

This participant used multiple sources of food to meet their needs over the course of several placements and articulated their difficulties in accessing food whilst on placement. These participants detailed the repetitive meals they ate on placement and the reduced nutritional quality of this limited variety. Participants attempted to prioritise nutritious food during placement but found this often came at a cost in other areas of health or financial security. Other participants felt the social impacts of food insecurity as they were unable to participate in social occasions centred around food. ‘The other girls on placement were … having like a different meal each night and then she was worried I was gonna get mercury poisoning because I was having so much tuna (laughs).’ Participant 10, 21 year old, female, domestic student ‘It's one thing for somebody who doesn't have any sort of understanding of the nutritional impacts of just surviving on … a lack of, like, quality in the diet and, y'know, it's like, oh, I could survive on two‐minute noodles, but I'd feel really sick and I'd feel probably worse than I do now’ Participant 8, 29 years old, female, domestic student Cognitive dissonance in dietetics The discordance between students' extensive food and nutrition knowledge and the inability to implement this in their own life during placement was keenly felt by some participants. When facing food insecurity whilst on placement, student dietitians expressed frustration and shame at their inability to meet their own nutritional requirements despite high food and nutrition literacy due to their degree. This impacted students' confidence on placement as they felt their food security status reflected a lack of ability in their chosen field, culminating in impacts on academic performance, mental health and physical health. ‘You can have all of the knowledge in the world, but if you don't have the resources it's, y'know, and that in and of itself is incredibly demoralising, to be studying dietetics and then to not even be able to look after yourself, … it's been a pretty traumatising experience’ Participant 8, 29 years old, female, domestic studentHousing security The ability to maintain suitable and reliable housing both at home and away on placement added to the burden felt by some participants. Participants who had access to free accommodation on placements away from home expressed relief provided by this aid, whilst other participants reflected on accommodation expenses as a barrier to undertaking rural and interstate placements. Placements away from home led to increased financial stress associated with housing security, particularly for participants who rented or had a mortgage and had to face increased costs. Participants also detailed calculated risks taken, such as unauthorised subletting whilst away from home, or this participant risking housing status altogether. ‘So that (placement is) five weeks … but I'm managing that one by leaving my current flat, so I'm not paying for rent, … and then just dealing with finding a flat once I get back. … So I wouldn't recommend that method, but we'll … give it a go.’ Participant 10, 21 years old, female, domestic studentPrioritisation of health The capacity to prioritise personal health during placement, and choices made regarding which aspects of health are prioritised due to limited time, money and energy was discussed by participants. Participants revealed facing levels of financial stress that necessitated not only decisions on whether to prioritise health, but also being forced to choose between multiple aspects of personal health. Time and energy were also major barriers, especially when it came to sleep and regular exercise. Participants noted that these types of changes in lifestyle also led to impacted performance on placement and greater quality of life. Participants frequently attempted to prioritise nutritious food during placement, but many found this led to other types of financial stress or came at the cost of worse health outcomes in other areas. ‘At times I feel sort of angry and annoyed that I put my health last a lot of the times when it comes to placement and uni’ Participant 3, 26 years old, female, domestic student ‘A lot of us prioritise our, I guess our nutrition and probably, uh, reduce other things, … because we know how important nutrition and good food is, especially whilst we're studying long days,’ Participant 3, 26 years old, female, domestic student 3. Participants detailed the importance of social support from a variety of sources for their wellbeing, and the impact of placement on their ability to receive this from friends, family and university staff. Friends and family were key pillars of social and emotional support for students on placement. Participants described support or lack of support impacting mental health and motivation to make it through the remainder of placement. ‘I do definitely have some peers that I can talk to about it and they're, yeah, still understanding of my situation, even though they might not be in the same, um, but I think it's definitely not something that I can talk to a lot of people about’ Participant 4, 21 years old, female, domestic studentSupport from university placement coordinators The role of university placement coordinators as a source of information and resources before and during placement was discussed, some students did not feel they were supported on placement or that they could reach out for help when needed. University Placement Coordinators emerged as a key support system who either enabled students to be best prepared and facilitated access to key services and resources, or alternatively left students feeling as if they had no one to reach out to when struggling. Some participants saw university coordinators as a source of valuable information and an initial point of contact when struggling whilst on placement, whilst others were disappointed at the lack of information shared with students prior to placement and therefore did not see their university coordinators as an asset to support their placement experience. Participants also identified that they did not feel their sacrifices made to undertake placement were taken seriously when university staff did not explicitly discuss student wellbeing on placement. ‘Even just feeling appreciated on placement, uh, I don't think, sometimes, uh, whether it's our lecturers or placement supervisors, realise how much students have to give up for placement, uh, whether that's, y'know, sports, work, relationships, time, sleep, all that is, it's things that we have to give up’ Participant 3, 26 years old, female, domestic student ‘Maybe at the start, um, of the course, explaining to them the number of placements you have to do and the financial aspects of it as well, just so that students are prepared more, um, and also just making sure students know what, um, what help they can get from the university, or even like government, other places as well’ Participant 5, 25 years old, female, international student 4. Income Sources Sources of money throughout placement was variable and affected students' capacity to maintain their wellbeing. Participants articulated their restricted ability to obtain some incoming money whilst on placement, leading to increased financial stress. Multiple sources of income were utilised by participants, with international students in particular finding difficulty obtaining any financial support. Some participants expressed awareness of the deeper financial implications of lost income such as noted by this participant. ‘You're paying for the privilege of being at placement, and then losing out on the income of not being able to work, so you've kind of got the double whammy there … having that kinda little bit of funding through wherever it could possibly come from, just to keep the students having that little bit of cash, just to, like, keep their head above water while they tread water during their degree’ Participant 6, 41 years old, male, domestic studentEmployment Employment as a source of income during placement was critical for some students, with normal working capacity severely stretched to unhealthy levels for some. Employment during and in between placements was a common source of income for participants but was connected to decreased time for overall self‐care and impacted mental health, physical health, academics and social connection. Some participants also expressed difficulty in finding employment due to limited and sporadic availability between placements. Participants also reflected on the impact of weekend work on their performance on placement and overall wellbeing. ‘Working … seven days, … at one point during my most recent placement I'd worked, like Saturday, Sunday, full week, Saturday, Sunday, full week, and then like a Saturday and then had a Sunday off … I was pretty tired’ Participant 9, 23 years old, female, domestic student ‘One girl in my cohort is just like, was cutting her sleep hours so she could work extra and fit everything in so that she can make all the ends meet.’ Participant 10, 21 years old, female, domestic student Alternative income sources Students' access to sources of income other than traditional employment, for example, placement grants, youth allowance, student loans, etc., was crucial for some students whilst not accessible for others. Alternative income sources provided financial relief for many participants but grants and government payments were often not accessible for international students. Students described a range of impacted areas of wellbeing, including academic, social, mental and physical impacts of placement experiences and the relationship between them. Financial security during placement was a key theme, and it heavily contributed to Overall Wellbeing on Placement. The lower level of financial security articulated by student dietitians had impacts in multiple areas, including housing security, their ability to prioritise different aspects of personal health, and food security, as described in key quotes below. Students facing lower levels of food security detailed both a lack of access to food preferences, as well as a lack of economic access to enough food to meet their needs. This experience of food insecurity, as well as the inability to prioritise personal health, was discussed as creating a feeling of cognitive dissonance for students on placement, as they explored the contradiction of having a high level of nutrition and health literacy, but the experience of being unable to implement preferred health strategies whilst educating patients to implement the recommended behaviours.

Income sources during placement were key to financial security and came from a variety of sources, including traditional employment, alternative income sources such as scholarships and welfare payments, financial support from others such as from partners or parents, and financial literacy encompassing the students' ability to manage their budget as well as advanced planning and saving money prior to placement.

Course structure also heavily impacted financial security during unpaid placements. As detailed through key quotes (see Table 3), student dietitians who undertook placements that were only 4 days per week expressed relief at the freedom of an extra day to earn money or to rest and recover. In comparison, students who were given placements that were 5 days a week for up to 10 weeks at a time discussed how this led to further deterioration in financial condition. Students described incurring expenses directly as a result of placement, particularly for placements away from home. This was notably when the accommodation costs and increased travel expenses were connected by some students to housing security issues due to the impact of ‘double rent’ (home rental and placement accommodation). Support from university coordinators was identified as key to accessing support when needed throughout placement. However, this support was variable, with some students feeling valued and prepared, whilst other students identified that a lack of information provided prior to placement commencing was problematic. University coordinators were perceived to be connected to awareness of support services available, including foodbanks and counselling services, and considered a source of information on alternative income sources such as scholarships, grants and welfare payments.

The support provided by university coordinators was connected by student dietitians to the impacts of other social support, such as emotional support from friends, family and classmates. Emotional support was identified as a key motivator to do well on placement, whilst students who did not receive social support expressed that it was an isolating experience that directly impacted wellbeing. Social support was also connected to financial security and income sources, as some students relied on financial help from family or partners. Other students instead identified a lack of financial help from others as a disadvantage in comparison to their peers. Social support, both financial and emotional, was said to help mitigate the impacts of placement on students' overall wellbeing, as evidenced by key quotes in Table 3.

DISCUSSION

This study found that unpaid mandatory placement had varying impacts on overall student wellbeing and food security. This work also highlights the need for reflection within the dietetics profession regarding the implications of existing placement models. Factors that supported or negatively impacted student wellbeing on placement were identified within key themes, and their relationship to each other clarifies the substantial impact of unpaid placement on student wellbeing. The articulation of these factors enables meaningful strategies and/or policy changes to be developed at the government (federal and state) professional organisational level and tertiary (university and program level).

Students described distress due to the experience of cognitive dissonance throughout placements, particularly in relation to food and their inability to retain agency over food choices, as well as in relation to overall health and wellbeing such as the sacrifice of preferred health behaviours. This is similar to findings from a previous study of more than 16 000 Canadians 40 which found that cooking skills were not related to food insecurity and food insecure households were equally likely to adjust recipes to optimise health, but economics prevented them from doing so. Food literacy programs in Australia, such as OzHarvest's Nutrition Education Skills Training program, also have found improved food security among low socioeconomic adult participants when economic access to food also improved simultaneously. 41 This was reflected in the results of this study where some student dietitians still experienced food insecurity during unpaid placements despite high nutrition and health literacy levels. 33 , 34 , 35 This demonstrates a need for greater support for all students within degrees containing unpaid placements, as health literacy alone is not enough to address decreased food security during placement periods. This may include clear articulation of approaches such as the use of one‐on‐one mentoring for students to address issues or concerns as they arise within their placement across the year.

Food insecurity is typically categorised as either transitory, describing temporary disruption in access or availability of food, or chronic, where access is insufficient over the long term. 42 Within the context of placement, it is difficult to define the type of food insecurity that is occurring as many students face ongoing barriers to acquiring sufficient food. However, for some students, food insecurity appears to be most severe during placement and then eases with the end of each placement block. For others, students may experience ongoing food insecurity after the conclusion of placement due to severe depletion of income across the placement experience and potential difficulty finding or increasing employment after completion of the degree.

Housing security was also discussed by student dietitians, with accommodation costs of placements away from home leading to increased financial burden for students with existing housing costs. A longitudinal analysis of the impact of housing stress on mental health found that even intermittent exposure to housing stress came with negative mental health impacts. 43 Food insecurity is also associated with increased risk of mental illness, particularly anxiety and depression. 44 With housing stress defined as spending greater than 30% of income on housing, 43 and food stress similarly defined as more than 25% of income spent on food, 45 it would be unlikely for student dietitians on full‐time unpaid placements to be able to achieve income levels high enough, or essential costs low enough, to meet these thresholds.

Many students spoke about the importance of financial preparation for placement through saving and budgeting strategies, but still faced financial stress whilst on placement despite demonstrated skills in financial literacy. A 2021 study found that increased financial literacy in university students did not increase financial wellbeing and instead found that higher income levels were the factor more commonly associated with increased financial wellbeing. 46 This is reflected in the results of this study, with students experiencing decreased financial security despite budgeting money or having saved prior to placement. This needs to be considered in the development of strategies to address financial insecurity during unpaid placements, as increased financial literacy will not lead to better placement experiences without additional supports.

Long weekly working hours have been shown to contribute to adverse outcomes across multiple health domains, including mental health, physiological health and even health behaviours. 47 As described in this study, student dietitians are, at best, working 40‐hr weeks at placement sites and experiencing the impacts of financial stress on their overall wellbeing. In other cases, they are engaging in paid work commitments on weekends and nights out of necessity and are experiencing the negative effects of extended working weeks. A 2022 systematic review on the impact of reduced work hours on employee health and wellbeing found benefits across both sleep and stress levels from a decrease in weekly work hours. 48 Within the constraints of the current placement system, student wellbeing on placement could potentially be maintained by reduced placement hours. As articulated by student dietitians, fewer placement days per week allowed time for recovery between placement hours and paid work commitments, even if total weekly work hours were still excessive.

Despite recently updated competency standards from Dietitians Australia, 26 acknowledging the socioecological determinants of health and recognising marginalised populations, dietetics has a history of, and still maintains, a monolithic culture of majority Australian‐born, middle‐class women. 24 We suggest that unpaid placements contribute to furthering a discipline comprised mainly of health professionals from higher income backgrounds, resulting in a homogenous workforce that does not reflect the population of Australia. 22 , 24 For instance, some demographics of Australians, including people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, people living with disabilities, and First Nations people, experience higher rates of poverty than average. 27 These communities also face the highest burden of diet‐related diseases, 28 , 29 , 30 and therefore are often in need of dietetic care. Therefore, it would be optimal for these communities to be represented within the profession of dietetics. However, financial stress during university study combined with unpaid placements is likely to dissuade people from marginalised demographics from pursuing a career within placement‐oriented degrees such as dietetics. This ultimately exacerbates poorer health outcomes experienced by these same communities, as they are not involved in the design or provision of their own healthcare. 31 , 32

The current structure of unpaid placements also appears to be influenced by a neoliberal approach to tertiary education. 21 Expectations of students to be individually responsible for financial wellbeing despite full‐time unpaid placement commitments serve to enhance socioeconomic barriers to university education. 2 This division of responsibility also contributes to furthering healthism, 49 as the focus on individual decision‐making places blame on the actions of those that face barriers to health‐affirming behaviours, rather than addressing the barriers themselves. This can be seen in the experiences of student dietitians on placement within students' articulated beliefs that they ‘should’ be able to maintain nutritional health and food security whilst on placement due to their own health literacy. Neoliberalism also interacts with the social and financial support received by student dietitians. Students discussed differing levels of support from friends, family and university coordinators and connected this to resulting impacts on wellbeing. Neoliberal ideology serves to promote loneliness by increasing competition between individuals and thereby eliminating support systems and community. 50 , 51 The result of individual responsibility when applied to unpaid placements is a qualification achievable to those who have access to financial support, and heavily detrimental to quality of life for those who don't. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Diversity within dietetics, as well as other placement‐based degrees, 24 , 32 will not improve whilst the current system of unpaid placements remains unchanged.

A limitation of this study is the novice status of the lead researcher, as lack of experience in analysis of qualitative research could impact the depth of this study. This was tempered by including supervision of analysis by a diverse research team with extensive qualitative research experience. The lived experience of unpaid placements of the lead researcher posed a risk of a biased perspective in analysis; however, reflexivity was incorporated within the study to address this issue, and understanding of the student dietitian experience allowed for in‐depth critical appraisal of the data. Other limitations include the small numbers of dietetic student voices from other states and territories, directly as a result of the sampling strategy of the parent study. Whilst intentional recruitment of students with lived experience of financial stress enabled rich insights to be obtained, it is possible those with limited impacts may be less likely to have responded to the study, and thus we may not have adequately captured the experience of all student dietitians. This study must also be interpreted in light of survivorship bias, 52 as the students recruited had all completed at least one placement, meaning that students who were unable to commence placement, or the degree as a whole, were not captured in this data.

Whilst there was consistency in the experiences described by student dietitians, we lack insights from those who drop out before or during placement. Further research should be undertaken to explore the experience of students who were unable to complete their dietetics degree due to the requirements of placement as well as those who opted not to commence study due to the mandatory nature of unpaid placements. Given the findings of the parent study regarding the impact of unpaid placements leading to worse societal inequity, 22 it would be useful to explore the experiences of low‐income groups such as single parents, those from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, and international students. The perspective of university placement coordinators should also be explored to examine factors that may impact student support as well as any impacts of the role on coordinators' mental health and emotional wellbeing.

Unpaid mandatory placements come with financial implications that directly impact student dietitians' ability to maintain their own health and wellbeing. Student dietitians are arguably the best suited of any discipline to manage nutritional health and avoid food insecurity within the financial constraints of unpaid placements; however, it is clear that health literacy is not enough to combat a lack of economic access to food. Students had difficulty maintaining their own nutritional health whilst on placements, leading to a range of impacts on their overall wellbeing as well as a cognitive dissonance between their own knowledge about a healthy diet and the inability to implement it in their own lives. Unpaid placements may pose a major barrier to entry into dietetics for anyone already experiencing disadvantage. This could contribute to a discipline comprised of a majority of health professionals from higher income backgrounds, resulting in a homogeneous workforce that does not reflect its consumers and may not be suited to provide best practice care.
