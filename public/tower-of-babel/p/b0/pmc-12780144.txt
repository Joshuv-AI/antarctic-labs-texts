Aging Mediates Inflammatory Transformation of Kidney-Resident Macrophages via Thrombospondin-1 Signaling
Authors: Chaelin Kang, Donghwan Yun, Boyoun Jang, Yongjun Kim, Minki Hong, Juhyeon Hwang, Haein Yoon, Jeongmin Oh, Hyun Mu Shin, Kyung Chul Moon, Dong-Sup Lee, Yon Su Kim, Hyun Je Kim, Seung Seok Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKidney-resident macrophages (KRMs) are long-lived immune cells crucial for maintaining kidney homeostasis, with roles that vary depending on kidney condition. Their phenotypes can exert different effects on kidney status, but how the phenotypes of KRMs evolve in aged kidneys and the resulting impacts on kidney homeostasis remain poorly understood. Through single-cell RNA sequencing, we identified a phenotypic shift in KRMs within aged kidneys characterized by increased oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), which stemmed from crosstalk with surrounding tubules. Among these interactions, thrombospondin-1 (THBS1), derived from tubules, has emerged as a pivotal factor driving OXPHOS in KRMs. Experiments confirmed that THBS1 increased OXPHOS in cultured macrophages, polarizing them toward an inflammatory phenotype marked by the production of IL-1β and IL-10. This shift contributes to the inflammatory state of aged kidneys. Finally, we validated these findings in human kidney tissues from elderly individuals. In conclusion, aged kidneys harbor phenotypically altered KRMs that promote an inflammatory microenvironment, with THBS1 playing a central role in this process. These results underscore the importance of exploring therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway to mitigate the inflammatory shift in aged kidneys.Keywords: Aging, Inflammation, Kidney-resident macrophage, Oxidative phosphorylation, Thrombospondin 1

INTRODUCTION

Aging is an intrinsic and inevitable process that affects the structure and function of various organs, including the kidneys. Kidney aging typically begins in individuals in their 30s to 40s (1,2) and is characterized by structural and functional changes, such as a reduction in the number of nephrons and impaired tubular function (3,4). These age-associated changes manifest as a gradual decline in the glomerular filtration rate, indicating an overall reduction in renal capacity (5). This decline is primarily driven by a combination of nephron loss and increased tubulointerstitial fibrosis (6). Remarkably, healthy adults lose approximately 48% of their nephrons by the age of 70–75 (7). Understanding age-related declines in kidney function is critical because reduced kidney function, whether stemming from natural aging processes or other factors, is often associated with an increased risk of comorbidities (8,9).

One of the key changes in the aged kidney is an overall increase in inflammation, which is associated with the progressive loss of tubulointerstitial integrity and a decline in renal function (10,11). This phenomenon, often referred to as inflammaging, is characterized by chronic low-grade systemic inflammation in the absence of infection, also known as sterile inflammation (12). Persistent inflammation drives tissue and organ fibrosis through mechanisms involving cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and immune system dysregulation (13,14,15). Furthermore, this inflammatory state makes the kidneys susceptible to acute injuries, such as ischemic or toxic insults, thereby exacerbating the risk of severe dysfunction (16,17). In clinical practice, elderly individuals face a heightened risk of acute kidney injury and its progression to chronic kidney disease, ultimately leading to dialysis dependency (18,19). Excessive inflammation also impairs the recovery process from injuries, resulting in prolonged or incomplete repair (20). Despite its profound impact on overall health and longevity, the molecular and cellular links between aged kidneys and the inflammatory milieu remain poorly understood.

Each organ hosts tissue-resident macrophages, which are long-lived and capable of self-renewal within their microenvironment (21). These resident macrophages help maintain tissue homeostasis by acting as sentinels that respond to physiological changes and external challenges (22). They serve as the first line of defense against harmful microorganisms, mediate inflammation, and provide protection through the phagocytosis of dying cells (23,24,25). Kidney-resident macrophages (KRMs), which are located primarily in the tubulointerstitial area (26), also contribute to kidney homeostasis by exhibiting both pro- and anti-inflammatory characteristics (27,28). Notably, their phenotype and function are shaped by the surrounding parenchymal tissue environment, which plays a key role in regulating homeostasis (29). However, the specific relationship between KRMs and aging-related inflammatory processes in the kidney has not yet been elucidated.

Herein, we aimed to address aging-related changes in KRM phenotypes via single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis. Notable shifts, such as increased oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), were identified in aged KRMs. Thrombospondin-1 (THBS1), expressed by surrounding tubular cells, is a key factor driving OXPHOS changes in aged KRMs. Consequently, aged KRMs exhibited increased expression of inflammatory cytokines rather than their characteristic phagocytic signature, contributing to the establishment of an inflammatory milieu in the aged kidney. Collectively, these findings provide valuable insights into aging-related inflammatory processes and lay the groundwork for future therapeutic strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA), and male mice were used for all experiments. The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature and humidity and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at the animal facility of Seoul National University College of Medicine. For analysis, the mice were divided into 3 age groups: 8 wk, 6 months, and 22 months. At the designated time points, the mice were sacrificed following perfusion with PBS, and their kidneys were harvested for subsequent biochemical and histological analyses. We restricted our animal experiments to male mice, because kidney injury and fibrosis are more consistent in males than in females, partly due to hormonal effects (30). In aged female mice, the insult response may differ due to menopause; however, addressing sex-related differences was beyond the scope of this study. Therefore, only male mice were used to ensure consistency and clarity.

Flow cytometry

Kidney tissue was minced and digested with 40 µg/ml collagenase D (catalog number 11088858001; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) for 30 min at 37°C. The digested tissue was then filtered through a 40 µm strainer and centrifuged. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 40% Percoll Plus (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and carefully layered over 80% Percoll Plus. After centrifugation, the middle layer, which contained an enriched population of leukocytes, was collected. The cells were treated with red blood cell lysis buffer, centrifuged, and resuspended in staining buffer supplemented with 2% horse serum and 0.05% sodium azide. To prevent nonspecific binding, the cells were pretreated with an Fc receptor-binding inhibitor for 15 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for 30 min. The samples were analyzed with a BD FACSymphonyTM A3 (BD Biosciences, Milpitas, CA, USA), and data analysis was conducted via FlowJo software (version 10; BD Biosciences). The absolute number of live cells per kidney was determined via trypan blue staining and a C-Chip disposable hemocytometer. The counts of specific immune cell subsets were subsequently derived based on their proportions obtained from flow cytometric analysis (31). The antibodies used for flow cytometry are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry

Kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized using a xylene substitute and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed via heat-induced epitope retrieval, followed by cooling. The sections were then incubated in a blocking solution containing 10% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C. After washing, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured using an ECLIPSE Ci-L microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For Sirius red staining, after deparaffinization and rehydration, tissue sections were stained with the Picrosirius Red Stain Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). The sections were then washed with acetic acid and ethanol before imaging. The antibodies used for immunohistochemistry are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence

The initial steps prior to blocking were identical to those described in the immunohistochemistry protocol. The sections were incubated in a blocking solution containing 10% normal goat or donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was conducted overnight at 4°C. Secondary antibody incubation was conducted for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then quenched and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 15 min. Images were acquired using a Leica STED CW microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Cox5A intensity in F4/80+ cells was analyzed using the colocalization intensity function in Imaris software (version 9.3.1; Bitplane, Zurich, Switzerland). The antibodies used for immunofluorescence are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Real-time quantitative PCR

RNA was extracted from tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands) and from cells using the Direct-zol RNA MicroPrep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). The extracted RNA was then reverse transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa Bio, Inc., Kusatsu, Japan). Gene expression levels were assessed using real-time RT-PCR with the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Inc.) on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems™, Waltham, MA, USA). The expression levels were normalized to those of 18S rRNA, and the mean relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Experiment with cultured bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Bone marrow cells were isolated from 8-wk-old male mice and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer. The resulting cells were seeded in a culture medium containing 10% L929-conditioned medium (28). On days 3 and 6, the cell supernatant was removed and replaced with a fresh 10% L929-conditioned medium. On day 7, the cells were harvested for further analysis. BMDMs were subsequently treated with the indicated concentrations of recombinant mouse Thbs1 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). In experiments involving OXPHOS inhibition, cells were simultaneously treated with metformin (MFM, 0.5 mM) (GlpBio, Montclair, CA, USA) or IACS-010759 (IACS, 1 nM) (Selleckchem). After 24 h of treatment, the cells were collected for subsequent analyses.

scRNA-seq

The data for aged mice were generated in our original study, whereas the data for young mice were obtained from publicly available sources using the same strain (32,33,34,35). After the kidneys were digested with 0.2 mg/ml liberase (Roche) and 64 μg/ml DNase I (Roche) at 37°C for 30 min, libraries were prepared using the chromium controller according to the 10× Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ protocol (version 3.1; 10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Briefly, the cell suspensions were diluted in nuclease-free water to achieve a target count of 10,000. The cell suspension was mixed with master mix and loaded with gel beads and partitioning oil into a Chromium Next GEM chip G. The RNA transcripts were uniquely barcoded and reverse-transcribed within droplets. The cDNA molecules were pooled and subjected to an end repair process, the addition of a single A base, and ligation of the adapters. The products were purified and enriched using PCR to produce the final cDNA library. The purified libraries were qualified using an Agilent 4150 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA); and sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with a read length of 28 bp for read 1 (cell barcode and UMI), an 8-bp index read (sample barcode), and a read length of 91 bp for read 2 (RNA read). The Cell Ranger pipeline (version 7.1.0; 10× Genomics) was used to process the raw dataset.

Downstream analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2; Comprehensive R Archive Network) and the Seurat package (version 5.0.3). For quality control of pooled scRNA-seq data from whole mouse kidneys, genes detected in at least 10 cells were retained, along with cells containing 200–2,500 unique genes and ≤50% mitochondrial gene content. Additional filtering was applied during immune cell subclustering to include only cells with ≤10% mitochondrial genes. Following this quality control, the raw UMI counts were normalized and scaled, and the 3,000 most variable features were identified for principal component analysis, which included 50 dimensions. Data integration was performed using the SCTransform method from the Seurat package, combined with the HarmonyIntegration function. Clustering was achieved through unsupervised methods (FindClusters function), and cluster identity was confirmed through differentially expressed gene analysis (FindAllMarkers function). To explore ligand-receptor interactions, the CellChat package (version 2.1.0) was employed, which enables the characterization of cluster interactions. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the rWikiPathways package (version 1.18.2) to identify upregulated pathways between clusters.

Seahorse metabolic flux assay

The 2.5×104 BMDMs were plated into each well of Seahorse XF96 cell culture microplates (Agilent Technologies) and treated with vehicle, 10 ng/ml Thbs1, or 10 μg/ml anti-CD47 antibody for 12 h. For ATP production rate assays, Seahorse XF DMEM medium was supplemented with 4 mM L-glutamine, 25 mM D-glucose, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. Cells were equilibrated in XF base medium at 37°C for 1 h in the absence of CO2. Quantification of cellular ATP production rates was performed using sequential injections of oligomycin (1.5 μM) and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM). Samples were analyzed with an XFe96 Analyzer, and results were collected using Wave software (version 2.6; Agilent Technologies).

Human kidney samples

Human kidney biopsy samples were collected from 18 patients with nonspecific changes identified on biopsy, normal kidney function, and no proteinuria (Supplementary Table 3). Among these, 11 were in the young age group (younger than 30 years), and 7 were in the old age group (older than 55 years).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The data are expressed as the means ± SEs of the means or as proportions. Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using Student’s t-test, whereas differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way or 2-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05.

Study approval

All of the animal experiments were approved by the Seoul National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (No. 22-0324-S1A1). The use of human samples was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (No. H-2302-124-1408) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki; all of the patients provided written informed consent for the donation and use of their samples.

RESULTS

Subtle cellular injury and inflammation in aged kidneys

Kidney function can be evaluated by changes in blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels in the blood, as well as changes in proteinuria. Even in healthy individuals without any kidney issues, kidney function gradually declines over time, whereas proteinuria remains consistently minimal or absent (1,2,36). Similarly, in mice, renal function may deteriorate, and proteinuria may appear at a very old age (37,38). These findings suggest that subclinical insults may gradually worsen functional indicators. Herein, the mice were raised to 22 months of age without any apparent insults, and the above indicators were compared (Fig. 1A). Because no significant deterioration in these indicators was observed, we confirmed that the kidneys were representative of healthy aged kidneys. As the mice aged, their body weight gradually increased; however, kidney weight and creatinine clearance, as assessed through 24-h urine collection, did not change with age (Fig. 1B). These results indicate that overall kidney function remains well maintained from a macroscopic perspective.

We investigated changes in kidney injury indicators at the cellular level. As representative tubular injury markers, we analyzed the expression levels of neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin and kidney injury molecule-1 (39,40), both of which are significantly upregulated in the tubules of aged kidneys (Fig. 1C and D). Tubulointerstitial fibrosis was overwhelmed in aged kidneys (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the number of apoptotic cells was markedly elevated in aged kidneys, indicating disrupted tissue homeostasis and potential impairment of phagocytic clearance mechanisms (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted impact of aging on kidney injury, fibrosis, and cellular dyshomeostasis.

Immune cell composition in aged kidneys

To investigate the immunological background in aged kidneys with cellular-level changes observed above, we initially explored the features of immune cell subsets. Histological analysis revealed a greater abundance of CD45+ immune cells in aged kidneys than in younger kidneys (Fig. 2A). Using flow cytometry, we identified which immune cell populations were predominantly increased in aged kidneys. We classified the cells based on myeloid cells (e.g., macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells) and lymphoid cells (e.g., CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, NK cells, NK T cells, and B cells). Kidneys harbor 2 subsets of kidney macrophages, namely KRMs and kidney-infiltrating macrophages (KiMs), distinguished by their expression patterns of F4/80 and CD11b. KRMs were identified as CD45+ Ly6G– CD11bint F4/80high, whereas KiMs were characterized as CD45+ Ly6G– CD11bhigh F4/80int (25,27,28,41). Accordingly, the number of myeloid cells, including KRMs, did not change in aged kidneys (Fig. 2B; the gating strategy for these immune cell subsets is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1). However, analysis of lymphoid cell subsets revealed an increase in T-cell subsets, whereas the number of NK cells tended to decrease (Fig. 2C; the gating strategy for these subsets is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1). These changes in immune cell composition may represent one aspect of the aging-related inflammatory process in aged kidneys.

Aging-related alterations in KRMs

Because the number of cells in the KRM subset, the focus of this study, did not change in aged kidneys, we next investigated whether alterations in the KRM phenotype occurred. To this end, scRNA-seq was performed on aged (22-month-old) mice, and their gene signatures were compared with those from young (4 to 8-wk-old) mice obtained from publicly available datasets. After quality control, unsupervised clustering identified distinct clusters for 73,829 cells (Fig. 3A). Immune and parenchymal cell clusters were annotated based on previously established marker gene expression profiles (Fig. 3B) (27,34,42). Aged kidneys harbored a greater proportion of immune cell subsets than young kidneys, at 11.3% and 7.6%, respectively (Fig. 3C). Upon subclustering the macrophage cluster of 1,325 cells, KRMs were identified by their signature genes, including C1qa, Cd81, Cd74, Axl, and Scimp. KiMs were categorized into Ly6chigh and Ly6clow subsets based on Ly6c expression. Other dendritic cell subsets were defined using canonical transcription factors, such as Clec9a, Clec10a, and Irf8. Interestingly, we identified 2 distinct KRM subsets expressing signature genes (Fig. 3D-F). One of these KRM subsets was predominantly present in aged kidneys (Fig. 3G), whereas the other, namely the conventional KRM subset, was similarly distributed between young and aged kidneys (Fig. 3H).

Overall gene expression profile of aged KRM subset was distinct from that of conventional KRMs and other KiMs (Fig. 4A). We compared the gene expression profiles of conventional KRMs and aged KRMs (Fig. 4B). In aged KRMs, the expression of certain signature genes, such as Cd74 and C1qb, was reduced, whereas the expression of genes such as Spinl1, Dbi, and Ttc36 was notably increased. The expression of these genes is associated with stress-induced metabolic shifts (e.g., OXPHOS) or inflammatory signaling (43,44,45,46). Pathway analysis revealed that signaling pathways related to OXPHOS were highly ranked (Fig. 4C; genes in each pathway are listed in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). At the protein level, Cox5a, a representative protein involved in OXPHOS, was also elevated in the KRMs of aged kidneys compared with their counterparts in young kidneys (Fig. 4D).

We examined the crosstalk between parenchymal tissue cells and the conventional and aged KRM subsets (Fig. 4E), identifying strong interactions with the descending thin limb. Molecular ligand-receptor pair analysis between parenchymal tissue cells and KRMs revealed signals from Thbs1 (the mouse homolog of human THBS1), collagen 4, claudin 2, and amyloid precursor protein (Fig. 4F). Among these, we focused on THBS1 signaling, because this molecule is known to regulate macrophage phenotypes (47,48,49,50). THBS1 is also involved in the inflammaging of hematopoietic stem cells (51). However, its role and expression pattern in the context of kidney aging remain largely unexplored. The gene expression of Thbs1 was predominantly observed in the descending thin limb, whereas genes encoding receptors for THBS1, such as Cd47 and Cd36, were expressed in the macrophage cluster (Fig. 4G). Comparative analysis of young and aged kidneys revealed that Thbs1 expression was elevated in the descending thin limb of aged kidneys, whereas receptor expression in macrophages was not different (Fig. 4H). At the protein level, Thbs1 expression was also greater in the tubules of aged kidneys than in those of young kidneys (Fig. 4I and J). These findings highlight that aged kidneys harbor a distinct KRM subset characterized by OXPHOS, driven by THBS1 signaling from crosstalk with surrounding tubules.

Inflammatory shift in macrophages caused by THBS1 signaling

To investigate whether THBS1 signaling enhances OXPHOS in macrophages and induces a shift toward an inflammatory phenotype, we treated BMDMs with recombinant mouse Thbs1 protein. The gene expression of Cox5a and Cox8a, key components of OXPHOS, was upregulated in a concentration-dependent manner following Thbs1 treatment (Fig. 5A). These results support the link between THBS1 signaling and OXPHOS in aged KRMs, as identified through scRNA-seq analysis. To explore the impact of THBS1 signaling on cytokine production, we analyzed the gene expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10, which are representative cytokines in BMDMs (52,53). Thbs1 treatment notably increased the gene expression of IL-1β and IL-10 in BMDMs (Fig. 5B). This pattern was similar to that observed for gene and protein expression in mouse kidneys (Fig. 5C-E). Energetic map was generated to visualize changes in OXPHOS and glycolysis, which showed that Thbs1 treatment increased both pathways in BMDMs (Fig. 5F). Subsequently, we employed OXPHOS inhibitors, including MFM and IACS (54,55), both of which reduced the expression of OXPHOS-related genes (Fig. 5G). These agents also abrogated the expression of cytokine genes such as Il1b and Il10 (Fig. 5H). Notably, these changes occurred in the opposite direction to those induced by Thbs1 treatment, suggesting that THBS1-driven OXPHOS activation contributes, at least in part, to the regulation of cytokine expression in macrophages.

To validate these findings in human kidneys, we analyzed kidney biopsy samples from healthy individuals with no structural abnormalities or normal kidney function and divided them into young (under 30 years old) and old (55 years and older) age groups (Supplementary Table 3). The OXPHOS status of CD68+ human kidney macrophages was first evaluated, revealing increased COX5A intensity in the old age group compared with the young age group (Fig. 5I). Compared with those in the young age group, THBS1 and IL-1β expression in the tubular and interstitial regions, respectively, were elevated in the old age group (Fig. 5J and K). Notably, although IL-6 expression was elevated in some kidneys from the old age group, overall expression did not differ between the 2 age groups (Supplementary Fig. 2), and IL-10 was not detected in the interstitial area of these normal human kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, these results demonstrate that THBS1 signaling drives OXPHOS and cytokine production in KRMs, potentially contributing to the metabolic changes and inflammatory milieu observed in aged kidneys.

DISCUSSION

The inflammatory state of aged kidneys increases their susceptibility to acute injuries, but the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain poorly understood, particularly in relation to KRMs. A distinct subset of KRMs in aged kidneys, referred to as aged KRMs, exhibits metabolic reprogramming toward OXPHOS. Pathway analysis revealed that THBS1, which is expressed in the tubules surrounding age kidneys, is a key upstream regulator of these metabolic changes. Through this signaling pathway, aged KRMs promote the production of cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-10, contributing to the proinflammatory milieu in aged kidneys. These key molecular components are also reflected in aged human kidneys.

An inflammatory milieu develops with aging in various organs beyond the kidney, and tissue-resident immune cells may play a significant role in this process. In aged lungs treated with lipopolysaccharide, resident alveolar macrophages predominantly express proinflammatory cytokines and facilitate neutrophil infiltration (56). Aging reduces the ability of alveolar macrophages to mitigate lung damage during influenza infection and impairs their capacity to phagocytose apoptotic neutrophils (57). Aged macrophages are prone to secrete high levels of proinflammatory cytokines, further exacerbating the inflammatory conditions (58). Our findings in aged kidneys align with these observations, indicating that aged kidneys are vulnerable to acute injuries and may exhibit impaired recovery, resulting in a lack of effective homeostatic regulation (59). While IL-10 is generally regarded as an anti-inflammatory cytokine, its increase in the aged kidney does not necessarily indicate that active reparative processes are occurring. Rather, the concurrent upregulation of other cytokines, such as IL-1β, suggests that overall tissue homeostasis is shifting toward a proinflammatory state characteristic of inflammaging. Indeed, certain studies have suggested that elevated IL-10 in inflammatory contexts can paradoxically exacerbate inflammation (60,61). Therefore, IL-10 may also contribute to the progression of inflammaging when it increases alongside IL-1β, although fully elucidating these mechanisms is beyond the scope of the present study.

Among the key roles of macrophages, cytokine production has been reported to depend on OXPHOS (62). While previous studies suggest that OXPHOS is closely associated with anti-inflammatory responses and glycolysis with proinflammatory cytokine production (62), these associations may vary depending on the specific organ context (63,64,65). In our study, resident macrophages in aged kidneys exhibiting an OXPHOS-oriented metabolic profile showed increased expression of both proinflammatory (e.g., IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory (e.g., IL-10) cytokines. Interestingly, IL-6 expression remained unchanged, suggesting that OXPHOS-associated alterations in KRMs were selectively modulated by the aged renal microenvironment. These findings highlight that local cues within the tissue microenvironment may shape the metabolic and functional phenotypes of macrophages, including shifts toward or away from aerobic OXPHOS.

THBS1 mediates tissue inflammation and fibrosis, suggesting therapeutic implications across multiple organ systems. In diabetic cardiomyopathy, THBS1 acts as a central mediator of fibrotic complications, driven by the activation of the renin-angiotensin system (66). In the lungs, THBS1 promotes IL-10 production by alveolar macrophages via interactions with CD36 upon recognition of apoptotic neutrophils (50). In chronic kidney disease, THBS1 engages with CD36 to drive macrophage activation and fibrosis through increased proinflammatory signaling and oxidative stress (67,68). In diabetic kidneys, THBS1 induces podocyte apoptosis and dysfunction, thereby accelerating glomerular damage (69). From a therapeutic perspective, inhibiting THBS1-dependent activation of transforming growth factor-β reduces fibrosis progression in both kidneys and hearts (69,70). THBS1 regulates dynamin-related protein-1 signaling in ischemia-reperfusion injury in the aging heart (71). THBS1 deficiency also alleviates obesity-induced inflammation and improves insulin sensitivity, suggesting applications in treating metabolic syndrome (72). THBS1 overproduction triggers interferon responses in aged hematopoietic stem cells (51). The findings of this study support the impact of THBS1 signaling on immunological homeostasis in kidneys, particularly emphasizing its novel effects on aged KRMs. Furthermore, the validation of these mechanistic insights in human kidneys introduces another innovative dimension to this field.

In conclusion, this study highlights the critical role of aged KRMs in mediating inflammatory and metabolic alterations in aged kidneys. Through reprogramming toward OXPHOS driven by THBS1 signaling, aged KRMs promote a proinflammatory milieu that increases the risk of tissue injury and impaired homeostasis. Based on these findings, targeting THBS1 signaling in aged kidneys may represent a potential approach to modulate KRM reprogramming, thereby alleviating aging-associated kidney dysfunction and preserving kidney health.
