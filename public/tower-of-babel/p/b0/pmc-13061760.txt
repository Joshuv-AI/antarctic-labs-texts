Redox Regulation of the NLRP3 Inflammasome and its Implications for Immunization-related Neuroinflammation
Authors: Todd Terhune, Taravat Ghafourian, Rebecca Eshraghi, Adrien Eshraghi, Richard Deth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immunization is a critical public health intervention for limiting the impact of infectious diseases for individuals and for the population as a whole. While vaccination causes minor to moderate side effects for most, a smaller number of individuals suffer from unexpected reactogenicity and long-term effects, due to the unintended induction of an atypical inflammatory response. We hypothesize that the neurological sequelae of vaccine-induced inflammation can disproportionately affect people who have impaired cysteine status and antioxidant availability. Decreased antioxidant buffering capacity allows vaccine-induced inflammation to persist at a higher level and for a longer duration. Antioxidants are necessary to combat reactive oxygen species (ROS), produced by aerobic respiration, and when ROS levels are not effectively managed by cells, oxidative stress occurs. Oxidative stress is important for activation of the nucleotide-binding domain leucine-rich repeat [LRR] and pyrin-containing receptor 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and its downstream consequences, including actions of the cytokine interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). Activation of NLRP3 by oxidative stress is well appreciated, and we propose that oxidative stress from vaccines promotes NLRP3 activity and IL-1β release. This hypothesis article discusses this immunometabolic mechanistic cascade in-depth in the context of intensified short-term vaccine side effects, as well as serious inflammatory and neurological sequelae experienced by more vulnerable individuals. Research directions and alternative practices are suggested. The novel viewpoints presented here are meant to bring about changes that reduce the risk of adverse effects.

Introduction

Population-wide immunization programs play a vital role in public health by providing both individual immunity and decreasing the spread of communicable diseases. While this approach has proved safe and effective for a large majority of individuals and has prevented millions of severe illnesses and deaths [1], both beneficial and adverse consequences of immune activation are not shared equally among different subpopulations. The overall positive health impact of population-wide vaccination programs and the trust of general public in vaccine safety could be improved if the factors leading to adverse outcomes were better understood. Recent discoveries about the redox-dependent regulation of the nucleotide-binding domain leucine-rich repeat [LRR] and pyrin-containing receptor 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome allow a mechanistic examination of how variability in its activation in response to vaccination might lead to enhanced vaccine side effects.

Effective endogenous production of neutralizing antibodies in a high proportion of the public (i.e., herd immunity) takes priority when formulating public health policies for high-risk infectious diseases, especially during pandemics. Therefore, despite substantial benefit/risk assessments from clinical trial data during vaccine development, it is important to investigate mechanisms of unwanted side effects from vaccination and the potential impact on specifically affected subpopulations. Thus, while it is critical to define subpopulations at higher risk of serious disease from a threatening virus (e.g., the elderly, persons with diabetes, etc.), it is equally important to define subpopulations with an elevated risk of adverse vaccination effects. Knowledge of specific risk-associated factors could identify these subpopulations and lead to more optimal targeting of vaccine interventions through risk biomarker evaluation and the design of improved vaccines that optimize adaptive immune responses.

Here we focus on some of the mechanisms through which certain side effects of vaccination may originate, with an emphasis on the effects of adjuvant-induced inflammation, also known as reactogenicity. We hypothesize that individuals with impaired adaptive antioxidant responses by the nuclear factor (erythroid-derived 2)-like 2 (Nrf2) system are at higher risk of undesired vaccine consequences due to increased NLRP3 activation. Unlike older vaccines that were initially made from whole microorganisms, many modern vaccines contain refined DNA, RNA, or proteins as the antigen or antigen-producing elements to generate an immune response and immunity. These modifications, aiming to improve the safety profile and ease of manufacture, have resulted in reduced immunogenicity. Therefore, vaccines are frequently formulated with the addition of immunogenic adjuvants of various inorganic and organic natures that mimic pathogen-associated molecular patterns and function to stimulate the innate immune response to enhance the activation of adaptive immunity. Aluminum salts have been widely used as adjuvants since the 1930 s and may include amorphous aluminum hydroxyphosphate sulfate (AAHS), aluminum hydroxide, aluminum phosphate, or potassium aluminum sulfate (Alum) [2]. As a major component of the innate immune system targeted by adjuvants, the NLRP3 inflammasome is widely acknowledged to initiate cytokine production upon vaccination [2–4]. Attempts have been made to establish the efficacy and safety of these adjuvants in humans using reviews and meta-analyses of clinical data [5]. However, conclusions from vaccine studies are complicated because most clinical trials use adjuvants in both the experimental and control groups [5, 6]. In addition, vaccine clinical trials normally recruit volunteers from healthy populations and lack focus on specific vulnerable populations. Such focused studies are becoming even more crucial given the increasing impact of infectious diseases and the subsequent increase in the frequency and variety of vaccinations, including the recent SARS-CoV-2 pandemic and vaccinations.

Notably, a Center for Disease Control (CDC)-sponsored study published in 2023 [7] showed that aluminum adjuvants are associated with a dose-related increased risk of developing asthma, indicating induction of a T-helper 2 cell (Th2)-biased immune response in a vulnerable subpopulation. While inflammation is a normal and unavoidable component of an effective immune response to vaccination, this study raises important questions, including: (1) What factors are responsible for the increased inflammatory response of this vulnerable subpopulation? (2) What additional adverse outcomes might result from inflammation provoked by adjuvant-containing vaccinations? (3) What risk mitigation steps can be taken to support safer vaccination of vulnerable subpopulations?

Vaccine Safety

Vaccines are widely regarded as safe, but it is important to acknowledge that the immune system is highly nuanced, as are its responses to different types of vaccines. For example, some vaccines may provide long-term health benefits unrelated to their primary purpose. The Bacillus Calmette-Guérin vaccine (commonly referred to as the BCG vaccine) given for tuberculosis outside the United States, is associated with several additional positive effects [8], and the influenza vaccine has been linked with a lower risk of dementia [9]. The COVID-19 vaccination campaign was successful at preventing deaths from SARS-CoV-2 infection [10] and it was recently reported that it may prolong survival in cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy [11]. However, an increased incidence of myocarditis among young men upon receiving a second dose of the vaccine was also reported [12]. This highlights that few pharmacological interventions are entirely without risk.

The most obvious and common adverse effects of vaccines are their short-term side effects, which may include injection site swelling and pain, along with flu-like symptoms, such as fever, nausea, and fatigue. Less commonly, patients may even experience post-vaccination syncope. Febrile seizures are known to occur in infants and children 5 to 12 days after measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) and MMR and varicella (MMRV) vaccines. Certain groups of people with underlying conditions may experience severe symptoms or reactogenicity. For example, those with a particular human leukocyte antigen polymorphism (HLA-A*03:01) have an elevated risk for experiencing more intense side effects from the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine [13]. Critically, vaccination has been temporally associated with the onset of acute life-threatening reactions, such as Guillain-Barré syndrome [14, 15] and transverse myelitis [16–18]. Additionally, while meta-analysis and systematic reviews did not observe a correlation between vaccination and autism risk [19, 20], several studies have detected a positive association [21, 22]. Overall, however, there is a lack of concrete clinical evidence to clarify such a connection. Whether vaccines influence any aspect of neurodevelopment, with either benign or deleterious effects, remains unclear. Despite this, it is evident that the pro-inflammatory effects of vaccination can alert the brain to the presence peripheral inflammation via several afferent pathways and that the onset of certain autoimmune conditions (e.g., transverse myelitis), also indicates that overt neuroinflammation is possible following vaccination.

Knowledge of the factors that predispose certain individuals to experience adverse effects could open the way to increasing the safety profile of vaccines. We hypothesize that factors involved in vaccine reactogenicity and sequelae are related to the metabolic-based regulation of NLRP3 inflammasomes and are mediated through the key cytokine interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and its downstream effects. This article elaborates upon these factors and includes potential directions to mitigate the likelihood of adverse vaccination reactions below.

Introduction to NLRP3 Inflammasomes, Interleukin-1β, and their Implications for Vaccination

Inflammation and the formation of cytokine-generating inflammasome complexes are crucial components of the defense mechanism against pathogens and a key event in the innate immune response to vaccination [23–25]. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, an important factor in controlling viral and bacterial infections [26, 27], has also been identified as an early component of the immune response to vaccination. As illustrated in Fig. 1, initial priming of the NLRP3 response upon antigen exposure involves recognition by toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NF-κB-mediated transcription of genes coding for inflammasome components, including NLRP3, ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain), procaspase-1, in addition to pre-processed cytokines pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18. NLRP3 activity involves caspase 1-mediated cleavage to release active cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 [28]. Inflammasomes can also participate in the release of IL-1α [29, 30]. Multiple signals promote NLRP3 activity, including potassium efflux [31], which can be triggered by the ATP-activated P2X7 purinergic receptor (P2X7R) [32] and oxidative stress [33], a metabolic condition commonly associated with inadequate levels of the thiol antioxidant glutathione (GSH). Blockade of P2X7Rs with the anti-purinergic drug suramin decreases activation of NLRP3 inflammasome complexes, which may explain its positive effects in some children with autism [34]. NLRP3 activity is also regulated by several post-translational modifications, including ubiquitination [35], phosphorylation [36], and S-palmitoylation [37]. The most well-known of the proteins processed by NLRP3, IL-1β, is one of the main pro-inflammatory cytokines upregulated by aluminum-based vaccine adjuvants [38]. This cytokine is normally expressed at low levels in the body and its elevated release by leukocytes during exposure to immunological stressors is NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent. IL-1β is a crucial downstream effector of NLRP3 following vaccination and its production is predictive of vaccine side effect intensity [39]. However, it is important to recognize that NLRP3 activation and IL-1β production are not essential for the immunity-promoting action of aluminum-based vaccine adjuvants but can be considered to be a source of inflammation-related reactogenicity [40, 41]. Notably, IL-1β and NLRP3 expression have been reported to be increased in neuropsychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders and have been correlated with disorder subtype, severity, or outcome [42–49]. Additionally, NLRP3, P2X7R, IL-1β, and interleukin-1 type 1 receptor (IL-1R1) have been implicated as being contributing factors in animal models of neurodevelopmental disorders [50–52], manic behavior [53], stress-sensitization, depression, and anxiety [54–63]. When the foregoing findings are considered together, there appears to be a relationship between IL-1β and central nervous system (CNS) disorders of both short- and long-term duration.

IL-1β is important for neurodevelopment and proper CNS function but high levels can reflect pathological events [64, 65]. IL-1β binds to IL-1R1 which is present on many mammalian cell types [66]. Its binding depends on heterodimerization of IL-1R1 with interleukin-1 receptor accessory protein (IL1RAP). IL-1β causes both cell type-specific and systemic effects. Cell type-specific effects of IL-1β include regulation of neurotransmission [69–72], neurotransmitter receptor expression [73], and synaptic adhesion [74]. IL-1β exerts systemic effects via changes in hormonal systems, metabolic states, and redox status. Importantly, the cytokine transduces a pro-inflammatory signal from the periphery to the CNS through endothelial cells of the blood–brain barrier [75, 76]. It is the propagation of this message that leads to more observable behavioral influences of IL-1β. IL-1β stimulates the pyrogenic febrile response [77] and induces sickness behaviors [78]. The NLRP3 inflammasome is activated in brain leukocytes (i.e., microglia) by peripheral inflammatory stimuli, thus creating an inflammatory response in the CNS that mirrors peripheral inflammation, marked by increased IL-1β [79, 80]. For example, IL-1β-exposed endothelial cells increase expression of hyaluronidase enzymes, which cleave high-molecular weight hyaluronic acid to low-molecular weight species that are suspected to contribute to microglial activation [76], which includes upregulation of microglial IL-1β mRNA expression [76]. Furthermore, peripheral IL-1β mediates sickness behaviors through brain endothelial cell cyclooxygenase-2 [75, 81, 82]. When the diverse roles of IL-1β are considered, it is likely that NLRP3-depdendent adverse effects from vaccination require this cytokine. Since NLRP3 inflammasomes serve as the rate-limiting determinant for the post-vaccination systemic elevation of IL-1β, we propose that a promising resolution towards preventing adverse vaccine effects can be achieved through an enhanced appreciation of the influence of redox status on NLRP3 activation.

NLRP3, Oxidative Stress, and Inflammation

Healthy cells balance reactive oxygen species (ROS) production with the synthesis and regeneration of small molecule antioxidants, as well as with enzymes that perform antioxidant, detoxification, and repair functions. ROS production is vital for maintaining homeostasis, in part because it participates in post-translational modifications that modify protein activity. High aerobic metabolism in the brain makes it especially dependent upon antioxidant resources. Accessible cysteine residues in many proteins allow the prevailing redox status within a cell or subcellular compartment to exert significant control over protein function [83]. The pKa for protein thiols is normally in the range of 8.2–9.9, with the reduced thiol (-SH) state predominating over the oxidized thiolate (-S−) state at physiological intracellular pH. However, thiol reactivity is subject to prevailing redox status, and the highly reactive thiolate (S−) state is more prevalent under conditions of oxidative stress (Fig. 2C). Inflammation and redox status are closely intertwined. Typically, the presence of pro-inflammatory cytokines, pathogens, or sterile inflammatory stimuli activate responses that generate higher ROS levels. Many proteins involved in pro-inflammatory pathways and their mediators have oxidizable cysteine residues that regulate immune cell activity [85, 86]. Immune activation by the antigens and adjuvants found in vaccines creates a more pro-oxidative state, permitting a full response by these pathways. NLRP3 itself and other inflammasome components are redox-regulated and share a sensitivity to oxidative stress [86]. S-glutathionylation of cysteine residues on NLRP3 [87] or in inflammasome-associated components caspase-1 [88], NEK-7 (NIMA-related kinase-7) [89] and the linker protein ASC [90], serves to suppress NLRP3 activity. Intracellular levels of GSH dramatically decrease upon P2X7R activation by extracellular ATP and this decrease is essential for NLRP3 activation, while higher extracellular GSH decreases NLRP3 activation [91]. ROS are crucial for gasdermin D pore formation, contributing to pyroptotic cell death [92, 93]. It should also be noted that the extracellular signaling by IL-1β is inhibited by excessive ROS, but is permitted by ROS-dependent S-glutathionylation, which protects its functionality [94]. Extrusion of thioredoxin and peroxiredoxin upon NLRP3 activation also promotes oxidative stress [95]. Thus, multiple redox-related mechanisms combine to initiate and maintain NLRP3-mediated inflammation and downstream IL-1β activity.

Examination of the cysteine-rich leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain of NLRP3 reveals a molecular mechanism by which redox status can regulate its activity. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, highly conserved LRR cysteine residues exist in a regular pattern, corresponding to the sequences of its multiple repeats, which are approximately 56 amino acids in length. Within the NLRP3 LRR structure [96], these repeating cysteine residues form four bands (Fig. 2B) whereby they can potentially react with other thiols to form disulfide linkages. Indeed, it was reported that S-palmitoylation of a vicinal Cys-Cys pair (Cys837/Cys838) in the NLRP3 LRR facilitates binding of NEK7 and is required for NLRP3 activation [97] (Fig. 2C), while S-palmitoylation of other vicinal Cys-Cys pairs may promote autophagy of the NLRP3 inflammasome [98]. S-palmitoylation of NLRP3 appears to be a highly complex mode of redox-based regulation [37].

As illustrated in Fig. 2C, NLRP3 cysteine residues can be modified by S-glutathionylation, persulfidation, and S-palmitoylation, with S-glutathionylation and S-persulfidation serving to decrease cytokine release and inflammation, while S-palmitoylation promotes inflammasome activity. An additional form of post-translational modification is N-homocysteinylation. Intriguingly, N-homocysteinylation of specific lysine residues in NLRP3 blocks its degradation and a recently characterized anti-inflammatory metabolite homocysitaconate, which is increased by LPS, decreases NLRP3 N-homocysteinylation [99].

This recently recognized mode of thiol-based regulation provides a detailed cysteine-based molecular mechanism and helps to explain how oxidative stress promotes inflammasome activity. Moreover, it is consistent with studies showing that other inflammasome proteins, including ASC, NEK7 and caspase-1, must first be de-glutathionylated for NLRP3 to be active [87–90]. This redox-based mechanism is also consistent with the observation that a dramatic decrease in the antioxidant GSH upon ATP activation is essential for NLRP3 inflammasome activation [91], along with other redox-related metabolic changes [100].

Since NLRP3 activation is promoted by oxidative stress, it follows that individuals exhibiting oxidative stress may be more sensitive to inflammation-provoking stimuli, such as vaccination, and should therefore be recognized as a vulnerable subpopulation for untoward consequences. Oxidative insults tend to provoke alterations in methionine cycle, transsulfuration, and glutathione pathway metabolite levels. This is often reflected as decreased SAM, cystathionine, cysteine, and GSH, along with increased HCY and GSSG [101, 102]. Such changes are a highly replicable finding in certain disorders with pronounced oxidative stress, including autism [103, 104].

Nrf2, Cysteine, and the Resolution of Inflammation

In addition to cytokine production, acute inflammasome activation downstream of oxidative stress initiates broad metabolic reprogramming, associated with significant changes in DNA methylation and gene expression. Notably, a study of the humoral immune response to influenza vaccination found a strong correlation between differential DNA methylation and changes in gene expression involving metabolic pathways providing glutathione regulation [105]. Post-vaccination changes in gene expression are normally associated with resolution of the acute inflammatory process, which involves activation of NFE2L2, the gene encoding the antioxidant transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2). Thus, oxidative stress turns on NLRP3 activity, while the subsequent antioxidant effects of Nrf2 are important for turning it off. However, a couple of studies have reported NLRP3 activation and IL-1β release to be impaired in Nrf2 knockout animals [106, 107]. It remains unclear whether those results are adaptive responses to the developmental absence of Nrf2.

Nrf2 activity increases in response to oxidative stress and exerts a broad range of transcriptional activity that serves to normalize redox status and decrease inflammation. Nrf2 augments expression of the cysteine uptake transporter EAAT3 (excitatory amino acid transporter 3) and the cystine-glutamate antiporter xCT, as well as transsulfuration pathway enzymes cystathionine beta synthase (CBS) and cystathionine gamma lyase, also known as cystathionase (CSE) [108–111]. These actions combine to increase intracellular cysteine availability for both GSH synthesis and for CBS and CSE-mediated production of hydrogen sulfide (H2S), which promotes persulfide formation at protein thiols, and several studies have reported inhibitory effects of H2S on NLRP3 activity [112, 113]. Indeed, a 2023 study demonstrated the importance of Nrf2, cysteine, and persulfide formation in suppressing prolonged inflammation after LPS-induced NLRP3 activation [114]. They found that early activation of Nrf2 by oxidative stress up-regulated xCT expression and cystine uptake, leading to increased formation of persulfide metabolites, which terminated IL-1β production. The importance of Nrf2-induced xCT activity and persulfidation of cysteine residues to inhibit inflammasome activity was recently confirmed in a study which directly demonstrated persulfidation of NLRP3 cysteine residues [115]. Taken together, these observations indicate that availability of sulfur-containing metabolites is crucial for limiting and terminating NLRP3-mediated inflammation and that Nrf2 activity is an important factor for mobilizing these metabolites.

Based upon these recent insights, it is apparent that NLRP3 inflammasome activity may be more prominent and incompletely suppressed in individuals exhibiting low Nrf2 activity and low cysteine levels, which could lead to a state of chronic inflammation. In macrophages, activation of pro-inflammatory pathways induces expression of aconitate decarboxylase 1 or (ACOD1), which produces itaconate from the Krebs cycle intermediated cis-aconitate. Itaconate reacts broadly with protein thiols and promotes Nrf2-depndent transcription by binding to kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (keap1) cysteine residues [116]. Itaconate also dampens NLRP3 activity after prolonged LPS exposure, playing an important role in the resolution of inflammation [117], and suppression of itaconate can contribute to chronic inflammation. A similar metabolic mode of negative regulation of NLRP3 inflammasomes occurs via nitric oxide [118–120] generated downstream of pro-inflammatory stimulus-mediated transcription of the nitric oxide synthase 2 gene or inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). Nitric oxide can inhibit protein S-palmitoylation by S-nitrosylating cysteine residues [121], which could be how it interferes with NLRP3 activation. It can furthermore promote Nrf2 activity by suppressing keap1 [122]. Thus, nitric oxide and itaconate may share certain immunological roles. Although some metabolic factors can limit NLRP3 activity during acute inflammation in a Nrf2-dependent manner, the Nrf2 response can become blunted. For example, chronic conditions with elevated cytokines or high basal ROS levels are commonly associated with reduced Nrf2 expression and function [123–127]. Thus, recognition of the influence of oxidative stress in promoting NLRP3 activity provides a framework for a better understanding of factors that can contribute to excessive activation of inflammation following vaccination.

Implications

A wide range of contemporary environmental factors (food and air quality, toxic exposures etc.) can converge to deplete antioxidant resources within and across generations, progressively increasing the prevalence of individuals with oxidative stress and related impairment of methylation [128]. As summarized in Fig. 3, we propose that while vaccination-induced inflammation is normally mild and self-limiting, individuals with abnormally low antioxidant resources (i.e. low cysteine, low GSH, and/or an impaired Nrf2 response) exhibit a higher level of NLRP3-mediated inflammasome activation in response to provocative stimuli and particularly in response to adjuvanted vaccines, which are known to promote NLRP3 activity. We also propose that augmented acute responses in this vulnerable subgroup are also accompanied by an inability to fully suppress cytokine production to below threshold levels, due to deficient antioxidant resources and low Nrf2 activity, leading to a chronic inflammatory state (Fig. 3). Thus, activation of inflammation by vaccine antigens provides an acute triggering event for a metabolically vulnerable subgroup of individuals, and the inclusion of oxidative stress-promoting aluminum adjuvants further augments their risk of chronic inflammation. NLRP3 activation and inflammation are associated with numerous disorders, suggesting an important underlying role for environmental factors such as oxidative stress which interact with genetic factors to determine individual risk. It is also crucial to appreciate that the immune system of infants and young children differs from that of adults, although in many cases the difference in their vaccines is simply a reduction in dose. In early life, the immune response is shifted toward a Th2 pattern associated with hypersensitivity and allergic responses [129], which may partially explain the findings of Daley et al. Future Directions for Research and Practice

The perspective we present herein is subject to several limitations. First and foremost, it is a hypothesis that must be subjected to rigorous testing and verification. For example, studies should evaluate the magnitude and duration of vaccination-induced inflammasome activation and inflammation in animals with normal versus depleted thiol resources, including a comparison of aluminum-adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccines. Increased oxidative stress markers and decreased antioxidant enzyme levels have been reported following vaccination [130, 131]. Additional studies will be needed to delineate the role of oxidative stress in determining the magnitude and duration of NLRP3 activation following vaccination with and without adjuvanted vaccines and the types of adjuvants. Such research should include adequately powered prospective studies to examine the benefits and limitations of supplementing antioxidant resources before and/or after vaccine administration for at-risk populations. Family history of long-lasting inflammatory states (such as long COVID) and/or prior vaccination experience provide an opportunity to identify and prioritize at-risk individuals for testing and/or intervention, along with other factors such as hypersensitivity/allergy, gastrointestinal dysfunction, casein/gluten intolerance, etc.

New practices and advancements in vaccine technology should be adopted to ensure desired vaccination outcomes by minimizing the chance of unanticipated sequalae for those who seek immunization. New practices could include pre-screening of potentially at-risk individuals to assess antioxidant status and/or biomarkers of inflammation. Identification of genetic and epigenetic factors predisposing to excessive reactogenicity could also help identify at-risk individuals who would benefit from mitigation strategies. Delaying certain vaccinations until older childhood age is followed in areas outside of the United States, which may decrease adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. However, careful consideration based on epidemiological risk–benefit analysis is needed for determining the age at which each vaccine is best administered. Vaccine formulations should also be adjusted to minimize the potential for excessive inflammation (reactogenicity), including the eventual replacement of aluminum-based adjuvants, which are known to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, with alternatives that do not cause NLRP3 activation [132]. Precaution should be taken when replacing aluminum with other adjuvants to ensure that these adjuvants do not unintentionally activate receptor for advanced glycation endproducts (RAGE), which can stimulate NLRP3 activity [133].

New advancements in vaccine technology may also be of significant value. One particularly promising advancement could be to change the dosing strategy by modifying the kinetics of antigen and adjuvant exposure to produce immunity with less inflammation. Preconditioning is a broad term for an adaptive phenomena whereby a small or tolerable dose of a biological or chemical insult strengthens the resilience of an organism to harsher conditions [134]. Preconditioning with low doses of an antigen or adjuvant over a couple of days may increase anti-inflammatory responses and/or diminish pro-inflammatory responses, while also training the immune system to recognize the antigen of interest. An intriguing type of immune system preconditioning, known as euflammation, involves administration of escalating doses of an antigen over a couple of days. In mice, euflammation prevented large increases in CNS cytokine mRNA, as well as sickness behaviors that would normally appear after sufficient doses of LPS or E. Coli [135–137]. Euflammation and related immune preconditioning paradigms may promote a neuroprotective phenotype [138], which makes it an appealing technique to explore for informing novel human vaccination strategies. Future studies should assess whether euflammation and related preconditioning methods lead to an adaptive immune response. A caveat of euflammation is that it is generally not practical for patients to be given injections over several days. However, this could be overcome with a single injection of micro- or nanoparticles that temporarily remain in circulation and engage in timed release of a specific number of antigens while dissolving.

Mitigating the impact of NLRP3 activation in susceptible patients is already attainable. Drugs that inhibit gasdermin D, such as dimethyl fumarate and disulfiram, are already used for other health conditions and could be repurposed as needed for individuals affected by excessive reactogenicity. Mild antagonism of IL-1β signaling downstream of its processing by NLRP3 inflammasomes with already existing drugs would be another approach. Notably, the anti-IL-1β drug canakinumab did not impair antibody development in a trial of patients receiving influenza or meningococcal vaccination [139]. Pharmacological inhibitors of IL-1β/IL-1R1 signaling have been characterized [140] and could become a less expensive alternative to anti-IL-1β or anti-IL-1R1 antibodies for reducing vaccine reactogenicity and sequalae.

Some evidence suggests that antioxidants may both lower immune cell NLRP3 activity and decrease the propagation of peripheral inflammation into brain parenchymal tissue [76, 141–143]. Given the importance of oxidative stress for NLRP3 inflammasome activity, sulfur amino acid repletion or targeted antioxidant therapy prior to vaccine injection may represent a potential metabolic point of intervention to address vaccine sequalae and reactogenicity. Targeting antioxidants towards endothelial cells of the blood–brain barrier can decrease the probability of propagating peripheral to CNS immune signals even with peripheral NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Such considerations invoke the question of whether it is feasible to identify higher-risk individuals with low antioxidant resources prior to vaccination and/or to reduce their risk of excessive inflammation? Identification of risk-related SNPs in Nrf2, NLRP3, or other inflammasome-related genes may be possible as genome sequencing/genotyping becomes more generally accessible. Unfortunately, laboratory measurement of plasma cysteine/GSH redox status is not a routine clinical assay and currently is largely restricted to research studies. However, Jones and colleagues demonstrated a direct relationship between levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in healthy human subjects and the plasma cysteine redox potential, as determined by the concentration of reduced cysteine to cystine, its oxidized disulfide form [144]. Moreover, in animal studies they showed that dietary supplementation of sulfur amino acids cystine and methionine decreased IL-1β and TNF-α levels after LPS treatment [145]. These findings illustrate a potential metabolic approach to mitigate the risk of excessive inflammation in susceptible individuals and/or effectively treat their inflammation-related sequelae by supplementing thiol resources. Additional approaches could include promotion of Nrf2 activity or decreasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) with antioxidants. Development and administration of novel treatments may necessitate attention towards preventing a transition towards a state of reductive- or antioxidative stress, in which there is an excessive abundance of antioxidants [146, 147]. Like oxidative stress, reductive stress has also been observed in inflammatory conditions, although it can cause impaired immune activity, since ROS are important for intracellular signaling and neutralizing pathogens [146, 147]. Therefore, any new adjunctive or preventative therapeutics developed will need to be designed around maintaining ROS and antioxidant levels within an acceptable range, which may require longitudinal monitoring of one or more oxidative stress biomarker(s) [146] in patient blood cells.

Promotion of NLRP3 activity by oxidative stress is likely to play a prominent role in a wide range of poorly understood inflammation-related conditions, such as myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), long COVID, Alzheimer’s disease, and other disorders associated with aging. Indeed, a progressive age-dependent decrease in cysteine/cystine and GSH/GSSG redox potentials forms the basis for a “Redox Theory of Aging” [145]. While epigenetic-based metabolic adaptations triggered by oxidative stress and decreased methylation may serve to maintain redox status within a desirable homeostatic range over the lifespan, exhaustion of these compensatory mechanisms can result in pathological consequences, including activation of inflammation.

Conclusions

Recent advances in our understanding of NLRP3 inflammasome regulation by oxidative stress require a re-examination of the relationship between redox status, inflammasome activation, and vaccination. We hypothesize that antioxidant impairments may be a significant contributing factor to disparate effects of vaccination among the population. By acknowledging and addressing this concern, vaccination programs can be optimized to better support vulnerable subgroups, while preserving their benefit for the general population. A thorough mechanism-based understanding of the potential adverse consequences of vaccinations in stratified populations could facilitate fine-tuning and personalization of this crucial public health measure, thereby increasing public confidence and optimizing the risk versus benefit profile.
