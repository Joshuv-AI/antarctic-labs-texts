Advancing early diagnosis of colorectal anastomotic leakage using drain fluid biochemical analysis
Authors: Robert Polak, Pavel Prokop, Jachym Rosendorf, Jiri Molacek, Richard Palek, Ondrej Vycital, Jan Bruha, Daniel Rajdl, Vaclav Liska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anastomotic leakage is a common and very serious complication in colorectal surgery. While minor leaks can often be managed conservatively, advanced leaks with peritonitis require surgical reintervention. Leakage is frequently diagnosed late, which we attribute to the absence of an effective screening method and a standardized postoperative protocol. The current classification of leaks is predominantly retrospective and descriptive, offering limited clinical utility. Our goal is to develop a functional postoperative protocol, and a new clinical classification of leaks. In this initial study, we focused on advancing early diagnosis of leakage, by incorporating biochemical analysis of drain fluids. As early markers we chose Calprotectin, Neopterin and D-Lactate. We included 96 patients into this study. We analyzed the drainage fluids during the first postoperative days and compared concentrations of markers between groups with and without leakage. We proved a statistically significant difference in concentrations and its dynamics in the first postoperative days in calprotectin in the leakage group. We further report a statistically significant difference in concentration of calprotectin on the 5th postoperative day, and of Neopterin on the 4th and 5th postoperative days in the leakage group. Taken together, these results suggest promise for calprotectin and neopterin as diagnostic biomarkers.

Introduction

Dehiscence of anastomosis and leakage in colorectal surgery is one of the most common and serious complications of the field. Statistically, its incidence is reported around 10%, but the incidence varies in different sources according to various factors. However, it does not fall below 3% in the long term, and in some studies the incidence reaches up to 30%1. This complication represents a serious health and psychological burden for patients as well as an enormous socioeconomic problem. It significantly prolongs the period of hospitalization, rehabilitation and the patient’s return to active life. Along with this, the costs not only of the patient’s treatment increase several times1–3.

The mechanism of anastomotic leakage development is not sufficiently explained in detail, it is very likely multifactorial and individual4,5. It seems, that every anastomotic failure begins with a small leak, which may or may not progress2,6. According to the classification by the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer we distinguish three levels of severity6. Whereas small leaks can be treated conservatively, an advanced dehiscence needs to be treated by reoperation. It is characterized by high mortality, morbidity, and usually the need for stoma. It has been shown in a number of clinical studies that with a proactive approach, early detected leaks can be successfully treated conservatively or minimally invasively and may prevent progression to diffuse peritonitis2,6,7.

Early diagnosis of a leakage is challenging. The human body reacts to any surgical procedure as well as to the development of a leak with inflammation. Distinguishing between an adequate course and a leak is difficult in the first days. Symptoms of a leak become noticeable typically on the 4-5th postoperative day (POD), but later detection is not an exception1,8. Monitoring of the clinical condition is an indispensable part of the diagnosis of a leak. However, in the first days it is not possible to reliably distinguish patients with a complicated course9. The basic warning markers include pain, fever, changes in drainage fluid quality, psychical disorders and GIT passage disorders. All of them are considered to be late and relatively unreliable signs10.

In the diagnosis of leak, the gold standard today is the examination using contrast computed tomography (CT). It reaches up to 70% sensitivity and 80% specificity. This examination, however, represents a significant radiation burden for the patients and risks from the application of contrast medium. Last but not least, CT is a relatively expensive diagnostic method to perform and evaluate. It can therefore be used occasionally, but not as a daily screening method. Due to these facts, it is indicated selectively, based on clinical manifestations and laboratory results.

Laboratory parameters of inflammation are another established method of detecting complicated anastomosis. However, they are usually elevated after any uncomplicated operation. Another disadvantage is their significant latency. Their elevated values ​​persist even 5 days after surgery, even in uncomplicated cases.

In this study we focused on advancing the postoperative diagnosis by using non ordinary methods. Although there is a trend towards abandoning routine abdominal drainage, it is still an integral part of colonic surgery in many settings11. We consider the drained fluid to be an untapped opportunity in the diagnosis of leaks. Since the fluid is collected from the immediate vicinity of the anastomosis, we consider it to be potentially diagnostically beneficial.

Many studies have attempted to identify a reliable marker for the early detection of colorectal anastomotic leaks. However, none has gained widespread adoption in clinical routine. This may be due to the fact that many promising candidates were tested in small patient cohorts or involved markers that are not routinely measured in clinical practice12. In this study, we focused on monitoring three thermostable molecules that are easy to analyze in drainage fluids, assessing changes in their concentrations during the postoperative period in relation to complications and clinical outcomes.

Chosen markers are well-known routinely analyzed molecules but yet not deeply studied in association to anastomotic leakage:

Calprotectin is a protein predominantely released during neutrophil activation. Its concentration in feces is widely used today for the diagnosis and monitoring of idiopathic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Its concentration can also be measured in serum and elevated serum levels in the early postoperative period have been associated with a higher risk of anastomotic leak13. However, the concentration of calprotectin in drainage fluid and its relation to leak has not been investigated in a sufficiently large patient cohort. We hypothesized that measuring calprotectin levels in abdominal drainage fluids would offer greater sensitivity for detecting complications related to anastomotic leaks.Neopterin is a pteridine. It is an aromatic substance that is synthesized in macrophages. Determination of its concentration can be used in monitoring cellular immunity. Elevation of its serum values in the first postoperative days is associated with a higher incidence of leak resolution14. However, it has never been studied in sufficiently large patient cohorts.D-Lactate is a metabolite that is produced by the fermentation of carbohydrates by the colonic microbiota15. Its relationship to leak has not yet been determined. We hypothesized that there would be minimal bacterial presence in the abdominal cavity after uncomplicated surgery that would be able to produce D-Lactate. By the opposite implication, we assumed that the concentration of D-lactate would be elevated in case of leak. The parameter has not previously been investigated in this context.

Methods

Between January 2020 and December 2023, a total of 96 patients enrolled in the study. The study was conducted with approval of the ethics committee of the University Hospital in Pilsen. We obtained signed informed consent from every patient enrolled in this study. All patients received the same high quality care in accordance to current medical an institutional guidelines regardless of participation in the study. All research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Only adult patients with written consent participated. We included only patients who underwent a procedure on the large intestine, where any type of colorectal anastomosis was performed, and abdominal drain was used. We included patients undergoing open, laparoscopic or robotic surgery in the University Hospital in Pilsen.

We excluded children, patients with protective stoma, as well as patients who underwent abdominoperineal amputation. We did not include patients with small intestine surgery or cases where more than one anastomosis was performed. Age, diagnosis, duration of surgery, previous radiotherapy, comorbidities and others were not considered as exclussion criteria.

Samples were collected each postoperative day (POD) ranging from the first to the fifth. Samples were collected from drainage bags. The sample collection times varied in between the patients as well as drainage collection bags changes, since there is no protocolar routine of such procedures. Every sample was collected until 11 am, so the time difference of sample collection was +/- 2 h. At least one millilitre of fluid was always taken. The goal was to analyze at least three PODs. All samples were then frozen at -80 °C. Once all the samples were collected they were handed to biochemical analysis. Chosen markers of leakage were Calprotectin, Neopterine, D-Lactate. Concentrations were measured using the ELISA method (see below). We analyzed individual values of marker concentrations and their dynamics across the first PODs. Then we retrospectively compared the results to the subsequent development of leakage.

The presence of leak was determined based on clinical evaluation, laboratory tests, CT scan, perioperative finding or colonoscopy. Diagnostics varied case to case. Clinical evaluation took place every day. Based on clinical evaluation and laboratory tests usually a CT was performed to confirm clinical diagnosis of leak. In case of surgical revision, the diagnosis of leak was confirmed by perioperative finding. Alternatively if colonoscopy was performed the leak was confirmed during the procedure. Finally if a radiologic intervention was carried out or antibiotics treatment was indicated resulting from a CT, we classified these patients into the leak group.

Patients with leakage were divided according to the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer into three groups16. Patients with no clinical manifestation and accidently diagnosed leak that required no intervention were classified as group A. Patients with diagnosed leak who were treated with antibiotics combined with or without minimally invasive procedure were classified as B leak. Patients with advanced leak who required a surgical intervention were classified as C leak group. Each C leakage was confirmed by perioperative finding that confirmed advanced intra abdominal leak with peritonitis. In these patients Hartmann’s procedure was performed – resection of anastomosis, proximal stoma and closure of distal stump or creation of diverting stoma and redrainage.

To determine neopterin, calprotectin, and D-lactate levels, commercial ELISA immunoassay kits and enzymatic methods were employed, offering precise biomarker quantification due to their high sensitivity and specificity. The biomarker levels were measured using the VIDIMAT automated analyzer (VIDIA, Vestec, Czech Republic), based on the ThunderBolt® platform (Gold Standard Diagnostics, Davis, California, USA). This fully automated and compact system is designed for ELISA and chemiluminescent (CLIA) assays, emphasizing precision and laboratory efficiency.

Neopterin levels were measured using the Neopterin ELISA KIT (Cat. No. 3410, Alpha Diagnostic Intl., San Antonio, Texas, USA), which operates on a competitive binding principle. In this assay, neopterin in the sample competes with enzyme-labeled neopterin conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for binding sites on specific antibodies immobilized on a microtiter plate. After washing, the chromogenic substrate tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) is added, producing a color reaction in the presence of HRP. The reaction is stopped by adding a sulfuric acid-based stop solution, and absorbance is measured at 450 nm. The color intensity is inversely proportional to the concentration of neopterin in the sample. The assay has a measurement range of 0.7–111 nmol/L, with a minimum detection limit of 0.7 nmol/L. The intra – and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) are less than 10% for concentrations ranging from 2 to 50 nmol/L. The method exhibits minimal cross-reactivity with structurally related compounds, such as 7,8-dihydroneopterin (< 2%). Samples were analyzed without prior dilution, enabling direct quantification of neopterin.

Calprotectin levels were determined using the BÜHLMANN MRP8/14 ELISA kit (Cat. No. EK-MRP8/14, BÜHLMANN Laboratories AG, Schönenbuch, Switzerland), based on the sandwich ELISA principle. Microtiter plates are coated with monoclonal antibodies specific to the MRP8/14 heterodimer (calprotectin). After sample addition, calprotectin binds to the immobilized antibodies. Subsequently, a detection antibody conjugated to HRP is added. Following a washing step, TMB substrate is introduced, and the reaction is stopped by adding a sulfuric acid-based stop solution to stabilize the color product. Absorbance is measured at 450 nm. The assay has a dynamic range of 0.4–24 µg/mL at a 1:100 dilution, with a minimum detection limit of 0.2 µg/mL. The intra-assay CV is 4.3%, and the inter-assay CV is 5.8%. The method demonstrates excellent linearity (97.8%) across the tested dilution range. Cross-reactivity with the individual monomers MRP8 and MRP14 is negligible (< 0.1%). Samples were manually diluted 1:100 before analysis, and automated dilution according to the protocol ensured consistency.

D-lactate was quantified using the D-Lactate Assay Kit (Colorimetric) (Cat. No. ab83429, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), which relies on the specific oxidation of D-lactate by D-lactate dehydrogenase. This reaction generates a colored product, the intensity of which is directly proportional to the concentration of D-lactate and is measured at 450 nm. The assay offers a broad measurement range of 0.01–10 mM with a very low minimum detection limit. Interference from NADH or NADPH is minimized by simultaneously measuring blank samples. Prior to analysis, samples were deproteinized using 4 M perchloric acid (PCA) to remove proteins and stabilize metabolites, and subsequently neutralized with 2 M potassium hydroxide (KOH). Undiluted samples were measured for concentrations ranging from 0.018 to 0.357 mM. Samples with higher concentrations were diluted 1:20, enabling measurements up to 7.147 mM.

Marker concentrations were recorded for 3 to 5 consecutive post-operation days (POD). Besides these basic measurements, average values for the first three PODs (mean123), as well as changes between the 1st and 2nd POD (Δ12), 1st and 3rd POD (Δ13), and 2nd and 3rd POD (Δ23) were calculated for each marker and each patient. The Δ values were calculated by subtracting the initial (earlier) value from the terminal (later) one. Subsequently, all of these quantitative variables were compared between patients with and without anastomotic leakage using the Mann-Whitney U test. For variables that showed a statistically significant difference, their diagnostic value was further assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. An ROC curve was plotted for each variable and the area under the curve (AUC ROC) was then calculated to summarize the predictive strength of the variable (with AUC ROC of 1 representing a perfectly reliable prediction and AUC ROC of 0.5 representing random guessing). Sensitivity and specificity values were then determined for specific empirically set threshold values. All reported p-values are two-tailed, and the level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

From January 2020 till December 2023, 96 patients were enrolled into the study. A total of 39 women and 57 men enrolled. For every patient, we collected samples from at least three PODs. Obtaining samples from the fourth or fifth POD was not always possible, most often due to low amount of fluid or due to early drain removal. The leak rate was 17.02%. The day of detection ranged POD 3 up to months later. There were 7 leaks diagnosed ranging from POD 3–5 and 10 leaks diagnosed on POD 6 and later. Only two patients (2.12%) were classified with an A leak (Incidentally diagnosed leak that required no intervention). Another 5 patients (5.32%) were treated conservatively without reoperation and were classified as having a type B leak. There were 9 patients that required reoperation (9.57%) classified as having a type C. Four patients with C leak were diagnosed on POD 4–5. Five patients were diagnosed on POD 6 and later. We compared the marker concentrations for each individual postoperative day in relation to leak development. In addition, we analyzed the differences in marker concentrations across the leak severity subgroups.

Neopterin levels were found to be significantly higher in patients who developed anastomotic leakage. On postoperative day (POD) 4, patients with leakage exhibited a clear elevation in neopterin concentrations compared to those without, as reflected by a higher median and broader interquartile range (Fig. 1a). This difference became even more pronounced on POD 5, where the median neopterin concentration in the leakage group further increased, accompanied by a wider spread of values (Fig. 1b), suggesting a progressive inflammatory response in affected individuals.

A similar trend was observed for calprotectin. On POD 5, calprotectin levels were markedly elevated in patients with anastomotic leakage (Fig. 1c). The leakage group not only demonstrated higher median levels but also a broader distribution, with some individuals reaching extreme concentrations above 2000 mg/L, indicative of pronounced intestinal inflammation. Dynamic changes in calprotectin levels over time (Δ-values) further enhanced the distinction between the two groups. The change from POD 1 to POD 3 (Δ13) showed a striking difference: while calprotectin levels tended to decrease or remain stable in patients without leakage, they increased substantially in those with leakage (Fig. 1d). This pattern was also evident for the change from POD 2 to POD 3 (Δ23), albeit to a slightly lesser extent (Fig. 1e). In both cases, the leakage group consistently exhibited positive Δ-values, while the non-leakage group showed minimal or negative changes.

D-Lactate concentrations did not correlate with the occurrence of leakage, neither in individual values or in Δ values.

These results confirm that both absolute concentrations and early postoperative dynamics of neopterin and calprotectin are significantly associated with the occurrence of anastomotic leakage, supporting their potential use as early diagnostic markers in clinical practice.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of neopterin and calprotectin levels in predicting colorectal anastomotic leakage.

Neopterin concentrations showed a moderate ability to discriminate between patients with and without leakage. On postoperative day (POD) 4, the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.745. At a threshold of 40 nmol/L, neopterin yielded a sensitivity of 0.70 and a specificity of 0.77. The diagnostic accuracy improved slightly on POD 5, with an AUC of 0.812. At a threshold of 25 nmol/L, sensitivity and specificity reached 0.71 and 0.89, respectively, suggesting that neopterin may be a more reliable marker on the fifth postoperative day.

Calprotectin levels on POD 5 similarly demonstrated a good discriminatory capacity, with an AUC of 0.756. A threshold of 700 mg/L provided a sensitivity of 0.71 and a specificity of 0.84. As expected, increasing the threshold further improved specificity (e.g., 0.92 at 1200 mg/L) at the cost of reduced sensitivity.

To capture dynamic changes in calprotectin expression, we also evaluated relative changes between timepoints. The change in calprotectin levels from POD 1 to POD 3 (Δ13) showed particularly strong predictive value, with an AUC of 0.879. A threshold of 250 mg/L yielded a sensitivity of 0.62 and a specificity of 0.55, whereas a more sensitive threshold of 500 mg/L increased sensitivity to 0.94 but lowered specificity to 0.27. Similarly, the change from POD 2 to POD 3 (Δ23) yielded an AUC of 0.714. A threshold of 130 mg/L achieved a sensitivity of 0.88 and specificity of 0.53.

Overall, both neopterin and calprotectin demonstrated diagnostic potential, with the strongest performance observed for neopterin on POD 5 and for the Δ13 change in calprotectin, indicating that both absolute levels and early postoperative dynamics may aid in early identification of anastomotic leakage (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Early diagnosis of anastomotic leakage remains a challenge. No specific marker has been discovered yet. CT scan is a golden standard in diagnosing a leak, but we lack both a marker for its indication as well as clear indications for a management protocol9. The goal of this study is to target new markers for early leak diagnostic, which could participate in a proposition of an innovative leakage risk scoring system and drive the postoperative regimen in order to decrease incidence of clinically serious leakages. One that could be used routinely in day-to-day postoperative care. The current classification of leakage is simple, yet it is purely descriptive and retrospective, merely reflecting the postoperative management already performed. From the perspective of clinical practice, it remains impractical9,16.

Calprotectin and neopterin are non-specific markers of inflammation. Elevation of their concentrations may be false positive in patients with any other intra-abdominal complication that may not be related to the development of leakage17,18. In this study we discovered the elevation of concentration values is related to the occurrence of leakage itself with a relatively good sensitivity/specificity ratio. We also found that higher concentration values are associated with the severity of leakage.

Analysis of D-Lactate concentrations is a demanding methodology. Measuring D-Lactate required a lot of calibration. Due to these technical complications we were only able to analyse a smaller portion of the patients. According to our results, it does not seem to be a suitable marker for the detection of colorectal anastomosis leakage. However its elevated concentration in serum was reported in ischemia of colon in rat models. Elevated levels of D-Lactate in blood were also associated with dysbiosis and impaired gut barrier function15,19. Therefore we suggest more trials, with larger numbers of patients to verify our conclusios.

Study limitations

Due to a relatively small number of patients, application of the results may be limited in subgroups A and B. Yet the goal of the study was not to estimate the type of leak. Leak is considered to be a pathology that may or may not worsen without proper treatment20. Our aim was primarily to discover a leak early rather than to estimate its behaviour. If a leak is discovered early a proper treatment can be introduced as soon as possible. This may help to reduce the number of necessary reoperations in the future. With an incidence of 17.6% and 9.57% respectively we consider our results to be representative. And even for subgroups of Leaks A, B may still be applicable as well.

The “early detection” is hard to define since a leak and its symptoms may develop at any time afer surgery. The goal of this study is not to define early and late detection. The aim itself is to encourage active search for complications and provide tools and markers for such action. From our perspective each Grade B leakage successfully treated, and every Grade C leakage prevented is a success.

Neither this study provides good answers on what to do in case of elevated values of our markers. It is up to subsequent study to provide such answers. We only presume, that patients with increased calues of our will undergo a CT scan immedietally – which would possibly mean sooner compared to patients without such monitoring. According to our results 50% + of patients may benefit from such action. But how or whether it will impact the overall outcome of the patients remains unclear.

In this study there was great adherence towards Hartmann´s procedure. There was only one case of salvage operation, meaning creation of diversion stoma and redrainage sparing the failed anastomosis. In many centers it is more common praxis rather than Hartmann´s procedure. Both procedures mean reoperation and stoma creation. Regardless the management such patients belong into leak C group. We consider our results to be reperesentative and generally applicable.

The low occurence of patients with small leaks (leak A) within the study population is surprising. Statistically, it is expected to be the most common leak. In this study the only two type A leaks were detected. They were discovered during colonoscopy, which took place three months after surgery. There were no patients with CT verified A type leak. In order to detect all Leak A patients, an early postoperative CT scan in all patients including asymptomatic would have to be performed routinely (this will be considered in in a future validation study). Although we are sure we missed some A leaks, the main goal is to avoid advanced C leaks. This result only emphasises the need for a new approach to early screening and detection of leaks. For this purpose, we don’t consider the low number of A leaks to be a bias of this study.

According to the ERAS protocols, routine abdominal drainage is abandoned21. The drain fulfilled a drainage and signal function. However, according to recent opinions, resorption is performed by the peritoneum11,21. The signal function of the drain, when the nature of the drain changes, is considered to be a late marker of leakage22,23. In addition, the presence of drains is considered to be a possible route of infection to the abdominal cavity23. Routine use of abdominal drain thus does not offer any advantage22. However, given the untapped possibility of using drainage fluid biochemical analysis as an integral part of detection of leakage, we consider abdominal drainage to play a an important role in the future leakage detection strategies.

In this study, we did not stratify patients based on the surgical technique used. Leakage is regarded as the same clinical complication regardless of surgical approach. Although minimally invasive techniques are associated with a lower incidence of leakage, the clinical impact remains comparable. We therefore consider our results applicable across all surgical methods. The analysis was done retrospectively from frozen samples. A randomized prospective trial would be possible, but much more demanding. This study was designed as a pilot study to discover new markers. Prospective validation study should follow.

Sample collection times varied among patients, as did the timing of drainage bag changes. However, the markers selected are thermostable, and prolonged storage in the drainage bag is not expected to have affected the results24. Our aim was to design a study that reflects real-world conditions, including less controlled or suboptimal situations. The results suggest that reliable analysis with good predictive value is still achievable.

Although our statistical analysis yielded promising results, it remains possible that better markers exist. From a biochemical perspective, the markers investigated in this study are well-characterized and inexpensive, making them suitable for routine clinical analysis.

In future studies, we aim to develop a comprehensive screening protocol for the early diagnosis of colorectal leakage. This would include the markers studied here, as well as a leakage risk score designed to guide clinical management throughout the postoperative period.

Conclusion

Early diagnosis of colorectal anastomotic leakage remains a significant clinical challenge. This study offers a new perspective on the value of several biochemical markers in postoperative drainage fluids. D-lactate, evaluated as a potential early marker of leakage, did not yield the expected results. No statistically significant changes in its concentration or dynamics were observed in association with leakage. In contrast, elevated neopterin levels on the 4th and 5th postoperative days were significantly associated with anastomotic leakage. However, neopterin did not show a statistically relevant elevation during the first few PODs, making it a suitable semi-early marker rather than an immediate one. Calprotectin demonstrated stronger early predictive potential. Elevated concentrations on POD 5 were significantly associated with leakage, and importantly, dynamic changes during the first three PODs correlated with the occurrence of leakage. This positions drain fluid calprotectin as a promising early marker.

These findings highlight the need for a new approach to the early diagnosis of anastomotic leakage. A leakage risk scoring system incorporating novel biomarkers could enable more systematic and responsive postoperative management, ultimately reducing the incidence of severe leaks. Calprotectin and neopterin appear to be promising candidates for integration into such a strategy. We intend to validate these findings in a randomized clinical trial involving a larger patient cohort.
