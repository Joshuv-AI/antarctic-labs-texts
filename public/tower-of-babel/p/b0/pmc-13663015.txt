Association between varying fermentation levels of tea and bone health in a community-based population
Authors: Junzhi Chen, Hao Xie, Guangliang Shan, Yaoda Hu, Huijing He, Tong Feng, Ruohan Zhou, Ping Yuan, Miaochan Lao, Yuping He, Qiong Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe association between teas with different fermentation levels and bone health remains unclear. We aimed to investigate the association between teas with different fermentation levels and bone health in a community-based population.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study. Participants were recruited using a multistage probability sampling method in China between 2021 and 2023. At baseline, participants self-reported the type (unfermented green, semi-fermented oolong, fully-fermented black, and post-fermented dark) and frequency of tea consumption. Bone mineral density was measured using calcaneal ultrasonography.ResultsA total of 8,490 participants were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 51.5 years. The prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis was 2.8% and 18.6%, respectively. The average duration of tea consumption was 29.3 years. Compared to the non-tea group, the adjusted odds ratios (OR) for osteopenia/osteoporosis associated with regular intake (≥ 3 days per week) of green tea, oolong tea, black tea, and dark tea were 0.79 (95% CI: 0.67–0.93), 0.84 (95% CI: 0.73–0.97), 1.03 (95% CI: 0.86–1.25), and 0.98 (95% CI: 0.78–1.23), respectively. Linear associations were observed only between the frequency of green or oolong tea consumption and osteopenia/osteoporosis (P for trend < 0.05). Similar results were found regarding the level of fermentation and bone health (P for trend < 0.05). The associations between tea type, consumption frequency, and bone health remained robust in multiple sensitivity analyses.ConclusionTea fermentation level was significantly associated with bone health. Regular consumption of unfermented and semi-fermented teas was associated with lower odds of having osteopenia/osteoporosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-026-01367-x.Keywords: Tea fermentation level, Bone health, Osteoporosis, Community health

Introduction

Osteopenia and osteoporosis are characterized by decreased bone mass and destruction, leading to increased bone fragility and susceptibility to fractures, especially among aging populations [1]. The global prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia is 19.7% and 40.4%, respectively [2]. In China, osteoporosis-related fractures are expected to reach 4.83 million per year by 2035, costing approximately $19.92 billion annually [3]. Also, the indirect costs associated with fracture-related morbidity and mortality are substantial. Clearly, the clinical and economic consequences of osteoporosis call for an early intervention when appropriate.

Lifestyle modifications, particularly dietary patterns, are increasingly recognized as modifiable risk factors for bone health. Tea, after plain water, is the world’s most popular beverage, surpassing coffee, beer, wine, and carbonated soft drinks [4]. Tea polyphenols are the most significant bioactive components in tea. Catechins constitute 70% of tea polyphenols and serve as the primary active ingredients [5]. Based on their respective manufacturing processes, tea is classified by polyphenol oxidation levels (fermentation level) into four categories: non-fermented green tea, semi-fermented oolong tea, fully-fermented black tea, and post-fermented dark tea. Green tea retains abundant native catechins and exhibits the highest total flavonoid content due to zero fermentation. As fermentation progresses, native catechins degrade and transform into more complex flavonoids [6, 7].

Animal and cellular studies have demonstrated that tea polyphenols promote osteoblast proliferation and differentiation while inhibiting osteoclast formation, thus benefiting osteoporosis prevention [8]. However, some epidemiological studies have reported a positive association between tea consumption and bone mineral density (BMD) [9–12], while others found no correlation [13–16]. Additionally, existing meta-analyses exploring the correlation between tea consumption and osteoporosis or osteoporotic fractures have failed to reach a consensus [17–19]. Importantly, most epidemiological studies have largely overlooked the wide variation in catechin content across different tea types [20]. Instead, participants were typically classified simply as tea drinkers or non-drinkers, without evaluating the effects of specific tea types on BMD or osteoporosis. However, differences in fermentation levels alter the polyphenol composition of tea, which may partly explain the heterogeneity in existing findings. Therefore, investigating the associations between teas with different fermentation levels and bone health is warranted.

In this study, we aimed to assess the associations between tea types, as well as their consumption levels with bone health in a community-based cohort.

Study methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the Diverse Life Course Cohort (DLCC) study [21]. Participants were enrolled from Guangdong province, China (Figure S1) during two survey waves (2021 and 2023). We used a multistage stratified cluster sampling procedures to generate a representative sample, details of the design, objectives, and survey methods of this cohort have been published elsewhere [21, 22]. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older, had local household registration, and had resided in the area for at least six months prior to the survey. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, severe mental or physical conditions, and active-duty military personnel. A total of 12,162 participants were enrolled, of whom 2,220 were excluded due to incomplete data, including missing bone mineral density data (n = 593), consumption of two or more types of tea (n = 1,428), missing tea consumption information (n = 59), and missing tea consumption duration or duration < 1 year (n = 140). Among the remaining 9,942 participants, an additional 1,452 were excluded because of missing covariates required for the multivariable regression models, resulting in a final analytical sample of 8,490 participants (Figure S2).

This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and all procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (approval number: GDREC2020221H(R1) and KY-Z-2022-391-03). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Data collection

Baseline data were collected by trained personnel according to standard operating procedures. The survey process was assisted by community organization staff, community health center physicians, statisticians, and epidemiologists. Unified questionnaires were used throughout the survey. A face-to-face questionnaire was used to collect information on socio-demographics, personal medical histories, lifestyle factors, and physical activity. The completeness and accuracy of each questionnaire were checked by an epidemiologist through a face-to-face review of the subjects. Tea drinking habits, including tea type, consumption frequency, tea strength, and the age at which tea consumption began, were obtained through self-report. The physical examination included weight, height, blood pressure, and waist circumference. Body weight, height and waist circumference were measured according to standard protocols. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight/height squared (kg/m2) and divided into three groups according to the criteria for Asian populations [23]: normal or low weight: < 23; Overweight: 23-27.5; And obese: ≥27.5. Blood pressure was measured three consecutive times in a seated position after at least 5 min of rest using a digital sphygmomanometer (Omron HEM-907, Japan). Fasting venous blood samples (≥ 8 h) were obtained from all participants.

Assessment criteria

Medical illnesses were defined based on self-reported history, including hypertension, diabetes, history of fracture and tumor. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined based on self-reported or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≤ 60 ml/min/1.73 m². eGFR was calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation [24]. Newly diagnosed hypertension was defined as a blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg during physical examination without prior diagnosis by a physician. Smoking was defined as continuous or cumulative tobacco use for more than 6 months. Alcohol consumption was defined as consumption of ≥ 2 drinks per month.

Quantitative ultrasound assessment

Heel quantitative ultrasound (QUS) measurements of the calcaneus were performed using the PEGASUS system (MEDILINK, France). All measurements were conducted by a trained ultrasound technician. QUS measurements were performed on the right heel. If the participant had a history of a previous fracture in the right lower extremity, measurements were taken from the left heel. Speed of sound (SOS, m/sec) and broadband ultrasound attenuation (BUA, dB/MHz) were measured. The stiffness index (SI) was calculated by the following formula: SI = (0.67 × BUA + 0.28 SOS)– 420, which was reported as the QUS T-score. This metric represents the deviation in standard deviations (SD) from the mean T-score of a reference population of healthy young adults [25]. QUS is recognized as a portable, non-radiative, and cost-effective method for osteoporosis screening and BMD assessment [25–27].

The T-score was computed by subtracting the mean BMD of healthy young adults from the subject’s measured BMD, which serves as the key diagnostic value in older adults. This value was then compared to the Z-score, calculated using age-matched reference BMD from healthy young adults [28]. Given that the mean age of subjects in our study was 51.5 years, the T-score was used in this study.

BMD results were categorized into three groups based on WHO criteria [29]. Osteoporosis: T-score ≤ − 2.5 SD; Osteopenia: T-score between − 1.0 and − 2.5 SD; Normal BMD: T-score ≥ − 1.0 SD.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as the mean ± SD for continuous variables and as numbers (%) for categorical variables. Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess significant differences for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables.

The proportional odds assumption, assessed using the Brant test, was violated (χ² = 83, P < 0.001), indicating an inconsistent relationship between the predictors (tea type) and outcome categories (normal bone mass, osteopenia, and osteoporosis) across thresholds. As a result, a multinomial logistic regression model was used to calculate the odds ratios (OR) for osteopenia/osteoporosis based on the tea type, including green tea, oolong tea, black tea, and dark tea, with no tea intake as the reference group. Model 1 included tea type and demographic variables (age, sex, marital status, and education). Model 2 further adjusted for comorbidities (diabetes, history of tumors and fractures, CKD, and BMI). Model 3 additionally controlled for lifestyle factors (smoking status, drinking status, work intensity, and the frequency of meat, dairy, vegetable, and fruit consumption). Trend tests were performed by modeling the differential tea categories as continuous variables. In detailed analyses examining the dose-effect relation between the intake of green tea or oolong tea or black tea or dark tea with bone health, we categorized tea consumption frequency into three groups: regular (3–7 days per week), occasional (1–2 days per week or ≤ 3 days per month), and never. Due to the small number of individuals with osteopenia across tea consumption frequency subgroups (Table S1), bone health was grouped into two categories: normal bone mass and osteopenia/osteoporosis. A binary logistic regression model was then applied to assess the association between tea consumption frequency and bone health. Trend tests were performed by modeling the differential categories of tea consumption frequency as continuous variables.

Moreover, we stratified the population by age (≥ 60 vs. <60, years), sex, BMI (low weight/normal vs. overweight/obesity), history of fracture (no vs. yes), current smoking (no vs. yes), and current drinking (no vs. yes) to assess the sensitivity and interactions within different subgroups. Subgroup analyses were performed using multivariable logistic regression models. To test for interaction, tea type was treated as a categorical variable and cross-product terms between tea type and each stratification variable were added to the fully adjusted model. The overall interaction P value was calculated by comparing models with and without the interaction term using the likelihood ratio test. If the overall interaction was significant (P < 0.05), category-specific interaction terms were further examined to determine which tea type differed in its association with bone health across strata. We also validated our results using the full sample after multiple imputation. Table S2 shows the number of subjects with missing covariate data in the logistic regression model, as well as the covariates that were handled using multiple imputation. All data analyses were performed by R software, version 4.3.3 (http://www.r-project.org) and EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.net/cn/index.php). A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the study participants according to tea type. The mean age of the participants was 51.5 years. Among the participants, 14.6% consumed green tea, 23.9% consumed oolong tea, 6.7% consumed black tea, and 4% consumed dark tea. The mean duration of tea consumption among tea drinkers was 29.3 years. The prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia was 18.6% and 2.8%, respectively. Participants who consumed green tea had the lowest prevalence of osteoporosis (17.7%) and osteopenia (1.5%). Compared with women (Table S3), men were older and exhibited a higher prevalence of osteoporosis, tea consumption, hypertension, diabetes, history of fracture, chronic kidney disease, overweight, current smoking, and alcohol consumption. Additionally, men had a higher proportion of heavy physical work, and consumed dairy products and fruits less frequently.

Association of type and frequency of tea intake with bone health

Table 2 presents the association between tea consumption and bone health. Tea consumption was significantly associated with lower odds of osteopenia across all adjusted models. In Model 1 (demographically-adjusted), tea drinkers had 47% lower odds of osteopenia (OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.42–0.68; P < 0.001). This protective effect persisted in Model 2 (further adjusted for comorbidities; OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.42–0.70; P < 0.001) and Model 3 (further adjusted for lifestyle factors; OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.45–0.76; P < 0.001). When examining tea types, both green tea (Model 3: OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.26–0.71; P = 0.001) and oolong tea (Model 3: OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.36–0.76; P = 0.001) were significantly associated with reduced osteopenia risk across all models, whereas black or dark tea showed no significant association (P > 0.05). A significant ordered trend across tea-type categories according to fermentation level was observed for osteopenia (P for trend = 0.019; Fig. 1). Adjustments for covariates modestly attenuated the effect sizes without altering statistical significance.

For osteoporosis, the results paralleled those for osteopenia; however, only green tea demonstrated a robust protective effect (Model 3: OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.66–0.93; P = 0.006), while no significant associations were observed for black, dark, or oolong tea (P > 0.05). Similar results were obtained in datasets processed by the multiple imputation method (Table S4).

Table 3 shows the relationship between the consumption frequency of different tea types and bone health. For green tea, regular consumption was consistently associated with lower odds of osteopenia/osteoporosis in all models (Model 3: OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.67–0.93; P = 0.006), whereas occasional consumption showed no significant association (P > 0.05). A significant dose-response trend was observed (Fig. 2). Oolong tea exhibited similar patterns, with regular consumption associated with lower odds of osteopenia/osteoporosis (Model 3: OR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.73–0.97; P = 0.006) and a consistent dose-response trend (Fig. 2). Occasional consumption of oolong tea remained non-significant (P > 0.05). For black and dark tea, neither occasional nor regular consumption demonstrated significant associations with bone health in any model (P > 0.05). Trend tests for these tea types were non-significant (Fig. 2). Similar results were obtained in datasets processed by the multiple imputation method (Table S5).

Discussion

In this large community-based study, tea consumption was significantly associated with lower odds of osteopenia or osteoporosis. Further analyses showed that habitual consumption of non-fermented green tea and semi-fermented oolong tea was associated with 21% and 16% lower odds of osteopenia/osteoporosis, respectively, whereas black tea and dark tea showed no significant associations. These findings suggest that, beyond tea consumption itself, the degree of tea fermentation may play an important role in bone health.

Potential biological mechanisms may explain these findings. Tea catechins, particularly epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), possess strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, promote osteoblast differentiation, and inhibit osteoclast activity, thereby helping to maintain bone homeostasis [8]. During the fermentation process, catechins are progressively oxidized and polymerized into theaflavins, thearubigins, and other derivatives, resulting in a substantial reduction in catechin content. This process may weaken the protective effects of tea on bone metabolism and partly explain why lower-fermentation teas, such as green tea and oolong tea, showed stronger associations with better bone health in our study.

The association between tea consumption and BMD remains inconsistent in human studies. Hegarty et al. [9] examined self-reported tea consumption in relation to BMD at the lumbar spine, greater trochanter, and Ward’s triangle among 1,256 postmenopausal women in the UK and found that tea consumers had approximately 5% higher BMD across all three anatomical sites compared with non-consumers. Similar positive associations between tea drinking and BMD have also been reported among postmenopausal women in other populations [9–12]. However, Chen et al. [13], in a prospective cohort study of 91,465 postmenopausal women, reported that habitual tea drinking had only a small effect on BMD and did not significantly reduce fracture risk. In addition to postmenopausal women [14], studies in younger [16] and older adults [15] have also found no significant association between habitual tea consumption and BMD. These inconsistent findings suggest that tea consumption alone may be insufficient to explain its potential skeletal benefits. Our results indicate that tea fermentation level may be an additional key factor influencing bone health.

Catechin content differs substantially across tea types because of variations in processing methods. Green tea, as an unfermented tea, retains the highest levels of catechins. With increasing fermentation, catechins are gradually oxidized and converted into theaflavins and thearubigins. Green tea contains four major catechins: EGCG, (−)-epigallocatechin (EGC), (−)-epicatechin-3-gallate (ECG), and (−)-epicatechin (EC). Previous studies have shown that the antioxidant activity of catechins depends largely on their structural characteristics and the number of hydroxyl groups [30]. EGC, EGCG, (−)-epiafzelechin, and EC have been reported to promote the proliferation, differentiation, and maturation of osteoblasts [8], while tea polyphenols can inhibit osteoclast formation, with EGCG showing the strongest effect [8]. EGCG is the most abundant catechin in green tea, accounting for approximately 30–50% of total catechins in a typical brewed cup [31].

Animal and human studies have consistently supported the protective effects of green tea on bone health. In ovariectomized rats, Shen et al. [32] showed that green tea polyphenols mitigated bone loss and improved bone microstructure and quality. Similar protective effects have also been observed in various bone-loss models [33]. Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial involving 171 postmenopausal women demonstrated that supplementation with green tea polyphenols increased serum bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BAP) levels after 4 weeks and improved the BAP/tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) ratio after 3 months [34]. These findings provide biological support for the inverse association between green tea consumption and osteopenia/osteoporosis observed in our study.

Black tea is the most widely consumed tea worldwide, accounting for approximately 78% of total tea consumption. Unlike green tea, black tea undergoes full fermentation, during which more than 90% of catechins are oxidized and oligomerized by endogenous oxidases and peroxidases. This process produces metabolites such as theaflavins, theasinensins, and thearubigins, which contribute to its antioxidant activity [35]. Among these compounds, theaflavin-3,3′-digallate (TF3) exhibits the strongest antioxidant activity [36]. TF3 can activate several signaling pathways, including tumor necrosis factor-α-suppressed mitogen-activated protein kinase, Wnt/β-catenin, and BMP/Smad cascades, thereby enhancing the transcription of osteogenesis-related factors such as RUNX2 and Osterix and promoting osteoblast differentiation [37]. However, some studies have suggested that black tea may also exert pro-oxidative effects. TF3 has been shown to increase H₂O₂ production and induce apoptosis in human bronchial epithelial 21BES cell lines [38, 39], and oxidative damage to isolated and cellular DNA has also been reported. Therefore, the biological effects of black tea on bone metabolism remain uncertain.

High-quality epidemiological evidence regarding black tea and bone health is still limited. Myers et al. [40] conducted a prospective cohort study involving 1,188 women aged over 75 years and found that participants who consumed three or more cups of black tea daily had a 30% lower risk of osteoporosis-related fractures compared with those consuming less than one cup per week. Interestingly, no significant association was found between black tea consumption and BMD. Similarly, regular black tea consumption was not significantly associated with osteopenia or osteoporosis in our study. Further prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to clarify the effect of black tea on bone health.

Previous studies comparing the antioxidant effects of green tea and black tea have reported inconsistent findings. In a crossover study involving 24 volunteers [41], participants consumed 300 mL of black tea, green tea, or hot water on separate days. Both green and black tea significantly increased plasma ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), but green tea produced significantly higher FRAP values than black tea. In contrast, Leung et al. [36] found no difference in antioxidant capacity between black and green tea in vitro and reported that TF3 exhibited stronger antioxidant activity than EGCG. However, Lee [42] argued that these findings did not account for differences in antioxidant concentrations among tea types. Because the overall antioxidant capacity of tea depends on multiple compounds, including polyphenols and phenolic acids, rather than a single antioxidant, it may not be appropriate to conclude that black tea has antioxidant effects equivalent to green tea.

Oolong tea, a semi-fermented tea, contains polyphenol levels intermediate between green tea and black tea. Epidemiological studies have shown that postmenopausal women consuming 1–5 cups of oolong tea daily had significantly higher BMD [43]. Consistent with these findings, our study found that regular oolong tea consumption (≥ 3 days per week) was associated with significantly lower odds of osteopenia/osteoporosis. Unlike other fermented teas, dark tea undergoes unique microbial post-fermentation, and no theaflavins or thearubigins have been detected in dark tea [5]. Instead, theabrownins are considered its primary antioxidant compounds [44]. Although the antioxidant properties of dark tea are well documented, epidemiological evidence regarding its effects on bone health remains limited. Our study found no significant association between regular dark tea consumption and bone health, suggesting that further investigation is needed to clarify its potential skeletal effects.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its ability to examine the relationship between the level of fermentation of tea and bone health in a large sample. This study also has several limitations, among which the absence of DXA measurements and quantitative dietary flavonoid data should be particularly emphasized. First, bone mineral density was assessed using QUS rather than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which remains the gold standard for osteoporosis diagnosis. Although QUS-derived measurements have been shown to correlate with DXA and to be associated with osteoporosis [28–30], the absence of DXA may limit the diagnostic accuracy and comparability of our findings. Second, we did not collect quantitative data on flavonoid intake from tea or other dietary sources, which precluded further evaluation of the potential role of flavonoids in bone health. Third, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, we were unable to establish a causal relationship between tea consumption and bone health. Finally, participants were mainly recruited from community-based populations in southern China, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Regional differences in tea consumption habits, dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, and socioeconomic characteristics may influence bone health and the observed associations. Nevertheless, this study provides the first evidence from a community-based cohort on this topic and may offer valuable context for interpreting global data.

In conclusion, our results indicate that regular intake of green or oolong tea was associated with lower odds of having osteopenia or osteoporosis in the general population. Future studies should further validate these findings using longitudinal cohorts or randomized controlled trials and clarify the dose-response relationship between tea consumption and bone health across different fermentation degrees.
