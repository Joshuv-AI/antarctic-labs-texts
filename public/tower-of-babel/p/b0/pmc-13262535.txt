Conservation of Human IgSF Proteins Throughout Eukaryotic Evolution
Authors: Steven Grudman, Andras Fiser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) encompasses hundreds of proteins involved in cell–cell adhesion, neural connectivity, junctional organization, and immune regulation, with many serving as key checkpoint proteins. To elucidate the evolutionary history of human IgSF members, we systematically analyzed all available eukaryotic reference genomes to determine when each IgSF subfamily first appeared. The human IgSF was partitioned into six major evolutionary timeframes: Metazoa, Vertebrata, Gnathostomata, Tetrapoda, Amniota, and Mammalia. Although proteins were grouped solely by their conservation across eukaryotes, their biological functions clustered naturally, reflecting how new physiological systems create selective pressures that drive the appearance, retention, and diversification of protein architectures suited to those functions. Conservation and functional analyses indicate that human IgSF members arising in tetrapods and amniotes primary regulate the strength and duration of immune responses and form many of the critical components of the immune synapse, while IgSF genes that appear first in mammals have evolved to fine-tune immune activation thresholds to support maternal–fetal tolerance. Case studies are provided to illustrate three key evolutionary themes: (i) highly conserved yet catalytically inactive proteins retain essential regulatory functions, (ii) functional convergence among evolutionarily distinct IgSF families, and (iii) compensatory evolution within adaptive immunity following a lineage-specific loss of an IgSF member. Together, these findings establish an evolutionary framework for organizing the human IgSF by both ancestry and function, providing a foundation for assessing IgSF importance. Notably, this study facilitates the identification of conserved but understudied proteins that emerged alongside the development of the adaptive immune system, highlighting them as promising candidates for future experimental investigation.

This study investigates the evolutionary history of the biomedically important immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) through the analysis of 4,614 eukaryotic genomes. The study shows that patterns of protein conservation across eukaryotes naturally cluster according to biological function. Major expansions of the IgSF coincided with the emergence of adaptive immunity in gnathostomes, while proteins that evolved later in tetrapods, amniotes, and mammals progressively specialized immune signaling functions, including immune synapse organization and maternal–fetal tolerance. The study also introduces a searchable database designed to identify conserved yet understudied proteins that arose alongside the evolution of the adaptive immune system.

Introduction

All eukaryotes have evolved mechanisms to defend themselves against harmful agents such as pathogens, toxins, and environmental or cellular stress. Even unicellular eukaryotes can detect conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as bacterial lipopolysaccharides, and mount chemical defenses like oxidative bursts in response (Kagan 2017; Gauthier et al. 2022). Among multicellular eukaryotes, various physical barriers like skin, exoskeletons, and mucous membranes have evolved to further prevent the entry of harmful agents (Riera Romo et al. 2016).

Molecular clock analyses estimate that metazoans first appeared between 1,000 and 720 million years ago (Ma) (Dohrmann and Worheide 2017). In addition to possessing physical and chemical defenses, all metazoans produce immune-like cells capable of detecting and destroying invading microbes (Muller 2003). For example, protostomes (e.g. insects, worms) generate hemocytes that can phagocytose pathogens and secrete antimicrobial compounds (Mahanta et al. 2023). Even poriferans (e.g. sponges) and cnidarians (e.g. jellyfish, corals), the most simple metazoans, possess amoeboid-like cells that engulf and digest foreign particles (Muller 2003).

It was not until around 430 Ma, during the Late Ordovician period (Irisarri et al. 2017; Zhu et al. 2022), that the first adaptive immune systems evolved in jawed vertebrates (gnathostomes) and jawless vertebrates (cyclostomes). Commonly, the adaptive immune system is thought of as a network of lymphocytes (B and T cells) that specifically recognize, remember, and eliminate foreign pathogens (Cooper and Alder 2006). While this is true for gnathostomes, this is not the only form of adaptive immunity. Cyclostomes possess a distinct adaptive immune system based on variable lymphocyte receptors (VLRs) instead of T-cell (TCR) or B-cell (BCR) receptors (Flajnik and Kasahara 2010). Transcriptomic analyses of jawless-fish leukocytes show no evidence of major histocompatibility complex (MHC), BCR, or TCR precursors, supporting the view that the VLR system of adaptive immunity arose through convergent evolution (Suzuki et al. 2004; Cooper and Alder 2006). While both adaptive immune systems require precise regulatory control to prevent autoimmunity, the adaptive immune system in gnathostomes is primarily regulated by members of the immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF).

The IgSF represents one of the largest and most functionally diverse protein families in the human proteome (Orengo et al. 1994). Proteins in this family contain at least one Ig domain, a structural unit composed of antiparallel β-strands, sequentially labeled A through G, which fold together into the domain's characteristic β-sandwich. Ig domains are typically ∼70 to 110 amino acids in length and feature a conserved disulfide bond connecting the B and F strands (Williams and Barclay 1988). They are classified into four main types: V, C1, C2, and I domains, based on whether they contain the C′ and/or C″ strands and on which β-sheet incorporates the A strand (Fig. S1) (Williams and Barclay 1988; Harpaz and Chothia 1994).

IgSF proteins are widely distributed across the tree of life, including in organisms as evolutionarily distant as bacteria (Bodelón et al. 2013). However, the presence of Ig-like genes in such distant species does not necessarily imply a shared common ancestor. Rather, it has been proposed that the high prevalence of Ig-like folds in various organisms is a reflection of their energetically favorable and stable structure (Orengo et al. 1994; Shapiro et al. 1995). This also partly explains why many IgSF proteins, despite sharing the same fold, share less than 35% sequence identity (Chattopadhyay et al. 2009). This extensive sequence variation has driven functional diversification, implicating IgSF proteins in many processes such as cell–cell communication, immune recognition, and development (Barclay 2003). Although they are involved in a variety of functions, they are most often associated with the adaptive immune system, as they form the key functional units that enable antigen specific recognition.

The adaptive immune system provides long-lasting, highly specific protection against pathogens through the recognition of their unique molecular features. In gnathostomes, this specificity is mediated by just two immunoglobulin receptor complexes, the TCR and the BCR. Upon activation, B cells secrete antibodies encoded by the same genes as the BCR, with antigen-binding domains identical to those of the membrane-bound receptor. TCRs recognize peptides presented by MHCs, whereas BCRs bind intact antigens directly. The ability of TCRs and BCRs to specifically engage a vast assortment of antigens relies on the genetic processing of these protein complexes. In developing T and B cells, the enzymes RAG1 and RAG2 mediate V(D)J recombination, which assemble variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) segments into unique complementarity-determining regions (CDRs). BCRs additionally undergo somatic hypermutation where activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) deaminates cytidines in the heavy, κ, and λ chains of developing B cells producing U:G mismatches. When repair pathways process these mismatches, mutations are introduced, further contributing to BCR diversity (Di Noia and Neuberger 2007).

While TCRs and BCRs exemplify the IgSF's role in generating antigen-specific recognition, the majority of IgSF proteins instead display invariable interfaces and function as co-receptors, shaping the threshold, strength, and duration of adaptive immune response without directly contacting antigens (Cannon et al. 2010). A classic example is the Ig–Ig interaction between CD80 on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and CD28 on T cells, which promotes T-cell activation (Vandenborre et al. 1999). By contrast, engagement of CD80 with the IgSF protein CTLA-4 dampens T-cell activation (Vandenborre et al. 1999). Many additional IgSF interactions that fine-tune adaptive immunity are already known (Barclay 2003; Li et al. 2025), and many more remain to be discovered (Grudman et al. 2025).

The goal of this study was to examine the human IgSF through an evolutionary framework, grouping IgSF proteins into distinct categories based on their conservation and first appearance over time across eukaryotes. To achieve this, we downloaded and analyzed all available eukaryotic reference genomes to trace the presence of human IgSF proteins across time. This evolutionary perspective provides insight into the origins and diversification of these proteins and may help infer functional roles for relatively understudied IgSF members. Although it is not feasible to describe every IgSF protein in detail, we highlight several interesting examples and provide a user-friendly program that allows readers to further explore the conservation of any human IgSF protein of interest.

Results

In total, 4,614 genomes were retrieved from the NCBI. BUSCO quality assessment of genome completeness removed 30.7% of assemblies, leaving 3,199 genomes. Among these, all metazoans and only 36 of the 2076 non-metazoans were found to contain Ig proteins. Of the retained complete genomes, 477 were non-gnathostome metazoans and 646 were gnathostomes, yielding a final dataset of 1159 genomes.

Phylogenetic Tree

While the evolutionary history of gnathostomes has already been well established using hundreds (Steinke et al. 2012) to thousands (Chen et al. 2015) of genes, we sought to reproduce this trajectory with only three genes: RAG1, RAG2, and AID. These proteins were selected because they play essential roles in enabling the gnathostome adaptive immune system to detect and effectively respond against a diverse and continuously changing range of pathogens. Additionally, while these genes are universally conserved among gnathostomes, they display enough sequential diversity to yield a tree with sufficient lineage separation between taxonomic classes (Fig. S4).

Consistency Among Missing BUSCO-Detected Universal Orthologs

When filtering out assemblies with low BUSCO completeness scores, we noticed 280 of the 349 (around 80%) of the excluded gnathostome genomes belonged to Aves (birds) (Fig. S7a and b). This raised the question of whether birds lack a subset of these so-called “universal” vertebrate orthologs. To examine this, the Aves genomes were first grouped by taxonomic order to determine whether any order consistently lacked a universal ortholog (Fig. S7c). However, no such pattern was observed. The completeness of Aves genomes showed no correlation with order, suggesting technical sequencing or annotation issues rather than genuine biological gene loss.

We next assessed whether any universal ortholog was consistently missing among all Aves. Strikingly, every Aves assembly lacked the highly conserved and essential gene mitochondrial transcription elongation factor (TEFM). To double check this finding, the Crocodylus porosus TEFM sequence (UniProt A0A7M4E0G3) was queried using NCBIs blast server, specifically against Aves, and identified only one low-confidence hit: annotated as “LOW QUALITY PROTEIN” XP_075026227.1 from the seabird Calonectris borealis. This sequence belonged to a genome deposited after our dataset was downloaded and showed an e-value (1 × 10−20) with very limited coverage (27%). No other hits with an e value <1 were found.

These observations suggest several possibilities: (i) Aves TEFM may have diverged extensively, making it undetectable by standard homology methods; (ii) technical issues leave every Aves genome incomplete; (iii) TEFM has been functionally replaced by another protein; or (iv) proteins with overlapping functions have evolved to compensate for its loss. We repeated this analysis across all major evolutionary lineages included in this study recording the missing universal orthologs for each taxon (Fig. S7d–f, Table S4). However, these results should be interpreted cautiously, as many groups have a limited number of reference assemblies available (Fig. S2a).

Functional and evolutionary classification of the human IgSF

Human IgSF genes were grouped according to their evolutionary origin and predominant biological functions. Representative examples from each group are included, accompanied by brief functional descriptions.

To quantitatively assess whether evolutionary groupings correspond to functional categories, we performed GO enrichment analysis across IgSF groups using PANTHER GO-Slim biological process annotations (FDR < 1 × 10−2) (Ashburner et al. 2000; Thomas et al. 2021; Aleksander et al. 2026). The results are summarized in an UpSet plot (Fig. 1b), which captures both the number of significantly enriched terms per group and the extent of overlap between groups. Complete enrichment results across four annotation sets (PANTHER GO-Slim biological processes, GO biological processes, PANTHER GO-Slim molecular functions, and GO molecular functions) are also provided (Table S5).

Each evolutionary group exhibits a distinct set of enriched GO terms, with relatively few higher-order intersections shared across multiple groups. For the Orange group, the top enriched terms include immune response–regulating signaling pathway (GO:0002764), regulation of immune response (GO:0050776), and regulation of immune system process (GO:0002682). The Green group is enriched for T-cell activation (GO:0042110), lymphocyte activation (GO:0046649), and leukocyte activation (GO:0045321). The Red group is enriched for in cell adhesion (GO:0007155), immune system process (GO:0002376), and lymphocyte activation (GO:0046649). The Pink group is enriched for positive regulation of immune response (GO:0050778), positive regulation of immune system process (GO:0002684), and immune system process (GO:0002376). The Brown group is enriched for developmental processes, including cellular anatomical entity morphogenesis (GO:0032989), cellular developmental process (GO:0048869), and cell differentiation (GO:0030154). And the Purple group is enriched for cell–cell adhesion (GO:0098609), multicellular organism development (GO:0007275), and cell adhesion (GO:0007155). Many of the mammal-specific IgSF genes appear to have evolved to fine-tune immune activation thresholds to support maternal–fetal tolerance. Within this group, killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) primarily recognize classical MHC class I molecules (Pollock et al. 2022), whereas the closely related leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptors (LILRs) engage nonclassical MHC class I ligands such as HLA-E, HLA-F, and HLA-G, which are highly expressed on trophoblasts and placental cells (Redondo-Garcia et al. 2023). These interactions recruit inhibitory signaling pathways that suppress cytotoxic responses, thereby promoting immune tolerance essential for successful pregnancy. Many of the mammal-specific IgSF members are also key regulators of NK cell activity, including those represented in the Orange group in Table 1 (Pollock et al. 2022; Colonna 2023; Redondo-Garcia et al. 2023; Andrzejczak and Karabon 2024; Chen et al. 2025; Luca 2025), as well as non-represented receptors in the Orange group such as CD244, CD48, and CD160 (McNerney et al. 2005; Tu et al. 2015). Although NK cells are not exclusive to mammals, they play a central role in mediating maternal–fetal tolerance (Riley and Yokoyama 2008; Zhang and Wei 2021; Joo et al. 2024).

By contrast, IgSF genes restricted to Tetrapoda and Amniota are largely composed of proteins that modulate the strength and duration of immune responses and form many of the critical components of the immune synapse between APCs and T cells. Interestingly, proteins in the Amniota (Green group), such as CD80, CD86, ICAM1–3, and ICOS, are less evolutionarily conserved than their canonical binding partners CTLA-4 and CD28, found in the Tetrapoda (Red group), even though all are considered essential for forming the APC—T-cell synapse. The low conservation and repetitive nature of these proteins suggest that gnathostomes outside of mammals may rely on alternative molecules to fulfill similar immune-synapse roles. Moreover, even when homologs are present among Tetrapoda, their poor sequence conservation implies that they likely do not function identically to their human counterparts (Lu et al. 2022).

IgSF proteins that emerged within the gnathostomata exhibit a wide range of functions, including the foundational establishment of co-stimulatory signaling through cell–cell adhesion mediated by families such as the butyrophilins (Abeler-Dörner et al. 2012) and nectins (Takai et al. 2008). While these molecules play an important role in shaping adaptive immune communications, the most transformative advancement central to the adaptive immune system was the appearance of the CD1 and MHC class I and II molecules (Wieczorek et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2023). These proteins form the core machinery enabling antigen presentation to lymphocytes. Accessory proteins, such as β2-microglobulin (B2M), which is essential for assembling the MHC class I complex (Wieczorek et al. 2017), and TAPBP/TAPBPL, chaperones that load and stabilize peptides within the MHC (Margulies et al. 2020), arose concurrently.

A major expansion of IgSF proteins occurred in vertebrates prior to the emergence of the gnathostomata. These ancestral IgSF members, such as JAMs (Ebnet 2017), IL1 receptors (Boraschi et al. 2017), and IGLONs (Salluzzo et al. 2023), primarily functioned in cell adhesion, axon guidance, and innate defense. Although several of these proteins later acquired roles supporting adaptive immunity, such as CADM/CRTAM (Sukhanov et al. 2021), VSIGs (Zhou et al. 2022), and Basigin (Muramatsu and Miyauchi 2003), this functional diversification likely coincided with the evolution of the gnathostome adaptive immune system.

Outside of vertebrates, the set of IgSF clusters present across metazoans is relatively small. While many of these proteins retain roles in cell–cell adhesion, others use their Ig domain to primarily contribute to structural stability, domain spacing, or receptor organization rather than receptor binding. For instance, in proteins that also contain leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains, adhesion is primarily mediated through the LRR domains rather than the Ig domains since this group of proteins (cluster 33) always have their LRR domain located at the N terminus. Similarly, metazoan IgSF proteins that use their Ig domains chiefly as structural scaffolds include TTN (Gigli et al. 2016), OBSCN (Grogan et al. 2020), and HSPG2 (Hayes et al. 2022). Importantly, IgSF families that do mediate cell–cell interactions are not restricted to immune regulation. Several evolutionarily conserved families, including those involved in neural development, like the UNC5 family (Zhu et al. 2021), rely on Ig-mediated interactions for processes like axon guidance and tissue organization. Despite their deep conservation, many of these families have undergone substantial expansion in vertebrate genomes, where lineage-specific diversification may have enabled the acquisition of distinct or specialized functions.

Receptor Tyrosine Kinases and Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Receptors

To this point, our investigation has taken a global view of the human IgSF. However, a more granular approach can also be taken. Suppose you were interested in ancient, catalytically inactive receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) and protein tyrosine phosphatase receptors (PTPRs). PTK7 is one such RTK (Mossie et al. 1995). PTK7 is essential for planar cell polarity through the non-canonical Wnt pathway and its disruption has been shown to cause severe morphogenetic defects in mammals, Xenopus, and Drosophila (Lu et al. 2004; Lhoumeau et al. 2014). Similarly, the phosphatase PTPRU is likewise catalytically inactive but functionally essential, binding, rather than dephosphorylating, β-catenin and p120-catenin in a redox-dependent manner controlling their availability for signaling (Hay et al. 2020). While these regulatory activities occur through the intracellular noncatalytic domains of PTK7 and PTPRU, their extracellular Ig-like regions mediate cell–cell adhesion. PTK7's extracellular Ig domains interact with VANGL2, CELSR1, and other PTK7 receptors (Paudyal et al. 2010), while PTPRU's extracellular Ig domains interacts with other R2B family members (Hay et al. 2020).

Across evolutionary lineages, PTK7 is deeply conserved across metazoans, whereas PTPRU is restricted to gnathostomes, despite PTPRs first appearing in metazoans (Fig. 2a). The number of PTPR paralogs remains relatively stable across metazoans. In contrast, the numbers of RTK paralogs differ substantially in more ancient taxa, and their numbers only stabilize among vertebrates (Fig. 2b).

B7 Ligands

The B7 ligand family has an outsized role as co-signaling molecules that modulate T-cell activity and exhaustion. To further examine this family within our dataset, we identified all clusters containing annotated B7 ligands. Cluster 40 in our set includes PD-L1 (B7-H1, CD274), PD-L2 (B7-DC, CD273), CD276 (B7-H3), and ICOS-L (B7-H2, CD275); cluster 83 includes CD80 (B7-1) and CD86 (B7-2); cluster 89 includes VTCN1 (B7-H4, B7x) and HHLA2 (B7-H7, B7y); cluster 90 includes NCR3LG1 (B7-H6); and cluster 126 includes VSIR (B7-H5).

Despite their shared naming convention, the B7 ligands are not all evolutionarily related, as reflected by their separate groupings. However, when the human IgSF dendrogram (Fig. S3) is cut at a slightly more relaxed threshold of 0.7 instead of 0.63, clusters 40 and 89 merge (PD-L1, PD-L2, CD276, ICOS-L, VTCN1, and HHLA2). In contrast, for CD80/CD86 to join this group, the dendrogram would need to be cut at an extreme value of 0.98, and for VSIR, at 0.99. Meanwhile the butyrophilins (cluster 14), non-B7 ligands merge at a cutoff of 0.85. At a cutoff of 1.0, all IgSF proteins including the last ungrouped B7 ligand, NCR3LG, converge. However, NCR3LG1 more readily clusters with TAPBP and TAPBPL (cluster 25) at the cutoff 0.85.

CD80 and CD86, though among some of the best-characterized IgSF proteins, are poorly conserved across gnathostomes, whereas VSIR and CD276 are present among all gnathostomes (Fig. 2c). Interestingly, CD276 is far more conserved than its cluster partners (PD-L1, PD-L2, and ICOSLG), suggesting that it retains a function closest to the cluster's ancestral homolog, while the others likely adapted lineage-specific immune-modulatory functions (Fig. 2c). Among gnathostomes, the number of B7 ligand paralogs is generally consistent, except in Testudines (turtles) and Cladistia (ray-finned bony fish), which exhibit a marked expansion in paralogs related to proteins in clusters 40, 89, and 90 (Fig. 2d).

CD58

Large differences in conservation can be observed among the mammalian specific members of the IgSF. In most cases, there is a large drop in conservation between the three mammalian lineages (Monotremata (egg laying mammals), Marsupialia (marsupials), and Eutheria (placental mammals)), though with some exceptions. For example, CD58 (LFA-3) is absent from Rodentia and Lagomorpha, despite being retained across all other mammalian taxa (Fig. 2e and f). Given that Rodentia (rodents) and Lagomorpha (rabbits, hares, pikas) form part of the Euarchontoglires (a clade within Eutheria including Dermoptera (flying lemurs) and Primates), this absence reflects a lineage-specific loss in their common ancestor. Although the loss of CD58 might imply that its function is dispensable, this is not the case. CD58 acts analogously to CD80: both are expressed on APCs, but whereas CD80 binds CD28, CD58 binds CD2. Both interactions stabilize the immunological synapse and promote T-cell activation (Selvaraj et al. 1987). To maintain this function, CD48 has evolved to functionally replace CD58 in Rodentia and Lagomorpha (Davis et al. 2006). While the rodent CD2–CD48 interaction exhibits a roughly 10 to 30-fold lower affinity than the human CD2–CD58 interaction, it remains sufficient to support T-cell adhesion and activation (Davis et al. 2006).

Discussion

Two major expansions of the IgSF occurred, first, with the emergence of metazoans, and later, with the emergence of gnathostomes (Fig. S2e and f). Each expansion coincided with increased regulatory needs in the corresponding organisms. In early metazoans, IgSF molecules evolved primarily to mediate cell–cell recognition, adhesion, and communication, processes fundamental to multicellularity, tissue organization, and barrier integrity. The ability to distinguish an organism's own cells from foreign agents, conferred largely through Ig domains, provided a foundation which eventually gave rise to the innate immune system. Many invertebrates still thrive with only innate immunity, which raises the obvious question: why did the adaptive immune system emerge? It is evolutionarily costly, requiring intricate mechanisms to prevent autoimmunity (Hedrick 2004), and in the absence of strong selective pressure, vertebrates, including Atlantic cod (Solem and Stenvik 2006), seahorses (Matsunaga and Rahman 2006), and axolotl (Kaufman et al. 2006), lose key adaptive immunity components.

The earliest gnathostomes are thought to have been predatory, using their newly evolved jaws to prey on a diverse range of ecological niches. The consumption of foreign tissue and increased susceptibility to wounds accompanying this lifestyle caused more exposure to diverse pathogens. Moreover, early gnathostomes such as placoderms and ancestral sharks appear to have been larger-bodied creating greater internal microbial diversity and new demands for immune regulation of commensal versus pathogenic microbes. Finally, the most ancient surviving gnathostomes, sharks, are notably long-lived (Stevens 2000). Extended lifespans often evolve in species with prolonged reproductive investment, and indeed, numerous shark species exhibit diverse forms of viviparity that allow them to protect and nourish developing offspring (Katona et al. 2023). Collectively, these adaptations would have exerted strong selective pressure for the emergence of an immune system capable of generating durable, specific, and tightly regulated responses.

The human IgSF comprises a vast and highly diverse set of proteins whose functions cannot be inferred from sequential or even structural homology alone (Rubinstein et al. 2013; Yap et al. 2014). Adopting an evolutionary perspective enables these proteins to be organized into groups in which related biological functions naturally cluster together. We note that inference becomes less reliable with increasing evolutionary distance from humans, particularly for IgSF proteins, which exhibit substantial sequence divergence across metazoans. Our framework was intentionally designed to be conservative, prioritizing specificity over sensitivity, and thus may miss deeply diverged orthologs, especially within rapidly evolving families. Accordingly, the reported “first appearance” of a given protein or cluster should be interpreted as the earliest robust detection under our methodology, rather than a definitive statement of evolutionary origin.

In protein research, not all proteins are studied equally (Edwards et al. 2011). This disparity does not reflect a lack of importance, but rather the challenge of justifying research investment for proteins that lack a solid research foundation (Grudman et al. 2025). Moreover, determining biological significance is not as simple as assessing evolutionary conservation. While strong conservation is consistent with functional importance the opposite is not true. For example, several B7 family ligands show limited conservation across gnathostomes, yet in humans they play indispensable roles in T-cell and NK regulation. So, while proteins within the Red, Green, and Orange groups from Table 1 cannot be excluded as functionally important, those in the Pink group are particularly compelling, as their emergence coincided with the appearance of the adaptive immune system. Some examples of poorly studied proteins within this group include VSTM4, VSTM5, TMIGD1, the MFAP family, and the PTGFRN family (Grudman et al. 2025).

Although all B7 ligands share the same naming convention, they are not necessarily evolutionarily related, as indicated by an alternative clustering approach (Yap et al. 2014). Rather, the B7 designation appears to persist for both pragmatic and sociological reasons. From a pragmatic standpoint, members share at least one Ig fold and function as co-signaling molecules that modulate T-cell activation, though often through distinct pathways. Sociologically, the label “B7” has become a kind of branding mechanism, where attaching it to a newly characterized receptor generates immediate attention and excitement in the field. This trend is far removed from the original context, where CD80 (the first B7 ligand) was named simply because it was the seventh antigen identified on B cells (Freeman et al. 1989). Ironically, many of its so-called relatives are not even expressed on B cells. While protein names may seem trivial to most researchers, it poses a real source of confusion for those examining the evolutionary relationships between proteins.

This study highlights several IgSF proteins as representative examples, but readers may wish to explore additional genes of interest. To facilitate this, we developed a user-friendly Python script that enables the quantification of IgSF sequence conservation and changes in cluster composition across four evolutionary scales (eukaryotes, gnathostomes, mammals, and primates). The script allows searches by GeneID, gene name, accession number, human cluster ID, or evolutionary group and can be found here: https://github.com/StevenGrudman/IgSF-Conservation.

Materials and Methods

Genome Collection

All annotated eukaryotic reference genome assemblies were downloaded from the NCBI Datasets (O’Leary et al. 2024) in September 2024 (Fig. 3a). The taxonomies of each assembly were determined by NCBI's E-utilities tool (Table S1) (Sayers 2009).

Methods overview. Top) Summary of the analytical steps performed. Middle) Databases and computational tools used for each step. Bottom) The number of human proteins retained at each stage or representative visualizations of the corresponding analyses. Panel 5 displays the clustered human IgSF, with the size of each square being proportional to the number of proteins in each cluster.

Clustering of Genome Assemblies

To reduce protein redundancy and computational workload, genome assemblies were clustered at 85% sequence identity using CD-HIT (Fig. 3b) (Fu et al. 2012). This threshold was chosen as sequences with >85% sequence identity are expected to share identical Gene Ontology (GO) biological process and molecular function annotations (Joshi and Xu 2007). Blast Libraries were then constructed on the clustered protein genomes using BLAST + v2.13.0 (Camacho et al. 2009).

Quality Assessment

Although we restricted our downloads to the NCBI reference genomes, their quality varied considerably. To assess completeness, we applied Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) (Seppey et al. 2019) which identifies universal orthologs within each genome. Only genomes containing >90% of the expected BUSCO set were retained (Fig. 3c) (Hanschen et al. 2020). The number of genomes within each major evolutionary lineage was then quantified (Fig. S2a–c), and the total number of sequences per taxonomic group was also recorded (Fig. S2d).

Identification of IgSF Proteins

The retained genomes were analyzed with InterProScan (Jones et al. 2014) using the SUPERFAMILY database (Gough and Chothia 2002) to identify proteins belonging to the IgSF (Fig. 3d). The SUPERFAMILY database accomplishes this by utilizing a library of hidden Markov models based on the SCOP protein domain classification system (Andreeva et al. 2014; Murzin et al. 2020). We did not use the CATH-Gene3D database (Dawson et al. 2017), as it proved to be less conservative and frequently misclassified proteins with folds resembling Ig domains (e.g. fibronectin type III, major sperm protein, polycystic kidney disease domains).

Clustering of IgSF

The Brotherhood algorithm, which determines protein relatedness through shared intermediate BLAST hits (Rubinstein et al. 2013), was used to cluster IgSF sets (Fig. 3e) (Rubinstein et al. 2013). The human IgSF was clustered alone, while each additional species-specific IgSF set was clustered jointly with the human IgSF. For each set, every Ig protein was queried against a non-redundant (nr) BLAST database (downloaded in 2019 and clustered at 90% sequence identity using CD-HIT (Fu et al. 2012)). Hits with e-value < 1 × 10−10 were retained, and an n × n similarity matrix (where n = the size of the combined IgSF set) was generated, with each cell representing the proportion of sequences identified in the database that are shared between two IgSF proteins, normalized to the size of the smaller set. The similarity matrix was converted into a distance matrix using the correlation metric and condensed using scipy.spatial.distance.squareform (Virtanen et al. 2020). Agglomerative clustering was then performed using the weighted method of the scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage function, and the resulting hierarchical tree was cut at the empirically determined threshold 0.63 (Figs. S2e–g and S3). This threshold was chosen to effectively distinguish several evolutionarily distinct groups, specifically separating Cluster 9 (neuronal axon guidance and synapse formation; e.g. L1CAM/DCC/ROBO/CNTN) from Cluster 19 (cell junction and synaptic maintenance; e.g. NCAM/KIRREL/MDGA), as well as Cluster 13 (large sarcomeric scaffolding proteins; TTN/OBSCN/HSPG2) from Cluster 16 (smaller filament organizers; MYBPC/MYOM).

Identification of Membrane-Bound IgSF Proteins With Extracellular Ig Domains

We are particularly focused on co-stimulatory IgSF proteins that modulate adaptive immune responses through membrane-mediated interactions. To this end, we considered filtering each species IgSF using Phobius (Käll et al. 2004) to identify Ig proteins with a transmembrane domain, and interproScan (Jones et al. 2014) to confirm the presence of at least one extracellular Ig domain. However, this approach proved imperfect, as Phobius (Käll et al. 2004) occasionally misclassified proteins leading to the incorrect exclusion of relevant IgSF proteins. This limitation was not unique to Phobius (Käll et al. 2004); substituting it with AlphaFold (Jumper et al. 2021) yielded similar results. Ultimately, we chose not to include this filtering step in our analysis to not preclude any relevant orthologs, although we will still provide the corresponding information for the human IgSF (Table S2).

Creation of Gnathostome Phylogenetic Tree

Within our dataset of complete gnathostome genomes, BLAST searches were conducted using the protein sequences for RAG1, RAG2, and AID from three evolutionarily distant gnathostomes: Homo sapiens (human, GCF_000001405.40), Latimeria chalumnae (West Indian Ocean coelacanth, GCF_037176945.1), and Carcharodon carcharias (great white shark, GCF_017639515.1). For each gene, the top hit from any of the searches was retained, and the resulting sequences per species were concatenated. Gnathostomes were separated into eleven groups and representative species were chosen based on the strongest combined e-values for RAG1, RAG2, and AID and the concatenated sequences were aligned using ClustalW (Larkin et al. 2007). The alignment was analyzed with IQ-TREE (Lanfear et al. 2020), which reconstructs phylogenies under the maximum-likelihood framework using the Felsenstein pruning algorithm (Felsenstein 1981). Within IQ-TREE, ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) identified JTT + R3 as the best-fitting substitution matrix (Jones et al. 1992) and rate heterogeneity model (Soubrier et al. 2012) using a Bayesian approach. The resulting Newick tree file was visualized with FigTree (Rambaut 2014) (Fig. S4).

Ortholog Assignments Between IgSF Proteins

To determine the orthologous relationships between the human IgSF and the 1,158 additional eukaryotic genomes identified to contain Ig proteins, a custom Python script was developed to assign one-to-one orthologs between two IgSF proteomes using reciprocal best BLAST hits, normalized by self–bit scores, and query coverage (Fig. 3f). For each comparison, ortholog searches were performed as follows: an IgSF protein from human (species 1) was BLAST against its own IgSF to obtain its “self–bit score.” The same protein was then BLAST against the IgSF of another species (species 2), and all hits with e-values < 1 × 10−5 were retained. Bit scores from this interspecies search were normalized by the self-bit score of the query protein.

Following this, ortholog assignments were resolved to ensure one-to-one mappings. When multiple queries from species 1 matched the same subject in species 2, only the subject for the query yielding the highest normalized bit score was retained. Remaining queries were iteratively reassigned to their next best available subject until no two queries shared the same subject. This entire process was then repeated with the species reversed (species 2 → species 1). Ortholog pairs were retained only when both species reciprocally identified each other as their top ortholog candidate. The confidence of each ortholog pair was evaluated based on its normalized bit score and query coverage.

To determine an appropriate threshold for excluding false positives, we benchmarked our ortholog assignment method against other established ortholog assignment methods compiled by DIOPT (Hu et al. 2011). DIOPT integrates multiple ortholog-finding tools and assigns scores based on the agreement of tools independently tasked to predict ortholog pairs. We compared our custom script against DIOPT for the six model organisms present in our dataset. This comparison confirmed the reliability of our method and allowed us to define optimal filtering thresholds: ortholog pairs with a normalized bit score <0.2 or query coverage <0.4 were excluded (Fig. S5). Although these thresholds are strict, they help reduce false assignments. In many cases, our custom script and the DIOPT-supported methods produced matching results below these thresholds; however, further inspection of these matches showed them to be frequently incorrect.

Due to these strict cutoffs, our approach may fail to assign orthologs that diverge dramatically over large evolutionary distances. Accordingly, the inferred “first appearance” of a human IgSF cluster should be interpreted as the earliest robust detection under our pipeline rather than definitive evidence of the clusters true evolutionary origin. Detecting more distantly related orthologs will likely require complementary approaches, such as synteny-based methods (Haas et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2012)

Evolutionary Presence of Human IgSF Proteins

To evaluate the evolutionary distribution of human IgSF clusters across 20 major eukaryotic taxa, we checked for the presence of at least one ortholog from each human IgSF cluster. For each taxon, the fraction of genomes containing members of a given cluster was calculated, producing a presence–absence matrix (133 human IgSF clusters × 20 taxa). This matrix was hierarchically clustered using the scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage function (Virtanen et al. 2020) using the Ward method and Euclidean distance metric (Fig. 1a, Fig. S6, Table S3).

IgSF Sequence Conservation Across Evolutionary Lineages

To assess the degree of sequence conservation for each human IgSF gene, normalized bit scores were compiled for all orthologs within a given taxon and averaged to produce a single mean conservation value per gene–taxon pair. When multiple isoforms were present for the same gene, only the isoform with the highest normalized bit score was used in the average. The resulting gene-by-taxon conservation matrix thus represents the mean normalized sequence similarity of each human IgSF gene across major evolutionary lineages. These data provide a basis for distinguishing genes exhibiting deep evolutionary conservation from those that are lineage specific (Fig. 2a, c and e).

Change in IgSF Cluster Size Across Evolutionary Lineages

To quantify changes in IgSF cluster composition across evolutionary lineages, the human IgSF was clustered with each extracted eukaryotic IgSF using the Brotherhood algorithm (Rubinstein et al. 2013). The number of IgSF members per cluster was counted separately for human and non-human assemblies, and the difference in cluster size was calculated. Positive values indicate clusters with more human proteins relative to non-human counterparts, while negative values represent the opposite (Fig. 2b, d and f).
