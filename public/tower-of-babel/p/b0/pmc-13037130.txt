Global, regional, and national burden of early-onset prostate cancer, 1990–2021: a systematic analysis for the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: You Ma, Yiwei Liu, Yiming Liu, Jiali Li, Bin Wu, Weidong Yu, Cheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly-onset prostate cancer (EOPC), commonly defined as prostate cancer diagnosed before age 55 years, may differ from later-onset disease in epidemiology and clinical impact. However, the global burden of EOPC among younger adults and its variation by socio-demographic development, including patterns during the COVID-19 period, remain incompletely characterized.MethodsUsing the Global Burden of Disease 2021 dataset, we quantified the burden of EOPC among individuals aged 20–54 years from 1990 to 2021 at global, regional, and national levels. We evaluated incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and corresponding age-standardized rates. Temporal trends were assessed using Joinpoint regression and summarized as average annual percent change. We decomposed changes in DALYs into contributions from population growth, population aging, and epidemiologic change. Health inequality was assessed across Socio-demographic Index (SDI) strata using inequality metrics. The COVID-19 period (2019–2021) was analyzed as a predefined subperiod. Future burden through 2045 was projected using the Nordpred model.ResultsGlobally, incident EOPC cases and age-standardized incidence increased over 1990–2021, while age-standardized mortality and DALY rates declined. Burden patterns varied substantially by SDI, with an increasing relative concentration of DALYs in lower-SDI settings. Decomposition indicated that population growth and aging were major contributors to rising DALYs. During 2019–2021, the age-standardized incidence rate persisted in rising across middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions. Projections suggested a continued, moderate increase in EOPC burden through 2045.ConclusionsThe global burden of EOPC is increasing, with widening disparities and a growing relative burden in lower-SDI settings. These findings support risk-stratified early evaluation and strengthening timely diagnosis and standardized care in resource-limited regions, alongside more equitable allocation of cancer control resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04261-2.Keywords: Early-onset prostate cancer, Global burden of disease, Disability-adjusted life years, Incidence, Mortality

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most common malignancies affecting men globally, ranking as the second most frequently diagnosed cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. According to the United States cancer statistics for 2024, prostate cancer is projected to account for approximately 299,010 new cases and 35,250 deaths. These figures underscore the significant and ongoing threat prostate cancer poses to men’s health [2]. Since the widespread implementation of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening in the 1990s, there has been a notable increase in the global incidence of prostate cancer [3]. Age distribution data reveal a distinct trend of prostate cancer incidence clustering in older populations. The incidence rate increases progressively with age, reaching its peak in the 70–74-year age group [4]. Early-onset prostate cancer (EOPC), defined as prostate cancer diagnosed in men younger than 55 years [5], has not shown a clear stabilization or decline in most populations. On the contrary, some countries, such as Australia, have reported a rapid increase in its incidence [6]. Consequently, EOPC has become an increasing area of clinical focus.

Compared with older patients, individuals with EOPC face more severe clinical challenges. These patients often experience diagnosis during critical stages of their careers and family life, leading not only to impaired physical health but also substantial economic and social burdens. In recent years, substantial evidence has indicated that EOPC represents a distinct clinicopathological phenotype. It is characterized by higher aggressiveness, a shorter latency period, and poorer prognosis [7, 8]. A large population-based cohort study further revealed that prostate cancer-specific mortality among EOPC patients was 22% higher compared to those diagnosed between 60 and 70 years of age. Further analysis showed that among patients classified as high-risk based on clinical T stage (≥ T3a), pre-treatment PSA levels (> 20 ng/ml), and biopsy Gleason scores (8–10), those with EOPC had the highest risk of undergoing secondary cancer treatment within 180 days following primary therapy [9]. In recent years, EOPC has gained increasing attention due to its distinct clinical and epidemiological characteristics. However, previous assessments of its disease burden have mostly been limited to specific regions and relied on relatively outdated data [10, 11]. Existing analyses based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study have primarily focused on describing temporal variations in incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), serving largely descriptive purposes [12, 13]. Therefore, additional analyses are needed to supplement current evidence and enhance understanding of the evolving patterns of EOPC in younger populations.

GBD study is a standardized and continuously updated epidemiological database that enables comparable estimates across countries, time periods, and age groups [14–16]. Using the GBD 2021 dataset, this study comprehensively evaluated the global, regional, and national burden of EOPC among individuals aged 20–54 years from 1990 to 2021. We assessed core indicators of disease burden, including incidence, mortality, DALYs, and their corresponding age-standardized rates. Temporal trends were characterized using average annual percent change (AAPC) derived from Joinpoint analysis, and fluctuations during the COVID-19 pandemic were additionally examined. Furthermore, decomposition analysis was applied to quantify the relative contributions of demographic and epidemiologic changes to DALYs, while health inequality analysis was conducted to explore disparities across socioeconomic strata. Future disease burden was projected to 2045 using the Nordpred model; importantly, internal validation based on GBD data demonstrated favorable predictive accuracy. Through these complementary analyses, our study provides updated evidence to support optimized allocation of medical resources, inform guideline development, and advance targeted strategies for the prevention and control of EOPC.

Methods

Data Sources

Data were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study 2021 (GBD 2021), coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. The dataset provides annual estimates of incidence, mortality, and DALYs for malignant neoplasms across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 [17]. Based on the GBD cause definition for prostate cancer and the corresponding ICD-10 code C61, we extracted the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR), age specific rates, and counts for EOPC among individuals aged 20 to 54 years. Because GBD reports estimates in standard 5-year age bands, we aggregated data for ages 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, and 50–54 years. Age standardization followed the GBD 2021 standard population, and the corresponding age group proportions are provided in Table S1 as the global reference structure. All uncertainty intervals were taken directly from GBD estimates and reflect input data heterogeneity and model uncertainty. Data are available from the Global Health Data Exchange at https://healthdata.org.

Statistical analysis

We assessed the burden of EOPC using incidence, deaths, DALYs, and age-standardized rates, including ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR per 100,000 population, with 95% uncertainty intervals as reported by GBD 2021. Analyses were stratified by the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) quintiles, where SDI is a composite measure of socioeconomic development and countries are grouped into five categories from low to high (Table S2) [14, 18]. We calculated percentage changes in ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR from 1990 to 2021 and summarized patterns globally and across SDI strata. Temporal trends were assessed using Joinpoint regression to estimate annual percent change(APC) and AAPC [19]. Statistical significance was determined using the 95% confidence interval, and APC and AAPC estimates were considered statistically significant when the interval did not include zero, using a two-sided significance level of 0.05 [20]. We also evaluated the COVID-19 period from 2019 to 2021 as a predefined subperiod and compared it with the preceding long-term period from 1990 to 2019. To quantify SDI related health inequality in DALY burden, we conducted inequality analyses using regression-based and concentration-based measures.

We applied the Das Gupta decomposition to partition the net change in incident cases, deaths, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021 into contributions from population growth, population aging, and epidemiologic change [21]. The method uses a counterfactual framework to estimate the change attributable to each component while holding the other components constant. We report component contributions as absolute changes and as percentages of the total net change, with percentage contributions calculated using the net change as the denominator, defined as Outcome in 2021 minus Outcome in 1990. Percentage values may exceed 100% or be negative when components move in opposite directions or when the net change is small, and interpretation should therefore consider absolute contributions together with percentages [22, 23].

We projected incidence, deaths, DALYs, and the corresponding age-standardized rates from 2022 to 2045 using the Nordpred model [24–26]. Projections were treated as baseline forecasts that assume continuation of recent patterns and do not explicitly model future changes in screening policy, diagnostic pathways, or treatment access. Internal validation was performed by fitting the model using data from 1990 to 2015 and comparing forecasts with GBD estimates for 2016 to 2021. Model performance was summarized using mean absolute error and mean absolute percentage error.

All analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 and Stata version 16. Additional implementation details are provided in the Supplementary Methods section.

Results

Global level

In 2021, there were 58,694.33 incident cases (95% UI: 52,071.67–63,631.91), 7,926.79 deaths (95% UI: 6,320.94–8,974.08), and 366,400.62 DALYs (95% UI: 296,427.19–416,858.60) attributable to EOPC (Table 1). The global ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR were 2.87 (95% UI: 2.55–3.12), 0.39 (95% UI: 0.31–0.44), and 18.09 (95% UI: 14.62–20.59), respectively (Table 1). From 1990 to 2021, the ASIR exhibited an increasing trend, with an AAPC of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.23–1.30). However, the ASMR and ASDR showed decreasing trends, with AAPCs of -0.44 (95% CI: -0.47 to -0.42) and − 0.29 (95% CI: -0.31 to -0.28), respectively (Table 1).

Regional level

In 2021, the highest ASIR was observed in the High SDI region (7.7 per 100,000, 95% UI: 7.21–8.25), while the lowest was in the Low SDI region (1.3 per 100,000, 95% UI: 0.79–1.72) (Table 1). Conversely, the highest ASMR and ASDR were in the Low SDI region, at 0.66 (95% UI: 0.40–0.89) and 27.77 (95% UI: 16.73–37.29) per 100,000, respectively (Table 1). The lowest ASMR and ASDR were observed in the High-middle SDI region, at 0.33 (95% UI: 0.29–0.38) and 15.93 (95% UI: 13.61–18.45) per 100,000, respectively (Table 1). The Low-middle SDI region showed the steepest upward trends from 1990 to 2021, with AAPCs of 2.47 (95% CI, 2.40–2.52) for ASIR, 0.73 (95% CI, 0.71–0.76) for ASMR, and 0.79 (95% CI, 0.76–0.82) for ASDR (Table 1). Table 2 summarizes changes during the COVID-19 period. During 2019–2021, ASIR exhibited an increasing trend in low, low-middle, and middle SDI regions, with AAPCs of 2.17 (95% CI: 1.21–3.15), 2.25 (95% CI: 1.57–2.94), and 2.82 (95% CI: 2.38–3.26), respectively. ASMR and ASDR remained generally stable, as did the overall burden globally and in other SDI regions.

National level

Marked heterogeneity was observed across 204 countries and territories. Kuwait exhibited a rapid increase in ASIR, with an AAPC of 4.72% (95% CI: 4.08–5.51) (Fig. 1D; Table S4). Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had the highest ASMR and ASDR, at 2.81 (95% UI: 2.26–3.42) and 123.54 (95% UI: 99.28–150.60) per 100,000, respectively (Fig. 1B, C; Table S5). Zambia showed the fastest increase in ASMR and ASDR, with AAPCs of 3.14 (95% CI: 3.06–3.23) and 3.18 (95% CI: 3.10–3.27), respectively (Fig. 1E, F; Table S4). Health inequality analysis in DALYs

Health inequality analyses showed a reversal in SDI-related inequality in DALY rates between 1990 and 2021. In 1990, the SII for DALY rates was 3 (95% UI: -4.23 to 10.31) (Fig. 2A). By 2021, the SII had reversed to -12 (95% UI: -20.28 to -3.95) (Fig. 2A). Changes in relative inequality were consistent with this pattern. The Concentration Index for DALY rates in 1990 was 0.14 (95% CI: 0.09 to 0.19) (Fig. 2B), and in 2021 it shifted to -0.07 (95% CI: -0.13 to 0) (Fig. 2B).

Joinpoint regression analysis

Joinpoint regression was used to characterize temporal trends in ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR globally and across SDI regions (Fig. 3; Table S6). In SDI-stratified analyses, ASIR in high SDI regions remained substantially higher than in other regions and peaked around 2010 before declining, whereas ASMR and ASDR in low SDI regions were higher and continued to rise (AAPC = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.42–0.45, and AAPC = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.47–0.50, respectively) (Fig. 3). During the COVID-19 period (2019–2021), ASIR continued to rise in middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions (Table 2).

Decomposition analysis

This study examined the contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes to shifts in EOPC burden (Fig. 4, Tables S7–S9). Globally, DALYs increased by 160,810.40, driven mainly by population growth at 124,184.88 (77.22%) and aging at 62,237.26 (38.70%), partly offset by epidemiologic change at minus 25,611.74 (-15.93%). Deaths increased by 3,321.66, with positive contributions from population growth at 2,736.85 (82.39%) and aging at 1,440.53 (43.37%), offset by epidemiologic change at minus 855.71 (-25.76%), while incident cases increased by 38,853.30, attributable to population growth at 15,896.36 (40.91%), epidemiologic change at 14,124.52 (36.35%), and aging at 8,832.42 (22.7%). Across SDI strata, population growth dominated increases in low and low middle SDI settings, whereas epidemiologic change was generally burden reducing for deaths and DALYs in higher SDI strata; percentage contributions exceeding 100% occurred when components offset and the net change was small, so interpretation should emphasize absolute contributions.

Future forecasts of global burden of EOPC

We used the Nordpred model to project the global burden of EOPC from 2021 to 2045. The number of incident cases, deaths, and DALYs were projected to rise from 58,694, 7,927, and 366,401 in 2021 to 81,142, 9,890, and 451,163 in 2045, respectively (Fig. 5, Table S10). The ASIR is predicted to increase slightly from 2.87 to 3.16 per 100,000 over the same period, whereas the ASMR is projected to remain approximately stable at around 0.39 per 100,000. The ASDR remains broadly stable, showing a slight decline from 18.09 to 17.86 per 100,000 between 2021 and 2045 (Fig. 5, Table S10). Internal validation demonstrated good predictive performance. Specifically, for age-standardized rates, the MAE and MAPE were 0.103 and 3.533% for the ASIR, 0.004 and 1.023% for the ASMR, and 0.174 and 0.961% for ASDR. For absolute counts, the MAE and MAPE were 2104.066 and 3.653% for incident cases, 77.309 and 1.002% for deaths, and 3350.624 and 0.939% for DALYs (Figure S1). These results support the overall reliability of the Nordpred-based projections.

Discussion

In this systematic analysis based on GBD 2021, we found that from 1990 to 2021, incident EOPC cases and ASIR increased overall, whereas ASMR and ASDR declined. At the same time, the burden showed marked heterogeneity across SDI strata, and the relative concentration of DALYs shifted toward lower SDI settings. Decomposition analysis indicated that demographic factors were the main drivers of burden change. We also observed short term divergence across SDI strata during the predefined COVID-19 subperiod from 2019 to 2021. Collectively, these findings suggest that, in resource limited settings, strengthening risk stratified evaluation, timely diagnosis, and access to standardized treatment may be central to reducing the burden of EOPC and narrowing disparities.

At the global level, EOPC incidence rose substantially from 1990 to 2010 and then declined gradually after 2010, while mortality and DALY rates declined throughout the study period. The early increase in incidence may reflect population growth and improved early detection [27]. In some countries and regions, greater PSA testing and higher diagnostic intensity may have increased the detection of early stage and asymptomatic disease, while also raising concerns about overdiagnosis and overtreatment [28]. Subsequently, more cautious screening practices and improvements in diagnostic pathways, including the use of MRI for lesion assessment and risk stratification, may be consistent with the observed decline in incidence [29, 30]. Over the same period, advances in treatment and management, greater health awareness, dietary changes, healthier lifestyles, and wider use of multidisciplinary care may have contributed to the sustained declines in mortality and DALY rates [31, 32].

Substantial heterogeneity was also observed across GBD regions and SDI strata. In low and low-middle SDI settings, incidence continued to rise, yet lower recorded incidence may still be underestimated because of limited PSA testing, constrained pathology and imaging capacity, and incomplete cancer registration. Although GBD integrates multiple data sources using statistical models, residual bias may remain in data sparse settings [33, 34]. In contrast, in high SDI settings, broader diagnostic availability may increase the detection of indolent tumors, which can inflate recorded incidence and increase the risk of overdiagnosis, a pattern consistent with the disease burden observed in high-income North America and Australia [28]. Persistently rising mortality and DALY rates in low SDI settings are more consistent with late diagnosis, limited treatment resources, and barriers to access to effective care [35, 36]. Inequality analyses further indicate that the EOPC burden is shifting from higher SDI to lower SDI settings. Decomposition analyses show that population growth and aging are the main contributors to the increasing burden, consistent with prior evidence on global prostate cancer burden patterns. However, because GBD estimates are aggregated, we cannot exclude the influence of unmeasured factors such as lifestyle, environmental exposures, and genetic differences [37]. Emerging molecular evidence, including mosaic loss of the Y chromosome, may help explain heterogeneity in risk and prognosis, but this cannot be evaluated directly in the current GBD based analysis and requires future clinical and molecular studies [38]. During the COVID-19 period from 2019 to 2021, ASIR diverged across SDI strata. ASIR increased in low, low-middle, and middle SDI settings but remained stable in high SDI settings. Because this time window was predefined for descriptive analyses, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis generating rather than causal. A plausible explanation is that the pandemic more strongly disrupted diagnostic services in resource limited settings, leading to delays and accumulated unmet need, whereas high SDI health systems were more resilient [39]. These hypotheses should be evaluated using real world indicators such as screening volume, diagnostic delays, and stage distribution.

At the national levels, policy and diagnostic intensity may influence observed incidence trends. For example, incidence decreased in the United States after the 2012 USPSTF recommendation against routine PSA screening [40, 41]. In Lithuania, a national PSA screening programme has been reported to coincide with a phase of increased incidence followed by a decline [42]. In the Bahamas, evidence from a prostate cancer screening clinic and decision analytical modeling suggests that PSA based screening is feasible, but its population level impact depends strongly on downstream diagnostic and treatment capacity [43, 44]. In Zambia, studies from the national context highlight delays in reaching diagnosis and initiating treatment related to health system barriers, which is consistent with higher mortality and DALY burden despite moderate recorded incidence [45, 46]. In addition, regional differences in environmental exposures, lifestyle, and dietary patterns may contribute to geographic variation. Higher obesity prevalence and bisphenol A exposure in high SDI settings may increase risk, whereas diets rich in lycopene and isoflavones in sub-Saharan Africa may be protective [47–50]. Recent national and regional studies also indicate that trends are complex. Registry based analyses in younger men in the United States show stage specific changes over time, and recent statistics suggest an increasing proportion of distant metastasis among men younger than 55 years [51, 52]. These observations indicate that rising incidence should not be interpreted solely as an effect of increased screening. Further validation using national cancer registries, screening volumes, diagnostic delay indicators, and stage distributions is needed.

Nordpred projections suggest that the global burden of EOPC will continue to increase through 2045. These projections underscore the need to strengthen early evaluation, reduce delays in diagnosis and treatment, and improve equitable access to care [53]. Tissue based genomic testing is increasingly used to support risk adapted management in prostate cancer, but real world uptake remains low, at about 1 to 2% in large national cohorts [54]. Where resources allow, more standardized integration of such testing into risk stratification and follow up for EOPC may help optimize outcomes and reduce unnecessary treatment.

Reducing these regional disparities requires targeted interventions. Evidence suggests that PSA screening can reduce prostate cancer mortality but may also increase overdiagnosis, overtreatment, quality of life loss, and costs [28]. Therefore, a risk stratified approach with shared decision making is preferable [55]. Low risk disease should be managed with active surveillance when appropriate, with escalation to invasive treatment when clear benefit is expected [29, 56]. The cost effectiveness of screening is highly sensitive to programme design, and targeted strategies with limited screening rounds within defined age windows are more likely to be cost effective. Screening should be considered a pathway rather than a single test, ranging from PSA testing with repeat confirmation to more comprehensive evaluation using MRI and targeted biopsy, with each step affecting detection yield and costs [57]. For younger patients, management decisions should place greater weight on long-term functional outcomes, since urinary continence and sexual function can be affected for years after definitive treatment, and patients require adequate counselling, follow up, and rehabilitation support [58, 59]. At the national level, countries should develop context appropriate early detection guidance and ensure coverage for diagnostic confirmation and definitive treatment. At the regional and international levels, strengthening registries, diagnostic and treatment capacity, and validation of GBD estimates against national cancer registries and vital registration systems in low SDI settings may improve the evidence base for policy and resource allocation.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this analysis relies on the GBD framework. In resource limited settings, incomplete cancer registration, limited diagnostic coverage, and weaker vital statistics systems increase reliance on model based estimation rather than empirical observation. Age misreporting and misclassification within standardized 5 year age bands may also contribute to underestimation in low SDI settings. Second, as an ecological analysis, the study cannot incorporate key individual level or policy level factors, including screening coverage, diagnostic intensity, stage distribution, treatment access, and genetic susceptibility. This limits causal inference and may affect comparisons across SDI strata and over time. Where feasible, future work should triangulate GBD estimates with alternative data sources, particularly national cancer registries and vital registration systems in low SDI settings, and use sensitivity analyses to reflect higher uncertainty in data sparse locations. Third, Nordpred projections assume continuation of past trends and do not explicitly model future changes in health policy, screening practice, diagnostic technology, or treatment access, so realized trajectories may differ. Scenario based forecasting that incorporates dynamic health system indicators may improve policy relevance. Despite these limitations, GBD provides an important resource for describing long-term trends and geographic disparities and for informing priorities in cancer control.

Conclusion

In summary, ASMR and ASDR declined globally from 1990 to 2021, whereas ASIR increased, with the relative burden increasingly concentrated in low-SDI settings. During the predefined COVID-19 subperiod, continued increases in ASIR in low and low-middle SDI regions highlight potential vulnerability of diagnostic pathways in resource-limited health systems. Projections suggest that the global EOPC burden will continue to rise through 2045. These findings support context-specific, risk-stratified early evaluation, strengthening timely diagnosis and standardized care in low-SDI settings, and more equitable allocation of cancer control resources.
