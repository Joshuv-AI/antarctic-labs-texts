Protocol to predict gene expression from transcriptomic data using PREDICT
Authors: Ming-Ren Yen, Kai-Cheng Pan, Ming-Jung Liu, Chia-Yi Cheng, Ting-Ying Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLinking DNA sequence variation to context-specific transcriptional programs is a critical challenge in regulatory genomics, especially for non-model organisms. Here, we present PREDICT, a modular Python package for discovering cis-regulatory elements and transcription factor binding motifs. We describe steps to identify enriched k-mers from differentially expressed genes, map them to known motifs, quantify their impact on gene expression, and visualize motif co-occurrences. PREDICT provides a robust, k-mer-based approach to uncover regulatory logic in diverse genomic systems.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Yen et al. and Liu et al.1,2Subject areas: Bioinformatics, Sequence analysis, Genomics, RNA-seq, Plant sciences, Systems biology

Before you begin

Environmental and developmental adaptations are governed by transcriptional regulation,3 where transcription factors (TFs) interact with cis-regulatory elements (CREs) to control gene expression dynamics. These CREs often function as modules, enabling cooperative regulation by multiple TFs.4,5 Variability in TF binding and CRE architecture drives the evolution of gene regulatory networks.1,6 Therefore, identifying transcription factor binding motifs (TFBMs) and their associated regulatory logic is critical for understanding the context-specific transcriptional outcomes. This protocol details PREDICT (Predictive Regulatory Elements Discovery and Integration of Cis-motif Toolkit), a pipeline linking de novo sequence (k-mer) discovery to predictive modeling. Users can rank expression-associated k-mers, map them to known TFBMs, evaluate combinatorial patterns, and quantify their predictive contribution via machine learning. The pipeline is versatile, requiring only sequence (FASTA) and annotation (GFF3) files, alongside defined gene lists (e.g., DEGs vs. non-DEGs). While we utilize Arabidopsis thaliana heat stress-responsive genes as a case study, the procedures are directly applicable to any model or non-model species.

Innovation

Standard motif analyses usually focus on known TFBMs, often failing to quantify their actual regulatory impact on gene expression or discover novel elements.7,8 PREDICT improves upon this by integrating de novo k-mer discovery directly with expression-based predictive modeling and combinatorial analysis. Unlike fragmented tools, this unified workflow prioritizes sequence features based on their true predictive power and visualizes their spatial organization. This enables the robust discovery of functional regulatory mechanisms even in systems lacking extensive in vivo binding information (e.g., non-model organisms), providing a streamlined end-to-end solution.

KmerImpact: Identification of predictive k-mers enriched in responsive genes

Timing: 0.5–4 h (depending on genome size and promoter length)

This core module operates in two steps: (i) identification of statistically enriched k-mers (referred to as significant k-mers) and (ii) evaluation of their predictive contribution to gene expression classification. The analysis begins by comparing a positive gene set (DEG) against a negative set (nonDEG) (Figure 1A).

Note: First, the DEG and nonDEG datasets are divided into 80%, called the training datasets, and 20%, called the testing datasets. The training datasets are split into five folds using k-fold partitioning with shuffling, and class balance is enforced by random downsampling of the majority class. Next, k-mer enrichment is assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Resulting p-values are adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and only k-mers with adjusted p-values below a user-defined threshold (--pThreshold, default: 0.05) are retained. Finally, the module applies a machine learning classifier (specified via --alg; supporting Random Forest, Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting, or SVM) using these significant k-mers to train a model to discriminate DEG from nonDEG genes. In total, five models are trained using five-fold cross-validation. Feature importance scores are calculated and averaged for each k-mer based on models built from the five-fold balanced datasets. The testing datasets are used for evaluating the performance of the trained models.

Note: The message about the missing DatabaseOp module in the log file is just an informational note and does not affect the KmerImpact function. DatabaseOp belongs to ParseBlast.py (an external script integrated into KmerImpact) and is not essential for the core PREDICT workflow.

CRITICAL: If the analysis yields a limited number of significant k-mers, users should consider redefining the DEG and nonDEG sets (e.g., by adjusting fold-change cutoffs or selecting different experimental contrasts) or adjust the significance threshold (--pThreshold) to improve signal detection. We recommend testing the pipeline on a small subset of genes before processing the full dataset.

Timing: 10–60 min

This module maps significant k-mers identified by the KmerImpact module to known TFBMs (Figure 1B). By connecting sequence-level features to biologically meaningful regulatory elements, it bridges the gap between k-mer enrichment and potential TF-target interactions.

Expected outcomes

Upon completion of the PREDICT workflow, users obtain a ranked set of enriched k-mers that are predictive of gene expression, along with their mappings to known TFBMs (Figures 1F–1H). These outputs enable the identification of putative CREs and combinatorial regulatory modules, effectively detecting and quantifying co-occurring motif pairs for potential cooperative function. Furthermore, by comparing motif rankings and profiles across datasets from different species, users can assess the evolutionary conservation or diversification of key CREs across specific conditions, tissues, or developmental stages.

Additionally, interactive visualizations of k-mer and motif distributions across gene loci enable the intuitive exploration of positional patterns and promoter enrichment, facilitating hypothesis generation (Figure 1I). For example, in the Arabidopsis heat-stress case study, top-ranked motifs corresponded to the Heat Shock Factor (HSF) and NAC transcription factor families, with predictive models achieving MCC values of 0.3–0.4, indicating biologically meaningful regulatory signals. Overall, PREDICT provides reproducible, interpretable, and quantitatively informative results that guide downstream experimental validation and regulatory network analysis across diverse biological systems.

Limitations

The reliability of PREDICT depends strongly on the quality and definition of the input positive and negative gene sets. Small sample size, severe class imbalance, or ambiguously defined gene sets may reduce classification performance, resulting in unstable or spurious k-mer rankings. Additionally, although the co-occurrence analysis identifies motifs with statistically coordinated presence across genes, it does not infer direct physical interactions or causal cooperativity between transcription factors.

Because PREDICT relies exclusively on sequence-derived features, it may be less informative in regulatory contexts where transcriptional control is dominated by epigenetic modifications, chromatin accessibility, or post-transcriptional mechanisms. Furthermore, the motif annotation steps (e.g., Kmer2Motif) are inherently limited by the coverage and quality of the provided motif library. While de novo k-mer extraction and ranking (e.g., KmerImpact) can be applied to organisms without a high-quality motif database, utilizing custom or incomplete libraries can lead to ambiguous motif assignments, unannotated k-mers, or matches restricted to a subset of TF families. Therefore, when database coverage is limited, motif annotations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require further validation experiments.

The current framework also does not explicitly model motif spacing, orientation constraints beyond strand annotation, or higher-order positional grammar within regulatory regions. Finally, computational demands increase significantly for large gene sets or complex genomes, particularly during interactive visualization with ViewKmers. Careful curation of input gene sets, thoughtful parameter selection are therefore recommended to ensure robust and biologically interpretable results.

Gene identifiers in DEG or nonDEG lists do not match annotation entries (related to Steps 1–3).

Genome FASTA and GFF3 annotation files originate from different genome assemblies (related to Steps 1–3).
