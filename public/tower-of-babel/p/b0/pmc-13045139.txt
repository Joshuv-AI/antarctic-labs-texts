A randomized controlled trial of drug-coated balloon versus conventional balloon angioplasty in diabetic patients with lower extremity arteriosclerosis obliterans: outcomes in ankle-brachial index and restenosis rate
Authors: Bolong Zhu, Ruoyu Cui, Hanchong Liu, Shaoyong Tuo, Yangchun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic lower extremity arteriosclerosis obliterans (DLASO) is a severe complication with high risks of limb loss and mortality. While drug-coated balloons (DCBs) are effective in peripheral artery disease, robust evidence for their use in specifically diabetic populations is limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of paclitaxel-coated balloon angioplasty versus conventional plain balloon angioplasty (POBA) for the treatment of DLASO in a randomized controlled trial.MethodsIn this prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial, 60 patients with DLASO were equally assigned to the DCB group or the POBA (control) group. The primary endpoints were primary patency rate and change in ankle-brachial index (ABI) at 12 months. Secondary endpoints included Rutherford classification, target lesion revascularization (TLR), and complications. Follow-up assessments occurred at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months.ResultsThe primary patency rate at 12 months was significantly higher in the DCB group (83.3% [95% CI, 71.8% to 96.7%]) than in the control group (56.7% [95% CI, 41.2% to 78.0%]) (hazard ratio 0.32; log-rank P = 0.023). The mean increase in ABI from baseline to 12 months was also greater in the DCB group (0.31 ± 0.12 vs. 0.21 ± 0.11; mean difference 0.10 [95% CI, 0.04 to 0.16]; P < 0.001). Clinical improvement (Rutherford classification) was more pronounced, and the 12-month TLR rate was lower (0% vs. 3.33%) in the DCB group. Complication rates were comparable between groups (10.00% vs. 13.33%, P > 0.05).ConclusionFor patients with DLASO, paclitaxel-coated balloon angioplasty is superior to conventional balloon angioplasty in maintaining vessel patency, improving perfusion, and promoting clinical outcomes at 12 months, with a similar safety profile.

Introduction

Diabetic lower extremity arteriosclerosis obliterans (DLASO) represents a severe and accelerated form of peripheral artery disease (PAD) in patients with type 2 diabetes. Its complex pathophysiology is driven by a convergence of metabolic and vascular insults, including endothelial dysfunction, heightened chronic inflammation, and pronounced oxidative stress, which together promote an aggressive atherosclerotic process [1–3]. The clinical impact of this condition is profound, leading to debilitating functional consequences such as intermittent claudication and rest pain, which severely compromise patient mobility and quality of life. More critically, DLASO is a primary contributor to the development of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), with devastating outcomes including high rates of lower extremity amputation and mortality; meta-analyses indicate that up to 31% of patients with DFUs undergo amputation, and among those, 5-year mortality can exceed 50% [4–6]. The significant clinical consequences of DLASO translate into a substantial burden on both patients and healthcare systems. The profound negative impact on quality of life is a major concern, and effective revascularization is a cornerstone of management aiming to alleviate symptoms and improve patient outcomes [7–9]. From a socioeconomic perspective, the global healthcare expenditure related to diabetic foot disorders is staggering, estimated at hundreds of billions of dollars annually, with the costs associated with major amputations being particularly high [10, 11]. Historically, plain old balloon angioplasty (POBA) has been a primary endovascular treatment for these patients. However, its long-term efficacy is severely hampered by high rates of restenosis, a limitation that is particularly exacerbated in the diabetic population. A key meta-analysis confirmed that diabetes is an independent predictor for primary restenosis after endovascular therapy [12], and a landmark study revealed a recurrent in-stent restenosis rate as high as 71.8% within one year for diabetic patients treated with POBA alone [13, 14]. This high failure rate represents a critical unmet need for a more durable therapeutic solution.

In response to the limitations of conventional angioplasty, the drug-coated balloon (DCB) was developed as a superior technological alternative. By delivering a potent antiproliferative agent, typically paclitaxel, directly to the vessel wall during balloon inflation, the DCB aims to inhibit the neointimal hyperplasia that drives restenosis. An extensive umbrella meta-analysis has robustly demonstrated the superiority of DCB over POBA across several key endpoints, showing a significant reduction in target lesion revascularization (TLR) and restenosis rates, alongside an improvement in primary patency [15]. Despite these clear advantages, a critical knowledge gap remains. There is still a notable lack of Level I evidence, specifically from randomized controlled trials, confirming the efficacy and safety of DCB in patient populations with complex, purely diabetic lower extremity arteriosclerosis obliterans, especially those with advanced disease patterns [16].

Therefore, we hypothesized that for patients with DLASO, endovascular treatment with a DCB would be superior to conventional POBA in maintaining vessel patency and improving clinical outcomes at one year. The primary objective of this randomized controlled trial was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of DCB compared to POBA for the treatment of DLASO, with the main outcomes being primary patency rate and the change in the ankle-brachial index (ABI).

General information and methodology

General information

This was a prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial conducted at a single center. Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the DCB group or the conventional balloon angioplasty (control) group. In this study, 60 patients with DLASO who were hospitalized in our hospital from October 2022 to March 2024 were selected as the research subjects. To ensure the randomness and scientific nature of the study, the method of generating a random number table by computer was used for grouping. All patients were numbered according to the order of admission, and random numbers were generated through a computer program, with each number corresponding to a patient number. Then, the patients were randomly divided into a study group and a control group according to the parity of the random numbers, with 30 patients in each group. This study was carried out after ethical review and approval.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) meet the diagnostic criteria of the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diabetic Foot 2020 [17] and have a confirmed diagnosis, and have an indication for surgery; (2) Rutherford grade 3−4−5−6, Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) shows that there is at least one lesion with a length greater than 40 mm. And the CTA calcification scoring method is adopted, in which a specific software is used to calculate the volume and density of calcification within the vascular wall to obtain the calcification score. According to the Agatston scoring method, a score of 0 represents no calcification; 1−100 indicates mild calcification; 101–400 is moderate calcification; and exceeding 400 means severe calcification. (3) At least one infrapopliteal artery has continuous and sufficient blood flow signals within a specific segment, and the lesions of the femoral-popliteal artery and infrapopliteal outflow tract are evaluated through Color Doppler Ultrasound (CDU) examination; (3) The patient’s clinical data is complete and the informed consent form is signed.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Patients with bleeding tendency, active bleeding, or those currently undergoing thrombolytic therapy; (2) Severe coagulation dysfunction; (3) Patients with hemodynamic instability or recent cardiac and cerebrovascular accidents; (4) Patients with a known allergy to paclitaxel; (5) Patients with a history of major surgery or trauma within 2 weeks; (6) Patients with incomplete essential clinical data, such as missing electrocardiographic (ECG) records or baseline laboratory parameters.

Methods

The control group was treated with balloon dilation of lower extremity arteries, and under the guidance of Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA), unilateral retrograde femoral artery or popliteal artery retrograde puncture, heparinization, implantation of 5–6 F tubes, and localization of the arteries of the affected lower extremities under DSA guidance to determine the location, extent and blood supply of collateral stenosis. At the end of the imaging, a new balloon (Mustang, Boston Scientific; balloon diameter range 3.0–5.0 mm, length 40–120 mm) was removed, a 3−5 M balloon was inserted, the air pressure was 8–12 kPa, and the expansion was 3 min. If the patient is not satisfied with the results of the treatment, the treatment can be repeated 3 times. Once the vasodilation is complete, angiogram is done again to look at blood flow in the arteries of the lower extremities and the presence of intimal damage and dissection (angiographic success was defined as residual stenosis < 30% without flow-limiting dissection). After the surgery is completed, the catheter is removed, the occluder is closed, and the puncture site is pressurized with an elastic bandage and gauze. The affected limb was immobilized for 24 h after surgery.

The operation of the study group was the same as that of the control group, and after the contrast was completed, the blood vessels were pre-dilated, and then paclitaxel drug balloon dilation (Xianruida, China; paclitaxel dose density 3.5 µg/mm²) was implemented.

The selection of the pre - dilation balloon is based on the diameter of the blood vessel. A balloon with a diameter slightly smaller than the normal diameter of the target blood vessel is recommended. Generally, it is suggested that the balloon diameter should be 1–2 mm smaller than the expected diameter of the target blood vessel. If the expected diameter of the target blood vessel is 5 mm, a balloon with a diameter of 3–4 mm should be selected for pre - dilation. The air pressure for pre - dilation is set at 6–8 kPa. Each dilation lasts for 2 min. Depending on the blood vessel’s response to the dilation, the dilation can be repeated 2–3 times, with an interval of 1 min between each dilation. The criteria for the completion of pre - dilation are that the diameter of the blood vessel reaches 80%−90% of the expected target blood vessel diameter, and there are no obvious tears, dissections or other abnormal conditions in the blood vessel wall (assessed by on-table DSA and classified according to the NHLBI dissection grading system).

For the DCB dilation, select a DCB with a diameter matching the pre-dilated vessel and 1–2 mm larger than the pre-dilation balloon. The length of the DCB should exceed the proximal and distal ends of the diseased vessel by 3–5 cm. Once the DCB package is opened, immediate operation is required to prevent drug efficacy loss due to prolonged exposure. The continuous dilation time of the DCB is 3 min (timer used to ensure precise inflation duration). Post-dilation with a conventional balloon was not performed unless required to address a flow-limiting dissection (Type D–F).

In addition, to ensure the reliability of the study, all surgical operators involved in this study have at least 5 years of experience in vascular interventional procedures and independently perform no fewer than 50 lower extremity arterial interventional procedures annually. Meanwhile, all surgical operators must undergo unified standardized training, which covers surgical operation procedures, specifications for the use of various instruments, and methods for handling intraoperative emergencies, among others.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

Primary Patency Loss at 6 months: Defined as a ≥ 50% stenosis (by diameter) of the target lesion on follow-up computed tomography angiography (CTA) or the occurrence of clinically driven target lesion revascularization (TLR) within 6 months post-procedure.

Change in Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) at 12 months: The difference between the pre-procedure ABI and the ABI measured at 12-month follow-up. ABI was calculated for both the posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries of the target limb, and the higher value was used for analysis.

Secondary outcomes

Hemodynamic improvement: ABI values of the posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries measured pre-procedure and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-procedure.

Clinical improvement: Assessed using the Rutherford classification at pre-procedure, 3, 6, and 12 months. Improvement was defined as a decrease of at least one Rutherford category.

Target Lesion Revascularization (TLR) / Reintervention rate: The proportion of patients requiring any repeat percutaneous or surgical intervention of the target lesion within 12 months due to restenosis or occlusion.

Vessel Patency: The maximum luminal diameter of the target lesion as measured on CTA at 3, 6, and 12 months post-procedure.

Complication rate: The incidence of procedure-related and device-related adverse events within 30 days and up to 12 months, including access site complications (e.g., hematoma, pseudoaneurysm), distal embolism, vessel dissection, acute limb ischemia, and systemic complications (abnormal liver/kidney function, major bleeding).

Baseline Characteristics

Data on demographics (age, sex), clinical comorbidities (hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease), lesion characteristics (target vessel, lesion length, degree of calcification per Agatston score, presence of chronic total occlusion), and baseline laboratory parameters (glycated hemoglobin, lipid profile, coagulation function) were collected to assess group comparability.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated based on key published literature regarding restenosis rates in patients with diabetes mellitus and peripheral artery disease (PAD) following endovascular treatment. The expected 12-month restenosis rate for the control group (conventional balloon angioplasty) was set at 40%, an assumption based on the design and reported clinical outcomes of the large-scale randomized controlled trial, IN.PACT SFA [18]. For the investigational group (drug-coated balloon, DCB), the expected restenosis rate was set at 15%, a figure derived from a prospective, randomized study specifically focused on diabetic patients with PAD [19].

With a statistical power of 80% and a two-sided significance level of 0.05, the sample size was determined by a standard statistical method for comparing two independent proportions. This calculation takes into account the anticipated restenosis rates in both the control and investigational groups, along with the specified power and significance level, to ensure the study is large enough to detect a true difference between the treatments if one exists.

Based on this method, a minimum of 27 patients per group was required. To account for potential patient dropouts during the study, an attrition rate of approximately 10% was anticipated. Therefore, the planned enrollment was increased to 30 patients per group, for a total of 60 participants. This sample size provides adequate power to detect the expected 25% absolute difference in restenosis rates between the two groups.

1.6 Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding (masking).

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the DCB group or the control group. The randomization sequence was computer-generated by an independent statistician using block randomization (block size of 4). To ensure allocation concealment, the assignment for each sequence number was placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE). When a patient provided informed consent and met all inclusion criteria, the research nurse opened the next consecutive envelope to reveal the group assignment. This process ensured that the enrolling investigators and surgical operators were unaware of the upcoming allocation until the moment of intervention.

Regarding blinding, this study employed a single-blind design. Due to the physical characteristics of the devices (drug-coated versus conventional balloons), the interventional operators performing the angioplasty procedures could not be blinded to group assignment. However, to minimize assessment bias, all postoperative outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded throughout the study. Specifically, the researchers responsible for evaluating hemodynamic parameters (ABI), clinical efficacy (Rutherford grade, ulcer healing), imaging outcomes (vascular patency on CTA), laboratory data, and complications remained unaware of patient group allocation during data collection, evaluation, and the initial statistical analysis.

Statistical Methods

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). An intention-to-treat (ITT) approach was adopted for the primary analysis. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For repeated measures over time (e.g., ABI, vessel diameter), a linear mixed-effects model was employed, with time, treatment group, and their interaction as fixed effects, and a random intercept for each subject to account for within-individual correlation. If the sphericity assumption was violated (Mauchly’s test), the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons at different time points were performed using the Bonferroni method. All patients completed the 12-month follow-up, resulting in no missing data for the primary endpoints; thus, no imputation for missing data was required. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Primary outcomes

Primary patency

Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to assess primary patency, defined as freedom from both a ≥ 50% stenosis of the target lesion on imaging and clinically driven TLR. At 6 months, the primary patency rate was significantly higher in the DCB group compared to the control group (90.0% [95% CI, 81.4% to 99.4%] vs. 63.3% [95% CI, 47.9% to 83.7%]; hazard ratio [HR] for loss of patency 0.24 [95% CI, 0.08 to 0.75]; log-rank P = 0.014). This benefit was sustained at 12 months (83.3% [95% CI, 71.8% to 96.7%] in the DCB group vs. 56.7% [95% CI, 41.2% to 78.0%] in the control group; HR 0.32 [95% CI, 0.12 to 0.85]; log-rank P = 0.023) (Fig. 1). Clinical improvement (rutherford classification)

The Rutherford classification improved significantly over time in both groups. At 12 months, the median Rutherford grade was lower in the DCB group (2 [IQR 2–3]) than in the control group (3 [IQR 2–3]) (P = 0.032). A higher proportion of patients in the DCB group achieved an improvement of at least one Rutherford category at 3, 6, and 12 months compared to the control group. Discussion

This study demonstrates that in the specific and challenging patient population of DLASO, the use of a DCB results in a higher primary patency rate and more significant improvement in the ABI at 12 months post-procedure compared to conventional balloon angioplasty. Although the advantages of DCB technology in the general PAD population have been confirmed by landmark randomized controlled trials such as IN.PACT SFA [20, 21] and LEVANT I [22], to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to provide direct, evidence-based support for its application in DLASO patients within a rigorously designed randomized controlled framework. These findings effectively bridge the gap between general PAD guidelines and the specific needs of this high-risk group, offering robust data to support clinical adoption.

The superior efficacy observed in this study is underpinned by specific biological mechanisms. Paclitaxel, the core drug component, stabilizes microtubules to inhibit the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells. This inhibition significantly reduces neointimal hyperplasia, addressing the primary driver of restenosis [23, 24]. Such a mechanism is particularly crucial for DLASO patients, in whom chronic hyperglycemia triggers severe endothelial dysfunction, persistent inflammation, and accelerated calcification [25]. Against this pathological backdrop, paclitaxel may potentially exert vascular protective effects beyond mechanical dilation. Current evidence suggests it may modulate the local inflammatory microenvironment and improve downstream microcirculatory function [26–28]. This biological plausibility aligns with and explains the sustained hemodynamic improvements (ABI) and clinical upgrades recorded in our DCB cohort.

Placing these findings within the context of current guidelines reveals a high degree of alignment with prevailing trends. The 2024 ESC Guidelines [29], the 2023 Global Vascular Guidelines [30], and the 2024 ADA Standards of Care [31] have all recognized the therapeutic value of drug-coated technologies. However, direct comparisons with pivotal trials require caution due to methodological heterogeneity. While landmark trials like IN.PACT SFA [20, 21] and LEVANT I [22] targeted femoropopliteal lesions with strict anatomical criteria, our study enrolled a purely diabetic cohort involving both femoropopliteal and infrapopliteal (below-the-knee) arteries. This territory is notoriously prone to calcification and recoil. Furthermore, unlike the CHOCOLATE TOUCH trial [32], which utilized a specific constrained balloon for femoropopliteal disease, our protocol managed longer, more diffuse lesions (mean length > 85 mm). Consequently, our findings provide refined supplementary evidence for treating complex, multi-level diabetic vascular disease in real-world scenarios [33, 34].

The positive results also offer inspiration for applying DCB technology in wider clinical scenarios. Recently, research has explored DCBs in special-risk populations, including patients at high bleeding risk [35–37], those with small vessel disease [38], and complex bifurcation lesions [39]. While these groups possess distinct pathophysiologies, the safety and efficacy confirmed here enhance confidence in using DCBs for challenging vascular anatomies. Further validation through prospective studies targeting these subgroups remains necessary.

When interpreting these outcomes, the role of concomitant medical therapy warrants explicit consideration. Hypertension and advanced age are recognized prognostic factors [40–42]. More critically, the synergistic potential of statins and antiplatelet agents with DCB therapy must be acknowledged [43, 44]. Studies indicate that statin therapy is associated with improved patency in diabetic patients undergoing endovascular treatment, potentially by stabilizing plaque and reducing systemic inflammation [45]. Although our randomization ensured that baseline medication use was well-balanced, the interaction between optimal medical therapy and local drug delivery remains a key determinant of long-term success. Additionally, promising early outcomes have been noted for DCB in diabetic foot infrapopliteal lesions, consistent with our findings [46].

In terms of real-world decision-making, the improved patency and ABI rates observed here support the prioritization of DCB angioplasty for limb salvage in diabetics. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the single‑center design and sample size may limit generalizability. Although baseline characteristics were balanced, the study was not powered to analyze specific confounders like HbA1c variability or neuropathy depth. Second, operator blinding was not feasible due to device differences, though outcome assessors remained blinded. Third, the 12‑month follow‑up, while sufficient for mid‑term patency, cannot confirm long‑term durability. Finally, while cost-effectiveness is a critical consideration for resource allocation, a formal economic analysis was beyond the scope of this clinical efficacy trial.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial confirms our hypothesis that paclitaxel-coated balloon angioplasty is superior to conventional balloon angioplasty for the treatment of DLASO. By demonstrating significantly higher primary patency rates and greater improvement in the ankle-brachial index at 12 months, this study provides critical Level I evidence specifically for this high-risk population, addressing the knowledge gap regarding the efficacy of DCBs in complex diabetic vascular disease. These findings validate that the anti-proliferative mechanism of DCBs effectively counters the aggressive restenotic physiology inherent to diabetes.

The clinical implications of these results are substantial. The significantly reduced rate of target lesion revascularization, coupled with marked improvements in Rutherford classification, indicates that DCB angioplasty offers a durable solution that may enhance limb salvage and functional recovery. Consequently, this supports the elevation of DCB angioplasty to a preferred endovascular strategy for patients with diabetic peripheral artery disease, moving beyond the limitations of plain balloon angioplasty.

While these promising findings should be interpreted within the context of the study’s single-center design and 12-month follow-up, they underscore the necessity for broader adoption and equitable access to this technology. Future research should prioritize validating these outcomes in larger, multicenter cohorts and conducting formal cost-effectiveness analyses to optimize resource allocation. Ultimately, this study establishes DCB as a decisive therapeutic advancement, offering a viable pathway to improve long-term vascular patency and quality of life in the vulnerable diabetic population.
