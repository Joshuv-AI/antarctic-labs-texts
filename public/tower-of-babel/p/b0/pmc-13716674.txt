Evolving practices in pretransplant cardiovascular screening: moving toward personalized assessments
Authors: Arzu Velioglu, Tansu Celik, Harun Coban, Dilek Barutcu Atas, Altug Cincin, Serhan Tuglular
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiovascular (CV) disease remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with end-stage kidney disease, including those evaluated for kidney transplantation. Pretransplant CV screening has traditionally focused on detecting occult coronary artery disease and guiding revascularization to reduce perioperative risk. However, emerging evidence, most notably from the ISCHEMIA-CKD trial, has challenged this paradigm, demonstrating no clear benefit of routine invasive strategies or prophylactic revascularization in stable, asymptomatic patients over optimal medical therapy. Risk assessment in kidney transplant candidates should rely on an integrated clinical evaluation that incorporates symptoms, functional capacity, baseline electrocardiography and echocardiographic findings, and the presence of major CV risk factors. When further evaluation is indicated, stress echocardiography is generally preferred as the noninvasive modality. Invasive coronary angiography should be reserved for patients with cardiac symptoms or findings suggestive of significant ischemia, including reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, regional wall motion abnormalities, or moderate-to-large ischemia on stress testing. Pre-emptive revascularization should be limited to established guideline-based indications, such as left main disease, multivessel disease with reduced ejection fraction, or refractory angina despite optimal medical therapy. Noncoronary CV disorders, including heart failure, valvular disease, and pulmonary hypertension, require systematic assessment and cardiology referral. In conclusion, pretransplant CV assessment is shifting from protocol-driven screening toward individualized, clinical suspicion-based evaluation, prioritizing optimal medical therapy, avoidance of unnecessary testing, and timely access to transplantation.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular (CV) diseases remain the leading cause of death in patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), including those on the transplant waiting list and kidney transplant (KT) recipients [1–3]. Pretransplant CV screening has long been a standard component of transplant evaluation, especially in candidates with diabetes mellitus (DM), long dialysis vintage, and prior CV disease. The main goal of pretransplant CV evaluation is to identify the patients who may benefit from specific management strategies prior to transplantation, thereby reducing major CV events (MACE), particularly perioperative CV complications and early CV death, while also improving post-transplant survival. This evaluation also helps to identify patients for whom transplantation may be deemed too risky or contraindicated. CV disease comprises a spectrum of disorders affecting the heart and vascular system, including coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure (HF), valvular diseases, arrhythmias, cerebrovascular events (CVE) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD) (Fig. 1). Among these, CAD has traditionally been the primary focus because of its strong association with myocardial infarction (MI) and CV mortality. The risk of MI is highest immediately post-transplant and declines in subsequent years [2]. Early after KT, CV risk transiently increases, driven not only by pre-existing traditional risk factors but also by transplant-specific stressors. In the peri- and early post-transplant period, hemodynamic instability, altered vascular resistance, increased myocardial excitability, and plaque vulnerability contribute to this heightened risk. Additional factors such as dialysis vintage, immunosuppression, and acute rejection further amplify CV disease burden in this critical phase [4].

Spectrum of CV diseases in KT candidates.

Accordingly, preventive percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for coronary stenosis had been attempted to reduce perioperative CV event risk in KT candidates in some observational studies [5–9]. However, overall evidence has shown that prophylactic PCI did not consistently provide benefit in advanced CKD. In these patients, CV pathology is dominated not by discrete, stent-responsive epicardial stenosis but by diffuse microvascular dysfunction, severe medial vascular calcification, autonomic abnormalities, marked left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, and high arrhythmic burden (Fig. 2). Despite the long-standing use of pretransplant CV screening, the optimal strategy in KT candidates remains uncertain and increasingly debated. Indeed, the International Study of Comparative Health Effectiveness with Medical and Invasive Approaches–Chronic Kidney Disease (ISCHEMIA-CKD) trial, published in 2020, has further challenged the role of routine pretransplant coronary intervention and prompted a re-evaluation of how and when ischemia testing should be used in transplant candidates [10]. Therefore, this review aims to summarize the current practice, discuss the evolving role of ischemia testing and coronary intervention, and provide a practical approach to CV evaluation in KT candidates based on current guidelines.

PRETRANSPLANT CARDIOVASCULAR SCREENING PRACTICE

Many transplant centers perform routine screening for ischemia based on the presence of cardiac symptoms, functional status and/or CV risk factors. Generally, candidates with high burden of CV disease risk factors, defined as those with DM, aged >60 years, prior CV disease, or prolonged dialysis duration were often subjected to noninvasive stress testing [e.g. myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) or stress echocardiography] [11, 12]. Notably, the definition of ‘prolonged dialysis duration’ varies across studies and guidelines, with thresholds ranging from ≥1 to ≥5 years [13–15].

Cardiac symptoms

Cardiac symptoms include chest pain or discomfort consistent with angina pectoris, exertional dyspnea, reduced exercise tolerance, palpitations, syncope or presyncope, and symptoms of HF such as orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, and peripheral edema [16]. Angina is classically described as substernal pressure or tightness precipitated by exertion or emotional stress and relieved by rest or nitrates; atypical presentations or anginal equivalents, including isolated dyspnea or fatigue, may also occur [16]. Thus, careful clinical assessment by clinicians is essential to establish the clinical suspicion of underlying ischemia before any ischemia testing is considered. It should also be noted that cardiac symptoms in CKD are often confounded by volume overload, anemia, hypertension, arteriovenous fistula with high-output physiology, and pulmonary vascular disease. Angina in this population may be atypical, silent, or overshadowed by noncoronary mechanisms [17]. Misclassification of volume status can lead to false ischemia signals, and pulmonary hypertension or high-output states may mimic or overshadow angina [18]. Sudden cardiac death in this population is more commonly related to arrhythmia than acute plaque rupture in this population, further diminishing the relevance of noninvasive ischemia screening in stable transplant candidates [19]. Therefore, ‘symptom-driven’ evaluation should be interpreted within a broader framework of clinical suspicion rather than relying solely on typical anginal symptoms, integrating functional status, electrocardiography (ECG)/echocardiography findings, and individualized risk assessment when determining the need for further ischemia testing.

Functional capacity

Functional capacity reflects overall cardiopulmonary reserve and may help to determine the perioperative cardiac risk status [20].

Functional capacity is mostly estimated using metabolic equivalents of tasks (METs), thus <4 METs indicating poor functional capacity, approximately corresponding to inability to climb two flights of stairs. However, METs-based assessment relies on subjective reporting and may be inaccurate in patients with CKD due to anemia, sarcopenia, volume overload, and noncardiac exercise limitation [21].

Structured activity-based tools have therefore been developed. The Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) is a validated, patient-reported questionnaire that provides a semi-quantitative estimate of functional capacity and has demonstrated better discrimination for perioperative death and myocardial infarction than subjective clinician assessment. Lower DASI scores are consistently associated with increased short-term postoperative CV risk [20, 22].

Importantly, functional capacity should not be viewed as an isolated metric but integrated within the overall clinical framework. In candidates with preserved, good functional capacity (≥4 METs or high DASI scores) and no clinical suspicion of ischemia, additional noninvasive testing can be reasonably deferred.

Risk assessment scores

Perioperative CV risk assessment scores are widely used to estimate the likelihood of MACE in patients undergoing noncardiac surgery. Commonly applied tools include the Revised Cardiac Risk Index, the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program risk calculators, and other multivariable models developed in large surgical cohorts. Although population-based CV risk models which aim to predict life-long CV risk had evaluated for this purpose, they have not been validated for predicting peritransplant CV events or guiding CV screening in KT candidates [23, 24].

In KT candidates, the clinical utility of traditional risk scores is further limited. Kidney failure itself constitutes a major CAD risk factor, and most transplant candidates harbor multiple additional non-CKD risk factors result in accumulation near high-risk profile and reduced ability of conventional indices to meaningfully distinguish more risky individuals. Accordingly, neither the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) perioperative guideline nor the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) noncardiac surgery guideline endorses one specific risk score over another, emphasizing that risk calculators should be used only as adjuncts to clinical judgment rather than as standalone decision tools [22, 25].

Recognizing the high baseline CV risk in this population, the 2012 AHA/American College of Cardiology Foundation scientific statement on cardiac evaluation of KT candidates recommended that noninvasive stress testing may be considered in asymptomatic candidates with three or more CAD risk factors, irrespective of functional capacity [13]. These risk factors include DM, prior CV disease, dialysis duration exceeding 1-year, age >60 years, smoking, hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy, and dyslipidemia. However, the updated 2022 AHA scientific statement refined this approach by defining as the candidates without known CAD, cardiac symptoms, significant echocardiographic abnormalities, or left ventricular ejection fraction <40%, those with none of the major risk criteria (age >60 years, DM, CVE, PAD, or dialysis duration >5 years) or evidence of silent ischemia on ECG, as low-risk group, in whom routine noninvasive stress testing is not recommended [26]. On the contrary, the KT candidates with any of the above conditions are considered for cardiology referral and further cardiac testing.

Noninvasive testing

Abnormal results of noninvasive stress tests typically prompt coronary angiography and, in some cases, revascularization if needed. However, this approach often causes delays in waitlisting or in proceeding with living donor transplantation due to need for mandatory period of dual antiplatelet therapy in case of stent placement.

In contemporary practice, noninvasive testing is increasingly reserved for candidates with symptoms suggestive of angina, unexplained decline in functional capacity, or severely reduced exercise capacity [26]. Resting ECG and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remain first-line baseline investigations in all transplant candidates, providing essential information on rhythm disturbances, prior silent ischemia, left ventricular hypertrophy, systolic and diastolic function, valvular disease, and pulmonary hypertension. However, their ability to detect obstructive CAD is limited, and abnormalities on ECG (silent MI suggested by qR or QS pattern, Q wave, reversal of R wave progression) or TTE [new or unexpected regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMAs) or new or unexpected left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF <50% or absolute global longitudinal strain <18%)] should be interpreted primarily as markers of global CV risk, which may raise clinical suspicion but do not independently establish flow-limiting CAD [26]. When additional cardiac testing is considered clinically appropriate, the choice of noninvasive modality should be individualized, accounting for patient-specific characteristics and the inherent limitations of each technique [13, 15, 27].

Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) provides functional assessment by evaluating myocardial perfusion and viability and is the most frequently used noninvasive screening method for MPI. SPECT-MPI can provide complementary information in selected cases, however its diagnostic accuracy is reduced by balanced ischemia and microvascular dysfunction, both of which are common in CKD [28]. Despite its low accuracy rate, SPECT-MPI with abnormal findings has been associated with increased mortality, even in the presence of nonobstructive CAD, which may be a consequence of moderate microvascular disease or moderate but widespread epicardial stenosis [29].

Stress echocardiography is frequently favored because it avoids ionizing radiation and contrast exposure while allowing dynamic assessment of ventricular function, valvular disease, and pulmonary pressures. Nevertheless, its diagnostic performance may be compromised by left ventricular hypertrophy, chronotropic incompetence, or suboptimal acoustic windows [30]. Compared with SPECT, stress echocardiography demonstrates superior diagnostic and prognostic value and is recommended as a first-line test by the AHA 2022 statement [26].

In recent years, PET (positron emission tomography)-MPI has emerged as an important addition to the noninvasive cardiac imaging armamentarium. Compared with SPECT, PET-MPI offers lower radiation exposure and shorter acquisition times [31]. Beyond the detection of obstructive CAD, PET-MPI enables quantification of coronary flow reserve but is limited by cost and availability [32]. PET MPI has been shown to correlate with abnormal myocardial perfusion scintigraphy findings and has been associated with post-transplant CV events in KT candidates [33].

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), particularly when combined with coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring, offers excellent negative predictive value for excluding significant epicardial CAD in selected patients with low-to-moderate calcific burden [34]. However, in advanced CKD, the utility of CCTA is limited by extensive medial vascular calcification, which can impair image interpretability and lead to overestimation of stenosis severity [35]. Additionally, concerns regarding iodinated contrast exposure further restrict its routine use in nondialyzed transplant candidates.

Serum biomarkers

Biomarkers add further complexity in CKD. High-sensitivity troponin is chronically elevated in many dialysis patients because of structural heart disease and impaired clearance; therefore, isolated troponin levels cannot diagnose ischemia. In patients with chronically elevated baseline troponin values, dynamic changes (>20% rise from baseline) together with symptoms, ECG abnormalities, or worsening cardiac function should prompt further evaluation [13, 36]. BNP and NT-pro-BNP are frequently elevated due to LVH, diastolic dysfunction, volume excess, or pulmonary hypertension and should not be used as triggers for ischemia testing. Instead, serial changes should be interpreted relative to dialysis status and estimated dry weight, as persistently rising levels despite apparently stable volume conditions may suggest worsening HF or other structural cardiac abnormalities [37, 38]. LDL-cholesterol levels may appear deceptively low due to inflammation and malnutrition-inflammation complex, and do not reliably indicate atherosclerotic burden [39]. These laboratory parameters contribute to overall CV assessment but cannot independently guide ischemia evaluation.

Invasive coronary angiography and interventions

Coronary angiography has traditionally been considered the definitive diagnostic modality for CAD in KT candidates with abnormal noninvasive test results or classical acute cardiac symptoms. In many centers, detection of inducible ischemia has routinely triggered invasive coronary angiography, often followed by PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting. However, this practice has largely been extrapolated from evidence in general population and has not been supported by robust transplant-specific randomized data. The benefit of direct CAD screening with invasive coronary angiography in CKD has historically been demonstrated in a single center small randomized controlled trial. Manske et al. compared pre-emptive coronary revascularization with medical therapy in KT candidates with insulin-dependent DM and >75% stenosis in at least one epicardial coronary artery. The study was stopped early at a median follow-up of 8.4 months due to benefit in the revascularization arm [9]. However, the standard medical therapy used in this 1992 study consisted primarily of calcium channel blockers and was implemented before the widespread use of statins, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, and contemporary antiplatelet therapy. Therefore, it differed substantially from current optimal medical therapy. This likely represents an important explanation for the apparent superiority of revascularization observed in the study. In contrast, subsequent large-scale observational studies did not demonstrate survival advantage of preventive or prophylactic revascularization in asymptomatic ESKD patients before KT [5–7]. Finally, the ISCHEMIA-CKD randomized controlled trial, addressing this question in advanced CKD, found no benefit of a routine invasive strategy over optimal medical therapy alone, even among patients listed for KT, effectively challenging the rationale for routine preventive revascularization in asymptomatic transplant candidates [10, 40]. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of lower revascularization rates and lower event rates than initially anticipated, which reduced the statistical power of the trial. In addition, exploratory subgroup analyses suggested possible heterogeneity according to ischemia severity. Of note, listed patients were younger, more likely on dialysis, and less symptomatic, yet underwent more coronary angiography and revascularization regardless of treatment assignment. This may reflect transplant evaluation protocols rather than true clinical indication and should be considered when interpreting these findings [40].

This study emphasized that revascularization should follow guideline-directed indications, including left main coronary artery stenosis ≥50%, proximal left anterior descending artery disease, multivessel CAD with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, persistent angina despite optimal medical therapy, or large ischemic burden on noninvasive testing [41]. Revascularization should not be performed solely to reduce perioperative risk in asymptomatic transplant candidates.

It should also be noted that coronary angiography poses distinct risks, including contrast-induced kidney injury in those with residual function, peripheral vascular complications, bleeding events associated with antithrombotic therapy in patients with advanced CKD, and coronary complications due to diffuse and high arterial calcification burden [10]. This concern is particularly important in pre-emptive KT candidates. In these patients, minimizing contrast volume, using low- or iso-osmolar contrast agents, avoiding repetitive contrast exposure, and careful hydration when feasible are reasonable mitigation strategies, although evidence regarding optimal timing before transplantation remains limited. In addition, ISCHEMIA-CKD demonstrated a higher stroke incidence in the invasive-strategy arm, a finding particularly relevant in transplant candidates because neurological disability may adversely affect waitlist status and transplant eligibility [10]. Moreover, subsequent coronary interventions frequently necessitate prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy, which can delay waitlisting or living donor transplantation and increase perioperative bleeding risk [42]. These consequences are especially important in transplant candidates, for whom delays in transplantation adversely affect survival.

NONCORONARY CARDIAC DISEASES

Beyond CAD, CKD patients experience a broad spectrum of structural and functional cardiac changes that worsen progressively with disease advancement.

HF is both prevalent and frequently unrecognized in ESKD, partly because its symptoms are often attributed to dialysis-related conditions such as anemia and fluid overload. As a consequence, it tends to be under-investigated outside the context of transplant evaluation [43]. Overt HF predictably correlates with poorer outcomes in both dialysis and transplant population. Notably, even subclinical degrees of systolic dysfunction appear to independently predict increased mortality in patients on the transplant waiting list, as well as all-cause mortality, cardiac death, and nonfatal CV events, regardless of ischemic burden [44]. Accordingly, in a US study, left ventricle ejection fraction (EF) measured by MPI emerged as the independent predictor of mortality among KT candidates (n = 3698), with each 1% decline in EF corresponding to a 2.7% rise in mortality risk [5]. HF with preserved EF, an important contributor to CV morbidity in ESKD, has been shown to improve after KT, likely due to restoration of sodium and fluid homeostasis [45]. Given its close association with hypertension and volume overload, fluid balance assessment should be a component of pretransplant cardiac evaluation.

Valvular heart disease, particularly degenerative calcification, is much more prevalent in patients with CKD than in the general population and is associated with worse outcomes even following surgical correction [46, 47]. Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is similarly prevalent among ESKD patients and independently linked to increased mortality [48]. Approach to the PH includes aggressive volume management pretransplant (ultrafiltration as tolerated), ligation of AVF in those with high AVF flow and referral to a PH specialist for other treatment options [47].

Taken together, these findings underscore that CV risk in KT candidates extends well beyond coronary artery status and cannot be adequately addressed. Accordingly, comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic strategies targeting left ventricle EF optimization, PH management and hemodynamic optimization, and valvular disease assessment and its timely intervention are also warranted in this population.

RECENT GUIDELINE UPDATES

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) clinical practice guideline on the evaluation and management of candidates for KT, which was published in April 2020, suggested that following initial assessment, patients without signs or symptoms of CV disease but at high risk for CAD (e.g. DM, previous CAD), or with poor functional capacity, should undergo noninvasive CAD screening. It is then recommended that asymptomatic candidates with known CAD should not be revascularized solely to reduce perioperative cardiac events [15].

The AHA released a scientific statement endorsed by AST in 2022 outlining an updated approach to screening for CV disease in KT candidates. In their proposal, they introduced an algorithm for screening patients, distinguishing between those with a known history of CAD, including previously revascularized or who underwent coronary artery bypass grafting, and those without CAD. For asymptomatic patients lacking a history of CAD, the AHA recommended considering coronary angiography only if a resting TTE indicates a left ventricular EF below 40% or if there are RWMAs. In the case of asymptomatic patients who do have a history of CAD, the AHA suggested referring them for a functional cardiac assessment, with stress echocardiography as the preferred method. Subsequent coronary angiography would only be advised if stress echocardiography reveals an LVEF below 40% or significant ischemia. Regarding revascularization, the AHA’s scientific statement concluded that there is insufficient evidence to demonstrate the benefits of revascularization in asymptomatic KT candidates [26].

The recent ESC Non-Cardiac Surgery guideline (2022) and ACC/AHA perioperative guideline (2024) further reinforce the general principle, also applicable to transplant candidates, that noninvasive testing should be ordered only if results would alter clinical management, and that asymptomatic, functionally stable patients should not be tested merely to stratify theoretical risk [22, 25]. The ACC/AHA Revascularization guideline also supports this approach, reflecting decades of trial data showing no benefit from routine revascularization in stable patients without clear clinical indications, as it does not improve perioperative or post-transplant outcomes and may delay transplantation due to dual antiplatelet therapy requirements [15, 41, 42]. The recently announced British Transplantation Society guideline emphasizes that CV findings should be interpreted within the broader context of frailty, comorbidity burden, functional capacity, patient expectations, and the overall anticipated benefit of transplantation [49]. Regarding non-CADs, The KDIGO guideline for transplant candidates suggests that patients with symptomatic New York Heart Association class III/IV HF with uncorrectable left ventricular dysfunction (ejection fraction <30%) despite adequate fluid removal on dialysis, be excluded from KT alone, unless mitigating factors indicate an acceptable estimated survival [15].

Unfortunately, KDIGO provides fewer specific thresholds for noncoronary CV diseases (arrhythmias, CVE and PAD), largely deferring decision-making to cardiology, neurology, or vascular medicine consultation and multidisciplinary assessment reflecting the limited evidence in this area [15].

CARDIAC SURVEILLANCE AFTER WAITLISTING

Given that patients often remain on the deceased donor transplant waiting list for several years, maintaining medical fitness for transplantation represents a major challenge for transplant programs. The unpredictable timing of deceased donor organ availability requires candidates to remain in a continuous state of surgical readiness despite evolving comorbidities over time. Consequently, many centers continue periodic surveillance testing after waitlisting, to identify patients who develop severe heart disease considered prohibitively high-risk for transplantation. However, the clinical benefit of routine surveillance testing in asymptomatic wait-listed candidates remains uncertain. Earlier guidelines supported periodic surveillance strategies, whereas more recent statements have adopted a more cautious and individualized approach [15, 26]. A cost-utility analysis study from the US showed that further screening for CAD after waitlisting is not likely to be cost-effective and may not improve survival [50].

ONGOING DEBATES AND INSTITUTIONAL VARIABILITIES

Despite the ISCHEMIA-CKD trial’s findings, implementation of its results has been uneven. Some centers continue to perform routine ischemia screening, addressing concerns about perioperative myocardial infarction risk, medico-legal considerations, or local cardiac policy. Available evidence suggests that routine CV screening in KT candidates does not translate into improved early or long-term clinical outcomes. Large observational data and cohort studies consistently show no significant reduction in peri-transplant mortality, myocardial infarction, or other MACEs. Importantly, these findings largely exclude the highest-risk patients, indicating that the benefit of screening (if any) may be limited to selected subgroups rather than applied universally [51, 52].

An additional challenge lies in the distinction between ‘asymptomatic’ and ‘symptomatic’ patients. Patients with CKD frequently present with atypical or absent symptoms of CAD, including less frequent chest pain compared with the general population. This discrepancy is largely attributed to the high prevalence of DM and uremic neuropathy in this population. Importantly, the distinction between symptomatic and asymptomatic transplant candidates may also be blurred in frail patients or those with impaired functional capacity, in whom exertional symptoms may be underrecognized. Therefore, frailty and functional status may be incorporated into the overall CV risk assessment of KT candidates.

Also, it is not yet clear whether the pretransplant cardiology assessment should remain limited to a perioperative surgical clearance (‘fit for surgery’ vs. ‘not fit’) or whether it should include formal risk stratification (‘moderate risk,’ ‘high risk’). Classically, all ESKD patients are considered to be at moderate risk for KT surgery [15]. However, certain clinical characteristics, such as longer dialysis vintage, DM, prior CAD, cerebrovascular disease, or pulmonary hypertension, automatically shift patients into a high-risk category. Some clinicians still view CV testing as a tool to ‘screen out’ high-risk patients and preserve organ utility. However, such an approach must be weighed against potential delays in listing and the risk of denying transplantation to patients who could have benefited. Importantly, despite the increased perioperative CV risk associated with KT, long-term CV outcomes and overall survival remain superior compared with continued dialysis therapy. Therefore, CV assessment strategies should primarily focus on evidence-based approaches aimed at minimizing perioperative and early post-transplant MACE risk without unnecessarily delaying access to transplantation. Close perioperative monitoring, optimizing hemodynamic status during and after surgery, continuation of medical therapies as possible and timely intervention are critical components of the care [20].

Following the publication of ISCHEMIA-CKD, several transplant programs may have revisited their pretransplant CV screening protocols. Recent observational data have further challenged the paradigm of extensive pretransplant cardiac screening. In a contemporary cohort of 895 KT candidates in whom CV testing was deliberately minimized, perioperative CV mortality was 0% and remained 0.2% at 1 year, comparable to more intensively screened cohorts [53]. Importantly, candidates in this study underwent structured baseline assessment including symptom evaluation, ECG, echocardiography, and functional capacity assessment using DASI. Cardiology referral was reserved only for candidates with cardiac symptoms or known cardiac disease. Nevertheless, an external validation study of the 2022 AHA statement showed that only about one-third of candidates qualify as truly low-risk and can safely forgo further cardiac evaluation; the remaining two-thirds have sufficient risk factor to warrant cardiac screening. The proposed risk stratification effectively discriminated waitlisted patients according to subsequent CV events and all-cause mortality, supporting an individualized risk-based approach rather than a universally permissive screening strategy [54]. Additional real-world experience is needed to better define selective screening strategies in KT candidates.

CV risk profiles may differ according to dialysis modality. Hemodialysis is more commonly associated with intradialytic hypotension, arrhythmia, and LVH, whereas peritoneal dialysis is often linked to chronic volume overload and metabolic complications. However, current evidence, including ISCHEMIA-CKD, does not support modality-specific CV screening recommendations in KT candidates.

The contemporary era supports the concept that optimal medical therapy (OMT), including statins, antiplatelet agents, and blood pressure control, should be the cornerstone of CV disease management. Importantly, evidence supporting OMT in ESKD remains less robust than in the general population. Major stable CAD studies in the general population, including COURAGE, BARI-2D, ISCHEMIA, STICH, and REVIVED-BCIS2, consistently show that OMT, not routine revascularization, determines the long-term prognosis [55–59]. However, many pivotal CAD trials excluded patients with eGFR <30 ml/min, leaving substantial evidence gaps for OMT and revascularization in patients with ESKD. Trials such as AURORA, 4D, and SHARP demonstrated variable statin benefits in dialysis cohorts, and ACE inhibitor/ARB trials showed inconsistent CV risk reductions [60–63]. A meta-analysis demonstrates that revascularization is not superior to optimal medical therapy in reducing all-cause mortality, CV mortality, or MACE in waitlisted KT candidates with CAD who eventually underwent KT [64]. The specific OMT regimen used for CAD was not described within the individual studies. Therefore, it is unclear what the composition and dosage of the medications used were. Beyond traditional CV risk management, the novel cardiorenal protective agents, including sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonists, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), have demonstrated CV and kidney benefits in the patients with CKD stages 3–4; however, their efficacy and safety in waitlisted patients and their impact on pretransplant CV event rates remain to be established in dedicated trials. In addition, hyperkalemia risk with MRAs, gastrointestinal intolerance with GLP-1R agonists, ketoacidosis risk with SGLT2 inhibitors, and perioperative management concerns may complicate their use in transplant candidates.

Given the CV screening data derive predominantly from heterogeneous study populations, it should not be interpreted as evidence against revascularization in all patients. While OMT remains the basis of management, patients with refractory angina or a high likelihood of severe ischemia may warrant early invasive evaluation alongside OMT. Moreover, when coronary angiography identifies prognostically significant lesions, such as left main disease or critical multivessel CAD, deferring revascularization or coronary artery bypass grafting solely on the basis of ESKD status or transplant candidacy may be inappropriate, as delays in intervention could increase the risk of MI and arrhythmic death.

Lastly, although updated guidelines provide more structured recommendations, the implementation of screening strategies may reasonably vary across centers reflecting differences in local resources, imaging availability, operator expertise, cost considerations, and logistical constraints. Ideally, the cardiology assessment should be performed by an expert cardiologist and should remain an integral part of the multidisciplinary transplant evaluation process.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Future work is needed to determine which patients, if any, benefit from noninvasive testing and whether a subset of transplant candidates (e.g. those with severely calcified vessels or reduced ejection fraction) might still derive benefit from early detection and intervention. Cost-effective, radiation limited, highly sensitive noninvasive tests may provide easy decision-making on which patients require pretransplant invasive coronary angiography. Prospective registries and real-world outcome data will help refine best practices. Regarding surveillance after wait-listing, the results of Canadian Australasian Randomized Trial of Screening Kidney Transplant Candidates for Coronary Artery Disease (CARSK) study, a randomized controlled trial examining the costs and benefits of periodic screening in patients already wait-listed are awaited. Also, future studies are needed to define the role of SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP1R agonists and MRAs in reducing CV risk during the pretransplant waitlist period or post-transplant.

CONCLUSION

The ISCHEMIA-CKD trial represents a pivotal moment in the evolution of CV screening in patients with advanced CKD, including KT candidates. It has challenged long-held assumptions and driven a shift toward more selective, individualized evaluation strategies. While routine coronary angiography and intervention in asymptomatic high-risk patients is no longer clearly supported, thoughtful clinical judgment remains essential, particularly in the context of clinical suspicion-driven decision-making. Ultimately, the focus is shifting from detection and revascularization of silent disease to holistic risk reduction through OMT, lifestyle interventions, and timely access to transplantation.
