Psychological distress and drinking patterns among working-age adults in Japan: a population-based analysis
Authors: Shimpei Hanaoka, Shigeru Fujita, Takefumi Kitazawa, Kanako Seto, Kunichika Matsumoto, Tomonori Hasegawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe self-medication hypothesis proposes that psychological distress may contribute to increased alcohol use, yet population-level evidence in Japan remains limited. To examine population-level associations between psychological distress (K6) and drinking status among working-age adults in Japan, with comprehensive adjustment for sociodemographic and behavioral covariates.MethodsWe analyzed anonymized official statistical microdata from the 2019 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (Japan). Respondents aged 20–64 years with complete data were included (n = 3,935). Psychological distress was measured using K6 and categorized as low (0–4), moderate (5–12), and high (≥ 13). Drinking status was derived from frequency and amount per drinking day: heavy drinking was defined as estimated ethanol intake ≥ 44 g/day (≥ 2 gou/day); non-drinkers included “rarely,” “quit,” or “do not drink”; remaining participants were moderate drinkers (reference). Multinomial logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for heavy vs. moderate and non-drinkers vs. moderate, adjusting for age, gender, household composition, education, employment, income quartile, smoking, and psychiatric outpatient care.ResultsOf 3,935 participants, 20.6% were heavy drinkers (n = 810), 38.8% moderate drinkers (n = 1,527), and 40.6% non-drinkers (n = 1,598). High distress accounted for 4.5% (n = 177) and moderate distress for 25.3% (n = 995). Psychological distress was not associated with drinking status in cross-tabulations (p = 0.578). In adjusted models, distress was not significantly associated with heavy drinking or non-drinking. Moderate distress showed a borderline tendency toward heavy drinkers (aOR 1.21, p = 0.073), while high distress was null (aOR 1.02, p = 0.927). Covariates showed robust associations, including lower odds of heavy drinkers and higher odds of non-drinkers among women, higher odds of both heavy drinkers and non-drinkers among adults aged 20–39 (vs. 60–64), and strong positive association between smokers and heavy drinkers.ConclusionsPsychological distress was not an independent predictor of drinking status after adjustment, suggesting limited support for a simple population-level self-medication pathway in Japan. Longitudinal studies and subgroup analyses are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28449-z.

Background

Comorbidity between mood/anxiety symptoms and alcohol-related problems is widely recognized [1–3]. Patients presenting with depressive mood, anxiety, insomnia, irritability, or suicidal ideation often report related drinking or alcohol-related problems [4, 5]. One influential framework to explain such co-occurrence is the self-medication hypothesis [6, 7], which posits that individuals may escalate substance use to alleviate psychological distress. While alcohol may provide short-term relief from distress or sleep disturbance, it can contribute to symptom persistence, worsening, and the development of dependence over time [1].

Alcohol consumption has traditionally played an important role in Japanese social and workplace culture, particularly through after-work social gatherings (“nomikai”), although overall drinking prevalence has declined in recent decades. Nevertheless, hazardous alcohol consumption remains an important public health issue because of its association with multiple adverse health outcomes [10]. It remains uncertain, however, whether the distress-to-drinking pathway is consistently observable at the population level in Japan. Clinical samples are inherently selected toward individuals who have reached healthcare settings or meet diagnostic thresholds and thus may overrepresent severe cases. In contrast, psychological distress in the general population is distributed along a continuum, and the moderate-distress segment is numerically large even if many individuals do not meet diagnostic criteria. Testing the self-medication hypothesis from a public health perspective therefore requires a large national dataset that captures the full spectrum of distress and drinking behaviors. Moreover, distress and drinking patterns are likely confounded or shaped by multiple sociodemographic and behavioral factors, such as age, gender, household structure, education, employment, income, smoking, and mental healthcare utilization [8–10]. These factors influence exposure to drinking opportunities, social norms, coping resources, and health-related constraints, and may obscure or modify distress–drinking associations. Accordingly, this study uses anonymized official statistical data from the Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions 2019 [11] to examine the association between the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) [12, 13] and drinking status (heavy drinkers, moderate drinkers, and non-drinkers) among working-age adults in Japan. In this study, the self-medication hypothesis was operationalized as an association between current psychological distress and current drinking status at the population level. Using multinomial logistic regression with comprehensive adjustment for sociodemographic and behavioral covariates (reference outcome: moderate drinkers), we aimed to examine whether psychological distress was independently associated with drinking status in a nationally representative sample of working-age adults in Japan and to identify other factors associated with drinking patterns.

Methods

Data source

The data used in this study were derived from the Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions, a national survey conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan [11]. The 2019 survey was a large-scale survey year. Survey areas were selected by stratified random sampling of 5,530 areas nationwide based on the Population Census of Japan 2015 [14], and questionnaires were distributed to all households in each area.

The survey forms included questions on income, health status, savings, long-term care, and household composition, and comprised multiple forms (Household, Health, Long-term Care, Income, and Savings forms). The anonymized data [11, 15] used in this study were provided with approval under Articles 35 and 36 of the Statistics Act. To ensure confidentiality, potentially identifying information was removed or categorized prior to data provision. The anonymized dataset included 4,470 households and 11,697 individuals. According to the survey instructions, family members or caregivers could assist respondents who were unable to complete the questionnaire themselves. Because the anonymized dataset does not identify whether assistance was provided, this could not be examined in the present study.

Study population

As shown in Fig. 1, of the 11,697 respondents included in the anonymized dataset, 5,991 were aged 20–64 years. We excluded 2,056 participants with missing values in drinking status, psychological distress (K6), or any covariates and conducted a complete-case analysis. The final analytic sample comprised 3,935 individuals. The lower age limit (20 years) was chosen because alcohol consumption by individuals under 20 is prohibited under Japan’s Minor Drinking Prohibition Act. The upper age limit (64 years) was set to exclude adults aged ≥ 65, a commonly used threshold for older adulthood and a major transition in employment and social roles in Japan, which may substantially alter both drinking behavior and psychological distress and increase heterogeneity and confounding. This range also aligns with the widely used definition of the OECD working-age population (15–64 years).

Measures

Outcome: drinking status

Drinking status was classified into three categories using the survey items on participants’ usual drinking frequency and the amount consumed per drinking day, reported in units of gou, a traditional Japanese unit for alcohol consumption. One gou corresponds to approximately 22 g of ethanol, equivalent to about 180 mL of sake, 500 mL of beer, or 200 mL of wine, regardless of beverage type [10]. Weekly ethanol intake was estimated by combining the reported drinking frequency with the amount consumed per drinking day, and average daily ethanol intake was calculated by dividing the estimated weekly ethanol intake by seven. Heavy drinkers were defined as an estimated average intake of ≥ 44 g/day (≥ 2 gou/day), a threshold commonly used in Japanese studies and associated with increased risk of lifestyle-related diseases [10, 16]. Participants who answered “rarely drink,” “quit drinking,” or “do not drink” were classified as non-drinkers, and the remaining respondents with valid information were classified as moderate drinkers [10]. In regression analyses, moderate drinkers served as the reference outcome (Table 2).

Main independent variable: psychological distress (K6)

Psychological distress was assessed using the Japanese version of the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6), a six-item measure of nonspecific psychological distress during the previous 30 days. The six items assess feelings of nervousness, hopelessness, restlessness or fidgetiness, depression (so depressed that nothing could cheer them up), that everything was an effort, and worthlessness. Each item is scored on a five-point scale from 0 (“none of the time”) to 4 (“all of the time”), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 24. Respondents were categorized as low (0–4; reference), moderate (5–12), and high (≥ 13), consistent with previous validation studies of the Japanese version of the K6 [12, 13]. No recoding of K6 items was performed. Participants with missing K6 data were excluded as part of the complete-case analysis.

Statistical analysis

We summarized participant characteristics across the three drinking status categories using cross-tabulations and χ² tests. In Table 1, results are presented as column percentages within each drinking status category. To examine the association between psychological distress (K6) and drinking status with covariate adjustment, we performed multinomial logistic regression with drinking status as the dependent variable. Moderate drinkers were used as the reference outcome, and we estimated two contrasts: (i) heavy drinkers vs. moderate drinkers and (ii) non-drinkers vs. moderate drinkers. All covariates were entered simultaneously (forced-entry approach). Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model fit was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test comparing the final model with the intercept-only model. We also reported pseudo R² values (Nagelkerke) as indices of model fit. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). All VIF values were below 1.3, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 31.0).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess potential heterogeneity within the non-drinkers category and the “sick-quitter” concern, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding participants who reported having quit drinking (“former drinkers”). We then re-estimated the multinomial logistic regression using three categories (heavy, moderate [reference], and non-/rare drinkers[“rarely drink” or “do not drink”]), with the same covariate adjustment as in the main model.

Results

Participant characteristics (cross-tabulations)

Among the 3,935 participants, 49.6% were women (n = 1,952) and 50.4% were men (n = 1,983). Participants aged 40–49 years constituted the largest age group (28.3%), followed by those aged 50–59 years (26.5%). Table 1 summarizes the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the study population across the three drinking-status categories.

Among the participants, 20.6% were heavy drinkers (n = 810), 38.8% were moderate drinkers (n = 1,527), and 40.6% were non-drinkers (n = 1,598). High psychological distress (K6 ≥ 13) accounted for 4.5% (n = 177), moderate distress for 25.3% (n = 995), and low distress for 70.2% (n = 2,763).

In cross-tabulations, psychological distress was not significantly associated with drinking status (p = 0.578). By contrast, age group, gender, household composition, educational attainment, employment status, household income, and smoking were significantly associated with drinking status (all p < 0.001). Psychiatric outpatient care was not significantly associated (p = 0.141) (Table 1).

Multinomial logistic regression

In adjusted multinomial models (reference outcome: moderate drinkers), psychological distress was not significantly associated with either heavy drinkers or non-drinkers. Compared with low distress, moderate distress showed a non-significant tendency toward higher odds of heavy drinkers (aOR 1.208, p = 0.073), while high distress showed no association (aOR 1.021, p = 0.927). For non-drinkers, neither moderate distress (aOR 0.923, p = 0.358) nor high distress (aOR 0.855, p = 0.409) differed significantly from low distress (Table 2).

Several covariates showed robust associations. Women had markedly lower odds of heavy drinkers than men (aOR 0.480, p < 0.001) and higher odds of non-drinkers (aOR 2.089, p < 0.001). Younger age groups (20–29 and 30–39) had higher odds of both heavy drinkers and non-drinkers compared with ages 60–64. Smoking was strongly associated with heavy drinkers (smokers vs. non-smokers: aOR 1.917, p < 0.001). Living in a three-generation household was associated with lower odds of heavy drinkers (aOR 0.617, p = 0.026) and higher odds of non-drinkers (aOR 1.594, p = 0.011) relative to living alone. Lower educational attainment (vs. college or higher) was associated with higher odds of both heavy drinkers and non-drinkers. Household income quartiles (Q1–Q3 vs. Q4) were strongly associated with non-drinkers but not with heavy drinkers after adjustment. Psychiatric outpatient care was not significantly associated with drinking status after adjustment.

The final model provided a better fit than the intercept-only model (likelihood ratio χ²(42) = 664.934, p < 0.001), with modest pseudo R² values (Nagelkerke = 0.177).

Sensitivity analysis (excluding former drinkers)

Excluding former drinkers (n = 60) did not materially change the association between psychological distress and drinking status. Relative to low distress, moderate distress remained borderline for heavy drinkers (aOR 1.209; 95% CI 0.983–1.487; p = 0.072), while no association was observed for high distress (aOR 1.010; 95% CI 0.647–1.577; p = 0.964). Distress was not associated with non-/rare drinkers in this restricted sample (moderate distress: aOR 0.925; 95% CI 0.778–1.100; p = 0.377; high distress: aOR 0.787; 95% CI 0.536–1.155; p = 0.221), supporting the robustness of the main findings. Sensitivity analysis results are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

Using a large national dataset of working-age adults in Japan, this study tested the self-medication hypothesis by examining whether psychological distress (K6) is associated with drinking status. U.S. longitudinal evidence suggests that drinking to alleviate mood symptoms predicts subsequent alcohol dependence [2], and epidemiological reviews have summarized associations between mood/anxiety symptoms and substance use consistent with self-medication processes [3].

In contrast, evidence from Japan indicates that heavy drinking is strongly patterned by sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics [10].

Consistent with this Japanese evidence, psychological distress was not independently associated with drinking status after comprehensive adjustment in our analyses; instead, drinking patterns were more strongly explained by factors such as gender, age, education, household structure, income, smoking, and employment status.

Self-medication hypothesis at the population level in Japan

Clinical observations and prior epidemiological work support the idea that some individuals use alcohol to cope with distress, and self-medication remains a plausible mechanism in selected subgroups. However, our findings suggest that, at the population level, distress–drinking associations may not emerge as a robust main effect once contextual determinants are comprehensively accounted for. Several considerations may explain the absence of a strong population-level association. First, the self-medication hypothesis inherently implies temporal ordering—psychological distress preceding escalation in alcohol use—which cannot be established using cross-sectional data. Moreover, alcohol consumption itself can worsen sleep, mood, and anxiety, resulting in bidirectional relationships that may attenuate net associations in cross-sectional analyses. Second, K6 captures non-specific psychological distress [12, 13]; heterogeneous symptom profiles may relate to alcohol use in different directions, potentially diluting associations when aggregated into broad categories. Third, the non-drinkers category is heterogeneous [19], including lifetime abstainers, former drinkers who stopped because of health problems, and individuals who avoid alcohol for social or cultural reasons. However, our results were unchanged in a sensitivity analysis excluding former drinkers, suggesting that heterogeneity among non-drinkers did not materially bias the estimated distress–drinking associations. Nonetheless, this group remains conceptually heterogeneous, and a “sick-quitter effect”—in which individuals with poorer mental or physical health are overrepresented among non-drinkers—could still obscure transitions between drinking categories.

The null association for high distress may partly reflect limited statistical power due to the small proportion of participants with K6 ≥ 13 (4.5%). The borderline association observed for moderate distress (K6 5–12) and heavy drinking (p = 0.073) may suggest a weak or non-linear relationship that warrants further investigation in larger or longitudinal samples.

Sociodemographic structuring of drinking patterns

The strong associations observed for covariates highlight that drinking behaviors in Japan are embedded in social roles, norms, and behavioral clusters [10]. Workplace-related factors have also been linked to problem drinking among Japanese workers [20] and harmful alcohol consumption has been associated with occupational stress and work–family spillover in Japan [21]. Importantly, these prior findings were consistent with our adjusted results (Table 2). Specifically, women had substantially lower odds of heavy drinkers and higher odds of non-drinkers than men, plausibly reflecting differences in social drinking opportunities and workplace drinking contexts as well as role-related constraints such as work–family demands [20, 21]. Younger age groups (particularly 20–39 years) showed higher odds of both heavy drinkers and non-drinkers relative to those aged 60–64, suggesting polarization of drinking behaviors in earlier adulthood. This pattern may reflect generational changes in drinking culture in Japan, characterized by the coexistence of socially intensive drinking among some young adults and increasing alcohol abstention among others. Smoking was strongly associated with heavy drinkers, consistent with clustering of health-risk behaviors [17]. Educational attainment also differentiated drinking status: compared with those with college education or higher, participants with lower education had higher odds of both heavy drinkers and non-drinkers, aligning with evidence that educational gradients shape harmful drinking and problem drinking in Japan [9]. Household structure further differentiated drinking patterns: living in a three-generation household was associated with lower odds of heavy drinkers and higher odds of non-drinkers, suggesting that living arrangements may impose different social constraints and norms around alcohol use.

Finally, household income showed a clear association with non-drinkers (Q1–Q3 vs. Q4) but not with heavy drinkers after adjustment, indicating that socioeconomic context may differentially structure abstention and heavy consumption.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and temporal sequencing. Second, alcohol intake and distress were self-reported, potentially leading to misclassification and under-reporting. Third, non-drinkers are heterogeneous; however, a sensitivity analysis excluding former drinkers yielded results consistent with the main analysis, suggesting that this heterogeneity did not substantially bias the estimated associations. Fourth, although we adjusted for key sociodemographic and behavioral covariates, residual confounding is possible. Fifth, respondents who were unable to complete the questionnaire could receive assistance from family members or caregivers according to the survey instructions. Because such assistance could not be identified in the anonymized dataset, some responses may have been influenced by assisted completion.

Implications for public health and future research

From a public health perspective, strategies to reduce harmful drinking and related harms may need to prioritize behavioral clustering and socioeconomic contexts rather than focusing solely on psychological distress severity. At the same time, the absence of a strong population-level main effect does not preclude distress-related drinking within specific vulnerable subgroups. Future research should examine effect modification by factors such as sex, age, smoking status, and employment, and use longitudinal data to clarify temporal ordering and transitions between drinking categories. Distinguishing lifetime abstainers from former drinkers would further improve interpretability and reduce outcome heterogeneity.

Conclusions

In a large national dataset of working-age adults in Japan, psychological distress (K6) was not independently associated with drinking status after adjustment, providing limited support for a simple population-level self-medication pathway. These findings underscore the importance of social, behavioral, and socioeconomic contexts in shaping drinking behaviors, and highlight the need for longitudinal and stratified analyses to identify subgroups in which distress-related drinking may occur.
