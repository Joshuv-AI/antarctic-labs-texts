Acalabrutinib alone or in combination with rituximab for follicular lymphoma: An open‐label study
Authors: Paolo Strati, Rebecca Champion, Morton Coleman, Sonali M. Smith, Parameswaran Venugopal, Peter Martin, Andrew Wood, Kara Miller, Beth Christian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcalabrutinib is a selective, second‐generation Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor. In this open‐label, parallel‐group study, patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) follicular lymphoma (FL) were randomised to either acalabrutinib monotherapy or acalabrutinib plus rituximab. An additional cohort of patients with treatment‐naive (TN) FL received only the acalabrutinib‐rituximab combination. Acalabrutinib‐rituximab was well tolerated and active in R/R and TN FL; in the TN cohort the overall response rate was 92.3% with most remissions lasting over 4 years. Acalabrutinib monotherapy was also well tolerated and active in R/R FL. These results support further study of acalabrutinib alone and in combination with rituximab in FL.

Approximately 20% of patients administered anti‐CD20 antibody–based first‐line chemoimmunotherapy for follicular lymphoma (FL) experience disease progression within 24 months (POD24), 1 and subsequent treatments are increasingly less effective. 2 , 3 Underlying mechanisms of FL tumourigenesis involve tumour‐associated macrophages (TAMs), which are crucial for FL cell survival in vitro, 4 and Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) activity, which mediates B‐cell receptor (BCR) signalling required for the development and survival of normal and malignant B cells. 5 BTK inhibitors (BTKis), which block BCR signalling, have demonstrated efficacy in chronic lymphocytic leukaemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma and marginal zone lymphoma. 6 BTKis interrupt cross‐talk between FL cells and TAMs through BCR/dendritic cell–specific intercellular adhesion molecule‐3–grabbing non‐integrin (DC‐SIGN)–mediated mechanisms. 7 In the ROSEWOOD study in relapsed/refractory (R/R) FL, addition of zanubrutinib to obinutuzumab significantly improved the overall response rate (ORR) versus obinutuzumab alone, 8 suggesting an advantage of BTKi/anti‐CD20 antibody therapy. In contrast, addition of ibrutinib to rituximab‐based chemoimmunotherapy did not significantly improve progression‐free survival (PFS) in the SELENE study. 9 Acalabrutinib, a potent, selective, second‐generation BTKi, 10 may be a promising candidate for combination therapy, as it does not directly impact anti‐CD20 antibody–dependent phagocytosis, unlike ibrutinib. 11

We assessed outcomes with acalabrutinib in B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphoma in a 3‐part, phase 1b/2 multicentre clinical trial (NCT02180711). We report the results of part 1, an open‐label, randomised, parallel‐group study that evaluated the safety and activity of acalabrutinib, alone or in combination with rituximab in R/R FL and in combination with rituximab in treatment‐naive (TN) FL.

Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of FL, TN (TN cohort) or had R/R FL with ≥1 prior therapy (R/R cohort), required treatment per National Cancer Institute or International Working Group guidelines, and provided written informed consent. Other key inclusion criteria were radiographically measurable lymphadenopathy or extranodal lymphoid malignancy (≥1 lesion ≥2.0 cm in longest dimension and ≥ 1.0 cm in longest perpendicular dimension on computed tomography [CT] scan) and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≤2. Individuals with prior B‐cell receptor inhibitor exposure, known central nervous system lymphoma or leptomeningeal disease, ongoing systemic or enteric immunosuppressive therapy, significant cardiovascular disease, or uncontrolled active systemic infection were excluded. The trial was approved by each site's Institutional Review Board/Independent Ethics Committee before initiation and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation/Good Clinical Practice, applicable regulatory requirements, and the sponsor's standard operating procedures.

Patients in the R/R cohort were randomised 1:1 to 28‐day cycles of acalabrutinib 100 mg orally twice daily (acalabrutinib‐monotherapy‐R/R) or acalabrutinib 100 mg orally twice daily, plus rituximab 375 mg/m2 intravenously (IV) on days 1, 8, 15 and 22 of cycle 1 and day 1 of cycles 2–6 (acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R). The original protocol contained an arm in the R/R cohort for acalabrutinib 200 mg once daily; however, an amendment replaced it with acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R. Patients in the TN cohort were not randomised and received only acalabrutinib + rituximab as described for acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R (acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐TN). In all arms, acalabrutinib was administered until disease progression (PD) or unacceptable toxicity.

The primary end‐point was safety in the R/R cohort; secondary end‐points included safety (TN cohort), activity (all three arms), and acalabrutinib ± rituximab pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic profiles (not presented). Baseline disease was assessed by bone marrow biopsy and positron‐emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scan. Tumour response was evaluated via CT scan on day 1 of cycles 3, 5 and 7 and every three cycles thereafter up to 2 years, then every six cycles (at investigator discretion); PET/CT and (if bone marrow involvement documented at baseline) a repeat bone marrow biopsy were required to confirm complete response (CR). Investigators assessed ORR per Lugano criteria. PFS was analysed with the Kaplan–Meier method.

The primary end‐point of safety in this study was used to generate preliminary, descriptive safety data for further development of acalabrutinib treatments. As such, sample size was determined based on the probability of observing ≥1 instances of an adverse event with a background rate of 5% and 10%; with a total sample size of 36 these probabilities are 84% and 98%, respectively, and with 12 patients in each treatment arm the probabilities are 46% and 72%, respectively.

Continuous variables were summarised with descriptive statistics, and categorical variables by number and percentage of patients; confidence intervals (CIs) were constructed at the two‐sided, 95% level. Analyses were based on the population of treated patients.

All 40 enrolled patients received treatment. The analyses described here focus on the acalabrutinib‐monotherapy‐R/R (n = 12), acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R (n = 13) and acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐TN (n = 13) arms; baseline characteristics are summarised in Table 1. Two patients enrolled into the original acalabrutinib 200‐mg arm (median time on study, 2.8 months) discontinued for PD and initiated subsequent anticancer therapy; neither experienced grade ≥3 treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs). To preserve the integrity of the statistical analyses, a new randomisation schedule was started for the acalabrutinib monotherapy and acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R arms.

At data cut‐off (15 July 2022), median (95% CI) follow‐up duration by reverse Kaplan–Meier analysis was 8.1 (2.1, 61.0), 14.3 (1.9, 74.4) and 49.5 (19.5, 79.2) months for the acalabrutinib‐monotherapy‐R/R, acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R, and acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐TN arms, respectively. Median (range) acalabrutinib treatment duration in these arms was 7.0 (1.8–76.9), 6.0 (0.2–80.1) and 27.6 (1.8–80.7) months. Aside from patients who exited the study when it closed, the most common reasons for study discontinuation (acalabrutinib‐monotherapy‐R/R, acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R and acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐TN, respectively) were to start alternative anti‐lymphoma therapy (58.3%, 46.2% and 15.4%) and withdrawal of consent (16.7%, 7.7% and 30.8%).

Overall, 22 (55%) patients experienced grade 3/4 TEAEs. Hypertension, diarrhoea, increased alanine aminotransferase and increased aspartate aminotransferase were the only grade 3/4 TEAEs reported by ≥2 patients in any arm; Table 2 summarises TEAEs, events of clinical interest, and acalabrutinib dose modifications. Grade 3/4 neutropenia, thrombocytopenia and infections occurred in two, one and seven patients, respectively. Grade 2 atrial fibrillation was reported for one patient in the acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R arm and considered related to acalabrutinib. No ventricular tachyarrhythmias or grade 5 TEAEs were reported.

The ORR (95% CI) and CR rate were 92.3% (64.0, 99.8) and 38.5%, respectively, for acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐TN, 33.3% (9.9, 65.1) and 8.3% for acalabrutinib‐monotherapy‐R/R, and 30.8% (9.1, 61.4) and 15.4% for acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R (Figure S1A). The ORR (95% CI) among patients with refractory disease (n = 5) was 40.0% (5.3, 85.3); among patients with relapsed disease (n = 15), it was 33.3% (11.8, 61.6). Relapsed versus refractory status was unknown in seven patients. Figure S1B shows best percent change from baseline in sum of product diameters. Median (95% CI) PFS was not reached (16.5 months, not estimable [NE]) for acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐TN, 12.0 (1.9, NE) months for acalabrutinib‐monotherapy‐R/R, and 8.3 (1.8, NE) months for acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R (Figure S1C). One patient in the acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R arm died (PD >30 days after last acalabrutinib dose).

BTKis had not been considered for R/R FL treatment based on results with ibrutinib‐based therapy 12 , 13 ; however, new data suggest the usefulness of second‐generation BTKis in FL. In our small TN FL cohort, acalabrutinib‐rituximab combination therapy delivered a high ORR (92.3%) with durable remissions (median PFS not reached after median follow‐up of 49.5 months) and was well tolerated, with only one patient discontinuing due to TEAEs. In R/R FL, outcomes with acalabrutinib monotherapy were modest (ORR, 33.3%) and comparable to those with ibrutinib 12 and zanubrutinib 14 monotherapy. Addition of rituximab to acalabrutinib in R/R FL did not improve the ORR compared with acalabrutinib monotherapy, unlike addition of zanubrutinib to obinutuzumab in the ROSEWOOD study. 8 Differences may reflect that, in our acalabrutinib‐rituximab‐R/R group, 10/13 (76.9%) patients had prior rituximab and 5/13 (38.5%) were refractory to anti‐CD20 antibody–containing regimens and/or that, as shown preclinically, 15 obinutuzumab provides better antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity than rituximab. Limitations include small patient numbers, inability to make formal between‐group comparisons, and no data on POD24 status. The relatively low‐risk population (TN, low FLIPI score; R/R, few prior treatments) limited comparison with chemotherapy‐based treatments.

In conclusion, acalabrutinib, a non‐chemotherapy oral BTKi, was well tolerated as monotherapy in R/R FL and in combination with rituximab in R/R and TN FL. Further studies exploring acalabrutinib in FL are ongoing.
