Three-dimensional reconstruction technology facilitates surgical planning for endometrial cancer with pelvic ectopic kidney: a case report
Authors: Yachai Li, Jiarui Mi, Peixuan Wang, Ke Zhang, Liwei Yan, Yuanmei Deng, Meng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Reports a rare and high-risk case of endometrial cancer with a pelvic ectopic kidney.•Utilized 3D printing to visualize spatial anatomy between tumor and ectopic kidney.•Surgery guided by the 3D model was performed safely without unexpected injuries.•Provides an innovative safety strategy for gynecologic tumors with rare anomalies.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer is one of the most common gynecologic malignancies. Its surgical treatment typically includes total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, with or without lymph node assessment. The removal of pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes is critical for surgical staging, guiding prognosis, and identifying patients who may benefit from adjuvant therapy (Makker et al., 2021). The kidneys originate from the pelvic cavity; if they fail to ascend properly, they can remain in the pelvis, or in other cases, ascend excessively into the thoracic cavity. For a kidney located in the pelvis, not only is its anatomical position altered, but the associated vasculature, ureter, and other structures are also similarly altered (Eid et al., 2018). When endometrial cancer coexists with an ectopic kidney, the primary consideration is the increased probability of iatrogenic injury to the ectopic kidney, its renal vessels, and the ureter during surgery, which significantly increases surgical complexity. This article presents a case of endometrioid adenocarcinoma complicated by a right pelvic ectopic kidney. Preoperative application of three-dimensional reconstruction technology facilitated the assessment of surgical risks, which may help avoid complications such as iatrogenic injuries.

Case report

The patient was a 45-year-old Chinese female who was admitted to the hospital for vaginal bleeding lasting over one month. Diagnostic curettage pathology indicated: focal cancerous transformation-well-differentiated endometrioid adenocarcinoma. Pelvic plain and contrast-enhanced MRI showed irregular thickening of the endometrium with a depth of invasion greater than one-half of the myometrium, suggestive of stage IB endometrial cancer. Signal intensity characteristic of renal tissue was also detected in the right pelvis (Fig. 1c). Abdominal CT (Fig. 1a and b) revealed a right pelvic ectopic kidney situated anterior to the spine, with no enlarged lymph nodes in the abdominal cavity or retroperitoneum. The patient had normal renal function. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with stage IB endometrial carcinoma co-occurring with a right pelvic ectopic kidney. According to the NCCN guidelines, the preferred treatment for this patient is comprehensive staging surgery combined with lymph node assessment or sentinel lymph node biopsy (Abu-Rustum et al., 2023). However, considering that the ectopic kidney occupies the pelvic cavity, which may affect sentinel lymph node imaging, it was decided to perform an extra-fascial abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Given that the ectopic kidney was located adjacent to the lower abdominal aorta and right iliac vessels, this positioning increased the difficulty of lymphadenectomy. Preoperative assessment of the distribution and course of the ectopic kidney's blood vessels and ureter, as well as their spatial relationship with surrounding vessels and organs, was essential to mitigate the risk of iatrogenic injury. Although the available imaging studies provided information on the kidney's location and vasculature, they could not ascertain variations in the renal blood supply, and the course of the ureter was ill-defined. Therefore, precise 3D reconstruction technology was used preoperatively to generate a three-dimensional model of the patient's pelvic and abdominal vasculature and the ectopic kidney, thereby providing a clear visualization of the anatomical relationships between the ectopic kidney, vasculature, and surrounding organs. The established 3D model revealed that the right ectopic kidney was located inferior to the bifurcation of the abdominal aorta, closely associated with surrounding vascular structures, and exhibited renal duplication. The arterial supply originated from the terminal segment of the abdominal aorta and the origin of the right common iliac artery. Venous outflow drained primarily into the left common iliac vein, while tributaries from the medial and lateral cortical regions drained into the inferior vena cava and the right gonadal vein. Both ureters followed a normal course, entering the bladder beneath the uterine arteries. (Fig. 2a and b).Fig. 2(a,b) Anatomy of the patient's pelvic region and pelvic ectopic renal blood supply. Guided by the three-dimensional model, we successfully performed a transabdominal extra-fascial total hysterectomy, bilateral adnexectomy, pelvic lymph node dissection, and low para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Intraoperative findings showed an ectopic kidney in the pelvic cavity located at the bifurcation of the right iliac vessels (Fig. 3). The two sets of arteries, veins, and the right excretory system of the ectopic duplicated kidney were meticulously dissected and the lymph nodes in the adjacent region were resected. The surgery proceeded uneventfully, with 80 ml of blood loss and no intraoperative complications.Fig. 3Intraoperative Findings − Right Kidney Located in the Pelvic Cavity.

The patient was able to ambulate on the second postoperative day and was discharged one week after surgery. Postoperative pathological examination revealed well-to-moderately differentiated endometrioid adenocarcinoma invading the myometrium, with a maximum depth of invasion of approximately 0.8 cm against a total uterine myometrial wall thickness of 2.8 cm. The tumor extended to the mucosa of the lower uterine segment but spared the cervical mucosa. Lymph node dissection yielded no evidence of metastasis in the left pelvic (0/12), right pelvic (0/10), or para-aortic (0/5) regions.

Discussion

The incidence of endometrial cancer (EC) is increasing annually, and the number of EC diagnoses is projected to double by 2030 (Oaknin et al., 2022). Surgery remains the primary treatment for early-stage endometrial cancer, yielding favorable outcomes for most patients (Cinman et al., 2007). A pelvic ectopic kidney, a congenital anatomical variant, has an incidence of approximately 1 in 2,200 to 1 in 3,000 (ESGO, 2021). The coexistence of endometrial cancer coexisting with a pelvic ectopic kidney are extremely rare.

This case report presents a patient with endometrial cancer, who following preoperative evaluation, underwent an extra-fascial total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy; and pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. The altered anatomical architecture resulting from the concomitant pelvic ectopic kidney distorts the normal course of the associated vessels and ureters. Consequently, the procedure poses significant technical challenges and a high risk of complications. The primary reason for this is that ectopic kidneys are commonly associated with aberrant vessels, particularly in pelvic ectopic kidneys with the blood supply potentially originating from the internal iliac artery or aortic bifurcation. Intraoperative injury to these vessels poses a significant risk of massive hemorrhage or loss of renal function (ESGO, 2021). Ureteral abnormalities, including shortened or tortuous ureters, pose significant risks during endometrial cancer surgery (particularly during lymphadenectomy), in such cases, the ureters are vulnerable to inadvertent transection or electrothermal injury (ERUS-ESGO Consensus Statement, 2023).

Therefore, endometrial cancer surgery in patients with a concomitant pelvic ectopic kidney is classified as a “highly complex procedure”, which requires simultaneous fulfillment of two critical objectives: achieving R0 resection of the tumor (complete microscopic margin-negative resection), and preserving the ectopic kidney's function while avoiding urologic/vascular complications (Ebley et al., 2024). The ESGO guidelines recommend using 3D reconstructed imaging or intraoperative ultrasound to localize the ectopic kidney and ureters. To assess renal function, preoperative functional imaging (DMSA scan) should be performed to determine the split functional contribution of the ectopic kidney. Regarding intraoperative management, ureteral stent placement (ESGO Recommendation Grade:ⅡA) is recommended when clinically indicated. Additionally, monopolar electrocautery should be avoided near the ectopic renal vasculature, and the use of alternatives, such as bipolar energy devices or ultrasonic scalpels, is advised.

A preoperative a multidisciplinary team (MDT) consultation was conducted involving the urology and radiology departments. Three-dimensional reconstruction was utilized to map the ectopic kidney's position in the pelvis, its blood supply, and the displaced ureter's anatomical course. The model clearly visualized the spatial relationships between the ectopic kidney and surrounding structures, including the uterus, blood vessels, lymph nodes, and adjacent organs, with the aim of preserving the ectopic kidney's function and avoiding ureteral injury during pelvic lymph node dissection.

Intraoperatively, the model demonstrated high anatomical fidelity with the patient's actual anatomy. Guided by the 3D reconstruction, the surgical team accurately identified and preserved the ectopic kidney, ureter, and surrounding blood vessels while avoiding iatrogenic injury, thereby contributing to an uneventful surgical course. The 3D reconstruction technology optimized the surgical approach without significantly prolonging operative time. Intraoperative blood loss was minimal, and no unexpected injuries occurred. Notably, the avoidance of a ureteral stent mitigated the risks of urinary tract infections, stent-related complications, and associated healthcare costs. The patient ambulated on postoperative day 2, concurrent with a gradual restoration of gastrointestinal function. No infections or thromboembolic events occurred. The incision demonstrated excellent healing, and sutures were removed on postoperative day 7. The patient was discharged in stable condition, indicating a favorable clinical outcome.

This case highlights the broader potential of 3D reconstruction in surgical oncology, particularly for patients with rare anatomical variations or complex malignancies. While the high costs of this technology may limit its widespread application, its potential to enhance surgical safety and efficacy in challenging cases merits further investigation. Future research should focus on integrating such advanced imaging techniques into the management of various complex oncological conditions, to facilitate improved surgical outcomes and patient prognoses in similar clinical scenarios.

Conclusion

The co-occurrence of endometrial carcinoma and a pelvic ectopic kidney is a rare anatomical variant that significantly increases the risk of vascular and ureteral injury during pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Preoperative three-dimensional CT-based reconstruction delineated the anatomy of the ectopic kidney, including its vascular course and ureteral structure, thereby guiding the surgical procedure, preventing injury to critical organs, and improving surgical safety for patients with gynecological malignancies complicated by rare malformations. The successful outcome in this patient highlights the clinical utility of this approach.
