Swallowing Muscle Ultrasonography in Children With Cerebral Palsy and Dysphagia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Mazlum Serdar Akaltun, Bülent Alyanak, Ebru Umay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo determine whether the thickness of swallowing‐related muscles measured by ultrasonography (US) differs according to dysphagia status in children with cerebral palsy (CP) and to identify muscle‐specific associations between US measurements and swallowing symptoms.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, 66 children with CP aged 2–18 years were evaluated at a tertiary outpatient rehabilitation clinic. B‐mode US was used to measure the thickness of swallowing‐related muscles. Symptom‐defined dysphagia was defined as a Pediatric Eating Assessment Tool‐10 (Pedi‐EAT‐10) score of ≥ 4. Muscle thicknesses were compared between children with and without dysphagia. Associations between individual Pedi‐EAT‐10 items and muscle thickness measurements were explored using Spearman correlation and univariate linear regression analyses.ResultsOf the 66 children, 27 (40.9%) met the criteria for dysphagia. US‐measured muscle thickness did not differ significantly between children with and without dysphagia for the masseter (0.66 ± 0.12 vs. 0.68 ± 0.10 cm; p = 0.520), genioglossus (0.69 ± 0.15 vs. 0.64 ± 0.13 cm; p = 0.166), geniohyoid (0.60 ± 0.14 vs. 0.64 ± 0.13 cm; p = 0.208), anterior digastric (0.50 ± 0.12 vs. 0.50 ± 0.12 cm; p = 0.887), or mylohyoid muscles (0.15 ± 0.03 vs. 0.15 ± 0.05 cm; p = 0.959). Symptom‐level analyses demonstrated inverse associations between selected swallowing complaints and specific muscle measurements, with the strongest association observed between “coughs while eating” and genioglossus thickness.ConclusionsAlthough swallowing‐related muscle thickness did not differ between children with CP with and without dysphagia, US measurements were associated with specific caregiver‐reported swallowing complaints. These findings suggest that US may provide complementary, symptom‐oriented information in the clinical assessment of swallowing difficulties in children with CP.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) refers to a group of non‐progressive disorders resulting from non‐progressive disturbances in the developing brain occurring during the prenatal, perinatal, or postnatal periods, leading to permanent impairments in the development of movement and posture and consequent limitations in activity [1]. A recent systematic review reported that 81.5% of children with CP experience dysphagia [2]. In this population, dysphagia commonly occurs in parallel with other developmental impairments, such as cognitive deficits and limitations in fine and gross motor skills [3, 4]. Even in Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) levels I and II, clinical signs suggestive of pharyngeal‐phase impairment are common [5]. In children with CP, the presence of dysphagia may lead to feeding‐related complications that contribute to malnutrition and growth delay [6]. Awareness is increasing regarding the importance of preventing, early identifying, and treating feeding difficulties and malnutrition in this population to avoid acute and long‐term adverse outcomes. This has created a need for comprehensive assessments and individualized interventions, with the first step being the screening and diagnosis of dysphagia. Accordingly, a range of screening tools and instrumental methods are used to identify dysphagia in children with CP.

VFSS and FEES are essential instrumental methods for evaluating swallowing safety, including penetration, aspiration, and pharyngeal residue, at the individual patient level [7, 8, 9]. However, these methods do not directly and quantitatively measure the morphology of swallowing‐related muscles. In contrast, ultrasonography (US) offers several important advantages, including being noninvasive, radiation‐free, repeatable, and easily applicable at the bedside. In addition, it enables direct visualization of swallowing‐related muscles and may provide clinically relevant information regarding muscle morphology and swallowing function.

Current literature increasingly recognizes the use of US for evaluating the thickness, cross‐sectional area, mass, and quality of swallowing‐related muscles. Most studies in this field have been conducted in adult and geriatric populations, particularly in the context of sarcopenic dysphagia [10, 11, 12]. In contrast, data on US assessment of swallowing‐related muscle morphology in children with CP remain limited. Given that children with CP constitute a heterogeneous population in terms of motor function level, oral motor control, feeding performance, growth status, and swallowing symptoms, US‐derived muscle morphometry may contribute to a better understanding of different aspects of swallowing physiology in this population.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to compare US‐measured thickness of swallowing‐related muscles between children with CP with and without dysphagia and to explore muscle‐specific associations between US morphometry and caregiver‐reported swallowing symptoms.

Methods

Ethics Statement

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Advisory Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Gaziantep University (Approval date: 07.05.2025; Decision no: 2025/96) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all children included in the study.

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study was conducted at the Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation clinic between 10 May 2025 and 10 October 2025. A total of 66 children with CP aged 2–18 years who presented to the clinic during the study period were included. Children who were unable to cooperate during US measurements, could not maintain a sitting position, had a history of maxillofacial, head, or neck surgery, had structural oropharyngeal deformities, had a diagnosis of esophageal dysphagia or gastro‐esophageal reflux, or were receiving medical and/or rehabilitative treatment specifically for dysphagia were excluded.

Demographic Characteristics

Participants' age (months), sex, height (cm), weight (kg), medical and family history, comorbidities, motor functional status, and CP subtype (ataxic, diplegic, dystonic, hemiplegic, and tetraplegic) were recorded.

Assessment Tools

Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS)

Motor functional status was assessed using the GMFCS. The GMFCS is a classification system that describes a child's usual gross motor abilities and limitations. GMFCS levels range from I to V, where Level I indicates independent ambulation and Level V indicates the need to be transported in a manual wheelchair [13].

Pediatric Eating Assessment Tool‐10 (Pedi‐EAT‐10)

The Pedi‐EAT‐10, a dysphagia screening instrument, was used to evaluate the presence and severity of swallowing difficulties. The scale comprises 10 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale from 0 (“no problem”) to 4 (“severe problem”), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 40. Based on normative data, a Pedi‐EAT‐10 score ≥ 4 indicates dysphagia, with higher scores reflecting more severe symptoms [14]. This cut‐off value was used solely to classify participants according to caregiver‐reported symptoms of swallowing difficulty.

Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS)

Swallowing function was assessed using the Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS). This 7‐level ordinal scale documents patients' functional intake of food and liquids. Scores range from 1 to 7, with higher scores indicating better swallowing function [15].

Ultrasonographic Evaluation

All swallowing US measurements were performed in the sitting position with the head in a neutral position. A Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation specialist performed real‐time ultrasonographic measurements of the bilateral masseter, genioglossus, geniohyoid, mylohyoid, and anterior digastric muscle thicknesses using an ultrasound device (GE Logiq P5, General Electric, Korea) with a 7–12 MHz linear array transducer. For the masseter muscles and anterior digastric muscles, measurements were taken bilaterally and the mean of the right and left sides was used for the analyses.

Masseter Muscle

For masseter muscle thickness measurement, consistent with previously reported methods, a line was drawn connecting the corner of the mouth to the intertragic notch of the auricle and passing over the masseter muscle. Measurements were obtained at the thickest portion of the muscle—near the occlusal plane and at the mid‐lateral aspect of the mandibular ramus—with the transducer oriented perpendicular to the scanning plane [16].

Genioglossus Muscle

When measuring genioglossus muscle thickness, the transducer was placed along the midline of the tongue's long axis and submentally to take measurements [16].

Geniohyoid, Mylohyoid, and Anterior Digastric Muscles

Thickness measurements of these muscles were performed in a manner consistent with previous studies. For this purpose, the transducer was positioned in the submental region in a coronal plane to visualize the anterior digastric, mylohyoid, and geniohyoid muscles with clear delineation of their borders. Anterior digastric thickness was measured bilaterally at the thickest point as the distance between the upper and lower fascial boundaries enclosing the muscle. Mylohyoid thickness was measured in the same plane, immediately inferior to the digastric measurement level, again between the fascial boundaries. Geniohyoid thickness was measured after clearly identifying the muscle in the deeper plane adjacent to the mylohyoid, at the midportion where it appeared widest/thickest, as the distance between the fascial boundaries [17, 18].

Data Collection and Grouping

Participants' demographic data, assessment scale scores, and US measurements were recorded. Patients were divided into two groups based on Pedi‐EAT‐10 scores: those with dysphagia and those without dysphagia, as determined by the predefined Pedi‐EAT‐10 cut‐off. The groups were then compared in terms of sociodemographic characteristics, clinical variables, and US measurements.

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2). Because prior data on the association between Pedi‐EAT‐10 scores and US‐measured swallowing muscle thickness in children with CP were limited, a large effect size (r = 0.50) was assumed. With a power of 80% and a two‐tailed significance level of 0.05, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 29 participants.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For descriptive statistics, continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (minimum–maximum), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Between‐group differences were analyzed using the chi‐square (χ2) test and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, and the independent‐samples t‐test and Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Associations between swallowing‐related muscle measurements and individual Pedi‐EAT‐10 items were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. For correlations that reached statistical significance, univariate simple linear regression analyses were performed with swallowing muscle measurements as the dependent variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 66 children with CP were included. The mean age was 9.90 ± 4.01 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 16.67 ± 4.61. Overall, 74.2% of participants were male (n = 49) and 25.8% were female (n = 17). According to CP subtype, 53.0% were diplegic, 19.7% hemiplegic, 15.2% tetraplegic, 7.6% ataxic, and 4.5% dystonic. The distribution of GMFCS levels was as follows: Level I, 24.2%; Level II, 31.8%; Level III, 21.2%; Level IV, 18.2%; and Level V, 4.6%. The median GMFCS level was 2.0 (min–max: 1.0–5.0). Overall, 24.2% of participants required diet modification, including 13.6% who received a blenderized diet. The mean feeding duration was 21.89 ± 7.58 min. Regarding feeding independence, 12.2% of participants were fully dependent, 24.2% partially dependent, 13.6% required supervision only, and 50.0% were independent. The mean FOIS score was 6.53 ± 0.93, and most participants (68.2%) were classified as Level 7. The median Pedi‐EAT‐10 score was 1.5 (range, 0–27). On US, mean muscle thicknesses were 0.67 ± 0.11 cm for the masseter, 0.66 ± 0.14 cm for the genioglossus, 0.63 ± 0.14 cm for the geniohyoid, 0.50 ± 0.12 cm for the anterior digastric, and 0.15 ± 0.04 cm for the mylohyoid (Table 1).

Discussion

This study explored the relationships between US‐derived swallowing muscle morphometry and caregiver‐reported swallowing symptoms in children with CP. At the group level, no significant differences were observed in muscle thickness across the measured muscles. In contrast, symptom‐level analyses revealed a muscle‐specific pattern: coughing during meals showed a moderate negative association with genioglossus thickness and a weaker negative association with the masseter, whereas the complaint of “inability to gain weight” was negatively correlated with geniohyoid and anterior digastric thickness. No significant association was identified for the mylohyoid. Collectively, these findings suggest that specific swallowing symptoms may relate to different muscle groups, potentially reflecting distinct clinical dimensions of swallowing dysfunction.

In the literature, the role of US in evaluating swallowing‐related muscles—particularly with respect to their area, mass, thickness, and quality—is increasingly recognized [10, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Most of these studies have been conducted in geriatric populations and in patients with sarcopenic dysphagia. Previous US studies have reported that tongue, geniohyoid [21] and masseter [10] muscle thickness decreases with aging, that in patients with sarcopenic dysphagia the tongue muscle cross‐sectional area and echo intensity area are significantly lower than in patients with non‐sarcopenic dysphagia [12], and that the US geniohyoid cross‐sectional area cut‐off value for diagnosing sarcopenic dysphagia has been identified as 172.5 mm2 in women and 194.7 mm2 in men [22]. Although swallowing muscles function as an integrated unit, each muscle plays a distinct and specialized role in swallowing; moreover, differential rates of muscle atrophy may disrupt swallow coordination. Atrophy affecting one or multiple muscles can impair swallowing function. Identifying which muscles undergo atrophic change—and at what rate—may improve the specificity of both diagnosis and treatment planning for swallowing disorders [19].

Our findings showed that coughing during meals was moderately negatively correlated with genioglossus thickness and, in univariate models, provided the highest explanatory power (R 2≈0.17). The complaint of “inability to gain weight” demonstrated weak‐to‐moderate negative associations with geniohyoid and anterior digastric thickness, whereas no clear association was observed for the mylohyoid. In the clinical literature, coughing has been described as the most frequent and reliable clinical sign suggestive of pharyngeal dysphagia in preschool children with CP, with high interobserver reproducibility, and has been reported at rates ranging from 17% to 100% across studies [4, 5]. Coughing during meals may not be discriminative on its own; however, the negative association between coughing and genioglossus thickness suggests that reduced tongue muscle thickness may be related to impaired bolus control or airway protection. This finding suggests that the limited discriminative value of coughing alone may become more clinically informative when interpreted in conjunction with genioglossus thickness. Vijayakumar et al. [23] reported that, using genioglossus electromyography (gEMG) in children with suspected dysphagia, neurogenic changes identified in this muscle were closely associated with long‐term feeding modality and strongly predicted the need for alternative feeding methods. These findings suggest that neurogenic involvement of the genioglossus may serve as a clinically meaningful indicator for determining feeding prognosis at critical decision points, such as gastrostomy placement. In our study, the same muscle was instead evaluated morphometrically using US, and the relationships between genioglossus thickness, Pedi‐EAT‐10 scores, and feeding modality were examined. The negative associations of geniohyoid and anterior digastric thickness with the complaint of “inability to gain weight” may be consistent with reduced swallowing efficiency and diminished effective intake, given the roles of these muscles in hyoid excursion. However, this symptom is multifactorial and may also be influenced by overall nutritional status, motor impairment severity, general muscle mass, caloric intake, and caregiving‐related feeding factors. Overall, these muscle‐specific patterns may reflect different symptom dimensions of swallowing difficulty: genioglossus thickness may be related to symptoms suggestive of bolus control or airway protection, whereas geniohyoid and anterior digastric thickness may be related to symptoms suggestive of feeding efficiency. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory because no instrumental swallowing assessment was performed.

The weak‐to‐moderate negative association between the masseter and coughing during meals suggests that this muscle is unlikely to be a primary marker of airway safety; rather, it may exert indirect effects through oral preparation and bolus formation. This pattern supports the notion that the masseter could represent an adjunctive target, particularly in cases where oral‐phase inefficiency is prominent; however, this interpretation should be prospectively tested in interventional studies. Nevertheless, some studies assessing swallowing muscle activity have reported insufficient supra‐ and infrahyoid muscle activation and prolonged swallowing duration in the absence of masseter activity [24]. This study draws attention to a potential role of the masseter muscle in the pathophysiology of pharyngeal dysphagia. Nonetheless, in adult head‐and‐neck cancer cohorts, preoperative geniohyoid CSA has been reported to correlate with postoperative dysphagia severity, whereas masseter CSA has been associated with indicators of oral intake and functional status [20]; this pattern conceptually supports our findings—despite methodological and population differences—that the masseter may contribute predominantly through an indirect, oral‐phase–mediated pathway.

Lagarde et al. [25] reported that, in children with spastic CP, muscle echogenicity was significantly increased on US, whereas muscle thickness was generally not discriminatory. In particular, masseter thickness was similar to that in healthy children, and only the temporalis muscle was found to be significantly thinner in children with CP. Although we did not demonstrate morphometric differences at the group level, the presence of negative correlations and significant univariate regression relationships between item‐level Pedi‐EAT‐10 symptoms and genioglossus, geniohyoid, and anterior digastric measurements suggests that structural alterations (echogenicity) and symptom‐related morphometric patterns may be captured in a complementary manner using US.

Pedi‐EAT‐10 is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing the severity of swallowing symptoms in children, and its discriminative ability for penetration/aspiration has been demonstrated in various populations [26]; therefore, it reflects a symptom‐based “safety” dimension. In contrast, FOIS captures the “efficiency” dimension by grading functional oral intake [15]. Interpreting the muscle‐specific patterns observed in this study in conjunction with Pedi‐EAT‐10 and FOIS may provide a clinically meaningful framework within a morphometry–symptom–function triad.

This study has several limitations. Because the sample was derived from a single center and included a relatively limited number of children with CP, generalizability to different CP subtypes and clinical profiles may be restricted. Swallowing symptoms were assessed using caregiver‐reported Pedi‐EAT‐10 and FOIS, and concurrent VFSS or FEES was not performed; therefore, dysphagia classification was based on symptom screening rather than instrumental confirmation, and inferences regarding airway safety and swallowing efficiency rely on indirect measures. The ultrasonographic evaluation was limited to two‐dimensional morphometric indices such as muscle thickness, and US parameters reflecting muscle quality were not included. The operator‐dependent nature of US measurements represents an additional methodological limitation. Intra‐ and inter‐rater reliability analyses were not performed. Finally, because the item‐level correlation analyses were exploratory and involved multiple comparisons, the possibility of type I error should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Conclusion

In this cross‐sectional exploratory study of children with CP, US‐measured swallowing muscle thickness did not differ according to Pedi‐EAT‐10‐defined dysphagia status. However, item‐level analyses suggested possible symptom‐specific associations between selected caregiver‐reported swallowing complaints and muscle thickness. These findings suggest that US may provide complementary, symptom‐oriented morphologic information in the clinical assessment of swallowing difficulties in children with CP, although it should not be considered a standalone diagnostic method. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of multiple testing, lack of adjustment for potential confounders, and absence of instrumental swallowing confirmation. Further large‐scale, prospective studies incorporating instrumental swallowing assessments are needed to validate these findings and clarify the clinical relevance of US‐based swallowing muscle assessment in children with CP.
