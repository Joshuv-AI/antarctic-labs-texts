Identification of key measurement points for accurate surface radiation dose assessment in cephalometric radiography
Authors: Muhammed Ilhan, Roman Menz, Carlalberta Verna, Michael M Bornstein, Dorothea C Dagassan-Berndt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe objective of this study was to evaluate variations in entrance skin dose across different anatomical regions of a head phantom and to present entrance skin dose results obtained from a limited number of measurement points. By identifying key anatomical landmarks, the study aims to rationalize the number of entrance skin dose measurement points using thermoluminescence dosimeters (TLDs).Materials and MethodsA tissue-equivalent head and neck phantom was examined with a 1-shot cephalometric X-ray machine under standardized settings. A detailed measurement protocol (140 TLDs for protocol 1) was compared to 3 alternative protocols using fewer measurement points (40 TLDs for protocol 2, 30 TLDs for protocol 3, and 6 TLDs for protocol 4).ResultsProtocol 1 yielded an average entrance skin dose of 1.042 mSv, with higher doses at the parotid gland and above the left eye corner. Protocols 2 and 3 produced similar mean doses (1.003 mSv and 1.04 mSv, respectively), demonstrating that fewer dosimeters can still generate reliable results. Protocol 4, which targeted radiosensitive sites such as the thyroid and parotid glands, provided a comparable mean entrance skin dose for the beam-facing side (1.76 mSv) relative to the other protocols.ConclusionThese findings suggest that the selected dosimetry approach is effective in assessing exposure to key tissues such as the skin. The results provide a foundation for a more representative, reproducible, and cost-effective dosimetric method in cephalometric radiography.Keywords: Cephalometry, Radiography, Radiation Dose, Skin, Radiation Exposure

Introduction

Radiographic cephalometry was introduced nearly 100 years ago and has remained a routine imaging modality in orthodontics.1 Cephalometric radiographs are used in both daily clinical practice and research. Today, conventional lateral cephalograms are standard in orthodontic diagnostics for evaluating craniofacial morphology before treatment.1,2,3,4,5 In growing patients undergoing treatment with functional appliances, as well as in complex cases requiring orthognathic surgery, an additional cephalometric radiograph may be obtained either after appliance removal or preoperatively.1,2 Cephalometric radiographs are also crucial for assessing treatment-induced and natural growth changes, accomplished by superimposing images at different stages to evaluate shifts in jaw and tooth positions.1,2 Although post-treatment cephalograms have yielded valuable insights, their routine use is discouraged unless they are part of long-term research on malocclusion or specifically indicated in severe cases.2 Consequently, children aged 5 to 7 years through adolescence may undergo up to 3 lateral cephalograms, depending on treatment requirements.6,7

Children and adolescents receiving orthodontic treatment are considerably more radiosensitive than adults due to ongoing cellular growth, organ development, and longer life expectancy, which make them 2 to 5 times more susceptible to radiation-induced carcinogenesis.2,8 The thyroid gland, in particular, is highly sensitive to radiation. Increased and repeated exposure during childhood and adolescence is strongly associated with a higher risk of thyroid carcinoma, often diagnosed in young adults in their mid to late 30s.9,10,11

Dose measurements serve as essential references for quantifying the effective dose of radiological procedures and for comparing different imaging methods. Records of effective dose determination include entrance skin dose measurements across different regions. In this context, the thyroid gland is of particular importance. As one of the most radiosensitive tissues in the head and neck, it is critical to determine radiation exposure in this area. Because the thyroid gland lies directly beneath the skin, its risk of exposure can be inferred from entrance skin dose. This is especially important given the established link between diagnostic radiation and increased thyroid cancer risk.12 In lateral cephalometric radiographs, the most common X-rays used in orthodontic treatment planning, the beam enters from the side of the skull. Variations in entrance skin dose occur depending on the measurement site. Due to the oval shape of the head, some regions, such as the ear, receive the beam first, while others, such as the nose or neck, are exposed later as they lie closer to the detector. As the beam interacts with different areas at varying energy levels, entrance skin dose measurements differ by location. To calculate the effective dose for head and neck imaging, all radiosensitive organs must be accounted for, and the morphology of the head demonstrates that dose distribution varies by region. This raises the critical question of where representative measurement sites should be located to obtain reproducible entrance skin dose data for comparing protocols. The use of large numbers of TLDs introduces substantial logistical and financial challenges, including labor-intensive handling, calibration, and readout. A smaller number of representative measurement sites would therefore offer both practical and financial advantages.

The objective of this study was to characterize the distribution of entrance skin dose in cephalometric radiographs and to determine the average entrance skin dose for the entire head. This value was then compared with measurements obtained from a limited number of selected sites, with the goal of establishing a representative and reproducible protocol that is also cost-effective.

Materials and Methods

A tissue-equivalent anthropomorphic head and neck phantom (ATOM Dosimetry Phantoms; Model 702-HN, Norfolk, VA, USA) representing an adult female was used in this study.13 The phantom is composed of tissue-equivalent epoxy materials and consists of 10 sections, each 2.5 cm thick, designed specifically for organ dose investigation (Fig. 1).

Positioning of the phantom head in the 1-shot cephalometric X-ray device. A. Anterior view. B. Lateral view.

A PaX-i 1-shot cephalometric X-ray machine (Vatech, Seoul, Korea) was employed for all radiographic measurements. Lateral cephalograms were acquired with an X-ray tube voltage of 90 kV, a tube current of 10 mA, and an exposure time of 0.7 seconds. For each experimental protocol, 10 consecutive exposures were performed, and the values reported in the text and tables represent the mean of these 10 exposures.

The phantom’s head was aligned with the midsagittal and Frankfurt horizontal planes using supportive measures to ensure consistent positioning (Fig. 1). Accurate orientation was achieved with the aid of ear rods and a nose support, stabilizing the phantom’s head relative to the X-ray machine, with the left side facing the X-ray source.

Four TLD placement protocols and corresponding exposure dose analyses were performed.

Protocol 1 (140 TLDs): In this protocol, 140 TLDs were positioned around the phantom head. Fourteen TLDs were distributed at equal intervals across the central area of each section by 1 operator (Fig. 2). The first defined point for TLD placement was the outermost ear point in section 4, on both sides of the head. By overlaying a grid across the phantom, a vertical column was established through this point in each section, and a TLD was placed in the center of each section within this column. These columns, symmetrically positioned on both sides of the head, marked the boundary between the anterior and posterior halves of the head, referred to as the “transition front/rear” (T1/T2). A second reference point was the nasal tip, located in the midsagittal plane of section 4, with TLDs positioned paramedially on both sides of the nose. Using the grid, the remaining TLDs in this column were placed on the other sections, designated “left/right side of the head front 3” (LF3/RF3). On the posterior aspect of the head, TLDs were positioned at equal distances from the midsagittal plane as their anterior counterparts, designated “left/right side of the head back 3” (LB3/RB3). At this stage, 6 TLDs had been positioned in each section, dividing the phantom head into 4 quadrants. Within each quadrant, 2 additional TLDs were placed at regular intervals between the “front/rear transitions” and the “left/right side front/back 3” positions.

Distribution of the 140 TLDs around the phantom head for protocol 1. A. Anterior view. B. Lateral view. C. Posterior view.

The TLDs were calibrated by the dosimetry department of the Swiss Accident Insurance Fund (SUVA, Luzern, Switzerland). Two unexposed dosimeters served as controls for environmental radiation. Following irradiation, all TLDs were analyzed at the SUVA dosimetry department, and the results were provided to the examiners. Results from protocol 1 were used to calculate the average dose across all measurement points. The initial set of 140 TLDs was subsequently reduced to create 2 smaller protocols in order to evaluate whether these were similarly representative. The mean values of the total surface doses from the reduced protocols were then compared with those of protocol 1.

Protocol 2 (40 TLDs): Every second column on both sides of the head was selected, starting with the columns nearest to the midsagittal plane. Additionally, every second section was chosen, beginning with section 2 (Fig. 3).

Distribution of the 40 TLDs around the phantom head for protocol 2. A. Anterior view. B. Lateral view. C. Posterior view.

Protocol 3 (30 TLDs): Every second column on both halves of the head was selected, starting with the second columns from the midsagittal plane. Every second section was also chosen, beginning with section 1 (Fig. 4).

Distribution of the 30 TLDs around the phantom head for protocol 3. A. Anterior view. B. Lateral view. C. Posterior view.

Protocol 4 (6 TLDs): Protocol 4 was developed based on dose mapping from protocols 2 and 3, which revealed values exceeding 2.0 mSv. These high-dose sites, located nearest to the primary beam path, included the ear, nasal tip, and outer eye corner. Such regions, along with radiosensitive organs like the thyroid gland, parotid gland, and eye lens, were designated as critical reference points. Protocol 4 therefore focused on 6 key measurement sites on the beam-facing side of the head. TLDs were positioned at these sensitive organs and high-exposure entry points to provide a streamlined yet representative measurement. Two TLDs were placed at each point to calculate average values (Fig. 5).

Distribution of the 6 TLDs on the phantom head for protocol 4 on the side facing the beam. A. Anterior view. B. Oblique view. C. Lateral view.

All irradiated TLDs were analyzed by the SUVA dosimetry department, and 2 unexposed dosimeters again served as controls for environmental radiation during transport.

The comparison of protocols 1, 2, and 3 with protocol 4 was based exclusively on the average doses measured on the beam-facing side of the head. Because protocol 4 included only beam-facing measurements, this approach enabled consistent evaluation focused on the regions receiving the highest exposure. In contrast, the average dose comparisons for protocols 1, 2, and 3 were calculated using measurements from both sides of the head, including the non-beam-facing side.

For each protocol, the average dose across all measurement points was calculated: (a) for both sides of the head combined (protocols 1–3), and (b) for the beam-facing side alone (protocols 1–4).

In the second step, the mean values and standard deviations of the calculated doses in (a) were determined. A Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to assess whether significant differences existed among the 3 protocols, using a 95% confidence level (CL).

In the third step, the mean values and standard deviations of the calculated doses in (b) were determined. A Kruskal-Wallis test was again conducted to examine differences among the 4 protocols, also assuming a 95% CL. Because the individual measurement points could not be assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was chosen.

For statistical evaluation, the online tool DataTab (https://datatab.de/statistik-rechner/hypothesentest/kruskal-wallis-test-rechner) was used.

The average dose across all measurement points was 1.04±0.85 mSv. Regions closest to the source consistently received higher doses than those located further away.

Doses exceeding 2.0 mSv were observed primarily in the anterior region of the left side of the head, particularly between sections 2 and 8, as well as within the transition zone between the frontal and posterior regions. The highest doses recorded were 2.7 mSv and 2.5 mSv in the parotid region (S6, LF1) and above the left corner of the eye (S3, LF2), respectively. Measurable doses of approximately 1.0 mSv were also detected on the detector-facing side, where no direct beam was present, in both the anterior and posterior facial regions, particularly between sections 1 and 9.

In protocol 2, the maximum dose of 2.6 mSv was recorded in the left temporal region (S4, LF1). In protocol 3, the highest dose of 2.4 mSv was measured in the left infraorbital region (S5, LF2) and at the left corner of the mouth (S7, LF2). The average doses for protocol 2 (1.00±0.80 mSv) and protocol 3 (1.04±0.83 mSv) closely matched the average dose from protocol 1 (1.04±0.85 mSv). In protocol 4, the highest doses were measured at the left corner of the eye (S4, LF2) and the left parotid gland region (S6, LF1), with both sites registering 2.3 mSv (Table 1). The lowest absorbed dose, 0.3 mSv, was observed in the thyroid region (S10, LF2). The average dose was calculated from the 2 TLDs placed at each measurement site.

The average mean doses for the beam-facing side were comparable across protocols 1–4: 1.71±0.66 mSv for protocol 1, 1.54±0.75 mSv for protocol 2, 1.83±0.38 mSv for protocol 3, and 1.76±0.75 mSv for protocol 4. Table 2 summarizes the average doses measured for the half of the head facing the beam. A comparison of these values across the 4 protocols revealed no statistically significant difference (P=0.761). For the comparison of protocols 1–3 (doses measured across both sides of the head combined), the Kruskal-Wallis test yielded a P-value of 0.938.

Discussion

Lateral cephalograms are low-dose radiographic examinations widely used in orthodontics, particularly for children. Despite the low individual dose (2–4 μSv),14 children’s higher radiosensitivity and the possibility of repeated exposure (up to 3 times) must be considered. Consistent dose measurements are essential for comparing devices, protocols, and studies. However, there is currently no established standard for measuring entrance skin dose at sites on the head and neck, and detailed entrance skin dose data for cephalograms have not yet been published.

To date, only limited information on entrance skin dose distribution in cephalometric radiographs is available. One study measured doses at several facial regions (e.g., nasion, lens, infraorbital skin, philtrum, neck, and thyroid), reporting the highest values near the infraorbital region and parotid gland, which are closest to the X-ray source. Visser et al.14 also recorded low doses on the contralateral side, likely attributable to scatter and exit radiation. Gijbels et al.15 briefly mentioned skin measurements, but did not provide details. Alcaraz et al.16 meanwhile, found that the parotid gland received the highest surface dose among measured regions. Unlike other dentomaxillofacial imaging methods, where exposure is localized, lateral cephalograms expose the entire skull. Due to the curved anatomy of the head, some areas are closer to the beam and consequently receive higher entrance skin doses. To address this, TLDs were evenly distributed across the head using a spherical coordinate-like system, and measurements were performed with an adult phantom to establish a reference for future protocols.

Although most lateral cephalograms are performed on children, adolescents, and young adults, an adult phantom was selected for 2 main reasons. First, the larger size of adult heads produces more distinct dose differences, thereby improving assessment accuracy. Second, adolescents and young adults (despite being more radiosensitive)17 often have head sizes similar to adults, making the adult phantom representative.

For dose assessment, each TLD was exposed to 10 standard lateral cephalograms. A single cephalogram has an effective dose of 2–4 μSv,15 and an entrance skin dose of 0.25 mGy.18 The sensitivity of the TLDs used in this study was approximately 0.1 mSv. The tenfold exposure therefore ensured that dose differences could be reliably measured and evaluated. Although repeating the measurements would have increased methodological precision, this was not performed due to cost constraints.

To derive a representative entrance skin dose, an initial uniform point distribution was implemented, followed by reduced-point protocols to evaluate deviations from full-head measurements. Consistent dose patterns across protocols confirmed that selected high-dose and average-dose regions (e.g., left eye corner, parotid gland, thyroid, nose tip) adequately represented overall surface dose, thereby enabling reproducible and meaningful dose estimation.

In principle, the effective dose can be calculated from the organ dose.19 However, because this study focused on entrance skin dose, effective dose calculations were not performed. Effective dose measurements are costly and time-consuming in some countries, limiting their practicality for routine assessment of head and neck imaging.

Table 1 illustrates how head morphology affects entrance skin dose distribution. Protocol 1, which used 140 TLDs, produced a detailed map with an average dose of 1.04 mSv. Higher doses were found in anterior regions, with the peak value (2.7 mSv) recorded in the left parotid region. The middle sections absorbed more radiation than the neck, and doses were greater on the beam-facing side due to attenuation and scatter.

Simplified protocols were developed to reduce cost and time while retaining accuracy. Analyses demonstrated that fewer TLDs could still provide representative data, particularly when the head was divided into quadrants (RB2, RF2, LF2, LB2) and approximate central points were chosen. Protocol 4 focused exclusively on the beam-facing side, targeting radiosensitive organs (thyroid, parotid gland, and lens) and prominent anatomical points (ear, left eye corner, parotid gland region) that receive the initial radiation impact,20 resulting in a total of 6 TLDs versus 140 for protocol 4.

Comparable measurements in protocol 4 showed nearly identical doses to those in protocols 2 and 3. Variations among protocols may be explained by slight differences in phantom head inclination, as positioning support was not always standardized, leading to variation in beam incidence at the measurement points. Similar findings have been reported in other studies.18,20

Despite reducing the number of TLDs from 140 to 40 and 30, the average mean doses remained stable for protocols 1–3. Table 2 demonstrates that average doses are comparable within the standard deviation across all measurement point selections. Although protocol 3’s mean value closely matched that of protocol 1, pronounced local dose variations ere evident on the beam-facing side. Protocol 4, which reduced the number of TLDs to 6, yielded results comparable to the more extensive protocols.

Table 2 shows that the average doses for all 4 protocols, considering only the beam-facing side of the head, were comparable within the standard deviation. Statistical testing confirmed that no significant differences existed among the different TLD selection methods.

The impact of protocol 4 on the calculated effective dose was also evaluated. Because the organ weighting factor for skin is 0.01 and the skin of the head accounts for only 5% of total body surface area, the uncertainty in effective dose derived from entrance skin dose is reduced by a factor of 0.0005. Accordingly, the uncertainty from entrance skin dose averages on the head is approximately 0.02%.

The precise positioning of dosimeters is essential for ensuring measurement accuracy while minimizing the number of detectors required, thereby improving both efficiency and cost-effectiveness in dosimetry studies. Because skin surface dose varies considerably across different regions of the head, random placement of measurement sites can lead to significant variability in effective dose estimation. By carefully positioning dosimeters across the head and strategically reducing their number while accounting for anatomical differences, a limited set of measurements can accurately represent the overall dose. This approach identifies the most relevant locations for lateral cephalogram dose assessment and provides a practical protocol for dose measurement.

A limitation of this study is that phantom positioning was not perfectly reproducible, which can substantially influence radiation dose. For example, a 10° rotation of the phantom has been shown to increase thyroid dose by as much as 92%, underscoring the importance of consistent positioning to prevent skewed results.21 In this study, positioning was based on clinical principles, specifically aligning the mid-sagittal plane and the Frankfurt horizontal plane of the phantom head.

Given that cephalometric radiographs deliver a relatively low dose, one might question the necessity of such detailed dose evaluation. However, although literature suggests potential cost savings from simplified approaches, it remains impossible to quantify the long-term return on investment associated with improved health outcomes.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that 6 carefully selected measurement points are sufficient to provide a representative estimate of average skin dose in cephalometric lateral radiographs performed on adult heads. Simplified dosimetric protocols can therefore yield reliable and reproducible results. Protocol 4, which employed 6 TLDs placed at critical anatomical sites, offered a practical and cost-effective method for surface dose measurement while maintaining accuracy comparable to more comprehensive protocols.22
