Integrative genomic profiling of iron homeostasis predicts clinical outcomes and identifies HAMP/hepcidin as a therapeutic target in colorectal cancer

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common and fatal cancers worldwide, often diagnosed at advanced stages. Iron homeostasis, a crucial metabolic pathway in cancer, significantly impacts tumor progression.ResultsThis study systematically investigated the function and significance of iron homeostasis-related genes (IHGs) in CRC, leading to the establishment of iron homeostasis-related molecular subtypes (IHMS) and a prognostic model based on their expression profiles of IHGs. Both IHMS and the model showed strong predictive accuracy for prognosis, clinical parameters, and response to immunotherapy. Patients classified as IHMS-B or exhibiting high model risk scores had poorer prognoses, while those categorized as IHMS-C or with low model risk scores were associated with more favorable outcomes and increased sensitivity to immunotherapy across multiple datasets. The model key gene HAMP (encoding hepcidin) was identified as a key oncogenic regulator that promotes CRC progression; elevated levels of HAMP/hepcidin were linked to peritoneal metastasis. Knocking out HAMP resulted in reduced tumor burden and inhibited proliferation and cancer stemness in both in vivo and in vitro models. Additionally, integration of in-house bulk-seq and tissue-array data further substantiated the clinical significance of HAMP/hepcidin and its association with immune landscapes.ConclusionsIn conclusion, both IHMS and the model serve as effective predictors for CRC prognosis and patient stratification. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies globally, arises and progresses through multifaceted mechanisms [1]. Iron is an essential trace element involved in multiple physiological processes, including DNA synthesis, energy metabolism, and cellular proliferation. Dysregulated iron metabolism, however, can drive tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as the induction of oxidative stress, DNA damage, and modulation of the immune microenvironment [2, 3]. Emerging evidence highlights the critical involvement of iron homeostasis in CRC pathogenesis, positioning it as a potential biomarker for early detection, therapeutic response prediction, and risk stratification [4–7]. Systemic iron homeostasis is tightly regulated through coordinated interactions among key molecules such as hepcidin (HAMP), transferrin receptor (TFRC), ferritin, and divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1). Current research demonstrates that dysregulation of these iron regulatory components in CRC tissues correlates with enhanced tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. These alterations drive metabolic reprogramming and iron recycling within the tumor microenvironment [8–11]. Building upon these insights, in-depth exploration of iron homeostasis-related molecules in CRC will not only advance our understanding of its molecular pathology but also hold promise for identifying novel biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and precision therapeutic targets.

With continuous advancements in bioinformatics technologies and machine learning algorithms, an increasing wealth of genomic and biological data is being leveraged in fundamental and clinical oncology research. Recent developments in clinical predictive models have significantly enhanced early diagnosis, disease stratification, prognostic prediction, and treatment efficacy evaluation in CRC [12–14]. Systematic integration and evaluation of iron homeostasis-related molecules in CRC—particularly the construction of clinical predictive models based on core molecular features—hold substantial value for establishing early diagnostic criteria, assessing patient prognosis, and developing novel therapeutic strategies. Not only does this approach deepen our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC pathogenesis, but it also provides clinicians with iron homeostasis-based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, enabling more precise prognostic assessments and personalized treatment for patients. These efforts offer critical theoretical and empirical foundations for reducing CRC-related mortality.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

To ensure a robust and clinically relevant dataset for our analysis, we systematically retrieved CRC-related datasets from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. For TCGA, complete transcriptomic sequencing data and matched clinicopathological information for colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) patients were downloaded from the TCGA portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). For GEO datasets, we utilized the R package ‘GEOquery’ to retrieve datasets, strictly adhering to the following criteria: only datasets generated from the unified Affymetrix® GPL570 platform containing complete overall survival (OS) information were included, each requiring a total patient number exceeding 100 cases, and critically, only datasets in which no patients had received neoadjuvant therapy were included. Based on these stringent selection parameters, we identified and retrieved three qualifying datasets: GSE17538, GSE38832, and GSE39582. Furthermore, complete RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinical characteristics for the IMvigor210 cohort [15] (a bladder cancer cohort treated with anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy), the Chan cohort [16] (a melanoma cohort treated with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy), and the Schadendorf cohort [17] (another melanoma cohort treated with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy) were also obtained from their respective sources to evaluate the model’s predictive utility for immunotherapy response. For all cohorts considered in this study, patients with an OS of less than one month were excluded to focus on more clinically relevant long-term outcomes and avoid early, potentially unrelated mortality. Transcriptomic data preprocessing included conversion of TCGA RNA sequencing fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM) values to transcripts per million (TPM), followed by log2 transformation. Similarly, RNA-seq count data from the IMvigor210 cohort were normalized to TPM and log2-transformed. The TCGA-CRC cohort was constructed by merging TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ datasets. Batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm, after which the TCGA-CRC dataset was combined with GEO datasets to generate a unified meta-cohort for downstream analyses.

Screening and functional analysis of iron homeostasis-related genes (IHGs)

Using the Gene Ontology database and searching for the keyword “Iron”, iron homeostasis-related pathways were identified, and the genes associated with these pathways were summarized. The “clusterProfiler” package in R was utilized to perform Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis on these IHGs.

Screening of prognosis-related genes and differentially expressed IHGs

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed using overall survival (OS) data from the TCGA-CRC cohort to identify prognosis-associated genes within the IHGs (P < 0.05). Differential expression analysis between tumor samples and normal controls in the TCGA-CRC dataset was conducted using three distinct algorithms: DESeq2, edgeR, and limma (voom), to obtain the relevant differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Construction and evaluation of iron homeostasis-related molecular subtypes (IHMS) in bulk and single-cell sequencing cohorts

To analyze the scRNA-seq data from dataset GSE132465, we initiated our process by integrating all samples obtained from multiple patients. This comprehensive multimodal analysis was performed using the ‘Seurat’ R package [18]. For quality control, we first filtered out genes expressed in fewer than three cells. We also excluded cells that either expressed fewer than 200 genes or more than 6000 genes to remove low-quality and potential doublet cells. Furthermore, cells where the mitochondrial gene proportion exceeded 25% were discarded. To account for cell cycle effects, we scored cells for S phase and G2M phase, subsequently calculating their predicted cell cycle states. Following these stringent filtration steps, a total of 33,694 cells were retained for all subsequent analyses. To classify single cells into distinct subsets, we followed these steps: the selection of variable genes using variance stabilizing transformation (VST). We utilized the ‘harmony’ R package to address and minimize batch effects that might exist among different samples [19]. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to the scaled data of the identified highly variable genes. The top 30 principal components (PCs) derived from this analysis were then employed for both cell clustering and subsequent subtype classification using non-linear dimensionality reduction, specifically t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Cell clusters were precisely defined by implementing a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity optimization-based clustering algorithm, with the optimal resolution determined empirically. This involved using Seurat’s ‘FindClusters’ function, which computes k-nearest neighbors and constructs the SNN graph. The original Louvain algorithm (specified as algorithm = 1) was employed for the modularity optimization process. To visually represent the expression patterns of various known marker genes and the 33 prognostic IHDEGs across all identified clusters, we leveraged Seurat’s ‘AddModuleScore’, ‘FeaturePlot’, and ‘VlnPlot’ functionalities. Finally, the “epithelial cells” population within the CRC tissues underwent a secondary round of re-clustering and re-analysis at a higher resolution.

Unsupervised consensus clustering of the TCGA-CRC cohort and epithelial cells in GSE132465 cohort, were performed as previously described [20]. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) were applied to observe the overall distribution of the Iron Homeostasis-related Molecular Subtypes (IHMS) identified in both bulk and single-cell contexts. The “GSVA” R package was used to quantify functional pathways in epithelial cells, and differential pathway analysis among clusters was performed using “limma”. In the TCGA-CRC cohort, OS for different subtypes was assessed by Kaplan–Meier analysis (“survival” and “survminer”). GO pathway enrichment analysis for inter-subtype DEGs was performed as previously detailed. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated with “maftools”, and microsatellite instability (MSI) data were derived from Firebrowse. Immune cell infiltration was estimated using QUANTISEQ, TIMER, and XCELL algorithms, while immune/stromal/ESTIMATE scores and tumor purity were calculated via “estimate”. Immune checkpoint markers were collected from published literature. Predicted responses to CTLA-4 and PD-1 inhibitors were retrieved from TCIA. Plots were generated with “ggplot2”.

Construction of iron homeostasis-related prognostic signature (IHPS) based on machine learning algorithm

To develop a robust iron homeostasis-associated predictive model with high accuracy and stability, we systematically evaluated 101 combination approaches derived from 10 machine learning algorithms: CoxBoost, Elastic Net (Enet), Generalized Boosted Regression Modeling (GBM), Lasso regression, Partial Least Squares Regression for Cox models (plsRcox), Random Survival Forest (RSF), Ridge regression, Stepwise Cox regression, Supervised Principal Component Analysis (SuperPC), and Survival Support Vector Machine (survival-SVM). These methods were applied to 33 prognosis-related DEGs associated with iron homeostasis using a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework in the TCGA-CRC cohort to construct predictive models. Model performance was rigorously assessed in both the training cohort and all validation cohorts (GSE17538, GSE38832, GSE39582, and Meta-cohort). Each model was evaluated based on Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) across training and validation sets, and the model with the highest average C-index of the validation set was selected as the optimal model (IHPS). Evaluation and comparison of the predictive effectiveness of IHPS

The predictive performance of the iron homeostasis model was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values via the R package “survivalROC”. Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis was applied to assess the prognostic predictive capacity of previously reported CRC gene signatures across all cohorts. The C-index of different prognostic signatures was compared using the R package “CompareC”.

Construction of nomogram

In the TCGA-CRC cohort, univariate and multivarable Cox regression analyses were conducted using OS. A nomogram was constructed based on the results of the multivariate analysis to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for CRC patients. Calibration curves corresponding to the results of the multivariate Cox regression analysis were plotted using the “rms” package in R. Time-dependent ROC analysis and corresponding AUC values were generated as previously described.

Investigation of molecular biology and immune microenvironment variations in IHPS high and low-risk groups

DEGs between high- and low-risk groups of IHPS (adjusted P < 0.05, |logFC|> 1, filtered using the limma R package) were screened using the ‘limma’ package in R and used for GO and KEGG analyses, as previously described. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea) was employed to identify significantly enriched pathways related to DEGs. Immune cell infiltration levels and immune checkpoint markers expression in the TCGA-CRC cohort were analyzed using the methods outlined earlier. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained pathological images for the TCGA-CRC cohort were obtained from the Cancer Digital Slide Archive (CDSA, https://cancer.digitalslidearchive.org/). Heatmaps and boxplots were visualized using the R package “ggplot2”.

Genomic somatic mutation analysis

Somatic mutation and copy number variation data for the TCGA-CRC cohort were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The R package “maftools” was utilized to analyze and visualize somatic mutation data from files across high- and low-risk groups. The R package “maftools” was used to analyze and visualize the somatic mutation data for high- and low-risk groups.

Prediction of immunotherapy response

The predictive sensitivity scores for CTLA-4 and PD-1 inhibitor responses in the TCGA-CRC cohort were derived as previously described. To further evaluate the predictive value of iron homeostasis for anti-PD-L1, anti-PD-1, and anti-CTLA-4 therapies, the IHPS was applied to the IMvigor210, Chan, and Schadendorf immunotherapy cohorts. Patients exhibiting stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) were classified as non-responders, while those achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) were categorized as responders. Visualization of violin plots and stacked bar plots was performed using the R package “ggplot2”.

Prediction of potential drugs in high and low risk groups Prediction of potential drugs for IHPS high and low-risk groups

Expression profiles of human cancer cell lines (CCLs) were obtained from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE). Using the R package “clusterProfiler”, co-clustering was performed on CCLs following the previously described methodology. GO pathway enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted on the DEGs identified between different subtypes. Drug sensitivity data for CCLs were sourced from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP, https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp) and PRISM Repurposing Dataset (https://depmap.org/portal/prism/). Drug sensitivity was quantified using the area under the dose–response curve (AUC), with lower AUC values indicating higher drug sensitivity. To improve the accuracy of drug response prediction and eliminate confounding transcriptional signals from non-tumor components, the ISOpure algorithm was employed to generate a purified tumor expression matrix for the TCGA-CRC cohort, using non-tumor expression profiles as reference. Subsequently, the R package “pRRophetic” and its embedded ridge regression model were applied to predict drug responses in clinical samples based on the purified expression profiles, thereby estimating AUC values for each compound in TCGA samples. For cell line drug sensitivity analysis, estimated AUC values and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) data were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). A nearest centroid classifier was developed using the R package “lolR” to predict risk group classifications for CRC cell lines based on key gene expression patterns from TCGA-CRC. Prior to comparative drug sensitivity analysis, missing AUC values were imputed via k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) imputation, and compounds with > 10% missing data were excluded.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human colonic epithelial cell line CCD841CoN and colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines T84, COLO205, and NCI-H508 were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Additional CRC lines (HCT116, SW1116, HT29, SW1463, SW480, SW620, LOVO) were sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere in appropriate media (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 5–10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma, St. Louis, MO), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China). SW480 and SW620 cells were maintained under standard conditions excluding the specified CO2 requirement.

Construction of stable HAMP-overexpressing and -knockdown CRC cell lines

HAMP overexpression and knockdown constructs were generated using the pGMLV-CMV-Puro vector (Genomeditech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). To establish stable cell lines, cells were plated in 6-well plates 24 h before lentiviral infection. The lentiviruses were then added to the wells with 2 ml of complete culture medium. After 24 h of incubation, the viral medium was removed and replaced with fresh complete culture medium.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, APExBIO, Houston, USA). Different CRC cell lines were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution and 90 µL of culture medium were added to each well. Following an additional 2 h of incubation under the same conditions, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm as previously described [21].

Clone formation

Cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 800–1000 cells per well and incubated for 10–14 days. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.25% crystal violet. Colony formation was evaluated under a light microscope, and colonies were manually counted in a blinded manner from at least three random fields [22].

Ethynyldeoxyuridine (EdU) staining

Cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. EdU (Meilunbio, Dalian, China) was then added to a final concentration of 5 µM, followed by an additional 2 h of incubation. Subsequent procedures were performed according to standard protocols in a light-protected environment. Fluorescent images were captured using a Bio-Rad fluorescent cell imager (Hercules, USA), with analysis conducted on at least three random fields per well.

Wound healing assay

NCI-H508 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until approximately 90% confluence was reached. A uniform linear scratch was generated across the cell monolayer using a sterile 100-μL pipette tip. Detached cells were gently removed by washing twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fresh serum-free medium was added. Images of the wound area were captured at 6 h and 48 h after scratching using an inverted microscope [23].

Transwell assay

Cell migration assays were performed following established protocols [21]. For invasion assays, 3 × 105 NCI-H508 cells were resuspended in 120 μL of serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (8-μm pore size, 24-well format; Corning Costar, NY, USA). All other experimental conditions and procedures were consistent with those applied in the migration assays.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence assays were conducted as previously outlined. Cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for an additional 30 min. After permeabilization, cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by one hour of incubation with species-specific secondary antibodies at room temperature. Confocal images were captured using a ZEISS LSM 880 confocal microscope with Airyscan (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Details of the antibodies used are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed following standard protocols. Optimal tissue sections were prepared through deparaffinization. IHC analysis employed the specified antibodies (Supplementary Table 1). Scoring criteria for IHC staining followed previously established methods, as detailed in our earlier reports [22].

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

RNA isolation and real-time PCR were conducted in accordance with established methods [20]. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2. β-actin was employed as the internal control for normalizing mRNA levels, and relative RNA expression was calculated using the 2 −ΔΔCT method.

Western bolt

Western blotting was conducted following previously established methods [24]. The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Animal experiments

Animal study-1#: generation of Hamp knockout mouse model

Hamp knockout mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. Frameshift mutations were introduced into the Hamp coding region through non-homologous end joining, resulting in functional disruption of the gene (Shanghai Southern Model Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Cas9 mRNA and guide RNA (gRNA) were synthesized by in vitro transcription and microinjected into fertilized C57BL/6 J mouse zygotes to generate F0 founder mice (gRNA1: CAGCTTCTCCCCAGAGATTTAGG; gRNA2: GGAATAAATAATGGGGTTGAGGG). Genomic DNA from F0 mice was subjected to PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing to identify mutant alleles (Forward primer sequence: GGCACTCAAGGCAATCACAAGAC; Reverse primer sequence: CAGCGTTCCAGCAGCCTCAT). Confirmed F0 founders were crossed with wild-type C57BL/6 J mice to obtain F1 heterozygous offspring, which were used for subsequent animal experiments (schematically illustrated in Fig. S10A).

Animal study-2#: a mouse model of AOM/DSS induced colitis-associated cancer

Male C57BL/6J Hamp-KO and Hamp-WT mice (2 months of age, 18–20 g, specific pathogen-free) were obtained from Shanghai Southern Model Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed under standard controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All mice (n = 6 per group) received a single intraperitoneal injection of azoxymethane (AOM; A5486, Sigma, USA) at a dose of 10 mg/kg. One week later, mice were administered 2.5% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; MB5535, Meilunbio, China) in the drinking water for 7 days, followed by 2 weeks of regular water. This treatment cycle was repeated three times. Body weight was monitored weekly throughout the experiment. At week 11, mice were euthanized and subjected to necropsy. The colon was excised, its length measured, and tumor numbers were recorded. Colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical analyses, including H&E, IHC, and IF staining (schematically illustrated in Fig. 6F).

Animal study-3#: subcutaneous xenograft models in nude mice

Male BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks of age, 16–18 g, specific pathogen-free) were purchased from Shanghai Southern Model Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed under standard controlled conditions. CRC xenograft mouse models were established by subcutaneously injecting 5 × 106 HCT116 or HT29 (HAMP-overexpressing or wild-type) cells in 0.1 ml PBS into the right axillary region. Tumor growth was monitored every 3 days using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated according to the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached the ethical endpoint or at the predetermined experimental endpoint. Tumors were excised, weighed, and either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological and immunohistochemical analyses or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent molecular analyses (schematically illustrated in Fig. 6A).

In-house cohort collection and preprocessing

High-throughput transcriptome analysis of CRC individuals

The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ruijin Hospital (clinical trial registration: NCT04714814). From a prospective cohort, we rigorously screened 64 treatment‑naïve CRC individuals for bulk RNA sequencing, collectively designated as the RJ-BK-Cohort [23]. Total RNA was extracted from surgically discarded paired normal and tumor colorectal tissues from these individuals. cDNA libraries were prepared and subjected to high-throughput sequencing at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China) using the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

Tissue microarray–based IHC of CRC individuals

A colorectal cancer tissue microarray (TMA) containing 42 matched pairs of normal and tumor tissues was constructed at Ruijin Hospital and referred to as the RJ‑TMA‑Cohort [25]. Immunohistochemistry was conducted according to established procedures. After selecting optimal sections, slides were deparaffinized and prepared for antigen detection. Each array was reviewed independently by three pathologists applying a semi‑quantitative evaluation based on the German semi‑quantitative scoring system.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were heated at 60 °C for deparaffinization and subsequently processed through xylene and decreasing concentrations of ethanol. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. After antigen retrieval, sections were washed and incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) to minimize nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies against CK19 (Abcam, ab52625, 2 μg mL⁻1; mouse and human), hepcidin (Abcam, ab190775, 1:100; mouse), hepcidin (Abcam, ab30760, 10 μg mL⁻1; human), α-SMA (Abcam, ab124964, 1:250; mouse and human), CD3 (Abcam, ab16669, 1:150; mouse and human), CD20 (Abcam, ab64088, 1:100; mouse and human), and CD68 (Abcam, ab303565, 1:100; mouse and human) were applied sequentially and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Sections were then incubated with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies, mounted, and visualized by fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All data processing, statistical analyses, and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.1.1). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was employed to assess associations between continuous variables, while categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. Differences in continuous variables across groups were evaluated via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Definition, biological functions, and differential expression of iron homeostasis-related genes (IHGs) in CRC

A comprehensive search of the Gene Ontology (GO) database and current literature identified 317 genes involved in iron homeostasis, based on 24 iron-associated pathways. GO, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses revealed these genes are mainly involved in iron ion homeostasis, oxidative stress response, cellular metabolism, regulated cell death, and immune modulation (Fig. S1A–B). To characterize their role in colorectal cancer (CRC), transcriptomic profiles of CRC tumors and normal tissues from the TCGA dataset were analyzed using DESeq2, edgeR, and limma (voom), yielding highly consistent results (Fig. S1C). Cross-referencing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with the iron homeostasis gene set, we identified 215 iron homeostasis-related DEGs (IHDEGs) with tumor-specific expression alterations in CRC (Fig. S1D).

Expression patterns and molecular subtypes of IHGs are associated with prognosis and molecular characteristics in CRC patients

Previous studies have shown that prognostic prediction, treatment efficacy, and therapeutic strategies vary significantly across CRC molecular subtypes. Among these, Consensus Molecular Subtypes (CMS) and Microsatellite Instability (MSI) are well-established classifications with proven clinical value. Therefore, identifying new molecular subtypes could further enhance individualized management for CRC patients. The analytical process for iron-homeostasis subtypes classification is summarized in the workflow diagram (Fig. 1A). Using OS data from the TCGA-CRC cohort, univariate Cox regression of 317 iron homeostasis-related genes identified 36 prognosis-related genes (PRGs). Among these, 33 genes overlapped with IHDEGs (Fig. 1B): MYC, PPAT, RRM2, CYP2S1, BRIP1, SLC11A1, HSPA9, CYP39A1, ATP6AP1, P4HA1, NDOR1, HMOX2, HAMP, CYP19A1, ASIC3, CYP26A1, CISD2, BOLA3, CYP4A22, SRI, PTGIS, CYP11A1, UQCRFS1, ALAD, FBXL5, SDHB, SFXN1, CLTC, AGMO, SLC39A8, ACO1, CCDC115, and ALOX12. Based on the expression profiles of these 33 prognostic IHDEGs, consensus clustering and unsupervised learning stratified CRC patients into three distinct molecular subtypes: IHMS-A, IHMS-B, and IHMS-C (Fig. 1C–D). Notably, IHMS-B patients exhibited significantly poorer prognosis, while IHMS-C showed the most favorable outcomes (Fig. 1E). GO pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these subtypes are mainly characterized by differences in cellular metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, immune responses, cell adhesion, and programmed cell death pathways (Fig. 1F). Given that tumor mutation burden (TMB), MSI, and immune cell infiltration levels are closely related to the clinical prognosis and efficacy of immunotherapy in CRC patients, this study further explored the relationship between iron homeostasis-related subtypes and immune features. Results demonstrated that IHMS-C showed the highest proportion of MSI-H cases among the three subtypes (Fig. S2C), while also exhibited significantly higher TMB levels compared to Classes A and B (Fig. S2D). Using QUANTISEQ, TIMER, and XCELL algorithms, immune cell infiltration levels were evaluated based on RNA sequencing data from the TCGA-CRC cohort. Correlations between iron homeostasis subtypes and immune checkpoint-related mRNA expression were also analyzed (Fig. S2A). IHMS-B displayed markedly increased infiltration of multiple immune cell types, including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, myeloid dendritic cells, and macrophages. Furthermore, the mRNA expression levels of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, immune stimulators, immune stimulator receptors, immune suppressors, and immune suppressor receptors were also found to be elevated in Subtype B (Fig. S2B). ESTIMATE analysis revealed higher immune scores, higher stromal scores, and lower tumor purity scores in IHMS-B. Further comparison of immunotherapy responses indicated that IHMS-B had significantly lower predicted treatment sensitivity scores for both CTLA4 and PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy and combination regimens compared to Classes A and C in the TCGA-CRC cohort (Fig. S2E). Combining these findings with the results from the previous paragraph provides evidence that iron homeostasis subtypes can significantly distinguish CRC patients based on clinical prognosis, molecular characteristics, and immunotherapy responsiveness.

Single-cell Transcriptome Analysis of CRC Cells Based on IHMS

To address the heterogeneity caused by diverse cell populations in bulk tissue analyses, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing analysis using the GSE132465 dataset. Our analysis commenced with 33,694 high-quality single cells, meticulously chosen after a thorough quality control process. Employing graph-based clustering on the merged and normalized cell populations, we successfully resolved distinct cellular clusters. Each cluster was characterized by the presence of specific canonical markers, including CD79A+ for B cells, EPCAM+ for epithelial cells, KIT+ for mast cells, CD68+ for myeloid cells, DCN+ for fibroblasts, and CD3D+ for T cells (Figs. 1G and S3A–B). Across the different cell clusters, we examined the expression patterns of 33 prognostic IHDEGs related to iron homeostasis, noting an elevated expression within epithelial cells compared to other cell types (Fig. 1H). To further assess the role of iron homeostasis subtypes specifically within epithelial cells, 17,469 epithelial CRC cells were subsequently extracted from the single-cell dataset, and three distinct iron homeostasis-related subtypes (scIHMS-A/B/C) were similarly identified through an unsupervised clustering approach (Fig. 1I–J). Echoing the results from bulk-level enrichment, GO and KEGG analyses revealed that these three subtypes also exhibit biological differences mainly in pathways related to cellular metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, intercellular adhesion, and regulated cell death (Fig. S3C). GSVA analysis demonstrated that epithelial cells in scIHMS-B had higher scores for stemness-associated pathways, epithelial cell proliferation pathways, and fibroblast activation pathways (Fig. 1K). Stacked bar charts were used to further examine the differences in molecular and clinical feature distributions among the three subtypes (Fig. 1L). Clinically, the majority of epithelial cells in scIHMS-B were sourced from patients with TNM stage III & IV disease, right-sided colon cancer, and those with poorly differentiated or mucinous CRC. Regarding CMS, scIHMS-A tumor cells primarily originated from CMS2 CRC patients at both single-cell and bulk levels, which is associated with more favorable prognoses. In terms of MSI state, tumor cells in scIHMS-A showed the highest proportion of the MSS subtype, suggesting a greater likelihood of benefiting from immunotherapy. For gene mutation type, scIHMS-A cells had the highest levels of TP53 and APC mutations, while scIHMS-B cells had the highest levels of KRAS mutations. The findings above demonstrate that iron homeostasis-related subtypes exist specifically within CRC epithelial cells, each displaying unique molecular and clinical characteristics with potential prognostic and therapeutic significance.

Construction of the iron homeostasis-related prognostic signature (IHPS) using a comprehensive approach based on 101 machine learning combinations

Iron homeostasis molecular subtypes can to some extent distinguish clinical prognosis and molecular characteristics in patients; however, there remains a challenge in accurately quantifying iron homeostasis-related gene expression levels to obtain an individualized quantitative indicator. To address this, we developed a novel iron homeostasis predictive model using machine learning algorithms based on the 33 iron homeostasis-related prognostic DEGs. Within the TCGA-CRC cohort, 101 prediction models were evaluated under a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework, with C-indices calculated across all cohorts (TCGA-CRC, GSE17538, GSE39582, GSE38832, and the meta-cohort) (Fig. 2A–B). The CoxBoost algorithm achieved the highest mean C-index (0.630) across validation cohorts, thus being defined as the optimal model-building algorithm for subsequent analyses (Fig. 2C). The iron homeostasis risk model (IHPS) built on CoxBoost algorithm selected 21 genes (HAMP, HMOX2, MYC, CYP39A1, CCDC115, CYP26A1, SDHB, ACO1, SLC39A8, CLTC, ALAD, SRI, CYP19A1, ALOX12, ATP6AP1, CYP11A1, P4HA1, NDOR1, CYP2S1, FBXL5, BRIP1) to calculate an iron homeostasis-related risk score for each patient. Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups by median risk score. Survival analysis revealed escalating mortality with increasing risk scores (Fig. 2D). High-risk patients exhibited significantly worse OS than low-risk patients in the TCGA-CRC training cohort and all independent validation cohorts (Fig. 2E–I), confirming the prognostic predictive capacity of the model.

Evaluation and comparison of predictive performance of IHPS

To comprehensively evaluate the predictive accuracy of IHPS, we conducted time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Results demonstrated area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.709 (1-year), 0.769 (3-years), and 0.775 (5-years) in the TCGA-CRC cohort, indicating the model performed well in short-term and mid-term prognostic predictions. The GSE17538 cohort showed slightly lower yet clinically informative AUC values (0.606, 0.617, and 0.680). Additional validation in the GSE38832 (AUC: 0.727, 0.709, 0.631) and GSE39582 (AUC: 0.645, 0.585, 0.596) cohorts confirmed the robustness of the model. In the Meta-Cohort, the AUC values were 0.669, 0.651, and 0.653, demonstrating consistent predictive capacity (Fig. S4B). The C-index along with the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all cohorts were as follows: TCGA-CRC = 0.732 [0.679–0.785], GSE17538 = 0.598 [0.539–0.658], GSE38832 = 0.705 [0.591–0.819], GSE39582 = 0.575 [0.530–0.619], and Meta-cohort = 0.641 [0.612–0.669] (Fig. S4A). To further explore the prognostic predictive ability of IHPS, it was compared with 69 other CRC prognostic prediction signatures developed based on machine learning that have been reported so far. The results showed that the C-index of IHPS ranked consistently among the top across all cohorts, demonstrating its superior performance (Fig. S4C). Furthermore, univariate Cox regression analysis further validated the clinical relevance of the model, with results indicating that only IHPS was significantly associated with prognosis across all five cohorts (Fig. S4D). In summary, this study confirms the value of IHPS in clinical application and prognostic prediction, and it holds promise for providing new perspectives for future precision therapy.

IHPS risk score as an independent prognostic factor and development of a high-predictive performance nomogram

To determine the independent prognostic value of the IHPS risk score and other clinical characteristics, univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed. Univariate analysis identified significant associations between OS and the following factors in the TCGA-CRC cohort: IHPS risk score, AJCC stage (P < 0.001; HR = 2.23), N stage (N1: P = 0.005, HR = 1.97; N2: P < 0.001, HR = 4.13), and M stage (M1: P < 0.001, HR = 4.41) (Fig. S5A). Multivariable analysis confirmed the risk score (P < 0.001; HR = 2.73) and AJCC stage as independent prognostic factors (Fig. S5B). Subsequently, integrating AJCC stage, N stage, M stage, and risk score, a nomogram prediction model was developed for the TCGA-CRC cohort to predict 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival probabilities (Fig. S5C). Calibration curves demonstrated that nomogram had high predictive performance at these time points. Moreover, compared to the risk score alone, the nomogram exhibited higher AUC scores in predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival outcomes, demonstrating superior discriminatory capacity (Fig. S5D). Overall, these findings underscore that IHPS has an independent role in predicting survival beyond the effects of various clinical factors and can be integrated with other clinical characteristics to develop a more efficient predictive model. To further explore molecular biological differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups of IHPS, GO, KEGG, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed based on DEGs between the two groups. Notably, pathways related to the cell cycle, such as mitotic nuclear division and cell cycle checkpoint signaling, were significantly enriched in the low-risk group. Additionally, pathways related to oxidative stress response, apoptosis, ion metabolism, and the p53 signaling pathway also showed important statistical differences (Fig. S5E–F).

Significant differences in somatic mutation profiles and immune characteristics between IHPS high-risk and low-risk groups

To investigate the relationship between iron homeostasis and individual genetic mutations, we systematically analyzed single nucleotide variation (SNV) data from the TCGA-CRC cohort. In the low-risk group, APC (76%), TP53 (54%), KRAS (44%), TTN (44%), and PIK3CA (28%) exhibited the highest mutation frequencies (Fig. S6A, C), all of which are critically implicated in CRC pathogenesis. APC mutations drive early carcinogenesis, while TP53 and KRAS alterations correlate with aggressive progression and poor prognosis. The high-risk group demonstrated more pronounced mutational heterogeneity, with APC remaining the most frequently mutated gene (71%), followed by elevated TP53 mutation rates (63%). TTN (49%), KRAS (42%), and SYNE1 (29%) showed high mutation frequencies (Fig. S6B, D), suggesting enhanced dysregulation of cell cycle control and signaling pathways. Specifically, TP53 showed a significantly high mutation rate in both groups, their mutation frequency was significantly higher in the high-risk groups (Fig. S6E, F). Additionally, the forest plot clearly depicted the differences in the mutation profiles (Fig. S6G). In summary, these findings confirm that the mutation rate of classical CRC pathogenic genes in high-risk group patients is significantly higher than that in low-risk group patients.

Further analyses employing the TIMER, XCELL, and QUANTISEQ algorithms were conducted to evaluate immune cell infiltration levels using RNA-seq data from the TCGA-CRC cohort. Additionally, correlations between the iron homeostasis risk score and immune checkpoint-related mRNA expression levels were investigated. Results demonstrated that higher IHPS risk score were associated with increased infiltration of multiple immune cell types, including B cells, T cells, neutrophils, and macrophages. Furthermore, expression levels of MHC molecules, immunostimulators, immunostimulator receptors, immunosuppressors, and immunosuppressor receptors showed positive correlations with the IHPS risk score. ESTIMATE analysis revealed higher immune scores, stromal scores, and lower tumor purity in the high-risk group (Figs. 3B and S7A–B). To validate the relationship between immune infiltration and iron homeostasis, histopathological features of samples with varying risk scores were assessed using H&E staining data from the TCGA-CRC cohort. Tissue sections with higher risk scores exhibited elevated immune cell infiltration (Fig. 3A, C). Collectively, these findings indicate that IHPS risk score effectively discriminates immune characteristics among CRC individuals.

IHPS exhibits superior predictive ability for immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity

Given the capacity of IHPS in distinguishing immune and mutational characteristics in CRC patients, we further investigated its predictive efficacy for immunotherapy outcomes. Analysis of immunotherapy prediction scores in the TCGA-CRC cohort revealed that high-risk patients exhibited inferior predicted immunotherapy responses (Fig. 3D–F). Applying IHPS to public immunotherapy datasets revealed similar results. Patients with low-risk scores in the IMvigor210 cohort had significantly better OS compared to those with high-risk scores (Fig. 3G), while patients with higher risk scores generally had poorer clinical outcomes in immunotherapy (Fig. 3H). Notably, samples with immune-desert phenotypes displayed significantly higher risk scores than those with immune-inflamed or immune-excluded phenotypes (Fig. 3I). Further evaluation of correlations between risk scores and tumor immune microenvironment features revealed negative associations between risk scores and PD-L1 expression levels in both tumor-infiltrating immune cells (ICs) and tumor cells (TCs) (Fig. 3J). Validation in the Chan and Schadendorf immunotherapy cohorts also confirmed worse prognosis and treatment outcomes in high-risk patients (Fig. S7C–F).

Given the distinct immunotherapy responses between iron homeostasis risk groups, we further investigated the difference in drug sensitivities through human cancer cell lines. Unsupervised clustering was performed on these human cancer cell lines, which similarly identified three distinct iron homeostasis-related subtypes (clIHMS-A/B/C) (Fig. S8A). GO analysis showed that these three subtypes also exhibited biological differences in pathways related to cell cycle, cellular metabolism, oxidative stress, cell‒cell adhesion, and immune regulation (Fig. S8B). The differential pathways identified among different subtypes were largely consistent with findings obtained from single-cell and bulk-seq sub clustering. These findings indicate that our cellular models exhibit similar iron homeostasis-related heterogeneity as observed in clinical cohorts, thus providing a relevant biological context for our subsequent drug sensitivity analyses.

Subsequently, drug resistance characteristics of human cancer cell lines were analyzed using the CTRP and PRISM datasets. After removing duplicates and incomplete data, 355 compounds from CTRP and 1286 compounds from PRISM were selected for analysis (Fig. 3K). Using a ridge regression model, we predicted AUC values for TCGA-CRC samples based on CRC cell line AUC values and expression data from CRC cell lines, as well as the purified tumor expression profiles from the TCGA-CRC cohort. We then selected the top 20% and bottom 20% of samples based on risk scores and compared their AUC values, identifying compounds with significantly higher predicted AUC values in the high-risk group (log2FC > 0.15). Subsequently, we performed Spearman correlation analysis on AUC values and risk scores to further screen drugs that exhibited a positive correlation between the two indicators (R > 0.25 in CTRP, R > 0.3 in PRISM). Ultimately, we identified six compounds from the PRISM dataset (KX2-391, Ro-4987655, RITA, PD-0325901, 10-HCPT, TPA) (Fig. 3L) and six compounds from the CTRP dataset (KX2-391, Brefeldin A, SR-II-138A, Triazolothiadiazine, Austocystin D, Rigosertib) (Fig. S8C), suggesting these agents—particularly KX2-391—may be unsuitable for high-risk patients.

Additionally, after the application of z-score normalization to IHPS in the TCGA-CRC cohort, a nearest centroid classifier was developed and subsequently applied to all CRC cell lines in the GDSC dataset to predict IHPS risk groups. The GDSC database contains a total of 398 compounds, of which 248 were tested in over 90% of CRC cell lines. These 248 drugs were used for subsequent screening analysis. By comparing the AUC and IC50 values of drug responses between risk groups, we observed that the AUC and IC50 values of oxaliplatin and KU-59933 were significantly higher in the high-risk group. Conversely, YM-155 and OBATOCLAX showed significantly lower AUC and IC50 values in high-risk CRC cells, indicating that these patients may exhibit better sensitivity to these drugs (Figs. 3M–N and S8D).

Exploration and validation of key IHGs expression in the IHPS model

Based on the above bioinformatics analysis results, we preliminarily confirmed the critical role of IHGs in CRC patients and the clinical application potential of the predictive model. However, overreliance on big data and bioinformatics analyses may lead to insufficient biological validation of experimental results and a lack of in-depth mechanistic studies. Therefore, to further analyze the characteristics of iron homeostasis alterations in CRC, we measured the expression levels of 9 key IHGs in normal intestinal epithelial cells (CCD841CoN), primary CRC cell lines (RKO, HCT116, SW48, SW1116, HT29, SW1463), paired lymph node metastatic CRC cell lines (SW480 and SW620), lymph node metastatic CRC cell lines (LoVo), and distant metastatic cell lines (COLO205, SKCO1, NCI-H508). Results showed significant dysregulation of iron homeostasis molecules in almost all CRC cell lines compared to normal intestinal epithelial cells (P < 0.05). Specifically, HAMP, DMT1, TFRC, IREB2, and SLC39A14 were upregulated in tumor cells, while SLC40A1, TF, ACO1, and HFE were downregulated. These findings indicate that CRC cells exhibit an “iron addiction” phenotype characterized by enhanced iron uptake (upregulation of HAMP, TFRC, and DMT1) and suppressed iron efflux (downregulation of SLC40A1), suggesting intracellular iron overload. Notably, the paired lymph node metastatic cell line SW620 showed significantly higher expression of HAMP and TFRC compared to its primary counterpart SW480 (fold change > 5, P < 0.05), implying that HAMP and TFRC may collaboratively drive lymph node metastasis in CRC. Intriguingly, HAMP expression in distant metastatic cell lines was upregulated over tenfold compared to normal cells and significantly higher than in other CRC subtypes (P < 0.01), suggesting HAMP as a potential novel biomarker for CRC peritoneal metastasis (Fig. 4A). In the TCGA-CRC dataset, HAMP, SLC11A2, SLC40A1, TFRC, and TF exhibited different expression levels between normal and tumor tissues of CRC patients (Fig. 4B). Moreover, based on the HPA datasets, numerous proteins of these key IHGs also displayed significant expression changes between normal and tumor tissues in CRC patients (Fig. 4C). Notably, our analysis revealed significant differential expression of these key IHGs in CRC patients stratified by IHMS (IHMS A-C) and IHPS model (high- and low-risk) (Fig. 4D–E). These results indicated distinct expression patterns of the key IHGs in patients and cell lines representing different types of CRC, underscoring a close association between iron homeostasis and CRC.

Investigation of the biological mechanisms underlying key gene HAMP in the IHPS model

Given the consistent identification of HAMP as a pivotal molecule in the IHPS Model across bioinformatics analyses, predictive modeling, and cellular validation, we further investigated its functional role. Analysis of the TCGA database demonstrated a significant upregulation of HAMP expression in paired CRC tissues (Fig. 5A). Prognostic analysis revealed a significant correlation between elevated HAMP expression and shorter OS, disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) in CRC patients, indicating its potential role as a negative prognostic marker (Fig. 5B). This finding was further validated in the GSE38832 database (Fig. 5C). To explore the biological mechanisms of HAMP in CRC, in vitro experiments were performed. Firstly, HCT116 and HT29 cell lines with HAMP over-expression were established via gene editing technology (Fig. 5D). CCK-8 assays, EdU assays, and colony formation assays were performed to investigate the influence of HAMP on CRC cell proliferation (Fig. 5E–G). The results indicated that HAMP over-expression significantly enhanced the proliferation and capacity of CRC cells. To further validate the functional role of HAMP, we next generated stable HAMP-knockdown NCI-H508 cells, a cell line derived from CRC peritoneal metastasis. Functional assays revealed that HAMP silencing markedly suppressed CRC cell proliferation, as assessed by CCK-8 assays, and significantly impaired migratory and invasive abilities, as determined by wound-healing and Transwell assays (Fig. S9A–E). Together, these gain- and loss-of-function experiments provide complementary evidence supporting a critical role for HAMP in promoting CRC cell growth and invasive phenotypes. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying HAMP-mediated carcinogenesis, we conducted GO, KEGG, and GSEA enrichment analysis of transcriptional profiles from CRC patients stratified by HAMP expression levels using the TCGA-CRC dataset. The results indicated that the differentially enrichment pathways were mainly enriched in response to oxygen levels/external stimuli, cell adhesion, ion metabolism, immune response, oxidative stress, Wnt signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, and ECM − receptor interaction etc. (Fig. 5H–J). Based on the findings from the enrichment analysis, we speculated that HAMP may play a role in tumor stemness in CRC. Then, we validated this hypothesis through soft agar colony formation assays, which revealed that HCT116 and HT29 cells overexpressing HAMP exhibited a heightened tendency for sphere formation (Fig. 5K). From a molecular biology perspective, we further assessed the mRNA and protein expression of key regulatory molecules associated with tumor proliferation and stemness, including CD44, PROM1 (CD133), YAP1, WWTR1 (TAZ), CDNNB1 (β-Catenin), and MYC. The findings revealed a positive correlation between HAMP/hepcidin and these molecules (Figs. 5L–N and S9F).

HAMP/hepcidin as a pro-tumorigenic molecule and a therapeutic target in CRC

In vitro experiments demonstrated that the HAMP/hepcidin enhances the proliferation and stemness of CRC cells, while its pro-tumorigenic roles and underlying mechanisms in vivo remain unclear. Thus, subcutaneous tumorigenesis experiments in nude mice were performed and revealed that HAMP overexpression significantly promoted the tumorigenic capacity of CRC cells (HCT116 and HT29) in vivo (Fig. 6A–D). Moreover, we assessed the protein expression levels of KI67, panCK, CD44, CD133, hepcidin, β-Catenin and YAP1 in subcutaneous tumors from different mouse groups, indicated a significant increase in all indicators within the HAMP overexpression groups (Fig. 6E). Our findings demonstrated that HAMP/hepcidin promotes the in vivo growth of CRC cells, likely through upregulating molecules associated with tumor proliferation and stemness. To investigate the potential of HAMP/hepcidin as a therapeutic target for CRC, we generated and validated Hamp knockout (KO) mice (Fig. S10). Then, we further established a CRC mouse model using the AOM/DSS treatment to observe changes in colon length and tumor incidence in Hamp-KO mice compared to wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 6F). The results indicated that Hamp-KO mice exhibited a significant reduction in tumor burden, as demonstrated by an increased colon length and a reduced number of tumors (Fig. 6G–I). We also performed immunohistochemistry to evaluate the expression of tumor proliferation markers (Ki67 and panCK), tumor stemness markers (Cd44 and Cd133), and hepcidin in the colon tissues of both groups of mice. The results revealed a significant downregulation of these markers in the Hamp-KO mice (Fig. 6J). Besides, given that hepcidin is a central regulator of iron homeostasis with secretory properties and may have the potential to exert paracrine effects within the tumor microenvironment, we further employed multiplex immunofluorescence to examine the relationship between HAMP/hepcidin expression and distinct cellular compartments (malignant epithelial cells, CK19; fibroblasts, α-SMA; T cells, CD3; B cells, CD20; and macrophages, CD68) in tumors derived from Hamp knockout and wild-type mice. These analyses revealed that hepcidin predominantly colocalized with tumor cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, showing a positive association with epithelial (CK19) and fibroblast (α-SMA) markers, whereas an inverse association was observed with the T-cell marker CD3 (Fig. 6K). In summary, we confirmed that HAMP/hepcidin is a pro-tumorigenic molecule and a potential therapeutic target in CRC.

Validation of the expression and clinical significance of HAMP/hepcidin in our in-house cohorts

To address the scientificity and reliability of the above results, the transcriptome cohort (RJ-BK-Cohort) and tissue-array (RJ-TMA-Cohort) of our center (Ruijin hospital) were applied to verify the expression levels and clinical significance of HAMP/hepcidin in CRC. For the transcriptome cohort, the differential analysis was performed between the normal and tumor tissues from 64 CRC individuals in our center. The results showed that the mRNA expression levels of HAMP were statistically up-regulated in tumor samples consistent with the above results in the TCGA-CRC cohorts (Fig. 7A–B). Then, for the clinical significance, the mRNA expression levels of HAMP were also up-regulated in M1 stage and Stage III&IV groups (Fig. 7C–D). We also confirmed that CRC patients with high-expression HAMP levels exhibited significantly poorer prognosis in our RJ-BK-Cohort (Fig. 7E). Besides, HAMP mRNA expression in CRC individuals was significantly and positively correlated with the expression of CD44 (R = 0.311), YAP1 (R = 0.393), WWTR1 (R = 0.608) and CTNNB1 (R = 0.395) (Fig. 7F). We also further verified the protein expression levels and clinical significance of hepcidin in our RJ-TMA-Cohort by IHC (Fig. 7G). Consistently, the results showed that the expression of hepcidin was increased in tumor tissues, especially in CRC individuals with poor-stage (Fig. 7H–I). Meanwhile, hepcidin was also associated with the prognosis of CRC individuals in our center (Fig. 7J). To further investigate the association between HAMP/hepcidin expression and the tumor immune microenvironment, multiplex immunofluorescence was performed to assess its spatial relationship with multiple cellular markers, including malignant epithelial cells (CK19), fibroblasts (α-SMA), T cells (CD3), and macrophages (CD68). Consistent with our in vivo findings, HAMP/hepcidin in human CRC samples predominantly colocalized with tumor cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, with the strongest spatial associations observed in tumor cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 7K).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of IHGs in CRC, highlighting their significance in tumor progression, prognosis, and therapy response. By integrating multiple datasets (encompassing over 1000 CRC cases) and leveraging diverse machine learning algorithms, we developed and validated an iron homeostasis-associated predictive model for CRC (IHPS). Multidimensional validation demonstrated its robust prognostic performance. The model effectively stratifies CRC patients based on tumor biological features, immune microenvironment composition, mutational profiles, and immunotherapy responsiveness. In vitro and in vivo phenotypic experiments combined with molecular analyses revealed dysregulated expression of iron homeostasis genes in most CRC cell lines. The core gene HAMP significantly promoted CRC progression, highlighting its potential therapeutic relevance. As the first study to systematically decode the roles of iron homeostasis genes in CRC prognosis, biological characteristics, and treatment response using large-scale public databases and bioinformatics, this work elucidates their clinical value and provides a theoretical foundation for further exploration of HAMP-mediated mechanisms in CRC pathogenesis. We first defined a novel iron homeostasis-related gene set (317 genes) through existing evidence. Consistent with prior reports, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed significant associations with cellular metabolism, regulated cell death, and immune regulation pathways [5]. Multi-algorithm differential analysis of the TCGA-CRC cohort identified 212 iron homeostasis-associated DEGs, with 33 showing prognostic relevance. Unsupervised clustering of these prognostic genes defined three iron homeostasis-related molecular subtypes (IHMS-A/B/C). IHMS-B exhibited significantly worse prognosis, molecular features linked to metabolic activation, oxidative phosphorylation, and immune microenvironment. We hypothesize that the poorest prognosis of IHMS-B may be fundamentally linked to its consistently low predicted immune therapy sensitivity and aggressive epithelial cell proliferative characteristics. Results showed that IHMS-B displayed the lowest predicted immune therapy sensitivity across key combinations, suggesting limited responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Furthermore, our analysis revealed IHMS-B’s significant enrichment in the epithelial cell proliferation pathway compared to IHMS-A and IHMS-C in both single-cell and bulk datasets, which might imply a more aggressive malignancy. Conversely, the best prognosis is observed in IHMS-C subtypes, which we presume is likely attributable to its distinct immunological profile, specifically its highest TMB and a high proportion of MSI-H status. Both high TMB and MSI-H are well-established biomarkers associated with enhanced immune checkpoint inhibitor efficacy due to increased neoantigen load and an active tumor immune microenvironment [26, 27]. We therefore hypothesize that the synergistic effect of these factors in IHMS-C substantially enhances its immunogenicity, potentially making these tumors more amenable to immune surveillance and therapy, thus contributing to its superior clinical outcome.

To further extend our understanding of IHMS at a cellular resolution, we performed unsupervised clustering of epithelial cells from scRNA-seq data, successfully identifying three distinct iron homeostasis-related epithelial cell subtypes (termed scIHMS-A/B/C). While our single-cell findings largely corroborated the clinical and functional characteristics observed in the bulk analysis—for instance, scIHMS-B consistently deriving from patients with more advanced clinical stages and showing altered pathways activation such as oxidative stress, intercellular iron homeostasis and epithelial cell activation levels—a precise one-to-one correspondence between bulk and single-cell derived clusters remained elusive. We contend that this is an expected outcome, reflecting the inherent methodological and biological complexities in integrating data across different scales. Bulk RNA-seq averages gene expression across millions of cells, offering a broad transcriptional landscape, whereas scRNA-seq captures gene expression at the individual cell level, revealing nuanced cellular heterogeneity and specific functional states often masked in bulk analyses. Consequently, while the single-cell data validates the core biological relevance of our IHMS subtypes by recapitulating key clinical and functional associations, the lack of perfect cluster overlap also underscores the limitations of directly comparing these distinct data modalities. It highlights that while IHMS provides a robust framework at the bulk level for prognosis and treatment, its manifestation at the single-cell level reveals additional layers of cellular diversity and context-dependent regulatory mechanisms that warrant further investigation.

The AJCC staging system, as the cornerstone of tumor diagnosis and treatment management, has significantly enhanced the comparability of assessments across medical institutions through its standardized tumor classification framework, while providing critical guidance for global treatment protocol standardization [28]. However, the prognostic accuracy of this system remains constrained by its reliance on conventional pathological parameters, which inadequately address tumor biological heterogeneity and individualized patient characteristics, potentially leading to biased prognostic risk assessments for high-risk patients and suboptimal precision therapeutic decisions. Recent advancements in machine learning technologies have opened new avenues for innovation in prognostic evaluation. Studies demonstrate that machine learning methods can effectively uncover prognostic patterns undetected by traditional staging systems through the integration of multidimensional clinical data and molecular profiles [29]. In CRC research, despite the identification of numerous potential molecular prognostic markers, robust predictive models validated across multicenter large-scale cohorts remain scarce [30]. Building on prior findings from iron homeostasis-related molecular subtyping, this study systematically evaluated 101 machine learning algorithm combinations to develop a quantitative iron homeostasis-associated prognostic model. To optimize model performance and mitigate overfitting risks [31], a stringent cross-validation strategy was implemented, completely excluding training set data during the calculation of the model’s average C-index. Through multidimensional feature selection, the CoxBoost algorithm integrating 21 iron homeostasis-related genes was ultimately selected to establish a predictive model within a multi-cohort dataset comprising 1488 public CRC samples. This model demonstrated precise survival probability prediction and stratification by analyzing iron homeostasis-related gene expression profiles. Despite the exponential growth of machine learning-driven prognostic models in CRC research, their clinical translation remains hampered by insufficient reproducibility validation [32]. To address this, we conducted a comparative analysis of 69 previously reported CRC prognostic gene prediction models, revealing the superior predictive performance of our model [33]. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, combined with prognostic nomogram construction, confirmed the independent prognostic value of the risk score and its synergistic predictive capacity with multidimensional clinical parameters, underscoring its clinical potential as a precision prognostic tool.

The clinical applicability of the model necessitates a robust biological foundation. Systematic dissection of the molecular features underlying iron homeostasis risk stratification revealed significant gene and pathway heterogeneity between high- and low-risk groups. Differentially expressed genes were predominantly enriched in biological pathways such as immune response regulation, programmed cell death, ion homeostasis, oxidative phosphorylation, and cellular stress response—consistent with prior studies and mechanistically linked to intracellular iron homeostasis [34–40]. Intriguingly, the p53 signaling pathway exhibited marked activation in the low-risk group, aligning with somatic mutation trends. Concurrently, the high-risk group displayed significantly higher tumor mutational burden, with TP53 missense mutations being the most prominent difference. As a critical tumor suppressor gene, TP53 encodes the p53 protein, which maintains genomic stability and cellular homeostasis by regulating downstream targets [41]. Emerging evidence reveals a bidirectional regulatory network between p53 and iron homeostasis: p53 transcriptionally activates key iron regulatory genes such as HAMP to modulate systemic iron absorption and recycling [42], while simultaneously upregulating iron-sulfur cluster assembly enzymes to mitigate iron overload-induced oxidative damage [43]. Conversely, intracellular iron levels reciprocally regulate p53 activity—iron chelators activate the p53/p21 signaling axis under iron-deficient conditions to suppress proliferation [44, 45], whereas iron overload suppresses p53 expression through heme-dependent mechanisms [46].This dynamic interplay suggests that iron metabolism dysregulation may drive CRC pathogenesis through perturbation of the p53 signaling pathway.

The iron homeostasis-associated predictive model developed in this study demonstrates significant prognostic predictive capacity and molecular feature discrimination, with unique advantages in immunotherapy response evaluation. To address the scarcity of CRC-specific immunotherapy data, we innovatively integrated pan-cancer immunotherapy cohorts—including IMvigor210 (urothelial carcinoma) and Chan/Schadendorf (melanoma)—for comprehensive analysis. Notably, high-risk patients consistently exhibited significantly reduced immunotherapy response rates and poorer clinical outcomes across biologically distinct cohorts, suggesting iron homeostasis-immunotherapy interplay. Applying the immune phenotype framework (immune-inflamed/excluded/desert) [47], we observed significant risk score variations among subtypes, with immune-desert phenotypes showing the highest scores. Paradoxically, multi-algorithm immune infiltration analysis combined with H&E validation revealed elevated immune cell infiltration (B cells, CD8+ T cells, macrophages) and upregulated immune checkpoint molecules (PD-1/CTLA-4) and MHC expression in high-risk tumors—features discordant with typical “cold tumor” characteristics. This apparent contradiction may reflect dynamic immunosuppressive reprogramming: despite immune cell infiltration, their functionality may be compromised by co-expressed inhibitory factors (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10) [48, 49], ultimately forming a dysfunctional “immune-desert-like” microenvironment. To address therapeutic resistance in high-risk patients, systematic drug screening identified potential strategies. Analysis of PRISM and CTRP databases revealed that KX2-391 (Tirbanibulin), an FDA-approved Src kinase inhibitor for actinic keratosis/psoriasis with broad antitumor activity in early-phase trials for AML/prostate cancer [50, 51], showed limited efficacy in high-risk CRC but enhanced response in low-risk subgroups. Mechanistically, KX2-391 synergizes with FGFR inhibitor BLU-554 to suppress ELF4-driven colon cancer metastasis [52], suggesting combination therapy as a novel direction. GDSC database analysis further uncovered risk-stratified drug sensitivity: oxaliplatin efficacy was significantly reduced in high-risk groups, aligning with evidence that iron dysregulation induces oxidative iron pool depletion and ferroptosis resistance [53], while pro-apoptotic compound YM-155 and pan-BCL-2 inhibitors showed enhanced therapeutic potential for high-risk patients. Collectively, this iron homeostasis risk model provides a robust framework for CRC drug discovery and personalized therapy optimization, particularly for high-risk populations.

While bioinformatic analyses and public databases have yielded numerous discoveries in life sciences research, they often lack the resolution to investigate molecular mechanisms at finer biological scales. Therefore, this study further investigated the expression patterns and biological significance of iron homeostasis-related molecules in CRC cell lines and animal models through in vivo and in vitro experiments combined with molecular biology techniques, building upon prior findings. Our results demonstrated dysregulated expression of iron homeostasis-related molecules in nearly all CRC cell lines, indicating abnormal iron regulation in CRC pathogenesis. Notably, HAMP and TFRC expression levels were significantly elevated in CRC cell lines derived from metastatic subtypes (lymph node and distant metastases) compared to primary tumor cells and normal controls, suggesting their dual roles as core iron regulatory molecules in both CRC initiation and metastatic progression. Subsequent functional validation confirmed that HAMP overexpression enhances the proliferative capacity of HCT116 cells in vitro and promotes subcutaneous tumor growth in nude mice in vivo, consistent with previous studies [54]. Importantly, we observed markedly elevated HAMP expression in CRC peritoneal metastasis cell lines, though its potential as a molecular marker for peritoneal metastasis requires further exploration. Importantly, we identified and validated the central role and significance of HAMP/hepcidin in the IHPS model. Additionally, based on in vivo and in vitro experiments alongside molecular biology techniques, we demonstrated the pro-tumorigenic effects of HAMP/hepcidin in CRC cells, and we preliminarily found that its mechanisms may be closely related to the regulation of core molecules associated with tumor proliferation and stemness. Finally, we utilized Hamp-KO mice to elucidate its therapeutic potential and value in CRC. We also used the transcriptome cohort (RJ-BK-Cohort) and tissue-array (RJ-TMA-Cohort) to further verify the expression and clinical significance of HAMP/hepcidin in CRC. These findings provide new insights for the personalization and precision treatment of CRC, suggesting that HAMP may serve as a therapeutic biomarker. In addition, our analyses revealed that HAMP/hepcidin play an important role in CRC microenvironment, colocalizing with tumor cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages in both mouse and human samples. These findings provide new insights for the personalization and precision treatment of CRC, suggesting that HAMP may serve as a therapeutic biomarker for CRC.

While this study provides valuable insights, we acknowledge several limitations. Primarily, our findings are derived from a retrospective analysis of publicly available bulk genomic data, which, despite rigorous data processing and stringent patient selection, necessitates prospective validation under strictly standardized clinical protocols before definitive clinical application. Furthermore, the inherent averaging nature of bulk transcriptome analysis may obscure cell-type-specific nuances. Our future research will therefore focus on elucidating the cell-type-specific roles and interactions of IHGs within the tumor microenvironment using advanced single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomic technologies. Third, while our immunofluorescence analyses suggest an association between HAMP/hepcidin expression and specific cellular compartments within the tumor microenvironment, further studies using larger cohorts and higher-resolution approaches will be required to define its cell-type–specific roles. Moreover, although genetic approaches were employed to interrogate the role of HAMP/hepcidin in this study, the lack of well-established pharmacological inhibitors targeting tumoral hepcidin currently limits therapeutic validation. Future work will focus on the development and evaluation of small-molecule modulators of iron metabolism and hepcidin signaling in CRC.

In conclusion, we developed and validated a novel iron homeostasis-based prognostic model using multi-omics data and machine learning techniques. This model effectively stratified patients by risk and predicted responses across diverse immunotherapy cohorts, and identified potential targeted therapeutic strategies for high-risk CRC patients through drug response analysis. In vitro and in vivo studies established the oncogenic functions and metastatic properties of the core iron homeostasis regulator HAMP in CRC, identifying it as a peritoneal metastasis biomarker and a therapeutic target. These findings clarify the mechanistic role of iron homeostasis in CRC progression and establish a solid foundation for future studies investigating the specific pathways by which HAMP-mediated iron regulation contributes to tumor pathogenesis.
