Geospatial analysis of diarrheal disease burden and associated health determinants among under-five children in Karnataka using NFHS data
Authors: Mounika Sree Manivasagan, Chaithra Mallaiah, Suraj B Manjunath, Murali Aadhitya Magateshvaren Saras, Sahana K Suresh, Manasapriya Dwadasi, Madhu Basavegowda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiarrhoeal disease remains a major contributor to under-five morbidity and mortality in India, despite significant public health investments. This study investigates the spatial and temporal distribution of diarrhoeal disease among children under five across Karnataka, using data from two rounds of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-4: 2015–16 and NFHS-5: 2019–21). District-level prevalence data were extracted, georeferenced, and analyzed using Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) and Moran’s I statistics to identify clustering patterns and spatial dependencies. The analysis revealed considerable spatial heterogeneity, with emerging hotspots in northern districts such as Bidar and Kalaburagi and declining trends in southern districts like Mysuru and Chikkamagaluru. Univariate LISA identified high-high clusters in NFHS-4, which weakened in NFHS-5, indicating a shift in spatial concentration. Bivariate spatial analysis demonstrated evolving associations between diarrhoeal prevalence and determinants such as sanitation, undernutrition, and dietary adequacy. Notably, consistent spatial co-clustering was observed between diarrhoea and underweight prevalence. These findings provide critical insights into the evolving spatial dynamics of childhood diarrhoeal disease and highlight the importance of localized surveillance to identify high-burden districts and prioritize context-specific public health interventions.Keywords: Diarrhoeal disease, Under 5 children, National Family Health Survey, Geospatial analysis, Karnataka, Bivariate Moran’s ISubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Diarrhoea is defined as the passage of three or more loose or liquid stools per day—or more frequently than is normal for the individual. It does not include the passage of formed stools or the loose stools passed by breastfed infants1. Diarrheal diseases (DD), commonly caused by bacterial, viral, or parasitic infections, are predominantly transmitted through contaminated food, unsafe water, or inadequate sanitation and hygiene practices. Despite being preventable and treatable, diarrhoea remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among children under five, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)2,3.

Globally, DD account for an estimated 1.7 billion cases and over 500,000 deaths each year among children under five, ranking third after pneumonia and complications related to preterm birth4. The risk of death from diarrhoea is approximately eleven times higher than from HIV in children, with Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia bearing the greatest burden5. Although modest progress has been made in reducing childhood diarrhoeal mortality, the decline has not been rapid or equitable enough to meet Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) targets. Achieving the global goal of reducing under-five mortality to fewer than 25 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030 necessitates intensified, geographically targeted, and evidence-based interventions2,6.

This global challenge is mirrored at the national and subnational levels in India, where states like Karnataka continue to exhibit considerable spatial variation in diarrhoeal disease burden, underscoring the need for localized, evidence-based public health responses. According to the National Family Health Survey (NFHS), the prevalence of diarrhoea among children under five increased slightly from 9.0% in 2015–16 (NFHS-4) to 9.2% in 2019–21 (NFHS-5)7. In 2014, the country reported approximately 11.6 million diarrhoeal cases and over 1300 deaths, with the state of Karnataka attributing 18–20% of under-five mortality to diarrhoea alone8. District-specific studies, such as one conducted in Karkala Taluk of Karnataka from 2015 to 2018, documented high incidence rates and seasonal and spatial clustering of diarrhoeal cases among under-five children, highlighting the need for localized surveillance and intervention strategies9.

Children’s survival during a diarrhoeal episode is intricately linked to their geographic and social environment. In LMICs, over 90% of diarrhoea-related deaths occur in areas with limited access to safe drinking water, adequate sanitation, and timely medical care10. Only one in three children receives the recommended treatment with oral rehydration therapy (ORT) and zinc supplements11. In India’s complex and diverse landscape, the district forms the most critical governance and implementation unit for public health and development programs. Districts vary significantly in terms of infrastructure, resources, service delivery capacity, and sociodemographic characteristics. Therefore, conducting district-level analyses enables better understanding of the micro-level disparities that national or state averages often mask, allowing for more precise and effective public health interventions12.

Over the past decade, India has implemented major national initiatives to reduce the burden of diarrhoea and other childhood illnesses. Programs such as Swachh Bharat Abhiyan13, Jal Jeevan Mission14, POSHAN Abhiyaan15, and components of the National Health Mission aim to improve sanitation, safe drinking water access, and nutritional outcomes. While these initiatives have shown progress in improving coverage, their actual impact on health outcomes like DD varies across regions, and there is limited evidence assessing their effectiveness at the subnational level.

Understanding where and why DD burden remains high is critical for optimizing these programs. While previous studies have primarily reported national or state-level prevalence, they often fail to capture the spatial heterogeneity and localized risk factors that drive disease dynamics. Moreover, there is a lack of comparative spatio-temporal analyses using successive rounds of NFHS data to assess how the distribution of DD and its determinants has evolved in response to policy interventions.

Recognizing this, it is imperative to systematically explore spatial variations and clustering patterns of diarrhoeal prevalence across Karnataka—a state with well-documented inter-district disparities in child health and nutrition. While national health programs have aimed to improve sanitation, drinking water access, and child nutrition through phased, district-based implementation, little is known about how these interventions have influenced disease patterns over time. Understanding the spatial distribution and determinants of DD at the district level is especially relevant in the context of India’s ongoing efforts to achieve SDG-3 and reduce child mortality through data-driven and geographically targeted health strategies.

In this context, we hypothesize that the prevalence of diarrhoeal disease among under-five children in Karnataka exhibits significant spatial clustering and that higher prevalence is associated with key health and environmental determinants. Hence the present study aims to investigate the spatio-temporal distribution of DD among under-five children across Karnataka, using data from NFHS-4 and NFHS-5. Specifically, it seeks to: (i) examine the geographic patterns and evolution of diarrhoeal prevalence at the district level, and (ii) assess spatial associations between diarrhoeal burden and key health and environmental determinants, including sanitation coverage, access to improved drinking water, dietary adequacy, and anthropometric indicators of child undernutrition (underweight, stunting, and wasting). By applying geospatial statistical methods such as univariate and bivariate LISA and Moran’s I, the study seeks to generate high-resolution evidence that can guide data-driven, district-specific public health planning, support the targeting of interventions, and improve program efficiency toward achieving national child health goals.

Materials and methods

Study design and period

This cross-sectional observational study utilizes data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) 4, conducted from February to July 2015, and NFHS 5, conducted from July to December 2019, in Karnataka, India.

Conceptual framework

The analysis was guided by a conceptual framework adapted from previous studies on the social and environmental determinants of child health16–21. It postulates that the prevalence of DD is shaped by a constellation of factors, including environmental exposures (e.g., sanitation and water access), nutritional status (e.g., underweight, stunting, wasting), and maternal/infant care practices (e.g., breastfeeding and immunization). Spatial characteristics and clustering of these determinants are hypothesized to influence the geographic distribution of risk. The conceptual model provides the basis for both univariate and bivariate spatial analyses conducted in this study.

Unlike previous NFHS/DHS studies that largely focus on national-level patterns, this study applies the framework at the subnational level in Karnataka, enabling district-level spatial analysis and detection of local heterogeneity. By comparing NFHS-4 and NFHS-5, the study also captures temporal variations in both prevalence and determinants, offering a more targeted basis for intervention.

Data source and study population

This study utilized data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) rounds 4 and 5, conducted as part of India’s implementation of the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) program. Both NFHS-4 (2015–16) and NFHS-5 (2019–21) are nationally representative, cross-sectional surveys that provide extensive information on population health, nutrition, maternal and child health, and household environmental conditions across all Indian states and union territories, including district-level estimates.

In Karnataka, NFHS-4 was conducted between February 25 and July 20, 2015, collecting data from 23,842 households and 26,291 women aged 15–49 years. The NFHS-5 survey was carried out from July 10 to December 11, 2019, and included 26,574 households and 30,455 women in the same age group. The surveys employed a multistage stratified sampling design to ensure representativeness at the district level, and collected information through standardized interviews, anthropometric measurements, and household questionnaires.

For the purpose of this study, we extracted data specific to children under 5 years of age residing in Karnataka. In NFHS-4, a total of 7,443 under-five children were surveyed, of whom 343 (4.7%) were reported to have experienced diarrhoea in the 2 weeks preceding the survey. In NFHS-5, the sample included 8140 under-five children, among whom 461 (5.3%) had diarrhoea during the same reference period. The outcome variable-diarrhoeal disease-was based on maternal recall of whether the child had symptoms of diarrhoea within the 2 weeks before the interview, consistent with standard DHS methodology.

The NFHS datasets are publicly available and can be accessed upon request through the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program portal (https://dhsprogram.com/), with datasets downloadable from https://dhsprogram.com/data, following data-use approval protocols.

Sampling design and technique

The NFHS surveys employ a multistage stratified sampling method to ensure that the data are representative at the district level. In each district, the Primary Sampling Unit (PSUs) are villages in rural areas and Census Enumeration Blocks (CEBs) in urban areas. Households within each PSU are selected through systematic sampling. For this study, we utilized the existing NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 sampling frameworks, incorporating appropriate stratification and weighting to account for the complex survey design and to ensure representativeness. The representativeness of the data was cross-checked with the district-level NFHS fact sheets, which provide key indicators and trends for Karnataka. These fact sheets are publicly available at http://rchiips.org/nfhs/districtfactsheet_NFHS-5.shtml. The flow chart of the Methodology is shown in the Fig. 1.

In this study, the primary dependent variable is the prevalence of diarrhoea among children under 5 years of age in the districts of Karnataka, measured as the percentage of children who experienced diarrhoea within the 2 weeks preceding the survey. Data integration and pre-processing

The data for this study were extracted from the children’s files of NFHS-4 (IAKR74FL) and NFHS-5 (IAKR7EFL) using STATA software version 15 (StataCorp, TX, USA) (https://www.stata.com/). Appropriate sampling weights provided by NFHS were applied to ensure representativeness at the district level. Relevant indicators were then extracted and exported to MS Excel for further organization and cleaning.

During the cleaning process, records with missing values for the outcome variable (diarrhoea prevalence) or for key independent variables were excluded from the analysis to maintain data integrity. Additionally, data consistency was verified against NFHS district fact sheets, and variable coding was harmonized across survey rounds to ensure comparability.

District boundary shapefiles were obtained from the Karnataka Geographic Information System (KGIS) website in ‘.shp’ format. The cleaned NFHS datasets were then integrated with the spatial geometry using ArcGIS pro (Licensed to JSS AHER), & Geoda 1.22 software, linking district-level health indicators with corresponding geographic boundaries for subsequent geospatial analysis.

Statistical analysis

To identify and visualize spatial clusters within the study area, Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) were employed-specifically the Local Moran’s I, also known as cluster and outlier analysis. This statistical approach classifies regions into distinct categories based on the similarity of attribute values in relation to neighboring units. In this analysis, Local Moran’s I was applied to determine clusters of DD prevalence among under-five children at the district level.

The LISA output generated a spatial cluster map identifying statistically significant patterns, categorized into four types: high–high clusters, where districts with high diarrhoeal prevalence are surrounded by districts with similarly high values; low–low clusters, where both the district and its neighbors have low prevalence; and two types of spatial outliers-high–low, indicating a high-prevalence district bordered by low-prevalence neighbors, and low–high, representing a low-prevalence district surrounded by high-prevalence neighbors.

The Moran’s I statistic ranges from − 1 to + 1, where values close to + 1 indicate strong positive spatial autocorrelation (i.e., clustering of similar values), values near − 1 suggest negative spatial autocorrelation (i.e., spatial dispersion of dissimilar values), and values close to 0 imply a random spatial distribution.

While univariate Moran’s I assesses the degree of spatial autocorrelation for a single variable by comparing the variable’s value to that of its spatial lag, bivariate Moran’s I is used to explore the spatial correlation between two distinct variables. In this study, bivariate Moran’s I was applied to evaluate the spatial association between diarrhoea prevalence and potential explanatory variables, including sanitation coverage, access to drinking water, and indicators of nutritional status (e.g., stunting, underweight, and wasting). The spatial lag of the second variable is plotted against the observed values of the first, allowing detection of co-clustering patterns across geographic units.

The results from both univariate and bivariate analyses were further used to generate bivariate LISA cluster maps, which visually represent areas where spatial co-dependencies exist. This method provides a robust framework for identifying localized clusters or outliers where high or low values of diarrhoeal prevalence spatially align with the values of specific health determinants, thereby offering insights for geographically targeted interventions.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive spatial and temporal examination of DD prevalence among under-five children in Karnataka using NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 data. The findings reveal substantial intra-state heterogeneity in disease burden and provide evidence of evolving spatial patterns and determinants over time. The integration of choropleth mapping, univariate and bivariate LISA, and spatial autocorrelation statistics offers valuable insights into the dynamic interplay between geographic location, infrastructure access, and nutritional status in shaping childhood diarrhoeal morbidity.

The analysis of choropleth maps underscores both persistent and emerging geographic disparities in DD prevalence across Karnataka. Districts such as Gadag exhibited consistently high prevalence in both NFHS-4 (9.5%) and NFHS-5 (10.4%), indicating entrenched vulnerability. This persistence is likely driven by structural & social determinants—long-standing systemic factors such as inadequate and uneven access to safe drinking water and sanitation services, limited health infrastructure, low maternal education, and widespread socioeconomic disadvantages17. Conversely, districts such as Bengaluru (Urban), Mysuru, and Chikkamagaluru demonstrated marked reductions, suggesting effective implementation of sanitation, water supply, and healthcare interventions. These improvements may reflect the cumulative impact of state-level efforts aligned with national programs, including the Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM) and Jal Jeevan Mission, both of which aim to improve household-level sanitation and access to safe drinking water. However, the sharp rise in DD prevalence in districts such as Bidar (3.3–9.0%) and Kalaburagi (0.9–9.4%) between survey rounds indicates the emergence of new hotspots and underscores the uneven reach or effectiveness of these interventions. Geospatial studies within Karnataka highlight that the prevalence of diarrheal disease is not evenly distributed, with certain villages or micro-regions becoming persistent hotspots due to the spatial clustering of risk factors like poverty, inadequate infrastructure, malnutrition and water contamination9.

The spatial clustering results reveal a notable shift in the epidemiological landscape between the two time points. In NFHS-4, strong spatial autocorrelation was observed, with significant high-high clustering in Shivamogga and low-low clusters in northern Karnataka (Bidar, Yadgir, and Vijayapura). This clustering pattern points to a localized concentration of risk, potentially linked to shared environmental exposures, water quality, or healthcare accessibility. However, by NFHS-5, these clusters had largely dissipated, with no significant high-high or low-low groupings, and the emergence of low–high clusters in districts such as Yadgir, Bagalkote, and Dharwad. This spatial dispersion suggests a transition from tightly bounded areas of disease concentration to a more diffuse and unpredictable pattern, highlighting the need for enhanced district-level surveillance and early warning systems.

The emergence of Low–High spatial clusters in NFHS-5, which were not significant in NFHS-4, likely reflects relative changes in neighbouring district prevalence rather than absolute improvement in the district. In Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) analysis, a Low–High cluster represents a district with low prevalence surrounded by districts with high prevalence, indicating a sharp spatial disparity. Between NFHS-4 (2015–16) and NFHS-5 (2019–21), targeted health interventions—such as improved access to safe water, sanitation, and maternal–child health services—may have disproportionately benefitted certain districts, lowering their prevalence rates18.

The bivariate spatial association analysis further elucidates the role of key determinants in shaping DD burden across districts. Sanitation, a critical driver of enteric infections, showed a shifting spatial relationship with DD prevalence. In NFHS-4, certain southern districts such as Kodagu and Chikkamagaluru displayed unexpectedly high DD prevalence alongside high sanitation coverage. Several studies have found that the mere presence of sanitation infrastructure does not guarantee its effective or consistent use22,23. For instance, cultural preferences, social norms, and longstanding practices such as open defecation can persist even in communities with improved latrine access, undermining potential health benefits. Additionally, hygiene behaviors—particularly regular and proper handwashing with soap—often lag behind increases in toilet construction, leaving residual risks for faecal-oral transmission of pathogens24,25. However, by NFHS-5, a more expected negative spatial association emerged, with high DD prevalence co-locating with poor sanitation access in Bidar, Kalaburagi, and Vijayapura signaling the long-term benefit of improved sanitation access in reducing disease risk when adoption and usage become widespread. This finding is supported by a previous study conducted by Shahid et al. in Pakistan, which reported that the likelihood of child malnutrition significantly decreases with access to improved water sources and adequate sanitation facilities at the household level26.

The spatial association between adequate diet and DD prevalence was strong in NFHS-4 but weakened in NFHS-5, reflecting a possible dilution of dietary inequities or programmatic improvements in nutritional coverage. This attenuation may also indicate the growing role of other determinants—such as water quality or maternal education—in influencing disease outcomes. In contrast, the relationship between undernutrition indicators—particularly underweight—and DD prevalence remained strong and consistent across both survey rounds. The emergence of high-high clusters in Vijayapura, Kalaburgi and Koppal in NFHS-5 points to the persistence of the malnutrition–infection cycle, where repeated infections exacerbate undernutrition and vice versa1. A study conducted by Ghosh et al., which included 707 districts across India, found that underweight children are at a significantly higher risk of experiencing DD. These findings emphasize the continued importance of nutrition-specific and nutrition-sensitive interventions, particularly in vulnerable regions26.

Interestingly, the spatial correlation between access to improved drinking water and DD prevalence remained weak and inconsistent across both rounds. This could be attributed to a mismatch between infrastructure availability and water safety, household storage practices, or intermittent service delivery. It highlights that while infrastructure expansion is crucial, complementary efforts in behavior change, household hygiene, and water quality monitoring are essential to translating access into impact. Similar study was conducted in Ethiopia found that Access to improved water and sanitation was significantly associated with reduction of DD among under-five children27.

Spatial dependence between stunting and DD revealed a geographic shift from southern districts (Chamarajanagar and Mysuru) in NFHS-4 to northern regions (Vijayapura and Kalaburagi) in NFHS-5, indicating a changing epidemiological relationship likely driven by intersecting factors such as poverty, maternal health, and early childhood care practices. The northward shift in bivariate spatial dependence between stunting and diarrheal disease from NFHS-4 to NFHS-5 likely reflects relative improvements in southern districts and slower or uneven gains in parts of northern Karnataka.

The existing literature and recent reports on inter-state poverty differentials suggest that high stunting prevalence is often closely associated with high poverty levels28. Moreover, the persistence of stunting despite large-scale implementation of national programs-such as POSHAN Abhiyaan and ICDS—points toward challenges in program reach, acceptability, and sustainability. Several studies have noted that poor implementation, lack of monitoring, insufficient resources, and the socio-cultural diversity among districts can undermine the effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing childhood undernutrition. Stunting, as a chronic outcome of prolonged nutritional deprivation, reflects cumulative exposure to inadequate dietary intake, repeated infections, and poor caregiving environments—conditions that are more likely to persist in socioeconomically disadvantaged regions28.

Wasting, a marker of acute undernutrition, showed reduced spatial association with DD over time, suggesting that while wasting remains a concern, its geographic overlap with diarrhoea has become less predictable, potentially due to localized health interventions or rapid-onset food insecurity events that do not spatially align with chronic enteric disease patterns29.

The comparison of DD prevalence between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 reveals an overall slight increase from 4.8 to 5.3% across the state. Similar trends are observed in Delhi, where DD prevalence increased from 9.6 to 10.6%, emphasizing the need for enhanced public health measures. In contrast, Uttarakhand saw a significant decrease from 17 to 4.4%, reflecting successful interventions that could serve as models for other regions30.

Collectively, the findings emphasize the value of district-level spatial analysis in capturing localized shifts in disease burden and identifying emerging areas of concern. The results underscore the necessity for context-specific, multisectoral strategies that integrate WASH (Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene), nutrition, and maternal–child health interventions. As Karnataka continues to implement and expand health initiatives under the National Health Mission, POSHAN Abhiyaan, and Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS), insights from spatial epidemiology can serve as an essential decision-support tool to optimize resource allocation and monitor program impact.

Given the dynamic nature of DD distribution, periodic spatial surveillance and integration of real-time health data at the district level are recommended. Future studies could benefit from incorporating climatic, behavioral, and service delivery variables to more accurately model and predict the distribution of diarrhoeal risk. Such integrated spatial approaches are essential to support India’s broader commitment to achieving SDG-3 targets and reducing preventable child deaths.

While this study offers important insights into the spatial distribution of DD among under-five children, a few limitations are mentioned. First, the analysis is based on district-level aggregated data, which may mask intra-district disparities and household-level heterogeneity. Although the NFHS provides data at both the district and PSU levels, this study did not explore sub-district clustering or account for urban–rural stratification, which may have yielded finer spatial insights. Second, temporal comparisons between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 should be interpreted with caution, as differences in the timing of data collection and potential seasonal variation in diarrhoeal incidence could influence prevalence estimates. Additionally, recall bias may have affected reported diarrhoea episodes. Despite these limitations, the findings offer valuable district-level guidance. Future research will aim to incorporate finer spatial scales, adjust for Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP), and integrate environmental and service delivery variables to enhance the precision and programmatic relevance of DD mapping.

Conclusion

Despite notable improvements in infrastructure and nutrition programs, DD remains a persistent threat to child health in Karnataka. Identification of spatial hotspots enables the formulation of targeted, district-specific strategies rather than adopting uniform statewide approaches. Strengthening sanitation, safe water access, and child nutrition-especially in high-burden regions-will be critical in reducing diarrhoeal morbidity. In addition to existing national schemes, region-specific interventions, localized resource allocation, and culturally appropriate awareness campaigns are essential. Integrating spatial intelligence into public health planning will enhance surveillance, optimize interventions, and contribute to reducing the diarrhoea burden among under-five children more effectively.
