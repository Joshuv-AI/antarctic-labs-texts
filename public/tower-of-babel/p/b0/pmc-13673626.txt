A novel biological interpretation of neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with allergic rhinitis: insights from brain functional connectivity gradient and serum multi-omics analysis
Authors: Ting Yang, Tongpeng Chu, Xiaoyu Song, Hanrui Wang, Yao Wang, Ning Mao, Kaili Che, Fanghui Dong, Wanchen Liu, Anan Li, Chao Ren, Yakui Mou, Xicheng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe “nose-brain axis” has been proposed as a key mechanism linking allergic rhinitis (AR) to central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction; however, alterations in functional connectivity (FC) gradients remain unexplored in AR. This cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the correlation between brain FC gradients and AR-related peripheral multi-omics profiles, as well as their clinical significance, through multimodal cross scale analysis.MethodsWe enrolled cross-scale data from 22 AR patients and 20 healthy controls (HCs), including resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI), serum proteomics and metabolomics, and clinical assessments. FC gradient analysis was employed to investigate hierarchical brain functional organization, with gradient values compared at global, regional, and network levels. Multivariate partial least squares (PLS) analysis was used to integrate the multidimensional associations among FC gradients, peripheral molecular signatures, and behavioral phenotypes.ResultsNo significant differences were observed at the global or Yeo’s seven canonical networks. However, refined regional analysis revealed significant FC gradient alterations predominantly within the Default mode (DMN), Somatomotor (SMN), and Limbic (LN) networks, particularly in RH_Default_Temp_5 (p = 0.0031) and LH_Limbic_OFC_4 (p = 0.0034). These regional gradient abnormalities correlated significantly with AR severity and neuropsychiatric symptoms (p < 0.05), indicating “local fine-tuning” rather than “global collapse” in brain functional remodeling. Multi-omic profiling identified 121 differentially expressed proteins, 197 positive-ion-mode metabolites, 134 negative-ion-mode metabolites. The integrative analysis demonstrated significant associations between these peripheral molecular signatures and AR-related FC gradient alterations. These differentially expressed molecules were primarily enriched in immune response, complement activation, lipids and lipid-like molecules, and cholesterol metabolism pathways.ConclusionThis study provides the first evidence of distinct FC gradient alterations in AR that are coupled to peripheral multi-omic shifts, offering novel insights into CNS mechanisms of AR and identifying potential molecular-neuroimaging biomarkers candidates for precision diagnosis and targeted therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02602-x.

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a highly prevalent chronic inflammatory disorder of the upper airway, affecting approximately 10-40% of the global population. Classified as a type I hypersensitivity reaction mediated by immunoglobulin E (IgE), the primary symptoms include nasal congestion, nasal itching, and sneezing. Increasing evidence suggests AR can lead to sleep disorders, anxiety, depression, olfactory dysfunction, cognitive impairment, memory loss [1–5]. Research has shown that 36.5% of AR patients experience sleep disorders, and AR is associated with various poor sleep parameters [3], and sleep disorders are also somewhat correlated with the severity of AR nasal symptoms [6]. Additionally, the prevalence of anxiety and depression in AR patients reaches 25%―significantly higher than that in normal individuals [4]―and AR is associated with an increased risk of anxiety and depression [7]. A previous study has also shown that, olfactory thresholds were significantly higher in AR patients than in control subjects [8]. The occurrence of these disorders may exacerbate AR or impair its prognosis, forming a vicious cycle that severely reduces the quality of life of patients and impose a heavy burden on the patient’s family and society. Traditionally, these symptoms have been regarded as mere comorbidities or secondary consequences of nasal congestion; however, accumulating evidence indicates that they reflect fundamental pathophysiological alterations driven by AR-related immune-inflammatory processes that directly inflict damage upon the nervous system. In 2012, Van Gerven et al. have speculated that there may be potential “nose-brain communication” mechanisms in AR that rely on neuro-immunity [9]. Studies have confirmed that allergen-induced IgE-mediated inflammation causes sustained neuroinflammation and neuronal damage in the olfactory bulb, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex, leading to persistent olfactory dysfunction, cognitive deficits, and anxiety- and depression-like behaviors [10–13]. Additionally, IL-4-mediated microglial inflammation in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC) contributes to central sensitization in AR [14]. Furthermore, AR impairs working memory through disrupted hippocampal-prefrontal functional coupling [15]. Neuroimaging studies have since corroborated altered resting-state spontaneous brain activity in AR patients, including hypoactivity in the precuneus (PCUN) and hyperactivity of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [16]. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging, Ina Callebaut et al. further observed activation of different brain regions in AR patients upon nasal histamine provocation, such as the right anterior insula, right middle occipital gyrus, right middle/superior frontal gyrus, and left cerebellum [17]. This evidence supports the “nose-brain axis” as a novel framework for understanding AR’s effects on brain structure, function, and behavior, offering new strategies for treatment, symptom relief, and precision diagnosis.

The human brain operates through interconnected networks whose disruptions manifest as neurological or psychiatric symptoms. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI)-based functional connectivity (FC) gradient analysis reveals the brain’s hierarchical functional organization, offering advantages over traditional discrete network methods. This approach has identified disease-specific FC gradient changes in conditions including mild cognitive impairment (MCI), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), autism, and depression [18–22]. Notably, FC gradient and connectivity analyses are well-established in olfaction research [23, 24]. In the context of nasal inflammatory conditions, altered FC has been observed in patients with sinonasal inflammation [25] and chronic rhinosinusitis with olfactory dysfunction [26]. Despite increasing interest in AR-related brain alterations, previous studies have focused on symptomatic observations or regional activity changes, while FC gradients remain unexplored [16]. Investigating FC gradient changes in AR may provide a novel framework for understanding the “nose-brain axis” mechanisms.

Proteomics and metabolomics provide new perspectives for interpreting AR mechanisms because AR is a systemic inflammatory disease characterized by IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation, complement activation, and lipid mediator release-processes that directly alter the serum proteome and metabolome. This makes blood-based multi-omics particularly suited for precise AR endotyping and treatment response prediction. Prior proteomic analyses in AR have identified differentially expressed proteins enriched in coagulation pathway [27], with potential utility as biomarkers for early monitoring and immunotherapy efficacy prediction [28], including altered levels of lactoferrin (LTF), Nipsnap homolog 3 A (NHP3A), and S100A7 (Psoriasin protein) [29–31]. Metabolomic studies have revealed disrupted lipid and organic acid pathways [32], with three lipid metabolism-related genes (LPCAT1, SGPP1, SMARCD3) showing diagnostic potential [33]. Critically, these AR-characteristic molecular perturbations—particularly complement activation and lipid dysregulation།are not merely peripheral inflammatory markers but have been implicated in neuroinflammation, microglial activation, and synaptic dysfunction in the central nervous system (CNS) [34–36]. Despite advancing AR pathophysiology understanding, clinical translation of AR’s proteomic and metabolomic discoveries remains nascent, with scattered research lacking systematic integration and large-scale validation. Integrative multi-omics approaches, particularly combining these peripheral molecular signatures with neuroimaging, are essential for AR research because they directly test whether AR-specific inflammatory profiles map onto distinct patterns of brain network disorganization. This approach also mechanistically links peripheral disease processes to central neuropsychiatric manifestations through the “nose-brain axis”, providing a powerful framework for elucidating AR symptom mechanisms, intersystem regulatory networks, and novel therapeutic targets.

In this study, we collected a cross-scale cohort of serum protein and metabolomics sequencing, magnetic resonance imaging, psychological emotion, and symptom data from 22 AR patients and 20 healthy controls (HCs) to investigate the relationship between individual proteomics and metabolomics variations and brain FC gradient changes in AR patients, as well as the underlying biological mechanisms. We hypothesized that, (1) AR patients and HCs differ in FC gradients; (2) these differences correlate with symptom severity and psychological status; (3) regional FC gradients associate with disease-related proteins and metabolites. The goal was to characterize brain network abnormalities and explore potential biological mechanisms in AR.

Participants and clinical assessment

This study recruited a total of 22 AR patients. In addition, 20 HCs were recruited from the local community, who were matched to AR patients by sex, age, and body mass index (BMI). Inclusion criteria for AR patients: (1) A history of AR symptoms lasting > 2 years, with clinical manifestations conforming to the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Allergic Rhinitis (Revised 2022 Edition); (2) A positive allergic response to one or more of the six allergens, including dermatophagoides farinae, alternaria, artemisia annua pollen, humulus pollen, cat hair and poplar tree pollen determined via skin prick test and/or serum IgE radioallergosorbent test (RAST; QML Pathology) exceeded the reference range; (3) Right-handed and native Han Chinese; (4) Age between 18 and 65 years; (5) No history of psychotropic medication use including antidepressants, mood stabilizers, and antipsychotics; (6) No magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination contraindications and abnormalities in MRI structure imaging. Exclusion criteria for AR patients: (1) Clinically uncontrolled active infections (e.g., acute upper respiratory tract infections); (2) Treatment with glucocorticoids, antihistamines, leukotrienes, or decongestants within previous one week; (3) Comorbid systemic immune disorders or other systemic autoimmune diseases that required treatment or were under active control; (4) Sinus surgery within the past 6 months; (5) A personal or first-degree family history of mental illness; (6) Severe somatic diseases or neurological disorders confirmed by medical history or laboratory test; (7) History of brain tumors or injuries; (8) History of major medical illness; (9) Substance abuse; (10) Left-handed; (11) Pregnant or breastfeeding; (12) MRI contraindications (e.g., claustrophobia), or inability to undergo MRI scanning; (13) Visible nasal polyps, polypoid mucosal changes, or chronic rhinosinusitis. Inclusion criteria for HCs: (1) No history of AR and negative results for the six aforementioned allergens involving Dermatophagoides farinae; (2) No history mental illness; (3) No use of antidepressants or other antipsychotics. The exclusion criteria for the HC group are identical to those of the AR group. All participants underwent screening at the Yantai Yuhuangding hospital for evaluation of allergen sensitization and clinical characteristics. Eligible participants were instructed to cease use of all intranasal and immune modulating medications for 14 days before their follow-up visit. At follow-up, participants provided blood samples and completed symptom surveys in the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery at Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yuhuangding Hospital (Approval No. 2024 − 574) and abided by the ethical guidelines of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. All the subjects were fully informed of the study procedures, and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Symptom analysis

The severity of AR symptoms as well as neurological and psychiatric issues such as sleep and mood of all subjects were evaluated through a series of self-reported symptom surveys completed prior to sample collection. The AR-specific symptoms severity was investigated by the Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ). Sleep quality, anxiety, depression severity and olfactory function of participants were evaluated by Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), and Olfactory Function Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), respectively.

Blood sample collection and MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Intravenous blood from the upper forearm was drawn from each participant. Blood samples were collected on the same day as MRI scanning, without a requirement for the exact collection time and fasting. Blood samples were centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to extract serum, which was stored at -80 °C for subsequent omics analysis.

All subjects in the discovery cohort were scanned with a 3.0 Tesla General Electric Discovery MR750W scanner (Discovery 750 W, GE Healthcare, USA) equipped with an eight-channel receiver array head coil. T2 flair imaging of the brain was performed for disease diagnosis. And the rs-fMRI images were acquired. The image acquisition parameters are provided in Supplemental information.

The preprocessing of resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data included slice-timing correction, motion correction, normalization, delinear trending, temporal bandpass filtering, nuisance regression, and smoothing. The details of image preprocessing, tractography, and quality control are provided in Supplemental information.

Functional connectivity gradient analysis

From the perspective of the functional subnetwork, in order to compare gradients between networks, the 400 regions of interest (ROIs) were assigned to Yeo’s seven functional networks [37]: Visual network (VN), Somatomotor network (SMN), Dorsal Attention network (DAN), Ventral Attention network (VAN), Limbic network (LN), Frontoparietal network (FPN) and Default mode network (DMN). We also compared the differences in functional gradient values of 400 ROIs between the AR group and HCs in detail.

Proteomic and metabolomic analysis

Serum proteomic and metabolomic profiling were performed using LC-MS/MS-based approaches. For proteomics, serum proteins were extracted, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by DIA mass spectrometry (Vanquish Neo UHPLC-Orbitrap Astral) with identification against the human UniProt database using DIA-NN software. DEPs were defined by fold-change ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 0.83 with p < 0.05. For metabolomics, serum metabolites were extracted by cold methanol precipitation and analyzed by untargeted UHPLC-MS/MS (Vanquish UHPLC-Orbitrap Q Exactive HF) in positive and negative ion modes. Differential metabolites were identified by Compound Discoverer 3.3 and screened by PLS-DA (VIP > 1.0, fold-change > 1.5 or < 0.667, p < 0.05). Functional enrichment analyses for both proteomics and metabolomics were conducted using Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and other relevant databases.

Associations between proteome, metabolome and functional connectivity gradient

Multivariate partial least squares (PLS) correlation analysis [38] was performed to examine associations between differences in protein and metabolomics (that is, normalized count) of the serum and FC gradient.

Additional materials and methods were mentioned in detail in the Supplementary information.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the sample

A total of 22 AR patients (4 female, age 40.590 ± 10.055 years) and 20 HCs (2 female, age 37.900 ± 7.196 years) were included in the analysis. The demographic and clinical characteristics of these participants are shown in Table 1. There was no significant difference in distribution of age (p = 0.329), sex (p = 0.753) or BMI (p = 0.743) between AR and HC groups. Patients with AR had higher scores on RQLQ (p < 0.05), PSQI (p < 0.05), HAMD (p < 0.05), and HAMA (p < 0.001), and significantly lower VAS scores (p < 0.05) compared with the HC group (Supplementary Fig. S1). These findings indicate that AR patients experience neurological and psychiatric comorbidities (sleep disorders, anxiety, depression), impaired olfactory function, and reduced quality of life.

Functional connectivity gradient differences between AR and HC groups

The spatial distribution of FC gradients for all participants and between-group differences are displayed in Fig. 2. We compared the FC gradient between AR and HCs on two gradients. The average FC gradient scores for 22 unrelated AR individuals is shown in Fig. 2A, and the average gradient scores for 20 unrelated individuals in the HC group is shown in Fig. 2B. We found that the span of FC gradient is large and unevenly distributed, and there are significant differences across cortical regions. The inter-group difference map is shown in Fig. 2C. While global comparisons showed no significant between-group differences for Gradient 1 (p = 0.3884) or Gradient 2 (p = 0.9144) (Supplementary Fig. S2), and Yeo’s seven functional networks showed no significant differences (Fig. 2D, E), refined regional analysis revealed altered FC gradients in 30 ROIs (Fig. 2F, G). Specifically, Gradient 1 differences were concentrated in DMN, VAN, and LN, with notable changes in RH_Default_Temp_5, LH_Limbic_OFC_4, and RH_Limbic_TempPole_4 (Table 2A). Gradient 2 differences were mainly found in SMN, FPN, and LN, particularly in LH_Limbic_OFC4 (Table 2B).

Differentially expressed proteins and their function-related metabolites in AR and HC groups

Proteomic analysis identified 2,104 proteins (1,671 in AR, 1,628 in HCs), with 121 differentially expressed in AR (86 up-regulated, 35 down-regulated) (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Metabolomic analysis identified 1,593 positive and 1,174 negative ion mode metabolites, with 197 positive (142 up, 55 down) and 134 negative (29 up, 105 down) differentially expressed in AR (Supplementary Fig. S3B, C).

Proteins and metabolites expression covaries with brain functional connectivity gradient

Multivariate PLS analysis revealed significant cross-scale associations between serum protein/metabolite expression and brain FC gradients in both groups (p < 0.05, Bonferroni correction across 10 latent components (LCs); see details in the Supplementary Methods). In Gradient 1, the first LC (LC1) accounted for 26.4% of the proteins-FC covariance and described positive correlations between individual-level protein expression composite scores and brain FC composite scores in AR patients (r = 0.526) and HCs (r = 0.591) (Fig. 4A). The description results of positive and negative-ion-mode metabolites in Gradient 1 and 2 and proteins in Gradient 2 were detailed in Supplementary Materials (Fig. 4B, E, F, I, J). Regional contributions (z-scores, FDR corrected) showed distinct patterns: in AR, Gradient 1 positively correlated with bilateral precentral/postcentral, inferior frontal, and temporal cortices, but negatively with precuneus, cuneus, and occipital cortices (Fig. 4C); Gradient 2 showed positive associations in frontal/parietal regions and negative in occipital/cuneus (Fig. 4C). HC patterns differed (Fig. 4D). Network-level analysis revealed significant between-group differences in SMN and DAN (Gradient 1), and SMN, DAN, VAN, and DMN (Gradient 2) (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Metabolite-FC regional contributions are detailed in Supplementary Materials (Fig. 4G, H, K, L).

Enrichment pathways associated with altered functional connectivity gradient in AR

To gain further insights into the functions and pathway associations of the associated differentially expressed proteins obtained in the previous step which may be involved in the brain function changes in AR patients, GO and KEGG analyses were performed. In both gradients, GO analysis revealed enrichment in humoral immune response and complement activation (Fig. 5A, B). KEGG analysis showed significant up-regulation of complement and coagulation cascades and down-regulation of cholesterol metabolism and PPAR signaling pathways (Fig. 5C, D). We explored the functional and pathway associations of differentially expressed metabolites using the same method, but we did not enrich the corresponding pathways in GO and KEGG analyses. Next, we performed enrichment analysis on the identified differential metabolites using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). Metabolite enrichment (HMDB database) revealed that both positive and negative ion-mode metabolites were primarily annotated to lipids and lipid-like molecules and organic acids and derivatives pathways in both gradients (Fig. 5E-H), confirming serum metabolite differences between AR patients and HCs.

Discussion

This study integrates blood multi-omics and neuroimaging to identify proteomic and metabolomic correlates of brain FC gradients in AR, providing novel insights into its central nervous system mechanisms.

This study utilized rs-fMRI data and applied a novel FC gradient approach to investigate the hierarchical structure of the cerebral cortex in ARs and HCs. Two FC gradients were identified capturing distinct macroscopic dimensions of brain organization. We employed FC gradient analysis to assess changes in the FC gradients of patients with AR at global, regional and network levels, and analyzed correlations between regional gradient alterations and clinical data at the individual participant level. In this study, we quantified the differences in FC gradients between brain regions of AR and HC participants. No significant between-group differences were observed at the global level or across Yeo’s seven resting-state networks, suggesting that the overall macroscale architecture remains intact in AR. However, refined network-level analyses revealed significant gradient alterations localized predominantly within the DMN, VAN, LN and FPN. These regional gradient abnormalities correlated significantly with clinical scores-including the RQLQ, PSQI, HAMA, HAMD, and VAS།indicating that AR induces “local fine-tuning” rather than “global collapse” in in brain functional remodeling. This selective microcircuit disruption may reflect low-grade inflammation effects of the “nose-brain axis” and holds potential for early diagnosis and symptom prediction. Critically, individual variations in serum protein and metabolite levels were significantly correlated with FC gradient alterations in AR patients. Proteins associated with these gradient changes were enriched in humoral immune response and complement activation pathways, with up-regulated of the complement/coagulation cascades and down-regulated of cholesterol metabolism, whereas metabolites were predominantly annotated to lipids and organic acid pathways. These cross-scale associations align with established links between specific brain networks and neuropsychiatric symptoms: the DMN, FPN, and SMN with depression; the DMN, FPN, and VN with sleep disorders; and the DAN, FPN, and DMN with anxiety [39–46]. Immune-inflammatory responses play a key role in AR pathogenesis. Anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders are linked to immune inflammatory responses [1, 47, 48]. Notably, the complement/coagulation cascade།a hallmark of allergic reactions [49]།correlated with FC gradients and has been implicated in depression [50], anxiety [51], and sleep apnea [52], while dysregulated lipid/cholesterol metabolism has been implicated in both AR pathogenesis [53] and neuropsychiatric disorders [54–57]. Collectively, these convergent lines of evidence suggest that peripheral immune-metabolic dysregulation may propagate centrally via the nose-brain axis, contributing to the network-level functional alterations observed in AR.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate neuroimaging FC gradient mapping with serum proteomic and metabolomic profiling in AR, offering a novel framework for exploring the variability of FC coupling and advancing understanding of the central nervous system mechanisms underlying AR. Previous studies have identified abnormal regional brain activity in AR (e.g., within the PCUN) but have not employed FC gradient methods [12, 13, 16, 17]. By compressing whole-brain connections into two principal gradient axes, we constructed a continuous “neural topographic map” and demonstrated that AR patients exhibit distinct regional FC gradient disturbances that correlate with anxiety, depressive symptoms, and sleep impairment-further supporting the notion that “nose-brain axis” imbalance serves as a core driver of neuropsychiatric complications in AR.

Multi-omics research is driving AR from “empirical medicine” toward “precision medicine”, and our findings provide a concrete molecular-neuroimaging framework for this transition. Specifically, the convergence of complement pathway activation, lipid/cholesterol metabolic imbalance, and FC gradient disruptions within the DMN, FPN, DAN, and SMN suggests a novel neuroinflammatory endotype of AR-one characterized by peripheral immune།metabolic dysregulation that predisposes patients to comorbid anxiety, depression, sleep disorders, and olfactory dysfunction. This endotype carries direct clinical implications for precision management. First, the identified protein-metabolite signatures།which are significantly correlated with specific FC gradient patterns།could serve as biopsy biomarkers for early identification of AR patients at elevated risk for central nervous system complications, enabling preemptive interventions before overt neuropsychiatric symptoms emerge. Second, the molecular pathways we identified are pharmacologically tractable།complement inhibitors (e.g., C3a/C5a receptor antagonists) [58–60] and lipid-regulating agents (e.g., statins, PCSK9 inhibitors) [61, 62] are already in clinical use for other indications and could be repurposed as adjunctive therapies alongside conventional anti-allergic treatment to mitigate central nervous system sequelae in high-risk AR patients.

Our research also has limitations and precautions, and several considerations should be taken into account when interpreting our results. First, blood-derived proteomics/metabolomics may not fully reflect brain tissue expression due to potential-central heterogeneity. While this constrains our ability to infer direct blood-brain molecular links, future studies could leverage single-cell transcriptomics and spatial multi-omics to profile cell-type-specific expression patterns in nasal mucosa and brain tissue, thereby refining the molecular cartography of the nose-brain axis at cellular resolution. Second, brain phenotypes were only acquired on the basis of rs-fMRI imaging. Indeed, the integration of multimodal imaging data, such as structural MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, and positron emission tomography (PET)/single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), offers superior pathological and functional characterization of brain disorders compared with any single modality in isolation [63, 64]. Future research can integrate multiple sequences and methods to explore the potential associations more deeply. Third, blood collection lacked standardized timing and fasting controls. Fourth, the small sample size and high male proportion limit generalizability. Previous studies have shown that men, smoking, and alcohol consumption are associated with AR [65] and neurological and psychiatric symptoms [66]. Although we matched each group based on gender, future research should use larger sample sizes and more comprehensive indicators than those used in this study to examine the impact of gender and daily habits on regional FC gradients. Fifth, this study used the VAS to assess olfactory function rather than standardized olfactory tests such as Sniffin’ Sticks or the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT). While VAS is convenient to administer and clinically practical, it is inherently subjective and lacks objective quantification of olfactory threshold, discrimination, and identification capacities. This may introduce bias or information loss in the assessment of olfactory dysfunction. Future studies should incorporate validated psychophysical tests for standardized olfactory assessment to more accurately characterize the severity of olfactory impairment and its neurobiological correlates. Sixth, this study did not incorporate other omics data, such as transcriptomics and microRNA. Gene transcription underlies protein and metabolic processes, and microRNA has emerged as a research hotspot in recent years. Tracing gene and microRNA expression holds diagnostic value and therapeutic application prospects for AR. Seventh, the identified cross-scale associations identified cannot establish causal or sequential relationships. Novel computational approaches, such as sparse generalized canonical correlation analysis for multi-block modeling, may provide further evidence to clarify interactions among proteomics, metabolomics, neuroimaging and behavioral phenotypes. Finally, this study employed a cross-sectional design. Future work should validate the reliability of our findings using longitudinal data (e.g., pre-treatment and post-treatment) as potential targets for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention. Addressing these challenges will demand concerted efforts in multi-center cohort building, interdisciplinary collaboration, and the development of robust, interpretable machine learning frameworks for multi-modal data integration. Only through such systematic endeavors can the nose-brain axis signatures we have identified be successfully translated from discovery to routine clinical practice.

In conclusion, we identified associations between blood protein and metabolic expression and brain FC gradient in AR patients at the individual patient level, providing neuropsychiatric features of the disease, offering a new perspective for imaging neuroscience, and providing new evidence for understanding the central nervous mechanism of AR. Establishing specific brain FC gradient features and peripheral multi-omics correlations is expected to construct potential biomarkers and target brain regions for improving AR diagnosis and evaluation, and recommend early intervention and treatment of AR related brain symptoms.
