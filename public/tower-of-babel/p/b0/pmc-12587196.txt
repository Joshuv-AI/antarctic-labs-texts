Facility-Based Uptake of Colorectal Cancer Screening in 45- to 49-Year-Olds After US Guideline Changes
Authors: Alyssa H. Harris, Hannah R. Murphy, Madeleine McDowell, Margaret E. Wright, M. Courtney Hughes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This cohort study examines facility-based uptake of colorectal cancer screening among adults aged 45 to 49 years after changes in US guidelines.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the US.1 Although incidence among adults aged 65 years and older has declined, rates among those younger than 50 years increased 2.4% annually between 2012 and 2021.2 In response, the ACS in 20183 and USPSTF in 20214 recommended screening at age 45 years for average-risk individuals, which expanded eligibility to approximately 20 million adults aged 45 to 49 years; however, absolute uptake remains low.5 Although increases in screening have been noted among privately insured individuals, little facility-based, all-payer evidence exists on the impact of guideline changes.5

Methods

This cohort study evaluated CRC screening encounters using the Vizient Clinical Data Base, which includes administrative data from more than 1350 US hospitals.6 CRC screening encounters among individuals aged 45 to 75 years between January 2016 and December 2024 were identified (eTables 1 and 2 in Supplement 1). This study was determined to be exempt by the Northern Illinois University institutional review board. Data from the Vizient Clinical Data Base were used by permission of Vizient, Inc (all rights reserved). Results reporting adhered to the STROBE reporting guidelines.

The study periods were prechange (January 2016 to May 2018), interim (June 2018 to May 2021), and postchange (June 2021 to December 2023), corresponding to ACS and USPSTF guideline updates. Primary outcomes were the proportion of individuals aged 45 to 49 years among all screenings in those aged 45 to 75 years and change in monthly volume. Secondary outcomes included sociodemographic screening patterns, including race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic vulnerability. The Vizient Vulnerability Index, which is derived from zip code–level factors (income, education, and health care access), was used to assess socioeconomic vulnerability. Race and ethnicity were collected from the electronic health record via patient self-report. χ2 Tests were used for categorical variables, and analysis of variance was used for continuous variables, including postchange comparisons between individuals aged 45 to 49 years and those aged 50 to 75 years. Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < .05. Analyses were performed in SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Among 7 802 606 CRC screenings, the proportion involving individuals aged 45 to 49 years increased from 2.9% (prechange period) to 4.3% (interim) to 17.8% (postchange period). In the postchange period, most screened individuals aged 45 to 49 years were female (57.0%), non-Hispanic White (53.3%), commercially insured (74.2%), and from average vulnerability neighborhoods (59.5%). Compared with the prechange period, the postchange period included fewer non-Hispanic Black individuals (−4.1%), Medicare enrollees (−2.3%), and individuals from high-vulnerability neighborhoods (−1.6%), while Hispanic representation increased by 2.8% (Table). Discussion

The findings of this cohort study suggest that facility-based CRC screening among adults aged 45 to 49 years increased nearly 10-fold following USPSTF guideline changes, far exceeding growth among those aged 50 to 75 years. This rapid adoption highlights early implementation by practitioners and patients. Although screening rates among adults aged 50 to 75 years increased during the study period, the accelerated growth among those aged 45 to 49 years reflected guideline integration into facility practices. Increases during the interim period across both groups may reflect public health campaigns and early uptake of the ACS recommendation before USPSTF changes.

This study was limited to facility-based screening, not capturing at-home tests or hospital type. Variation in coding and underrepresentation of smaller or rural hospitals may affect generalizability. Study periods were aligned with key guideline changes, but relatively short intervals may limit assessment of longer-term patterns.

These findings reflect CRC screening in hospitals, where guideline adoption is critical to shaping clinical practice and building momentum toward broader public health impact. Continued efforts to sustain this momentum may help optimize uptake. Although opportunities remain to address disparities and investigate at-home screening, this highlights the early success of facility-based guideline implementation.
