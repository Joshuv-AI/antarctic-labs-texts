Background

Implementation of evidence-based practices into routine clinical care within a health system remains a challenge. Rigorous evaluation of implementation efforts with data collection guided by an implementation science framework can provide significant insight into variation in outcomes across the health system as well as learnings related to the contextual factors that may contribute to both clinical and implementation outcomes. To improve the uptake of research evidence into practice at the health system level, our large, integrated health system applied for and was accepted into an innovative program and learning collaborative created by the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI), the Health Systems Implementation Initiative(HSII) [1]. Supported in part by the PCORI HSII, our health system will implement two separate programs addressing two chronic diseases- obesity and uncontrolled hypertension (HTN) - in all of our Northeast Ohio primary care practices. Addressing these two clinical topics is aligned with health system priorities and health system operational leaders support participation in the 2 implementation projects. We report here our implementation and evaluation protocol and plan for both programs.

The objective of the first project titled Implementation of Intensive Lifestyle Treatment for Weight Loss in Primary Care Settings is to optimize the availability of treatment of obesity, defined as body mass index >/= 30 kg/m2, in the adult population of patients who receive care within our health system primary care practices in Northeast Ohio. Utilizing the group visit model from the evidence generated from the Befort et al. PCORI funded study [2], we will significantly expand current health system obesity treatment resources and build on our health system model of shared medical appointments (SMAs) led by multidisciplinary teams to deliver the intervention. The objective of the second project titled Implementation of Effective Hypertension Management Approaches is to improve blood pressure control utilizing home blood pressure monitoring for patients with a diagnosis of HTN and uncontrolled blood pressure, defined as a blood pressure reading of > 150/95 mmHg, based upon evidence generated from the Margolis et al. PCORI funded Hyperlink 3 study [3], also in all Northeast Ohio primary care practices in our health system.

Methods

These implementation projects and evaluation protocols were both approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board.

Program descriptions

Intensive lifestyle treatment for weight loss

The obesity SMA program will be rolled out utilizing a randomized stepped wedge design to all 56 health system primary care practice sites in Northeast Ohio. After the initial 9-month pre-implementation period, the rollout will occur in 3 steps, with the 56 practices combined to 27 practice groups as the unit of analysis to ensure at least 1000 eligible patients per practice group. Each step will include rollout to 9 practice groups at a time and will occur every 3 months over 9 months. The practice groups will be in the active implementation phase for 18 months, then in maintenance phase for 6 months, with the last group completing the maintenance phase by month 39, 3 months before the project period ends (Fig. 1, Panel A).

Project timelines

Eligible patients will be identified through the electronic medical record (EMR) with an our practice advisory (OPA, formerly best practice alert or BPA). The entry into the obesity treatment program will be offered by a patient’s primary care provider (PCP). Once the referral to the comprehensive, multidisciplinary program is ordered, the patient will complete an individual appointment, offered as both in-person or virtual, which will occur with an obesity-trained PCP, an endocrinology provider, or a Wellness Medicine provider. If the patient accepts the referral to the SMA, the first appointment will be scheduled. We anticipate 10% of those attending the individual appointment will choose an SMA program.

The SMA intervention will then be conducted for 18 months, with weekly sessions for the first 3 months, biweekly sessions for the next 3 months, then monthly sessions for 12 months. Patients will have the option for a “make-up” session. In Months 1–3, an obesity-trained primary care or endocrinology provider or advanced practice provider (APP), a Wellness Medicine provider, or psychologist will lead each weekly SMA. A dietician and/or an exercise physiologist will be present up to 1–2 times a month with an endocrinology provider. In months 4–6, the biweekly sessions will be led by an obesity-trained primary care or endocrinology provider or psychologist, alternating with a Culinary Medicine cooking class SMA with a Wellness Medicine provider. In months 7–18, each monthly SMA session will be led by an obesity-trained primary care or endocrinology provider, a Wellness Medicine provider, or psychologist. Dieticians and exercise physiologists will join up to every 2–3 months with an endocrinology provider. Social workers will also be available throughout the program on a referral basis to address patients’ social needs.

Implementation of effective HTN management approaches

The intensive HTN management program will also be rolled out utilizing a randomized stepped wedge design to all 56 health system primary care practice sites in Northeast Ohio. After the initial 12-month pre-implementation period, the rollout will occur in 3 steps, with the 56 practices combined into 21 practice groups as the unit of analysis to ensure at least 100 eligible patients per practice group. Each step will include rollout to 7 practice groups at a time and will occur over 4 months each over a total of 12 months. The practice groups will be in the active implementation phase for 24 months, with the last group in active implementation for a full 12 months, then all groups will be in maintenance phase for 9 months, with the last group completing the maintenance phase by month 45, three months before the project period ends (Fig. 1, Panel B).

We will identify eligible patients through the EMR and the referral to the intensive HTN management program will be made by the PCP for an intake appointment, in-person or virtual if preferred by the patient, to be scheduled with an APP or pharmacist within 2 weeks. As part of the referral order there will be a question addressing whether the patient has a home blood pressure monitor. At the time of the intake appointment, the patients who do not have a home blood pressure monitor will be offered a device and training to use the device. For patients who have a home blood pressure monitor, we will confirm this is an arm blood pressure monitor and a validated device [4] and will ensure understanding of how to measure blood pressure at home [5].

After program enrollment, patients will be provided a standard blood pressure log book (notebook) and will be asked to measure their blood pressure once a day, with 2 readings 2 min apart, with a minimum of 4 days of readings a week [6]. A pharmacist or APP will conduct phone or virtual appointment follow-up every 2–4 weeks (with an in-person appointment option if preferred by the patient), during which patient education will be provided, including reinforcement of lifestyle modifications related to diet and exercise, medication adherence reviewed, and medication adjustments recommended based upon the report of home blood pressure readings, and with communication with the PCP. Regarding treatment protocol and decisions, the pharmacists and APPs in discussion with the patient’s PCP as required will follow accepted guidelines [7] and recommendations will be individualized to each patient. The follow-up appointments will occur until ≥ 75% of the home blood pressure readings are < 135/85 mmHg, as in the Margolis et al. study [3].

Patients will be offered 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring at 3, 6, and 12 months after enrollment if the blood pressures remain uncontrolled. Routine follow up care with the PCP will be continued throughout the active implementation and maintenance phases of the project. The primary effectiveness outcome will be the change in systolic blood pressure at 12 months. Patients will be asked to bring their home blood pressure cuff to their in-person appointment at baseline and at least once a year to verify that readings obtained from the home and office monitor are within +/- 5 mmHg. Referral to a hypertension specialist will be considered if blood pressure is resistant to treatment with at least 3 medications.

Care delivery setting for implementation

The care delivery settings where our program will be implemented include 56 primary care clinic sites including all Internal Medicine and Family Medicine practices of the Cleveland Clinic health system in Northeast Ohio. These are primarily community practices with 1 hospital-based practice. The 56 primary care sites include 47 suburban practices, 8 urban practices, and 1 rural practice.

Patient population

Intensive lifestyle treatment for weight loss

Our target population will be primary care patients aged 20–75 years and a Body Mass Index (BMI) >/=30 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria based upon unique clinical situations affecting BMI as well as conditions that may affect SMA participation will include a prior history of bariatric surgery, current pregnancy, current cancer diagnosis, or cognitive impairment. Prior participation in an obesity treatment SMA will not be an exclusion for participation in the proposed program.

Implementation of effective HTN management approaches

Our target population will be primary care patients ages 18–85, who have at least 2 health system encounters with a HTN diagnosis within the past 24 months, including an encounter within the past 12 months at their PCP’s assigned location, had a documented systolic blood pressure of > 150 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of > 95 at the most recent encounter at the primary care location, and a previous encounter within the health system within the past 24-month qualifying time frame with a documented systolic blood pressure of > 150 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of > 95mmHg. As in the Margolis et al. study,3 patients who are pregnant, have stage 5 chronic kidney disease (or end stage renal disease), or enrolled in hospice care will be excluded.

Logic models

The Logic Models for both studies incorporate the RE-AIM framework [8, 9]. Our implementation approach will utilize a combination of implementation strategies [10–12].

For our Intensive Lifestyle Treatment for Weight Loss project (Fig. 2, Panel A), we start with a health system context of a large population of patients with obesity who are at risk for development and progression of obesity-related comorbidities and with no current obesity treatment options that are implemented across all primary care delivery sites. We propose as our intervention the Obesity SMA program, a group visit program currently delivered by a subset of providers in our health system, to be widely implemented across all Northeast Ohio primary care practices. Our planned implementation strategies will map to each of the components of the RE-AIM framework [8, 9] and will impact both implementation and effectiveness outcomes. The long-term impact will be sustained, improved penetration of obesity programs within all health system primary care practices, and for our patients-weight loss maintenance, improved control of cardiovascular risk factors, reduced acute healthcare utilization, and improved experience.

Logic models

For our Implementation of Effective HTN Management Approaches project, our Logic Model (Fig. 2, Panel B) shows our health system context of our patients with uncontrolled HTN who are at risk for cardiovascular disease. The intervention we propose is the Intensive HTN management program to be delivered to our primary care patients in Northeast Ohio by pharmacists and APPs in our health system. Our implementation strategies address each component of the RE-AIM framework [8, 13] and will contribute to the measured effectiveness and implementation outcomes. We expect the long-term impact of improved and sustained blood pressure control for patients in all health system primary care practices will result in decreased cardiovascular risk, reduced acute healthcare utilization, and improved quality of life for our patients.

Each project will have a separate, dedicated employee/patient advisory panel to inform activities throughout the project time frame.

Evaluation design

For both programs, we will conduct a stepped wedge cluster randomized crossover cohort rollout to assess both implementation and effectiveness outcomes of implementation of each program to all health system Northeast Ohio primary care practices. The time of crossover will be randomized, and the unit of randomization will be a Practice Group/Cluster with 4 time periods including baseline and 3 steps with clusters switching from control to implementation. Outcomes will be measured on each unit at each time step. We will collect practice level quantitative implementation outcomes (reach, adoption, fidelity, effectiveness, cost) and qualitative data through interviews and observations to assess patient and provider acceptability and feasibility of the program and will capture any adaptations at the practice group levels.

Analytic plan

Intensive lifestyle treatment for weight loss: quantitative analysis

Our randomized stepped wedge rollout involves rollout to a total of 27 practice groups (as the 56 Northeast Ohio primary care practices were combined to create a total of 27 practice groups, ensuring at least 1000 patients per practice group). There will be 9 new practice groups rolling out the intervention every 3 months over 9 months. Our intent to treat analysis refers to outcome measurement on each unit at each time step. The comparison group will be the non-implementation sites during the 3-phase stepped wedge rollout. When Group 1 rolls out, Groups 2 and 3 will be the non-implementation comparison group. When Group 2 rolls out, Group 3 will be the non-implementation comparison group. The Practice Groups will serve as the unit of analysis.

Outcomes

The intent to treat analysis will assess the effect of timing of exposure to offering of the intervention on the primary outcome and will allow the most comprehensive assessment of the implementation intervention. The 4 time periods (including the baseline) and 3 steps (with 9 clusters switching from control to treatment at each step) will be used to test whether the Group 1 (treatment) mean (µ1 = 97.4 kg as in Befort et al. [2]) is different from the Group 2 (control) mean (µ2 = 102.9 kg as in Befort et al. [2]) (H0: µ1 - µ2 = 0 versus H1: µ1 - µ2 ≠ 0).

The primary outcome of weight loss in pounds will be analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, which will include the treatment arm as a fixed effect and a random-intercept at the randomized Practice Group level.

We will adjust for the following baseline covariates: patient age, sex, race, insurance status, comorbidities, and Area Deprivation Index (ADI) [14] and self-reported data on diet and exercise routines collected using the Obesity SMA questionnaire. Our health system collects social determinants of health (SDOH) data in the following 11 domains: Tobacco use, Financial Resource/Strain, Transportation Needs, Stress, Intimate Partner Violence, Housing Stability, Alcohol Use, Food Insecurity, Physical Activity, Social Connections, and Depression. We will have the opportunity to assess associations between SDOH and program acceptance and participation, as well as relationship between SDOH and response to the diet and physical activity questions included in the Obesity SMA questionnaire (administered as part of routine care) and weight loss outcomes. These topics will be discussed during the planned Obesity SMA sessions and will also be explored with qualitative data collection.

The primary comparison is to compare primary and secondary effectiveness outcomes (Supplemental Tables 1–2) before and after implementation of the obesity SMA intervention. We will measure equity in outcomes by demographic groups and ADI. We will define “Enrolled in Program” as agreed to Obesity SMA participation and attended first appointment and Completed Program as agreed to Obesity SMA participation and attended all appointments over the 18-month period. We will measure Reach Equity related to program referrals, enrollment, and completion. To address the non-adherence limitation(poor fidelity to intervention: % of SMA attended/intended), we will conduct two secondary analyses [15]. We will conduct an as-treated analysis: comparison of outcomes in those who participate in the SMA versus outcome in those who do not participate, regardless of randomization. We will calculate complier average causal effect (CACE) estimation, which allows for unobserved confounding by using randomization as an instrument and provides a randomization-respecting estimate [16]. In addition, we will use per-protocol analysis: comparison of those who complied with random allocation in the intervention group with all of the controls. We will use an inverse probability weighting approach to minimize the selection bias [17]. In addition, we will conduct the described above secondary “as-treated” and “per protocol” analysis in subgroups of patients who were (versus were not) taking anti-obesity medications. We will also measure the treatment effect heterogeneity (effect modification) by using anti-obesity medications, by adding an interaction term in the models. For the “as treated” analysis, we will conduct separate analyses on those patients who enrolled in the Obesity SMA program and attended at least 1 SMA session, those who attended at least 50% of the SMA sessions, and those who attended 100% of the SMA sessions.

Implementation of effective HTN management approaches: quantitative analysis

A complete stepped-wedge cluster-randomized design with 4 time periods (including the baseline) and 3 steps (with 7 Practice Groups/Clusters switching from control to treatment at each step) will be used to test whether the Group 1 (treatment) mean change in SBP (-18 mmHg) is different from the Group 2 (control) mean change equal zero (no change) based upon Margolis et al. [3].

The data analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle. The comparison group will be the non-implementation sites during the 3-phase stepped wedge rollout. When Group 1 rolls out, Groups 2 and 3 will be the non-implementation comparison group. When Group 2 rolls out, Group 3 will be the non-implementation comparison group. The Practice Groups will serve as the unit of analysis.

Outcomes

The intention-to-treat analysis will assess the effect of timing of exposure to offering of the intervention on the primary outcome of systolic blood pressure control (mm Hg) at 12 months and will allow the most comprehensive assessment of the implementation intervention. When our intervention is delivered to the practice groups, there may be unmeasured group/spillover effects. The primary outcome of systolic blood pressure control (mm Hg) at 12 months will be analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, which will include the treatment arm as a fixed effect and a random intercept at the randomized Practice Group level.

We will adjust for the following baseline covariates: patient age, sex, race, insurance status, comorbidities, and ADI. The primary comparison is to compare primary and secondary effectiveness outcomes (Supplemental Tables 1,2) before and after implementation of the intensive HTN management program intervention. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. As we use a priori stratification (by the size of Practice Group), we will examine differential intervention effects by pre-defined stratum. To address the non-adherence limitation (poor fidelity to intervention: % of follow-up sessions attended/intended), we will conduct two secondary analyses [15]. We will conduct an as-treated analysis: comparison of outcomes in those who participated in the follow-up appointments versus outcomes in those who did not participate, regardless of randomization. We will conduct separate analyses on those patients who enrolled in the intensive HTN management program and attended at least 1 session, and those who completed the program to the goal blood pressure. Also, if during our project, we find patients assigned to practice groups who are not yet rolled out to offer the intervention somehow become enrolled in the program, we will identify these patients as not being part of the per-protocol analysis. These patients will be part of the “as treated” analysis. We will calculate CACE estimation, which allows for unobserved confounding by using randomization as an instrument and provides a randomization-respecting estimate [16]. The complier average causal effect estimate is the comparison of the average outcome of the compliers in the treatment arm with the average outcome of the comparable group of would-be compliers in the control arm. In other words, complier average causal effect estimates the intention-to-treat effect in the subgroup of participants who would always comply with their treatment allocation. In addition, we will use per-protocol analysis: comparison of those who complied with random allocation in the intervention group with all the controls. We will use an inverse probability weighting approach to minimize the selection bias [17].

To control for time trends, pre- and post- observations/measurements will be conducted using similar time intervals in all clusters. We will use Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) with appropriate links for the primary analysis (change in systolic blood pressure) considering random effects for a practice and patients, and fixed effects for steps. To account for cluster treatment heterogeneity, we will include a random cluster by treatment interaction term and for time-varying intervention effect, will include an intervention by time interaction. We will adjust the effect of the intervention on the primary and secondary outcomes for demographic and socioeconomic factors including our health system’s routinely collected data on social determinants of health in the following 11 domains: Tobacco use, Financial Resource/Strain, Transportation Needs, Stress, Intimate Partner Violence, Housing Stability, Alcohol Use, Food Insecurity, Physical Activity, Social Connections, and Depression. The GLMM models will allow for variation between clusters and between times within clusters [18]. We will inspect the patterns of missing data and use multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) to jointly impute data on missing exposure and covariates to preserve an unbiased association estimate if the data are missing at random conditional on measured variables.

Quantitative analysis of other implementation outcomes

We will assess implementation outcomes while accounting for potential confounders in generalized linear models as discussed in the modeling approach for the effectiveness outcomes. We will describe changes in implementation outcomes over time and across the three implementation phases using plots and descriptive statistics and perform subgroup analyses. For any outcomes collected using published surveys, interpretations recommended while validating those tools will be used.

Implementation cost analysis

The economic costs will be used to perform a budget impact analysis to assess the affordability of the program from the perspective of the health system. Costs will be classified as fixed, variable, and time-dependent and further grouped as start-up and ongoing to adequately inform future implementation efforts beyond the local context. Costs will be approximated using production costs, and along with utilization, these data will come from finance, operations, and the EMR. For more accurate estimates, we will use activity-based costing—a widely used micro-costing approach—to map each activity to an implementation strategy in each phase of implementation [19]. For each activity, we will multiply resources utilized by the unit cost and frequency to obtain the cost of that activity. For reporting, mean, median, and total costs will be used. We will use simple arithmetic implemented in Microsoft Excel and generalized equations with a gamma distribution, an exchangeable correlation structure, and an identity link to estimate mean costs and account for potential clustering. Activities will include need for adaptation of educational materials, time to deliver education, creation of clinical decision support tool, provider time spent delivering intervention, time required for patient navigation and practice facilitation and time for audit and feedback activities.

We will perform both one-way and multiway scenario sensitivity analyses to identify cost drivers and evaluate the robustness of our findings to different scenarios relevant for replication elsewhere. In one-way sensitivity analysis, we will identify parameters and assumptions driving the implementation costs by varying the inputs individually while holding other parameters at the base values. In multiway scenario analysis, we will examine the robustness of our estimates to simultaneous changes of input parameters. For this, we will create the best-case scenario (a more optimistic combination of input estimates) and worst scenario (a more pessimistic combination of input estimates). In each sensitivity analysis, we will report new mean, median, and total costs.

Qualitative data collection and analysis plan

Pre-implementation

During pre-implementation, we will conduct a formal qualitative assessment of context of high and low referral (obesity) or high and low control (HTN) practices to understand barriers and facilitators to uptake of treatment/control, informed by the domains in the Clinical Sustainability Assessment Tool [20]. This will entail semi-structured qualitative interviews with non-patient key stakeholders (e.g., site directors, primary care physicians trained in obesity management, clinic staff), direct field observations of practice sites, and observations of quarterly meetings with employee and patient advisory panels. Interview respondents will be sampled from the referral sites, with attention to capturing urban/suburban/rural diversity. These interviews will examine usual care for obesity or HTN, knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding the intervention and anticipated barriers and facilitators to implementation. Interviews will last 20–30 min and will be transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. Fieldnotes taken during field observation sites will capture contextual data (e.g., usual care workflow, provider/patient behaviors and interactions) to assess anticipated barriers and facilitators to uptake. These direct field observations align with the continuous improvement practice of “going to the gemba," [21] i.e., “the real place” where a process unfolds in order to observe and understand the process firsthand. If needed, additional field site visits will be conducted to reach information power [22]. Fieldnotes taken during pre-implementation quarterly advisory panels will also examine anticipated barriers and facilitators to implementation. Findings from the pre-implementation evaluation, including identified problems and potential plans for process improvement that align with continuous improvement principles such as A3 [21], will inform implementation strategies such as practice facilitation used during active implementation.

Active implementation & maintenance

Periodic reflections, [23] an ethnographically-informed approach to guided discussion, will be conducted with agents of implementation by the evaluation team throughout active implementation and maintenance to capture features of the context of implementation (including adaptations to the intervention or implementation plan), as well as provider experience delivering the intervention, including perceptions of acceptability and feasibility of the intervention, challenges and problem-solving efforts, and barriers and facilitators to implementation. This method allows for consistent documentation of implementation phenomena in near-real-time, over the course of implementation. Reflections will be completed as 15- to 30-minute virtual guided discussions with a subsample of staff across practice locations, occurring monthly and bi-monthly depending on the level of study activity (more frequent during early implementation and less frequent during routine implementation). Discussion participants will represent sites from each of the three rollout groups. PhD-level qualitative methodologists will facilitate reflection sessions and document discussion content in detailed fieldnotes.

During active implementation, semi-structured interviews will be conducted with a subsample of patients receiving the intervention to assess perceptions of acceptability of and satisfaction with the program. Baseline interviews will examine patients’ usual care; knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding the program; and anticipated barriers and facilitators to the care model. The mid-implementation and late implementation interviews will assess the following: (1) usual care vs. new care model (relative advantages, content, delivery, complexity etc.), (2) barriers and facilitators to the care model, and (3) perceptions of acceptability and feasibility of the care model. Interviews will last 30 min and will be transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. If needed, additional interviews will be conducted to reach information power [22].

Observations of quarterly meetings with employee and patient advisory panels will continue throughout active implementation to assess ongoing implementation activities and will inform periodic reflections and semi-structured interviews.

Analysis plan

Two PhD-level qualitative methodologists will collect and analyze the qualitative data (transcripts and descriptive fieldnotes), drawing upon flexible coding [24] and rapid analysis techniques [25]. Transcripts and fieldnotes will be coded to reflect key domains of interest and emergent themes.

Discussion

The two project protocols described present unique opportunities as part of the PCORI HSII program to implement evidence-based care delivery programs within primary care settings into routine practice and to significantly improve treatment for both obesity and uncontrolled HTN in outpatient primary care practices in one health system. The plan to collect rigorously both clinical effectiveness and implementation outcomes and assess feasibility of the implementation strategies utilizing mixed methods, including understanding adaptations required for success at the practice level, will inform health system efforts for sustainability of the current programs. Knowledge will be gained to guide scale-up of the two programs or need for tailoring of future health system implementation efforts in other clinical areas of focus or environments.

Our health system has both recognized and prioritized the need for collaborative efforts among clinical, operational, and evaluation experts to promote evidence translation into practice through these two projects, bringing together diverse teams to support implementation. The protocols described in this report address implementation of care delivery innovations that have been areas of disease focus and quality initiatives for the health system and thus the health system supports these efforts to supplement the PCORI HSII project plans. The Implementation of Intensive Lifestyle Treatment for Weight Loss in Primary Care Settings project is currently in the active implementation phase and titled ACTIVATE-OC (Advancing care through intensive validated approaches for treatment and engagement with obesity care) and the Implementation of Effective HTN Management Approaches project is in the pre-implementation phase and titled IN-HOME BP. We will carefully monitor our implementation outcomes, the success of our planned implementation strategies, and any adaptations needed for program delivery throughout the project periods. We look forward to sharing the results of the programs once full implementation and analyses are completed.
