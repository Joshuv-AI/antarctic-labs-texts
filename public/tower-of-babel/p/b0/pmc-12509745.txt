Biodistribution of AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ serotypes after intra-cisterna magna delivery in non-human primates
Authors: Takuro Okai, Sho Sato, Hironobu Yasuno, Miyu Nakayama, Syunsuke Yamamoto, Sebastian Sjöqvist, Kentaro Otake, Masato Nakashima, Mugdha Deshpande, Elizabeth Galbreath, Jeong-Ho Oak, Saku Miyamoto, Gabriele Proetzel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Delivering drugs effectively to the central nervous system (CNS) is a major challenge in drug development, including adeno-associated virus (AAV) gene therapy. The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulates through the ventricular system and the subarachnoid space, surrounding both the brain and spinal cord, making it an attractive target for CNS drug delivery. Here, we compare intra-cisterna magna (ICM) administration of four AAV serotypes, AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ, carrying glucosylceramidase beta 1 (GBA1) cDNA encoding the lysosomal enzyme β-glucocerebrosidase (GCase), in non-human primates (NHPs). Mutations in GBA1 are causative for Gaucher’s disease (GD) and the most important genetic risk factor for Parkinson’s disease (PD). AAV delivery of GBA1 is a promising therapeutic approach for GD and PD, assuming effective delivery to the target tissues. Our data show no significant difference in biodistribution among AAV serotypes. ICM administration effectively delivers GCase to the spinal cord and dorsal root ganglia, though distribution to deep brain regions is limited. The ICM procedure was well tolerated with no significant toxicity. To improve delivery to deep brain regions, AAVs with blood-brain barrier (BBB)-penetrating capabilities, protein-engineered GCase enzymes optimized for secretion and cross-correction, or delivery methods such as convection-enhanced delivery or ultrasound-focused technology may be required.

Introduction

Effective brain delivery is essential for treating central nervous system (CNS) disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and neuropathic Gaucher’s disease (GD). Native adeno-associated virus (AAV) serotypes have limited ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB).1 As delivery method direct cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) delivery has been explored to target AAVs to the CNS, including intrathecal (IT/lumbar puncture), intracerebroventricular (ICV), and intra-cisterna magna (ICM). In this paper, we are evaluating ICM delivery of AAVs, which have been reported to be safe and effective in preclinical studies2,3,4,5,6 and used in multiple clinical trials, e.g., RegenxBio, Prevail Tx,7 PassageBio, SioGene Tx, and Lexeo Tx. We have selected four neurotropic AAVs, AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ for comparison.8,9,10,11,12

We selected the glucosylceramidase beta 1 (GBA1) transgene, encoding the lysosomal enzyme β-glucocerebrosidase (GCase) that converts glucosylceramide (GlcCer) to glucose and ceramide within cells. As GCase is secreted, there is potential of cross-correction—the uptake of secreted protein/enzyme by neighboring cells to compensate for deficiency in these cells. Such would improve the therapeutic effect after AAV transduction, specifically beneficial for lysosomal storage disorders.13 The GCase protein can either be transported directly to the lysosome or secreted. In the brain, GCase is released into the extracellular fluid such as CSF, where it can be taken up by neighboring cells through endocytosis.14 The importance of this secretion and endocytosis cycle has been demonstrated in several mouse models15 and is thought to be beneficial for the gene therapy approach by enabling widespread brain distribution from GCase overexpression in only a subset of cells.

Mutations in GBA1 cause GD due to accumulation of glucocerebrosides16,17 and represent the major risk factor for PD and dementia with Lewy bodies.18,19,20 Preclinical models with reduced GCase activity due to GBA1 mutations or administration of the GCase inhibitor conduritol-β-epoxide (CBE) show disruptions in lipid metabolism, autophagy-lysosome cycle, mitophagy with elevated reactive oxygen species, neuroinflammation, α-synuclein aggregation, and progressive neurodegeneration in vitro and in vivo.21,22 Overexpression of GBA1 reduces α-synucleinopathy and is neuroprotective in GBA1-deficient rodent models, indicating that increased GBA1 expression can protect vulnerable nigral dopamine neurons.23,24,25,26

One promising strategy for delivering GCase to target tissues is AAV gene therapy to restore GBA1 expression in target tissues, to slow or halt disease progression of GD and GBA-associated PD (GBA-PD).27,28,29 We have previously reported that AAV-GBA1 delivery reduces substrate accumulation and corrects α-synuclein pathology in mouse models.30 For clinical translation, validating CNS delivery in large animals such as non-human primates (NHPs) is essential due to brain complexity and interspecies differences.31,32

This study compares AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ, each packaged carrying an identical GBA1 expression cassette. AAV1, AAV5, and AAV9 were selected based on their regulatory and translational relevance, including their readiness for large-scale manufacturing and ongoing clinical evaluation for CNS applications. AAVDJ, a synthetic capsid derived through DNA shuffling of multiple AAV serotypes, was included based on reported improved properties in transduction efficiency after ICM delivery to the CNS.11 All serotypes show similar biodistribution, with efficient transduction of the spinal cord and dorsal root ganglia (DRG), but minimal penetration into deep brain regions such as the substantia nigra. In summary, ICM delivery of the tested AAVs effectively achieved effective transduction of the spinal cord but showed limited penetration into deep brain regions.

Results

Design of AAV constructs for ICM administration studies in NHPs

We have developed an expression cassette EFS-hGBA1 consisting of the elongation factor 1 alpha (EFS) promoter followed by a chimeric intron, wild-type human GBA1 gene linked with a C-terminal a human influenza hemagglutinin (HA) tag, a modified Woodchuck Hepatitis Virus Posttranscriptional Regulatory Element (WPRE) enhancer, and human growth hormone (hGH1) polyA (Figure S1A). The HA-tag has been extensively used as a general epitope tag to allow to distinguish the transgene from endogenous expression.33

The EFS-hGBA1 expression cassette was packaged into four different AAV serotypes: AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ. The quality of each AAV was examined by checking titers and endotoxin level (<5 Endotoxin Units (EU)/mL) (Table S1). We assessed transduction efficiency of all AAVs in human iPSC-derived dopaminergic (DA) (iPS-DA) neurons carrying GBA1 L444P biallelic mutations (L444P/L444P) that have reduced GCase protein levels.30 All four AAVs, AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ showed dose-dependent increase of expression. AAV1, AAV5, and AAV9 yielded similar GBA1 expression levels. Notably, AAVDJ transduction showed a higher gene expression, plateauing at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 × 105, resulting in a ∼3-fold increase in GCase levels compared to other AAVs at the same MOI, suggesting a superior transduction efficiency for AAVDJ in this assay (Figure S1B).

Design of NHP studies 1 and 2

Biodistribution of vector VG and GCase protein

In the NHP study 1, the animals were sacrificed 30 days after AAV administration and tissues were collected for analysis. For brain tissue, punches with a diameter of 2 mm were collected from one hemisphere for VG analysis, GCase expression, and activity assessment (Figure S2; Table S3). VG amounts for AAV9 and AAVDJ were similar, with AAVDJ in general being slightly higher in most of tissues except for the liver, heart, and kidney (Figure 1A). In the liver, AAV9 yielded a 10-fold higher transduction level compared to AAVDJ (Figure 1A). In kidney and heart, VG levels were around 1× 104 VG/μg DNA, a low level as expected after ICM administration. In summary, both AAV serotypes exhibited similar VG distribution in the CNS and assessed peripheral tissues, except for AAV9, which reached a 10-fold exposure in the liver.

Transgene expression for GCase protein was examined using a GCase activity assay and Western blot (WES) analysis. We observed a robust increase in GCase activity in the spinal cord with both AAV vectors, but no significant change in any of the tested brain regions (Figure 1B). WES analysis using an anti-HA antibody to differentiate between AAV-derived expression of human GCase (with HA tags) and NHP endogenous GCase revealed HA-positive bands in the spinal cord, hippocampus and liver (AAV9 only), but not in other brain regions (Figure S3). In the WES analysis, the AAV9 groups showed greater GCase expression in spinal cord and liver compared to the AAVDJ group (Figures S3B and S3C).

NHP study 2: Three-month evaluation of AAV1, AAV5, and AAV9

For the three-month study, three male NHPs were assigned to each treatment group, AAV1, AAV5, or AAV9, and two male NHPs for the vehicle control (Table 1). The route of administration was ICM, with the dose of 2.0 × 1013 VG for the AAV vector in 1-mL volume, the same as in NHP study 1. Screening for NAbs was conducted to select NAb-negative animals and the presence of NAbs was monitored throughout the study (Table S4). On day 1 before dosing (pre-dose), we observed a modest NAb signal increase in two of three animals in the AAV5 group (animals no. 6 and 8) which moved them above the set threshold of 50%. On day 2, animal no. 6 showed the same result, while animal no. 8 had NAb levels below the set threshold. On days 57 and 90, anti-AAV NAbs for each serotype turned to positive in all animals, indicating that all animals developed treatment-induced anti-AAV NAbs after AAV dosing. In the control group, we included one AAV1 and AAV5 sero-positive NHP, and detected subtle changes in their NAb status over the study duration (Table S4).

Biodistribution of VG, mRNA, and GCase expression for NHP study 2

Animals were sacrificed 90 days after AAV administration. AAV biodistribution and mRNA expression were analyzed in the same brain regions as in the NHP study 1 and additional tissues, i.e., optic nerve, motor cortex, globus pallidus internal (GPI), deep cervical lymph nodes, lung, thymus, pancreas, bone marrow, testis, epididymis, kidney, gastrocnemius muscle, and trigeminal ganglion (Table S3). VG analysis comparing AAV1, AAV5, and AAV9 revealed a similar pattern for these 3 serotypes (Figure 2A). VG levels from 1× 105 to 1× 107 VG/μg DNA were detected in the spinal cord, olfactory bulb, DRG, trigeminal ganglia, optic nerves, deep cervical lymph nodes, liver, and spleen. The second highest VG levels were observed in cortex regions, substantia nigra, hippocampus, pons, medulla oblongata, dentate nucleus, and cerebellar cortex. Low VG levels were noted in deep brain regions such as caudate head, putamen, and thalamus (Figure 2A). Overall AAV1 showed a higher transduction in most CNS tissues. All three serotypes achieved solid biodistribution to the spinal cord and DRGs. In peripheral tissues, the highest VG amount in liver was detected for AAV9, being about 10-fold higher—similar as seen in the NHP study 1 (Figure 2A). GBA1 mRNA expression was examined using a quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and a commercially available primer/probe set from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. The primers and probes used cross-reacted with the NHP sequences as seen by the vehicle group, which allowed to calculate the expression above endogenous levels in each tissue. The highest GBA1 mRNA expression was detected in spinal cord and DRGs (up to 100-folds for AAV1, up to 50-folds for AAV9, and up to 5-folds for AAV5), followed by trigeminal ganglia, olfactory bulb, cortex, pons, medulla oblongata, thalamus, dentate nucleus, and cerebellar cortex (Figure 2B). Overall AAV1 showed the highest expression, followed by AAV9 and with AAV5 being the lowest.

Biodistribution of AAV vectors in the CSF and blood: Analysis of VG biodistribution and GCase activity in CSF and plasma

We examined the dynamics of AAV after ICM administration, focusing on the distribution of AAV VG and GCase activity in the CSF and blood over time following the administration of AAV1, AAV5, and AAV9 (Figure S4A). For CSF, the earliest time point collected after dosing was day 57, and at this time, VG levels in CSF were below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 400 VG/μg, which supports reports about rapid AAV clearance in the CSF.35,36 On the other hand, the blood VG levels were detectable throughout the duration of the study, i.e., 90 days post-administration. AAV9 showed ∼25-fold higher VG than AAV1 or AAV5 at day 3, but after that, AAV1 and AAV9 showed similar VG levels throughout the study. The VG level for AAV5 dropped to about 7-fold lower on day 29 compared to AAV1 and AAV9 and remained at that level until the end of the study (Figure S4B).

GCase activity was also examined in CSF and plasma. Increase in CSF GCase activity was observed for all AAVs, with the highest activity for AAV1 and AAV9, while AAV5 showed the lowest CSF GCase activity among AAV serotypes (Figure S4C). No plasma GCase activity increase was observed throughout the study (Figure S4D), possibly due to higher endogenous GCase activity in plasma and lower biodistribution of AAVs in peripheral tissues than that in CSF.

Immunohistochemistry

To examine spatial distribution of GBA1 expression in brain, spinal cord, DRGs, and peripheral tissues, the anti-HA antibody was used that specifically detects the AAV delivered transgene product and does not cross-react with the NHP endogenous GCase protein. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) results showed multifocal IHC signals in sporadic neurons within the cortex (anterior cingulate cortex, dorsal pre-motor cortex, parietal cortex, primary motor cortex, primary visual cortex, prefrontal cortex, and temporal cortex), GPI, medulla oblongata, nucleus accumbens, olfactory bulb, pons, thalamus, and cerebellum (Tables 2 and 3; Figure 3). In the cerebellar cortex and the hippocampus, neurons including Purkinje cells and pyramidal neurons showed a positive reaction focally. Glial cells also showed positive reaction in cerebellar cortex, cerebellar medulla, and hippocampus, though it was difficult to accurately classify the neurons and glial cells in the sections. Strong HA immunoreactivity was detected in DRG neurons (cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral), spinal cord neurons (cervical, thoracic, and lumbar), and spinal cord glial cells in the dorsal root entry zone or ventral cord (cervical, thoracic, and lumbar). HA signals were also seen in arachnoid membrane in the brain, and ependymal cells in the brain and spinal cord (cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral) (Figure 4). The positive neurons in each brain region or tissue were sporadic (Figure 4). Positive reactivity of the neurons tended to be more prominent in sacral DRG, thoracic, and lumbar spinal cord from AAV 1 and 9 when compared to the brain regions (Tables 2 and 3). Toxicity endpoints

In both studies, we observed no mortality, abnormal clinical signs, body weight decline, changes in hematology, coagulation, or clinical chemistry or gross pathology (Tables S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S10, and S11). Degeneration of DRG neurons and associated axons has been reported as a potential concern associated with AAV treatment.5,35,37 Therefore, cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral DRG sections were examined in detail in the three-month study. Neuronal degeneration and necrosis with the presence of mononuclear cell infiltrate was detected only in a sacral DRG of one animal of the AAV5-treated group (Figures 5B and 5D, arrows). Discussion

In this study, we have evaluated the biodistribution of four AAV serotypes—AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ—in large animals after ICM administration. The results support that ICM delivery is safe with all of these AAVs. The ICM administration was well tolerated in all NHPs (five NHPs in study 1 and eleven NHPs in study 2). There were no AAV-related fatalities or no toxicity in clinical observations, and no changes in body weight, hematology, and clinical chemistry. Histopathological analysis detected minimal findings in one sacral DRG in one animal treated with AAV5 in the three-month study. These were determined as degeneration and necrosis of sacral DRG neurons with infiltration of mononuclear cells. DRG findings were previously reported in other studies for AAV9.5,35,37 Since we observe this only in one sacral DRG in one NHP, it is difficult to conclude whether this finding is caused by AAV5 treatment directly or not. The mechanism of DRG toxicity is not yet fully understood but is thought to be unlikely to be the AAV capsid. It has been hypothesized that overproduction of the transgene product and/or increased vector genome transduction can cause cellular stress, ultimately leading to neuronal loss.38,39 GBA1 expression is high in the degenerated DRG neurons as indicated by HA tag IHC staining. However, in all other groups, including the two additional NHPs dosed with AAV5, the DRGs also exhibited high GBA1 expression by HA tag IHC staining, with no evidence of degeneration. This finding correlates with the VG results that show high VG distribution in sacral DRGs for all AAVs (Figures 2A, 5E, and 5F). Since the expression cassette (promoter and transgene) is the same for all AAVs, we conclude that there is no clear evidence linking the transgene overexpression/increased vector genome transduction to the DRG findings, and the mechanism behind these observations remains unclear. In summary, since no correlated abnormal findings were noted in other toxicity endpoints in the animal with DRG findings, we conclude that all four AAV serotypes, including AAV5, demonstrate an acceptable safety profile.

Our study shows that ICM delivers all AAV serotypes efficiently to the spinal cord and DRGs showing the highest VG levels, with the exception of liver for AAV9. The tissues with the next highest transduction are from regions close to the CSF, e.g., cerebral cortex, hippocampus, pons, and cerebellum. However, delivery to deep brain regions, such as the putamen and midbrain, was about 10-fold lower (e.g., caudate head, globus pallidus external, putamen, and thalamus ventro-anterior nucleus). Even in the brain regions with higher VG levels, we could not detect robust transgene expression using mRNA and protein assays, while transgene expression was strong in spinal cord and DRG. Our findings are consistent with recent studies showing limited delivery to deep regions.9 The physical properties of CSF, with a turnover rate of four to five times per day, and its tendency to first distribute to regions proximal to the CSF-filled ventricles, may account for the observed limited distribution.36,40 CSF flows from the lateral ventricles through the foramen of Monro into the third ventricle, then through the cerebral aqueduct into the fourth ventricle. From the fourth ventricle, CSF can flow into the central canal of the spinal cord or exit into the subarachnoid space surrounding the brain and spinal cord via the foramina of Luschka and Magendie. Within the subarachnoid space, CSF circulates around the brain and spinal cord.41 We observe a strong correlation, particularly in regions near to ventricles, between the VG of AAV9 in the one-month study (NHP study 1) and three-month (NHP study 2) study. However, we also notice some differences, e.g., in substantia nigra, which we are unable to explain (Figure S5). One limitation of our study is that targeting of the cisterna magna was guided solely by anatomical landmarks and CSF outflow, without the aid of real-time imaging. As a result, there may have been variability in the accuracy of cisterna magna targeting between animals that may explain some of the variability.

Between the different AAVs, we saw only slight differences, with AAV-DJ performing better than AAV9 regarding transduction, e.g., 1 × 106 VG/μg in cervical spinal cord for AAV-DJ vs. 1 × 105 VG/μg for AAV9 in the one-month NHP study 1, and in the three-month study (NHP study 2), AAV1 reached 1 × 106 VG/μg in cervical spinal cord vs. AAV9 around 1 × 105 VG/μg. We also observed the tendency of very low transduction by AAV5 compared to the other AAVs. However, considering the variability in each AAV group, these differences are not robust, and we could not conclude which AAV would be superior. We also studied potential differences in cell type preference, as a previous report indicated that AAV1 transduces ependymal cells far more efficiently than AAV5 and AAV9 variants (AAVhu68).9 While we observed a slightly higher transduction of ependymal cells with AAV1 compared to AAV5, its efficiency is comparable to AAV9, and potentially there is a difference between AAV9 and AAVhu68 (Figure S6). We could not find any robust differences for neuron or glial transduction dependent on the AAV serotypes, either.

We examined the VG threshold required for proper mRNA and protein expression by analyzing the correlation between VG copy number and mRNA levels. Our data indicate that mRNA expression begins to be detectable when VGs exceed approximately 5 × 104 VG/μg (Figure S7). In contrast, at VG levels below 1 × 104 VG/μg, transgene mRNA expression and GCase activity were undetectable (Figures 1, 2, and 3). These findings are reasonable, assuming 1 × 105 VG/μg corresponds to ∼0.6 VG per nucleus.34 In contrast, other studies using the GFP transgene have reported detectable transgene expression in deep brain regions, which may be attributable to the inherent sensitivity of GFP, enabling visualization of even very low-level expression in scattered cells. Additionally, differences in promoters and regulatory elements likely contribute to these discrepancies. A previous study using stronger promoters, such as cytomegalovirus or chicken beta-actin, reported detectable GFP signals in deep brain regions. In contrast, our study used the EFS promoter with the GBA1 transgene, which may account for the differences.3

Comparison of VG levels in the CNS and peripheral tissues showed high VG levels in liver followed by spleen, and low in heart and kidney, indicating the AAV flow from CSF to peripheral circulation as previously reported.3,36 Interestingly, given the similar VG levels in liver, DRG or spinal cord, mRNA expression was much lower in liver than those in lumber and sacral DRG or spinal cord, suggesting that EFS promoter works more effectively in CNS than liver in NHPs (Figure S8). In both studies, AAV9 had the highest liver exposure compared to AAV1, AAV5, and AAVDJ. This result is in accordance with our previous finding that the liver tropism after ICV administration in mouse showed a significant leakage to the liver in the case of AAV9 but minimal or AAV5.30

For the treatment of PD, delivering GCase to the most affected regions such as striatum or substantia nigra is crucial. However, this study shows that it is challenging to achieve the biodistribution to these deep brain regions with ICM administration. One potential solution is the development of engineered AAV vectors capable of crossing the BBB via vascular delivery, with reports indicating certain vectors efficiently distribute throughout the brain not only in rodent42,43 but in large animals.11,44,45 Alternatively, the use of focused ultrasound to transiently open the BBB for the delivery of AAVs is being proposed as method to increase deep brain biodistribution.46 Another delivery approach could involve adding brain-penetrating signals (e.g., transferrin receptor or other BBB surface protein binding sites) to the GCase protein along with enhancements to its stability and secretion, facilitating its migration into the brain even when expressed peripherally.47,48,49,50 We believe that the next-generation gene therapy treatment for GBA-PD patients will consist of a combination of these strategies.

Materials and methods

Animals

For the one-month study (NHP study 1), experiments were conducted in 2–3 kg male (2–3 years old at dosing), captive-bred cynomolgus macaque purchased from Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) and housed in the iPark Institute (Kanagawa, Japan). The experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. AU-00030869) and was performed in accordance with the animal welfare bylaws of iPark, accredited by AAALAC International.

For three-month study (NHP study 2), the experiments were conducted in 2–3 kg male (2–3 years old at dosing), captive-bred cynomolgus macaque purchased from Tian Hu Cambodia Animal Breeding Research Center, Ltd. (Cambodia) and housed in the Shin Nippon Biomedical Laboratories, Ltd (SNBL, Kagoshima, Japan). The experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. IACUC010-490) and was performed in accordance with the animal welfare bylaws of SNBL, AAALAC International accredited.

We selected primates that had no or low levels of neutralizing factors against AAV serotypes 1, 5, 9, and DJ in the serum. The animals were tested 2 weeks before dosing in study 1 and study 2.

iPS-DA neurons

Culture of iPS-DA neurons was performed as previously described.30 Wild-type parent iPS line 201B751 and iPS cells with GBA1 biallelic mutations for L444P (L444P/L444P) generated from control 201B7 iPS by genome editing were used.52 IPS-derived DA neuronal differentiation was performed as previously described.53

AAV vector cloning and production

All AAV vectors were designed by the authors and produced at the University of Massachusetts Medical School Viral Vector Core. The expression cassette used for all AAV serotypes contains inverted terminal repeats from AAV2, the ubiquitous truncated 255 bp short form of the human EF1α promoter (EFS),54,55 the chimeric chicken β-actin and rabbit β-globin intron,56 wild-type human GBA1 cDNA sequence with a C-terminal HA tag, the HA-tag encoding for YPYDVPDYA, a 597 bp modified woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element removing the ATGs (WPREmut6delATG) element,57 and a human growth hormone polyadenylation signal sequence followed by a 100-bp sequence used for VG analysis by a quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)58 (Patent, US 20210275614A1) (Figure S1A). AAV1, AAV5, AAV9, and AAVDJ vectors were produced using standard triple transient transfection methods as previously described.30

ICM AAV administration and CSF collection

The animals were anesthetized by intramuscular injections (0.2 mL/kg, 10 mg/kg) of ketamine (Ketalar for Intramuscular Injection 500 mg, Daiichi Sankyo Propharma Co., Ltd., 50 mg/mL) and medetomidine hydrochloride (Domitor, Orion Corporation, 1 mg/mL, 0.08 mL/kg). After placing the animals in the ventral decubitus position, the injection needle (25G, Terumo Corporation) was manually slowly inserted into the cisterna magna using anatomical landmarks and approximately 1 mL of the outflowing CSF was collected (flow rate ∼1 mL/min) into Protein LoBind Tubes (Eppendorf) followed by administering 1 mL of the test article at a rate of 1 mL/min manually.3 The animals were not positioned in the Trendelenburg orientation. After dosing, atipamezole hydrochloride (Antisedan, Orion Corporation, 5 mg/mL, 0.08 mL/kg), an antagonist of medetomidine hydrochloride, was administered intramuscularly. For immunosuppression, methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-medrol, Pfizer, 20 mg/mL or 40 mg/mL) was administered intramuscularly to the femoral or gluteal muscle weekly; at 40 mg/body (40 mg/mL, 1 mL/body) at 5 days before dosing, and at 20 mg/kg (40 mg/mL, 1 mL/body) from day 3 to day 24 in study 1, and at 40 mg/body from 5 days before dosing to day 24, and at 20 mg/body from day 31 to day 87 in study 2.

Clinical follow-up

For all NHPs, clinical signs and body weight were monitored during the study. Blood sampling for hematology, coagulation, clinical chemistry, gross pathology at necropsy, and histopathology were conducted. Blood for clinical chemistry, hematology or coagulation was collected twice before dosing (15 or 14 days before dosing and 8 or 7 days before dosing) and once on days 29 and 90 in addition to once on days 3, 10, and 57 for the clinical chemistry. For hematology, samples were analyzed using the fully automated hematology analyzer XN-1000V (Sysmex). For coagulation, the automated blood coagulation analyzer CS-5100 was used.

Tissue and blood collection

The animals were sacrificed 30 days (study 1) or 90 days (study 2) after AAV administration. For euthanasia, the animals were anesthetized via intramuscular injection into gluteal muscle with ketamine (Ketalar, Daiichi Sankyo Propharma Co., Ltd., 50 mg/mL, 0.3 mL/kg), and medetomidine hydrochloride (Domitor, Orion Corporation, 1 mg/mL, 0.08 mL/kg), followed by euthanasia via exsanguination. Tissues were collected for analysis. For brain tissue, punches with a diameter of 2 mm were collected from one hemisphere (Tables 2 and 3; Figure S2). For peripheral tissues, 30–120 mg of each tissue was isolated, weighed, and transferred into appropriate tube for each analysis. Blood samples were drawn from the femoral vein using a syringe (2.5, 5, or 10 mL, Nipro Corporation) and transferred into an appropriate tube for the analysis.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry for HA-GCase

Brain, spinal cord, DRG (cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral), trigeminal ganglion, liver, and nasal cavity were collected and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h (Table S3). The fixed organs and tissues were trimmed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and evaluated microscopically. The other section was stained with the anti-HA antibody (anti-HA-Tag [C29F4] Rabbit mAb no. 3724, diluted 1:500), and for detection an anti-rabbit secondary antibody horseradish peroxidase polymer was added for 30 min, followed by Tris-buffered saline rinses and application of a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate to each section. The sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin to visualize nuclei. Immunohistochemical analysis was performed qualitatively. The percentage of IHC-positive cells on tissue sections were estimated qualitatively based on the proportion of visibly positive cells relative to the total number of cells within each evaluated region. Severity grades were assigned as follows. ±: <5% positive cells; +: 6%–30% positive cells; ++: >31% positive cells. For the brain, the analysis was conducted in each brain region of interest independently. Histopathological evaluation was conducted by a Japanese Society of Toxicologic Pathology (JSTP) board-certified pathologist, and peer reviewed by another JSTP board-certified pathologist.

Quantification of VG and hGBA1 mRNA

The quantitation of VG and hGBA1 mRNA in monkey samples were performed as described previously.30,59 Briefly, DNA was extracted using the MagMAX DNA Multi-Sample Ultra 2.0 Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and the KingFisher Apex system according to manufacturer’s protocol with RNase treatment. qPCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7500 FAST Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) with specific primers and TaqMan probes to detect non transcribed tag sequences in the AAV expression cassette.30 To detect human GBA1 mRNA, the probe set from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Hs00986836_g1) was used. For the conversion of Ct values to VG, we followed the method described previously.59 Reactions for each sample were performed in triplicate, and the mean Ct values of the non-transcribed tag sequence in the AAV expression cassette and the extracted DNA-derived reference gene were obtained from the qPCR reactions for each sample. For all tissue samples except for blood, 100 ng of DNA was used. In case of blood, the DNA input varied due to low yield of extracted DNA, and PCR reactions were done using 2 μL of isolated DNA. The DNA concentration per μL of reaction was determined using qPCR with standard DNA derived from NHP spleen. Using standard curves generated by linear regression of Ct values against nominal copy numbers per μL of reaction, the Ct values were converted to VG per μL of reaction and subsequently to VG per μg of DNA. The calibration curve was constructed using the equation X = 10 (Y−b)/a, where “X” represents the nominal concentration (VG/μL of reaction), “Y” is the Ct obtained from PCR for each sample, and “a” and “b” are the slope and y-intercept, respectively. LLOQ per sample was determined as follows. For the lowest concentration sample used in the standard curve, 2 μL of plasmid standard solution at 20 VG/μL was input into the PCR, resulting in 40 VG per PCR measurement. For all tissue samples except for blood, 100 ng of DNA was used for the PCR reaction, with an LLOQ of 400 VG per μg of DNA. In case of blood, the DNA input varied due to low yield of extracted DNA, and PCR reactions were done using 2 μL of isolated DNA. The quantification was done using a DNA reference curve developed with normal NHP spleen tissue and qPCR for the internal control gene alpha actin. The DNA concentration (μg/μL) in the blood samples was determined based on the DNA reference curve. The concentration was calculated using the equation: X = 10(Y−b)/a, where “X” is the DNA concentration (μg/μL), “Y” is the mean Ct value in PCR of the sample, and “a” and “b” represent the slope and y-intercept of the standard curve, respectively. For blood, varied LLOQs with maximum of 11,525 VG/μg DNA (mean ± SD = 4809 ± 2326 VG/μg) were obtained.

Detection of AAV1-, 5-, 9,- and AAVDJ-directed neutralizing factor

Cell-based measurement of neutralizing factor detection was performed as described previously with some modification.60 Serum samples were heat inactivated at 56°C for 35 min, each NHP serum and each AAV-vector containing Luciferase gene (as follows) was mixed and incubated for 1–1.5 h at 37°C, and then the mixture was added to 96-well plates seeded with 2.5 × 105 HEK293 or HEK293T cells/well. After 2-h incubation at 37°C, each well was supplemented with fresh growth medium to 200 μL and incubated for 3 days at 37°C with 5% CO2. Then, luminescence intensity was measured by adding One-Glo reagent (Promega) followed by using PE EnVision Xcite. Then signal ration was calculated with the equation of Signal ratio (%) = analysis value of sample/mean of analysis value of negative control (NC) sample (AAV-Luc with medium) × 100. Then, analyzed samples with ≤50.0% of signal ratio was judged as positive, and samples with >50.0% of signal ratio was judged as negative.60 Each AAV-vector containing Luciferase gene used are; AAV1-CAG-fLuc (SignaGen Laboratories, no. SL101434); AAV5-CAG-fLuc (SignaGen Laboratories, no. SL101539); AAV9-CAG-fLuc (SignaGen Laboratories, no. SL101440); AAVDJ-fLuc (GeneCopoeia, no. AA272).

GCase activity assay in tissues

GCase activity assay was performed as previously described.30 Briefly, weighed tissues are homogenized with 10–20 mL/g tissue homogenization buffer (250 mM Sucrose, 10 mM Tris [pH 7.5], 1 mM EDTA-2Na, 1% Triton) and then supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 15,000 g for 30 min at 4°C. GCase activity was determined by the amount of conversion from 4-methylumbelliferyl-β-D-glucopyranoside substrate (4MU-Gluc; Sigma-Aldrich, no. M3633) to 4MU by measuring fluorescence using a Cytation 5 plate reader (BioTek) (excitation, 365 nm; emission, 445 nm; top read).

GCase assay in CSF

GCase activity in CSF was performed with commercially available SensoLyte Blue Glucocerebrosidase (GBA) Activity Assay Fluorimetric Kit (ANASPEC, no. AS-72258). Briefly, CSF was centrifuged for 10 min at 1,000 g at 4°C. After careful verification of the assay with NHP CSF, neat CSF without dilution was enough to obtain less than 20% error in dilution linearity and spike-recovery. CSF samples or calibration standard was mixed with equal volume of the substrate and incubated for 3 h at 37°C in dark. The fluorescence intensity was acquired using Cytation 5 plate reader (BioTek) (excitation, 365 nm; emission, 445 nm; top read). GCase enzymatic activity was calculated from calibration curve generated by four-parameter logistic analysis using Prism 9.5.1 software (GraphPad) in a unit of nmol/mL/h. LLOQ was determined as the lowest reference standard point on the calibration curve that yielded a coefficient of variance of less than 20% and relative error below 20% in the interpolated values. This corresponds to 9.8 ng/mL in the solution, which was converted to nmol/mL/h, i.e., an LLOQ of 0.0065 nmol/mL/h.

MSD GCase ELISA assay

GCase protein levels in the cell lysates were determined by using a customized immunosorbent assay, using MSD technology (Mesoscale discovery) as previously described.30 Briefly, cells were lysed with 1% Triton in HEPES buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and total protein concentration was measured with the microBCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Cell lysates were captured by anti-GCase antibody generated in in-house (clone 9E4) and detected by secondary antibody (anti-GCase antibody generated in in-house, clone: TK1E11-E5-G3-002) conjugated with SulfoTag. Plates were read by MSD SECTOR S600.

GCase-HA detection by WES

GCase levels in tissue homogenates were determined by using a capillary based immunoassay system (WES ProteinSimple). Samples (0.25 mg/mL protein) were applied to a 25-capillary cartridge with a 12-to-230-kDa matrix, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After samples and antibody (anti-HA-Tag [C29F4] Rabbit mAb no. 3724) had been pipetted into the pre-filled assay plate from the manufacturer, sample loading, separation, immunoprobing, washing, detection, and analyzed Compass software V.4.0.0.
