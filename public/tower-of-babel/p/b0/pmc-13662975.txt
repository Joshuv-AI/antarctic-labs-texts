Intrahospital handovers before and after an educational intervention: a quality improvement study among ICU nurses
Authors: Atefe Fallah, Mohsen Adib-Hajbaghery, Mahdieh Sabery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective intra-hospital handovers (IHHs) are fundamental to patient safety and high-quality care in critical care settings. Research identifies a lack of standardized knowledge and handover protocols. This quality improvement study aimed to enhance nurses’ performance in intra-hospital handover in ICUs by implementing an educational package.MethodsThis quality improvement study used a pre-post, single-group design. Of 167 ICU nurses, 155 nurses met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled. Each nurse’s performance in intra-hospital handover was assessed twice: once before and once after the intervention, with each nurse serving as their own control. The intervention consisted of a 90-minute lecture, a printed educational booklet, and an educational poster. A validated 38-item checklist was used to evaluate nurses’ performance across pre-transfer, during-transfer, and post-transfer phases. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 16, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsHigher post-intervention frequencies were observed across all three handover phases (p < 0.001). The total median IHH score increased from 103 (IQR = 15) before the intervention to 138 (IQR = 9) afterward. For safety-related practices, checking vital signs before transfer was documented in 51.0% of pre-intervention observations compared to 89.0% post-intervention. Coordinating with the destination department rose from 54.2% to 92.3%. Providing explanations to patients or families was observed in 17.4% of pre-intervention cases versus 89.7% post-intervention. During transfer, the responsible nurse remained with the patient in 30.3% of pre-intervention observations compared to 96.1% post-intervention. In addition, clinical handover by the accompanying nurse increased from 51.6% to 93.5%.ConclusionThe educational intervention was associated with improvement in adherence scores across multiple domains, including patient safety, patient/family communication, documentation, coordination, and equipment/route preparation. These findings suggest that a low-cost, multimodal educational intervention may contribute to improving handover quality in critical care settings. Background

Intra-hospital handovers (IHHs) or intra-hospital transportation (IHTs) are the transfer of responsibilities of patient care through hospital and serve multiple functions such as relaying information and awareness, developing of shared decision-making, and improving the institutional practices to ensure continuity of care and prevent disruptions that could compromise patient outcomes [1, 2].

IHH particularly from the intensive care unit (ICU) to other wards are critical transition point in healthcare delivery because ICU patients often have complex and unstable conditions requiring multifaceted monitoring and interventions [3]. Effective IHHs are fundamental to patient safety and high-quality care in critical care settings. IHH is a well-recognized risk factor for adverse events such as medication errors, diagnosis or treatment delays, readmission, failures in follow-up care and increased healthcare costs [4–6].

Nurses have a great responsibility to protect patients from complication-related handovers [7]. Nurses should assess the patient’s health status before, during and after IHH to avoid any unexpected adverse events [8]. However, evidence reported that 73% of nurses are unfamiliar with the safe handover protocols [9]. Studies illustrated that lack of knowledge of nurses about safe and standard handover has been directly associated with increased adverse events during IHH. [5, 9–10] Some studies have identified the nurses’ training as a key prerequisites for safeguarding standard and safe handovers [11]. Choi et al. reported that educational program for medical staff; particularly nurses reduced the number of adverse events related to handovers from 36.8% to 22.1% [12]. Studies from Iran revealed that the quality of most IHHs is poor and do not follow any standard protocols [13–15]. A review of the literature highlights two main reasons for this suboptimal quality. First, there are no standards and clear policies for IHHs in Iran and many clinical settings continue to rely on informal and unstructured IHH practices. Second, there are many insufficiencies in both nursing curricula content and in-service training program for Iranian nurses. [15–16] Therefore, it is necessary to educate nurses to improve the quality of handovers and reduce dangerous complications [17]. Additionally, some quality improvement studies disclose a structured ISBAR communication tool (Identification, Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation) has improved handover structure, enhanced efficiency, and decreased many communication-related adverse events [18–20]. Based on Standards for Quality Improvement, multifaceted educational packages—including workshops, instructional videos, checklists, and practical guidance—enhance nurse confidence, improve interprofessional communication, and sustain long-term improvements, however, they are underutilized and as a result, nurses’ potential to standardize processes is limited [21]. This lack of targeted education underscores the need for focused quality improvement efforts to address practice gaps, minimize communication breakdowns, and enhance patient safety during these high-risk transitions. To addresses these challenges, this quality improvement study aimed to enhance nurses’ performance in intra-hospital handover in ICUs by implementing an educational package.

Rationale (theoretical framework)

This quality improvement project was guided by a five-phase model for quality improvement, which includes the following sequential steps: prepare, plan, do, evaluate, and follow up [22]. This model was selected because it provides a structured yet flexible roadmap for systematically detecting areas needing improvement, testing interventions, and adjusting them until the desired result is achieved and sustained [23]. We applied this framework as follows:

Prepare phase

This study was based on the patient safety department of the Shahid Beheshti hospital in Kashan, Iran. The patient safety department identified gaps and multiple errors and failure in nurses’ IHH performance, because nurses performed this process orally and there is no valid tool and consistent recording. As a result, two members of the research team subsequently searched databases for evidence-based practices. The project was narrowed to IHHs between ICUs and general-medical wards to ensure it remained manageable.

Plan phase

Because no appropriate audit instrument existed for this context, a checklist was developed and evaluated psychometrically during the primary phase of this research and published as a valid and reliable instrument [24]. A baseline practice assessment was conducted, and the educational package was formulated according to what was feasibly achievable within the constraints of intensive care units.

Do phase

The intervention with using educational package was implemented.

Evaluate phase

A follow-up audit was conducted to measure whether desired improvements had been achieved.

Follow-up phase

Members of research team and the patient safety department reviewed the findings, reflected on them, and planned new interventions aimed at potentially enhancing results further.

Methods

Setting (SQUIRE 2.0)

The study took place in six ICUs at Shahid Beheshti Hospital in Kashan, Iran: two medical ICUs (24 beds), a surgical ICU (8 beds), a neurosurgical ICU (7 beds), a pediatric ICU (8 beds), and an open-heart ICU (5 beds). Nurses’ IHH performance from these six ICUs to four surgical wards (24 beds/each) and four medical wards (24 beds/each) were observed. The study was conducted in 2024 as part of the hospital patient safety program.

Facilitators: (a) strong support from the hospital patient safety department; (b) leadership commitment to reducing handover errors.

Intervention (SQUIRE 2.0)

Description of the educational intervention

An educational package was developed based on literature review and existing protocols. It included three formats: (1) a 90-minute lecture (delivered as part of continuing education), (2) a printed educational booklet, and (3) an educational poster placed on the main wall of each ward as a reminder. Lecture content covered patient safety, pre-transport assessment, patient/equipment/environment preparation, transport team communication, safe transfer techniques, monitoring, risk management, standard checklists, and continuous evaluation. Two experts from the University of Medical Sciences confirmed the accuracy of the package.

Study design (SQUIRE 2.0)

This quality improvement study used a pre-post, single-group design to evaluate IHH performance of nurses from ICU to medical-surgical wards before and after implementing an educational package. The study is reported in accordance with SQUIRE 2.0 [22]. Quality improvement is a continuous process of detecting areas needing improvement, testing interventions, and adjusting them until the desired result is achieved and sustained [23]. A five-phase model (prepare, plan, do, evaluate, and follow up) guided this Quality improvement study [24]. Of 167 ICU nurses, 155 met the inclusion criteria (more than 6 months ICU work experience, active involvement in clinical handover performance and no managing duty).

Phases of data collection

The study was conducted in three phases over eight months (March–October 2024).

Phase 1 (baseline/pre-intervention)

Baseline observations were performed using the validated intra-hospital transportation checklist. The unit of analysis was the nurses’ performance in IHH. In this phase, 155 IHHs were observed (one IHH per nurse).

Phase 2 (intervention)

The educational package was delivered to all ICU nurses, with a 90‑minute lecture serving as its main component. A total of twenty lecture sessions were held, each attended by seven to ten nurses. Participation was mandatory.

Phase 3 (post-intervention)

Two months after the completion of the intervention, IHH from same nurses were observed yielding 155 paired observations.

Instruments

2.Intra-hospital transportation checklist

Intra-hospital transportation checklist

This valid and reliable checklist was developed by Saberi et al. in previous stage to evaluate IHH nurse’s performance in ICUs. Intra-hospital transportation or Intra-hospital handover (IHH) checklist passed all stages for psychometric evaluation (qualitative and quantitative face validity, content validity (CVR, CVI) and construct validity and reliability). The validation process presented a CVI (content validity index) score of 0.9 to 0.99, indicating very good content validity. The interrater reliability was 86% and the Kappa score 0.79 with subscale values ranging from 0.70 to 0.83, representing a very good reliability. Finally, a checklist with 38 items represents care procedures to be followed pre- transfer phase (15 items), during transfer phase (10 items), and post-transfer phase (13 items). All items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale and scored on an ordinal scale: 0 (“Not done” or “Not applicable), 1 (“Incompletely done”), 2 (“Completely done”) [24].

Observers, training, and data accuracy

Data collection was conducted by two master’s degree candidates in critical care nursing, whose role entailed observing IHH nurses’ performance and completing the 38-item checklist.

Observer training: Before formal data collection, both observers underwent a standardized training protocol: (a) a two-hour didactic session reviewing all 38-item checklist, including operational definitions and examples for each item; (b) five practice handovers (not included in the main study) were independently scored by both observers, with discrepancies discussed until consensus was reached; (c) an inter-rater agreement of κ ≥ 0.88 was achieved on the practice set before formal data collection began.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences (Ethical code No.: IR.KAUMS.NUHEPM.REC.1399.034) and is conducted in accordance with the current ethical guidelines, and in accordance with the last version of the Helsinki Declaration, good clinical practice, and national regulation. The handover training program was implemented as part of a mandatory hospital-wide patient safety initiative. However, participation in data collection (observation of handover practices) was entirely voluntary. Study participation was preceded by oral and written information, which explicitly stated that nurses could decline observation without any negative consequences to their employment, performance evaluation, or workplace standing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating nurses prior to any data collection.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 16 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. The unit of analysis was the clinical handover episode. Since the handover adherence scores were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.05) and the data consisted of paired observations (one pre-intervention and one post-intervention handover from the same nurse), non-parametric tests were employed throughout.

To compare the total ordinal scores and phase-specific scores (pre-transfer, during transfer, post-transfer) between baseline and post intervention, the Wilcoxon signed rank test was used. Effect sizes (r) were calculated as Z/√N (N = 310), where r ≥ 0.5 indicates a large effect, r ≥ 0.3 a medium effect, and r ≥ 0.1 a small effect. Median differences with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the Hodges–Lehmann estimator.

Each checklist item has four response categories: “Not done”, “Incompletely done”, “Completely done”, and “Not applicable“(Not applicable – meaning the item did not apply to that particular patient/handover (e.g., the patient had no bandaged areas, no expensive belongings, etc.). When calculating percentages for “Completely done”, “Incompletely done”, and “Not done”, the researchers excluded the “Not applicable” responses from the denominator. The remaining cases are called “Applicable N” (shown in the table’s Applicable N column).

The researchers performed a sensitivity analysis to check whether excluding “Not applicable” responses might distort the statistical results. They wanted to be sure that the way they handled “Not applicable” did not create false positives or miss real differences.

In the sensitivity analysis, the researchers compared two approaches to handling “Not applicable” responses across all 38 handover items. In the main analysis (Approach A), “Not applicable” responses were excluded from the denominator, so percentages for “Completely done,” “Incompletely done,” and “Not done” were calculated based solely on the applicable number of handover episodes (Applicable N). In the sensitivity approach (Approach B), “Not applicable” was treated as a separate ordinal category alongside the other three response options, and the denominator was fixed at the total number of observations (N = 155 for both pre- and post-intervention). The Wilcoxon signed rank test was then applied under both approaches to assess pre-post differences.

Based on the data, certain changes in end-of-transfer handover practices were observed following the intervention period. In the post-intervention period, higher frequencies were documented for several practices. For example, providing explanations to the patient and family about the new department was observed in 5.8% of cases before the intervention compared with 84.5% afterward. Reporting of patient connection status (e.g., catheters, IV lines) was documented in 41.9% of pre-intervention observations compared to 89.7% of post-intervention observations. Similarly, reporting any line-related complications was documented in 3.2% of cases before the intervention and 80% afterward. Notably, clinical handover performed by the accompanying nurse was observed in 51.6% of cases before the intervention and 93.5% afterward (Table 5).

Discussion

Summary of key findings (SQUIRE 2.0)

The educational package was associated with notable increase in adherence scores in patient safety, patient/family communication, documentation, coordination, and equipment/route preparation. Adherence to the practice of providing essential explanations to patients or their families increased from 17.4% at baseline to 89.7% post-intervention. Although this practice was infrequently documented initially, subsequent records demonstrated markedly greater consistency. This improvement is consistent with established ethical and patient-safety guidelines, which underscore the importance of communicating transfer rationale, anticipated clinical outcomes, and potential risks to patients or surrogates. Such disclosure is critical for mitigating anxiety, facilitating informed consent, and reinforcing family-centered care throughout the handover process [26]. Adherence to pre-transport documentation demonstrated substantial improvement across multiple critical domains. Documentation of patient level of consciousness (LOC) increased from 8.4% to 92.3%, while criteria pertaining to inter-departmental coordination and verification of vital signs (blood pressure, temperature, pulse, and respiratory rate)—which ranked among the most deficient items at baseline—exhibited comparable post-intervention gains. Accurate neurological assessment at handover is critical importance for establishing a baseline against which subsequent changes can be measured. It also enables early recognition of clinical deterioration, and guiding therapeutic decisions regarding secondary brain injury or residual sedative effects [28]. In parallel, systematic pre-transport documentation of vital signs ensures physiological stability prior to departure, provides receiving teams with an essential reference point for ongoing comparison, and reduces the risk of unrecognized hemodynamic compromise during transfer. Furthermore, structured communication between sending and receiving departments mitigates information loss, clarifies patient acuity, and facilitates seamless continuity of care. Collectively, these improvements reflect greater fidelity to standardized safety protocols that prioritize pre-transfer stabilization, continuous physiological monitoring, and closed-loop interdisciplinary communication—interventions essential to minimizing physiological instability and preventing adverse events during intra-hospital transitions [29–31]. Together, these pre‑to‑post findings suggest that targeted education may help bridge practice gaps and support more systematic preparation, which could contribute to safer and more reliable care.

The findings further indicated higher post‑intervention frequencies for specific safety‑related practices during IHHs. For example, prior to the intervention, only 7.7% of nurses accompanied the patient the entire clinical handover process, whereas in the post‑intervention period, approximately 90% of nurses remained with their patients from the beginning to the end of handover. According to established guidelines, all patients must be accompanied by a nurse during patient handover. Furthermore, a qualified team should be present during clinical handovers of critically ill patients. For unstable or mechanically ventilated patients, the inclusion of a critical care physician is often advised [32]. These recommendations emphasize multidisciplinary accompaniment (e.g., nurse, physician, and potentially a respiratory therapist or physiotherapist) to ensure that the level of care during patient handover is equivalent to what provided in the originating unit [33]. Previous studies have reported the presence of trained nurse improved handover quality, reduced patient anxiety, and decreased errors (from 35% to 14%) [34–35]. Blakeman et al. found that 30% of adverse events were associated with the absence of the nurse during clinical handovers [36].

In addition, some modest changes occurred in full infection control protocol implementation. Consistent with this, Mouresh et al. showed that educational intervention formed only a negligible, non-significant enhancement in hand hygiene compliance [37]. These findings reflect challenges in area of adherence to infection prevention in busy clinical settings and highlight limits of stand-alone educational approaches in hospital infection control. To secure lasting improvements, comprehensive and multimodal strategies, ongoing monitoring, feedback, and organizational support are likely required.

Explicitly linking the findings to the COM-B model and adult learning principles provides a robust framework for interpreting the mechanisms underlying the success or failure of the intervention. The COM-B model posits that behavior is driven by the interaction of capability, opportunity, and motivation, which is essential for understanding behavioral diagnoses and systematically designing interventions. By applying this lens, barriers such as lack of psychological capability (e.g., knowledge and skills) or insufficient social opportunity (e.g., lack of peer support or role modeling) can be identified and addressed [27]. Similarly, adult learning theory, with its emphasis on self-directed, experiential, and immediately applicable learning, offers a complementary framework that explains why certain educational strategies are more effective than passive knowledge transmission. Recent studies have demonstrated the value of integrating these frameworks; for instance, theory-driven interventions grounded in both behavior change models and adult learning principles have been shown to significantly enhance motivation and intention to change behavior. Thus, a clear linkage between these theoretical models and the findings not only enriches the interpretation of the data but also provides a stronger evidence base for future intervention design [38].

An important methodological consideration in interpreting our findings is the nested structure of the data, with nurses clustered within six ICUs. Nurses working in the same ICU are likely to share common organizational practices, leadership styles, communication norms, and workplace culture, which may create within-group similarities that violate the assumption of independent observations. This clustering effect may have important implications for our findings. Specifically, because nurses within the same ICU may be more similar to each other than to nurses in other ICUs, the observed improvements in handover performance could partly reflect ICU-level contextual factors—such as unit leadership, team dynamics, or pre-existing safety culture—rather than the educational intervention alone. Since we did not perform cluster-adjusted analyses (e.g., multilevel modeling or generalized estimating equations), the reported standard errors and confidence intervals may be underestimated. This could lead to narrower confidence intervals and an increased risk of type I errors, meaning that some statistically significant findings might be partially attributable to shared ICU-level characteristics rather than the intervention itself. Therefore, while our findings demonstrate promising improvements in handover adherence, they should be interpreted with caution, and the magnitude of the intervention effect may vary across different ICU contexts.

Despite this limitation, the consistency of improvements across multiple domains and the significant within-group changes observed over time suggest that the intervention had a genuine positive effect on handover practices. However, future studies with larger sample sizes and a greater number of ICUs should consider employing multilevel analytic approaches to account for the nested data structure and to better isolate the intervention effect from contextual ICU-level factors. Additionally, qualitative research exploring ICU-level differences in implementation and staff engagement could provide valuable insights into how contextual factors moderate the effectiveness of such educational interventions.”

It is also important to acknowledge that the handover checklist used in this study was developed and validated by members of the research team. Although its content validity and inter-rater reliability were established and found to be acceptable, the use of an internally developed instrument may introduce potential measurement bias, including the possibility of overestimating performance improvements due to researcher expectations or limited generalizability of the validation sample. Future studies should consider validating this checklist in independent and diverse clinical settings, as well as exploring the use of objective performance measures or direct observation by independent assessors, to further strengthen confidence in the findings and enhance the instrument’s external validity.

Study limitations (SQUIRE 2.0)

This study has several limitations. First, the single-group pre-post design without a control group cannot establish causality, and we cannot rule out the effects of temporal trends or history. Second, the study was conducted in a single hospital with six ICUs, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other settings, countries, or healthcare systems. Third, observation bias (Hawthorne effect) may have occurred despite integrating observers as team members, and observers were not fully blinded to the study phase because educational posters and booklets were visible on the units. Fourth, we did not assess long-term sustainability of the improvements, as post-intervention data were collected only two months after the intervention. Fifth, we measured handover adherence using a checklist rather than clinically relevant patient outcomes (e.g., adverse events, handover-related errors, patient safety incidents). Sixth, contamination between pre- and post-intervention periods is possible because the same nurses participated in both phases. Seventh, nurses were clustered within six ICUs, and we did not perform cluster-adjusted analyses due to the small number of clusters. Finally, differences in patient severity, ICU type, destination ward, shift, or transfer complexity between the two observation time points were not taken into consideration.

Conclusion

Usefulness of the work (SQUIRE 2.0)

The educational intervention was associated with improvement in adherence scores across multiple domains, including patient safety- related items, patient/family communication, documentation, coordination, and equipment/route preparation. Several critical preparatory steps that were incompletely performed at baseline—such as consciousness assessment, provision of explanations to family members, route safety verification, and documentation of special measures—were observed to be completely done in the vast majority of cases following the intervention. These findings suggest that a low-cost, multimodal educational intervention may be effective in improving handover quality in critical care settings. Nevertheless, infection control practices remained an area requiring ongoing reinforcement, highlighting the need for continued quality improvement efforts.

Sustainability plan (SQUIRE 2.0)

To ensure sustainability of the observed improvements based on SQUIRE 2.0 reporting, the following strategies have been integrated into routine practice: (a) the educational poster remains permanently displayed on the main wall of each ICU and general ward as a visual reminder; (b) the educational booklet was uploaded to the hospital’s internal digital library for new nurses and ongoing reference; (c) the 90-minute lecture has been incorporated into the annual continuing education program for all newly hired ICU nurses; (d) handover performance has been added as a quality indicator in the hospital’s monthly nursing quality report.

Potential for spread (SQUIRE 2.0)

The educational package is low-cost, requires minimal materials (poster, booklet, lecture slides), and can be adapted to other ICUs within Iran or internationally. The intervention components could also be modified for IHHs in other clinical settings, such as emergency departments to medical wards or operating rooms to ICUs.

Implications for practice and further study (SQUIRE 2.0)

Collectively, these observed improvements suggest potential to strengthen the safety and continuity of care during IHH; however, alternative explanations such as temporal trends, increased awareness, or observer effects cannot be ruled out, and findings should be confirmed in controlled studies. Future research should evaluate long-term sustainability beyond two months, assess the impact on patient outcomes (e.g., adverse events, handover-related errors), and test the intervention in a randomized controlled design with a control group to establish causality more rigorously.
