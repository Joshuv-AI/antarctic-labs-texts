Clinical significance of coexisting Hashimoto’s thyroiditis in differentiated thyroid cancer: a retrospective cohort study

Background

Differentiated Thyroid Cancer (DTC) is the most common endocrine malignancy and is generally associated with a favorable prognosis when appropriately managed with surgical and adjuvant therapies [1]. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), the predominant histological subtype of DTC, frequently presents with cervical lymph node metastasis but is characterized by low disease-specific mortality [2]. Despite its typically indolent clinical course, DTC exhibits considerable biological heterogeneity, and a subset of patients develops locally aggressive disease or regional metastatic spread, highlighting the importance of accurate clinicopathological risk stratification [3].

Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis (HT) is a chronic autoimmune disorder of the thyroid gland characterized by diffuse lymphocytic infiltration, follicular destruction, and progressive thyroid dysfunction. It is commonly associated with elevated thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAb), thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAb), and diffuse hypoechoic changes on thyroid ultrasonography. HT predominantly affects middle-aged women and frequently coexists with other autoimmune conditions [2, 4, 5]. Given its high prevalence, HT is often encountered in patients evaluated for thyroid nodular disease, including those subsequently diagnosed with thyroid cancer.

The relationship between HT and DTC has been extensively investigated but remains incompletely understood. Several studies have suggested that HT may be associated with less aggressive tumor characteristics, potentially mediated by enhanced immune surveillance and antitumor immune responses that limit tumor growth and metastatic dissemination [6–8]. In contrast, other investigations have proposed that chronic autoimmune inflammation may facilitate carcinogenesis by promoting oxidative stress, genomic instability, and a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment [9, 10]. Previous studies have therefore reported conflicting findings, with some demonstrating a protective association of HT with less aggressive tumor features, while others have found no independent effect after adjustment for tumor-related factors.

Meta-analyses and retrospective cohort studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding tumor size, lymph node involvement, extrathyroidal extension, recurrence risk, and survival outcomes in patients with DTC and coexisting HT [11, 12]. Variability in diagnostic criteria for HT, heterogeneity of study populations, differences in pathological definitions, and inconsistencies in analytical approaches have limited the comparability of published results [13]. In particular, whether HT independently influences invasive pathological features after adjustment for tumor size remains uncertain. In addition, previous studies have varied in their use of statistical modeling approaches, with some lacking comprehensive multivariable analyses or standardized reporting frameworks, which further complicates interpretation and comparison of findings [14–16].

Against this background, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between coexisting Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis and clinicopathological characteristics of Differentiated Thyroid Cancer in a retrospective cohort of surgically treated patients. Specifically, we assessed differences in tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and pathological indicators of tumor aggressiveness, including capsular invasion and extrathyroidal extension. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine whether HT was independently associated with these features. In addition, exploratory modeling approaches, including receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and nomogram visualization, were applied to assess the relationship between HT and tumor characteristics. By applying a structured analytical framework and acknowledging the inherent limitations of retrospective data, this study seeks to contribute additional evidence to the ongoing discussion regarding the clinical significance of HT in patients with DTC.

Methods

Research design

This study was designed as a retrospective observational cohort study conducted at the Department of Thyroid and Breast Surgery, The Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, Jiangsu Province, People’s Republic of China, a tertiary academic referral center. Consecutive patients who underwent thyroidectomy between June 2023 and March 2025 were considered for inclusion.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and relevant ethical standards. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The reference by Sedgwick was included to support methodological considerations related to retrospective cohort study design and its inherent limitations [17].

Study population and group classification

A total of 355 patient records were initially screened during the study period, of which 198 patients were ultimately included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Eligible patients had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of Differentiated Thyroid Cancer (DTC) and complete surgical, pathological, and preoperative clinical data, including laboratory and ultrasonographic evaluations. All included differentiated thyroid carcinoma cases were histologically classified as papillary thyroid carcinoma.

Exclusion criteria comprised non-DTC thyroid malignancies, such as medullary or anaplastic thyroid carcinoma; coexisting autoimmune thyroid disorders other than Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis (e.g., Graves’ disease); and incomplete clinical or pathological records that precluded reliable analysis. A proportion of initially screened patients was excluded as a result of these criteria, reflecting the application of strict eligibility requirements aimed at ensuring data completeness and analytical consistency.

The final study cohort consisted of 198 patients. Patients were stratified into two groups according to the presence or absence of Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis (HT). The HT group included patients with DTC and coexisting HT, whereas the non-HT group included patients with DTC without evidence of autoimmune thyroiditis. This stratification enabled comparative analysis of clinicopathological characteristics between the two cohorts.

All included patients in the final cohort were male. This atypical sex distribution likely reflects institutional referral patterns and characteristics of the surgical population during the study period and represents an important limitation that may affect the generalizability of the findings.

Definition and diagnosis of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis

Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis was defined using a composite diagnostic approach based on data available in this retrospective cohort. The diagnosis of HT was established when at least one of the following criteria was met:

histopathological evidence of diffuse lymphocytic infiltration with or without germinal center formation in thyroid tissue obtained after thyroidectomy; and/orcharacteristic ultrasonographic features consistent with autoimmune thyroiditis, including diffuse hypoechogenicity and heterogeneous parenchymal architecture.

histopathological evidence of diffuse lymphocytic infiltration with or without germinal center formation in thyroid tissue obtained after thyroidectomy; and/or

histopathological evidence of diffuse lymphocytic infiltration with or without germinal center formation in thyroid tissue obtained after thyroidectomy; and/or

characteristic ultrasonographic features consistent with autoimmune thyroiditis, including diffuse hypoechogenicity and heterogeneous parenchymal architecture.

Biochemical markers of thyroid autoimmunity, including thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAb) and thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAb), as well as thyroid hormone levels, were not systematically available for all patients and therefore were not used as mandatory diagnostic criteria. When available, these parameters were recorded descriptively but were not required for group classification.

In some patients, HT had been diagnosed preoperatively on the basis of ultrasonographic findings and clinical records, whereas in others it was identified incidentally on postoperative histopathological examination. Due to limitations in retrospective documentation, a consistent distinction between preoperative and postoperative diagnosis could not be made across the entire cohort.

This diagnostic approach may reduce specificity and introduce misclassification bias, as lymphocytic infiltration or diffuse hypoechogenicity may also be observed in non-HT conditions. Consequently, some patients classified as HT-positive may not have fulfilled standard biochemical criteria, representing an inherent limitation of the retrospective design.

Data collection and variable definitions

Clinical and pathological data were extracted from institutional hospital information systems and compiled into a unified dataset. All variables were defined prior to analysis based on clinical relevance and availability in the medical records.

Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex), laboratory parameters (thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH], free triiodothyronine [FT3], free thyroxine [FT4], TPOAb, TgAb, thymidine kinase 1 [TK1], and lipid profile), and pathological features (tumor size, lymph node status, capsular invasion, and extrathyroidal extension).

Tumor size was obtained from formal pathology reports and recorded as a continuous variable. When necessary, numerical tumor size values were extracted from narrative pathology descriptions using regular expression parsing in R. Missing values for continuous variables were imputed using mean substitution, while missing categorical variables were imputed using the mode.

Definition of tumor aggressiveness

Tumor aggressiveness was defined on the basis of pathological evidence of capsular invasion and/or extrathyroidal extension identified on postoperative histopathological examination. Lymph node metastasis was analyzed as a separate clinicopathological characteristic and was not included in the composite definition of tumor aggressiveness in order to avoid conceptual overlap in multivariate analyses.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0) [18]. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between patients with and without Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables [19] and the chi-square test for categorical variables [20]. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate whether HT was independently associated with tumor aggressiveness, including HT status and tumor size as predictor variables [21]. Tumor size was entered into the regression model as a continuous variable measured in centimeters. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05. All statistical tests were two-tailed.

Predictive modeling and validation

Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with calculation of the area under the curve (AUC) [22]. A nomogram was developed using the rms package in R to provide a graphical representation of the multivariate model [23]. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 100 iterations. Model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots and mean absolute error.

The predictive model was developed for exploratory purposes to assess associations rather than to establish a clinically applicable prediction tool.

No formal sample size or power calculation was performed, as the study was based on a fixed cohort derived from retrospective data.

Results

Patient cohort and group characteristics

A total of 355 patient records were initially screened during the study period. Following application of the predefined eligibility criteria, 198 patients with histologically confirmed differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). All included tumors were histologically consistent with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), while no cases of follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) were identified in the analyzed cohort. Among these, 92 patients (46.5%) had coexisting Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis (HT), while 106 patients (53.5%) had DTC without evidence of HT.

All analyses presented below were performed on this final cohort of 198 patients, in accordance with the study design. No analyses were conducted on excluded patients.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinicopathological characteristics stratified by HT status are summarized in Table 1. All included patients were male. The mean age did not differ significantly between groups (HT: 42.43 ± 11.51 years vs. non-HT: 42.36 ± 11.45 years; p = 0.954).

Tumor size was significantly smaller in patients with HT compared with those without HT (median [IQR]: 9 mm [6.5–12] vs. 12 mm [9–17]; p = 0.003).

Lymph node metastasis was observed significantly less frequently in the HT group (4.1%, n = 8) than in the non-HT group (46.8%, n = 74; p < 0.001).

Tumor aggressiveness, defined as the presence of capsular invasion and/or extrathyroidal extension, was comparable between groups (HT: 41.1% vs. non-HT: 43.7%; p = 0.707).

Tumor aggressiveness

Tumor aggressiveness, defined as capsular invasion and/or extrathyroidal extension, did not differ significantly between the two groups (HT: 41.1% vs. non-HT: 43.7%; p = 0.707).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to assess whether Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis was independently associated with tumor aggressiveness. Tumor aggressiveness was defined exclusively as the presence of capsular invasion and/or extrathyroidal extension.

After adjustment for tumor size, Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis was not independently associated with aggressive tumor features (odds ratio [OR] = 1.10, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.70–1.73; p = 0.692). In contrast, larger tumor size was independently associated with increased odds of tumor aggressiveness (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.11–1.99; p = 0.008). Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical significance of coexisting Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis (HT) in patients with Differentiated Thyroid Cancer (DTC) within a well-defined retrospective cohort of 198 patients. The principal findings of this study were that patients with HT presented with significantly smaller tumors and a markedly lower prevalence of lymph node metastasis compared with patients without HT. However, after adjustment for tumor size, HT was not independently associated with pathological tumor aggressiveness, defined as capsular invasion and/or extrathyroidal extension.

The observed association between HT and smaller tumor size is consistent with previous studies suggesting a potential modulatory role of autoimmune thyroiditis in thyroid cancer biology. One plausible explanation is enhanced immune surveillance mediated by lymphocytic infiltration, which may constrain tumor growth and delay progression. In addition, patients with autoimmune thyroid disorders are more likely to undergo regular clinical and ultrasonographic monitoring, which may lead to earlier tumor detection at a smaller size. These mechanisms may collectively explain the reduced tumor size observed in HT-positive patients.

A similarly strong inverse association was observed between HT and lymph node metastasis. Although lymph node involvement was not included in the definition of tumor aggressiveness in this study, in accordance with contemporary risk stratification frameworks, its markedly lower prevalence in the HT group remains clinically relevant. However, lymph node metastasis was analyzed as a binary variable, and detailed pathological features such as the number of involved lymph nodes, metastatic size, and extranodal extension were not available. These factors are increasingly recognized as important determinants of recurrence risk and clinical outcomes and may provide a more nuanced understanding of disease behavior. Previous studies, including those by Kim et al. and Issa et al., have also reported reduced nodal metastasis and less aggressive pathological features in patients with HT, supporting a potential interaction between autoimmune processes and tumor spread.

Importantly, multivariate analysis demonstrated that tumor size, rather than HT status, was the only independent predictor of aggressive pathological features. After adjustment, HT was not associated with capsular invasion or extrathyroidal extension. This finding suggests that the apparent protective association of HT observed in unadjusted analyses is likely mediated through its association with smaller tumor size rather than a direct effect on tumor invasiveness. These results emphasize the importance of multivariable analysis in avoiding overinterpretation of unadjusted associations.

The predictive model developed in this study demonstrated limited discriminative performance, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.587. This indicates that the model has limited clinical applicability for predicting tumor aggressiveness. Although calibration analysis showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, the overall performance suggests that additional biological, molecular, or detailed pathological factors are required to improve predictive accuracy. Therefore, the model should be interpreted as exploratory rather than clinically applicable.

The relationship between HT and thyroid cancer remains complex and, at times, contradictory. While some studies have proposed that chronic inflammation may promote carcinogenesis through cytokine-mediated pathways and oxidative stress, the present findings do not support a pro-aggressive role of HT in established DTC. Instead, HT appears to be associated with less advanced disease at presentation without independently influencing invasive tumor behavior. Recent literature has further highlighted the role of the immune microenvironment in thyroid cancer progression, suggesting that autoimmune activity may influence tumor behavior through complex immunomodulatory pathways. Variations in diagnostic criteria for HT, patient selection, and outcome definitions likely contribute to inconsistencies across studies.

From a clinical perspective, HT may provide contextual information regarding disease presentation, particularly with respect to tumor size and lymph node involvement. However, given the lack of an independent association with pathological aggressiveness, HT status alone should not influence surgical decision-making or adjuvant treatment strategies. Current guideline-based risk stratification approaches remain appropriate, and HT should be considered an adjunctive clinical factor rather than a determinant of management.

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection and information bias, and the lack of detailed stratification of excluded cases limits assessment of recruitment bias. Second, the diagnostic criteria for Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis were based on histopathological and ultrasonographic findings without systematic incorporation of biochemical markers such as TPOAb and TgAb. This represents a significant methodological limitation, as the absence of standardized biochemical confirmation may have led to misclassification of HT status within the study cohort. Third, lymph node metastasis was evaluated in a simplified manner without detailed characterization, which may limit interpretation within modern risk stratification frameworks. Fourth, the cohort consisted exclusively of male patients, which is highly atypical for both DTC and HT and represents a major limitation. Additionally, all included tumors were papillary thyroid carcinoma, and therefore the findings may not be generalizable to other differentiated thyroid cancer subtypes such as follicular thyroid carcinoma.

The exclusively male composition of the study cohort warrants particular attention. Both DTC and Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis are known to be more prevalent in female populations, and sex-related biological differences may influence tumor behavior, immune response, and disease progression. Consequently, the observed associations in this study may not be generalizable to female patients and may either underestimate or overestimate the true relationship between HT and tumor characteristics. The findings of this study should therefore be interpreted as specific to a male patient population and should not be extrapolated to broader clinical settings without caution.

Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up data precludes evaluation of recurrence and survival outcomes, which are critical endpoints in thyroid cancer research. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating standardized diagnostic criteria, long-term outcomes, and molecular profiling are needed to further clarify the role of autoimmune thyroiditis in thyroid cancer biology.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort, coexisting Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer was associated with smaller tumor size and a lower prevalence of lymph node metastasis at presentation.

However, after adjustment for tumor size, Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis was not independently associated with pathological tumor aggressiveness, defined as capsular invasion and/or extrathyroidal extension. Tumor size remained the only independent predictor of aggressive pathological features.

These findings suggest that Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis may influence disease presentation but does not independently determine invasive tumor behavior. Accordingly, HT status should not be used as a standalone prognostic factor in clinical decision-making.

Further prospective, multicenter studies incorporating standardized diagnostic criteria, detailed pathological assessment, and long-term outcomes are required to better define the role of autoimmune thyroiditis in thyroid cancer progression.
