Targeted ankle proprioceptive training improves balance, gait, and functional mobility in chronic stroke survivors: a multicenter randomized controlled trial with longitudinal follow-up
Authors: Muslim Khan, Ayman Abdullah Alhammad, Mshari Alghadier, Saeed Mufleh Alnasser, Abdullah Basheer Alanazi, Hasan Ali Abdullah AlAidarous, Engy BadrEldin S Moustafa, Mohammad Ahmad Sharahily, Mohammed Ibrahim Amri, Edward Muteesasira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne of the most common predictors of post-stroke balance and gait problems is ankle proprioceptive impairment. Previous cross-sectional studies have shown strong links, especially with inversion proprioception, but causality, progression over time, and effectiveness in severe cases has not been proven.ObjectiveTo determine the causal effects of ankle proprioceptive training on balance, gait, and mobility in moderate-to-severe and non-ambulatory chronic stroke survivors, and to examine the long-term impact of proprioception training across different stroke stages.MethodsA total of 132 participants (mean age 58.4 ± 11.2 years; 18 to 72 months post stroke) completed the 12 weeks of intervention and immediate post-intervention assessments, (68 were randomly assigned to the intervention group(proprioceptive ankle training) while 64 to the control group(standard rehabilitation)).The primary outcome was weight-bearing ankle proprioception, assessed with the Active Movement Extent Discrimination Apparatus (AMEDA) while the secondary outcomes included the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Timed Up and Go Test (TUG), 10-meter walk test (10-MWT), Fugl-Meyer Lower Extremity Test (FM-LE), and Functional Ambulation Category(FAC). An assessment from acute to chronic stages was conducted on a longitudinal subsample (n = 42).ResultsThe intervention led to significant and clinically meaningful improvements in proprioception (inversion Δ = 0.21), balance(BBS + 12.4 points), gait speed (+ 0.32 m/s)(TUG), and mobility(10-MWT, FM-LE, FAC), which were sustained at 6-month follow-up (all p < 0.001). Benefits were evenly observed in severe/non-ambulatory individuals who experienced a stroke. This was supported by mediation analysis showing that 72% of the functional gains in severe/non-ambulatory chronic stroke participants were influenced by improved inversion proprioception. Longitudinal data indicated a progressive bilateral decline, with the earliest and steepest drop occurring in inversion.ConclusionTargeted proprioceptive exercises are causally efficacious in enhancing functional recovery across all levels of severity in post stroke populations and it therefore compliments routine clinical practice.Trial registration This study was retrospectively registered at Clinical Trials.gov (Registration Number NCT07420608) on 18th February,2026.Keywords: Stroke rehabilitation, Ankle proprioception, Inversion, Causality, Balance, Gait, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of long-term disability among adults worldwide, with millions of survivors experiencing lasting motor dysfunction that significantly impacts independence, living standards, and community involvement [1–3]. Deficits in balance and gait are two of the most common and debilitating sequelae, affecting more than 80% of survivors even years after the event. These impairments not only reduce functional mobility but also significantly increase the risk of falls, a serious complication in stroke recovery, with up to 73% of community-dwelling survivors experiencing at least one fall each year [3, 4]. Falls can lead to secondary injuries, fear of falling, social isolation, institutionalization, and substantial healthcare costs [3, 5]. Although motor weakness, spasticity, and coordination impairments have continued to take center stage in stroke rehabilitation paradigms, emerging evidence from the last 10 years has highlighted the critical and often under-reported role of somatosensory impairments specifically proprioceptive acuity in the ankle, amid ongoing balance and gait issues [5, 6].

Proprioception, which involves joint position and movement sense, is essential for controlling posture, weight distribution, and accurate foot placement during walking. In stroke survivors, ankle proprioceptive deficits are widespread and bilateral, and inversion/eversion movements are frequently affected due to biomechanical loading during dynamic stance stability and swing-phase clearance [4, 7]. Balanced ankle inversion proprioception, assessed with validated tools such as the Active Movement Extent Discrimination Apparatus (AMEDA) [8], has consistently been shown to be the strongest independent predictor of balance dysfunction (measured by the Berg Balance Scale) and slower gait speed in individuals that experienced a stroke. It also surpasses traditional predictors such as ankle strength or spasticity [8–10]. These correlations persist even when accounting for confounding factors, indicating a direct mechanistic connection. Bilateral deficits, often noticeable even on the so-called unaffected side, suggest central rather than peripheral sensorimotor impairments, potentially due to altered cortical somatosensory representation, interhemispheric inhibition, or increased reliance on compensatory visual and vestibular responses. Robotic-assisted ankle positioning, textured surfaces, whole-body vibration, and proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation are among the approaches reported to improve both proprioceptive acuity and downstream motor outcomes [7, 11–13].

Although these cross-sectional results are strong and consistent, several critical gaps hinder their translation into clinical practice. First, causality is not established: it remains unclear whether functional limitations are directly caused by impaired ankle proprioception or if they are simply indicators of extensive neurological injury and spontaneous recovery. Second, the progression of proprioceptive impairment throughout the stroke recovery process including acute, subacute, and chronic stages, has not been well characterized, and it is uncertain whether deficits can stabilize, improve spontaneously, or deteriorate gradually. Third, the generalizability of the observed associations and potential treatment effects among individuals with moderate-to-severe stroke or non-ambulatory status is uncertain because most previous studies do not systematically examine these groups [12–14].

These unresolved questions were intended to be systematically addressed in this multicenter randomized controlled trial. We hypothesized that improvements in ankle proprioception induced by the intervention would mediate better and clinically meaningful gains in balance, gait speed, and functional mobility. The second objective was to describe longitudinal proprioceptive changes between the acute and chronic phases within a subsample and to investigate lasting effects at the 6-month follow-up. The overall goal of this trial was to establish causality, clarify the temporal course, and assess the effectiveness of the protocol in severe /non-ambulatory individuals that experienced a stroke.

Methods

Study design & setting

This was a multicenter randomized controlled trial and the study adhered to the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) Statement. The study was conducted in the following centers; (I) Saidu Group of Teaching Hospital(STGHT), (II) Iqra National University, (III) Swat Psychiatric Care & Rehabilitation Center (SPCRC), (IV) Al-Makki Rehabilitation Center, (V) Hashoo Foundation Rehabilitation Center and lastly, (VI) Model Addiction Treatment & Rehabilitation Centre (MATRC) - Swat (Govt. of KP). These centers were selected according to the following criteria; (I) High annual stroke patient volume (> 200 cases/year), (II) Availability of certified physical therapists specialized in neurorehabilitation, (III) Access to robotic equipment, and lastly, (IV) Geographic diversity to ensure broad participant representation.

Participants

Participants selection, enrollment and follow-up was conducted from January 2023 to December 2024. Recruitment was done through physician referrals from neurology and rehabilitation clinics, electronic health record screenings, community advertisements in stroke support groups, and flyers at the participating centers. Potential candidates were screened via phone or in-person assessments to confirm eligibility.

Inclusion criteria; adults aged 18 years or older with a history of chronic ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke (18 to 72 months post-stroke), moderate-to-severe lower extremity impairment (Fugl-Meyer Lower Extremity score [15] ≤ 28 or NIHSS motor leg score [16] ≥ 2),documented ankle proprioceptive deficits (AMEDA, AUC < 0.7), ability to follow instructions, and capacity to provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria; acute medical conditions (e.g., uncontrolled hypertension, recent surgery), other neurological disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s disease), severe cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination score [17] < 24), contraindications to exercise (e.g., unstable cardiac conditions), or participation in conflicting treatments trials.

Sample size determination; The sample size of 140 (70 per group) was determined beforehand using G*Power software to detect a minimum clinically significant difference of 5 points on the AMEDA(primary outcome measure), assuming a standard deviation of 8 points based on prior studies [8–10], 80% power, alpha of 0.05 (two-tailed), and a 20% attrition rate. The sample was intentionally increased by approximately 40% compared to typical convenience samples (e.g., n = 100) to allow for robust subgroup analyses by severity (mild-moderate NIHSS ≤ 15 vs. severe > 15) and to improve generalizability, including non-ambulatory individuals (Functional Ambulation Category ≤ 2). Stratification ensured a balanced distribution across severity levels and sites. A longitudinal subsample (n = 42) was selected from participants with available acute-phase data (within 1-month post-stroke) through convenience sampling to track proprioceptive changes.

A total of 210 individuals that experienced a stroke were screened for eligibility, after applying our eligibility criteria,70 of them were excluded and 140 were randomly assigned to the intervention (n = 70) and control group (n = 70). High retention was achieved (91% at 6-month follow-up),with no significant differences in dropout reasons between groups as summarized in the above Fig. 1.

Randomization and blinding

Random sequence was generated via computer in blocks of four, stratified by center. Allocation was concealed using opaque, sealed envelopes. Participants in the control group received standard rehabilitation alongside “sham” robotic sessions where the ankle robot provided passive movement without the active discrimination tasks or weight-bearing challenges used in the intervention group. This ensured that participants remained unaware of their group assignment while receiving equal therapist contact time. Outcome assessors (independent therapists) were blinded to allocation while the data analysts coded data without group identifiers. Furthermore, therapists followed a rigid, audited protocol to minimize subjective influence on the delivery of standard versus targeted care. However, treating researchers were not blinded due to the nature of the intervention delivery.

Interventions

Both groups participated in 12 weeks of supervised therapy, three 60-minute sessions weekly which were conducted alongside routine care.

Intervention Group: The Intervention Group received a targeted protocol utilizing a 2-DOF (degrees of freedom) robotic ankle exoskeleton*. The device operated primarily in impedance control mode to facilitate active movement discrimination. This robotic system was used exclusively for training and is distinct from the AMEDA used for outcome assessment. The training focused on inversion/eversion and plantarflexion/dorsiflexion directions, with task complexity increased by reducing the discrimination angle and removing real-time visual biofeedback. In order to eliminate task-specific learning effects, the AMEDA was used strictly for assessment, and no AMEDA-based tasks were included in the training sessions. Outcome measures

Assessments were conducted at baseline, immediately post-intervention (12 weeks), and at 6-month follow-up by blinded assessors.

Primary Outcome measure: Weight-bearing ankle proprioceptive acuity was evaluated using the Active Movement Extent Discrimination Apparatus (AMEDA) [20], a validated and ecologically valid tool with proven reliability in stroke populations. It is important to note that the AMEDA is a specialized diagnostic instrument used solely for measurement in this study. Furthermore, it does not provide for robotic actuation or automated impedance control which was utilized during the training intervention. Separate scores (area under the curve, AUC; range 0.5–1.0, with higher scores indicating better performance) were obtained for inversion and plantarflexion directions on both the affected and unaffected sides.

Secondary Outcome measures:

Balance: Berg Balance Scale [21] (BBS; 0–56, higher scores indicate better balance).

Mobility and dynamic balance: Timed Up and Go Test [22] (TUG); seconds, fewer seconds indicate better mobility).

Gait speed: 10-Meter Walk Test [23] (10-MWT; at a comfortable pace, meters per second).

Lower-extremity motor impairment: Fugl-Meyer Lower Extremity Test [15] (FM-LE); 0–34, higher scores indicate less impairment).

Functional ambulation: Functional Ambulation Category [24] (FAC; 0–5, higher scores indicate better walking ability).

Fall-related self-efficacy: Falls Efficacy Scale-International [25] (lower scores indicate greater confidence).Adverse events (e.g., falls, pain, fatigue) were monitored and reported.

A separate longitudinal subsample (n = 42, balanced across groups) underwent additional AMEDA assessments at acute (< 4 weeks post-stroke), subacute (3 months), and chronic (12 months) phases to characterize proprioceptive trajectories. The longitudinal subsample subgroup was defined as those with baseline data within 1 month of stroke and the sample size for this subsample was limited by retrospective data availability.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using intention-to-treat principles to assess primary and secondary outcomes. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and baseline measures (means, standard deviations for continuous variables; frequencies, percentages for categorical). Missing data were handled via Mixed-Effects Models for Repeated Measures (MMRM), which accounts for missingness without requiring listwise deletion. Between-group differences in proprioceptive accuracy, balance (BBS), gait speed (10-MWT), and functional mobility (TUG) were evaluated with mixed-effects models for repeated measures, accounting for time (baseline, post-intervention, 6-month follow-up), group (intervention vs. control), and time-by-group interactions. Covariates included age, stroke severity (NIHSS), and time post-stroke. Mediation analysis examined whether proprioceptive improvements mediated mobility gains, using bootstrapped confidence intervals (5,000 resamples). Subgroup analyses explored effects by severity (moderate vs. severe). Statistical significance was set at alpha = 0.05 (two-tailed), with adjustments for multiple comparisons via Bonferroni correction. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) with packages lme4 for mixed models and mediation for mediation tests.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

One hundred and forty participants were randomized, and the study achieved high retention rates. A total of 132 participants completed the 12-week intervention and immediate post-intervention assessments (68 in the intervention group and 64 in the control group), and 128 participants (91%) were available at the 6-month follow-up. Unrelated medical reasons (n = 6) and voluntary withdrawals (n = 6) were the leading causes of dropouts, with no difference between groups in dropout reasons. Baseline characteristics were well balanced across both groups; the mean age was 58.4 ± 11.2 years, 69% were male respondents, and the average time since stroke was 32.6 ± 18.4 months. The distribution of stroke severity was even, with 41% classified as severe (NIHSS > 15), and approximately 30% were non-ambulatory or required monitoring to walk at baseline 2). Such demographic balance supports valid between-group comparisons.

Primary outcome measure: ankle proprioception

Building on the balanced baseline profile, the intervention group showed significantly greater improvements in the primary outcome measure, weight-bearing ankle proprioception compared to the control group. The most notable gains were seen in inversion acuity, especially on the affected side.

Specifically, the intervention group improved by 0.21 (95% CI;0.17–0.25) in affected-side inversion proprioception, compared to only 0.04 in the control group (group × time interaction p < 0.001) as shown in Table 2. Similar patterns appeared for plantarflexion (Δ = 0.18 vs. 0.03) and on the unaffected side, indicating bilateral benefits from the targeted training. Importantly, these proprioceptive improvements were largely maintained at the 6-month follow-up (Fig. 3), paving the way for examining downstream functional enhancements.

Longitudinal proprioceptive trajectories

To supplement the intervention findings, the longitudinal subsample (n = 42) provided informative background on the natural history of proprioceptive impairment. The AMEDA scores showed progressive bilateral decline during both the acute and chronic phases, with the fastest deterioration during inversion in the subacute period (Fig. 4). The affected side declined earlier and more rapidly than the unaffected side; therefore, early sensory-targeted interventions might help slow this progression.

Discussion

This multicenter randomized controlled trial provides strong evidence that a 12-week targeted proprioceptive ankle training protocol, which includes both robotic-assisted discrimination tasks and gradual weight-bearing challenges, has produced significant and lasting improvements in balance, gait speed, and functional mobility in chronic stroke survivors. These benefits were observed across the full range of severity, including both moderate-to-severe and non-ambulatory individuals who experienced a stroke, who are often underrepresented in rehabilitation trials.

The most notable results are significant improvements on the most-affected side inversion proprioception (D = 0.21 on AMEDA), which mediated most of the functional changes (72% overall), as well as significant gains in BBS (+ 12.4 points), gait speed (+ 0.32 m/s), and TUG (-4.8 s). Effect sizes (d 1.6–1.9) were higher than those typically observed with general balance or strength training (d 0.8) and comparable to or better than robotic gait training alone (d 1.1). These large effect sizes (d > 1.6) likely reflect the high sensitivity of individuals with a stroke to mechanism-driven sensory challenges compared to non-specific motor training. The long-term effects at 6-month follow-up may suggest lasting neuroplasticity rather than temporary compensation [11, 14, 27, 28].

The findings are consistent and expand on previous evidence regarding sensory-specific intervention after stroke. In a 2022 meta-analysis, Mao et al.2022,of proprioceptive training protocols, results showed a moderate to large effect on balance and gait, especially when the protocol emphasized discrimination training and weight-bearing exercises [11]. Likewise, studies on robotic ankle training have demonstrated improved motor control and walking symmetry, although they are usually conducted on smaller samples that exclude severe cases [29–31]. The causality between improved inversion proprioception and functional gains, along with our focus on non-ambulatory subjects and mediation analysis, is unique. Cross-sectional studies had previously shown that inversion acuity was the strongest predictor of balance and gait impairments [11, 32–34]. These results emphasize the importance of personalized proprioceptive training in rehabilitation, particularly for those with chronic strokes, as it could potentially enhance their recovery process.

The longitudinal subsample also observed ongoing bilateral proprioceptive impairment, most pronounced during inversion in the subacute phase. This pattern aligns with central sensorimotor disturbances that extend to what is referred to as the normal side. This highlights the importance of prompt sensory treatment to prevent worsening impairments and the perception of spontaneous recovery in the chronic phase [35, 36]. It also emphasizes the critical need for continued rehabilitation efforts to maintain proprioceptive deficits over time. These findings stress the importance of integrating proprioceptive training into stroke survivor rehabilitation programs, especially to address bilateral losses experienced in both the most affected and less affected sides [37, 38].

These results have clinical implications in that weight-bearing ankle proprioception assessment (e.g., through AMEDA or low-cost alternatives) and specific training should be included in stroke protocols, and sensory-based methods are generally the exception [39]. The protocol was found to be safe and feasible even in severe cases, with notable improvements in functional ambulation (e.g., + 2.4 FAC categories in the severe subgroup), likely because the baseline impairment was more severe and retained its plasticity [14, 40]. As we compare and contrast different approaches, such as whole-body vibration techniques, the use of textured insoles that enhance sensory feedback, or the broadest approach to general proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation, we can see that our innovative method of sensory-specific robotic integration has achieved remarkably large effect sizes. This highlights the effectiveness and potential of our method compared to these alternatives [7, 11–13].

The opportunity to introduce hybrid systems that combine robotic support with Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES) into proprioceptive training procedures represents a promising frontier for enhancing neuroplasticity during chronic stroke rehabilitation. This integration may enhance therapeutic effects, increase engagement in rehabilitation sessions, and provide real-time feedback. Recent articles indicate that both FES and robotic actuation can improve trajectory tracking and reduce muscle fatigue; therefore, training can be extended longer without compromising its effectiveness [41–44].This intervention can be especially beneficial for individuals with a stroke, who may have both proprioceptive and motor control impairments, leading to more comprehensive recovery results. Additionally, since ongoing research continues to explore the mechanistic roles of sensory feedback during rehabilitation, understanding how these technologies can be optimally integrated to maximize proprioceptive training will be crucial for enhancing rehabilitation outcomes in this population [45–48].

Limitations

The primary limitations of this study include the reliance on highly specialized robotic devices, which may limit clinical accessibility; additionally, the longitudinal subsample used convenience sampling, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. However, the high intensity of this protocol (180 min/week) may exceed standard outpatient care, potentially limiting direct clinical translation without increased resources which may hinder its implementation in resource limited settings. Future directions should include head-to-head comparisons between multidirectional and strength-only training trials during the acute phase to prevent early decline, neuroimaging to map plasticity, and broad application of cost-effectiveness in studies. In conclusion, retrospective registration in the trial registry can reduce methodological transparency and introduce a potential risk of outcome switching, where primary endpoints could be altered based on observed data trends. Although our study endpoints remained consistent with our original internal protocol throughout the trial, future studies should consider the retrospective timeline when evaluating the study’s transparency framework.

Clinical recommendations

Based on the trial findings, the following practices are recommended for clinical adoption:

I.Prioritize Sensory Assessment: Weight-bearing ankle proprioception (e.g., via AMEDA or similar validated tools) should be a standard part of post-stroke evaluations to identify “silent” sensory deficits that predict fall risk.

II.Focus on Inversion Specifically: Rehabilitation protocols should specifically target inversion proprioception, as this direction is the most effective mediator of balance and gait speed recovery.

Conclusion

In conclusion, proprioceptive ankle training causally and durably enhances recovery in stroke severity and addresses key gaps in causality, temporal evolution, and applicability. These findings support a shift toward sensory-prioritized paradigms to optimize outcomes for millions of stroke-survivors worldwide. This evidence highlights the importance of targeted proprioceptive rehabilitation interventions that include treating both sides of the body to enhance functional recovery and reduce fall risk in stroke survivors [49].

Future research should focus on assessing and designing rehabilitation protocols that combine proprioceptive training with conventional motor therapies to optimize recovery outcomes for this population. Incorporating proprioceptive training into rehabilitation guidelines would be highly beneficial for recovery and balance improvement, ultimately decreasing the risk of falls in stroke survivors [50]. This method can lead to more tailored rehabilitation plans that address the specific needs of stroke survivors and enhance overall recovery and quality of life [28].
