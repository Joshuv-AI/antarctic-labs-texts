Outcomes of cannulation strategies under antegrade cerebral perfusion in type a aortic dissection surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Francesco Zito, Pascal J Govers, Kevin M Veen, Jos A Bekkers, Jelena Sjatskig, Wouter J van Leeuwen, Giovanni Melina, Johanna J M Takkenberg, Jolanda Kluin, Bardia Arabkhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveArterial cannulation strategies remain a topic of debate in type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) repair surgery due to their variability and the inconsistent use of concomitant cerebral perfusion techniques. This study aims to compare clinical outcomes associated with different arterial cannulation strategies in patients undergoing ATAAD repair with antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP).MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted to identify studies comparing clinical outcomes of at least two arterial cannulation strategies (subclavian, femoral or direct aortic) in ATAAD repair surgery with ACP. Primary outcomes were both early mortality and postoperative stroke (in-hospital events or within 30-days after surgery).ResultsSix studies were included from 921 screened, comprising 1694 subclavian, 315 femoral, and 132 direct cannulation cases. The underlying patient characteristics of within-study comparisons were not balanced. Especially the femoral-subclavian comparison was characterized by more presenting symptoms (cardiac tamponade, hemodynamic shock) in the femoral group. Subclavian cannulation was associated with significantly lower early mortality compared to femoral cannulation (OR:0.64,95%CI:0.44–0.93, p = 0.022), but no significant difference in favor of direct cannulation was observed (OR:0.82,95%CI:0.37–1.85, p = 0.13) or between femoral and direct aortic cannulation (OR: 1.80, 95% CI: 0.22–15.33; p = 0.15). No significant differences in early postoperative stroke incidence were observed among the strategies: subclavian vs. femoral (OR:0.91,95%CI:0.55–1.49, p = 0.72), direct vs. femoral (OR:0.63, 95%CI:0.18–2.12, p = 0.45), and direct vs. subclavian (OR: 0.69,95%CI:0.19–2.51, p = 0.81).ConclusionsIn acute type A aortic dissection surgery with antegrade cerebral perfusion, existing evidence is limited by confounding from patient risk profiles and lack of power. Early mortality was lower with subclavian cannulation compared to femoral cannulation. No significant differences were observed between other strategies, although the direction of effect suggests a potential benefit of direct over femoral cannulation. Larger, adequately powered studies are needed to define technique-specific benefits in different patient subgroups.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04409-x.Keywords: Arterial cannulation, Aortic dissection surgery, Antegrade cerebral perfusion

Introduction

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a life-threatening condition that requires immediate surgical intervention in most cases. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative management, ATAAD remains associated with considerable morbidity and mortality, with an in-hospital mortality rate that has decreased from 31% to 22% due to better surgical outcomes [1]. Over the years, several aspects of surgical care have been refined to improve outcomes, including arterial cannulation strategies, which play a critical role in optimizing cerebral and visceral perfusion during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) [3].

Broadly categorized into antegrade and retrograde approaches, these techniques differ in terms of their physiological mechanisms, technical feasibility, and associated risks [4–6].

Antegrade cannulation strategies include direct cannulation of the dissected ascending aorta or the use of the subclavian artery. Antegrade flow is thought to reduce the risk of neurological complications by minimizing retrograde embolization and enhancing cerebral perfusion [7]. While direct aortic cannulation offers the advantage of rapid initiation of CPB, it might be technically demanding, particularly in the presence of fragile aortic tissue. The subclavian approach, on the other hand, provides a safer alternative in terms of vessel integrity but may delay the onset of CPB, posing challenges in emergencies.

Retrograde cannulation, performed via the femoral artery, is widely employed due to its technical simplicity and rapid deployment, which is particularly beneficial in emergency settings. However, studies have reported its possible association with cerebral and visceral malperfusion, as well as potential embolization caused by dislodged atherosclerotic debris taken by the retrograde blood flow [4, 7].

The optimal cannulation strategy remains a topic of debate, especially in heterogeneous patient populations where neurological outcomes may be influenced by various factors, including the cerebral perfusion techniques employed. Among these, antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP) is recognized as a key determinant of neurological outcomes in ATAAD surgery [2, 8, 9]. However, variability in the use of ACP across studies complicates comparisons between cannulation strategies, as observed differences in outcomes may reflect perfusion strategy rather than the cannulation site itself. Despite advances in the field, current guidelines are inconclusive, with limited high-quality evidence to definitively recommend one approach over the other [7, 10]. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to compare clinical outcomes of different arterial cannulation strategies in patients undergoing ATAAD repair with antegrade cerebral perfusion, focusing on early postoperative mortality and neurological outcomes.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. This study was approved by the institutional review board (MEC-2024-0721).

Search strategy and selection criteria

A biomedical information specialist conducted a comprehensive search on 14th of May 2024 in Embase, Medline, Web of Science, Cochrane, and Google Scholar databases (search terms are available in Supplementary Material Table S1-Text S1). Two researchers (PG and FZ) independently screened abstracts and full texts based on predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria. Studies that reported outcomes after different cannulation strategies in ATAAD repair surgery, published in English, were included. Reviews, case reports, poster publications, conference presentations, editorials, studies not defining or incomplete reporting of outcome and data were excluded. In case of disagreement on including a paper, an agreement was negotiated. Studies were eligible for inclusion in the network meta-analysis if they compared at least two study arms. Additionally, studies in which ACP was used in less than 80% of patients were excluded.

Data extraction

Microsoft Office Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) was used for data extraction. Two reviewers (P.G. and F.Z.) independently extracted the data and recorded all data with a standardised form. All two-arm (two groups reported) or three-arm (three groups reported) studies with a within‐study comparison of different cannulation strategies were included. Outcomes were considered early outcomes when they occurred within 30 days postoperatively.

Assessment of the quality of individual studies and the overall quality of evidence

In studies that included a within-study comparison of cannulation strategy, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the risk of bias in observational studie [12].

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were early mortality and the postoperative incidence of stroke, defined as a clinical neurological deficit lasting more than 24 h with or without radiological imaging confirmation. Early events were classified as those occurring either in-hospital or within 30 days postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of transient ischemic attack (TIA) and the need for surgical re-exploration for bleeding.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Baseline and surgical characteristics were summarised by sample size weighting, risk ratios (RRs) and mean differences (MDs) were used to compare baseline characteristics with the use of a fixed effects model exclusively to studies that compared the same cannulation technique. Early event risks were pooled using inverse variance weighting and pooled in a random-effects model using the random intercept logistic regression model and the maximum-likelihood estimator for τ2. In case a particular event didn’t occur in an individual study, it was assumed that 0.5 patients experienced the event for pooling purposes (continuity correction). Given the use of aggregated study-level data, adjustment for patient-level confounders was not possible. Random-effects meta-analysis was performed using ‘metafor’ and ‘meta’ packages in R (version 4.0.5., R Project for Statistical Computing).

Network meta-analysis

Odds ratios (ORs) were used to assess early outcomes, calculated by extracting raw data from the studies, as adjusted odds ratios were not available. The network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted using the frequentist approach with the generic inverse variance method, utilizing the ‘netmeta’ package in R (version 4.0.5, R Project for Statistical Computing) [13, 14], details are presented in Text S2. A random-effects NMA was performed to make both direct and indirect comparisons between two- and three-arm studies that examined different cannulation sites. To evaluate inconsistency in the NMA, conventional pairwise meta-analyses were conducted, comparing direct and indirect ORs using node-splitting. Additionally, quadratic net heat plots were generated to further investigate inconsistency. Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic and the I² index. For presentation, odds ratios (ORs) comparing A vs. B and B vs. A were expressed by inversion ORBvsA=1/ORAvsB; confidence intervals were inverted accordingly. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The literature search identified 921 studies; the selection procedure is shown in Fig. 1. After the full text review, 6 studies met the inclusion criteria. All were retrospective, single-centre observational studies comparing at least two cannulation strategies in ATAAD repair with consistent use of antegrade cerebral perfusion. Study origins included China (n = 3), Italy (n = 1), Germany (n = 1), and Bulgaria (n = 1)15–20. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and is summarized in Supplementary Material Table S2. There were three studies comparing subclavian vs. femoral cannulation, two studies comparing femoral vs. direct cannulation and one study comparing all three cannulation strategies Table 1.

Study and baseline patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Characteristics of each study are presented in the Supplementary Material Table S3. Pooled baseline and procedural characteristics for studies used in pairwise meta-analysis are presented in Table S4-S6. A total of 2141 patients were included in the analysis. Patients undergoing femoral cannulation were significantly older than those in the subclavian group (mean difference [MD] 2.47 years; 95% CI: 0.28–4.66; p = 0.026) and direct group (MD 5.3 years; 95% CI: 2.50–8.18; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the femoral group demonstrated a higher prevalence of prior cardiac surgery (RR 2.20; 95% CI: 1.38–3.52; p < 0.001), coronary artery disease (RR 1.75; 95% CI: 1.13–2.73; P = 0.012) and connective tissue disease (RR 2.08; 95% CI: 1.19–3.62; p = 0.009). Preoperative clinical presentation was more severe in the femoral cohort, with increased rates of cardiac tamponade (RR 1.97; 95% CI: 1.38–2.82; p = 0.002), hemodynamic shock (RR 5.2; 95% CI: 3.01–9.08) and cerebral ischemia (RR 2.13; 95% CI: 1.05–4.32; p = 0.034) compared to the subclavian cohort. No significant differences were observed in the pairwise comparison between femoral and direct groups in terms of preoperative presentation or surgery performed (Table S4). ACP was used in 100% (95% CI: 99.8–100) of the patient the subclavian and femoral group, and in 95.4% (95% CI: 94.0–100) in the direct aortic group. Patients in the femoral group experienced the longest CPB time, but a shorter aortic cross-clamp (ACC) time compared to those in the direct aortic group. Pooled clinical outcomes of individual arms are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Pairwise meta-analysis

Individual study events, odds ratios, and pairwise meta-analysis results are shown in Table 2. Subclavian cannulation was associated with a significantly lower risk of early mortality compared to femoral cannulation (OR 0.63; 95% CI: 0.43–0.93; P = 0.020). No significant difference in early mortality was observed between direct and femoral cannulation (OR: 1.80, 95% CI: 0.22–15.33; p = 0.15). Postoperative stroke, TIA, and the need for surgical re-exploration due to bleeding showed comparable risks across all three techniques, with no evidence of statistically significant heterogeneity.

Network meta-analysis

The network meta-analysis supported the findings of the pairwise meta-analysis. Subclavian cannulation remained significantly associated with lower early mortality compared to femoral cannulation (OR 0.64, 95% CI: 0.44–0.93, p = 0.022; Fig. 2). No significant differences in early mortality were observed between direct and femoral (OR 0.53, 95% CI: 0.25–1.10, p = 0.091), or between direct and. subclavian cannulation (OR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.37–1.85, p = 0.132).

Forest plot of net split result of direct, indirect evidence and network estimates for early mortality

Early stroke and TIA incidences were comparable across all cannulation strategies Figs. 3 and 4. Inconsistency testing revealed no significant heterogeneity across most comparisons, with low I2 values supporting the robustness of the pooled estimates (Supplementary Figure S2-S4). However, moderate heterogeneity was observed in the comparison of TIA incidence between subclavian and femoral cannulation (I2 = 36%), suggesting some variability in effect estimates for this outcome.

Discussion

The choice of arterial cannulation strategy is important for achieving optimal outcomes in patients undergoing ATAAD repair. Various approaches have been proposed, each with unique considerations, particularly regarding concomitant cerebral protection strategies Figs 3 and 4.

Subclavian artery cannulation offers a safe access point for establishing CPB because these vessels are rarely involved in ATAAD. It also enables easy initiation of ACP from the same site; however, due to its longer setup time, it may be unsuitable for unstable patients, where a fast CPB establishment is required. In contrast, femoral artery cannulation provides rapid access, but the retrograde flow potentially increases the risk of plaque rupture and cerebral embolism [21]. Direct aortic cannulation similarly allows for fast CPB initiation with antegrade perfusion, yet it carries the risk of cannulating the false lumen, which may lead to dissection progression, malperfusion, or aortic rupture.

While subclavian and femoral access have been widely used, interest in the direct aortic approach has been growing over the past decade. However, the limited data available continues to impact the strength of the evidence. It is worth noting that the choice of cannulation strategy is influenced by patient-specific factors, the surgeon’s expertise, and the common practice at the surgical center. For example, critical and unstable patients are more likely to receive either femoral or direct aortic cannulation due to their inherent faster CPB setup.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare clinical outcomes associated with subclavian, femoral, and direct aortic cannulation strategies in type A aortic dissection repair with concomitant antegrade cerebral protection. However, substantial variability in patient presentation and institutional practices in the setting of univariable comparisons introduces confounding. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted in the context of this underlying confounding, rather than as causal comparisons.

Mortality

Patients undergoing femoral cannulation had a higher early mortality compared to those receiving subclavian cannulation, consistent with earlier studies [22, 23]. No statistically significant differences were seen between subclavian and direct cannulation, or femoral and direct cannulation. In studies with femoral vs. subclavian comparisons, in both the observational data and the pairwise comparison, the femoral group presented with higher rates of cardiac tamponade, hemodynamic shock, and cerebral ischemia compared with the subclavian group. In contrast, the pairwise comparison between the femoral and direct cannulation groups showed no significant differences in baseline characteristics or patient presentation. Both cardiac tamponade and hemodynamic shock are independent predictors of early mortality and likely contributed to the observed adverse outcomes [24–30]. As such, higher mortality in the femoral vs. the subclavian group was expected, as previously reported [31]. It would have been in line with expectations, given the similar risk profiles and surgery performed, that the direct group also had higher mortality compared to the subclavian group. Nevertheless, the contrary was observed with an insignificant effect in favor of direct cannulation compared to both the femoral and subclavian group. This may be due to lack of power; however, the consistent direction of the effect in favor of direct cannulation does warrant further investigation. Femoral cannulation may carry an inherently higher risk of embolic events, particularly in patients with tortuous or calcified vessels. Additionally, the retrograde perfusion associated with femoral cannulation, absent in direct and subclavian cannulation, might predispose to adverse outcomes [16, 22, 23]. These factors could help explain the differences in mortality despite otherwise comparable baseline risks. In addition, surgical extent may represent an important confounder. More extensive procedures, such as total arch replacement, may influence both cannulation strategy and outcomes, particularly neurological complications. Although such data were reported, they were not consistently defined or accounted for in the analysis and may partly explain differences in outcomes between groups. Nonetheless, larger meta-analyses and individual studies have reported no significant outcome differences between cannulation techniques, suggesting that this issue remains open to debate [9, 31, 33]. In our study, the higher mortality observed with femoral cannulation is probably related to the higher preoperative risk profile of these patients rather than to the cannulation technique itself.

Cerebrovascular events

No statistically significant differences in stroke incidence were identified across the various cannulation strategies, although several factors should be considered. Patients receiving femoral or direct cannulation had higher rates of preoperative cerebral malperfusion symptoms than those in the subclavian group. Notably, subclavian cannulation was used in over 90% of patients undergoing total arch replacement. As reported in the literature, manipulation of the arch vessels during arch surgery may increase the risk of an air or atheromatous embolism, potentially contributing to the comparable stroke incidence across groups; however, this cannot be directly inferred from the present analysis [32]. This comparable incidence may also reflect a patient-specific approach in selecting the optimal cannulation site. Subclavian cannulation is often preferred for patients with atherosclerotic descending aorta or circumferential dissection, while femoral or direct cannulation is typically chosen for hemodynamically unstable ATAAD cases. This approach aligns with findings from other studies that also reported no significant differences in stroke incidence between different cannulation strategies [33, 34]. However, those studies acknowledged potential biases introduced by differences in preoperative patient conditions and the more frequent use of ACP primarily in subclavian cannulation, influencing brain protection strategies. Variability in the application of ACP (e.g., unilateral versus bilateral perfusion), although not consistently reported, may further contribute to heterogeneity in neurological outcomes. In addition, morphologic variability among patients having branch vessel involvement in the dissection plays a role in the cannulation strategy as well. In our study, ACP was universally applied in each included study, minimizing this source of bias and highlighting the importance of consistent cerebral protection across different cannulation strategies.

Limitations

This systematic review focuses on retrospective observational studies; therefore, the inherent limitations of combining data from such studies should be acknowledged [35]. Publication bias may have affected the observed outcomes since unpublished data, abstracts and presentations were not included. Furthermore, the included studies span more than a decade, during which the significant advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management have occurred and may have influenced outcomes. Moreover, it is important to consider the potential for selection bias in cases where direct aortic cannulation may not be feasible due to anatomical challenges, such as circumferential aortic dissection. In such instances, an alternative technique would need to be selected. We could not perform a subgroup analysis due to the lack of data and variation in study design. Ideally, a network meta-analysis should include only randomized populations to ensure that treatment effects remain unbiased; data scarcity has necessitated the inclusion of non-randomized studies in this meta-analysis. To our knowledge, however, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to explore outcomes across different arterial cannulation strategies in patients undergoing antegrade cerebral perfusion.

Conclusion

The choice of arterial cannulation strategy in acute type A aortic dissection repair should be guided by individual patient characteristics. This network meta-analysis highlights important differences in outcomes associated with various cannulation sites during antegrade cerebral perfusion. The observation that, for early mortality, the direction of the effect favors direct cannulation over femoral cannulation, albeit not significantly, warrants further study. Although differences in preoperative characteristics make direct comparisons challenging, it is noteworthy that the direct cannulation method does not appear to be associated with a higher risk of mortality or neurological complications. Adequately powered studies are needed to provide further clarification.
