Primary Amenorrhea With Neck Pain
Authors: AV Lalitha Alekya, Sravanthi Mashetty, Arun Guddeti, KVS Hari Kumar
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 15-year-old girl presented with nonattainment of menarche. The girl noticed breast development at 10 years of age and pubic hair at the age of 11 years. She also provided a history of constant neck pain and aggravation of pain during neck movements. Her birth and developmental history were unremarkable, with no significant medical history in the childhood. She had normal statural growth and scholastic performance. Her height was 164 cm (75th centile) and weight was 58 kg (75th centile), and she had a midparental height of 162 cm and upper/lower segment ratio of 0.8. Her sexual maturity rating as per Tanner staging was A3B5P5 (axillary hair 3, breast development 5, and pubic hair 5), and her external genitalia were normal with 3 separate openings and no pelvic mass or bulge. The rest of the systemic examination was normal, with no expressive galactorrhoea. Her pelvic magnetic resonance imaging scan and cervical radiograph are shown in the Figure.

What is the diagnosis?

Answer

The girl showed an absent uterus with rudimentary horn and fusion of C3, C4 cervical vertebrae, and the diagnosis was Meyer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) syndrome type 2.

The differential diagnoses considered in our case of primary amenorrhea were MRKH syndrome, mosaic Turner syndrome, hypothyroidism, premature ovarian insufficiency, hyperprolactinemia, and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Her hormone panel showed follicle-stimulating hormone 2.7 IU/L, luteinizing hormone 6 IU/L, estradiol 130 pg/mL, total testosterone 25 ng/dL, and prolactin 10 ng/mL, and she had a normal thyroid profile. Magnetic resonance imaging of the abdomen showed absent uterus with normal ovaries bilaterally and no renal anomalies. She had 46 XX karyotype with normal echocardiography and audiometry. She was advised to use a cervical collar for the neck pain and was referred for nonsurgical dilatation of the vagina. The long-term implications and fertility prospects were discussed with the parents.

MRKH syndrome is characterized by Müllerian duct aplasia in a 46 XX individual with a female phenotype and usually presents with primary amenorrhea.1 The incidence is 1 in 5,000, and it is divided into 2 types (MRKH1 and MRKH 2) based on systemic involvement. MRKH2, also known as MURCS (Müllerian duct aplasia, renal aplasia, cervicothoracic somite dysplasia) syndrome, is characterized by skeletal and renal anomalies along with absent uterus.2 The range of systemic abnormalities includes ectopic kidney, horseshoe kidney, straight spine, vertebral fusion, and cardiac anomalies. Klippel-Feil syndrome is a rare congenital condition characterized by fusion of ≥1 cervical vertebrae and manifests as a classical triad of short neck, restricted movement, and low posterior hair line. MURCS syndrome should be considered in patients presenting with atypical features of MRKH and Klippel-Feil syndromes.3

The tissues affected in the MRKH syndrome share a common embryonic origin and genetic differentiation involving WNT4, LHX1, HNF1B, and PAX8 genes.4 Proper development of mesoderm is critical for the formation of reproductive, skeletal, renal, and cardiac structures. The cardiovascular system develops from the lateral mesoderm, urogenital system from the intermediate mesoderm and musculoskeletal system from the paraxial mesoderm. Müllerian ducts are paired embryonic tubes arising from the bipotential gonads. In the male fetus, testes-derived anti-Müllerian hormone leads to regression of the Müllerian ducts. In female fetus, Müllerian ducts fuse distally to develop into uterus and upper vagina, and proximal portions remain separate as fallopian tubes. Our patient had features suggestive of MRKH2, and all affected organs originated from the mesodermal germ cell layer.
