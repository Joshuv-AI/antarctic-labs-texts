Comprehensive bioinformatic analysis reveals TRPM4 as a biomarker for pan-cancer progression and macrophage infiltration
Authors: Xu Huang, Mingyang Hong, Ling Gu, Xingsong Zhang, PanFeng Feng, Ruikun Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIon channels play key regulatory roles in cancer pathophysiology. They are also considered promising therapeutic targets. The transient receptor potential melastatin 4 (TRPM4) is a nonselective monovalent cation channel, recently identified as critical in necrosis by sodium overload. Multiple studies have demonstrated that this gene is a potential player in cancer biology; however, its comprehensive role in various cancer types remains largely unexplored.MethodsIn this study, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of TRPM4 across multiple cancer types, examining its expression patterns, prognostic significance, and clinical relevance. We investigated epigenetic modifications, the DNA damage repair response, as well as alternative splicing and intronic polyadenylation associated with TRPM4. In addition, we analyzed signaling pathways related to tumorigenesis and immune responses, alongside assessing immune cell infiltration in tumor microenvironments.ResultsWe systematically delineate the expression heterogeneity of TRPM4 across pan-cancer and its clinical implications: it acts as a risk factor indicating poor prognosis in ACC, LGG, PAAD, MESO, and UVM, whereas it exhibits a protective role in KIRP and UCEC. Mechanistically, TRPM4 is involved not only in epigenetic regulation and DNA damage repair responses but also modulates post-transcriptional processes such as alternative splicing and intronic polyadenylation. Furthermore, TRPM4 expression is significantly associated with multiple tumor-related signaling pathways and immunomodulatory molecules. More importantly, through tumor microenvironment infiltration analysis, we observed spatial co-localization of TRPM4 with CD68⁺ tumor-associated macrophages, suggesting that TRPM4 may exert a potential immunomodulatory function by shaping the tumor immune microenvironment through influencing immune cell infiltration.ConclusionOur research highlights TRPM4 as a promising biomarker and a therapeutic target for cancer treatment. Future investigations should focus on elucidating the mechanistic role of TRPM4 in modulating immune response and tumor progression, potentially paving the way for innovative therapeutic strategies in oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04806-7.

Introduction

In the current field of cancer therapy, beyond traditional approaches such as surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, a variety of innovative treatments continue to emerge—ranging from novel targeted inhibitors and photodynamic therapy to advanced technologies like antibody-drug conjugates, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and CAR-T cell therapy. However, it remains regrettable that despite the increasingly diverse therapeutic options, a substantial number of patients continue to face challenges such as suboptimal treatment outcomes, a pronounced tendency toward metastasis and recurrence, and the development of drug resistance, thereby exhausting existing regimens and leaving limited alternatives for subsequent therapy [1, 2].

Although current tumor biomarkers play an important role in auxiliary diagnosis and treatment monitoring [3, 4], their inherent limitations are increasingly apparent. These limitations are mainly reflected in the following aspects: the characteristics and concentrations of tumor biomarkers are influenced by various biological factors, such as post-treatment status, host heterogeneity, age, the presence of other diseases in different individuals, false-positive biomarkers generated by other physiological or pathological processes, and exogenous interfering substances, including food, drugs, and natural remedies [5]. Due to tumor heterogeneity, a single biopsy or a single biomarker often fails to comprehensively capture the full profile of the tumor and cannot accurately reflect the dynamic progression of the disease or the mechanisms of drug resistance. The dependency on histology means that the acquisition of many biomarkers relies on invasive tissue biopsies, making frequent and dynamic monitoring difficult. Therefore, to advance into a new era of more precise and efficient cancer diagnosis and treatment, the development of a new generation of biomarkers is urgently needed. Ion channels now rank as the third major class of drug targets after enzymes and receptors, underscoring their promise in developing improved cancer treatments [6].

Members of the transient receptor potential melastatin (TRPM) channel family have been reported to exhibit both pro-tumorigenic and anti-tumorigenic roles in various cancers, positioning them as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [6]. For instance, TRPM7 contributes to cancer progression by regulating tumor cell survival, cell cycle progression, proliferation, growth, migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), highlighting its significant value as a molecular marker and therapeutic target in human malignancies [7]. TRPM4, recently identified as a key protein in sodium-overload-induced necrosis, functions as a non-selective monovalent cation channel [8]. Previous studies have indicated that TRPM4 enhances β-catenin signaling and cell proliferation in precancerous lesions of the prostate by modulating Akt/GSK3-β activity [9]. In breast cancer, it participates in tumor development through regulating estrogen receptor signaling and the EMT process [10]. Paradoxically, in colorectal cancer, TRPM4 acts as a novel methylated tumor suppressor that inhibits cancer cell migration and invasion via calcium signaling [11]. Furthermore, inhibiting TRPM4 has been shown to restore sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents [12]. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of TRPM4 as a promising therapeutic target. Despite these findings, how TRPM4 promotes tumor progression and interacts with other signaling pathways remains unclear.

Moreover, the potential of TRPM4 as a prognostic marker in clinical settings requires further investigation. To address these research gaps, this study employs comprehensive bioinformatics analyses and uses large-scale genomic datasets to perform robust statistical analyses, aiming to identify significant associations between TRPM4 expression and various cancer-related factors. This study aims to delineate the pan-cancer expression profile, prognostic significance, and clinical relevance of TRPM4. Furthermore, we investigate its epigenetic features—including DNA damage repair response, alternative splicing, and intronic polyadenylation—along with tumor-associated and immune-related signaling pathways and immune cell infiltration. These analyses seek to advance the understanding of TRPM4 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in oncology, ultimately enhancing precision medicine strategies for cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Experimental Design

The experimental design procedure is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1.

Analysis of TRPM4 expression

The expression profile of TRPM4 mRNA and paired clinical information were downloaded from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), which comprises 11,123 samples across 33 cancer types. To ensure full transparency and accessibility of clinical annotations (including cancer type, pathological stage, tumor grade, and other relevant variables), the unique patient identifiers for all analyzed samples have been systematically compiled in Supplementary Table 1. These identifiers enable direct querying and retrieval of complete clinical metadata for each individual sample through the official TCGA data portal. For analysis and comparison, gene expression data were processed as follows. Raw read counts were converted to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) to adjust for gene length and sequencing depth. The TPM values were then transformed using log2(TPM + 1) to achieve a near-normal distribution and stabilize variance, thereby meeting the assumptions of parametric statistical tests. Finally, to enable integrated pan-cancer analysis, project-specific batch effects across different TCGA projects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm, minimizing the impact of technical variation on the results. The expression of TRPM4 in different cancer cell lines was analyzed using the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle). The expression levels of TRPM4 protein in pan-cancer were analyzed using the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) [13].

Analysis of the prognostic and diagnostic value

Kaplan-Meier plotting was performed using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter tool (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) based on the TCGA dataset. In addition to the P-value, the 95% confidence interval (CI) and hazard ratio (HR) were also reported. The R “pROC” package was used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to analyze the diagnostic capability of TRPM4 in pan-cancer. The association between TRPM4 expression and prognosis was assessed using univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses, treating TRPM4 expression as a continuous variable.

Analysis of genomic alterations and mutation burden

Pan-cancer genomic data for TRPM4 were obtained from the cBioPortal platform (https://www.cbioportal.org/) [14], which hosts the systematically curated “TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas” dataset. This dataset integrates multi-omics data from 10,967 tumor samples. Genomic alteration profiles—including mutations and copy number alterations (CNA)—across various cancer types were acquired by querying the gene symbol “TRPM4” on the platform. The prognostic relevance of TRPM4 CNV and single-nucleotide variants (SNV) was evaluated using the TIDE database (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) [15]. Data for tumor mutational burden (TMB), defined as the total count of somatic non-synonymous mutations, and microsatellite instability (MSI) were sourced from the “Mutation Analysis” module within the “Pan-Cancer” section of the Home for Researchers platform (https://www.home-for-researchers.com). This platform provides precomputed results based on TCGA data, with computational methods referenced from established publications [16, 17]. Correlations of homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), tumor purity, and aneuploidy score with TRPM4 were retrieved from the IMPACT database (http://www.brimpact.cn/) [18].

Epigenetic analysis of TRPM4

The methylation status of TRPM4 in pan-cancer was assessed using the UALCAN platform. The DNA methylation level was evaluated using β values, where a β value of 0 indicates no methylation and a β value of 1 indicates complete methylation. The “Mutation” module in the GSCA database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/) [19] was used to analyze the correlation between TRPM4 methylation and mRNA expression levels, as well as patient Overall Survival (OS), Disease Specific Survival (DSS), Disease Free Interval (DFI), and Progression-Free Survival (PFS). We evaluated the correlation between TRPM4 and the expression of genes related to N1-methyladenosine (m1A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), and N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modifications, presenting the results in the form of a heatmap.

Analysis of DNA damage repair

Using the TIMER2.0 tool (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/), we detected the correlation between the expression of TRPM4 and five mismatch repair (MMR) genes as well as sixteen homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes [20, 21]. We applied the OCLR algorithm developed by a previous study to calculate mRNAsi scores and evaluated their association with TRPM4 mRNA expression using Spearman’s correlation coefficient [22].

Alternative splicing and Intronic polyadenylation analysis of TRPM4

The “ClinicalAS” module in the OncoSplicing database was used to search for clinically relevant TRPM4 alternative splicing (http://www.oncosplicing.com/) [23]. Search for related TRPM4 alternative splicing events in the SpliceSeq and SplAdder modules. Box plots were used to display the percentage splicing (PSI) data of TCGA cancer and GTEx tissue data, and compare the PSI differences between tumors and corresponding normal tissues. Intronic polyadenylation (IPA) related data were obtained from from the “Dynamically changed IPA and Clinically relevant IPA” module of the IPA Cancer Atlas (http://www.tingni-lab.com/Pancan_IPA/#!/) [24], which compared the intronic poly(A) site usage index (IPUI) of each IPA event in tumors with that in adjacent normal tissues for cancer types with enough matched normal samples, and showed the IPA events associated with clinical information [24].

Acquisition of TRPM4-related signaling pathways

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to study the correlation between gene expression and pathway. UALCAN was used to explore somatic alterations at the pan-cancer pathway level for key pathways. The “Correlation plot” module of the CancerSEA tool was utilized to analyze the correlation between TRPM4 and the functional states of 14 types of cancer using single-cell sequencing data (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA/) [25]. Correlations between TRPM4 and immune-related genes and pathways (MHC Molecule, Immune Activating Gene, Immune Suppressive Gene, Chemokines, Chemokines Receptors, Immune Checkpoint Gene, and related-pathway) in pan-cancer were obtained using TIMER2.0 and the IMPACT database. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis for TRPM4 in a pan-cancer context was performed using the LinkedOmicsKB portal (https://kb.linkedomics.org/) [26] and the WebGestalt 2024(https://www.webgestalt.org) [27].

Expression of TRPM4 and its correlation with immunity

We used the ESTIMATE algorithm to calculate the immune scores, stromal scores, and ESTIMATE score values for relevant cancer types. The online tumor immune homologous tool TISMO (http://tismo.cistrome.org) [28] was used to assess the impact of ICB treatments (anti-PD1, anti-PDL1, anti-PDL2, and anti-CTLA4) on TRPM4 expression levels. The correlation of TRPM4 with various immune cells in different cancers was calculated using the QUANTISEQ and TIMER algorithms from the SangerBox (http://sangerbox.com/). The single-cell sequencing data source for pan-cancer comes from the IMMUcan SingleCell RNAseq Database (https://immucanscdb.vital-it.ch) [29], with the dataset accession number GSE154763. Macrophage abundance was obtained from the TISIDB dataset (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/index.php) [30].

Immunofluorescence

The tissue sections used in this study were obtained from Affiliated Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital of Nantong University, and the study protocol was approved by its Ethics Committee (Ethics Approval No.: Y2025024). Paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked with goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Appropriately diluted primary antibodies (TRPM4 Polyclonal antibody, Proteintech, 1:100; Anti-CD68 Mouse mAb, Servicebio, 1:200) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with appropriately diluted secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) and Cy3-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), Servicebio, 1: 200). Following removal of excess antibody solution, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a scanner and analyzed using CaseViewer software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between normal and tumor tissues were performed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. The association between TRPM4 expression levels and clinicopathological features was assessed via an online analysis platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com/). For dichotomous variables (e.g., Clinical M stage and Pathologic M stage), the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data, whereas the independent samples t-test (or Welch’s t-test for unequal variances) was used for normally distributed dichotomous variables. For multi-category variables (including Pathologic stage, Clinical stage, and Pathologic N stage), overall group differences were first evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test. If the overall test result was significant (P < 0.05), Dunn’s test was subsequently performed for post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction applied to the P-values. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Significance in TRPM4 expression differences was achieved in 14 of 33 cancer types using data from the TCGA database (Fig. 2A and Fig. S1). Data derived from the UALCAN database indicated that TRPM4 protein levels were upregulated in Breast cancer, Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC), and Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), whereas TRPM4 expression was downregulated in Colon cancer, Clear cell RCC, and Liver cancer (Fig. 2B). Additionally, expression levels of TRPM4 across various cell lines were obtained from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) data, as illustrated in Fig. S2A.

TRPM4 plays a role in the prognosis and diagnosis of various cancers

To assess the prognostic significance of TRPM4 as a indicator for DSS, DFS, OS, and PFS across 33 tumor types within the TCGA database, we conducted a series of analyses. Univariate Cox regression analysis identified TRPM4 as a risk factor for Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), Lower Grade Glioma (LGG), PAAD, Mesothelioma (MESO), Uveal Melanoma (UVM), whereas it served as a protective factor in Kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP) and UCEC (Fig. 2C). Further multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that TRPM4 served as an independent prognostic factor in LGG, MESO, KIRP, and UCEC (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, we also evaluated TRPM4 expression in a cohort of clinical samples categorized into distinct clinical stages (Fig. S2B). TRPM4 expression levels increased with advancing tumor stage in ACC, CHOL, and PAAD. In contrast, a converse pattern was observed in UCEC and KIRP, where expression decreased during progression. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were employed to evaluate prognostic outcomes (Fig. S3). High TRPM4 expression was associated with increased survival in patients with KIRP and UCEC, but with decreased survival in ACC, CESC, LGG, MESO, and UVM.

ROC curve analysis confirmed TRPM4 as a diagnostic biomarker across multiple cancer types, including breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), CESC, cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), PAAD, prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and UCEC, all showing AUC values greater than 0.7 (Fig. 3), indicating consistently high sensitivity and specificity in these malignancies.

Analysis of genomic instability and genomic alterations of TRPM4

Given that genomic instability and mutation are established drivers of tumorigenesis [31, 32] and that TMB-H and MSI-H are biomarkers of high clinical value in immunotherapy [33], we sought to evaluate the associations between TRPM4 expression and MSI, TMB, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), tumor purity, and aneuploidy score. In the context of MSI, a positive correlation between TRPM4 expression and MSI was identified in Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), Esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), PRAD, Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), KIRC, and UCEC. Conversely, a negative correlation was observed in BRCA and READ (Fig. 4A). Additionally, a positive correlation between TRPM4 expression and TMB was noted in Thymoma (THYM), Lymphoid Neoplasm Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBC), ESCA, PAAD, ACC, PRAD, UCEC, HNSC, and LGG, whereas a negative correlation was detected in LUAD and BRCA (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, as depicted in Fig. S4, a significant correlation between TRPM4 and HRD was observed in ACC, KIRP, Sarcoma (SARC), UCEC, KIRC, LGG and PRAD. Meanwhile, TRPM4 expression was positively correlated with tumor purity in Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma (BLCA), ESCA, and PRAD, and negatively correlated with SARC, MESO, Ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), LGG, LIHC, and Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM). Next, a negative correlation between TRPM4 and Aneuploidy Score was evident in READ, UCEC, and OV, while a positive correlation was observed in UVM and LGG.

To assess the potential genomic alterations of TRPM4 in specific cancers, a pan-cancer analysis of TRPM4 CNV and SNV was conducted. TRPM4 amplification was primarily detected in Uterine Carcinosarcoma and Adrenocortical Carcinoma, while deep deletions were commonly found in Lower Grade Glioma and Thymoma, and a significantly high SNV rate was observed in SKCM and UCEC (Fig. 4D and E). TIDE analysis, based on CNV, revealed that patients with triple-negative breast cancer and glioma, exhibiting high TRPM4 CNV levels, had lower survival rates, whereas the inverse was observed in KIRC (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these findings indicate a significant association between TRPM4 and genomic instability.

TRPM4 is associated with DNA repair and tumor stemness characteristics

The DNA damage response and repair continuously and actively preserve genomic integrity [34]. As shown in Fig. 5A, TRPM4 suppresses the expression of MMR genes in cancers, including OV, PRAD, READ, and UCEC, but promotes their expression in ACC, CESC, HNSC, KIRP, and Pheochromocytoma and Paraganglioma (PCPG). Figure 5B illustrates that TRPM4 expression is positively correlated with key molecules of the HRR signaling pathway in cancers such as ACC, CESC, DLBC, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, MESO, PCPG, Thyroid carcinoma (THCA), and UVM, while showing a negative correlation in BRCA, PRAD, THYM, and UCEC. Furthermore, cancer progression is often characterized by the loss of differentiated cellular phenotypes and the acquisition of progenitor or stem-like characteristics [35]. According to the tumor stemness score presented in Fig. 5C, TRPM4 is negatively correlated with the stemness index in cancers such as BRCA, LGG, lung cancer, OV, SKCM, SARC, Testicular Germ Cell Tumors (TGCT), THCA, and UCEC. Conversely, in ESCA, renal cancer, and PRAD, TRPM4 is positively correlated with stemness. These findings suggest that TRPM4 acts as a regulator of cancer progression by influencing DNA damage repair mechanisms.

TRPM4 is closely related to epigenetics

Studies demonstrated that DNA methyltransferase inhibitors such as decitabine restored sensitivity to NECSO in TRPM4-low/hypermethylated tumors, thereby revealing a significant association between epigenetic regulation and therapeutic response [36, 37]. We therefore sought to further investigate the epigenetic landscape of TRPM4. Initial analysis of its relationship with RNA modification patterns revealed a regulatory association (Fig. 5D). We next turned to DNA methylation and observed a strong inverse correlation between TRPM4 mRNA expression and its methylation levels across multiple cancers, a finding particularly prominent in PRAD, THYM, and DLBC (Fig. 6A). Further comparative analysis of TRPM4 promoter methylation showed hypomethylation in BLCA, LIHC, PRAD, and READ compared to normal tissues, whereas hypermethylation was evident in KIRC and LUSC (Fig. 6B). Critically, evaluation of the clinical impact of this epigenetic regulation demonstrated that high TRPM4 methylation was associated with prolonged patient survival in several cancers, most notably in LGG and KIRC (Fig. 6C-D).

Alternative splicing and intronic polyadenylation of TRPM4 are associated with cancer development

Aberrant regulation of alternative splicing, frequently associated with tumorigenesis and progression, generates diverse isoforms leading to proteome diversification. Moreover, it may disrupt protein interaction networks during tumor development [38]. Clinically relevant TRPM4 alternative splicing events were identified using OncoSplicing, including the intron_retention_72303 event in the TCGA SplAdder database and the TRPM4_AD_50901 event in the TCGA SpliceSeq database. The pan-cancer analysis of TRPM4_intron_retention_72303 events revealed elevated PSI values in BLCA, COAD, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, LUSC, PRAD, READ, and STAD, and reduced values in BRCA, when compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 7A). The PSI differences were statistically significant between tumor and normal tissues and were correlated with prognosis (Fig. 7B). The findings related to TRPM4_AD_50901 are presented in Fig. S5A and B.

Additionally, IPA has been implicated in the inactivation of DNA damage repair genes and tumor suppressor genes in cancer and has been reported to play important roles in cancer development [39]. TRPM4-related IPA events were identified in the IPA Cancer Atlas. Figure 7C compares the intronic poly(A) site usage index (IPUI) between tumors and adjacent normal tissues across cancer types, revealing significant disparities in BRCA, KICH, KIRP, and THCA. Figure 7D further correlates THCA IPA events with tumor subtype and disease stage.

TRPM4 is involved in multiple oncogenic pathways

We examined the link between TRPM4 and cancer-related pathways and TCGA oncogenic pathways, revealing its strong association with key cancer signaling pathways, as depicted in Fig. 8A and B. For instance, UALCAN data indicated that higher TRPM4 levels in UCEC are linked to changes in the mTOR pathway and SWI/SNF complex status (Fig. S5C). Mutations in the SWI/SNF complex exert widespread pro-tumorigenic effects in diverse cancers [40]. Similarly, TRPM4 levels are closely associated with RTK pathways and chromatin modifications in PAAD (Fig. S5C). To extend these observations, we performed a GSEA of TRPM4 in the pan-cancer context (Fig. S6). Furthermore, when we expanded our analysis to the immune context, TRPM4 exhibited a negative correlation with various immune-related pathways in BLCA, CESC, KIRC, and THCA (Fig. 8C). Conversely, a significant positive correlation was observed in LGG, OV, and UVM. Additionally, we assessed the association of TRPM4 with 14 cancer functional states, including Differentiation, Stemness, Invasion, EMT, Cell cycle, Metastasis, Proliferation, DNA damage, and DNA repair, across multiple cancer types (Fig. S7).

TRPM4 regulates immune cell infiltration and cytokine activity

Research indicates that cancer can evade immune-mediated elimination by acquiring traits that disrupt anti-tumor immunity. These immune evasion mechanisms are selected and reinforced during tumor evolution under immune pressure. Meanwhile, inflammation modulates both cancer development and therapeutic response, with dual roles in promoting or suppressing tumor progression [41, 42]. We employed TIMER 2.0 and IMPACT to analyze TRPM4’s association with MHC molecules, immune-activating genes, immune-suppressive genes, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and immune checkpoint genes across various cancer types, confirming its role in key immune responses (Fig. S8). Experimental studies have demonstrated that complete loss of the TRPM4 channel induces immune dysregulation, leading to frequent bacterial and fungal infections and severely impairing the expression of migration-related genes in T cells and monocytes [43]. Additionally, using the TISMO tool, we compared TRPM4 expression in samples before and after ICB treatments, finding higher expression in responders (Fig. S9).

Furthermore, we employed multiple algorithms to characterize the relationship between TRPM4 and the immunological profile of the tumor microenvironment (TME). The cellular composition and functional states of the TME may vary considerably depending on the tumor site, cancer cell-intrinsic features, tumor stage, and patient characteristics [44]. To specifically investigate immune associations of TRPM4 across 33 tumor types, we employed the ESTIMATE algorithm. Positive correlations between ESTIMATE and immune scores were detected in LAML, LGG, LIHC, MESO, OV, SRAC and UVM, whereas negative correlations were observed in the remaining cancer types (Fig. 8D).

TRPM4 is associated with macrophage infiltration in pan-cancer

TME constitutes a complex niche composed of diverse immune cells and matrix components. Within this context, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) exhibit marked spatial heterogeneity [45]. We demonstrated the expression and localization of TRPM4 in dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, monocytes, and neutrophils using single-cell sequencing data, revealing that TRPM4 expression is predominantly localized in macrophages across various cancers (Fig. 9A). Additionally, we assessed immune cell infiltration across pan-cancer using both the TIMER and QUANTISEQ methods (Fig. 9B). Using the TISIDB tool, we also demonstrated a significant association between TRPM4 and macrophage abundance across multiple cancer types. (Fig. 10). As the predominant immune component in the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) serve as an established diagnostic biomarker, with their abundance strongly correlating with prognostic outcomes [45].

Discussion

Through a pan-cancer analysis, we systematically characterize the expression landscape of TRPM4 and demonstrate its clinical prognostic value, highlighting its translational potential as a dual-purpose biomarker for cancer. Furthermore, we elucidate the integrative role of TRPM4 in tumor progression, which extends beyond a singular function. Its aberrant expression is closely associated with genomic instability, epigenetic dysregulation, and the activation of immune-related pathways. Through systematic analyses, we have robustly demonstrated that TRPM4 expression is strongly linked to significant enrichment of tumor-associated macrophages and the formation of the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings not only clarify the critical role of TRPM4 in tumorigenesis and immune regulation but also reveal its potential mechanism as a key coordinating factor bridging intrinsic cellular dysregulation in tumor cells and the state of the tumor immune microenvironment.

TRPM4 expression is closely associated with DNA damage repair and cancer stemness. CSCs play critical roles in tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and recurrence, and are major contributors to chemoresistance, largely due to their enhanced DNA repair efficiency [46]. Previous studies indicate that TRPM4 regulates breast cancer stemness, where its overexpression in tumorspheres maintains bCSC properties potentially through EMT [47]. Furthermore, the involvement of TRPM4 in chemoresistance may be attributed to its ability to drive the upregulation of P-gp in multiple cancer types. Mechanistically, TRPM4 downregulates the key transcription factor NF-κB, which in turn influences P-gp expression [12]. These TRPM4-driven mechanisms are direct contributors to resistance and tumor recurrence.

Prognostic tools leveraging genetic mutations show great potential for guiding clinical decisions, with key markers enabling precise risk stratification and targeted therapy in oncology [48]. Our data reveal that TRPM4 undergoes diverse mutational alterations across cancer types and demonstrates significant associations with both MSI and TMB. These genetic changes, particularly deletions, have been shown to disrupt mitotic progression and influence responses to immunotherapy, further implicating TRPM4 in key oncogenic processes [36, 49]. However, it remains challenging to definitively classify TRPM4 alterations as driver versus passenger events. Given that the same mutation may act as a driver, be neutral, or exert context-dependent functions in different biological settings [50], future studies are warranted to delineate the biological role of TRPM4 in tumor evolution within specific pathological contexts.

TRPM4 has been most extensively studied in prostate cancer, where it is recognized as a driver gene in androgen-independent disease and ranks among the most significantly upregulated genes [51–53]. Mechanistically, TRPM4 is negatively regulated by microRNA-150, and modulating this axis reduces proliferation, inactivates β-catenin, and alters cell cycle progression [54]. In CRC, TRPM4 has been characterized as a novel methylation-regulated tumor suppressor. It exerts its inhibitory function on cell migration and invasion by activating calpain-mediated FAK cleavage through intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling and modulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [11]. TRPM4 downregulation suppresses proliferation, reduces cyclin D1 expression, and induces cell cycle alterations in cervical cancer [55]. Notably, TRPM4 has been proposed as a protective diagnostic marker in UCEC [56, 57], which is consistent with our findings. This protective effect may be attributed to the role of TRPM4 in maintaining p53 expression and restraining the EMT and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways; indeed, decreased TRPM4 expression leads to reduced p53 levels and hyperactivation of these pathways, thereby promoting UCEC growth and migration [51, 58]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TRPM4 not only functions as an upstream regulator of multiple signaling pathways but also serves as an important biomarker in prostate cancer and UCEC.

TRPM4 links diverse immune cells and signaling pathways [35, 42, 43], suggesting a significant impact on the TME. The co-localization of TRPM4 with macrophages, as well as its correlation with M1/M2 TAM phenotypes across cancers, implicates TRPM4 in regulating TAM polarization. Evidence indicates that TRP channels modulate macrophage phagocytosis, chemokine/cytokine production, and polarization [44]. Meanwhile, primary T cells and monocytes from TRPM4-deficient patients exhibit significantly impaired migratory capacity [43]. Additionally, high TRPM4 expression predicts poorer outcomes following immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [59]. Owing to its complex role in immunity, TRPM4 is a promising predictor of immunotherapy response [59]. Metastatic MSI-H/dMMR cancers, due to genomic hypermutability caused by dMMR, display strong immunogenicity and immune cell infiltration, conferring high sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors across diverse histological types [60]. Further investigation into TRPM4 alterations in these specific cancer types will provide more robust evidence supporting its role as a significant predictive biomarker for immunotherapeutic response.

This study has several limitations. First, the integrated bioinformatics analysis utilized data from multiple platforms, potentially introducing batch effects. Moreover, the heavy reliance on TCGA data without independent cohort validation may compromise the generalizability and robustness of the findings. This limitation particularly constrains the translational potential of TRPM4 as a candidate immune-related biomarker. Second, although online platform analyses suggested a link between TRPM4 and immune infiltration, these methods provide only indirect evidence and are susceptible to inherent biases from third-party tools. Third, despite revealing TRPM4-macrophage co-localization by immunofluorescence, we could not distinguish TRPM4 expression between M1 and M2 macrophage subtypes due to limited resolution. Finally, the mechanistic basis of TRPM4 in macrophage polarization and tumor progression remains unclear. To address these limitations, we propose a multi-tiered investigative strategy. First, we will establish a cohort of fresh, clinically annotated pan-cancer specimens and employ integrated single-cell sequencing and flow cytometry to define TRPM4 mechanisms within the TME. Next, integrated multi-omics data will be analyzed using machine learning algorithms to facilitate systematic cross-omics investigations. Finally, we will establish knockout and overexpression models across diverse tumor lineages, combined with inhibitor-based treatments, to functionally delineate TRPM4-mediated regulation of macrophage polarization, associated signaling pathways, and its synergistic role in tumor progression.

This study identifies TRPM4 as a compelling therapeutic target. Its membrane localization enables targeting by extracellular macromolecular agents and intracellular membrane-permeant small-molecule inhibitors [51, 61]. Furthermore, given that ion channel dysregulation promotes tumor progression and therapy resistance in cancer by altering membrane potential, disrupting ion homeostasis, and activating downstream signaling cascades—and also participates in immune cell activation and cytotoxicity within the antitumor immune microenvironment [51, 61]. Therefore, we propose that TRPM4, through its central role in key pathways and dual effects on both tumor and immune cells, represents a highly druggable and promising novel therapeutic candidate.
