Elucidation of the binding interaction interface between AAV serotype 11 capsid protein and host nuclear import proteins
Authors: Mikayla Hoad, Sepehr Nematollahzadeh, Justin A. Roby, Gualtiero Alvisi, Jade K. Forwood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) are widely acknowledged as versatile vectors for gene therapy due to their non-pathogenic nature, inherent capacity for tissue-specific targeting, and their potential for customizable engineering. The N terminus of the AAV capsid protein VP1 plays a pivotal role in guiding AAV capsids into the cell nucleus. However, the precise dynamics of the interaction between the VP1 protein and host nuclear transport proteins, especially across diverse AAV serotypes, remain incompletely understood. AAV11 has emerged as a promising alternative for individuals with elevated antibody titers against AAV2, and in the field of neuroscience, it has demonstrated a strong capability for mapping and manipulating neural circuits, offering the potential for treating neurological and neurodegenerative disorders. In this study, we characterize the molecular interface between AAV11 VP1 and host importin-α (IMPα). Structural and biochemical analyses reveal that the basic regions BR1 and BR3 of the VP1 N-terminal domain engage IMPα in a bipartite nuclear localization signal (NLS)-like manner. These findings provide mechanistic insight into VP1-IMPα recognition and suggest a role for these interactions in AAV11 nuclear import. While direct functional evidence is pending, this work establishes the molecular basis for VP1-host protein binding and informs future capsid engineering.

Introduction

Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) are small single-stranded DNA viruses, each with a genome size of ∼4.8 kb, spanning 13 identified common serotypes across humans and non-human primates with more than 100 different variants. Their non-pathogenic characteristic, combined with the capacity for clinical customization and engineering, has positioned AAVs as preferred vectors for gene therapy.1,2,3 Renowned for their safety and versatility in DNA delivery, AAVs facilitate effective gene editing, knockdown, and overexpression, making them standout tools in the field.4

One challenge in using AAV-based gene therapy is the presence of pre-existing neutralizing antibodies against common AAV serotypes in human patients, notably AAV2,5 the first discovered serotype. Approximately 30%–80% of humans have antibodies against at least one AAV serotype,6,7 depending on geographic and demographic factors. While some AAV serotypes avoid cross-neutralization,8,9,10,11 cross-reactivity can vary significantly by species,12 tissue type, and route of admission.13,14 AAV11, which is phylogenetically related to AAV12 and AAV4,15 has shown limited cross-reactivity with AAV2 antisera and has also emerged as a potential vector for central nervous system (CNS) applications.16 However, AAV11 has so far been evaluated mainly in local CNS injections, where systemic antibodies have limited relevance.17 Its performance in systemic gene delivery or in the presence of circulating anti-AAV antibodies remains to be established. AAV2 and AAV9 vectors have been widely used in neuroscience to label and manipulate neural circuits,18 but both can show inefficient retrograde transport in some pathways, resulting in off-target effects.18,19 Although AAVs 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, and 9 have historically been used in neurobiological research,16,20 emerging data suggest that AAV11 may offer improved capabilities for mapping and modulating neural circuits,16 supporting its further development for neurological gene therapy.

The AAV genome is simple, composed of two primary genes: Rep21,22 and Cap,23 with two minor genes Aap and Maap nestled within the Cap gene,24 all flanked by inverted terminal repeats.25,26 The Cap gene is responsible for producing three capsid proteins, VP1, VP2, and VP3, which share an identical C terminus; however, they vary in the length of their N terminus due to the utilization of different translational start codons. VP1 is encoded by the full Cap gene from an ATG codon and encompasses the complete N-terminal domain. Both VP2 and VP3 arise from the same 2.3 kb mRNA, where VP2’s weaker initiation codon, ACG, allows for the inclusion of only a portion of the N-terminal domain (residues ∼137 onward),27 whereas readthrough translation to the next codon, ATG, encodes for the more abundant VP3 that lacks most of the N-terminal domain (residues ∼202 onward).27,28 These capsid proteins assemble into the AAV capsid in a ratio of 1:1:10 (VP1:VP2:VP3), with the C-terminal domains forming the capsid’s exterior and the N-terminal domains of VP1 and VP2 residing on the interior of the capsid shell. The N termini protrude through the capsid pores, facilitating virus escape from endosomes and its subsequent movement into the cell nucleus. Notably, the N-terminal domain harbors nuclear localization signals (NLSs), including a sequence of basic residues (basic region [BR]1–3).29,30,31,32 The proper exposure of this domain is crucial not only for the efficiency of AAVs as gene therapy vectors but also for their success in natural infections. Interestingly, unlike the majority of human and non-human primate serotypes, barring that of AAV4, AAV11 includes an additional BR sequence downstream of the canonical BR2 but upstream of the BR3. This additional site has been labeled BR2.2 (Figure 1). Previous studies have revealed that various AAV serotypes employ the classical nuclear import pathway mechanisms.29,30,31,32 Within this pathway, the adapter protein importin-α (IMPα) engages with the NLS on a cargo protein through its major and/or minor binding sites and forms a heterotrimer complex with host importin-β (IMPβ1). This complex is then actively transported from the cytoplasm to the nucleus via the nuclear pore complex and then dissociated by RanGTP in the nucleus.33,34,35,36 It has been found that AAV VP1 BRs can interact with different IMPα isoforms, indicating variability in the specificity of IMPα isoforms among AAV serotypes.29,30,31,32 Given that certain IMPα isoforms have tissue-specific expression patterns,37,38,39 there is potential for a link between the affinity of AAV for specific IMPα isoforms, the tissue specificity of IMPα, and consequently, the efficiency of AAV vectors in various tissues. Studying AAV11 nuclear import is essential to understand its transduction efficiency, particularly in non-dividing cells like neurons, where nuclear entry is a key barrier. Insights into its import mechanisms can inform vector optimization, enhance CNS-targeted gene delivery, and support the development of more effective and immune-evasive gene therapies.

In this study, we set out to determine the mechanisms which AAV11 VP1 utilizes to gain entry to the cell nucleus, including interactions with nuclear import through biochemical, cellular, and structural studies, providing a possible target for capsid engineering in therapy vectors.

Results

AAV11 VP1 basic region displays varied high affinities for IMPα isoforms but not with IMPβ1

Classical nucleocytoplasmic transport, which facilitates the movement of cargo proteins into the nucleus, relies on IMPβ1, binding one of seven adapter IMPα proteins, while non-classical nucleocytoplasmic transport does not utilize IMPα but rather IMPβ isoforms alone. To explore the interaction of AAV11 VP1 with IMPα or IMPβ1, we used electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) to evaluate AAV11 VP1-BR (Figure 2A) binding to various importin proteins, including IMPα1, IMPα2, IMPα3, IMPα5, IMPα7, and IMPβ1 (Figure 2B). The EMSA results revealed evident binding to all tested IMPα isoforms, encompassing members from all three IMPα subfamilies: SF1 (IMPα1 and IMPα2), SF2 (IMPα3), and SF3 (IMPα5 and IMPα7). In contrast, the BR region of AAV11 VP1 did not form a distinct band with IMPβ1; however, the smear observed could be indicative of a few things: non-specific binding, partial aggregation of the AAV11 VP1-BR peptide, which can occur at high concentrations. The faint smear suggests that there may be some degree of interaction with AAV11 VP1-BR and IMPβ1, even if, at higher concentrations, it is weak and not strong enough to result in a clear band. To further quantify these interactions, fluorescence polarization (FP) was employed to measure the binding affinity between the AAV11 VP1-BR peptide and various importins (IMPα1, IMPα2, IMPα3, IMPα5, IMPα7, and IMPβ1), as depicted in Figure 2C. Consistent with the EMSA findings, the AAV11 VP1-BR exhibited a strong binding interaction with all IMPα isoforms, with dissociation constants (KD) ranging from 13 to 106 nM. The strongest binding affinity was observed for IMPα7, followed by moderate affinities for IMPα1 and IMPα5, and weaker affinities for IMPα2 and IMPα3. Due to a very weak interaction, an accurate KD value for IMPβ1 could not be determined. Statistical analyses, including one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, indicated no significant differences in KD values among IMPα1, IMPα2, IMPα3, and IMPα5. However, the KD value for IMPα7 significantly differed from those for IMPα1, IMPα2, and IMPα3 but not from IMPα5 (Figure 2D).

AAV11 VP1 utilizes the IMPα/IMPβ1 mediated pathway for nuclear localization

To characterize the nuclear import pathway of AAV11 VP1, we investigated the effect of the IMPα/β1 inhibitor Bimax2 on its subcellular localization when transiently expressed in mammalian cells. To this end, HEK293A cells were transfected to express only full-length AAV11 VP1 monomers fused to GFP at the C terminus (Figure 3A) either in the absence or in the presence of mCherry-Bimax2. The transfection construct was designed so that only VP1 monomers would be produced in experiments. Additionally, studies have shown that VP1 cannot form a VP1-only assembled capsid. GFP fusions with human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) UL44, known to translocate to the nucleus using the classical IMPα/β1 pathway,40 and frog adenovirus 1 pre-pVII protein (FrAdV1 pVII), which is transported to the nucleus via numerous pathways,41 were also expressed as positive and negative controls regarding Bimax2 response, respectively. As expected, in the absence of Bimax2, HCMV UL44 localized to the nucleoplasm while FrAdV1 pVII strongly accumulated in the nucleoli (Figure 3C). However, in the presence of mCherry-Bimax2, UL44 was mainly retained in the cytosol, while the localization of FrAdv1 pVII was not altered (Figure 3D).

Cells transfected to express full-length AAV11 VP1 without mCherry-Bimax2 saw strong accumulation in the nucleus (Figure 3B) in the majority of transfected cells (Figure 3D) with a mean nuclear-to-cytoplasmic fluorescence ratio (Fn/c) of 11.4 (Figures 3C and 3D). Of importance, in the presence of mCherry-Bimax2-transfected cells, nuclear accumulation of full-length AAV11 VP1 is significantly impaired (Figure 3B), with a mean Fn/c of 1.3 (Figures 3C and 3D). This shows that AAV11 VP1 is translocated to the cell nucleus via the classical nuclear import pathway and is determined by the IMPα/β1 heterodimer.

Structural analysis of AAV11 VP1-BR complexed with IMPα2 unveils a bipartite NLS

To elucidate the binding mechanisms between AAV11 VP1 and IMPα, X-ray crystallography was utilized, revealing the complex structure of AAV11 VP1-BR with IMPα2 at a resolution of 2.6 Å. A recombinant protein construct encompassing VP1 residues 114–174 covering BR1, BR2, BR2.2, and BR3 was used for structural studies to increase the chances of obtaining protein complex crystals and for high-resolution diffraction data. Using full AAV11 capsids would make crystallization near impossible due to size, and the BR region of VP1 is predicted to reside in a flexible region. IMPα2 was chosen for structural analysis due to the ease of crystallization and diffraction of IMPα2 complexes, as preparations of AAV11 VP1-BR bound to IMPα3, IMPα5, and IMPα7 were not able to be crystallized despite multiple attempts.

Observed within the structure (consistent with typical IMPα structures), the IMPα2 model retains the α-helical arrangement across the ten sequential armadillo motifs.42 The AAV11 VP1-BR engages with both the major and minor binding sites on IMPα2, characteristic of a bipartite NLS:IMPα complex29,43,44,45,46 (Figure 4A). Within the minor binding site of IMPα, the interaction of AAV11 VP1-BR is facilitated by its BR1 segment, notably through the 123KR124 sequence (Figures 4B and 4C) (Table S2). In this context, Lys123 of AAV11 VP1-BR establishes the P1ʹ site by forming hydrogen bonds with IMPα2 at residues Val321, Gly323, and Thr328. Additionally, the Arg124 residue of AAV11 VP1-BR creates the pivotal P2ʹ site through hydrogen bonds with IMPα2 residues Asn361 and Glu396, complemented by a salt bridge with Glu396. Detailed descriptions of these hydrogen bonds and salt bridge interactions are available in the supplementary material (Table S2). Despite the sequence similarity between BR1 (“KKRVLE”) and BR2 (“KKRPVE”), BR1 was modeled into the minor binding site of IMPα2, based on the differential density observed during the modeling and refinement of Val vs. Pro (as underlined) within BR1 and BR2, respectively.

In our observed structure, analyses revealed that the AAV11 VP1-BR interacts with IMPα2 through its major binding sites, specifically P1, P2, P3, and P5 (Figures 4B and 4C). This interaction is characterized by a total of 15 hydrogen bonds between AAV11 VP1-BR BR3 and IMPα2’s major binding sites, with three of these hydrogen bonds occurring outside the P1–P5 regions. Specifically, the interaction at the P1 site involves a hydrogen bond between IMPα2 Asn235 and AAV11 VP1-BR Arg167. The interaction at the P2 site, which aligns with previously described cargo binding,43,47,48,49 involves AAV11 VP1-BR Lys168 forming hydrogen bonds with IMPα2 Gly150, Thr155, and Asp192, in addition to a salt bridge with Asp192. For the P3 site, AAV11 VP1-BR Arg169 forms hydrogen bonds with IMPα2 Asn188, Trp184, and Asn288. Lastly, the P5 site interaction involves AAV11 VP1-BR Asn171 engaging in hydrogen bonds with IMPα2 Asn146, Trp142, and Gln181. These interactions, including all hydrogen bonds and the salt bridge, are detailed in Table S2.

We used AlphaFold250 to further explore the mechanisms that AAV11 VP1 BRs could be using to interact with IMPα isoforms. IMPα5 and IMPα7 were chosen for modeling due to their high calculated binding affinity, while IMPα2 was chosen for predictive modeling as our structural data are with IMPα2. Each combination of submitted sequences gave five predictive models, which showed variations not only between IMPα isoforms but also between the five predictive models (Figure 4D). All predictions for IMPα2, IMPα5, and IMPα7 modeled BR3 of AAV11 VP1 into the major binding site of IMPα, as per our structure. For all IMPα isoform models, AAV11 VP1 BR3 and BR2.2 are never predicted to bind the minor binding site of IMPα. IMPα2 models place BR2 into the minor site in two of five predictions and never BR1, and IMPα5 models BR2 three out of five times and never BR1. Alternatively, IMPα7 predicts BR1 twice and BR2 twice. Of note is that all three isoforms have predictions (IMPα2; 4/5, IMPα5; 2/5, and IMPα7; 1/5) that overlap both BR1 and BR2 into the minor binding site of IMPα, bringing into question the credibility of using AlphaFold to conclusively determine protein:protein binding interactions. All AlphaFold predictive models are presented in Figures S1–S3.

Interestingly, in the absence of Bimax2, simultaneous mutations to BR1 and BR3 did not inhibit nuclear localization to any significant degree (Figure 5B) with an Fn/c of 8.5 (Figure 5C). Although BR1 and BR3 are resolved in our structure in IMPa binding sites, it is entirely possible that, due to the close nature of the BRs, AAV11 VP1 can switch between the four choices to bind IMPα. When BR1 and BR3 are inaccessible, it may switch to a BR2 and BR2.2 bipartite NLS. As expected in the presence of Bimax2, nuclear accumulation was abolished for AAV11 VP1 mBR1+3 (Figures 5B and 5C) with an Fn/c of 1.2 (Figure 5C).

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a combination of biophysical techniques, cellular assays, and structural analyses to explore the binding interaction between AAV11 VP1 and host nuclear import proteins responsible for nuclear translocation. Using EMSA, it was revealed that AAV11 VP1 engages the classical nuclear import pathway by interacting with IMPα adapter proteins, rather than directly binding to IMPβ1. This observation was further supported by cellular experiments. Quantitative FP assays were conducted to assess the binding affinities between AAV11 VP1 and various IMPα isoforms, demonstrating a preference for IMPα5 and IMPα7 compared to other isoforms. Furthermore, a structural investigation identified the specific regions and amino acid residues on AAV11 VP1 that engage with IMPα, which possibly facilitate its transport from the cytoplasm into the nucleus. This analysis revealed that AAV11 VP1 contains a bipartite NLS, binding to IMPα in a manner consistent with that observed in other AAV studies.29,30,31,32

For the structural analysis, IMPα2 was selected due to its high similarity with human IMPα1, its moderate affinity for binding with AAV11 VP1-BR, and its capacity to form crystals conducive to high-quality diffraction. The structure of the AAV11 VP1-BR in complex with IMPα2 was determined with a resolution of 2.6 Å, providing a detailed understanding of the interaction sites. Consistent with previous findings on AAV VP1 NLSs, the AAV11 VP1-BR was found to bind IMPα2 in a bipartite manner. Specifically, the N-terminal region BR1 of AAV11 VP1-BR interacted with the minor binding site of IMPα2, while the C-terminal region BR3 targeted the major binding site of IMPα2. The interaction pattern mirrored the classic bipartite NLS architecture, with the “KR” motif engaging with the P1՛ and P2՛ positions within the minor binding groove, and a key “K” residue binding into the P2 position of the major binding groove. Comparisons of this interaction with other structurally characterized cargoes bound to IMPα are provided in Table S3. While a recombinant protein construct was used in structural analysis covering the BR region of AAV11 VP1, it should be noted that a fully assembled AAV11 capsid shell may deviate slightly in interactions with nuclear transport receptors. The N terminus of AAV VP1 in fully assembled capsids only becomes exposed prior to localization to the nucleus due to conformational changes that occur in the capsid induced by the pH change during endosomal escape.51,52,53,54,55,56,57 This shorter N-terminus construct was used due to its predictive flexibility (common with NLS location on proteins) and the significant ease of use compared to complexing and crystallizing a fully assembled AAV capsid. It has been shown that this N-terminus region of AAV capsids is the driver behind AAV capsid nuclear import, and as such is reasonable to only work with this flexible and easier region of VP1. A much larger capsid complex most likely would not have been able to be crystallized, and as a result, a structure would be unable to be obtained or large parts would be unable to be resolved using crystallography due to size, making it hard to achieve a well-ordered crystalline form. Additionally, proteins with high flexibility or those that form transient or heterogeneous complexes (such as viral capsids interacting with cellular proteins like IMPα) often do not form good crystals for high-resolution diffraction data.

We confirmed that full-length AAV11 VP1 used the classical IMPα/IMPβ pathway for access to the nucleus; however, the basic regions 1 and 3 identified as the NLS in our structure appeared dispensable for nuclear import. Although structural data support a BR1 and BR3 bipartite NLS, it is entirely possible that wild-type AAV11 VP1 can change binding regions for suitability in a cellular context, with four possible BRs to utilize. Although cellular data showed that nuclear localization can still occur when BR1 and BR3 are not available, the presence of other basic regions within VP1 (BR2 and BR2.2) may facilitate a switch in the IMPα binding sequence, which shows in our results a slight decrease in localization efficacy but not enough to be of significance. Predicted AlphaFold2 models of AAV11 VP1 with respective IMPα also showed that AAV11 VP1 may utilize alternate BR in different contexts due to the four N-terminal BRs, something other AAV serotypes do not contain. Perhaps different combinations of the four regions could prove to be more efficient for nuclear localization and subsequently become a specific target for capsid engineering58 to yield maximum therapeutic results of gene therapy vectors. Although entirely possible that this is a result of using predictive AI to simulate binding, it is also possible that in a cellular context, wild-type AAV11 VP1 either changes binding sequences depending on protein conformations or can stabilize binding interactions via somehow utilizing two separate sequence regions. Without full protein structures, we cannot confidently determine which is the case. When predictions were run using the alanine residue mutations in BR1 and BR3 used in our cellular data, all models for each IMPα isoform clearly showed BR2 within the minor binding site and BR2.2 within the major binding site as theorized earlier. Further mutational analyses of AAV11 VP1, specifically examining the combined effects of BR2 and BR2.2, as well as the collective contribution of all N-terminal basic regions (BR1, BR2, BR2.2, and BR3), would provide valuable additional evidence to validate the findings presented in this study and to further elucidate the proposed “switching” NLS mechanism. From a vector engineering perspective, the observation that the AAV11 VP1 BR region exhibits only modest affinity for importin proteins during nuclear import suggests an opportunity to enhance nuclear trafficking efficiency by employing a VP1 BR region from an alternative serotype with stronger importin-binding affinity, thereby potentially obviating the need for extensive mutational studies and subsequent optimization rounds to achieve the desired interaction strength.

The successful nuclear localization of AAV capsids or their monomeric VP proteins is essential for the efficacy of AAV vectors in gene therapy, as it is a critical step for AAV transduction and overall vector performance. Recent research has shed light on the mechanisms behind the nuclear translocation of AAV VP1 across a select few AAV serotypes, showing a common trend of interacting with IMPα rather than IMPβ1 through the classical nuclear import pathway.29,30,31,32 However, the specific interactions between each AAV serotype and IMPα require detailed investigation, as there may be variations among AAV serotypes. Moreover, despite the N-terminal basic regions of AAV VP1 exhibiting high sequence similarity, differences in the affinity for IMPα isoforms and the precise binding interfaces have been observed across serotypes. For instance, a porcine strain utilizes a bipartite NLS for its binding mechanism with high affinity for all IMPα isoforms.29 In contrast, the bat-derived AAV 09YN variant, while also employing a bipartite NLS for its interaction, binds IMPα isoforms with various degrees of affinity.32 The AAV6 serotype, while maintaining the bipartite NLS mechanism, was shown to use BR1 in the minor site (as per both AAV11 in this study and the porcine strain) and BR3 within the major site (like the bat 09YN variant and AAV11). However, unlike AAV11, the AAV6 capsid only contains the standard three BRs, and as such, without BR1+BR3, NLS could not localize to the nucleus in cellular assays.30 This distinction illustrates the diversity in nuclear localization strategies even within closely related AAV serotypes, highlighting the evolutionary adaptations that influence their cellular trafficking and nuclear entry mechanisms, and underscores the necessity of thoroughly examining each AAV serotype’s unique aspects of nuclear localization. Such a detailed understanding is invaluable for refining AAV capsid engineering strategies in gene therapy applications.

Recent findings have identified AAV11 as notably more efficient in targeting projection neurons compared to AAV2.16 When combined with research indicating AAV11’s predilection for the cerebrum and spinal cord in non-human primates,59 a compelling case emerges for its application in gene therapies aimed at the CNS. Importins, which are abundantly present in dendrites, axons, and synapses,60,61,62 play a pivotal role in neuronal function not only through their traditional role in nuclear transport but also by facilitating synapse-to-nucleus signaling61,62,63,64,65 and influencing protein expression within neurons.60,66 For instance, the absence of IMPβ-11 in fly neurons leads to developmental defects, highlighting its role in the bone morphogenetic protein signaling pathway,60 while the replacement of IMPα2 with IMPα1 in mice significantly affects cell differentiation and survival.66

Understanding the finer details of the AAV transduction processes (particularly the aspects related to nuclear localization and the role of importin interactions) is crucial for customizing AAV gene therapy vectors more accurately and can lead to the improvement of transduction efficiency, gene expression, and therapeutic effects. This was clearly exhibited in a 2021 study,67 which demonstrated that swapping the VP1 N termini with those from different serotypes could drastically change transduction efficiency. Considering AAV11 VP1’s modest binding affinity for importin receptors, replacing this region with one from an AAV serotype with a higher importin protein receptor interaction affinity may increase vector effectiveness. This deeper insight is key to advancing AAV-based therapeutic approaches, allowing for more targeted and efficacious treatments.

Materials and methods

Gene construct design

The amino acid sequence of the AAV11 VP1 protein (accession number AAT46339.1) was retrieved from the GenBank database. The segment comprising residues 115–174, “GRAVFQAKKRVLEPLGLVEEGAKTAPGKKRPLESPQEPDSSSGIGKKGKQ PARKRLNFEE,” was optimized for codon usage in E. coli and synthesized by GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). This optimization included the addition of an upstream N-terminal tobacco etch virus (TEV) vprotease site to facilitate subsequent GST cleavage. The gene was inserted into the pGEX-4T-1 vector using a BamHI restriction site. This construct will henceforth be referred to as AAV11 VP1-BR.

IMPα proteins, modified to lack the N-terminal autoinhibitory IMPβ1 binding (IBB) domain (ΔIBB), included human IMPα1 (hIMPα1ΔIBB; with a His tag and TEV cleavage site), human IMPα3 (hIMPα3ΔIBB; with a His tag and TEV cleavage site), human IMPα5 (hIMPα5ΔIBB; with a His tag and TEV cleavage site), human IMPα7 (hIMPα7ΔIBB; with a His tag and TEV cleavage site), and mouse IMPα2 (IMPα2ΔIBB; with a His tag, lacking a TEV cleavage site). Genes encoding these regions were inserted into the pET-30a(+) expression vector, as detailed in previous publications.45,68,69 Additionally, human IMPβ1 was inserted into the pMCSG21 expression vector (hIMPβ1; with a His tag and TEV cleavage site).70,71 All importin constructs were synthesized by GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA).

Plasmids used for mammalian cell expression of C-terminal GFP fusions with full-length AAV11 VP1 protein, as well as the respective substitution derivatives, were synthesized by GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). Plasmid mediating the expression of fusion proteins between GFP and either HCMV UL44, which is transported into the nucleus by IMPα/β1, or FrAdv1 pVII, which can be transported into the nucleus in an IMPα/β independent fashion, has been previously described.41,72 The mCherry-Bimax2 plasmid, encoding for a competitive inhibitor of the classical IMPα/β1 nuclear import pathway,73,74 was a gift from Yoshihiro Yoneda and Mashiro Oka (Osaka, Japan).

Fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled peptide design

Synthetic peptide encoding a shorter AAV11 VP1-BR with an N-terminal Ahx-linker and a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) tag was sourced through GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). The peptide consists of amino acid residues 120–171; “QAKKRVLEPLGLVEEGAKTAPGKKRPLESPQEPDSSSGIGKKGKQPAR KRLN.”

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Expression and co-purification of AAV11 VP1-BR and IMPα2 constructs were achieved using a method previously described for porcine AAV NLS.31

For biochemical assays, overexpression of IMPα1, IMPα2, IMPα3, IMPα5, IMPα7, and IMPβ1 was performed in E. coli, and each recombinant protein was purified using methods previously described.29,75

EMSAs

EMSAs were performed using previously established methodology.29,32 Briefly, 10 μM FITC-tagged AAV11 VP1-BR synthetic peptide was mixed with 20 μM importin proteins (IMPα1, IMPα2, IMPα3, IMPα5, IMPα7, and IMPβ1) and 3 μL of 50% glycerol in a total volume of 20 μL. Samples, including both protein and peptide-only samples, were loaded onto a 1.5% agarose gel in TB buffer (45 mM Tris and 45 mM boric acid, pH 8.5) and run for 2 h at 75 V. The gel was imaged twice: once using a UV filter to detect peptide fluorescence, and again with visible light after staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, followed by destaining to visualize protein bands.

FP assays

FP was performed using a previously described protocol.29,32 FITC-tagged AAV11 VP1-BR synthetic peptide at 2 nm was mixed with a 2-fold serial dilution of importin proteins (IMPα1, IMPα2, IMPα3, IMPα5, IMPα7, and IMPβ1) starting at 4.5 μM over 23 wells. The final 24th well was left with peptide and buffer alone. All wells were made to a final volume of 200 μL. Measurements were taken using a CLARIOstar Plus plate reader from BMG Labtech (Ortenberg, Germany) in a 96-well black fluotrac microplate from Greiner Bio-One (Kremsmunster, Austria). Data were further analyzed using GraphPad Prism (Prism 9, version 9.3.1) (Boston, MA, USA), and a binding curve was fitted to one-site-specific binding least squares fit function to determine the KD.

Crystallization, data collection, and structure determination of the AAV11 VP1-BR and IMPα2 complex

Crystallization of the AAV11 VP1-BR:IMPα2 complex was performed using the hanging drop vapor diffusion method.76 Complex was mixed 1:1 with the reservoir solution and incubated at 22°C for 2 days. Rhombic-like crystals (100 × 70 × 15 μM) formed in 0.65 M sodium citrate, 0.01 M DTT, and 0.1 M HEPES, pH 7.0. Crystals were collected, cryoprotected in a mixture of reservoir solution with 20% glycerol for ∼10 s, and flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen.

X-ray diffraction data of AAV11 VP1-BR:IMPα2 crystals were collected on the MX2 beamline at the Australian Synchrotron (Clayton, VIC, Australia). Data were indexed and integrated using the software DIALS,77 while scaling and merging were performed via Aimless.78,79 Structure 4OIH68 from the Protein DataBank (PDB) was used as the search model for molecular replacement performed via Phaser.80 The final model was refined and rebuilt through cycles of Phenix81 and Coot,82,83 respectively. The final structure was solved to a resolution of 2.6 Å, with an overall Rwork/Rfree of 0.21/0.24 (Table S1). The final model (Figure 4) consisted of 426 residues of IMPα2, 17 residues of AAV11 VP1-BR, and 12 water molecules. The final stereochemistry and additional refinement statistics are presented in Table S1. Binding interactions between AAV11 VP1-BR and IMPα2 were determined via PDBsum database analysis.84

Tertiary structure model generation

Predictive models were produced using AlphaFold250 to compensate for a lack of structural data. Models of full-length AAV11 VP1 with IMPα2, IMPα5, and IMPα7, respectively, were created, emphasizing the predicted “binding” interface.

Cell culture and transfections

HEK293A cells were cultured using Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 50 U/mL penicillin, 50 U/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine. All cells and media were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were kept in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C and were passaged upon reaching confluence. Transfections were performed by seeding HEK 293A onto glass coverslips in a 24-well plate (5 × 104 cells/well). The next day, cells were transfected with varying amounts of expression constructs (5–250 ng) using Lipofectamine 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were then incubated with 5% CO2 at 37°C in complete medium85 until they were ready for processing for CLSM.

CLSM and image analysis

Cells were incubated for 30 min with DRAQ5 (#62251, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; 1:5,000 in DMEM without phenol red), 24 h post transfection. Cells were washed twice with PHEM 1× buffer (60 mM PIPES, 25 mM HEPES, 10 mM EGTA, and 4 mM MgSO4) and fixed with 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA), for 10 min at room temperature. Coverslips were washed three times with PBS and mounted on glass slides using Fluoromount G (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA). Subcellular localization of fusion proteins was analyzed using a Nikon A1 confocal laser scanning microscope (Minato City, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 60× oil immersion objective.86,87 Levels of nuclear accumulation of proteins of interest were determined using Fiji software88 from single-cell measurements of cytoplasmic (Fc) and nuclear fluorescence (Fn) and, following the subtraction of autofluorescence/background (Fb) fluorescence using the formula Fn/c = (Fn − Fb)/(Fc − Fb), as previously described.89 Statistical analysis of data was performed using the Welch and Brown-Forsythe ANOVA test through GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).
