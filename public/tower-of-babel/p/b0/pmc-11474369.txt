Microtubule function and dysfunction in the nervous system
Authors: Eun-Hae Jang, Harryn Choi, Eun-Mi Hur
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrotubules are core components of the neuronal cytoskeleton, providing structural support for the complex cytoarchitecture of neurons and serving as tracks for long-distance transport. The properties and functions of neuronal microtubules are controlled by tubulin isoforms and a variety of post-translational modifications, collectively known as the “tubulin code.” The tubulin code exerts direct control over the intrinsic properties of neuronal microtubules and regulates the repertoire of proteins that read the code, which in turn, has a significant impact on microtubule stability and dynamics. Here, we review progress in the understanding of the tubulin code in the nervous system, with a particular focus on tubulin post-translational modifications that have been proposed as potential contributors to the development and maintenance of the mammalian nervous system. Furthermore, we also discuss the potential links between disruptions in the tubulin code and neurological disorders, including neurodevelopmental abnormalities and neurodegenerative diseases.Keywords: Neural development, Neurodegeneration, Post-translational modification, Tubulin code, Tubulin isotype

INTRODUCTION

Microtubules represent a principal cytoskeletal component of neurons, performing pivotal functions in the developing and mature nervous system. In the developing nervous system, microtubules mediate fundamental cellular processes, such as neuronal migration, differentiation, and synapse formation (Dent, 2020, Marín et al., 2010, Stiess and Bradke, 2011). Given the highly polarized structure of neurons and the intricate architecture of the nervous system, it is evident that the dynamic remodeling of neuronal microtubules is essential to ensure the correct positioning of neurons and the establishment of functional neural circuits. In both the developing and mature nervous system, microtubules also serve as tracks for long-distance transport of various cargoes, such as RNAs, proteins, and intracellular organelles (Goldstein and Yang, 2000, Twelvetrees, 2020). The extreme polarity of neurons and the extensive arborization of axonal and dendritic processes necessitate the precise regulation of intracellular transport to properly sort and distribute cargos to their designated locations. The significance of neuronal microtubules is reflected in the association between mutations in tubulin isoforms (Jaglin et al., 2009, Keays et al., 2007, Poirier et al., 2010), microtubule-based motor proteins and proteins that regulate microtubule dynamics (Bodakuntla et al., 2019) and a wide range of neurodevelopmental disorders and neurodegenerative diseases.

Specialized functions of microtubules depend on (1) the α- and β-tubulin isotypes that are incorporated into the polymer, (2) the post-translational modifications (PTMs) of tubulins, and (3) a plethora of microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs). The concept of “tubulin code” was first proposed in the 1970s to describe the hypothesis that the diversity of tubulin isotypes and their PTMs collectively form a complex molecular code that can be read by a multitude of MAPs, thereby contributing to the functional specifications of microtubules (Verhey and Gaertig, 2007). In this review, we summarize the current understanding of the tubulin code, with a particular focus on tubulin PTMs, and discuss the functional implications of the tubulin code in the mammalian nervous system.

THE TUBULIN CODE IN THE NERVOUS SYSTEM

Microtubules are dynamic polymers, assembled from a head-to-tail arrangement of α/β-tubulin heterodimers. The number of α- and β-tubulin isoforms varies across species: Caenorhabditis elegans has 9 α and 6 β genes (Gogonea et al., 1999), Mus musculus has 9 α and 8 β genes (Hausrat, Radwitz et al., 2021; Tantry and Santhakumar 2023) and humans have 9 α (Khodiyar, Maltais et al., 2007) and 9 β genes (Nsamba and Gupta 2022). Tubulin isoforms show a high degree of homology in the GTP (guanosine triphosphate)-binding region, with 97% and 95% conservation of α- and β-tubulins, respectively, across vertebrate species (Sirajuddin et al., 2014). However, significant variability lies at the intrinsically disordered C-terminal tails, which protrude out from the microtubule lattice. The C-terminal tails serve as primary sites for the interaction with a number of MAPs and motor proteins, and are subjected to extensive PTMs (Box 1). The incorporation of distinct tubulin isoforms into the microtubule polymer may confer specific biochemical, physical, and/or functional properties (Baker, Rothwell et al., 1990; Newton, DeLuca et al., 2002; Vemu, Atherton et al., 2017; Ti, Alushin et al., 2018). This theme is supported by the discovery of tubulinopathies, a wide spectrum of neurological disorders caused by mutations in tubulin genes. Mutations in TUBA1A and TUBB2 are associated with cortical malformations, such as lissencephaly and asymmetrical polymicrogyria (Jaglin et al., 2009, Keays et al., 2007). TUBA8 and TUBB2B mutations cause polymicrogyria (Abdollahi et al., 2009, Jaglin et al., 2009) and TUBB4A mutations cause hereditary dystonia (Hersheson et al., 2013). It seems reasonable to posit that the transcriptional and translational regulatory mechanisms that control the expression of the isoforms, as well as the stoichiometry of different tubulin isoforms in the microtubule array, contribute to the functional specifications of microtubules.

Tubulin PTMs.

Detyrosination/tyrosination. Most eukaryotic α-tubulin isotypes end with a C-terminal tyrosine residue, which is subject to cleavage and readdition as part of a detyrosination/tyrosination cycle. Tyrosination is mediated by tubulin tyrosine ligase (TTL) (Raybin and Flavin, 1977, Schroder et al., 1985); detyrosination is catalyzed by vasohibins VASH1 and VASH2, in complex with small vasohibin-binding protein (SVBP) (Aillaud et al., 2017, Nieuwenhuis et al., 2017). The recently identified microtubule-associated tyrosine carboxypeptidase also has detyrosination activity (Landskron et al., 2022). Detyrosinated microtubules can be further processed by cytosolic carboxypeptidases (CCPs) to generate Δ2- and Δ3-tubulins. Tyrosinated microtubules are dynamic, while detyrosinated microtubules are typically more stable. Ttl−/− mice die perinatally (Erck et al., 2005), and mice lacking both SVBP and MAPCAP are viable but show a reduction of brain volume (Landskron et al., 2022).

Acetylation. Acetylation occurs on the highly conserved lysine 40 (K40) residue of α-tubulin. Unlike most other PTMs, acetylation occurs on the inner luminal side of the microtubule. Acetylation is mediated by tubulin acetyltransferase αTAT1 (α-tubulin acetyltransferase 1) (also called MEC-17); deacetylation is catalyzed by histone deacetylase 6 (Hubbert et al., 2002) and sirtuin 2 (North et al., 2003). Acetylation enhances microtubule flexibility and confers resilience to mechanical stress, possibly by weakening interprotofilament interactions (Portran et al., 2017, Xu et al., 2017). The function of α-tubulin acetylation in the nervous system is still controversial. Kalebic et al. (2013) generated a whole-body knockout of Atat1 and reported that Atat1−/− was viable with seemingly no major developmental defects in the nervous system. However, later studies showed that deletion of Atat1 resulted in a subtle disorganization of the dentate gyrus in the mouse brain (Kim et al., 2013) and increased axonal branching of cortical neurons in the developing somatosensory cortex (Wei et al., 2018). Conditional knockout mice have also been generated to deplete αTAT1 specifically in peripheral sensory neurons, and results from these Avil-Cre::Atat1fl/fl mice suggest that αTAT1 plays an important role in mechanosensation (Morley et al., 2016).

Glutamylation. Glutamylation is initiated by the addition of the first glutamate to the γ-carboxyl group of an internal, gene-encoded glutamate residues in the C-terminal tail of α- and β-tubulin, generating a branch glutamate (van Dijk et al., 2007). This branch can be elongated by adding subsequent glutamates to form a polyglutamate chain of various length (Janke et al., 2005, van Dijk et al., 2007). These initiation and elongation reactions are catalyzed by distinct tubulin tyrosine ligase-like (TTLL) family of enzymes. Humans have 9 TTLL glutamylases while deglutamylation is catalyzed by members of the CCP family (Kimura et al., 2010, Rogowski et al., 2010, Tort et al., 2014). Glutamylation is the most abundant tubulin modification in the mature brain. Depletion of functional CCP1 causes neurodegeneration in mice and humans (Berezniuk et al., 2010, Shashi et al., 2018).

Glycylation. Glycylation involves the addition of 1 or more glycine residues to the internal glutamate residue in α- and β-tubulin. Glycylation is catalyzed by members of the TTLL family, functioning as either an initiase or an elongase. Its reverse enzyme(s) has yet to be discovered. TTLL3 and TTLL8 have been suggested to control the stability and motility of ependymal cilia (Grau et al., 2013), but the role of this PTM in the nervous system remains poorly documented. Further research is needed to elucidate the potential roles of glycylation in the developing and mature nervous system, as well as whether and how dysregulation of this PTM contributes to neurological diseases.

Neuronal microtubules undergo extensive modifications by a variety of PTMs. Some modifications, such as phosphorylation, ubiquitylation, sumoylation, and acetylation, occur as in many other proteins. However, the functional significance of these modifications in the regulation of microtubule stability and dynamics remains unclear, not only in the nervous system but in other tissues in general. Other modifications, such as tyrosination/detyrosination, glutamylation, and glycylation, were initially identified in tubulin (Barra et al., 1973; Edde et al., 1990; Redeker et al., 1994) (Fig. 1), and increasing lines of evidence suggest that these tubulin PTMs control the intrinsic properties of microtubules and their interactions with MAPs. The identification of the enzymes responsible for the addition and removal of tyrosination, glutamylation, and acetylation has facilitated a more comprehensive understanding of the roles of PTMs in the nervous system (Akella et al., 2010, Janke et al., 2005, van Dijk et al., 2007).

TUBULIN PTMS IN THE DEVELOPING NERVOUS SYSTEM

Neurons undergo major developmental changes. They migrate, form axons and dendrites, and establish synaptic connections. The dynamic remodeling of microtubules contributes to all these cellular processes. Here, we summarize the involvement of tubulin PTMs during neural development (Fig. 2).

Neuronal Migration

Newborn neurons in the developing nervous system sense a range of environmental cues and transmit the information to the cytoskeletal machinery, as they migrate toward their destined locations. Microtubules modulate key processes of neuronal migration, including cell protrusion, adhesion, and contraction, and they also control the polarized trafficking in migrating cells. An accumulating body of evidence points to a role of tubulin PTMs in the regulation of microtubule dynamics during neuronal migration.

Detyrosination/Tyrosination

Knocking out Ttl, the major enzyme responsible for tyrosination of α-tubulin, results in perinatal death in mice due to neurodevelopmental defects (Erck et al., 2005). In Ttl−/− mice, neocortical layers were disorganized, which might be attributed to the defective radial migration of excitatory cortical neurons, and knockdown of its reverse enzyme, vasohibins, disrupted radial migration in the developing mouse neocortex (Aillaud et al., 2017). These studies suggest that the detyrosination/tyrosination cycle plays a critical role in the organization of the neocortex.

Acetylation

The transcriptional elongator complex subunit ELP3 was found to acetylate α-tubulin and regulate neural migration in the developing cortex (Creppe et al., 2009). The function of tubulin acetylation in the developing nervous system is still controversial. Kalebic et al. (2013) generated a whole-body knockout of Atat1 (α-tubulin acetyltransferase 1) and reported that Atat1−/− mice were viable with seemingly no major developmental and anatomical defects in the nervous system. However, Kim et al. (2013) reported that knocking out Atat1 resulted in a subtle disorganization of the dentate gyrus in the mouse brain, which may be attributed to defective migration. Further research is required to ascertain whether the depletion of Atat1 in specific cell types at a designated time point during development has any functional consequences.

Neurite Outgrowth and Axon Specification

Neurons are highly polarized cell types, and their morphological and structural asymmetry provides the foundation for the directional flow of information in the nervous system. A typical neuron acquires an axon and several dendrites through a process known as neuronal polarization, which is in part driven by an uneven distribution of microtubule PTMs (Stiess and Bradke, 2011).

Detyrosination/Tyrosination

Tyrosinated microtubules are enriched at the growth cone (Ahmad et al., 1993), and several studies have demonstrated that the detyrosination/tyrosination cycle is crucial for axon growth and growth cone pathfinding (Konishi and Setou, 2009, Marcos et al., 2009). Biallelic inactivation of small vasohibin-binding protein (SVBP) in humans causes many anomalies involving microcephaly, intellectual disability, and delayed gross motor and speech development (Pagnamenta et al., 2019). Microcephaly is recapitulated in Svbp−/− mice, and it has been postulated that the phenotype may originate from abnormal neuronal differentiation (Pagnamenta et al., 2019). Downregulation of SVBP or VASH1/2 leads to a delay in axonal differentiation, while overexpression of VASH2 induces premature axonal differentiation in culture (Aillaud et al., 2017, Wang et al., 2019). Consistently, neurons cultured from Ttl−/− mice display premature axonal differentiation and disruption of reciprocal connections between the thalamus and the neocortex (Erck et al., 2005). Collectively, these findings suggest that the tyrosination cycle plays a regulatory role in axon growth and that alterations in the tyrosination cycle cause neurodevelopmental disorders.

Acetylation

As newly formed neurites mature, one of the neurites accumulates tubulin acetylation and becomes the axon (Witte et al., 2008). In contrast to a previous study reporting no significant anatomical abnormality in the Atat1−/− mouse (Kalebic et al., 2013), a later study showed that knocking out Atat1 resulted in abnormally long axons with increased number of branches in the cerebral cortex (Wei et al., 2018). Branch formation requires microtubule debundling and invasion into protrusions along the axonal shaft (Gallo, 2011, Ketschek et al., 2015). αTAT1-deficient neurons displayed increased microtubule debundling, accounting for axonal overbranching.

Glutamylation

It has been suggested that glutamylase tubulin tyrosine ligase-like (TTLL7) plays a role in the growth of MAP2-positive neurites in PC12 cells (Ikegami et al., 2006). However, an in vitro study using tubulin with short and long glutamate chains has demonstrated that deglutamylated microtubules grow faster and longer than glutamylated ones, implying glutamylation as a negative regulator of axon growth (Chen and Roll-Mecak, 2023).

Synapse Formation, Remodeling, and Synaptic Transmission

Synapses are not only formed in excess and eliminated during development, but also undergo continuous turnover in the mature brain (Südhof, 2021). Spine composition and morphology are regulated by dynamic microtubules that enter the spine (Dent, 2020). In addition, synaptic function relies on the energy supply provided by mitochondria, which are trafficked primarily along microtubules. The continued delivery of materials into synaptic compartments is essential to replenish components, which also depends on microtubule-based trafficking. How microtubule dynamics controls synaptic structure and function is largely unexplored, but recent studies point to a role of tubulin PTMs.

Detyrosination/Tyrosination

Autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disabilities are often associated with synaptic dysfunction. Loss-of-function variants in SVBP have been identified in 2 families with intellectual disability, and further studies have shown that knocking down Svbp resulted in a reduction in the number of excitatory synapses in cultured hippocampal neurons (Iqbal et al., 2019).

Acetylation

Synaptic microtubules have been observed to increase acetylation in an activity-dependent manner (Pandey and Sharma, 2011). This increase might play a role in the reinforcement of microtubule stability in active synapses, but precisely how acetylated tubulin regulates the structure and/or function of synapses remains largely unexplored.

Glutamylation

ROSA22 mice lacking functional PG1, a subunit of a neuronal polyglutamylase complex, display a dramatic reduction in α-tubulin glutamylation and exhibit impaired synaptic transmission, accompanied by the mis-localization of microtubule motor proteins such as KIF1 (Ikegami et al., 2007). Increased neural activity, induced by the blockade of the glycine receptor, has been shown to enhance tubulin polyglutamylation, which is also accompanied by reduced motor protein mobility and cargo delivery (Maas et al., 2009). These transport defects are prevented by functional depletion of a polyglutamylase, providing evidence for activity-dependent crosstalk between synaptic transportation and tubulin glutamylation.

TUBULIN PTMS IN THE MATURE NERVOUS SYSTEM AND DYSREGULATION IN NEURODEGENERATIVE DISEASES

Once the neural circuits are established, the maintenance and stabilization of neuronal architecture becomes a significant challenge. Microtubules constitute major architectural elements of axons and dendrites, and they act as directional highways for long-distance intracellular transport. Trafficking defects are a common feature of neurodegenerative diseases, and there is substantial evidence to suggest that dysregulation of tubulin PTMs plays a role in the pathology of these diseases.

Detyrosination/Tyrosination

It has been reported that tubulin tyrosine ligase (TTL) expression is downregulated in both sporadic and familial Alzheimer’s disease (AD) brains, accompanied by excessive amounts of detyrosinated and Δ2-tubulins (Peris et al., 2022). The administration of oligomeric amyloid-β peptide causes spine pruning in primary neurons obtained from Ttl+/− mice, which exhibit age-dependent hippocampal synaptic dysfunction (Peris et al., 2022). These results suggest that the detyrosination/tyrosination cycle plays a role in synaptic maintenance and plasticity and that the loss of dynamic microtubules may contribute to the pathogenesis of AD.

Loss-of-function mutations in PARK2, which encodes for Parkin, lead to early-onset familial Parkinson’s disease (PD). In young Park2−/− mice, dopaminergic neurons accumulate tyrosinated tubulin (Cartelli et al., 2018). The absence of Park2 has been suggested to accelerate microtubule aging in dopaminergic neurons, but further investigation is needed to elucidate whether and how dysregulation of tubulin PTMs plays a role in the pathogenesis of PD.

Acetylation

A growing body of evidence suggests that dysregulation of tubulin acetylation plays a role in the pathogenesis of several neurodegenerative diseases. Decreased levels of acetylated tubulin have been reported in postmortem AD brains (Esteves and Cardoso, 2020, Hempen and Brion, 1996, Zhang et al., 2015). Hdac6−/− mice have been shown to rescue amyloid-β–induced deficits in mitochondrial trafficking and cognitive pathology in APPPS1-21 mice (Govindarajan et al., 2013), a widely used AD mouse model that overexpresses human mutant amyloid precursor protein (hAPP695swe) and presenilin-1 (L166P) (Radde et al., 2006). Moreover, the administration of histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) inhibitors has been shown to ameliorate spatial memory deficits and synaptic loss in animal models of AD (Govindarajan et al., 2013, Ma et al., 2014, Zhang et al., 2014).

Dysregulation of tubulin acetylation has been reported in postmortem PD brains and several experimental models of PD (Esteves et al., 2014, Godena et al., 2014, Mazzetti et al., 2024). In addition, many proteins associated with familial PD, such as Parkin, PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog)-induced putative kinase 1, leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), and α-synuclein, interact directly with microtubules (Alim et al., 2004, Snead et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2011, Yang et al., 2005). In young Park2−/− mice, the level of acetylated tubulin was reduced, which preceded mitochondrial transport defects. However, in aged Park2−/− mice, the situation was reversed, with loss of PARK2 increasing acetylation (Cartelli et al., 2018). Mutations in LRRK2 comprise the most common cause of PD, and pathogenic mutations in LRRK2 enhance its binding to microtubules (Kett et al., 2012). More specifically, LRRK2 mutants could bind to deacetylated microtubules, and increasing acetylation by inhibition of HDAC6 or sirtuin 2 (SIRT2) prevents LRRK2 binding to microtubules and restores the axonal trafficking defects caused by LRRK2 mutants (Godena et al., 2014). Neurotoxicity induced by α-synuclein has also been shown to be rescued by SIRT2 inhibitors (Mazzetti et al., 2024, Outeiro et al., 2007). Given that HDAC6 and SIRT2 have multiple substrates other than tubulin, it seems reasonable to posit that prevention of pathology by targeting deacetylases in neurodegenerative diseases likely involves a broad range of cellular processes and might extend beyond their roles in regulating tubulin acetylation.

Reduced levels of tubulin acetylation have also been observed in postmortem brains of Huntington's disease (HD). In cells derived from HdhQ109 knock-in mice, which is a well-established mouse model of HD, inhibition of HDAC6 improved microtubule-dependent transport by recruiting kinesin-1 and dynein/dynactin (Dompierre et al., 2007). By contrast, genetic depletion of Hdac6 in the R6/2 mouse model of HD failed to correct axonal transport defects and did not modify physiological or behavioral HD-related phenotypes (Bobrowska et al., 2011). These discrepancies underscore the necessity for the validation of pathogenic mechanisms through the use of more rigorous systems.

Polyglutamylation

Purkinje cell degeneration (pcd) mice were first identified through their ataxic behavior, which correlated with the degeneration of Purkinje cells (Mullen et al., 1976, Wang and Morgan, 2007). Later, the mutation was found to be located in the gene Ccp1 (Fernandez-Gonzalez et al., 2002), and it was shown that pcd mice accumulated excessive amounts of polyglutamylation (Rogowski et al., 2010). Ccp1−/− and Ccp1−/−Ccp6−/− mice also show hyperglutamylation and neurodegeneration, and trafficking defects have been suggested as an underlying mechanism for the neurodegeneration observed in these mice (Bodakuntla et al., 2020, Magiera et al., 2018). Loss of functional CCP1 in humans has also been associated with progressive neurodegeneration of the central and peripheral nervous system, and remarkably, the pathological manifestations resemble those of the pcd mice (Shashi et al., 2018). These studies suggest that pathologically upregulated tubulin polyglutamylation causes neurodegeneration. However, much remains to be done to elucidate the physiological significance of tubulin glutamylation, which represents the most abundant tubulin PTM in the adult mammalian brain.

CONCLUDING REMARKS

Neurons have long been recognized as the cell type with the most intricate cytoarchitecture. Consequently, these cells necessitate the sophisticated control of microtubules, which is achieved by a multilayered regulatory mechanism. The tubulin code (1) exerts direct control over intrinsic microtubule properties and (2) regulates the repertoire of proteins that read the code, which in turn, has a significant impact on the stability and dynamics of microtubules. The regulation of the tubulin code enables the orchestration of a symphony of molecular events in the developing nervous system; these include a range of cellular processes, such as neuronal migration, neurite outgrowth, and the formation and elimination of synapses. In the mature nervous system, microtubules continue to play a pivotal role in maintaining complex neuronal morphology and ensuring efficient intracellular transport. An increasing body of evidence suggests that the dysregulation of microtubules may be a contributing factor in the etiology of a number of neurological diseases. However, our understanding of the precise molecular mechanisms by which altered tubulin PTMs lead to the observed defects remains limited. Further research is required to unravel the mechanisms involved, as they are likely crucial for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. We expect that the rapid advances in single-cell RNA and protein analysis tools (Labib and Kelley, 2020, Ryu et al., 2023) will expedite the identification of novel links between neurological disorders and dysregulation of neuronal microtubules. Moreover, advances in high-resolution imaging technologies (Jeong and Kim, 2022) will prove invaluable for revealing nanoscale patterns of isoform distribution and tubulin PTMs in microtubule arrays, thereby contributing to the discovery of novel regulatory mechanisms governing neuronal microtubule function and dysfunction in cellular environments (Table 1).
