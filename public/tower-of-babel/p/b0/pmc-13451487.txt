Application of novel knotless barbed sutures in open posterior cervical spine surgery: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Kai Shi, Hongxiang Wei, Zhenyu Xu, Yue Luo, Wenming Zhang, Xuanwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOpen posterior cervical spine surgery is associated with higher rates of postoperative wound complications compared to anterior approaches. This study aimed to compare the incidence of complications and cost-effectiveness between barbed and traditional sutures in open posterior cervical spine surgery.MethodsA retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data (2022–2024) was performed on 270 adult patients undergoing elective posterior cervical surgery. Of these, 138 (51%) received traditional sutures and 132 (49%) received barbed sutures. The primary outcome was the incidence of wound-related complications, specifically surgical site infection, dehiscence, and hematoma; secondary outcomes encompassed patient-reported metrics (NRS and NDI), wound closure efficiency, and perioperative costs.ResultsThe barbed suture group showed significantly shorter operative time (P < 0.001), wound closure time (P < 0.001), and drain removal time (P = 0.013), along with lower rates of wound dehiscence and hematoma (P = 0.049 and P = 0.038, respectively). No significant differences were noted in infection rates, incision length, hospital stay, 90-day readmission, or total hospitalization expenses. Barbed sutures were associated with higher rates of MCID achievement at 3 months in NRS-neck pain (P = 0.016) and NDI (P = 0.046). BMI, diabetes, and suture type were independent predictors of complications.ConclusionBarbed sutures improve surgical efficiency, reduce mechanical wound complications, and enhance neck pain-related outcomes in posterior cervical spine surgery. Their potential benefit for arm pain requires further investigation.

Introduction

Posterior cervical spine surgery [1], as a cornerstone intervention for pathologies involving multilevel posterior spinal cord compression (e.g., spinal canal stenosis, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament/ligamentum flavum) [2–4], faces dual challenges in postoperative wound management: advancing the principles of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) to optimize efficiency, while concurrently addressing the 1.6%−12% risk of incisional complications [5]. Studies indicated that impaired wound healing and surgical site infections were leading causes of unplanned reoperations, particularly given the elevated surgical site infections incidence in posterior cervical approaches compared to anterior approaches [6]. Consequently, meticulous surgical closure is critical for preventing wound-related complications and facilitating rapid recovery. Conventional techniques employing interrupted absorbable sutures involve time-consuming knot-tying and fragmented procedural steps [7], prolonging closure times and presenting obstacles to Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, including complications like seroma, hematoma, and delayed paravertebral muscle necrosis—a risk factor for secondary debridement [8, 9]. Systematic optimization spanning hydrostatic fascial layer sealing to tension-reducing dermal closure has thus emerged as a pivotal strategy to balance surgical efficiency and healing quality.

Advancements in material science have introduced novel bidirectional barbed sutures, which have been successfully adopted across obstetrics and gynecology, general surgery, plastic surgery, and orthopedic joint procedures [10–14]. These absorbable sutures featured a bidirectional barbed configuration that provided tissue anchorage upon tensioning [15], eliminating the need for knots, enabling continuous suturing, and maintaining uniform tension distribution. This innovation reduced closure time, lowers infection rates, and decreased overall hospitalization costs. However, sparse data existed on the safety and efficacy of barbed sutures in spinal surgery, particularly for posterior cervical approaches [16–18]. To address this gap, this retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data, conducted at the Department of Spinal Surgery, First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, evaluated key outcomes—closure efficiency, complication profiles, and functional recovery metrics—in patients who underwent primary open posterior cervical spine procedures with barbed versus traditional sutures. This study aimed to validate the clinical feasibility of barbed sutures in this high-risk anatomical context.

Methods

Demographic characteristics

This study was a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data. We included 270 patients with degenerative cervical pathologies causing posterior spinal cord compression. These included multilevel cervical spondylosis, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, and thickening of the ligamentum flavum. All patients underwent posterior decompression and fusion across 4 or more vertebral levels. All patients were recruited from the Department of Spinal Surgery, First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, between January 2022 and December 2024. Diagnoses were confirmed through comprehensive clinical history review, physical examination, and advanced imaging studies (dynamic radiographs, cervical MRI/CT myelography).

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age ≥ 18 and ≤ 80 years; (2) No prior cervical spine surgery; (3) Definite indication for posterior cervical decompression and fusion; (4) Willingness to comply with protocol requirements.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Revision cervical surgery; (2) Comorbid spinal tuberculosis, neoplasm, metabolic bone disorders, severe systemic disease, or autoimmune conditions; (4) Local dermatologic pathology impairing wound healing; (5) Chronic corticosteroid use (> 3 months); (6) Incomplete clinical or imaging data.

Preoperative evaluation

Following admission, baseline laboratory profiles were obtained, including complete blood count (CBC), comprehensive biochemistry panel, coagulation studies, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Additional assessments included electrocardiography (ECG), chest CT, transthoracic echocardiography, bilateral lower extremity venous Doppler ultrasonography, and advanced cervical imaging (anteroposterior/lateral radiographs, triplanar CT reconstruction, and MRI). These evaluations ensured exclusion of absolute surgical contraindications (e.g., active infection, coagulopathy, cardiopulmonary instability).

Experimental group allocation

Among 307 initially screened patients, 37 were excluded: 18 declined participation, 17 opted against the novel bidirectional barbed suture technique due to concerns regarding its safety and efficacy, and 2 withdrew due to cost-related apprehensions associated with the barbed suture material. Consequently, 270 patients were prospectively enrolled in this cohort study and allocated into two groups according to the suture technique utilized: a control group (n = 138) receiving traditional interrupted absorbable sutures and an experimental group (n = 132) managed with barbed sutures (Fig. 1). Baseline demographic characteristics (age, sex, BMI) and comorbidity profiles (diabetes, smoking status) were comparable between groups (Table 1). Research procedures

Surgical and suturing methods

All patients underwent cervical spine surgery in this study were administered general anesthesia. A traditional posterior midline approach was employed for all patients, with the paravertebral muscle tissue being dissected subperiosteally from the spinous processes and laminae. The need for decompression varied depending on the different types of diseases. All patients received internal fixation treatment. All suturing procedures were performed by two attending physicians from the same medical team, who had undergone specialized training and had mastered suturing techniques.

Traditional suturing technique

In the control cohort, a standard three-layer closure was employed. The fascial layer was approximated using interrupted sutures of 0 Vicryl absorbable material (Ethicon Inc.). Subsequently, the dermal layer was closed with 2 − 0 Vicryl (Ethicon Inc.), and the subcuticular layer was approximated using 3 − 0 Vicryl (Rapide Inc.).

Novel barbed suturing technique

The experimental group underwent closure utilizing Fenkuill, an absorbable bidirectional barbed suture manufactured by Hainan Biomedical Device Co.,LTD. (formerly known as Hainan Jianke Pharmaceutical Co., LTD). Fascial closure was performed in a continuous, uninterrupted manner using size 2 Fenkuill. The dermal layer was approximated with either size 2 or size 1 Fenkuill, while the subcuticular layer utilized size 2 − 0 Fenkuill. For both the fascial and dermal closures, the procedure commenced at the midpoint of the incision, with two surgeons working simultaneously from the center toward each respective end, maintaining a suture spacing of 0.5–1 cm between throws (Fig. 2A). Upon reaching the incision terminus, a reinforcing backward suture was performed from both ends toward the midpoint with one to two additional throws. The remaining suture was then transected at its exit site (Fig. 2B). For the subcuticular layer, the technique was similar, beginning at the midpoint and progressing bilaterally; however, upon reaching the wound end, the suture was advanced approximately 2 cm beyond the incision margin before exiting, with the excess material subsequently removed (Fig. 2C and D). During the operation, the same assistant recorded the incision length, and the same circulating nurse recorded the surgical incision closure time, which is defined as the time from the first stitch of the deep fascia to the last stitch of the skin suture, and the time for each layer of suturing was also recorded. A sterile gauze roll was rolled and moderately pressed against the surface of the sutured deep fascia. If bloody exudate appeared at the incision site during pressing, it indicated a positive leakage test, suggesting poor quality of deep fascia suturing and the need for additional stitches. If no bloody exudate was observed at the incision site during rolling and pressing, it indicated a negative leakage test, suggesting good quality of deep fascia suturing. The circulating nurse recorded the time when the leakage test was performed.

Postoperative management

In line with guidelines for preventing surgical site infections following spinal surgery [19] and the perioperative ERAS rehabilitation protocol for orthopedic surgery [20], physicians diagnosed surgical incision complications, regularly inspected and recorded the status of the incision, and managed any wound complications that arose. Postoperative wound care was performed on a regular schedule with documentation of complications such as infections, necrosis of the wound edges, non-healing, dehiscence, bullae, fat liquefaction, and irregular wound edges. Routine postoperative blood tests, including CBC, ESR, and CRP, were conducted. Patients with hemoglobin levels below 80 g/L or albumin levels below 20 g/L received blood transfusions or albumin supplementation as needed. All patients underwent a standardized rehabilitation program led by a team of rehabilitation therapists. After discharge, patients continued their rehabilitation exercises according to a unified plan and returned for regular follow-up visits.

Outcome measures

The outcome measures in this study were divided into two dimensions: primary and secondary outcomes. Primary outcomes focused on early postoperative (0–3 months) wound-related complications, including wound dehiscence, surgical site infection requiring antibiotic intervention, and hematoma formation, with late-onset infections within one postoperative year also recorded. A composite adverse outcome endpoint (encompassing major complications, reoperation, or death) was defined secondarily for long-term prognostic assessment. Quantitative indicators covered operating room time (ORT), incision closure time (from the first stitch to complete closure), incision length, suture time per unit length of incision (total time divided by incision length), length of stay (LOS), 90-day readmission rate, and suture cost and total medical expenses, with standardized data collection ensuring study validity. The patient-reported outcomes (PROs), namely NRS - NP/NRS - AP and NDI, were collected pre - and three months post - operation via prospective electronic follow-up. For PROs data analysis, a 30% baseline improvement is defined as the MCID, with cases having a pre-operation zero score excluded to ensure validity. All outcomes were evaluated by independent assessors blinded to suture type where feasible: wound complications were adjudicated by surgeons not involved in the index procedure using standardized diagnostic criteria (surgical site infection defined by CDC/NHSN guidelines, dehiscence by fascial disruption, and hematoma by imaging or evacuation), while patient-reported outcomes (NRS-NP, NRS-AP, NDI) were collected via electronic questionnaires at predefined time points (preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively) to minimize recall bias. Additionally, management indicators like drainage tube removal time and perioperative costs were recorded. High NRS/NDI values indicated severe symptoms, and the MCID achievement rate was used as a core parameter for functional recovery assessment.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis in this study was performed using SPSS version 27.0. Variables were categorized into continuous and categorical types. Continuous variables, including ORT, incision closure time, incision length, hospitalization days, and medical expenses, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables (e.g., 90-day readmission rate, wound complications) were presented as frequencies/percentages. Parametric tests were applied for continuous variables assuming a normal distribution, with independent - sample t-tests for between-group comparisons and Pearson chi-square tests for categorical variables. Significance was set at p < 0.05. A multivariable analysis was conducted: multivariable logistic regression for binary outcomes (e.g., postoperative complications) and linear regression for continuous outcomes (e.g., PRO score changes), adjusting for potential confounders like age, sex, BMI, diabetes history, and pre-op NDI. The statistical models included univariate screening and multivariate adjustment to ensure robust validation of variable associations.

Results

Demographic characteristics

This study enrolled 270 patients undergoing elective posterior cervical fusion procedures, with 138 cases (51%) allocated to the conventional suture group and 132 cases (49%) to the barbed suture group (Table 1). The cohort demonstrated a mean age of 62.4 ± 11.7 years, with male predominance in the barbed suture group (73.5%, n = 97) and 47% (n = 62) of patients presenting with ≥ 1 comorbidity. Comparative analysis of baseline characteristics revealed that the barbed suture group exhibited significantly higher prevalence of coronary artery disease (CAD, p = 0.026), wheelchair-assisted admission rates (p = 0.003), preoperative diagnosis rates of cervical spondylotic radiculopathy (CSR, p = 0.041), and spinal cord injury (SCI, p = 0.004). Additionally, this group demonstrated significantly elevated baseline NRS-NP and NDI scores (p = 0.003/0.010). We acknowledge that significant between-group differences existed in CAD, wheelchair-bound status, CSR and SCI diagnoses, and baseline NRS-NP/NDI scores, which arose from non-random patient allocation inherent to this retrospective cohort study rather than true randomization. No significant intergroup differences were observed in age, gender distribution, or BMI.

Postoperative wound complications and PROs

The study group (barbed suture) demonstrated significantly shorter ORT (147.04 vs. 163.42 min, P = 0.014) and superior wound closure efficiency (closure time: 13.47 vs. 26.86 min, P < 0.001), with suture speed normalized to incision length reaching 0.84 vs. 0.41 cm/minute (P < 0.001). Earlier drain removal was observed in the study group (5.02 days, P = 0.013), while no significant intergroup differences were noted in hospitalization duration (14.12 vs. 14.41 days, P = 0.413), total costs (52216 vs. 57015 CNY, P = 0.289) (Table 2), or 90-day readmission rates (3.0% vs. 4.3%, P = 0.567) (Table 3). PROs revealed that the barbed suture group achieved significantly higher rates of MCID improvement in NRS-AP (65.9% vs. 51.4%, P = 0.016) and NDI scores (67% vs. 38.4%, P < 0.001) at 3-month follow-up, though no difference was observed in arm NRS-NP improvement (51.5% vs. 46.2%, P = 0.229). Importantly, both groups demonstrated no statistically significant differences in 1-year postoperative wound infection rates (5.1% vs. 3.6%, P = 0.223) or 3-month wound infection rates (2.3% vs. 3.6%, P = 0.513) (Table 3), indicating that barbed sutures did not effectively reduce incision infections but mitigated risks of dehiscence and hematoma.

Postoperative Wound Complication Regression Analysis

Univariate regression analysis demonstrated that the conventional suture group had a significantly higher risk of postoperative wound complications compared to the barbed suture group (OR = 3.44, 95%CI:1.28–9.28, p = 0.014). No associations were observed between complications and age (P = 0.789), gender (P = 0.142), or preoperative NDI scores (P = 0.080). However, elevated BMI (P < 0.001) and diabetes mellitus (P < 0.001) were identified as significant risk factors.

The multivariable model further validated the independent predictive value of suture type (OR = 3.20, 95%CI:1.14–9.00, P = 0.028), while confirming BMI (P = 0.002) and diabetes mellitus (P < 0.001) as independent risk factors. Analytical statistics indicated that, beyond metabolic parameters, the use of barbed sutures exerted a critical influence on wound healing complications. Discussion

Compared to anterior approaches, posterior cervical procedures are associated with significantly elevated risks of postoperative complications such as incision infection and delayed wound healing due to differences in anatomical architecture and biomechanical properties of the incision [21]. Operative duration, tissue traction intensity, and closure technique reliability have been identified as critical determinants of incision outcomes. Consequently, exploring efficient, minimally traumatic suturing strategies is imperative for reducing perioperative risks and facilitating early functional recovery. Traditional techniques utilizing non-absorbable sutures require repetitive needle passes and time-consuming knot tying, which not only prolong closure time but also leave residual knots that may trigger foreign body reactions postoperatively. These reactions, manifesting as chronic serous drainage or localized inflammation, directly compromise patient recovery experience and healthcare quality.

In recent years, knotless barbed suture technology has gained clinical traction owing to its unique biomechanical advantages. Its bidirectional helical barbed configuration maintains tissue tension through self-anchoring effects, theoretically streamlining the suturing process to reduce operative time, minimize manual coordination demands, and mitigate complications arising from foreign material retention [22]. In orthopedic surgery, barbed sutures have become a prevalent option for deep fascial closure in joint arthroplasty [16, 17], with studies suggesting enhanced closure efficiency, improved wound healing, and reduced early postoperative pain [23, 24]. However, debates persist regarding its dynamic stability and long-term biocompatibility: some researchers caution that the “cutting effect” of barbed structures in high-mobility regions may elevate microtrauma risks to fascial layers, predisposing to impaired wound healing or superficial infections [25, 26]. These divergent outcomes may be context-dependent—for instance, stringent postoperative activity restrictions in cervical procedures promote incision stability, whereas active rehabilitation protocols following joint arthroplasty may exacerbate dynamic friction between sutures and soft tissues, precipitating mechanical complications.

This study demonstrated that barbed sutures significantly reduced total ORT and wound closure duration compared to conventional sutures, particularly highlighting their efficiency advantage in posterior long-segment cervical surgeries. Specifically, barbed sutures significantly shortened total wound closure time by an average of 13.39 min (P < 0.001). They also improved closure speed by 0.43 cm/min (P < 0.001) after adjusting for incision length. These advantages are mainly due to the knotless design, which streamlines the procedural workflow. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups in incision infection rates, hospitalization duration, 90-day readmission rates, or total costs. These findings suggested that barbed sutures optimize ORT through enhanced closure efficiency, supporting their clinical utility in complex spinal procedures, though long-term tissue interaction effects warrant cautious evaluation. Furthermore, total hospitalization costs showed no significant difference between the two groups (52,216 CNY vs. 57,015 CNY, P = 0.289), a finding which diverges from most prior reports [27, 28]. Despite higher per-unit costs for barbed sutures (749.09 CNY vs. 584.49 CNY), they accounted for < 1% of total expenses, and ORT reduction may have offset ancillary resource expenditures in this study. The lack of cost correlation despite material price variance underscores that decision-making should prioritize clinical efficacy over local cost fluctuations.

Postoperative complication data revealed superior wound healing stability in the barbed suture cohort: 4 cases of wound dehiscence occurred in the conventional suture group, whereas no such events were documented in the barbed suture group. Hematoma formation was observed in 9 cases with traditional sutures versus 2 cases in the barbed suture group. Additionally, barbed sutures enabled earlier drain removal by an average of 0.53 days compared to traditional sutures (p = 0.013), suggesting reduced tissue trauma and fluid accumulation risk, likely mediated by homogeneous tension distribution and self-anchoring effects [29]. The marked reduction in wound dehiscence risk may stem from the barbed suture’s continuous mechanical support properties, which diminish repetitive penetration trauma and microvascular perfusion disruption caused by knots, thereby enhancing early-phase closure integrity.

Nonetheless, for spinal incision infection rates—a critical complication (incidence > 10%) [6] —this study, consistent with prior literature [7, 28], found no significant intergroup difference. This underscored that while novel suturing techniques may optimize biomechanical environments and mitigate mechanical complications, infection prevention remains contingent on rigorous aseptic protocols and systemic perioperative management. Our study further demonstrated that barbed suture closure conferred short-term improvements in postoperative neck pain among cervical surgery patients. At the 3-month follow-up, patients in the barbed suture group exhibited significantly higher MCID achievement rates for both NRS-NP and NDI compared to the conventional suture cohort. This disparity may be attributed to the barbed suture’s precise anatomical approximation capability within fascial and muscular layers or its enhanced mitigation of fascial closure tension, thereby facilitating muscular reparative processes and reducing postoperative nociception. Importantly, the pronounced reduction in NDI scores observed in the barbed suture group postoperatively still warrants cautious interpretation, as it may be confounded by small sample size limitations. Given the pivotal role of paraspinal musculature in biomechanical stability, this study provided a framework for evaluating the impact of closure techniques on PROs, though conclusions require validation through larger-scale studies.

While this study corroborated the clinical applicability of barbed sutures in cervical spine surgery, several limitations persist: the single-center design limited cohort size, and although the NRS and NDI are validated tools [30, 31], their subjectivity may introduce bias due to individual variability in pain perception. The 3-month follow-up may not fully capture long-term functional trajectories, wound remodeling, or potential delayed complications. A longer observation period—ideally 12 months or more—would provide more robust evidence for the sustained clinical benefits of barbed sutures in posterior cervical spine surgery, particularly for patient-reported outcomes such as neck pain and disability. Notably, suture type emerged as an independent prognostic factor for postoperative complications, suggesting potential for optimizing surgical protocols. Future studies should include multi-center randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up periods, integrate objective indicators such as wound healing time, infection rates, and scar formation, and explore subgroup analyses based on age, comorbidities, or surgical complexity to better define patient-specific benefits.

Conclusion

This retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data involving posterior long-segment cervical procedures demonstrated that the knotless barbed suture technique significantly reduced ORT and wound closure duration compared to conventional suturing, while concurrently mitigating risks of incision dehiscence and hematoma formation. No statistically significant intergroup disparities were observed in incision length, hospitalization duration, 90-day readmission rates, or total hospitalization costs. Furthermore, although PROs exhibited global improvement at 3-month follow-up, the barbed suture group demonstrated superior rates of achieving the MCID in NRS-NP and NDI scores. Based on its efficiency advantages, complication profile improvements, and critical PROs benefits, barbed sutures represent a highly efficient and safe closure option for posterior cervical fusion surgeries.
