Phubbing behaviors and psychological well-being: correlates and sociodemographic profiles in university students from Mexico, Colombia, and Peru

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe main objective of the study was to identify the degree of association between phubbing behaviors and psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru.MethodsThere was a sample of 1125 participants, who responded to the Phubbing Scale and the Psychological Well-being Scale for Adults (BIEPS-A), which demonstrated adequate psychometric properties for the sample, after the confirmatory factor analysis for validity and the Alpha of Cronbach for reliability. This was confirmed by performing a statistical analysis using the Structural Equations Model (SEM).ResultsWithin the results, it is observed that phubbing have a high degree of association with psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru. (-0.515, Sig. 0.001). There is an adequate fit of the proposed indices: the error of approximation (RMSEA) for Phubbing (0.080) and Psychological Well-being (0.054) reports an adequate difference between what was expected and what was actually found. The SRMR indicator for Phubbing (0.052) and Well-being (0.041) confirms that the differences between the real data and the model are minimal. The association of the two variables studied only indicates that, somehow they move together, although one cannot explain to the other. Background

The rapid technological development and the emergence of new digital platforms intertwine all aspects of modern life, generating a significant impact in the current world and establishing a new form of communication for society, which was adopted primarily by the younger population [1]. This reality raises the possibility of problematic or addictive use of these platforms among the youth in the 21st-century digital era, where social networks have become a key component influencing individuals’ impulses, emotions, perceptions, beliefs, and interpersonal relationships [2].

Technological media, widely accessible to children and youth, are mostly used for entertainment [3]. The mobile phone can generate a negative influence on the cognitive and psychological development of the user, which is initially evidenced by anxiety indicators upon separation from it, which in occasion requires professional help [4–8]. Anxiety and depressive disorders resulting from excessive internet use can pose a threat to the overall psychological well-being of the population, potentially hindering the achievement of sustainable development of objective 3 of the 2030 Agenda [9].

In this context, a new research topic is emerging that has begun to develop at a global level, it impacts psychological well-being, reduces academic engagement [10], increases mental exhaustion, promotes peer aggression, generates addiction to smartphones and physical/psychological dependence that leads to mood changes and life dissatisfaction, characteristics of depression [11–13]. This new topic is called Phubbing, a term that refers to the breakdown of communication between people due to the use of mobile phones, which could eventually lead to an alteration in neural patterns and behavior related to the fear of missing out [14, 15].

The use of smartphones, understood as a vital element for communication, has intensified to such an extent that, under current circumstances 6.92 million people have access to a smartphone, corresponding to 85.74% of the global population. This massification was accelerated starting from the COVID-19 pandemic, which led to the widespread adoption of smartphones for multiple activities, from everyday communication to teaching classes or conduction online consultations, with Colombia and Mexico being the Latin American countries that lead the use of mobile telephones worldwide [16, 17].

In the urban area of Peru, more than 11 million people aged ranging from 8 to 70 years old are users of the internet. The most common devices that are used to access the web include mobile phones, desktop computers, laptops, and tablets [18]. In the first trimester of 2022, 72.5% of the population made use of the internet [19]. As of 2023, the use of smartphones in Peru has increased from 88.4% which was registered in 2021 to 91.9% in 2022. It is important to mention while the use of smartphones increased in all geographical areas, the rural zone experienced the highest growth reaching 79.4% in 2022 [20].

Phubbing is a term that derives from the words “telephone” and “snubbing”, it also refers to the interruption of face-to-face communication with another person in any context to interact with a mobile phone [6, 21–23]. It is defined as the behavior of ignoring other people during a social interaction due to the use of the mobile phone [24, 25].

The quality of conversation and the feeling of connection between people may cause a negative impact that leads to an increase in a person’s stress levels, decreased levels of empathy and negative reactions may be observed due to lower-quality interactions, leading to dissatisfaction and distrust. It is emphasized that if phubbing occurs in a romantic relationship, the effect is more harmful, as it causes jealousy and disturbance due to the loss of attention from one partner [26–28].

In this context, Phubbing, represented by the widespread and excessive use of smartphones, would be directly related to and have an impact on well-being, making it necessary to understand psychological well-being as the subjective evaluation that people make of different aspects of their life experiences. Currently, psychological well-being is related to diverse aspects like human development, the stages of life, personality, family experiences, and health, among others [29, 30], so it becomes a construct of interest for scientific research and an important element that allows us to somewhat explain why people develop an excessive fear of missing out on information from social networks, which could lead to obsessive behaviors and significant communication disruptions [31, 32], resulting in anxiety, boredom, loneliness, and feelings of rejection and low academic performance [33–42].

In situations like these, people tend to evaluate their life experience based on positive and negative experiences [43], phubbing being one of those negative experiences that affect psychological functioning and therefore the well-being of the person. Therefore, it is important to propose educational actions, diagnosis and intervention, within prevention strategies, from public policies that allow for the care of human relationships by making better use of technologies -such as smart mobile phones-to improve people’s psychological well-being [44–48].

Phubbing and well-being and directly related. Phubbing is a disturbance present in a range of addictions associated with new technologies (internet addiction, video game addiction, social media addiction, etc.), demonstrating that nowadays many individuals center their well-being and sense of life on the speed of virtual interactions. This generates somatic symptoms, such as insomnia, social dysfunction, anxiety and depression, making phubbing and important psychosocial issue [31, 49]. Additionally, When phubbing occurs in romantic relationships, relationship satisfaction decreases, also increasing the likelihood of developing symptoms associated with depression, anxiety and stress, which results in lower psychological well-being [50–53]. This is confirmed because phubbing is a risk factor with a negative impact on psychological well-being [54]. However, despite the multiplicity of studies that point out the harm that the use of mobile devices generates in the psychological well-being of people, in some cases there are potential benefits for their psychosocial well-being to the extent that interacting face to face with others and at the same time using smart mobile phones is perceived as a greater connection between participants, which improves the quality of interpersonal relationships between them [55].

There have been a few identified investigations where the importance of the existing relationship between phubbing and psychological well-being is observed. In India a research study was conducted to assess the role of predictors and their subsequent impact on adolescents and young university students. A sample of 400 adolescents and young individuals whose data was collected through electronic questionnaires sent to them. Within the most important results, a prevalence of phubbing was found at 49.3%, and among the most important predictors, the following was identified: addiction to the internet, smartphones, fear of missing out, and lack of self-control. Among the consequences related to phubbing, significant indicators were found in social health and personal development, which were significantly related to depression and distress. The study concludes that adolescents and young individuals, like their families, need to participate in orientation programs to control phubbing and promote improvements in their overall health [56].

Furthermore, it was found that in Spain, a study was conducted to characterize users of smartphones according to their usage patterns and their impact on family and social groups. A sample of 275 individuals was used. Within their most important results, currently, there is a trend in the increase of internet addiction and the excessive use of smartphones. Among the conclusions it is observed that families should establish communication norms, smartphones being a limitant in the construction of a healthy family harmony. Additionally, it is suggested to formulate public policies focused on education that promote the prudent use of smartphones [57].

On the other hand, in Chile, the development of a research study whose principal objective was to analyze the relationship between university well-being, stress coping strategies, and family satisfaction in university students. A sample of 377 individuals was obtained through convenience non-probabilistic sampling. the strategies. Among the results, it is observed that on average psychological well-being is situated between medium and high levels. There is also evidence of a significant correlation between psychological well-being and family satisfaction [38].

An investigation conducted in Peru, which aimed to determine the relationship between phubbing, family climate and adolescent self-esteem, in the context of social isolation. A sample of 322 students was used. It was found that phubbing had a moderate and negative relationship with self-esteem and family climate. The negative relationship with self-esteem can generate dependence on the mobile phone and its applications [58].

The concepts of phubbing and psychological well-being are reflected in various assessment instruments that demonstrate their robustness in terms of psychometric properties, making it relevant to highlight some studies that have validated the quality of the instruments employed in the present research. Thus, Spain sought to analyze the psychometric properties of the Phubbing scale in a sample of adolescents and young adults and present a reliable and valid short form. The sample consisted of 1351 young people. The study positively associated phubbing with several indicators of psychological difficulties and negatively with psychological well-being and self-esteem [59, 60].

In Mexico, two investigations were identified, the first was performed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Psychological Well-Being Scale for Adults (BIEPS-A). The sample consisted of 188 university students and 94 individuals from the general population. In the discussion and conclusion, it is observed the scale BIEPS-A holds adequate validity and reliability to evaluate the psychological well-being in Mexico [61]. The second one’s purpose was to explore the psychometric properties of the Psychological Well-Being Scale for Adults (BIEPS-A) in Mexican undergraduate nursing students. It was a sample of 288 students was utilized. The study concludes that the BIEPS-A scale demonstrates adequate psychometric properties, suggesting studies with a larger population allow for a greater generalization [62].

Finally, in Peru, an investigation was developed with the purpose to analyze the Psychological Well-Being Scale for Adults (BIEPS-A) with the graded response model. A sample of 1016 university students between the ages of 18 and 25 years was used. Among the results, it is concluded that the scale is constructed in a unidimensional way, suggesting that further studies be conducted with university students [63].

This investigation was guided by the question “What is the degree of association between phubbing behaviors and psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru?”

This study justifies one of the first investigations that relates to phubbing behaviors to the psychological well-being in Latin American countries from a psychological perspective within a sustainability framework. This can also be taken as a limitation, due to the limited number of empirical studies conducted, as well as a strength as it serves as a formal research precedent for future studies. In the Latin American setting, specifically Mexico, Colombia, and Peru, there is a widespread use of smartphones compared to other countries in the region [16, 17]. It is important to mention that this study is part of a collaborative investigation in which researchers from the aforementioned countries participate.

Methods

Type and research design

The study is basic research, with a correlational design, as it characterizes two variables and identifies their relationship. The principal objective of the current study was to identify the degree of association between phubbing behaviors and psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru.

Population, sample, and sampling

The population was made up of 8,922,245 university students from Mexico (5,200,000) [64], Colombia (2,466,228) [65] and Peru (1,256,017) [66]. The inclusion criteria were the following: (i) Be a university student, (ii) Be between 16 and 60 years of age. The exclusion criteria were: (i) Not having accepted the informed consent, (ii) Not having answered all the evaluation items.

To select the sample, we decided to work with a maximum accepted estimation error of 3%, which statistically corresponds to 1,067 participants. However, the total study sample amounted to 1,125 university students with a 95% confidence level. Among the most relevant sociodemographic characteristics, 432 students were from Mexico, 290 from Colombia, and 403 from Peru; 685 students attended private universities and 444 attended public universities; 420 were men and 705 women; 975 students were single, 85 were married, 8 were divorced, and 4 were widowed.

Non-probability convenience sampling [67] was used since each country has different and particular characteristics and regulations regarding access and data collection in university students. The research team chose to collect information outside of university classrooms, using a Google Drive form implemented as a survey to be shared through a tablet or cell phone, which is detailed later in the procedure.

Instruments

The Phubbing Scale was developed by Karadağ et al. in 2015 [68]. It was adapted to Spanish by Blanca and Bendayan in 2018 [24]. This scale consists of 10 items evaluated on a Likert scale with five-response options where ‘never’ has a value of 1, ‘almost never’ has a value of 2, ‘sometimes’ has a value of 3, ‘almost always’. has a value of 4 and ‘always’ has a value of 5. The correction of the scale considers a general score obtained, which ranges between 10 and 50 points. Its interpretation has three levels: Low (10–24 points), Moderate (25–32 points) and High (33–50), whose classification depends on the score achieved. For the Spanish version, construct validity was analyzed through a confirmatory factor analysis, corroborating a two-dimensional structure: communication disturbance and telephone obsession. Communication disturbance, defined as the interruption of interpersonal communication that generates significant discomfort in others due to the frequent use of mobile phones, along with telephone obsession, understood as the uncontrollable need to use the mobile phone in different social contexts where it is not essential [24, 25].

The composite reliability for the scores of the two dimensions was 0.85 and 0.76, respectively. In the present study, a Cronbach’s Alpha index of 0.811 and a McDonald’s Omega index of 0.812 were obtained. The complementary statistical analysis to identify the Confidence Intervals (CI) of Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient [69] allows us to conclude that the total scale (CI = 0.790, 0.830) presents upper limits as required, worked with a confidence level of 95%. To confirm the psychometric properties of the instrument, the following were obtained: The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) of 0.080, the Standardized Root Mean Square Residuals (SRMR) of 0.052, the Tucker-Lewis Fit Index (TLI) of 0.859, and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) of 0.893, which confirmed its validity and reliability for the population of the present study [70–72].

The Psychological Well-being Scale for Adults (BIEPS-A) was developed by Casullo in 2002 [25]. This scale consists of 13 items evaluated on a Likert scale with three response options where ‘disagree’ has a value of 1, ‘neither agree nor disagree’ has a value of 2 and ‘agree’ has a value of 3. The correction of the scale considers a general score obtained, which ranges between 13 and 39 points. Its interpretation has three levels: Low (13–21 points), Moderate (22–30 points) and High (31–39), whose classification depends on the score achieved. For this scale, the construct validity was analyzed through a confirmatory factor analysis, corroborating a structure of four dimensions: control, autonomy, bonds, and projects. (i) control, understood as the ability to accept positive and negative aspects of environmental circumstances, experiencing the feeling that one can handle them; (ii) autonomy, defined as the ability of a person to assume responsibilities individually without the need for others; (iii) bonds, referring to an individual’s disposition to generate and maintain appropriate interpersonal relationships; (iv) projects, defined as the ability to establish clear and sufficient goals in life [25, 61, 62, 73]. The composite reliability for the four-dimension scores were 0.88, 0.84, 0.87, and 0.91, respectively. In the present study, a Cronbach’s Alpha index of 0.787 and a McDonald’s Omega index of 0.789 were obtained. The complementary statistical analysis to identify the Confidence Intervals (CI) of Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient [69] allows us to conclude that the total scale (CI = 0.764, 0.808) presents upper limits as required, worked with a confidence level of 95%. To confirm the psychometric properties of the instrument, the following were obtained: the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) of 0.054, the Standardized Root Mean Square Residuals (SRMR) of 0.041, the Tucker-.

Lewis Fit Index (TLI) of 0.909 and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) of 0.931, which confirmed its validity and reliability for the population chosen in this research [70–72].

Procedure

This current study was evaluated and approved through the Research Project Review Report of the Research Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology, César Vallejo University, in Peru, with code PID 056. Two researchers were invited to participate as collaborating members of the research group that developed the study. One of them of Mexican nationality and the other of Colombian nationality. They were integrated as independent researchers, that is, they did not formally represent the institutions to which they are affiliated. The task assigned to them was to collect information from university students in their countries, who agreed to complete a Google form that included informed consent, sociodemographic data, the phubbing scale, and the psychological well-being scale. When using non-probabilistic convenience sampling, the information was collected outside the perimeter of the universities, at the students’ class departure times, so it was not required to have approval letters from the different colleges to carry out the investigation. To participate in the study, the inclusion criteria were considered, therefore it was sufficient for the student to accept informed consent to complete the entire form, since his/her status as a university student gives him/her the power to agree to answer a questionnaire or not. For this reason and the type of sampling chosen, it was not required to include informed consent from the guardians of the minors.

Once the data collection process was completed, the database was downloaded into MS Excel, the data was cleaned and adapted for the use of the statistical package SPSS version 29 and its AMOS extension, with which the data collection processes were developed in the analysis of data.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for data analysis, identifying the levels and frequencies of the variables and their dimensions. Tables were prepared that included arithmetic mean, standard deviation, and sample percentage according to sociodemographic data. In addition, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test utilized, which showed significant differences between the sample and the population, which led to the use of non-parametric tests for statistical analysis. Inferential statistics were also used, such as the Goodman and Kruskal Gamma distribution test, creating association tables for ordinal data because they have two variables: phubbing behaviors and psychological well-being. This was confirmed by performing a statistical analysis using the Structural Equations Model (SEM).

In analyzing the fit model of the Phubbing construct, robust statistics such as the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residuals), the Tucker-Lewis Fit Index (TLI), and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) were used. It was found that, for the Phubbing and Psychological Well-being constructs, the fit model is consistent by obtaining robust indices [70–72].

Ethical aspects

The study was completed per ethical principles of the Declaration of Human Rights, the Nuremberg, the Belmont Report: Principle and ethical guidelines for the protection of human research subjects, the Declaration of Helsinki per The World Medical Association (WMA), the World Health Organization’s standards on studies and their ethical relationship when working with people, as well as international ethical guidelines for health-related research in humans. It is important to note that no compensation or incentives were offered to participants in this study.

Association between sociodemographic data and phubbing behaviors

The significant datasets are country (0.009*) and type of university (0.047*).

Association between sociodemographic data and the psychological well-being

The country dimension section is significant regarding psychological well-being (0.006*). University students in Peru have the lowest incidence of phubbing behaviors with 36.47%. Regarding the degree of association between the dimensions of Phubbing Behaviors and dimensions of psychological well-being, it is found that the communication disturbance dimension has a high degree of association with the dimensions control (-0.405, Sig. 0.001), autonomy (-0.198, Sig. 0.001), and projects (-0.273, Sig. 0.001), as well as with the total scale. Similarly, the Telephone Obsession dimension presents a high degree of association with the dimensions control (-0.492, Sig. 0.001), autonomy (-0.192, Sig. 0.001), and projects (-0.238, Sig. 0.003), as well as with the total scale. The psychological well-being has a high degree of association with all dimensions of phubbing behaviors: disturbance (-0.402, Sig. 0.001) and telephone obsession (-0.395, Sig. 0.001).

Fit model

The results of the goodness-of-fit analysis of the model show, in general terms, an adequate fit of the proposed indices. Firstly, it is observed that the error of approximation (RMSEA) for Phubbing (0.080) and Psychological Well-being (0.054) report an adequate difference between what was expected and what was actually found. Secondly, the SRMR indicator for Phubbing (0.052) and Wellbeing (0.041) confirm that the differences between the real data and the model are minimal. Thirdly, the TLI index for Phubbing (0.859) and Psychological Well-being (0.909) demonstrates adequate results. Finally, the CFI for Phubbing (0.893) and Psychological Well-being (0.931) demonstrates to some extent that this model is adequate and works much better than a simple or basic one. Therefore, the model adequately fits the reality it tries to explain. Although the TLI and CFI do not exactly reach the limit suggested as a criterion (≥ 0.95), the values ​​are on the limit, so it has been considered to report them as acceptable, since the objective of the research is correlational and not psychometric.

Linear regression analysis

The results of the linear regression analysis report a self-determination coefficient of R2 = 0.052 with a significance of p ≤ 0.001. Furthermore, the model coefficients were t=-7.89 with significance of p < 0.001. Furthermore, when performing the regression of all the chosen sociodemographic variables (country, gender, type of university, civil status, and age), values ​​below R2 = 0.003 are found for all of them.

Discussion

Currently, research studies are needed to find answers based on the analysis of theories and models that provide information on phubbing behaviors and their relationship with the interpersonal relationships of the young population [1, 3, 74]. In that sense, the main objective of the study was to identify the degree of association between phubbing behaviors and psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru.

Within the results, it is observed that phubbing behaviors have a high degree of association with psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru. This could lead to a potential biopsychosocial deterioration, as asserted by Erzen et al. (2021) and Jamadi et al. (2023) [5, 6, 21], leading to the search for professional help.

The results show that the phone obsession dimension of the phubbing behavior variable has an average of 14.8489, achieving the highest score when compared to the disturbance dimension, which only reaches 12.063. In respect to the psychological well-being dimensions, project dimensions have the highest score with an average of 11.0818, and bonds as the lowest score with 8.0587. To some context, this would help understand that young people show a higher tendency towards the permanent use of smartphones.

When analyzing the association between sociodemographic data and phubbing behaviors, it is found that the significant datasets are country (0.009*) and type of university (0.047*) (Table 1). The data comes from Mexico, Colombia, and Peru, where individuals either belong to a public or private university and are a certain age, which are also all significant factors regarding phubbing behaviors. The country dimension section is significant regarding psychological well-being (0.006*) (Table 2). From the sample, university students in Peru have the lowest incidence of phubbing behaviors with 36.47%. Belonging to a private university in any of the studied countries results in a lower frequency of phubbing behaviors (32.45%). As well as being a part of the middle age group − 41 years and older [75]- also leads to fewer phubbing behaviors (42.85%). While Mexico, Colombia, and Peru present high levels of psychological well-being, Colombia is in first place with 92.41%. From the above, it can be deduced, firstly, that while Mexico, Colombia, and Peru have similar cultural backgrounds, they also exhibit distinct social, demographic, educational, and other specificities that differentiate and set them apart. In Peru’s case, it can be identified that there is lower per capita income, lower population, higher rates of poverty, and informality, which may lead to limited access to technology, and consequently, fewer instances of phubbing behavior. Secondly, likely, students from private universities in the three studied countries have a higher power purchasing power, leading to a greater variety of internet connection possibilities. This does not exclusively confine them to smartphones. Finally, being 41 years or older, is synonymous with being a “digital immigrant” [76, 77], as lower technological knowledge leads to a decrease in the use of smartphones and allocates time to other types of activities.

The second specific objective was to establish the degree of association between phubbing behaviors and the dimensions of psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru. When analyzing the corresponding results it is evident that phubbing behaviors have a high degree of association with all dimensions of psychological well-being: control (-0.625, Sig.001), autonomy (-0.197, Sig.001), bonds (-0.142, Sig.013) and projects (-0.301, Sig.001) (Table 3). The statistical analysis through the structural equation model allows confirmation of the high degree of association between phubbing and the dimensions of psychological well-being (Table 4). This contributes to what has been found in the overall relationship between the phubbing behaviors and the psychological well-being. The results support Capilla et al. (2021), Maftei and Măirean (2023), as well as Mosley and Parker (2023) [26–28] when they argue that the quality of the relationships can be seen as negatively affected and produce difficulties that affect the following areas: personal, romantic and social relationships; resulting in an increase of stress levels, which is subsequently reflected in dissatisfaction and distrust. This aligns with what was proposed by Fernández-García et al. (2023) [43] by stating that phubbing behaviors could negatively associate the optimal psychological functioning of an individual when identifying an experience as positive or negative.

Regarding the degree of association between the dimensions of Phubbing Behaviors and dimensions of psychological well-being, it is found that the communication disturbance dimension has a high degree of association with the dimensions control (-0.405, Sig.001), autonomy (-0.198, Sig.001), and projects (-0.273, Sig.001), as well as with the total scale. Similarly, the Telephone Obsession dimension presents a high degree of association with the dimensions control (-0.492, Sig.001), autonomy (-0.192, Sig.001), and projects (-0.238, Sig.003), as well as with the total scale.

The third specific objective was to establish the degree of association between psychological well-being and the dimensions of phubbing behaviors in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru. The results found show that psychological well-being has a high degree of association with all dimensions of phubbing behaviors: disturbance (-0.402, Sig.001) and telephone obsession (-0.395, Sig.001) (Table 2). Once again, the aforementioned is fully confirmed after performing the statistical analysis using the structural equation model by dimensions (Table 3). This data is associated directly with what was mentioned by Incoll and Knowles (2025), Tandon (2022), Gao et al. (2023) [33–35], , in respect to the Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) syndrome, which refers to the perception that others are having gratifying experiences that one misses out on. It results in disturbances for the individual that are reflected in symptoms of stress, anxiety, boredom, and excessive use of mobile smartphones. This contributes to what was expressed by Bitar et al. (2023) and Wei et al. (2024) [14, 15] by stating that the consequences of improper use of smartphones lead to disruptions in circadian rhythms, confusing the sleep-wake cycle, as affirmed by Tacca et al. (2021) [58]. It leads individuals to sleep fewer hours due to staying connected to their mobile phones, according to Chen et al. (2021) and Chen et al. (2021) [7, 8]. Also the generation of mental health problems that impact impulse control, emotions, perceptions, beliefs, and interpersonal relationships [2].

According to the information presented previously on the degree of association between the variables studied and the information proposed by Sharma et al., (2025), Katevarapu et al., (2025), Al-Saggaf (2023), Denecker et al. (2023), Jiang et al. (2023), Marín et al. (2023), Soriano and Bellacetin (2022), as well as Pancani et al., (2021) [36–42, 49], when it is the parents who carry out phubbing behaviors, the emotional impact on the children is greater, producing feelings of rejection and eventually poor academic performance.

The fourth specific objective sought to determine the explanatory fit model for the constructs phubbing and psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia and Peru. The results obtained demonstrated that the proposed indices (RMSEA, SMRS, TLI, CFI) have an adequate fit. Although the TLI and CFI did not reach the criterion required by the authors Tuker and Lewis (1973) [71] and Schwarz (1978) [72], the values ​​are within the proposed limit (≥ 0.95) so it was considered to report them as acceptable, since the objective of the research is correlational and not psychometric.

The fifth specific objective was oriented to determine the explanatory causal relationship between phubbing behaviors, psychological well-being, and sociodemographic variables in university students. After linear regression analysis, a self-determination coefficient of R2 = 0.052 was found with a significance of p ≤ 0.001, a coefficient of the comparison model of the real model with the ideal model was t = -7.89 with a significance of p < 0.001, and a linear regression of all the chosen sociodemographic variables (country, gender, type of university, civil stadtus, and age), with values ​​below R2 = 0.003. This means that the model explains a very limited proportion of the variation in the data, and the remaining percentage of variability is due to other factors not included in the model or to random error. These results reflect a very irrelevant effect size (5%), which means that the phubbing model does not categorically explain psychological well-being. A first explanation could be given by the measurement context, in which multifactorial and complex psychological systems cannot be fully explained by a single model [79], due to various small-scale influences that interact in a non-linear way. In this sense, a modest effect size, such as the one found in the present study, helps to indicate significant components within a broader dynamic and, therefore, within the limitations of the case, could explain part of the influence that the phubbing variable has on psychological well-being [80, 81]. Another non-statistical explanation is presented in an interpersonal and especially generational context to explain the low associativity between variables based on daily communication between different generations [78], finding that younger people, belonging to Generation Z (or Centennials, Zoomers, or iGen) and even Generation Alpha, have normalized communication through smartphones as part of how they interact with others, blending in-person and virtual interactions without major interpersonal problems—something that does not occur in previous generations. Future research could delve deeper into these small variations found in psychological contexts, especially in the variables of the present study, which could better explain the interconnectedness. Furthermore, the sociodemographic variables may be explained by the fact that, currently, all three countries have widespread smartphone use, leading Latin America in this regard; this usage is not influenced by gender or type of university, as students from both private and public institutions have access to smartphones with unlimited connectivity. These results differ from the findings of Yahya et al. (2024) and Barbed-Castrejón et al. (2024), who state that higher levels of phubbing behaviors are associated with lower psychological well-being [21, 60]. From this comparison, it can be deduced that there is no definitive conclusion as to whether phubbing explains psychological well-being, and may agree with Capilla et al. (2021), Maftei et al. (2023) and Mosley et al. (2023) who indicate that phubbing is present in different aspects of an individual’s mental health, so it has not been sufficiently studied [26–28], and therefore the level of its impact on psychological well-being cannot be clearly established.

Finally, consistent with the results obtained, presented, and discussed above, it can be categorically mentioned that there is a high degree of association between phubbing and psychological well-being, which could generate a negative impact on human relationships in the intermediate and long term. In the long term, it directly harms the psychological well-being of people who could develop eventual difficulties and psychological disorders, generating a significant impact on the public health of nations. In this sense, this research is timely because it provides a preliminary diagnosis of the situation of university students, an important sector within society, since they are the ones who will have to face the future development of possible psychological problems associated with technology. Therefore, psychoeducational strategies that are supported by public policies must be proposed to guide the appropriate use of smartphones [44–48, 56, 57] for university students in Mexico, Colombia and Peru. This problem could become a limiting aspect for the achievement of Objective 3 of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, guaranteeing a healthy life that promotes the psychological well-being of all individuals [14].

Conclusions

Based on the analysis, observations, statistical processing, and the theoretical foundations found, it can be concluded that, for the population studied, phubbing and psychological well-being have an inverse and significant relationship. This means that as one increases, the other decreases, providing consistent and reliable statistical evidence to support this conclusion.

The association between the two variables studied only indicates that they are related in some way, although one cannot explain the other. This was confirmed through analysis using the SEM model, which revealed the structure of the relationship but did not demonstrate a causal effect of phubbing on psychological well-being.

In proposing this study, the research group assumed that phubbing could explain psychological well-being, which was not the case. However, through the comprehensive research analysis, it became evident that current generations (Z and Alpha) have been normalizing the use of smartphones as part of their daily communication without apparently causing interpersonal problems. This may differ from previous generations (X, Baby Boomers, etc.), who prioritize face-to-face interaction and are less reliant on these types of devices. This leads us to consider new forms of interaction between people, which could mean evolution in communicational aspects worldwide that would be difficult to predict and may serve as a basis for future research on the topic.

The main limitation of this study is the lack of formal background in indexed journals that allow the theoretical development of the variables in depth, which makes this research one of the first studies on the degree of association between phubbing and psychological well-being in university students from Mexico, Colombia, and Peru. With a psychological and sustainability approach, this makes it an important precedent for future research. The results of this study are contextualized in the countries where the data was collected, making it very difficult to link these results to other Latin American contexts. As mentioned in the justification, there are limited empirical studies, with research development and scientific publications on the topic being important [82]. Regarding broader global contexts, research focused on one or the other of the two variables studied [83–89] has been identified. However, this scarcity persists, except for the indexed article by Garrido et al. (2024) [46] developed in Spain, which addresses the two variables from a descriptive analysis that does not allow for a link with the results of this study. It focuses primarily on analyzing aspects of mental health in general and depression in particular, leaving aspects of phubbing in the background. Currently, other similar research in other countries around the world is unknown, which constitutes a significant limitation for this study. To avoid this limitation in future research, it is suggested to promote research networks on the variables studied to develop investigations that contributes to the quantity and quality of reliable data that can be located in indexed databases. Another possible limitation is not having conducted observations at different points in time, which would have allowed for a broader temporal view of the studied variables and their behavior. The use of a cross-sectional study, as it could identify factors not considered (level of knowledge about the use of digital technology, employment status, lifestyle, among others) that tend to generate confusion, as they can influence the observed associations, which could constitute a bias by distorting the true association between the variables. Added to this is the type of sampling used, which, being non-probability, intentional or convenience, restricts the generalization of the results, which must be understood contextually for populations with similar characteristics. It is therefore recommended that future research consider this aspect, enabling the development of policies in Latin American countries related to smartphone use and the psychological well-being of society. In addition, as often occurs in scientific research, it is likely that some bias may have been present, preventing a full assessment of the association between the studied variables, given that an effect of phubbing on psychological well-being had been expected, which was not observed in practice.
