The perfect match: Aortic valve sizing to maximize hemodynamic performance and survival
Authors: Barbara C.S. Hamilton, Shinichi Fukuhara, Michael Boulware, Robert B. Hawkins, Sachin S. Goel, Bo Yang, Himanshu J. Patel, Gorav Ailawadi, Stanley J. Chetcuti, Shuzhen Li, Mikhale Grundtner, Julie A. Linick, Michael J. Reardon, G. Michael Deeb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe assessed the value of using a size ratio of implanted valve opening diameter to native annular diameter to determine postoperative hemodynamics and survival after surgical aortic valve replacement.MethodsIn total, 1519 patients from 3 trials had a preoperative computed tomography scan. A size ratio was created using the implanted valve's Ebels' prosthetic valve−derived effective diameter (ED) and the patient's computed tomography perimeter−derived native annular diameter (CTD). Restricted cubic spline curves showed the association between size ratio and hemodynamic performance. Patients were divided into 3 ratio groups: <80%, 80% to 90%, and >90%. Hemodynamic performance was evaluated yearly for 5 years. Multivariable proportional hazard regression examined the association between size ratio and 5-year survival.ResultsMean valve gradient, peak velocity, effective orifice area (EOA), indexed EOA, dimensionless velocity index, and prosthesis-patient mismatch were significantly better advancing from the 80% to the 90% groups (P < .001). The >90% group had a single-digit mean valve gradient, peak velocity ≤2.0 m/s, EOA ≥2.0 cm2, indexed EOA ≥1.0 cm2/m2, dimensionless velocity index ≥0.5, and no prosthesis-patient mismatch ≥85% at 1 and 5 years. Size ratio was an independent predictor of mortality with survival benefit through 5 years (80%-90% vs <80%, hazard ratio, 0.752; 95% confidence interval, 0.611-0.927, P = .008; >90% vs <80%, hazard ratio, 0.712; 95% confidence interval, 0.506-1.001, P = .05).ConclusionsAn ED/CTD size ratio >90% is associated with optimal postoperative hemodynamic performance and improved 5-year survival. Preoperative ED/CTD ratio provides the ideal implanted valve size and helps determine if annular enlargement is necessary.

Central Message

It is not the size of the prosthetic valve that matters but the match of the inner prosthetic valve size to the patient's native annulus size that correlates with hemodynamics and survival.

Perspective

A novel, objective technique for SAVR sizing using the match ratio of Ebels' uniform prosthetic valve sizing technique (ED) and the patient's preop CT scan aortic valve perimeter-derived annular diameter (CTD), ED/CTD, resulting in superior hemodynamic performance, less heart failure, and improved survival at 5 years.

In 1978, Smith and colleagues2 published the first successful series of surgical aortic valve replacements (SAVR), describing the ideal prosthesis-patient match as a “prosthetic valve with a functioning opening that matches the patient's normal functioning valve opening.” Aortic prosthetic valve sizing has been controversial for years.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 The International Organization for Standardization Task Force was formed to establish universal, accurate methods for sizing and labeling surgical heart valves.10 Ebels and colleagues1 later published an independent study with uniform measurements for the effective opening diameter (ED) of the most common stented surgical valves using a mechanical conical technique.

We propose using a contrast preoperative computed tomography (CT) angiographic scan, as a uniform measuring tool, to obtain the patient's aortic valve perimeter-derived annular diameter (ie, CTD) in association with the ED for the surgeon's selected prosthetic valve to preoperatively determine the most suitable valve size for implantation. This study was designed to determine the best size match ratio between ED/CTD to provide optimal hemodynamic performance and establish the ratios' impact on survival.

Methods

Study Population

Patients from the surgical arms of the randomized CoreValve High Risk (clinicaltrials.gov, NCT01531374), Surgical Replacement and Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (SURTAVI) (intermediate risk; NCT01586910), and Evolut Low Risk (NCT02701283) trials were included.11, 12, 13 In summary, all patients had severe symptomatic aortic stenosis or asymptomatic critical aortic stenosis and were deemed of appropriate risk for the trial by the site assessment team, which incorporated the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) predicted risk of mortality (PROM) according to the study inclusion and exclusion criteria. Every patient underwent a preoperative CT scan. All candidates were screened for inclusion by a central screening committee, using echo data analyzed by an independent core laboratory and CT scan measured and sized by a central core laboratory to minimize variations in interpretation. Institutional review boards from each site approved the study protocol (Online Data Supplement), and all patients provided written informed consent, including permission to publish the study data. The study was designed and conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Valve Sizing and Type

Only xenograft bioprosthetic valves could be implanted. Mechanical, homograft, and autograft devices were excluded. The bioprosthetic valve size, model, and type for SAVR were determined at the implanting surgeon's discretion using the approved sizing tools and the manufacturer's labeled valve size (MLVS) according to the instructions for use. A minimal number of annular enlargements (<4%) were performed in the trials.11, 12, 13

Study Design

All patients who underwent SAVR implantation from the 3 randomized trials were pooled. Patients with missing CT annular sizing data or documented surgical valve data (model, type, and size) were excluded. Preoperative CT scans were used to calculate the CTD. The prosthetic valve model, type, and MLVS were identified in the patient's operative note. The ED was then determined using the Ebels' sizing table, and the size ratio (ED/CTD) was calculated for each patient (see the section Statistical Analysis). The most predominant valve types were stented bovine, stented bovine with externally mounted leaflets, and stented porcine.

Outcomes Studied

The hemodynamic parameters compared between the 3 ratio groups at 1 and 5 years included mean aortic valve gradient (MG), peak velocity, effective orifice area (EOA), indexed effective orifice area (iEOA), dimensionless velocity index, and incidence of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), as defined by VARC III criteria.14 Clinical outcomes included all-cause and cardiovascular mortality and degree of congestive heart failure (CHF), as evaluated by New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification.

Statistical Analysis

A total of 1519 patients with SAVR attempted and implanted were combined and included from the 3 trials. All data points required for analysis were consistently collected by the 3 trials. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation; comparisons between or across subgroups were evaluated using 2-sample t tests for 2 groups or analysis of variance between 3 or more groups. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact test. Ordinal variables were compared using the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to determine the rates of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality through the 5-year follow-up visit. The log-rank test was used to compare the time to events between subgroups. Restricted cubic spline curves with 5 evenly spaced knots at the 1/6, 2/6, 3/6, 4/6, and 5/6 quantiles were used to explore the relationship between 1-year hemodynamic characteristics and size ratio (%). The spline regression curves displayed the trend of the relationship and identified the knots (80%, 90%) in the spline, which were interpreted as potential cutoff values or thresholds for the groupings. A size ratio (%) was calculated as a percentage of inflow orifice diameter (millimeters), as determined by Ebels using a conical sizer in various bioprosthetic valves and named the effective diameter (ED), divided by the CTD (millimeters) per baseline preoperative CT measurements. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify univariable and multivariable potential predictors of all-cause mortality through 5 years of follow-up. In the multivariable analysis, stepwise selection was used with criteria for model entry set at P = .15 and remaining in model set at P = .1. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

A cohort of 1829 patients who underwent SAVR was included in this study with 310 patients excluded, leaving a final cohort of 1519 patients (Online Data Supplement).

Size Ratio Groups

When the relationship between hemodynamic performance and size ratio was analyzed, 2 cutoff points were identified where change in hemodynamic performance was consistently observed (80% and 90%) (Figure 1). Therefore, 3 size ratio groups were established on the basis of these cutoff points: <80%, 80% to 90%, and >90%.Figure 1Spline curves showing the relationship between size ratio group and hemodynamic performance at 1 year.

Patient Distributions

The distribution of patients from each trial stratified by size ratio group is displayed in the Online Data Supplement, and the distribution of patients from each size ratio group stratified by trial is shown in the Online Data Supplement.

Baseline and Procedural Characteristics

Baseline clinical and procedural characteristics were different between size ratio groups (Online Data Supplement). Specifically, in the <80% group, patients were older, smaller, more likely to have class III/IV CHF as well as a greater STS PROM, higher MG, have an isolated SAVR, and longer cardiopulmonary bypass times, whereas the >90% group were more likely to have a root enlargement, bicuspid aortic valve, and greater left ventricular ejection fraction.

Types of Prosthetic Valves

A variety of valves were used across the 3 trials (Online Data Supplement).

Hemodynamic Results

Prosthetic valve hemodynamics were different between all size ratio groups at both 1 and 5 years postoperatively. The <80% group had the greatest MGs, whereas the >90% group had the lowest MGs. Peak velocities were greater in the <80% group than the >90% group (Figure 2). Better hemodynamic performance was present in the >90% group compared with the <80% and 80% to 90% ratio groups for both EOA and iEOA, as well as for dimensionless velocity index (Figure 2). Clinical Outcomes

Mortality rates were different between size ratio groups (Figure 4) with the <80% group having the greatest rates of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. The percentage of patients with more severe CHF (NYHA class III/IV) increased within each size ratio group over time, as did the percentage of patients who died. At 5 years there were no patients with class IV failure and the combined endpoint of death and class III failure was greater for the <80% than the 80% to 90% and >90% groups (Online Data Supplement).Figure 4Five-year all-cause (A) and cardiovascular (B) mortality.

Discussion

This is the first reported study using a standardized sizing method for all types and brands of prosthetic valves that demonstrated a direct correlation between the proper sizing method for a bioprosthetic valve and mortality. This analysis demonstrated better hemodynamic performance and 5-year survival in patients who received a bioprosthetic valve whose functional opening closely matched the opening size of their normal native aortic valve, determined by using a novel ratio of the implanted valve ED to the patient's native annular diameter measured on preoperative CT scan (CTD). Hemodynamic performance improved significantly with increasing size ratio group, and a size ratio of ≥80% correlated with a survival benefit.

Proper sizing of the bioprosthetic valve in relation to the patient's valve opening has been debated for over 30 years. Dumesnil demonstrated the correlation of color flow Doppler EOA to body size area (iEOA) to define PPM.3 Pibarot and colleagues4 determined the iEOA necessary to avoid serious hemodynamic compromise and negative clinical outcomes. Deeb and colleagues8 used the preoperative CT scans of patients who underwent SAVR to measure the annulus and showed that different sizes and types of bioprosthetic valves were being implanted into the same size patient annulus using the manufacturer's sizing tools and methods. This study showed the variability and inconsistencies of the various MLVS and sizing tools. A subsequent journal commentary was written suggesting that a preoperative CT scan for patient annular sizing may be the most consistent and appropriate mechanism for determining the actual size of a surgical patient's annulus.9 To improve the sizing process and the accuracy of the MLVS, the International Organization for Standardization Task Force identified areas for sizing improvement and clarification of labeling: (1) reporting physical dimensions and characteristics of surgical valves, (2) determining and labeling of valve sizes, and (3) in vivo and in vitro testing and reporting of surgical valve hemodynamic performance and thrombogenicity.10 To independently and uniformly measure the effective opening of the various bioprosthetic valves in the market and provide accurate objective sizing data for surgeons, van Boxtel and colleagues1 used a standardized conical method. We sought to bring clarity to the sizing conundrum by using the more accurate CT scan measurement for the patient annular dimensions (CTD) and Ebels' uniform prosthetic valve sizing (ED) and match them for sizing as recommended by Smith and colleagues2 nearly 50 years ago. We believe this valve sizing technique is more accurate and reproducible than the current inconsistent MLVS and sizers because it demonstrates an objective measurement for sizing that correlates directly to mortality.

The association of a more accurate match between the ED of the prosthetic valve and the patient's native valve opening (CTD) as a predictor of mortality should not be surprising because this study showed the association of a closer match with better hemodynamics and lower incidence of PPM. The PARTNER and CoreValve/Evolut randomized trials produced several articles supporting the association of a postprocedure MG ≥20 mm Hg or the presence of PPM as independent predictors of mortality.15, 16, 17 Meta-analysis and propensity-matched studies have shown the association of incidence of PPM and mortality.18,19 In addition, studies comparing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with SAVR hemodynamics and valve size showed that the superior performance of self-expanding, supra-annular TAVR, including the incidence of PPM, was associated with TAVR sizing on the basis of CT scan as opposed to MLVS for SAVR.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 In these studies implanted TAVR size increased appropriately as annular size and indexed size increased; however, for SAVR the valve sizes remained the same as both annulus and indexed annulus size increased, providing evidence of inappropriate sizing and matching for SAVR using the MLVS.

In this study valve size was not an independent univariable or multivariable factor associated with death. This study provides additional evidence that neither isolated valve size, iEOA, nor annular index are the sufficient answer, but that the relationship of the ED of the prosthetic valve to the size of the patient's annulus (CTD) is the crucial factor influencing hemodynamics and associated clinical outcomes.25

Studying the distribution of trial patients between the 3 size ratio groups, it is easy to assume that the patient population in the <80% size ratio group, with the greatest mortality, was predominantly comprised with older, smaller, sicker female patients within the High Risk Trial, receiving a smaller valve, and that these were the driving forces for mortality. It is true that 51% of the high-risk cohort were in the <80% size ratio group, but the high-risk group comprised only 28% of the <80% cohort. Nearly one half of the patients (49%) were from the SURTAVI trial and 23% from the Low Risk Trial. Also, female sex and valve size were not univariable independent risk factors for mortality. There were other important univariable factors for predicting mortality that are listed in Table 1. However, multivariate analysis showed that only age (older), greater STS PROM with more CHF, and size ratio match (<80%) between the prosthetic valve and the patient's annulus were independent predictors of mortality. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed that survival increased as the size ratio increased and was best at 5 years for the >90% group. The 80% to 90% group had a larger independent survival benefit (P = .008) versus the <80% group than the >90% group (P = .05) due to the small number of patients in the >90% group and therefore the larger confidence interval. It makes sense that STS PROM and NYHA classification are significant factors associated with mortality: STS PROM is a validated tool for predicting mortality and the basis for determining inclusion in the various US PARTNER and CoreValve/Evolut TAVR trials,26 and CHF severity is the most common symptom accompanying the diagnosis of severe symptomatic aortic stenosis and its associated high mortality rate.27

Another important impact of the known size ratio will be the preoperative knowledge of whether a root or annular enlargement may be necessary to attain the desired match. The small number of patients in the >90% group was secondary to the low number of annular enlargements performed during valve implantation in the pooled cohort. To decrease the incidence of PPM, Yang and colleagues28, 29, 30 developed an enlargement procedure for better size match, improved hemodynamics, maximum probability of successful TAV-in-SAV, and possibly longer durability and better long-term clinical outcomes. There is concern with overenlargement of the SAVR causing compromise of coronary artery access for future diagnostic catheterization and percutaneous interventions. This study provides an objective parameter (size match ratio) to determine the best prosthesis-patient sizing match for optimal hemodynamics, improved survival, and avoidance of unnecessary oversizing to minimize compromise of coronary access with future TAV-in-SAV procedures.

Limitations

Our study is limited by implicit surgeon bias when selecting valve types and sizes. Also, we could not determine the true impact of additional larger size ratio groups on hemodynamics and clinical outcomes because of the lack of enlargement procedures and minimal number of patients with a size ratio >90%. Variability for CT scan annular measurements was minimized by using a small number of consistently trained core lab analysts and the 3mensio program (3mensio Medical Imaging) to perform the measurements in 30% systolic cardiac phase in all 3 randomized controlled trials. Likewise, ED measurement variability was minimized by having the measurements made with the same calibrated machine, in the same laboratory, and same personnel.

In addition, there were many confounding variables that could not be accounted for when performing the analysis due to the heterogeneity of the pooled trial groups; however, multivariable analysis minimized the impact of these variables and showed that only age, STS PROM, NYHA CHF classification, and the size ratio for matching the bioprosthesis ED and the patient's CTD were independent factors impacting both 1- and 5-year mortality.

Conclusions

The size ratio between the bioprosthetic valve's ED and the patient's CTD is an accurate and reproducible method to determine the appropriate size surgical valve that is associated with the best hemodynamics and clinical outcomes. It can also help determine whether enlargement is necessary. Valve size is not as important as the size ratio between the prosthesis ED and the patient's CTD.
