Washed Microbiota Transplantation as a Rescue Therapy for Refractory Unidentified Pathogen Intestinal Infections: Findings From a National Multi‐Centre, Real‐World Study
Authors: Sheng Zhang, Pan Li, Min Dai, Xia Wu, Chen Lu, Gaochen Lu, Qian Ren, Xuelin Wang, Yejun Si, Liuhui Chang, Suyu Tang, Jie Wu, Lan Yuan, Liwen Lyu, Honggang Wang, Fei He, Jinli Ding, Guangqing Huang, Xinzhi Pan, Yong Mei, Longqin Li, Shengjuan Hu, Xiujiang Huang, Bota Cui, Faming Zhang, Quan Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnidentified pathogen intestinal infections (UPIIs) represent a severe clinical dilemma, characterised by clear signs of intestinal infection yet no identifiable causative pathogen, often leading to prolonged, antibiotic‐refractory illness. A nationwide retrospective study based on the prospective cohorts from September 2015 to February 2025 was conducted in China to evaluate washed microbiota transplantation (WMT) on this challenging condition. Patients diagnosed with UPIIs and then underwent WMT were included. The primary outcome was the clinical response rate one month post‐WMT. Finally, among the 81 included patients, 71.6% were bedridden, 46.9% required ICU admission and 51.9% developed multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. Diarrhoea was the primary symptom, and over half received ≥ 3 empirical antibiotics. Despite the challenges, WMT achieved a one‐month clinical response rate of 63.0% and a cure rate of 43.2%. Multivariate analysis identified several baseline risk factors affecting WMT efficacy, including adverse events (AEs) related to WMT (β = 1.545, p = 0.026, OR = 4.690, 95% CI 1.208–18.206), total abdominal symptom scores (TASS) before WMT (β = 0.292, p = 0.047, OR = 1.340, 95% CI 1.004–1.788) and WHO performance status score ≥ 4 (β = 1.583, p = 0.031, OR = 4.867, 95% CI 1.160–20.423). The overall AEs rate was only 8.3% (18/216). A nomogram based on logistic regression [akaike information criterion (AIC) = 93.75] was developed to predict the clinical non‐response at one month after WMT. The favourable clinical outcomes observed in this study provide cohort‐based evidence on using WMT for treating refractory UPIIs. These findings implied that if WMT is available, earlier WMT may be beneficial for UPIIs.Keywords: intestinal infection, microbiota medicine, pathogen, washed microbiota transplant

Introduction

Intestinal bacterial infections result in a spectrum of symptoms ranging from self‐limiting diarrhoea to life‐threatening conditions such as severe dehydration, shock, malnutrition and even death (Petri Jr. et al. 2008). The primary treatment for enteric infections involves antibiotics, which paradoxically can exacerbate dysbiosis and provoke antimicrobial resistance, consequently contributing to the emergence of refractory infections (Shakoor et al. 2019; Timsit et al. 2025). These refractory infections, including Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI), multi‐drug resistant organisms (MDROs) and various bacterial infections with identified or unidentified pathogens, have become an urgent global public health and socioeconomic problem (Shakoor et al. 2019; Guh et al. 2020). Among them, unidentified pathogen intestinal infections (UPIIs) present a particular clinical challenge, including those with unrecognised, undetected and technologically undetectable pathogens. The lack of clear pathogenic evidence frequently leads to empirical antimicrobial therapy, which may prove ineffective and further aggravate dysbiosis and clinical deterioration. Therefore, alternative therapeutic approaches should be considered for patients with antibiotic‐refractory UPIIs.

Among microbiota‐targeted technologies, faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a promising therapeutic technology for dysbiosis‐related diseases by restoring gut microbiota. A systematic literature review on global FMT reported published from 2011 to 2021 has revealed its widespread use for infections in the gut or beyond the gut, such as CDI, MDROs, antibiotic‐associated diarrhoea (AAD), recurrent urinary tract infections and others (Wang et al. 2022). Since 2014, the methodology of FMT has been improved based on the automatic washing process and related delivery techniques. This refined approach has been developed into a more standardised medical technology, named washed microbiota transplantation (WMT) (Zhang et al. 2020; Fecal Microbiota Transplantation‐standardization Study Group 2020).

As a stool bank providing national non‐profit WMT service, we have observed a 44.4% (8/18) recovery rate among critically ill patients with AAD following WMT (Dai et al. 2019). Our previous cohort study further demonstrated that WMT could reach over 90% clinical cure rate of primary and recurrent CDI (Wu et al. 2023). The success of WMT in treating CDI and AAD has bolstered clinical confidence in potential application for other intestinal infections, including refractory UPIIs. However, whether WMT is a safe and effective treatment for refractory UPIIs requires further investigation. Therefore, given that the safety and efficacy of WMT in related intestinal and extraintestinal infections (Dai et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2023, 2021), this study aimed to evaluate the safety and potential benefit of rescue WMT in patients with UPIIs.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This is a retrospective analysis using prospectively collected data conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (NMU) and other twenty‐three tertiary hospitals. The rescue WMT was provided by China Microbiota Transplantation System (CMTS, fmtbank.org), supported by the Second Affiliated Hospital of NMU. This study obtained central ethics approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital of NMU. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or legal representatives. This study is a part of the registered study (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03895593) of the long‐term safety and efficacy of rescue FMT for refractory intestinal infections. All procedures were followed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

The diagnosis of UPIIs was confirmed by a consensus of three senior gastroenterologists, based on the presence of typical gastrointestinal infectious symptoms (e.g., diarrhoea, abdominal pain, hematochezia, abdominal distension or fever) and elevated inflammatory markers, with supportive imaging and/or endoscopic evidence and in the absence of any identified pathogen through available testing, including faecal cultures and PCR. Notably, elevated inflammatory markers were considered indicative of gastrointestinal infection when they remained persistently elevated despite controlled or non‐worsening extraintestinal infection symptoms, and in the presence of persistent, unrelieved gastrointestinal infection symptoms. The inclusion criteria included: (1) patients diagnosed with UPIIs as defined above; (2) patients who underwent rescue WMT; (3) patients who were followed up for at least three months. The exclusion criteria were: (1) patients whose abdominal and/or gastrointestinal symptoms were partially alleviated after treatment with antibiotics; (2) patients who had previously undergone FMT/WMT; (3) patients without complete medical data; (4) cases where elevated inflammatory markers were partly or entirely due to extraintestinal infections; (5) patients diagnosed with an established intestinal diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), ischemic enteritis, allergic enteritis and others.

The primary outcome was clinical response rate at one month post‐WMT. The secondary outcomes included clinical response rate at one‐week and clinical cure rate at one month post‐WMT, WMT‐related adverse events (AEs), and the factors of efficacy.

Data Collection and Clinical Assessment

A review was conducted on a database of patients with UPIIs who had applied for rescue WMT through their physicians from CMTS between September 2015 and February 2025. Pre‐WMT data of patients were collected through the medical records. Part of post‐WMT data was also collected through the medical records during the patients' hospitalisation. After discharge, patients were contacted via telephone and requested to complete a questionnaire designed to elicit post‐WMT data. A minimum follow‐up period of 12 weeks after rescue WMT was conducted.

According to the Preferred Reporting Items for Microbiotherapy (PRIM) guidelines of 2024 (Zhang et al. 2025), the details of WMT‐related information were also recorded and stated, including the indication, delivery route, source, preparation for products, classification, dosage, formulation, concomitant treatment, efficacy and safety.

Clinical response was defined as a reduction in total abdominal symptom score (TASS) of ≥ 50% from baseline. Clinical cure was defined as the resolution of clinical symptoms associated with intestinal infections, indicated by a TASS of ≤ 1 and a decrease in TASS of ≥ 75% from baseline, without requiring further treatment for intestinal infections. The TASS, a self‐developed tool for evaluating abdominal symptoms, was comprehensively described in Table S1, aligning with our previous study (Wu et al. 2023).

Charlson Comorbidity index (CCI) was used to assess the comorbidity status (Charlson et al. 2022). World Health Organization (WHO) performance status score was used to assess the functional status and overall performance of patients at baseline (Young et al. 2015).

Immunocompromised status was defined as patients (1) with primary or secondary immunodeficiency or (2) on immunosuppressive medications (immunosuppressants and/or corticosteroids 20 mg/day or higher for a minimum of one month, biologics or antineoplastic agents) at the time, or within one month, of WMT.

The Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, Version 5.0) was applied to describe AEs with WMT. The severity of AEs was graded as mild (Grade 1), moderate (Grade 2), severe/disabling (Grade 3), life‐threatening (Grade 4) and death (Grade 5). All AEs were divided into definitely, probably, and possibly related to WMT.

Donors and WMT Procedure

The donors aged from 6 to 24 years were screened by strict criteria. Protocol of donor screening and management has been described in our previous studies (Lu et al. 2022), and detailed in the consensus on the methodology of WMT (Fecal Microbiota Transplantation‐standardization Study Group 2020). The WMT procedure was performed following a standardised technical protocol using the GenFMTer automatic purification machine (FMT medical, Nanjing, China) followed by centrifugation plus suspension for three times (Fecal Microbiota Transplantation‐standardization Study Group 2020; Zhang et al. 2020). The time interval between faeces donation and the delivery of washed microbiota infusion or to cryopreservation (−80°C) is restricted to one hour (Fecal Microbiota Transplantation‐standardization Study Group 2020). The frozen washed microbiota suspension was sealed and transported under cryogenic conditions using dry ice when providing rescue WMT for other medical centres. The delivery routes for WMT included gastric tube, naso‐jejunal tube, gastroscopy in duodenum, colonic transendoscopic enteral tube (TET) (Yu et al. 2026), enema (Fecal Microbiota Transplantation‐standardization Study Group 2020). The protocol and workflow for rescue WMT were detailed in the Appendix S1.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with quartile, whereas categorical data were reported as frequencies with proportions. Differences in continuous variables were tested using the t‐test or Mann–Whitney nonparametric test, based on the normality of distribution. Categorical variables were analysed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test.

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the contributing factors of efficacy. And then the factors of univariate logistic regression, of which p value < 0.1, were involved in the multivariate logistic regression analysis by the method of forward. Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used for evaluating the multivariate model. A lower AIC indicated a relatively better generalisation ability (Cavanaugh and Neath 2025). Nomogram was made based on the best multivariable logistic regression analyses of the best AIC.

All above analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.1). A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among the 81 included patients, 63.0% (51/81) were male. Middle‐aged and young patients account for the highest proportion, reaching 50.60% (41/81). The median duration of the disease for patients exceeded one month. 71.6% (58/81) of the patients were bedridden (WHO performance status score ≥ 4), 46.9% (38/81) required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) for treatment, and 51.9% (42/81) developed multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). Additionally, patients had a high number of comorbidities, with a median CCI of 2 (0–4).

Efficacy and Influencing Factors of WMT

As shown in Figure 2B, about 65.4% (53/81) patients achieved clinical response at one week after WMT within the overall cohort. Furthermore, clinical response was observed in 63.0% (51/81), while nearly 43.2% (35/81) achieved clinical cure at one month after WMT.

As depicted in Figure 2C, significant reductions in the median TASS were observed at one week after WMT compared to baseline (p < 0.001). Univariate logistic regression revealed that antibiotic use during WMT (β = 1.761, p = 0.015, OR = 5.818, 95% CI 1.407–24.062), antibiotic use within two weeks after WMT (β = 1.153, p = 0.016, OR = 3.167, 95% CI 1.239–8.097), TASS before WMT (β = 0.299, p = 0.014, OR = 1.349, 95% CI 1.062–1.714), AEs related to WMT (β = 1.145, p = 0.042, OR = 3.143, 95% CI 1.045–9.452) and WHO performance status ≥ 4 (β = 1.759, p = 0.009, OR = 5.806, 95% CI 1.553–21.706) were the risk factors of the clinical response at one month after WMT. Furthermore, to better predict the outcome of WMT at the baseline level, considering the collinearity and generalisation ability, step multivariate analysis based on AIC optimisation was conducted (Tables S2 and S3). The result of multivariate analysis suggested that, after correcting for covariates (including antibiotic use during WMT [β = 1.075, p = 0.191, OR = 2.930, 95% CI 0.585–14.666] and types of antibiotic use before WMT [β = 0.213, p = 0.181, OR = 1.237, 95% CI 0.906–1.689]), AEs related to WMT (β = 1.545, p = 0.026, OR = 4.690, 95% CI 1.208–18.206), TASS before WMT (β = 0.292, p = 0.047, OR = 1.340, 95% CI 1.004–1.788), and WHO performance status score ≥ 4 (β = 1.583, p = 0.031, OR = 4.867, 95% CI 1.160–20.423) emerged as significant risk factors for the outcome of WMT (Table 2). In other words, UPIIs with higher TASS and WHO performance status scores before WMT, along with AEs during WMT, were more likely to fail to achieve clinical response.

Safety of WMT

A total of 81 patients completed 216 sessions of WMT. The overall rate of AEs related to WMT was only 8.3% (18/216) (Figure 2D and Table S4). A total of 27.8% (5/18) of AEs were classified as Grade 1, and 72.2% (13/18) were Grade 2. All AEs were attributed to microbiota‐related factors. No serious AEs related to WMT were found during the follow‐up.

Nomogram for Predicting the Efficacy of WMT

According to the multivariate analysis, a nomogram of logistic regression (AIC = 93.75) was made to predict the clinical non‐response at one month after WMT. As shown in Figure 3, the total points were the summary of AEs related to WMT, WHO performance status scores, TASS before WMT, antibiotic use during WMT, and types of antibiotic use before WMT. Each variable contributing to the total score was assigned a specific point value based on its weight. Higher total score, greater probability of failure in clinical response at one month after WMT.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a nationwide multicenter cohort to assess the efficacy and safety of WMT in treating refractory UPIIs. It is noteworthy that this is a systematic investigation of UPIIs, a patient population often overlooked in clinical practice. Our real‐world data provided the first description of the clinical characteristics of patients with UPIIs, with over 50.0% being critically ill, exhibiting poor functional status and having a short expected survival time. The core symptom was diarrhoea, with more than half of the patients having previously undergone treatment with three or more empirical antibiotics, leading to a protracted course lasting months. These patients were prone to severe complications, including MODS, extraintestinal infections and ICU admission, highlighting the gravity of the clinical issue. Despite recent advances in microbial sequencing technologies (Li et al. 2021; Dong et al. 2025), the causative pathogen in this patient group often remains undetected, similar to ‘dark matter’ beneath the surface (Wang et al. 2025), posing significant challenges in the clinical management of UPIIs. Nevertheless, the one‐month clinical response rate and cure rate reached 63.0% and 43.2% after WMT in our study, respectively. Our findings provide initial evidence supporting the implementation of WMT as an alternative therapeutic approach for patients suffering from UPIIs, particularly those who have failed antibiotic treatment. In some cases, early application of WMT during the initial stages of intestinal infection may be used as the minimum viable intervention to achieve therapeutic effect, while avoiding additional costs, such as sequencing and treatment.

The primary significance of this study lies in extending the application of microbiota‐targeted technology from single pathogen diseases like CDI to the complex clinical scenario of UPIIs. Recent research has revealed that complex infectious milieus are characterised by intense antagonistic or synergistic interactions among coexisting microbes and a strong host immune response induced by one pathogen may significantly suppress the replication of another coexisting pathogen, rendering it below the conventional detection limit (Deol and Miura 2025). This suggests that the ‘unidentified’ nature of UPIIs may stem not merely from technical limitations but could represent the net outcome of intricate interactions within the gut microecology and the host immune system. This perspective provides a shift thinking towards a ‘microbiome clinician’ (Porcari et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026.) and theoretical foundation for treating UPIIs by targeting and reconstructing the gut microbiota. In this study, over 60.0% of patients responded to WMT, providing clinical support for the hypothesis that dysbiosis plays a significant role in infection.

Multivariate analysis, optimised for generalisability via the relatively lower AIC model, identified AEs related to WMT, WHO performance status scores, TASS before WMT as significant risk factors for reduced efficacy at one month. As mentioned in the methodology, WHO performance status score was used to evaluate the physical functions, and TASS assessed clinical symptoms. These findings implied that if WMT is available, earlier WMT may be beneficial, even when symptoms were not yet severe. Besides, WMT‐related AEs may reflect the need for safer microbiota transplantation protocols. Our previous study has indicated that WMT improves the safety by removing certain bacterial fragments, pro‐inflammatory metabolites and toxicity molecules, constituting a key technical advancement over crude FMT (Zhang et al. 2020). In the present study, the incidence of WMT‐related AEs during the follow‐up period was only 8.3%. Consistent with the 20‐year FMT‐related AEs worldwide, the most common of WMT‐related AEs in this study is diarrhoea (Marcella et al. 2021). All WMT‐related AEs were mild or moderate. The safety of microbiota transplantation is determined not only by the microbiota solution itself but also by the delivery route. For critically ill patients with multiple comorbidities, intestinal dyskinesia or small bowel obstruction, colonic transendoscopic enteral tubing following bowel preparation with enema is the preferred route (Yu et al. 2026).

AIC was used to control model variable selection and enhance generalisation ability. Interestingly, the AIC value decreased when the terms ‘antibiotic use during WMT’ and ‘types of antibiotic use before WMT’ were added to the proposed model, but increased when ‘antibiotic use within two‐weeks after WMT’ and ‘ICU admission’ were included. Therefore, despite the lack of statistical significance for the two antibiotic‐related variables in the final multivariate model, they were retained based on AIC optimisation and clinical relevance (Table S3). The inclusion of these antibiotic‐related factors warrants further discussion. Several reasons may explain their retention despite lacking statistical significance. First, the limited sample size may have led to false‐negative results. Second, doctors and patients' uncertainty about WMT efficacy may prompt concomitant antibiotic use (Porcari et al. 2025). Third, early antibiotic exposure before and after FMT may disrupt microbial engraftment (Singh et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2019) and compromise treatment effectiveness, as supported by studies linking antibiotic use to FMT failure (Wang et al. 2019; Brunse et al. 2020). Considering AIC optimisation and clinical relevance, the nomogram offers a practical tool for identifying patients likely to benefit from WMT.

It is also noteworthy that age, a factor frequently identified as crucial in FMT outcomes (Baunwall et al. 2023; Parker et al. 2022), did not emerge as a significant predictor in our cohort (Table S2). This may reflect the specific characteristics of our population, where over 50% were critically ill with poor functional status, and most had received multiple antibiotics before WMT. In such severely ill patients, the impact of age may be overshadowed by acute factors like disease severity and antibiotic exposure. Additionally, the limited sample size may have reduced power to detect modest age‐related effects.

The present study exhibits several limitations. Firstly, the absence of a control group impedes the establishment of reliable causal inferences regarding the treatment effects. Secondly, there was a dearth of microbiota analysis before and after WMT, regarding the donor‐recipient relationship and outcome, which may limit the depth of mechanism exploration. Thirdly, as noted above, despite the use of AIC to improve model generalisability, the nomogram lacked external validation due to the limited sample size. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, this national multi‐centre, real‐world study provides the systematic evidence supporting the use of WMT as an effective and safe rescue therapy for patients with refractory UPIIs. The favourable clinical outcomes of this study may encourage us to extend the WMT indications to UPIIs. Additionally, this study provides new clinical evidence for the treatment of UPIIs, which may be the direction of future developments in complex infection treatment and hospital infection control.
