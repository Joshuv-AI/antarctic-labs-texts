SERSome-based metabolic profiling of aqueous humor for cataract subtyping with molecule-level interpretability
Authors: Li Ning, Xinyuan Bi, Yepei Qin, Yinghong Ji, Jian Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cataract is among the leading cause of vision impairment worldwide and it is essential to uncover molecule-level characteristics of different cataract subtypes for personalized treatment. Compared to existing diagnostic techniques, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) shows advantages in sensitive molecular fingerprinting, tractability and compactness, making it highly suitable for clinical adoption. To further address the current lack of SERS-based techniques for cataract subtyping, this work presented an integrated methodology for robust and interpretable metabolic profiling of aqueous humor. A two-step sample pretreatment protocol has been therein developed to efficiently extract metabolites while ensuring compatibility with subsequent SERS detection. SERSome strategy was employed to enable rapid and robust metabolic profiling. Molecule-level SERSome interpretation was achieved by matching its positive intra-correlation network with metabolites’ SERS barcode. Multiple analytical methods including statistical comparison, interpretable machine learning, and association analysis have been leveraged, evidencing key metabolite biomarkers such as ergothioneine, hypoxanthine and uric acid associated with cataract subtyping and diabetic retinopathy. In a word, this study has not only established a systematic methodology for metabolic analysis of aqueous humor but also provided a usable tool for both fundamental research and clinical applications in different diseases with high rapidness, cost efficiency and scalability.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04789-1.

Introduction

Cataract is among the leading causes of global visual impairment and the most prominent cause of blindness. According to the statistics provided by VISION 2020, a joint global initiative for the elimination of avoidable blindness between World Health Organization and the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness, cataract was estimated to account for about 15.2 million blind cases and 78.8 million cases of moderate and severe vision impairment [1, 2]. Beyond visual impairment, cataracts may be also related with lower rates of employment, higher rates of depression and anxiety [3], and some other diseases such as dementia [4]. The prevalence of cataract is expected to continue to rise especially as the global aging [5], putting a higher demand on effective intervention for prevention, treatment and rehabilitation.

The causes of cataracts can be diverse [6]. For instance, with advancing age, proteins in the lens may gradually denature and aggregate, leading to a decrease in lens transparency [7]. Cataracts could also result from conditions such as diabetes or high myopia [8]. Different subtypes of cataracts may involve distinct pathophysiological states, which can lead to varied treatment responses and long-term prognoses among patients receiving the same therapeutic intervention [9]. Currently, surgery is among the most effective clinical interventions for cataract [10], but this approach cannot address the underlying disease biological mechanisms for personalized prevention and treatment, and carries inherent risks such as infection, retinal detachment [11], and posterior capsule opacification [12], which may lead to poor post-operation visual recovery [13]. Conventional ophthalmic examination techniques, such as ultrasound biomicroscopy [14] and optical coherence tomography [15], can reveal the structural relationship between cataracts and ocular lesions, yet they also fall short of offering deeper insights into molecular-level pathological changes. Therefore, advancing molecular-level research on the pathogenesis of cataracts is of great significance for developing new prevention and treatment strategies, and potentially even identifying novel druggable targets.

Aqueous humor, the biofluid that directly bathes the lens, may contain a wealth of molecular-level information related to cataracts [16]. Among various molecular classes, metabolites are particularly sensitive indicators of physiological and pathological states [17]. Studies employing liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) or nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR) have identified potential cataract-associated metabolic alterations, including dysregulated oxidative stress-related markers (e.g., methyltetrahydrofolic acid, taurine, niacinamide, xanthine and uric acid) [18] and perturbed amino acid metabolism pathways (e.g., lysine and arginine) [19]. However, the extremely small volume of aqueous humor samples (on the microliter scale), the inability of repeated sampling and the low concentration of many metabolites have posed significant challenges [17]. Additionally, since aqueous humor sampling often occurs during surgery, the large size of the instruments and the relatively complex sample pretreatment make both LC-MS and NMR techniques difficult to implement in such settings. The high cost of equipment and testing also hinders their widespread adoption.

Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) is a molecular fingerprinting detection technique [20] that utilizes plasmonic nanoparticles to greatly enhance the Raman scattering signals of molecules under laser excitation [21]. It is even capable of achieving single-molecule detection sensitivity within the electromagnetic hotspots [22–24]. Coupled with efficient and rapid sample pretreatment methods, SERS-based techniques have been successfully applied to metabolic detection in human serum [25, 26], urine [27, 28], cells [29] and even extremely limited samples such as single cells [30, 31], making it a highly promising tool for aqueous humor analysis as well [32]. However, the current application of SERS for detecting metabolites in aqueous humor remains limited to a small number of specific molecules (such as glucose [33]), with a notable lack of research on other metabolites, especially those at low abundance. In fact, although some studies have attempted to identify metabolites based on specific characteristic peaks, relying solely on a single peak is insufficient for reliable metabolite identification [34]. Furthermore, the absence of systematic design and optimization of sample pretreatment protocols has led to overlapping signals from various molecular species [35]. These signals are further compromised by matrix effects, resulting in weak overall intensity and the inability to accurately distinguish metabolic signatures from other molecular classes [36]. These challenges significantly hinder the effective use of SERS in metabolic profiling and biomarker identification in aqueous humor.

To address the aforementioned challenges and obtain more reliable metabolic insights, this study developed an integrated SERS-based methodology for aqueous humor metabolic analysis, encompassing rapid and efficient metabolite extraction to reduce matrix effects and enhance detection sensitivity. Particularly, SERSome measurement strategy was applied for metabolic profiling of complex biosamples. SERSome, i.e., a spectral set (typically containing 200 spectra) acquired for each sample via pointwise mapping, presented statistical robustness, comprising any fluctuated and rare signals generated by diverse molecules and different electromagnetic hotspots. By incorporating intra-correlation analysis of SERSomes, the approach offered more credible evidence for metabolite identification based on coordinated spectral variations. Furthermore, machine learning method (i.e., least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-logistic regression) was applied to identify differential metabolite-related vibrational signatures, revealing biomarkers such as ergothioneine and hypoxanthine and related pathways across different cataract subtypes including diabetic cataract (DC), age-related cataract (ARC) and cataract with high myopia (HMC). Additionally, association analysis was conducted to excavate metabolic variations related to diabetic retinopathy (DR). This systematic workflow established a complete suite of sample pretreatment, detection, and analytical techniques characterized by rapidity, ease of use, and cost efficiency. It demonstrated strong potential for intraoperative detection and broad clinical adoption, thereby supporting further research into cataract mechanisms and paving the way for personalized diagnosis and treatment.

Experimental sections and methods

Materials and instrumentations

Trisodium citrate (TSC, ≥ 98%), ergothioneine (99.145%, HPLC), guanine (99.888%, HPLC) was purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Silver nitrate (AgNO3, 99.95%) was bought from Sinopharm (Shanghai, China). Tyramine (99.9%, GC), orotic acid (100%, titration), uric acid (100%, HPLC) and adenine (100%, HPLC) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Xanthine (99.90%) and hypoxanthine (99.50%) were bought from Beijing China Inspection Air Navigation Marking Technology (Beijing, China) and Cys-Gly (99.79%) was from Sinco Pharmachem (Shenzhen, China). All chemicals were used without further purification. Ultrapure water was used in all experiments.

The extinction spectrum of the nanoparticles (NPs) was collected by a UV1900 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Aucybest, Shanghai, China) and the NP morphology was characterized using a Talos F200X transmission electron microscope. All the Raman spectra were collected by on a confocal Raman system (ML-300 Raman Molecular Analyzer, Shanghai MoleTech Ltd) equipped with a 632.8 nm laser (power: 20 mW).

Synthesis of SERS NPs

The citrate-reduced silver (Ag) NPs were synthesized according to Lee and Meisel’s method [37] with minor modifications [38]. Briefly, 18.4 mg AgNO3 was dissolved in 150 mL ultrapure water and heated to boiling under stirring. Then, 4 mL of TSC solution (10.1 mg/mL) was added dropwise. The mixture was further kept boiling for 1 h without exposure to light and then cooled to room temperature under vigorous stirring. The product was stored in 4 ℃ refrigerator and protected from light exposure before being used.

Collection of aqueous humor samples

A total of 165 aqueous humor samples were collected from distinctive participants who underwent phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation at the Eye & ENT Hospital of Fudan University, including DC patients (69 cases), ARC patients (74 cases) and HMC patients (22 cases). The sample size was determined based on the actual availability of eligible clinical samples during the recruitment period. Herein, the DC group was further classified by whether the patient was also diagnosed with DR, thus comprising of 20 DC cases with DR, 47 DC cases without DR and 2 DC cases left with unknown DR conditions. The two DC cases with unknown DR condition were retained in analyses not involving DR stratification, but were excluded from DR-related subgroup comparisons to avoid potential bias from misclassification. Demographic information including sex and age was recorded for this study. Specifically, cases with a history of ocular trauma or prior eye surgery, a younger age less than 30 years old, presence of other ocular pathologies (including but not limited to macular hole, uveitis, glaucoma, retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage) or systemic complications that contraindicated cataract surgery were excluded from this study. Aqueous humor samples were collected between the creation of surgical incision and the injection of ophthalmic viscoelastic device using a 1 mL sterile syringe which was DNase, RNase and protease-free. All the aqueous humor samples were transferred to dry ice immediately after being collected and then stored in −80 ℃ without the addition of any other chemicals. The aqueous humor samples were thawed under 4 ℃ right before being measured. Repetitive freezing and thawing were strictly avoided.

Sample pretreatment and SERSome measurement

The 3 kDa-cutoff filter (Millipore, Amicon®Ultra-0.5) was used to centrifugate the original or diluted aqueous humor sample at 14,000 rcf for 10 min. Herein, different dilution factors (i.e., 2 times, 4 times and 8 times) have been used as indicated. A total of 10 µL of the sample extraction was mixed with 10 µL Ag NPs which were concentrated by three times in advance. The mixture was mixed by pipetting several times, followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min to allow more metabolite molecules to be adsorbed on the NPs. Ten µL incubated mixture was pipetted into a quartz capillary (inner diameter: 1 mm, outer diameter: 2 mm, purity: ≥ 99.9%) and placed on the sample stage of the portable confocal Raman spectrometer (ML-300, size: 34.5 × 34.5 × 20.7 cm for length × width × height). Raman measurements were performed using 1 s of acquisition time per spectrum via a 10× objective lens. The spectra were obtained within the wavenumber range from 400 to 1800 cm⁻¹. During the measurement process, the sample stage moved along the central longitudinal axis of the capillary at a fixed speed which enabled the acquisition of 200 spectra from different regions within each sample. The spectral set acquired for each sample is named as SERSome. Herein, the spectral number, i.e., 200, was determined according to the Pearson’s correlation coefficient as described in the Methods section “Spectral Analysis”. Thereafter, the total acquisition time for each sample was less than 4 min. The total number of spectra obtained in this work was 33,000 (= 165 samples × 200 spectra).

Pathway enrichment analysis

Intra-correlation analysis was performed using the subsampled spectra of all samples with only the highly positive-correlated spectral bands (Pearson’s correlation coefficient > 0.9) considered to evidence the barcodes of the contributing metabolites. Then, potential metabolic pathways were obtained by using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 based these contributing metabolites. Parallel coordinates plot was leveraged to indicate the affiliation of metabolites to the corresponding pathway where the node size indicated the significance (reflected by the p-value) of the pathway.

Validation of the detection of aqueous humor metabolites

The SERS detectability of metabolites was validated by laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry using Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometer (SolariX 7.0T, Bruker) equipped with MALDI ion source (355 nm, Azura Laser AG, Berlin, Germany). In brief, the mixture of pretreated aqueous humor and SERS NPs were centrifuged after incubation. The sediments, i.e., the NPs with metabolites adsorbed, were collected and redispersed in a small amount of water. This concentrated suspension was dropped on the mass plate for dry and then measured for the mass features of metabolites.

Results

This study aims to establish a methodology for metabolic detection and analysis of aqueous humor based on surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS). From January 2024 to January 2025, a total of 165 aqueous humor samples were collected from the Eye & ENT Hospital of Fudan University, including 69 DC cases, 74 ARC cases, and 22 HMC cases. In the DC group, 20 cases were diagnosed with DR, 47 showed no signs of DR, and 2 had unknown status due to the blurred fundus (Fig. 1). All samples were acquired during surgery, and both sample collection and disease diagnosis were performed by an experienced ophthalmologist (Y. J). Relevant demographic information (sex and age) and clinical indicators (axial length and fasting blood glucose (FBG)), were also recorded (Table 1). Except for indicators known to be directly associated with specific cataract subtypes such as age (generally higher in ARC), axial length (higher in HMC) and FBG (higher in DC), no significant differences were observed in other characteristics across the groups, allowing more reliable analysis. The study primarily involved the development of a pretreatment protocol for aqueous humor samples to efficiently extract metabolites and to be compatible with SERS measurements. By employing SERSome strategy to enhance the comprehensiveness and robustness of metabolic profiling, spectral features were analyzed with deeper understanding to identify potential metabolite biomarkers associated with different cataract subtypes and the presence of DR (Fig. 1).

For SERS detection, we employed citrate-reduced Ag NPs, which exhibited an absorbance peak at 409 nm (Fig. 2A) and a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 50 nm (polydispersity index: 0.284). Citrate-reduced Ag NPs presented a quasi-spherical shape according to the transmission electron microscopic image (Fig. S1) and have been proven high detection sensitivity of a wide range of human metabolites as reported in our previous works [25, 39, 40]. Apart from the stability during storage and after mixing with biosamples [41] (Fig. S2), citrate-reduced Ag NPs can be produced on a large scale (> 100 mL per batch) to support a large number of tests, given that each SERS measurement required only 10 µL of NPs to mix with 10 µL of biofluid. This scalability could promise broad adoption of the methodology. It is also worth noting that this study utilized a self-developed Raman system equipped with a confocal Raman spectrometer and a capillary-based sample holder (Fig. 2B). The instrument is compact, low-cost, and capable of rapid measurements, making it suitable for intraoperative use, point-of-care testing and decentralized community-level screening.

To achieve high sensitivity in SERS-based detection of metabolites, it is essential to develop an effective pretreatment protocol for aqueous humor, which was actually an aspect often overlooked in many SERS studies involving biofluids [34, 35]. Without proper pretreatment, the detected signals would be dominated by proteins, which could form a protein corona on the NP surface and block small metabolites from entering the electromagnetic hotspots [25, 42, 43]. Conventional methods such as organic solvent-based protein precipitation and solid-phase extraction may yield incomplete protein removal, thereby still hindering metabolite detection [44]. Moreover, the organic solvents themselves may produce strong Raman signals that could alias with those of metabolites, particularly those with weak signals or similar vibrational modes [39]. To address these challenges and adapt the pretreatment to noble metal NP-based SERS detection, we implemented an ultrafiltration strategy using a 3 kDa molecular weight cut-off membrane as proposed in our previous works [39, 40]. This approach effectively removed large molecules and significantly enhanced the detection sensitivity for small metabolites without introducing any interfering signals (Fig. S3). In addition, due to the potential clogging of membrane pores by abundant proteins [45], appropriate sample dilution was introduced to reduce matrix effects and improve the enrichment efficiency of small molecules [38]. Specifically, moderate dilution helped mitigate competitivity from highly abundant metabolites, allowing a broader range of molecules to be detected and thus better deciphering the different molecular compositions across samples (Fig. S4), while excessive dilution was avoided to prevent a loss of sensitivity due to overly reduced metabolite concentrations [46] (Fig. 2C). Through systematic optimization, we established a pretreatment procedure involving a two-fold dilution of aqueous humor samples followed by 3 kDa-cutoff ultrafiltration. The resulting metabolite extraction, when mixed with Ag NPs, yielded the richest SERS information, including the highest number of characteristic peaks and the strongest median signal intensity (Fig. 2C). The pretreatment method has also demonstrated high technical repeatability among independent operations (Fig. S5).

For metabolic profiling, we employed a spectral set measurement approach, namely SERSome, to profile the molecular composition of a complex sample with higher robustness and comprehensiveness (Fig. 2D). Compared to acquiring only a few spectra per sample, this strategy reduced bias caused by the heterogeneous distribution of molecules and electromagnetic hotspots throughout the detection volume [25, 47]. To determine the number of spectra adequate for robust profiling, we evaluated the consistency between two randomly selected spectral subsets comprised of different number spectra for each sample based on Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) [25, 29]. The results indicated that collecting 200 spectra per sample ensured a high robustness as PCC reached convergence (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, SERSome strategy was attempted to preserve molecular information specific to each probe volume in comparison of simply applying mean spectra for analysis, since variations in signals across different probe volumes could reflect the combined fingerprint contributions of diverse molecular species. This probe volume-specific spectral representation offered a more detailed foundation for identifying metabolic biomarkers, which will be elaborated later.

After acquiring the SERSomes of all aqueous humor samples, similar spectral shapes were found despite of variations in relative peak intensities which may evidence the underlying subtype-related dysregulated metabolism (Fig. S6). To facilitate the analysis, the whole SERS spectrum was divided into 13 distinct fingerprint regions based on primary spectral features (Fig. 3A). Thereafter, we first applied Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare the intensity distribution at each wavenumber among ARC, DC and HMC groups. The results revealed that significantly varied wavenumbers often formed continuous bands, a pattern consistent with the nature of Raman spectra where signals are not discrete but exhibit intrinsic peak width (Fig. 3B-C). Local minima in p-values (corresponding to maxima after -lg(p-value) transformation) frequently occurred at peak centers or shoulder positions, suggesting potential dominant vibrational modes of contributing metabolites. Typical differential peaks are displayed in Fig. 3D. By referring literatures on the SERS fingerprints of human metabolites, the significantly varied SERS bands represented by 721 and 1350 cm− 1 were generally regarded to be contributed by the purine ring skeleton vibration [48, 49], while 1022 and 1585 cm− 1 have been reported to relate with benzene ring stretching [50]. Spectral band at 486 cm− 1 could be attributed to the N-C-S bending mode and C-S stretching as mentioned for some molecules containing thione group and heterocyclic moiety [51] and 1218 cm− 1 could be generated by C-C stretching [50]. Machine learning combined with interpretable method, i.e., SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), was employed to further screen spectral signatures important for cataract subtyping. A logistic regression classifier achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.94 in distinguishing DC from ARC, and 0.77 for DC versus HMC in the test set (Fig. 3E). The classification accuracy in the test set were 0.86 and 0.84, respectively (see Methods for detailed machine learning and dataset assignment methods). Spectral bands showing the highest absolute SHAP values were aligned well with the wavenumbers identified by conventional methods (Fig. 3F-G). This consistency underscored the potential of these spectral signatures to evidence metabolite biomarkers for the distinguishing different cataract subtypes.

However, relying solely on individual spectral peaks makes it difficult to infer specific molecular species. To address this limitation, we leveraged SERSome intra-correlation analysis that established positive correlation networks among peaks to match the “spectral barcode” of metabolites, thereby improving the identification of potential key metabolites contributing to the observed differential peaks. In detail, owing to the SERSome-based representation of the molecular vibrational information, the spectral variations across different probe volumes can reflect the stochastic distribution of molecules and localized plasmonic hotspots. Therefore, for a certain kind of molecule under certain concentration and background conditions, variations in effective molecule count and hotspot intensity across probe volumes would lead to strong positive correlations among its characteristic Raman peaks. In this approach, groups of positively correlated bands can indicate the contributing molecule by matching such patterns to spectral barcodes of known metabolites [52, 53]. As shown in Fig. 4A, positively correlated band groups include (i) band No. 1, 9 and 12 (corresponding to 400–580, 1160–1260, and 1520–1620 cm− 1), (ii) band No. 9, 11 and 12 (corresponding to 1160–1260, 1400–1520, and 1520–1620 cm− 1), (iii) band No. 2, 5 and 8 (corresponding to 580–700, 830–940, and 1070–1160 cm− 1), (iv) band No. 2 and 13 (corresponding to 580–700, and 1620–1800 cm− 1), and (v) band No. 3 and 10 (corresponding to 700–780, and 1260–1400 cm− 1), among the others. Through molecular matching process, biomarkers such as orotic acid, ergothioneine, tyramine, hypoxanthine, adenine and uric acid have been identified with these positively correlated band groups (Fig. 4B). Evidence from spectral comparison and laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry further demonstrated that these metabolites were successfully detected by SERS (Fig. S7). Some characteristic peaks of these molecules have also been found significantly differentiated across cataract subtypes by both traditional statistical methods and machine learning. These dysregulated metabolites offered crucial insights into the underlying mechanisms of lens opacification. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis further revealed purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism and tyrosine metabolism related to cataractogenesis (Fig. 4C).

Herein, ergothioneine can be well inferred from band group (i) and (ii) with characteristic peak positioned at 485 cm− 1 for N-C-S bending mixed with C-S stretching belonging to band No. 1, 1215 cm− 1 for C-C stretching and C-N stretching belonging to band No. 9, 1445 cm− 1 and 1590 cm− 1 for C = C stretching and C = N stretching belonging to band No. 11 and 12 respectively [54]. Absorbed via specific OCTN1 transporters, ergothioneine provides antioxidative cytoprotection in neurodegenerative diseases [55], metabolic disorders [56] and ocular diseases [57], primarily by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibiting lipid peroxidation and modulating mitochondrial function [58]. The observed variation in ergothioneine among different subtypes underscores the heterogeneous nature of oxidative stress in lens opacification. This discrepancy suggests that the lens’s antioxidant defense system is compromised to varying degrees across subtypes, implying that oxidative triggers play a disproportionately dominant role in the development of certain cataract forms compared to others.

Similarly, the levels of adenine, hypoxanthine and xanthine which are essential intermediates in purine metabolism exhibited distinctive profiles across different subtypes, as characterized by ring breathing vibration related to band No. 2 and 3, ring deformation related to band No. 8, and ring skeletal vibration related to band No. 10 [59, 60]. Adenine serves as a vital precursor for ATP and NAD synthesis [61], while hypoxanthine accumulates under hypoxic conditions or depleted ATP levels [62]. Dysregulation of purine metabolism is associated with gout [63], energy metabolism impairments in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [64] and certain chronic kidney diseases [65], often manifesting as ATP depletion, increased ROS generation, and cellular dysfunction. Our findings suggest that the magnitude of purine dysregulation varies significantly among cataract subtypes, reflecting divergent levels of metabolic stress and energy impairment. In more metabolically-driven subtypes, the hyperglycemic or hyperosmotic environment triggers mitochondrial dysfunction [66] and activates the polyol pathway, which depletes NADPH [67] and shifts nucleotide metabolism toward a pro-oxidant state.

Notably, uric acid, a key downstream product of purine metabolism pathway, was further indicated by 640 cm− 1 for skeletal ring deformation within band No. 2, 890 cm− 1 for N-H bending within band No. 5 and 1135 cm− 1 for C-N vibration within band No. 8 [68]. In the context of cataractogenesis, uric acid exhibits a complex dual role that while it acts as a potent antioxidant at physiological levels [69], its overproduction often driven by the upregulation of xanthine oxidase can paradoxically promote lens opacification [70]. High levels of uric acid in the aqueous humor can stimulate the production of inflammatory cytokines [71] and further amplify the generation of superoxide radicals during its synthesis from hypoxanthine [72]. This subtype-specific elevation of uric acid may thus serve as both a marker of severe oxidative insult and a secondary driver of LECs’ apoptosis and protein cross-linking.

Other molecules including tyramine (tyrosine metabolism), orotic acid (pyrimidine metabolism), cys-gly and guanine were also deduced based on the positively correlated band groups (Fig. 4B-C). Although these molecules have not yet been directly linked to cataracts in existing literature, they have been implicated in other conditions such as ocular migraine [73], hypertension [74], and retinal detachment [75], which may worth further investigations for their pathological mechanisms in cataract and its subtypes.

Therefore, our method, facilitating molecule-level assignment of the aqueous humor SERSome signatures, could not only aid in identifying potential metabolite biomarkers and metabolic pathways, but also provide individualized molecular-level metabolic profiles for future research into precise prognosis and personalized treatment strategies. However, it should be noted that in complex mixtures such as aqueous humor, peaks from different molecules may overlap, causing weaker correlations (generated by low-abundance molecules or low-intensity peaks) masked by dominant signals. In this work, we have tentatively applied a high threshold (PCC > 0.9) for defining positive correlation networks, thus primarily focusing on the major characteristic peaks of metabolites. This allowed efficient identification of the most prominent potential contributing molecules, while more biomarkers should be further studied in future refined analysis.

Diabetic cataract has become a topic of increasing research interest in recent years. The presence or absence of diabetic retinopathy (DR) may influence the pathological progression of cataract and relate with the therapeutic and surgical strategy [76, 77]. Therefore, understanding DR-related molecule-level pathology would greatly benefit the prognosis and treatment of cataract. In this regard, we further investigated the spectral features associated with the presence of DR. Using machine learning method we identified wavenumbers important to classifying DC patients with and without DR based on SHAP values. These wavenumbers belong to band No. 1, 2, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12 (Fig. 5A), which have been identified to be primarily contributed by ergothioneine, uric acid and xanthine (Fig. 4B). As the limited case number of DC group alone, these wavenumbers could only explain for a part of the metabolic differentiation related with DR with the AUC of only 67% in the test group (accuracy in the test group = 0.57) (Fig. 5B). Therefore, to provide more reliable evidence, association analysis was further leveraged to unveil the variation in spectral signatures (Fig. 5C). Increased association may evidence the synergic regulation of two metabolic species or more profound spectral contribution of a certain kind of metabolite molecule in DR cases. For example, an increased association between band No. 2 and 10 may evidence a higher level of uric acid in DR, which may serve as a biomarker to promote DR severity probably due to its role in microvascular damage in retinal vessels according to literatures [78, 79]. While decreased associations could be attributed by the raised metabolite conversion in DR, for example, the conversion of 615 and 1015 cm− 1 related with purine metabolism, which has been previously found useful as an indicator of DR progression [80]. Though it should be admitted that conjectures based on spectral features require other analytical methods and biological experiments to solidify their pathological roles (not performed in this paper due to the focus of our work), our method was able to find DR-related metabolic variations, which showed high potential in evidencing potential biomarkers for both clinical and laboratorial use.

Discussion

In this work, we achieved SERSome-based metabolic profiling and molecule-level interpretation for aqueous humor, revealing cataract-related metabolic dysregulations. Herein, a two-step sample pretreatment protocol comprised of dilution and ultrafiltration was particularly established and optimized for SERS detection, enabling efficient reduction of matrix effects, improved extraction of metabolites, and introducing no interfering signals. It is worth mentioning that this pretreatment protocol is relatively simpler and more compatible with SERS detection system than conventional organic solvent-involved methods. SERSome detection strategy was applied for robust profiling of complex biosamples, requiring only ten microliters of metabolite extraction and less than 4 min of measurement. And owing to its probe volume-level representation of molecular fingerprinting information, specific metabolite molecules most probably contributing to the SERS metabolic profiles of aqueous humor have been identified by performing SERSome intra-correlation analysis and matching the positive correlation network with SERS barcodes of metabolites. Thereafter, multi-dimensional analytical approaches including traditional statistical methods, machine learning, and association analysis were leveraged, enabling the screening of metabolite biomarkers. Ergothioneine, hypoxanthine, and uric acid as well as purine metabolism pathways have been found associated with different cataract subtypes and DR, consistent to previous literatures. Other findings not yet reported in previous works including those related to pyrimidine metabolism and tyrosine metabolism, could offer potential clues and inspiration for further mechanistic studies. It is worth emphasizing that, molecule-level interpretation of SERSome data was achieved by matching the positive intra-correlation network of SERSomes with metabolites’ SERS barcode. This facilitated the identification of potential biomarkers which cannot be easily achieved by simply correlating the spectral features with vibrational modes [81]. Furthermore, the NPs, consumables, and equipment used in this method were cost-effective, and the procedure is relatively simple and rapid in comparison with conventional techniques. The potential of instrumentation miniaturization and process automerization further made the whole methodology highly suitable for clinical use. Future studies will continue to optimize the protocols and particularly, the turnaround time, making SERS-based metabolic detection methodology usable for intraoperative examination, point-of-care testing, and large-scale screening applications. However, this study should be considered exploratory in nature particularly considering the relatively small HMC group size. Therefore, we anticipate a larger and more balanced cohort to be recruited in the future for more validated pathological investigations. More comprehensive clinical information is also desired in future studies to facilitate deeper understanding of cataract pathology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a comprehensive SERS-based methodology for robust and molecule-level interpretable metabolic profiling of aqueous humor to investigate cataract subtypes and the metabolic crosstalk in DC patients with concurrent DR. Our work not only provided a practical and systematic methodology for aqueous humor-based cataract research and clinical applications, but may also advance the understanding of metabolic mechanisms underlying cataractogenesis and inform the development of detection and analytical strategies for other types of biological samples.
