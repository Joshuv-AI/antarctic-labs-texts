Temporal transcriptomic dynamics in rat spinal cord after tibial fracture unveil crosstalk between spinal cord and bone
Authors: Jin Deng, Shen Wang, Shaoxun Yuan, Qingguo Lu, Xinyi Gu, Chen Huang, Shuhang Guo, Dehong Yu, Xiaofeng Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury induces progressive bone loss and increases fracture susceptibility. While the neurological consequences of skeletal trauma remain poorly characterized, underlying molecular mechanisms require systematic investigation. To systematically characterize the temporal dynamics of spinal cord responses, L4–L5 spinal segments were harvested from Sprague–Dawley rats at 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28 days post-fracture for RNA sequencing (Illumina platform). Integrated bioinformatics analyses revealed temporally stratified differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Quantitative real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was used to validate the expression of key genes. Transcriptomic changes peaked at 3 days and 7 days post-fracture. By day 14, transcriptomic signatures shifted toward synaptic plasticity-related pathways. DEGs were substantially reduced by day 28. Key genes (PCP4 and RICTOR) identified by bioinformatics were validated via qRT-PCR, confirming their temporal expression patterns. This study elucidates temporal dynamics of spinal cord transcriptomic remodeling post-fracture, establishing a foundation for understanding neuro-osseous cross-talk during bone healing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17561-6.Keywords: Transcriptomic analysis, Bone, Fracture, Spinal cordSubject terms: RNA sequencing, Trauma, Bone, Spinal cord

Introduction

Fracture, defined as a structural discontinuity in mineralized bone tissue, is a common injury in clinical practice. The spinal cord plays a crucial role in regulating bone metabolism. Spinal cord injury (SCI) induces distinct neurogenic osteopenia characterized by sublesional skeletal fragility, particularly in the proximal tibia and distal femurs1. Unlike primary osteoporosis, post-SCI bone resorption demonstrates neuroanatomical specificity, with pronounced sublesional demineralization but minimal bone loss in supralesional regions2–4.

SCI-induced myelopathy induces region-specific osteopathology through neurotrophic dysregulation. Mechanical disuse following SCI provokes hind-limb unloading-mediated osteocyte apoptosis, which stimulates receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL) production, driving cortical and trabecular deterioration5. This mechanical unloading creates catabolic dominance in bone remodeling through osteoclast-mediated hyperresorption and osteoblastic suppression6. Consistent with these structural alterations, serum and urinary biomarkers, including deoxypyridinoline (DPD), N-telopeptide (NTX), C-telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-I), and hydroxyproline (HYP), show progressive elevation post-SCI7–10.

The spinal cord maintains bidirectional neuro-osseous communication via peripheral neural circuits. Bone harbors two distinct neural populations: autonomic efferents and somatosensory afferents. Sympathetic fibers originating from thoracolumbar sympathetic nuclei regulate vascular tone and hematopoietic niches11,12, while somatic afferents densely innervate periosteal and endosteal surfaces, transducing mechanical stimuli. Sympathetic denervation uncouples bone turnover, diminishing anabolic activity while exacerbating catabolic processes13,14. Notably, non-weight-bearing mandibles in sympathectomized animals exhibit similar osteopenia15, confirming that sympathetic regulation extends beyond load-bearing bones. Sensory nerves are also crucially involved in bone metabolism. Sensory neurotransmission equally modulates skeletal homeostasis, as evidenced by prostaglandin E2 receptor 4 knockout models demonstrating impaired osteogenesis16. These findings establish SCI as a polyneuropathic disorder affecting bone through dual neural pathways.

While existing studies focus on osseous repair mechanisms and neuroregulatory influences, the retrograde neuroplastic responses to skeletal injury remain poorly understood. This investigation employs longitudinal transcriptomics to identify genes responsible for spinal cord adaptations post-fracture, aiming to elucidate neuro-osseous interactions.

Methods

Animal models and tissue collection

Male Sprague Dawley rats (n = 30; age 6–8 weeks; weight 200–320 g; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (Protocol #2020PHC015 and #2024PHE029) and conducted by NIH guidelines. Rats were randomly allocated into five time-defined cohorts (n = 6/group): baseline control (0d) and post-fracture intervals at 3, 7, 14, and 28 days. The 0-day cohort served as surgical controls, with tissue harvest immediately post-osteotomy before biological response initiation.

Animals underwent anesthesia induction via inhalation of 2.5% isoflurane/oxygen (1.5 L/min flow rate) followed by maintenance at 1.5-2%. Under aseptic technique, a 2 cm longitudinal incision was made from the tibial tuberosity to expose the diaphysis. A hole was drilled at the tibial tuberosity using a dental drill, and an intramedullary needle (0.8 mm×1.5 cm) was inserted into the bone marrow cavity. Standardized closed fractures were induced 5 mm distal to the tuberosity using a three-point bending device. Care was taken not to clip the intramedullary needle, and the tissue was sutured layer by layer under sterile conditions. CO2 anesthesia was used to euthanize the rats on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28 after fracture. L4-L5 spinal segments ipsilateral to tibial fractures were isolated in our rodent model. The rat sciatic nerve comprises L3-L6 spinal nerves, with L4-L5 constituting its core components (98–99% neuronal somata localized in corresponding dorsal root ganglia (DRGs))17.

RNA isolation and preparation of sequencing libraries

The spinal cords were frozen in liquid nitrogen within 3 min after being harvested (n = 3/group). Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Implen, Westlake Village, CA, United States) was used to determine the concentration and purity of the isolated RNA. RNA integrity was assessed via RNA Integrity Number (RIN) measurement using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. All the samples were stored at -80 °C. For each sample, 3 µg of RNA was used for library preparation. A Ribo-zero™ rRNA Removal Kit (Epicenter, Madison, WI, United States) was used to remove ribosomal RNA. Sequencing libraries were generated with the NEBNext Ultra™ Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, United States). To ensure data quality, sample homogeneity was confirmed via principal component analysis (PCA) and fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM). All procedures followed established guidelines for RNA-seq quality control18.

mRNA sequencing

The Illumina platform (Beijing, China) was used for sequencing at Novogene. The libraries were sequenced according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Differentially expressed genes between samples were identified using the CuffDiff program within the CuffLinks package. An adjusted p-value < 0.05 was used as the cutoff criterion.

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed on the DEGs using the clusterProfiler R package to reduce gene length bias. DEGs with corrected P values less than 0.05 were considered enriched. ClusterProfiler v.4.2.2, Rn.eg.db v.3.14.0, and ggplot2 v.3.4.0 were used in R for KEGG enrichment analysis and image plotting19–21.

Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA)

The QIAGEN Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) bioinformatics tool (https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/) was used to analyze the specific changes in the spinal cord at different time points (p values < 0.05 were used as the cutoff criterion). In IPA, the Z score is used to predict pathway activation and inhibition. The higher the Z score is, the more pathways are promoted. The lower the Z score is, the more the pathway is suppressed.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from the spinal cord using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ESscience Biotech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (n = 3/group). Reverse transcription was performed with 5X All-In-One RT MasterMix (Abm, Vancouver, Canada) to generate cDNA. Quantitative PCR amplification was conducted on a Bio-Rad iQ™5 system (Bio-Rad, Mississauga, Canada). All primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were executed in GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SEM. Inter-group comparisons employed Student’s t-test for two-group analyses. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

GO analysis identified stage-specific functional signatures across three ontological domains, with temporal stratification of biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) (Fig. 3). Constitutive enrichment across all phases (3–14 DPF) included mRNA processing, RNA splicing, and proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process in biological processes, and ribonucleoprotein complex binding in molecular functions.

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs in the spinal cord at different time points after fracture. BP, biological process; CC, cell component; MF, molecular function.

The acute phase (3–7 DPF) demonstrated predominant enrichment in Golgi vesicle transport and regulation of intracellular transport. CC analysis revealed spliceosomal complex, nuclear speck, mitochondrial protein-containing complex, and catalytic step 2 spliceosome. In addition, transcription coregulator activity, mRNA binding, molecular adaptor activity, protein-macromolecule adaptor activity, transcription coactivator activity, ubiquitin-like protein binding, and ubiquitin binding were significantly enriched in molecular functions at 3 and 7 days after fracture.

Early transcriptional changes (3 DPF) specifically involved RNA splicing via transesterification reactions with bulged adenosine as nucleophile, mRNA splicing via spliceosome, RNA splicing via transesterification reactions, protein targeting and ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis in biological processes, and the mitochondrial matrix in cell components. Transitional phase signatures (7 DPF) shifted toward cellular component disassembly, histone deacetylation, negative regulation of supramolecular fiber organization, nuclear transport, and nucleocytoplasmic transport in biological processes. Late-phase dynamics (14 DPF) featured positive regulation of cellular catabolic process, regulation of protein catabolic process, regulation of ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process, autophagy, process utilizing autophagic mechanism, positive regulation of protein catabolic process, and regulation of cellular protein catabolic process in biological processes; interestingly, postsynaptic density, postsynaptic specialization, synaptic membrane, neuron to neuron synapse, and postsynaptic membrane were enriched in cell components.

Pathway prediction based on KEGG

KEGG pathway analysis revealed dynamic molecular cascades during post-fracture recovery (Fig. 4A-C). Early-phase responses (3 DPF) exhibited strong activation of neurodegenerative pathways: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Huntington’s disease, prion disease, and Parkinson’s disease. Transitional phase signatures (7 DPF) maintained neurodegenerative activation while introducing Alzheimer’s disease pathways and multi-system neurodegeneration. Late-phase analysis demonstrated metabolic reprogramming through the mTOR signaling pathway, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, sphingolipid signaling system, and neurotrophin signaling pathway.

IPA analysis of DEGs in the spinal cord at different time points

We conducted a comparative analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at multiple time points (0, 7, 14, and 28 DPF) by IPA. The analysis revealed significant enrichment of canonical pathways associated with cellular growth, proliferation, and developmental processes in spinal cord DEGs across post-fracture time points. These findings are summarized in Fig. 6, which also illustrates the enrichment of nervous system-related biological functions and disease pathways.

Comparative analysis of DEGs in the spinal cord at different time points after fracture. (A) Enrichment of classical pathways at different time points; (B) enrichment of neurological diseases and biological functions at different time points. (p-value < 0.05).

Among canonical pathways associated with cellular growth, proliferation, and development, CREB signaling exhibited biphasic regulation: significant downregulation at 7 DPF followed by marked upregulation at 14 DPF. The myelination pathway demonstrated peak activation at 14 DPF, while TGF-β and FGF signaling downregulation progressively diminished across post-fracture time points. Neurological function enrichment analysis showed temporal specificity: central nervous system (CNS) development was upregulated at 3–7 DPF but downregulated from 14 DPF onward. Neurodegeneration showed peak activation at 3 DPF, with sustained activity at 7 DPF. Progressive inhibition was observed in two pathways, motor dysfunction or movement disorder and movement disorders, throughout the post-fracture timeline. Interestingly, neurological sign activation exhibited temporal progression, reaching maximal intensity in spinal cord tissues at 28 DPF. The coordinative function displayed early-phase suppression (3–7 DPF), partial recovery at 14 DPF, and complete restoration by 28 DPF.

To delineate temporal-specific regulation, we systematically characterized DEG-mediated pathways across post-fracture phases by IPA. Early neurodegeneration activation (3 DPF) was shown (Supplementary Fig. 4). The regulation of cell survival showed phase-specific activation at 7 DPF (Supplementary Fig. 5), while CNS cell death regulation demonstrated significant enrichment at 14 DPF (Supplementary Fig. 6). Motor disorder suppression became statistically significant by 28 DPF (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Experimental validation of key genes

To validate our transcriptomic findings, we performed qRT-PCR analysis of two critical genes, PCP4 and RICTOR, identified by integrated bioinformatics. Spinal cord tissues from independent biological replicates (n = 3/time point) harvested at 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28 days post-fracture were analyzed. The qRT-PCR results confirmed significant temporal dynamics: PCP4 exhibited upregulation at 3 DPF, 7 DPF, and 28 DPF, with downregulation at 14 DPF (Fig. 9A). RICTOR demonstrated downregulation at 7 DPF and 14 DPF (Fig. 9B). These expression patterns strongly corroborated our RNA-seq data.

Discussion

Pioneering work by Kuntz and Richins employing selective neural degeneration techniques elucidated osteal innervation patterns22. Bone efferents predominantly consist of sympathetic-derived unmyelinated fibers that perivascularly mediate vasomotor control. The sciatic nerve integrates spinal/sympathetic components through lumbar gray rami communications23, containing visceromotor efferents. Nociceptive Aδ/C-fibers predominantly innervate periosteum (vs. cortical/marrow compartments), conferring high mechanosensitivity24. Osseous afferents synapse with ipsilateral dorsal horn interneurons via DRG-mediated spinal entry25. This neuroanatomical framework justified our focus on the ipsilateral L4-L5 segments for transcriptomic profiling post-fracture.

RNA sequencing revealed triphasic transcriptional dynamics in the spinal cord: neurodegenerative phase (3–7 DPF), transition phase (14 DPF), and recovery phase (28 DPF). Transcriptional activity subsided by 28 DPF, indicating 14 DPF as the critical transition point for spinal cord homeostasis restoration. Phase-specific master regulators were identified, with PCP4 (neurodegenerative phase) and KRIT1 (transitional phase) demonstrating dual neuro-osseous regulatory functions. While our transcriptomic data reveal temporal dynamics in neuro-osseous crosstalk, we recognize that fracture healing involves multifaceted interactions between neural, immune, and skeletal systems. The observed DEG patterns likely represent one axis of this complex network.

The nucleocytoplasmic shuttling protein PCP4 exhibited biphasic upregulation (3 DPF and 7 DPF). Mechanistically, PCP4 overexpression potentiates the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts26. Notably, PCP4 depletion is a hallmark of ALS pathology27, suggesting its fracture-induced upregulation may counteract neurodegenerative processes. As a calcium-binding protein signal regulator, PCP4 can enhance the resistance of neurons to calcium-mediated toxicity. Specifically, PCP4 is associated with calmodulin 1 (CALM1), which is crucial for neuronal movement28. The downregulation of PCP4 expression may disrupt the function of calmodulin 1, reduce calcium-dependent neuron migration, and increase the risk of neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS. Further research is needed to verify whether PCP4-mediated neuroprotection secondarily enhances fracture healing through neural-derived factors.

The protein encoded by Krit1 is located in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, and its expression is downregulated in the spinal cord 14 days after fracture. Previous studies have reported mutations in the Krit1 gene in spinal cord cavernous hemangiomas29–31. Moreover, the upregulation of Krit1 restricts the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells32. Propionic acid can reduce intracellular ROS levels by regulating Krit1, protecting human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells from oxidative stress damage, and increasing the secretion of nutrient factors, thereby improving cell migration ability. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells play an important role in bone regeneration and repair33, and their secreted extracellular vesicles have been shown to promote the healing of obesity-related fractures34. Therefore, KRIT1 suppression at 14 DPF may create dual pathology risks: neuronal vulnerability to ROS and impaired fracture callus mineralization. PCP4 and KRIT1 emerged as regulators in both the spinal cord and bones. Further research is needed to verify the effects of changes in genes in Table 1 on the spinal cord and bone.

The GO terms enriched in this study demonstrated triphasic activation (3–14 DPF): mRNA processing, RNA splicing, proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process, and ribonucleoprotein complex binding. The transitional phase (14 DPF) exhibited synapse-specific enrichment: postsynaptic density, postsynaptic specialization, synaptic membrane, neuron-to-neuron synapse, and postsynaptic membrane. Synaptic regeneration is known to play an important role in the recovery of neuronal function after spinal cord injury35. Emerging evidence implicates neuroplasticity-fracture healing crosstalk, potentially mediated through neural-derived exosomal miRNA regulation of osteoprogenitor differentiation and glutamatergic signaling-mediated bone metabolic reprogramming. These mechanisms warrant validation using in vivo optogenetic modulation and bone-targeted nanoparticle delivery systems.

KEGG analysis detected neurodegenerative alterations in the spinal cord at 3 DPF and 7 DPF, with key signaling pathways, including the mTOR signaling pathway, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, neurotrophin signaling pathway, AMPK signaling pathway, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, significantly enriched at 14 DPF. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that these pathways mediate critical functions in both neural and osseous tissues. For instance, PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway activation has been shown to ameliorate spinal cord lesions and potentiate osteogenesis36,37. The AMPK signaling pathway regulates glucose metabolism within spinal cord neurons, where its activation enhances functional recovery following spinal cord injury38. In skeletal systems, AMPK activation attenuates high-fat diet-induced osteoporosis39.

Concurrent pathway enrichment was observed for the sphingolipid signaling pathway, axon guidance, and dopaminergic synapses at 14 DPF. Sphingolipids constitute a class of bioactive lipids orchestrating cell-cell communication, neurogenesis, and synaptic maturation. Emerging evidence indicates that sphingolipid metabolism alteration contributes to neurodegenerative pathogenesis in ALS and Parkinson’s disease40. Notably, sphingomyelin is indispensable for central myelination - a process enabling saltatory conduction that optimizes neuronal synchronization while modulating transmission velocity and potentiating synaptic efficacy. Axon guidance mechanisms directing precise synaptogenesis demonstrate therapeutic potential for spinal cord injury rehabilitation41. Collectively, these pathway enrichments at 14 DPF suggest enhanced neural plasticity during this phase, warranting further investigation into its therapeutic implications.

The comparative IPA analysis uncovered temporally distinct transcriptional dynamics in the post-fracture spinal cord, highlighting phase-specific pathophysiological alterations. During the acute phase (3–7 DPF), elevated neurodegenerative activity was observed, peaking at day 3 - a finding that mirrored the concurrent enrichment of neurodegenerative disease pathways identified in KEGG analysis at this time point. Concurrently, robust activation of CNS development was detected, which potentially reflects neural tissue adaptation to traumatic stress. The subacute phase (14 DPF) revealed two critical findings: First, myelination pathways demonstrated peak activation, correlating strongly with synaptic-related GO term enrichment. Second, neuronal CREB signaling exhibited progressive downregulation during the acute phase, followed by significant upregulation at this stage. Mechanistically, CREB activation preserves blood-spinal cord barrier integrity, thereby facilitating neural repair processes42. This biphasic regulation suggests potential functional restoration of spinal circuitry, though its crosstalk with fracture healing mechanisms requires further elucidation. Transcriptional quiescence by 28 DPF suggests stabilization of neural homeostasis. This temporal pattern implies that fracture-induced spinal cord perturbations primarily manifest within the first 28 days, establishing a critical window for therapeutic intervention.

IPA identified Rictor, TEAD1, Torin1, DDX5, CAB39L, and NGF as key upstream regulators of neurodegenerative disease-associated genes. Notably, Rictor and NGF demonstrate pleiotropic functions in neuromusculoskeletal systems, with established therapeutic potential across neural and skeletal pathologies. Genetic augmentation of Rictor can prevent mutant Huntington-induced neurodegeneration43, while its overexpression rescues amyloid-β42-induced neuronal toxicity in Alzheimer’s disease cultures44. Osteoblast-specific Rictor knockout models exhibit compromised bone mechanical properties45,46, confirming its dual regulatory roles. Nerve growth factor (NGF) orchestrates multimodal repair processes, demonstrating tissue-regenerative capacity in both neural trauma and skeletal injury models47,48. While these regulators validate our predictions, the neuromodulatory functions of TEAD1, Torin1, DDX5, and CAB39L in bone-spinal cord cross-talk remain underexplored, warranting targeted mechanistic studies.

Emerging evidence supports a bidirectional regulatory axis between the spinal cord and bone, paralleling established neuron-muscle crosstalk mechanisms. While previous investigations have established spinal injury-induced osteopenia, our transcriptomic profiling reveals fracture-triggered neural adaptations, including CREB-mediated barrier restoration and myelination enhancement. This inter-tissue dialogue may involve neurotrophin-mediated metabolic coupling, as evidenced by concurrent AMPK and mTOR pathway activation. Our findings propose a novel paradigm where fracture-induced spinal cord plasticity potentially modulates fracture healing through neurotrophic factor secretion, influencing osteoprogenitor, synaptic remodeling, and myelin development. Validating these mechanisms could enable neural-targeted therapies to accelerate skeletal repair, exemplified by NGF-mediated dual tissue regeneration. Our data suggest two clinically actionable strategies: (a) Neuromodulatory interventions: Targeting RICTOR or NGF during the acute phase (3–7 DPF) may mitigate neurodegeneration while enhancing osteogenesis. (b) Diagnostic biomarkers: PCP4 (upregulated at 3/7 DPF) and KRIT1 (downregulated at 14 DPF) may serve as neural indicators of fracture healing progression. Validation in human cohorts and development of neural-targeted drug delivery systems remain critical next steps.

This is the first longitudinal transcriptomic profiling of spinal cord responses post-fracture, identifying biphasic neurodegeneration (3–7 DPF) and the critical transition at 14 DPF marked by synaptic plasticity pathways. While this study provides a comprehensive temporal transcriptomic profile of the spinal cord following tibial fracture, revealing intriguing neuro-osseous crosstalk dynamics, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, and most notably, our findings rely primarily on transcriptomic signatures. Many literatures examine transcriptome alterations in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease49,50, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis51; fewer studies have been conducted to address the transcriptome alterations in central nervous system neurodegeneration after fracture. While the significant enrichment of neurodegenerative pathways and identification of key regulators like RICTOR and TEAD1 strongly suggest neuronal stress and potential degeneration, a histological and morphological analysis of the spinal cord tissue (e.g., assessing neuronal loss, gliosis, axonal degeneration, or synaptic density changes) is crucial to demonstrate the presence and extent of actual neurodegeneration directly. Transcriptomic changes, while indicative, do not equate to structural damage. The observed “recovery” phase at 28 DPF, characterized by reduced DEGs and pathway enrichment, suggests a resolution of the acute transcriptional response. However, without histological confirmation, it remains challenging to definitively conclude whether this represents true neural repair or simply a return to transcriptional homeostasis, especially as such rapid functional recovery is atypical in established neurodegenerative disease models. Histological validation in future studies will be critical to confirm structural neurodegeneration. Secondly, although age-related transcriptional changes are minimal in young adult rats over 4 weeks, future studies may include non-fractured age-matched controls at each time point to fully decouple developmental and injury-induced effects. While our qRT-PCR validation confirms transcriptional alterations, elucidating functional neuro-osseous coupling requires targeted modulation of identified regulators (e.g., intrathecal delivery of RICTOR-expressing vectors) combined with fracture healing assessment. DNA origami-based delivery systems52 could enable spatiotemporal control of regulator expression in the spinal cord. Addressing these limitations will strengthen the link between peripheral skeletal injury and central neural consequences proposed in this study.

Our current findings on spinal cord transcriptomic remodeling post-fracture extend the neuro-osseous crosstalk paradigm established in our prior study of DRG responses53. Both investigations employed identical fracture models and longitudinal RNA-seq timepoints (0, 3, 7, 14, and 28 days). Both studies indicate that transcriptional changes in the spinal cord and DRG following fracture exhibit typical stage-specific characteristics. Our study on DRG suggests that the primary phase of fracture repair occurs 14 days post-fracture, with a peak in repair activity at 3 days post-fracture. At 28 days post-fracture, the number of differentially expressed genes in both tissues was very low. Additionally, both studies used the IPA tool to separately highlight the important role of nerve growth factor as an upstream regulatory factor in bone repair and neurodegenerative diseases. Furthermore, unlike the DRG, which showed activation of the neurodegeneration at the 7-day time point post-fracture, the spinal cord transcriptome exhibited the same pathway enrichment as early as 3 days post-fracture. Furthermore, the changes in the spinal cord transcriptome were more pronounced at different time points, such as the significant enrichment of the neurodegenerative pathway at 3-7 days post-fracture and the enrichment of the synaptic remodeling pathway and myelin formation signaling pathway at 14 days post-fracture. The spinal cord transcriptome changes involved a larger number of genes, making the conclusions more reliable. These studies collectively map a “bone-DRG-spinal cord” axis, where peripheral DRG participates in fracture repair and the spinal cord undergoes injury-induced neurodegeneration-to-neural plasticity transitions.

Conclusions

This study elucidated transcriptome changes in the spinal cord post-fracture and screened for important genes that may participate in regulating the spinal cord and bone, providing a sequencing basis for exploring the relationship between the bone and the spinal cord.
