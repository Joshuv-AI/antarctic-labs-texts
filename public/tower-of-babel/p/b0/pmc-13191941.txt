Joint torque estimation from daily living motion for passive sarcopenia monitoring in older adults
Authors: Jaebeom Jo, Kihyun Kim, Min-gu Kang, Kanghyun Ryu, Junhyoung Ha, Jiyeon Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia is a progressive condition in older adults associated with increased risks of falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality. Despite its clinical relevance, early and accurate diagnosis remains limited due to reliance on indirect and subjective screening methods. This study introduces a passive, camera-based framework, Motion-AI Integrated Surveillance for the Elderly (MAISE), that seamlessly estimates joint torque from natural daily movements to provide a functional assessment of lower-limb muscle performance.MethodsThe framework utilizes a CoP (Center of Pressure) limiter to enforce biomechanical constraints during ground reaction force estimation, facilitating precise torque calculation from motion data alone. A generalization evaluation was performed in 28 sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic older adults on movement tasks that were not included in the training dataset, allowing assessment of the model’s ability to generalize to unseen activities. For each lower-limb joint, Peak Torque (PT), Rate of Torque Development (RTD), and Power Range (PR) were extracted and analyzed in relation to established clinical metrics of Sarcopenia. These torque-based parameters were also compared between Sarcopenic and healthy groups to identify joint-specific functional differences.ResultsThe proposed CoP limiter improved the accuracy of joint torque estimation without force plates by reducing center of pressure error by up to 49.3 percent and ground reaction force error by up to 6.5 percent on unseen data. Across various model configurations, the framework achieved RMSE values of 0.034 m for CoP, 0.752 N/kg for GRF, and 0.243 Nm/kg for GRM during testing. The computed torque metrics demonstrated strong correlations with conventional Sarcopenia indicators such as grip strength, gait speed, and chair-stand time. These correlations were both task- and joint-specific, allowing identification of localized muscle weaknesses and compensatory strategies. Significant differences in torque profiles were also observed between Sarcopenic and healthy individuals, particularly in tasks involving rapid force generation or postural control.ConclusionsThe proposed framework enables quantitative assessment of joint-specific functional muscle performance using only motion data, providing preliminary insight toward personalized intervention. Its passive and non-contact implementation supports unobtrusive, longitudinal monitoring, which is essential for capturing the slow and often subclinical progression of Sarcopenia and for informing timely clinical decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01962-3.

Introduction

The global population is experiencing a significant demographic shift toward older ages, with the number of individuals aged 65 and older projected to increase by 225% by 2050, according to the United Nations [1]. This aging trend correlates with a rising prevalence of age-related diseases, including Sarcopenia, which specifically refers to muscle frailty. Unlike general frailty, Sarcopenia highlights declines in physical capacity [2]. Sarcopenia is characterized by a progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass accompanied by declines in muscle strength and physical function and has been recently classified as a muscle disease in the International Classification of Diseases [3, 4]. Recent systematic reviews report that Sarcopenia affects approximately 10% to 16% of older adults worldwide and increases mortality risk by 9.57 times [5, 6]. It constitutes a significant public health concern among older adults, with established associations to adverse outcomes, including falls, disability, hospitalization, and increased mortality [7]. In the United States, the direct healthcare costs attributable to Sarcopenia were estimated at 18.5 billion dollars in 2000 [8]. In addition, a systematic review found that patients with Sarcopenia can incur up to 1.9 times higher healthcare costs than those without Sarcopenia [9].

In response to this growing health concern, various expert groups have established consensus guidelines to promote early diagnosis and effective management of Sarcopenia. The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People recommends a ‘Find-Assess-Confirm-Severity’ algorithm. In this algorithm, cases are initially identified through screening, then assessed and confirmed through measurements of muscle strength, muscle quantity/quality, and physical performance [4]. The Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) has established consensus criteria specifically adapted for Asian populations. It defines Sarcopenia as low muscle mass combined with reduced muscle strength and/or impaired physical performance, using region-specific cut-off thresholds [10]. Other organizations, including the International Working Group on Sarcopenia and the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health, have proposed comparable diagnostic approaches [11, 12]. All these guidelines emphasize the importance of assessing muscle mass, strength, and functionality to ensure an accurate diagnosis.Fig. 1Framework of motion-AI integrated surveillance for the elderly (MAISE)

To implement these guidelines in clinical practice, muscle mass is indirectly estimated through imaging methods such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA) [13]. Muscle strength is commonly evaluated by measuring handgrip strength [14]. Physical performance is assessed through tests like gait speed over short distances [15]. Additional comprehensive assessments, including the Short Physical Performance Battery, Five Times Sit-to-Stand (FTSTS), or Timed Up-and-Go (TUG), are also utilized to capture lower-extremity strength, balance, and mobility [16–18]. Moreover, simple screening tools such as the SARC-F questionnaire and calf circumference measurement are widely employed to quickly assess individuals at risk of Sarcopenia [19–22].

However, these conventional methods present notable limitations. BIA provides an indirect estimate of muscle mass and may not accurately reflect true muscle quantity [23]. More precise techniques, such as computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, are generally impractical for routine clinical use due to high costs [24, 25]. Handgrip strength alone does not fully represent overall muscular strength [14]. Physical performance tests remain constrained by factors like temporary health fluctuations, the need for trained examiners, and specialized equipment [16, 17]. Additionally, regional variations in Sarcopenia assessment reflect population differences. This has led expert groups to establish diverse definitions, criteria, and thresholds for diagnosis [5]. Consequently, reported Sarcopenia prevalence varies substantially from 0.2% to 86.5% [26]. Although regional consensus exists, universally accepted diagnostic standards remain lacking [26, 27]. Therefore, there is a critical need to improve assessment methods and develop novel technologies for personalized systems capable of accurately tracking Sarcopenia progression.

Recent AI-enabled approaches show potential for Sarcopenia diagnosis. Movement analysis studies classify Sarcopenia risk by employing machine learning algorithms on spatiotemporal metrics, plantar pressure patterns, or limb kinematics derived from walking data captured by devices such as inertial sensors, smart insoles, computer vision systems, or IoT wearables [28–31]. Frailty phenotypes, which are strongly linked to Sarcopenia, have been classified using logistic regression models applied to Five Times Sit-to-Stand test data obtained from wearable sensors, particularly focusing on task execution speed [32]. Other screening frameworks used machine learning classifiers to predict Sarcopenia from anthropometric parameters and indirect indicators of physical capability, such as gait speed, hand grip strength, or calf circumference [25, 33]. Furthermore, electromyography-based support vector machine regression provided Sarcopenia classification even among patients with impaired voluntary muscle control [34]. However, these AI-based approaches still rely predominantly on indirect measurements or surrogate markers, lacking direct quantification of the muscles’ mechanical capabilities and actual force-generating capacity [35]. To address these limitations, we propose a novel approach based on joint torque estimation, which enables quantification of task-specific functional muscle performance. This method facilitates quantitative, objective assessment and allows for continuous monitoring of Sarcopenia progression.

Recent research has emphasized the importance of joint torque in assessing Sarcopenia. Knee extension strength has been frequently considered a key index for diagnosing or screening Sarcopenia, with several studies confirming its clinical cut-off values and association with frailty progression [36–39]. Another study also shows that knee and hip extension measurements can yield higher predictive accuracy for Sarcopenia than grip strength, using a portable device to assess lower limb torque [40]. Beyond peak torque, parameters such as torque complexity measured by entropy methods assessing the predictability and regularity of torque signals [41] and rate of torque development [42–45] offer deeper insights, capturing subtle deficits that may not be detectable through conventional metrics. Research on ankle torque further underscores its importance in balance, fall risk, and gait performance [46–48]. Additional studies examining multi-joint synergy show how hip and knee torque support functional movements like chair rising or fall prevention [49–53]. Lastly, the distal-to-proximal shift in older adults indicates a compensatory mechanism that preserves gait and stair climbing despite reduced ankle or knee torque [54–56]. Furthermore, the joint power range has been effectively used as a sensitive indicator of exhaustion in older populations [57].

Accurate ground reaction force information is fundamental for computing joint torques in musculoskeletal analysis. Conventional force plates provide reliable measurements but are costly and limited to laboratory settings [58], which has motivated efforts to estimate GRFs directly from kinematic data. Early studies reconstructed GRFs during walking and running from segmental kinematics [59]. However, resolving the double-support phases required heuristic assumptions (fixed foot centers of pressure, smooth transition constraints, speed-dependent weighting) as well as, in some studies, energetic cost–based optimization [60–63]. These approaches improved numerical stability but relied largely on curve-fitting procedures rather than physical descriptions of foot–ground interaction. As an alternative, physics-based simulations using Hunt–Crossley contact models were proposed, though their accuracy remains sensitive to the choice of contact parameters, limiting their generalizability across tasks and populations [58].

In early AI-based GRF estimation research, artificial neural networks were applied only to the double support phase of gait [64]. Subsequent studies expanded their applicability to broader movements such as gait and stair-walking, but their performance was limited because datasets were small and movement variability was narrow [65–69]. To overcome these limitations, more recent efforts have introduced large-scale motion datasets and simple regression models such as GroundLink [70] and AddBiomechanics [71]. Despite these advances, without integrating physical constraints into the model formulation, their GRF estimates frequently violate biomechanical plausibility when applied to new movements outside the training data set.

In summary, although diagnostic guidelines for Sarcopenia have advanced, current assessments remain constrained by indirect and episodic indicators (e.g., handgrip strength, gait speed) that fail to reflect the true mechanical functionality of muscles. Recent technologies have attempted to provide more quantitative evaluation, yet critical limitations remain: (1) prior torque-based assessments rely on isokinetic or isometric setups that cannot capture dynamic, task-specific compensation strategies observable in daily movements; (2) these approaches typically require lab instrumentation (force plates, dynamometers), limiting ecological validity and accessibility for continuous monitoring; and (3) existing wearable or vision-based methods do not estimate joint torque, but rather infer general mobility scores, lacking a biomechanically interpretable measure of functional muscle performance.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce MAISE (Motion-AI Integrated Surveillance for the Elderly), a torque-based assessment framework designed to continuously monitor functional muscle performance in older adults during daily activities, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Unlike conventional assessments that rely on episodic clinical tests, MAISE provides passive and real-time evaluations of muscle performance from natural movements. By estimating joint-level torque from motion data, the framework enables monitoring of task-specific functional changes to support timely, personalized interventions for Sarcopenia management.

This study presents three pivotal pipelines and key findings that are essential for designing the suggested framework. First, this study introduces a novel torque-based algorithm that enables the quantification of lower extremity joint torque during functional tasks. Our suggested method provides a dynamic and mechanistic based evaluation of muscle performance, capturing force generation in daily movements. By validating this method with laboratory-based motion capture, we establish a foundational step toward markerless, video-based estimation. Second, we incorporate a physics-informed constraint layer to improve the generalizability of ground reaction force estimation, supporting accurate torque computation without direct force-plate measurements and thereby increasing the feasibility of home-based assessments. Third, we establish potential torque-based biomarkers during multiple daily activities that are related to muscle mass, physical performance, and muscle strength. These relationships were determined by computing correlations with conventional metrics that characterize individual differences in muscle mass, physical performance, and muscle strength.

Estimation algorithm and torque analysis

Ground reaction force estimation

For GRF, we propose an estimation model trained on the AddBiomechanics Core Dataset (mean age 30.7 ± 15.8 years and mean BMI 22.8 ± 3.4 kg/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^{2}$$\end{document}; access date: Sep. 1, 2024) [71]. This dataset contains data and ground reaction force information derived from musculoskeletal simulation analysis using musculoskeletal models and marker data. We used the official AddBiomechanics codebase [73] to split the dataset into training and validation sets, yielding 236 participants for training and 26 for validation. Model inputs include joint angles, joint angular velocities, joint angular accelerations, joint center positions, history of the pelvic positions and angles, pelvic velocities and accelerations, as well as center of mass position, velocity, and acceleration. Outputs consist of mass-normalized ground reaction force, mass-normalized ground reaction moment, and the center of pressure. All data were represented in a pelvis-centered root frame and resampled at 100 Hz using the AddBiomechanics [74] to ensure consistent sampling rates across participants. A sliding window approach with a window size of 100 frames is used for model training. To ensure balanced data representation, the stride of the sliding window is adaptively adjusted between 10–50% of the window size based on data availability within different motion types in the AddBiomechanics Dataset. The description of virtual foot boundary (VFB)

Accurate estimation of Ground Reaction Force (GRF) and Ground Reaction Moment (GRM) requires that the Center of Pressure (CoP) remains within a physically realistic area. Without constraints, estimation models can predict CoP outside the actual foot contact area. These physically impossible predictions are reducing the reliability of biomechanical analyses, including joint torque calculations. To address this issue, we implemented a CoP Limiter. This approach explicitly constrains predictions within a physically plausible region by defining a virtual foot boundary (VFB). The VFB not only ensures physical realism but also explicitly guides the spatial learning process of the model by providing clear reference boundaries. It is constructed using key anatomical landmarks, as shown in Fig. 4.

The model architecture is designed to capture temporal characteristics of the motion data. To verify the effectiveness of adding the CoP Limiter, we experimented with three different encoder structures, as shown in Fig. 6. (1) Feedforward network (FFN), which uses a simple stack of Multi Layer Perceptron (MLP) layers, (2) Mamba architecture built upon Mamba-MLP blocks, (3) Transformer architecture employing Attention-MLP blocks. In the FFN variant, RMS normalization and dropout are applied. In the Mamba and Transformer variants, the MLPs incorporate swiglu activation [75], RMS normalization, dropout, and residual connections with drop path [76]. The Mamba blocks are based on a state space model approach [77], and the Attention blocks employ Flash Attention with Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE) [78, 79]. All three variants follow the pipeline illustrated in Fig. 5c. The encoder blocks then extract temporal features, after which a regression head predicts the Ground Reaction Force (GRF), Ground Reaction Moment (GRM), and Center of Pressure (CoP) using layer normalization and a linear projection. Finally, the CoP predictions are constrained through the CoP Limiter to ensure physical plausibility.Fig. 6Encoder Blocks to capture temporal characteristics. a Feedforward Neural Network block. b Mamba block. c Transformer block

The loss function combines multiple terms to enhance prediction accuracy and temporal consistency. First, Mean Squared Error (MSE) is computed separately for the x, y, and z components of GRF, GRM, and CoP predictions. Second, a Temporal Consistency (TC) loss encourages smooth temporal predictions by penalizing abrupt changes between consecutive frames [80]. Third, inspired by the Mean Per Joint Velocity Error (MPJVE) introduced in previous studies [81], we adapted this concept to GRF, GRM, and CoP predictions. Specifically, we compute the squared differences of the first-order differences between predicted and ground truth values to ensure accurate modeling of temporal changes in predictions. These three loss terms (MSE, TC, MPJVE) are combined into a weighted sum, with additional scaling factors applied independently for each three-directional component. This allows the model to emphasize biomechanically significant directions and metrics more effectively. Automatic mixed precision with bfloat16 is used to optimize GPU memory usage and accelerate the training process. Joint torque computation

Joint torques are calculated using the musculoskeletal simulator OpenSim with the Gait2354 model [83], which focuses primarily on the lower extremities and includes the torso but excludes the arms. The model consists of 13 body segments: torso, pelvis, right and left femur, right and left tibia, right and left talus, right and left calcaneus, and right and left toes.

The generic musculoskeletal model is individually scaled for each participant based on anatomical marker data collected via motion capture. In this procedure, the OpenSim Scale Tool fits segment geometry to each participant’s anthropometry by minimizing marker errors, whereas inertial parameters are proportionally scaled from the generic model to match the modified geometry [84]. Joint angles and moments are then calculated through Inverse Kinematics (IK) and Inverse Dynamics (ID) analyses, utilizing the motion capture marker trajectories and ground reaction forces measured by force plates. Due to a limited number of markers placed on the foot during data collection, the metatarsophalangeal joint is locked within the model. Finally, a residual reduction algorithm is applied to minimize residual forces, reducing discrepancies between the simulation results and measured experimental data. Errors in IK and ID analyses adhered to best practice recommendations [85].

Data analysis

For each task, segmentation methods are applied to exclude external forces other than ground reaction forces and standardize movement cycles across participants. Specifically, analysis for the Chair Stand task begins when the hips lift off from the chair to exclude chair-induced contact forces. In the Pick-Up task, segmentation starts when the upper body begins to bend forward and ends when the object is lifted, and the upper body returns upright. For the Step-Up task, participants start with both feet on a single force plate at ground level. The analysis begins as the leading foot lifts from the plate and ends just before the lag foot contacts the raised platform. This approach avoids the double support phase, where both feet are in contact with the same force plate. For visualization purposes, the segmented movement cycles are normalized to 101-time points (0-100%) to facilitate comparison across participants and trials.

Participant group characteristics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (IRB number: KIST-202408-HR-050, Approval date: August 9, 2024). Participants were recruited from local community centers and senior welfare facilities. The general inclusion criteria were as follows: participants had to be community-dwelling adults aged 65 years or older, free from any chronic illness that could significantly affect gait or movement, capable of independent ambulation without external assistance, and able to communicate and provide informed consent after understanding the study procedures. Participants were excluded if they had difficulty walking independently without assistive devices, were diagnosed with severe cardiovascular disease or other serious medical conditions that could interfere with study participation, or had a history of fractures, severe arthritis, or musculoskeletal surgery that could influence muscle strength or functional performance. Individuals with dementia or severe cognitive impairment, as well as those with significant visual or hearing impairments that could affect task performance, were also excluded. Inclusion criteria for individuals with Sarcopenia were based on [10] as following: Gait Speed (< 1 m/s) or FTSTS (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge $$\end{document} 12 sec), ASMI (M: < 7.0kg/m2, F: < 5.7kg/m2), and Hand Grip Strength (M: < 28 kg, F: < 18 kg). GRF estimation via motion data using CoP-constrained learning

We evaluated the performance of our proposed GRF estimation models using different encoder architectures, both with and without the CoP Limiter. To demonstrate a quantitative comparison with existing literature, we additionally implemented GroundLinkNet [70], a convolution-based benchmark model for predicting GRF and CoP from motion with physically plausible generalization to diverse unseen motions, and evaluated it with and without the CoP Limiter under the same protocol. Table 2 summarizes the quantitative results across all model configurations, reporting the average L2 norm error for the Center of Pressure (CoP), Ground Reaction Force (GRF), and Ground Reaction Moment (GRM) predictions.

Integrating the CoP Limiter improved CoP prediction accuracy across all architectures. Specifically, the CoP Limiter reduced prediction errors by 57.0% in the GroundLinkNet, 60.7% in the FFN model, 44.1% in the Mamba model, and 41.2% in the Transformer model. Among all configurations, the Mamba+CL model achieved the lowest CoP error (0.019 m) and GRM prediction error (0.022 Nm/kg), while the Transformer+CL model yielded the best GRF prediction (0.326 N/kg).

In the evaluation on the unseen elderly dataset without fine-tuning, similar performance was observed but with generally higher error magnitudes. The CoP Limiter improved CoP prediction accuracy: 51.5% reduction for GroundLinkNet, 49.3% for FFN, 42.1% for Mamba, and 40.4% for Transformer. For GRF prediction, error was reduced by 4.5% in the Mamba model and 6.5% in the Transformer model. The Mamba+CL configuration achieved the lowest CoP error (0.033 m), while the Transformer+CL model demonstrated superior performance in both GRF (0.752 N/kg) and GRM (0.243 Nm/kg) predictions. Consequently, compared with the GroundLinkNet baseline under the same protocol, the Mamba+CL configuration yielded a 66.7% reduction in CoP error (from 0.099 to 0.033 m) on the unseen test dataset. Furthermore, the Transformer+CL configuration resulted in a 20.5% reduction in GRF error (from 0.946 to 0.752 N/kg). Discussion

This paper proposes a novel framework called MAISE (Motion-AI Integrated Surveillance for the Elderly) for Sarcopenia assessment through dynamic joint torque analysis during functional tasks. We have developed and implemented this framework through several key innovations. First, we proposed a GRF estimation model with a CoP Limiter that significantly improved generalization performance, enabling accurate biomechanical analysis without expensive force plates. Second, we established clear relationships between existing Sarcopenia algorithms and joint torque metrics during daily activities, demonstrating that torque metrics during functional movements are associated with conventional indicators of muscle strength and physical performance. Third, we compared Sarcopenia patients with healthy individuals through comprehensive joint torque analysis, revealing distinct differences in joint torque patterns and identifying compensatory movement strategies among those with muscle weakness.

Main findings

Participant group characteristics

The prevalence in our study was 42.9%, similar to previous prevalence rates reported in Korea [90]. Significant differences between the Sarcopenia and healthy groups were observed in FTSTS and Gait Speed, representing physical performance. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in other metrics, the Sarcopenia group showed longer TUG times, weaker grip strength, smaller calf circumferences, lower ASMI values, and higher SARC-F scores. These results align with trends reported in previous studies [19, 20, 37, 90]. Conversely, BMI shows minimal differences between the groups and no clear trend, which differs from findings in previous research [25].

GRF estimation via motion data using CoP-constrained learning

Our proposed GRF estimation models demonstrated significant improvements in CoP prediction accuracy when incorporating the CoP Limiter. The primary objective of applying the CoP Limiter was to enhance CoP estimation by incorporating physical plausibility constraints, resulting in an improved CoP accuracy of up to 60.7% across all model architectures in both validation and test evaluations. Previous GRF prediction studies were often evaluated on limited datasets centered around specific tasks such as gait or stair climbing, which restricted the systematic comparison of generalization performance across diverse movements [64–69]. To address this, large-scale datasets including diverse motions, such as GroundLink and AddBiomechanics, have recently been proposed alongside representative baselines [70, 71]. GroundLink introduced GroundLinkNet, a CNN-based baseline designed to predict physically plausible ground reactions even for unseen motions [70], while AddBiomechanics included simple MLP-based regression baselines and GroundLinkNet [71]. However, it has been discussed that representing data in a pelvis-centered reference frame can result in an offset in CoP predictions, suggesting a need for a foot-based reference frame to improve GRF and CoP prediction accuracy [70]. Our CoP Limiter addresses this issue by explicitly enforcing physically feasible CoP bounds via a Virtual Foot Boundary (VFB), mitigating unrealistic CoP predictions on unseen motions as qualitatively demonstrated in Fig. 11 in the Appendix B. To demonstrate that this benefit is model-agnostic, we evaluated the CoP Limiter across FFNs (MLP-based), GroundLinkNet (CNN-based), and Mamba/Transformers (sequence models) under an identical protocol, confirming consistent improvements in CoP accuracy (Table 2). Among the models with matched model capacity and identical input window size, Transformer and Mamba architectures consistently outperformed the simpler MLP-based FFN. This improved performance is attributed to both architectures’ ability to effectively capture temporal dependencies in the data [77]. However, the inductive bias from Mamba’s selective and sequential state space model architecture appears to act more restrictively than that of Attention, leading to a trend of slightly higher error and lower generalization in GRF estimation. This discrepancy in performance can be explained by the signal characteristics of GRF, which often exhibit sharp transients, spikes, and nonlinear regime changes during dynamic movements. These features are more effectively captured by Attention’s flexible, global look-up mechanism. Previous studies show a similar level of accuracy [70, 71, 91]. Importantly, in the test evaluation with elderly subjects, the inclusion of CoP Limiter not only improved CoP prediction but also resulted in relatively accurate GRF predictions. This demonstrates that models incorporating spatially informed constraints for CoP estimation can lead to better performance and generalizability when applied to real-world data. Our results show that incorporating physically plausible constraints can yield more reliable ground reaction force estimates from human movement data, offering a practical approach for biomechanical analysis in non-laboratory settings where force plates are not feasible.

Correlation of torque-based metrics with conventional clinical sarcopenia indicators

A correlation analysis was conducted between quantified joint torque metrics (PT, RTD, PR) and Sarcopenia algorithms. In the 5-times Chair Stand task, correlations were identified across muscle strength, physical performance, muscle mass, and a screening test. All lower limb joint torque metrics exhibited strong correlations with hand grip strength (HGS), supporting research that FTSTS reflects muscle strength [4]. Significant correlations were also noted with physical performance indicators, such as FTSTS time and gait speed. Knee torque metrics (RTD, PR) showed strong negative correlations with FTSTS time, emphasizing the critical role of knee torque in rapid sit-to-stand movements. All hip torque metrics substantially correlated with gait speed, which is consistent with previous findings on the importance of hip joint torque in walking performance [27]. The relatively lower correlations with ASMI suggest that joint torque is more directly related to muscle strength and physical performance than to muscle mass. Additionally, ankle PT and knee RTD were also identified with the screening questionnaire SARC-F scores, indicating that subjective assessments indirectly relate to objectively measured joint torque.

In the Chair Stand task, correlations with muscle strength and physical performance were weaker than in the 5-times repetition. HGS moderately correlated with knee RTD and hip RTD, as well as gait speed correlated with hip PT and PR. These findings suggest even a single sit-to-stand movement reflects muscle strength and physical performance through knee and hip torque metrics. Also, the relatively higher hip torque observed in sarcopenic participants suggests the use of a proximal compensatory strategy driven by reduced knee extensor strength and neuromuscular efficiency. This pattern is consistent with the distal-to-proximal shift previously described in older adults as an adaptive mechanism to preserve functional performance under lower-limb muscle weakness [55, 56].

In the Pick-Up task, forward trunk bending shifts the center of mass anterior to the base of support, making ankle control crucial for maintaining balance. In this ankle-dominant movement, capability of using wider ankle power ranges were associated with stronger HGS and shorter FTSTS times. These correlations confirm that ankle PR effectively reflects both muscle strength and physical performance in this task. Knee PR similarly correlated with FTSTS, suggesting effective knee utilization relates to better physical performance. Hip PT correlated with gait speed, consistent with our Chair Stand findings that hip joint strength influences walking velocity.

In the Step-Up task, correlations were primarily observed in the supporting (lag) foot, where ankle PT correlated with HGS and FTSTS time. This demonstrates that ankle torque significantly reflects muscle strength and functional performance in the lag foot during step ascent. Additionally, knee PR of the lead foot negatively correlated with BMI, suggesting knee torque is influenced by body height and weight in this task.

Among the four tasks, the conventional clinical task (5-times Chair Stand) exhibited the most pronounced correlations with Sarcopenia criteria. Meaningful associations were additionally identified during daily activities such as Chair Stand, Pick-Up, and Step-Up. These findings suggest that joint torque metrics could potentially serve as quantifiable indicators associated with muscle strength and physical performance relevant to Sarcopenia assessment, providing measures that may capture subtle functional differences. We also found that joint contributions were task-specific, with the hip and knee prominent in the Chair Stand, the ankle crucial for the Pick-Up, and the Step-Up involving distinct roles for each foot. Therefore, selecting task-specific metrics is essential for accurate and interpretable monitoring of Sarcopenia progression across diverse daily activities.

In summary, torque-based metrics derived from the MAISE framework exhibited task and joint specific associations with conventional Sarcopenia indicators. In the 5-Times Chair Stand, knee and hip torques showed the strongest correlations with handgrip strength (HGS), appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASMI), and gait speed, with knee RTD (r = 0.72) and hip PT (r = 0.73) demonstrating particularly strong associations, while knee PR negatively correlated with FTSTS time (r = −0.68), indicating that faster torque generation was linked to superior functional performance. Hip RTD and PR also showed positive relationships with gait speed, supporting the biomechanical relevance of dynamic torque capacity to mobility. In contrast, simpler tasks such as the single Chair Stand exhibited weaker correlations, largely limited to the hip, while the Pick-Up and Step-Up tasks revealed moderate but more variable associations at the ankle due to greater balance and coordination demands. These results suggest that torque metrics, especially RTD and PR, reflect distinct aspects of task-specific neuromuscular function that align with established clinical measures of Sarcopenia severity.

Torque-based discrimination between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic groups

We conducted non-parametric tests on joint torque metrics and confirmed statistically significant differences between the Sarcopenia patient and healthy groups. In the 5-times Chair Stand task, significant differences were observed at every lower limb joint. Healthy individuals exhibited higher ankle PR indicative of superior power modulation and greater hip RTD associated with enhanced explosive torque generation. Significant differences in all knee metrics emphasized the critical role of knee strength in repeated sit-to-stand performance. Thus, joint torque metrics offer more detailed evaluations that can complement conventional approaches for characterizing Sarcopenia-related functional decline.

In contrast, the Chair Stand task showed no statistically significant group differences despite involving similar movements. This aligns with prior research suggesting that a sit-to-stand cannot be treated as a measure of muscle strength [52]. Nevertheless, observable trends indicated that the Sarcopenia group tended to utilize relatively more hip torque as compensation for reduced knee torque, consistent with previous findings [92].

In the Pick-Up task, only ankle PR demonstrated a statistically significant group difference, reflecting its critical role in balance during forward bending. Healthy individuals exhibited a wider ankle PR, indicating superior ankle control for stability. However, significant variability was observed in the knee joint, resulting from participants employing diverse flexion and extension strategies under natural movement instructions. This strategic diversity made quantitative comparison of torque metrics challenging for joints other than the ankle. Notably, a distinct strategy characterized by substantial knee flexion-extension and associated knee extension torque was predominantly employed by healthy participants. This suggests a potential difference in strategy adoption between groups and future studies may benefit from employing more constrained task designs that limit compensatory variability.

In the Step-Up task, joint torque metrics revealed statistically significant group differences across lower limb joints, separating Sarcopenia patients from healthy individuals despite relatively low correlations with clinical indicators shown in Table 3. This task also revealed distinct lead/lag foot roles during stair ascent. For the lead foot, healthy individuals showed significantly higher ramping of torque across all analyzed joints, indicating faster torque generation contributing to the lead leg’s elevation motion. Conversely, Sarcopenia patients exhibited higher lead foot hip PT and a tendency for compensatory hip reliance relative to the knee, consistent with Chair Stand observations and previous research [56]. The lag foot providing support and propulsion showed statistically significant differences. Healthy individuals demonstrated significantly higher ankle PT and RTD, consistent with greater push-off propulsion. While lag foot hip PR showed significant differences with higher values and notable variability in Sarcopenia patients, this may indicate challenges in generating consistent supportive torque for stability during single-leg stance.

Overall, significant differences in torque-based metrics were observed between healthy and Sarcopenic participants, particularly in the 5-Times Chair Stand task. Sarcopenic individuals exhibited substantially lower peak torque (PT), rate of torque development (RTD), and power range (PR) across the knee and hip joints (p < 0.001), consistent with global reductions in dynamic strength and power generation. The single Chair Stand revealed smaller between-group differences, showing only non-significant trends toward reduced hip and knee torques, suggesting the use of compensatory movement strategies to maintain performance. The Pick-Up task showed the highest inter-individual variability, with significant differences limited to the ankle PR metric (p = 0.001), indicating altered distal control rather than generalized weakness. In the Step-Up task, both leading and trailing limbs exhibited reduced ankle and knee torque parameters in the Sarcopenia group, reflecting asymmetrical motor adaptation during load transfer. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Sarcopenic participants display joint- and task-dependent deficits in torque generation, most pronounced during repetitive, strength-demanding tasks such as the 5-Times Chair Stand.

Our quantitative analysis of joint torque during dynamic functional movements revealed significant functional differences between Sarcopenia patients and healthy controls, clearly demonstrating the compensatory torque strategies employed by the Sarcopenia group. This approach offers a complementary perspective to previous related research, which primarily focused on static, maximal joint torque measurements (e.g., maximal voluntary contraction, isokinetic tests [36–40]), or static RTD evaluation [42–45]. Unlike these controlled assessments, the dynamic torques measured in our study reflect multi-factorial functional outcomes that integrate muscle strength, neuromuscular control, and task-specific coordination [49, 50]. The dynamic torque metrics that are provided in this present study offer preliminary insights for personalized monitoring of functional changes associated with Sarcopenia. Different movement patterns and kinetic demands are evident across diverse activities, suggesting that careful consideration of task-specific variations is important when applying these metrics.

Limitations

This study demonstrates the potential for monitoring Sarcopenia using joint torque generated during daily activities. However, several limitations are noted.

First, there are limitations related to the sample size and the biased data from community-dwelling elderly individuals. In particular, physical performance risk was observed in 83.3% of subjects, and participants classified as having Sarcopenia based only on muscle mass and strength risk accounted for only 16.6% of the total. Although prevalence and clinical practice results showed similar trends to previous studies, the characteristics of the Sarcopenia patient group biased toward reduced physical ability may have been reflected in the results. Although not all torque-derived features (PT, PR, RTD) showed statistically significant correlations with clinical assessments after correction, these findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the biomechanical relevance of the proposed metrics. The absence of strong correlations presumably reflects inter-individual variability and the multifactorial nature of Sarcopenia, rather than the inadequacy of the features themselves. Also, although correction was employed to account for multiple testing, the small sample size and the large comparison could lead to statistical errors. Therefore, these statistical associations should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory, warranting further validation with independent datasets. Future work will involve larger sample sizes and refined participant stratification to validate the sensitivity and specificity of these parameters for characterizing Sarcopenia-related functional decline.

Second, improvements in the performance of the ground reaction force estimation model are focused on CoP estimation. By applying our proposed CoP Limiter, we were able to improve CoP prediction on the test set by 40.4−49.3% depending on the model architecture. However, the improvement in GRF was minimal at 4.5−6.5% compared to CoP improvement, and GRM showed a moderate improvement. This limitation arises from the current approach, which implicitly learns the static-equilibrium relationship among GRF, GRM, and CoP from large-scale data. Therefore, methods to increase the generalizability of force prediction need to be further explored. In addition, because the estimated GRF, GRM, and CoP signals from the zero-shot configuration are used as inputs to compute joint torques, estimation errors can propagate to the resulting joint torque calculations and downstream metrics such as PT, RTD, and PR. Our pilot propagation sensitivity analysis without OpenSim RRA indicated that perturbations at error scales representative of the current zero-shot GRF/CoP estimation performance can introduce mean joint torque deviations on the order of 0.048 Nm/kg, which may influence the magnitude of the reported torque-derived metrics (see Supplementary Section S.3). Although the present study establishes the relevance of these torque-derived metrics, the absolute magnitude of the estimated torques obtained without optimization within the inverse dynamics framework may be affected by estimation errors in GRF, GRM, and CoP, and should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Besides, the observed lower performance in the test evaluation compared to the validation set can be attributed to several key factors. These may originate from differences in sensor data such as equipment specifications or post-processing differences in measuring GRF and GRM, and from learning data issues such as demographic bias and lack of diversity of tasks that primarily focus on the walking task. Another potential source of error could be the reliance of the proposed CoP Limiter on a virtual foot boundary defined by the ankle, subtalar, and metatarsophalangeal joint centers, meaning that inaccuracies in foot joint localization could be propagated to CoP errors.

Third, personalization of body-segment inertial properties relied on proportional scaling of a generalized OpenSim model. Inaccuracies in segment mass distribution and moments of inertia can bias inverse-dynamics joint moments [93], and such effects may be exacerbated in older adults with age-related loss of appendicular muscle mass [94]. Therefore, personalizing inertial properties is necessary to improve biomechanical accuracy, and imaging-based subject-specific modeling pipelines like NMSBuilder [95] or MAP Client [96] present alternatives for generating individualized musculoskeletal models. The present study used OpenSim Gait2354 model without arms was used; arm motion was not explicitly simulated but approximated through trunk movement to represent the combined effect of torso and upper-limb dynamics. Future extensions will adopt a full-body model to analyze dynamic coupling between the trunk and upper limbs for tasks such as reach-to-lift motions.

Lastly, this study evaluated a limited number of tasks rather than diverse activities of daily living. We assessed the clinical task of 5-Times Chair Stand and daily activities including Chair Stand, Pick-Up, and Step-Up, which represent only a small subset of diverse daily movements. In addition, each task significantly influences joint torque interpretation for Sarcopenia metrics. The 5-Times Chair Stand revealed significant differences across all joints, while the Chair Stand only revealed compensatory strategies without statistical significance. The Pick-Up task showed significant variance due to individual movement strategies, with meaningful differences only at the ankle joint. The Step-Up task demonstrated significant differences across all joints, but required different interpretative approaches between lag foot and lead foot. While joint torque offers a functional muscle function assessment [49, 50], it must be interpreted as a multi-factored functional outcome that could be influenced by neuromuscular control & movement strategies [97, 98], task balance control [99], and pain-related adaptations [100]. Consequently, our findings suggest that task selection should be carefully weighed, as the functional insight from each movement may be modulated by the various physiological and behavioral factors inherent in daily activities.

Future directions

This cross-sectional study confirms significant differences between healthy and Sarcopenia groups through joint torque analysis. Our next step is a longitudinal study that will track how individual torque patterns change as Sarcopenia progresses. We also plan to collect and analyze data from a broader, more diverse population to address current limitations related to sample size and demographic bias. In particular, we will analyze a wider range of Activities of Daily Living (ADLs). It will provide insights into torque variations across diverse movements, addressing the limited task scope of the current study. Although strong correlations were observed between estimated joint moments and conventional Sarcopenia measures, correlation alone does not fully characterize agreement or diagnostic validity. Future studies will therefore focus on establishing agreement metrics and evaluating individual-level classification accuracy to ensure the clinical reliability and applicability of the proposed torque-based approach. In addition, future work will focus on characterizing how errors in GRF, GRM, and CoP estimation propagate to inverse-dynamics–derived joint torque metrics across different tasks and movement conditions. Because the current predictions were obtained in a zero-shot setting without exposure to the target population, incorporating population-specific data into the training pipeline is expected to substantially reduce estimation errors and mitigate downstream effects on torque-derived metrics. In the longer term, validation in larger clinical cohorts will be necessary to establish the sensitivity, reliability, specificity, and discriminative ability of these metrics for monitoring Sarcopenia-related functional decline.

The MAISE framework in Fig. 1 can support these future objectives. By utilizing only motion data, this framework enables joint torque analysis using cameras without force plates. This capability makes MAISE particularly suitable for facilitating large-scale data collection with biomechanical analyses in real-world settings. To this end, this study represents the first step toward realizing a vision-based framework for ground reaction force estimation. Implementation of the proposed MAISE framework requires developing two key components. First, we will implement camera-based motion capture by embedding recent advances in 3D human pose estimation and non-laboratory movement analysis techniques [58, 81, 101]. Second, while we improved CoP prediction when applied to unseen data using the CoP Limiter, we also need to enhance generalizability of ground reaction force estimation. This can be accomplished by embedding physical laws into the learning architecture, the relationships among GRF, GRM, and COP can be learned explicitly [69]. Another method is to incorporate research demonstrating sim2real capabilities through imitation learning with physics-based simulation across various movements [91]. This approach will enhance the generalization and robustness of GRF predictions across diverse activities of daily living.

Lastly, future work will focus on integrating a decision tree–based classification method for Sarcopenia prediction to improve the interpretability of the proposed framework. This approach will enhance transparency and help clinicians understand the relative contribution of biomechanical and functional features to the final diagnostic decision.

Ultimately, this study lays the technological foundation for realizing ambient intelligence in Sarcopenia monitoring. While motion capture was employed in the present study to ensure biomechanical accuracy, the proposed framework is inherently designed to operate with passive vision systems embedded in everyday home appliances. This approach is especially critical for older adults for whom wearable sensors present practical challenges due to the burden of attachment, charging, and maintenance. By removing these barriers, our method supports long-term observation of functional muscle performance through naturally occurring daily movements, rather than relying on indirect proxies like walking speed, chair-stand time, or symptom-based questionnaires, which provide limited insight into task-specific muscle performance. Importantly, Sarcopenia represents a progressive degenerative condition characterized by gradual declines in muscle mass, strength, and function over extended periods, often spanning months to years, rather than manifesting through acute or abrupt onset. Therefore, continuous and naturalistic monitoring is essential for capturing early signs of decline and mapping individual trajectories of functional deterioration. This system will offer a practical and scalable solution, allowing clinically meaningful impairments or deterioration of function in specific joints or muscles, and enabling an intelligent, non-intrusive healthcare system in daily living environments.

Conclusion

This study introduces MAISE (Motion-AI Integrated Surveillance for the Elderly), a novel framework that supports Sarcopenia assessment through joint torque analysis. To analyze joint torque using motion data alone, we developed a ground reaction force estimation model with an integrated CoP Limiter, achieving improved generalization performance in various tasks. This improvement in estimation reliability facilitates biomechanical analysis in settings where force plates are not feasible, thereby supporting continuous monitoring in non-laboratory settings. By demonstrating the relationships between joint torque metrics during daily activities and conventional Sarcopenia indicators, our approach provides a quantitative and task-specific assessment of functional muscle performance that complements traditional clinical measures. Notably, significant differences between healthy and Sarcopenic groups suggest that torque metrics serve as a promising quantifiable indicator associated with Sarcopenia-related functional decline. Through this approach, the MAISE framework offers practical tools for early detection and ongoing monitoring of frailty in the elderly population. It can also support personalized treatment strategies that address individual functional deficits and contribute to improved clinical outcomes.
