The Incidence, Associated Factors and In‐Hospital Outcomes of Cardiac‐Surgery‐Associated Acute Kidney Injury Among Children Undergoing Cardiac Surgery at Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute. A Hospital‐Based Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Dure Shewar, Aimal A Khattak, Waleed Ahmad
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study “The Incidence Associated Factors and In‐Hospital Outcomes of Cardiac‐Surgery‐Associated Acute Kidney Injury Among Children Undergoing Cardiac Surgery at Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute – A Hospital‐Based Prospective Cohort Study” by Gimonge et al. [1]. The study adds a meaningful contribution to the available literature by providing invaluable information about the incidence, risk factors, and clinical outcomes of CSA AKI in children to guide clinical practice and policymakers in a low‐resource and real‐world environment. There are, though, various factors to be considered.

The study used Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcome (KDIGO) criteria to diagnose CSA AKI, which relies on serum creatinine levels (SCr) [1]. However, it has been observed that SCr in pediatric patients varies, peaking in neonates, decreasing in the first 2 months, and rising again at the age of 2–3 years [2]. Moreover, the study did not consider the Pre‐Op SCr levels that play a critical role in affecting the accuracy of AKI diagnosis. Previous research studies have highlighted the importance of considering these factors to avoid under‐ or overdiagnosis of AKI [2].

The other issue is the fact that the study used serum creatinine levels as the sole means to diagnose AKI. This approach might underestimate AKI diagnosis due to a delayed rise in serum creatinine levels. The AWAKEN study has reported that 6.6% of AKI cases might be missed without considering additional factors [3]. Using other diagnostic criteria, like urine output criteria and biomarkers like NGAL and Cystatin c, could help in the precise and early detection of AKI [4].

An additional point that needs consideration is the precision of the estimates, for instance, the adjusted relative risk (ARR) for mechanical ventilation > 2 days was reported as 2.3 with a confidence interval of 1.1–4.71 [1]. This wide confidence interval and marginal p values indicate low precision. Similarly, statistically significant link between CSA AKI and mortality is based on a small number of deaths (8 in total, with 7 in the CSA AKI group). This limited number of events may lead to an overinterpretation of the mortality association. A larger study sample would provide more accurate results.

In the study, although KDIGO staging of AKI was noted, the variation between the outcomes among different KDIGO stages was not analyzed. Machado and colleagues' study has associated stage severity with increased mortality reporting 30‐day mortality at 66% in patients with Stage 3 [5]. This was a fine‐grained analysis by AKI stages to determine the severity of outcomes, which this study failed to capture, which would have made it a better contribution and applicable to real clinical practice.

In addition to this, factors like renal replacement therapy (RRT) and RACHS1 score which measures the degree of surgical complexity and have been found to be a risk factor of AKI with a higher score predicting increased complications have not been included in the study as stated in a meta‐analysis by Van den Eynde et al. [2]. These factors are very influential in determining survival and thus their inclusion would have given a more realistic view of patient outcomes.

Finally, it can be mentioned that the review of nephrotoxic medications was simplified by using binary variables, which might not have been able to reflect exactly on the complexity of the problem. A wider perspective on these confounding factors, such as genetic predisposition, anesthesia procedures, infections, inotropes, pulmonary hypertension, and sepsis, that are more likely to contribute to the mortality outcomes [2], would have given a good perspective on the risk and outcome of AKI in this population.

Lastly, we would like to appreciate the contribution of the authors on the role toward learning more about CSA‐AKI in pediatric cardiac surgery, particularly in a resource‐limited environment. Although the article emphasizes relevant risk factors, identifying the limitations (including the use of age‐adjusted diagnostic criteria, consideration of further clinical parameters such as RACHS‐1 scores and RRT, and consideration of possible confounding factors such as sepsis) will make future research more robust.
