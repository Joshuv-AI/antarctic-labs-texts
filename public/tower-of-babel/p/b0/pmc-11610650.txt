Habitat structure and an introduced predator limit the abundance of an endangered ground‐nesting bird
Authors: David G. Parker, Matthew Cameron, Christopher E. Gordon, Mike Letnic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the factors that limit the abundance of threatened species is critical for the development of effective conservation strategies. However, gaining such knowledge from monitoring programs and using it to inform decision‐making for rare species can be difficult due to methodological issues posed by the problems of distinguishing true absences from false absences and the analysis of datasets dominated by zero counts. The plains‐wanderer (Pedionomus torquatus) is a critically endangered ground‐nesting bird that occurs in grasslands of southeastern Australia. Decline of the plains‐wanderer has been attributed to habitat modification but little emphasis has been placed on the role of introduced predators, such as the red fox (Vulpes vulpes), which have had a devastating effect on small ground‐dwelling vertebrates in dryland regions of Australia. Here, we use a 9‐year time series of spotlight counts to investigate the impact of vegetation structure and fox presence on plains‐wanderer occupancy and abundance. We used distance sampling to determine the effective strip width for sighting plains‐wanderers during spotlight surveys. We then used a hurdle model approach whereby binomial generalized additive models were fitted to presence/absence data within the effective strip‐width across all sites and negative‐binomial models were fitted to an index of abundance at sites where plains‐wanderers were observed. Plains‐wanderer occupancy and abundance fluctuated markedly through time. Where foxes were absent, occupancy (but not abundance) of plains‐wanderers showed a humped relationship with grass height with an optimal height between 50 and 150 mm. Where foxes were present however, this relationship broke down and plains‐wanderers were rarely recorded. Our results suggest that plains‐wanderers should benefit from management strategies that maintain grass height at optimal levels and exclude foxes or effectively suppress their populations. A key message from this study is that if statistical analyses of data generated by monitoring programs for rare species are intended to inform management decisions by identifying relationships between threatened species and drivers of their abundance, there should be consideration of analytic approaches that account for true and false zeroes, high prevalence of zeroes, and the possibility of nonlinear responses.

INTRODUCTION

The distribution and abundance of vertebrate fauna are determined by many factors, including climate, resource availability, and interactions with competitors, predators, and pathogens (Newton, 1998). Understanding which factors limit the distribution and abundance of threatened species, and how these factors interact, is critical to the development of effective conservation programs (Nichols & Williams, 2006; Scheele et al., 2018). The rarity of many threatened species makes obtaining this information difficult, and often requires intensive monitoring of the species and its environment over long periods (Scheele et al., 2018). Even so, the high prevalence of zero counts in monitoring datasets for rare and cryptic species may seriously constrain or prevent the use of inferential statistics to inform management decisions (Caley et al., 2022; Zuur et al., 2009). This is particularly likely to be a problem for species whose populations fluctuate markedly in response to seasonal conditions or whose population levels are critically affected by a threatening process (Gordon & Letnic, 2016; Moseby et al., 2019). This problem of too many zeroes is further compounded by the occurrence of “false zeroes” due to errors in sampling design or observer errors as opposed to “true zeroes” which occur because the organism being sampled was not present due to underlying ecological processes (Martin et al., 2005; Blasco‐Moreno et al., 2019). Thus, one major challenge for ecologists studying threatened species is distinguishing between false and true zeroes and then applying statistical approaches that can account for datasets with a large number of true zeroes (Blasco‐Moreno et al., 2019; Martin et al., 2005).

The plains‐wanderer Pedionomus torquatus (Figure 1a) is an endangered ground‐nesting bird that occurs in the sparsely vegetated plains of southeastern Australia (Figure 1b; Baker‐Gabb, 1988; Parker et al., 2021). Plains‐wanderers eat seeds and insects and spend almost all of their time on the ground (Baker‐Gabb, 1988). Plains‐wanderer nests typically consist of a shallow scrape in the ground nestled in or at the edge of vegetation, particularly grasses (Antos & Schultz, 2020). The distribution of the plains‐wanderer has contracted significantly since British colonization and has been attributed to widespread clearing and pasture improvement (Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016; Bennett, 1983; Llewellyn, 1975). Today, the species' strongholds are grasslands in the Riverina region of New South Wales (NSW) and the Northern Plains region of Victoria (Anonymous, 2016a). Within these regions, plains‐wanderer populations fluctuate markedly through time due to variations in habitat conditions (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Green, et al., 2022).

(A) Plains‐wanderer and (B) typical plains‐wanderer habitat in the Riverina region of southwestern New South Wales. Photographs by David G. Parker.

Current knowledge suggests that plains‐wanderers have specialized habitat requirements and avoid grasslands that are too sparse/short or too dense/tall or have too much litter cover (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016; Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Green, et al., 2022; Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Leonard, & Morgan, 2022). Accordingly, declines of plains‐wanderers have been attributed to interactions between climatic conditions and livestock grazing pressure affecting the suitability of their grassland habitat (Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016). Extended dry periods and associated overgrazing are hypothesized to result in vegetation becoming too short and sparse for plains‐wanderers, while during wet periods plains‐wanderers are hypothesized to avoid vegetation that is too tall and dense (Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016).

Introduced predators, such as the red fox (Vulpes vulpes) and feral cat (Felis catus), have had devastating impacts on small ground‐dwelling vertebrates in dryland regions of Australia (Johnson, 2006). Red foxes (Vulpes vulpes) are particularly common in the grasslands of the Riverina (Lunney et al., 2021) and have been implicated as a factor limiting nesting success, survival, and the abundance of several species of ground‐nesting birds in Australia (Gates & Paton, 2005; Gordon et al., 2017; Magrath et al., 2003; Marlow & Croft, 2016; Priddel & Wheeler, 1997). Anecdotal observations suggest that adult plains‐wanderers are particularly susceptible to predation by foxes which are a common predator in the grassland habitats of plains‐wanderers (D'Ombrain, 1926; Llewellyn, 1975); however, no empirical evidence exists linking trends in the abundance or mortality of plains‐wanderer populations to the abundance or occurrence of foxes. Further, due to plains‐wanderers' dependence on grasslands with specific structural characteristics (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Leonard, & Morgan, 2022), it is possible that vegetation structure may moderate the strength of predatory interactions with foxes. For example, it is conceivable that it may be easier for foxes to locate the nests of plains‐wanderers or capture plains‐wanderers when grass height is low (Laux et al., 2022).

One major challenge constraining the use of inferential statistics to inform decisions for the management of plains‐wanderers is the large number of zero counts in monitoring datasets (Antos & Schultz, 2020). The most widely used approach to monitor plains‐wanderer populations is to conduct nocturnal counts from a vehicle using a spotlight and more recently a thermal scope to detect birds (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Rowe et al., 2023). However, often no birds are sighted on sampling transects (Antos & Schultz, 2020). This poses a problem for data analysis for two reasons. First, it is unknown whether zero counts occur because the birds were not present (true zeroes) due to population fluctuations or because they were there but not detected during surveys (false zeroes). Second, the large numbers of zeroes in monitoring datasets prevent or constrain statistical analyses to identify the factors which drive their occupancy and abundance in the environment (Blasco‐Moreno et al., 2019; Martin et al., 2005).

The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of grassland structure and fox numbers on plains‐wanderer abundance, and the interaction between these variables using a 9‐year time series of spotlight surveys of plains‐wanderers and foxes from the Riverina region of NSW. We predicted that plains‐wanderer occurrence probability and abundance would be highest at low to intermediate grass heights due to their preference for relatively sparse grasslands (Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Green, et al., 2022) and would decrease with increasing fox activity due to increasing rates of fox predation. We also predicted that the relationship between plains‐wanderer occurrence/abundance and grass height would be moderated by fox activity because predation by foxes would reduce the number of birds available to occupy suitable habitat. In our analyses, we used a hierarchical approach to deal with the large number of zero counts of plains‐wanderers in the dataset. First, to increase our confidence that birds were not missed during sampling we used distance sampling to determine the effective strip‐width within which we had a high (≥75%) probability of sighting plains‐wanderers if they were present. Second, we used a hurdle model approach to analyze counts of birds within the effective strip‐width whereby binomial models were initially fitted to the entire dataset to determine whether the probability of observing a plains‐wanderer was impacted by the predictor variables. Negative binomial models (to account for overdispersion in the count data) were then fitted to a subset of the sampling transects where plains‐wanderers were observed to determine whether counts of plains‐wanderers were affected by the predictor variables.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area

This study was undertaken across native grasslands of the Riverine Plain bioregion in south‐western NSW (Figure 2). Here, the dry semiarid climate is characterized by hot summers and cool winters, with winter‐dominant mean annual rainfall ranging from 300 mm in the west to 450 mm in the east (Stern et al., 2000). Red‐brown and gray clays supporting native grasslands dominate the landscape (Porteners, 1993). The sparse grasslands favored by plains‐wanderers occur on exposed hard red‐brown clay subsoil and are characterized by a mix of perennial grasses (Rytidosperma spp., Austrostipa spp., Chloris spp., and Enteropogon spp.), forbs (Calotis spp., Chrysocephalum spp., Leptorhynchos spp., Minuria spp., Ptilotis spp., Rhodanthe spp., and Swainsona spp.), and small shrubs (Atriplex spp. and Maireana spp.) (Benson et al., 2010; McDougall, 2008).

Map of the study region showing the locations of the study sites, the towns of Hay and Deniliquin, and Oolambeyan National Park (shaded green). The inset shows the location of the study region within Australia.

Field surveys were conducted at 16 sites between October 2014 and September 2022, distributed across the region (Figure 2). Survey sites were located based on the presence of suitable grassland habitat (Porteners, 1993), historical information on plains‐wanderer occurrence (Llewellyn, 1975), and landholder consent. Sites were situated on private pastoral lands, national parks, traveling stock routes, and Commonwealth Department of Defence land.

Sampling protocol

Spotlight surveys for plains‐wanderers and foxes were conducted one to three times per year (autumn, winter, spring) at each site giving a total of 249 surveys. In some seasons, it was not possible to conduct surveys because of wet conditions. The surveys were conducted on permanently marked grids, with parallel grid‐lines spaced 50 m apart. Vehicle‐based nocturnal surveys of spotlight grids were conducted by following the grid‐lines in slow‐moving (5–6 km/h) vehicles with an observer searching for plains‐wanderers and foxes with a handheld 75‐W Lightforce spotlight. The same observer (DP) conducted all surveys. When detected, the perpendicular distance from the survey vehicle to the plains‐wanderer or fox was estimated visually. The total transect length varied between sites dependent on the extent and spatial arrangement of suitable plains‐wanderer habitat (mean 15.13 ± SD 3.52 km length: range 7–22 km length).

Distance sampling was used to identify the effective strip width within which we had a high (≥75%) probability of sighting plains‐wanderers and could be confident that zero counts represented true zeroes (Miller et al., 2019). Specifically, we used a detection function with a half‐normal distribution to identify the distance from the survey vehicle when plains‐wanderer detectability reached 75%. This assessment showed that plains‐wanderer detection probability decreased with distance from vehicle, with the 75% detectability threshold reached at a distance of 5.2 m from the survey vehicle (Appendix S1: Figure S1). Given this, we truncated the plains‐wanderer dataset to only include observations ≤5 m from the survey vehicle. This truncated dataset was then used to calculate plains‐wanderer relative abundance within each survey transect (n = 247). To account for differences in line‐transect length across sites, plains‐wanderer observations were standardized to represent the number of observations per 100 km during each sampling transect.

Fox relative abundance was calculated on each transect as the number of observations per 100 km. Distance sampling was not used because foxes were easily detected within the open grassland habitat. Fox relative abundance provided a qualitative tool to visualize temporal differences in fox activity across the survey period (Figure 3). However, we used fox presence/absence in our statistical analyses (see below). This was because the relatively small size of the survey transects may not have allowed a representative estimate of fox relative abundance, given their large home ranges.

We measured grass height at each site immediately following each spotlight survey. Maximum grass height was chosen as a surrogate for habitat structure because it is easy to measure and is known to influence plains‐wanderer habitat use (Antos & Schultz, 2020). Maximum grass height was assessed by measuring the maximum modal height (90th percentile of grass heights) of all grasses within 1 × 1 m quadrats at 200‐m intervals on each transect. Mean maximum grass height was then calculated by averaging maximum grass heights across all quadrats for each transect. Further, we conducted an exploratory generalized additive model, which showed that rainfall within the previous 1 and 3 months was a significant predictor of mean maximum grass height across all study transects accounting for 62% of the variance with the model (Appendix S1: Figure S2). This suggests that mean maximum grass height was a good proxy for primary productivity across the study period and region.

Statistical analyses

The first step in our analyses was to plot the time series for indices of plains‐wanderer occupancy and abundance, fox activity, and mean maximum grass height. Generalized additive models were subsequently used to determine whether mean maximum grass height and fox presence/absence influenced plains‐wanderer occurrence and relative abundance. Due to a prevalence of zeros within the plains‐wanderer dataset (plains‐wanderers were only detected on 16% of sampling transects) and subsequent issues in fitting zero‐inflated datasets (Mullahy, 1986; Zuur et al., 2009), we used a “hurdle model” approach for model inference. Binomial models were first fitted to the entire dataset (n = 249) to determine whether the probability of observing a plains‐wanderer was impacted by the predictor variables. Zero‐truncated negative binomial models (to account for overdispersion in the count data) were then fitted to a subset of the sampling transects where plains‐wanderers were observed (n = 41) to determine whether plains‐wanderer relative abundance was impacted by the predictor variables.

To allow us to investigate the effects of foxes on plains‐wanderers, we fitted four separate models exploring the additive and then interacting effects of mean maximum grass height and fox relative abundance on plains‐wanderer occurrence and relative abundance: (1) Additive effects fox presence and grass height on plains‐wanderer presence, (2) interacting effects of fox presence and grass height on plains‐wanderer presence, (3) additive effects of fox presence and grass height on plains‐wanderer relative abundance for surveys when plains‐wanderers were observed, (4) interacting effects of fox presence and grass height on plains‐wanderer relative abundance for surveys when plains‐wanderers were observed. Geographic location (interaction between Northing and Easting) was included as a fixed‐factor in all models to account for potential spatial autocorrelation.

Mean maximum grass height was treated as a continuous fixed‐factor and fit using a spline smoother term (knots = 9), and fox presence/absence was treated as a categorical fixed‐factor. The interaction between mean maximum grass height and fox presence/absence was fit using a tensor smoother term, whereby different smooth terms were fit where foxes were present and absent. Geographic location was treated as a continuous variable using a tensor smoother term (knots = 24). Statistical significance was assessed using Wald's χ2 values (continuous variables with smoother terms), model coefficient estimates (categorical variables with non‐smoother terms) and associated p‐values from the model outputs (alpha = 0.05). Model fit was assessed using deviance explained. All models were fit in R using the “mgcv” package (Wood, 2011). Moran I tests were conducted to identify potential spatial autocorrelation in the residuals of each model using the “ape” package in R (Paradis & Schliep, 2019).

We visualized the effects of significant predictor variables within the generalized additive models using model predictions. To relativize the model predictions within the results section, we describe how predicted plains‐wanderer probability and relative abundance varies within the lower 20th percentile, central, and upper 20th percentile of the mean maximum grass height site values.

Vehicle‐based spotlight surveys have long been used to survey for this cryptic species (Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016); however, a limitation of the survey methodology is that it focuses on the nocturnal habitats used by the species and therefore may not necessarily reflect their daytime habitat. In an assessment of multi‐scale habitat selection by the plains‐wanderer, Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Leonard, and Morgan (2022) observed GPS‐tracked individuals foraging during the daytime within denser swards than those used by the same individuals for nocturnal roosting. In our analyses, we treat all sampled quadrats as being potential foraging and/or roosting habitat and therefore do not separate them into a specific suitability category to the species.

RESULTS

Time series plots of plains‐wanderers, foxes, and mean maximum grass height

Plains‐wanderer relative abundance, mean maximum grass height, and fox relative abundance varied markedly across the sampling period (Figure 3). Across all sampling transects, plains‐wanderers and foxes were observed on 16.45% (mean 3.37 ± SD 11.14 observations per 100 km) and 8.87% (mean 1.79 ± SD 5.67 observations per 100 km) of transects, respectively. Feral cats, another potential predator of plains‐wanderers, were only observed on one sampling occasion. On sampling transects where they were observed, the relative abundance of plains‐wanderers and fox activity was, on average, 20.48 (±SD 20.28) and 9.49 (±SD 9.95) observations per 100 km, respectively. On average, mean maximum grass height was 165.36 mm (±SD 167.60) across all transects, 162.43 mm (±SD 161.38) on transects where plains‐wanderers were observed and 193.33 mm (±SD 170.99) on transects where foxes were observed.

Inclusive of both mean maximum grass height and fox activity (i.e., the interactive effect of foxes and grass height), the overall predicted probability of observing a plains‐wanderer was higher on surveys where foxes were absent (average 0.17 ± SD 0.22) than present (average 0.13 ± SD 0.16; Figure 4b).

When foxes were absent (Figure 4b), the predicted probability of observing a plains‐wanderer was highest at intermediate mean maximum grass heights, with detection probabilities of 0.03 (±SD 0.07) within the lower 20th percentile of mean maximum grass heights (12.60–40.69 mm), 0.32 (±SD 0.30) within the central 20th percentile of mean maximum grass heights (72.40–132.69 mm), and 0.13 (±SD 0.07) within the upper 20th percentile of mean maximum grass heights (251.66–893.18 mm). When foxes were present (Figure 4b), the predicted probability of observing a plains‐wanderer was not affected by mean maximum grass heights, with an average detection probability of 0.13 (±SD 0.16).

Both mean maximum grass height and fox presence/absence were nonsignificant predictors of plains‐wanderer relative abundance (Table 1c,d). Spatial autocorrelation was not observed in the residuals of the generalized additive models (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Our analysis of 9 years of monitoring showed that plains‐wanderer occurrence fluctuated markedly through time in response to variation in grass height. Our modeling suggests that the presence of plains‐wanderers was driven by nonlinear relationships with grass height and that fox activity decoupled the relationship between grass height and plains‐wanderer occupancy. Where foxes were absent, the occupancy of plains‐wanderers showed a humped relationship with grass height with an optimal height between 50 and 150 mm. However, where foxes were present this relationship broke down and plains‐wanderers were rarely recorded even when grass height was optimal. The differing responses observed in our analyses of presence/absence and relative abundance analyses highlight the importance of using appropriate methods when analyzing zero‐inflated datasets, which are common for rare and threatened species.

Our analyses of the factors influencing plains‐wanderer occupancy/abundance emphasize a key issue for generating model‐based inferences from data collected for many threatened species monitoring programs, the high frequency of zero counts. In the case of plains‐wanderers, zero counts occur frequently because the birds are usually rare and are also difficult to observe. The binomial (presence/absence) models that we used in the first part of our hurdle model provided evidence that the probability of sighting plains‐wanderers was greater when grass height was low and lower when foxes were present. However, the continuous part of the model, which was restricted to a relatively small (n = 41) number of sampling occasions when plains‐wanderers were present, yielded no significant results. We speculate that the poor predictive power of the continuous models was due to the small sample size for this analysis (Zuur et al., 2009), but acknowledge that it remains possible that the poor predictive power of the continuous models was because another process that was not considered in our models may have driven the variation in plains‐wanderer abundance. We recommend that further research is conducted to understand the factors driving variation in the abundance of plains‐wanderers.

Despite the discrepancy between the first and second parts of the hurdle model, we are confident that the results of the binomial models are insightful for two reasons. First, we are confident that absences were predominantly true zeroes because we restricted our analyses to observations within the effective strip where there was a high probability of sighting birds if they were present. Second, the relationship between plains‐wanderer occupancy and grass height is consistent with previous studies, which have found that plains‐wanderers prefer sparsely vegetated grasslands (Antos & Schultz, 2020). Placed in the context of threatening species monitoring programs, our results are instructive because they demonstrate the importance of using analytical approaches that reduce the number of false zeroes and can accommodate large numbers of true zeroes in order to identify the set of minimal environmental conditions required for a species to be present (Martin et al., 2005).

The occupancy of plains‐wanderers fluctuated markedly through time and showed a humped relationship with maximum grass height such that plains‐wanderers were rarely recorded when grass height was below 50 mm or above 150 mm (Figure 4). This finding is consistent with research showing that plains‐wanderer populations fluctuate markedly through time and that these birds are rarely observed in nocturnal surveys in grasslands with dense swards or where vegetation is extremely sparse (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016). Because of their distinct preference for relatively sparse grasslands, in some areas, the grassland habitat of plains‐wanderers is actively managed by grazing sheep to ensure that the sward does not become too dense or tall for plains‐wanderers (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016). However, there is also a risk that too much grazing by livestock and kangaroos, particularly during dry periods, may make grasslands unsuitable for plains‐wanderers by making the vegetation too short or too sparse (Mills et al., 2020).

The fluctuations in grass height that we observed were correlated with rainfall (Appendix S1: Figure S2) and were also likely to be influenced by grazing intensity (Mills et al., 2020). Previous authors have speculated that plains‐wanderers have such specific preferences for grassland structure because they require specific food resources or grasslands where the habitat structure is suitable, providing them with optimal shelter from predators and protection for nest sites (Baker‐Gabb, 1993; Baker‐Gabb et al., 1990; Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016). The latter hypothesis is supported by fine‐scale studies of the movements of plain‐wanderers, which show that birds will use grasslands with denser swards during the daytime than at night (Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Green, et al., 2022). In common with the plains‐wanderer, many other grassland birds display strong preferences for grasslands with specific vegetation structural characteristics (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Baines, 1996; Fisher & Davis, 2010; Winter et al., 2005).

A key finding of our study was that the probability of observing a plains‐wanderer was higher in areas where foxes were not detected; however, fox activity alone was a nonsignificant predictor. Our interpretation of this finding is that plains‐wanderers have a strong preference for habitats with intermediate grass height, and that high levels of fox activity decouple the humped relationship between plains‐wanderers and grass height. A plausible explanation for this decoupling is that foxes suppress the abundance of plains‐wanderers by preying on birds or their nests. That plains‐wanderers may be particularly susceptible to predation by foxes is supported by observations that plains‐wanderers rarely take flight when disturbed and that when they do, they fly only a short distance before landing and freezing to avoid subsequent detection (D Parker personal observation; Llewellyn, 1975). It is also not unusual for them to be caught by domestic hunting dogs (D'Ombrain, 1926; Sturt, 1849). While our analyses focused on the relationship between the occurrences of plains‐wanderers and foxes, it is pertinent to note that other potential predators of plains‐wanderers including feral cats, diurnal birds of prey, and owls also occur in the grasslands of the Riverina region of NSW. We recommend that further studies are conducted to identify predators of plains‐wanderers and the effects that they have on plains‐wanderer occupancy and abundance.

Although it is widely appreciated that red foxes in Australia prey on birds, particularly ground‐nesting birds (Gordon et al., 2017; Magrath et al., 2003; Marlow & Croft, 2016) and our models reveal a negative association between foxes and plains‐wanderers, there is no empirical evidence that foxes are significant predators of plains‐wanderers. Consequently, current management strategies for plains‐wanderers place little emphasis on fox management and focus primarily on maintaining appropriate habitat structure for plains‐wanderers (Anonymous, 2016a, 2016b). However, our modeling suggests that effectively suppressing fox activity is a strategy that could increase plains‐wanderer occupancy and abundance in situations when grassland habitat is optimal for plains‐wanderers. This hypothesis could be tested by experimentally excluding or suppressing fox populations in areas with suitable habitat for plains‐wanderers and monitoring the survival and population‐level responses of plains‐wanderers at experimental sites and sites with similar habitat attributes where fox numbers are not controlled.

Our study has important implications for conservation efforts for the plains‐wanderer. Our finding that there was a humped relationship between grass height and plains‐wanderer occupancy provides support for the idea that plains‐wanderers have a strict preference for grasslands that are not too tall and dense or too short and sparse (Antos & Schultz, 2020; Baker‐Gabb et al., 2016). It follows then that reducing the height and density of the sward by carefully managing the numbers of livestock within paddocks may be a useful strategy to optimize habitat structure for plains‐wanderers (Nugent, Baker‐Gabb, Leonard, & Morgan, 2022). In addition, our finding that the high levels of fox activity appeared to decouple the relationship between plains‐wanderers and grass height suggests that excluding foxes or effectively suppressing their populations is a management action that could also make grassland habitats more suitable for plains‐wanderers.

More broadly, our study has implications for thinking about the management and monitoring programs for rare and cryptic species. For many species, it is not possible to conduct controlled manipulative studies to investigate factors limiting their abundances. In such cases, we rely upon inferences made from correlative datasets, which examine the statistical relationships between the species of concern and hypothesized predictor variables. However, drawing useful conclusions to guide management actions from such correlative approaches can be difficult, particularly if the species of interest is rarely detected, their populations are influenced by multiple drivers, and the relationships with those drivers are nonlinear (Caley et al., 2022). This is particularly likely to be the case for species threatened by introduced predators where the functional relationships between the abundances of predators and their prey species are often characterized by threshold‐type relationships whereby prey density increases rapidly below a breakpoint predator density (Gordon & Letnic, 2016; Moseby et al., 2019). In such cases, traditional statistics which assume linear relationships between predictor and response variables may erroneously suggest that there is no numerical relationship between the abundances of introduced predators and their prey because they rarely if ever coexist. Similarly, predator suppression experiments that do not reduce predator populations below breakpoint densities may result in false rejection of the hypothesis that predators reduce prey abundance. In such instances, monitoring programs must have high levels of replication and consider using modeling approaches that explicitly account for the high prevalence of zeroes (e.g., hurdle models, zero‐inflated regression) and nonlinear responses to identify the relationships between threatened species and drivers of their abundance.
