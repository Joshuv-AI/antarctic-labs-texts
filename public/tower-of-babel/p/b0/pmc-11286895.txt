Developing a fair and interpretable representation of the clock drawing test for mitigating low education and racial bias
Authors: Jiaqing Zhang, Sabyasachi Bandyopadhyay, Faith Kimmet, Jack Wittmayer, Kia Khezeli, David J Libon, Catherine C Price, Parisa Rashidi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe clock drawing test (CDT) is a neuropsychological assessment tool to screen an individual’s cognitive ability. In this study, we developed a Fair and Interpretable Representation of Clock drawing test (FaIRClocks) to evaluate and mitigate classification bias against people with less than 8 years of education, while screening their cognitive function using an array of neuropsychological measures. In this study, we represented clock drawings by a priorly published 10-dimensional deep learning feature set trained on publicly available data from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS). These embeddings were further fine-tuned with clocks from a preoperative cognitive screening program at the University of Florida to predict three cognitive scores: the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) total score, an attention composite z-score (ATT-C), and a memory composite z-score (MEM-C). ATT-C and MEM-C scores were developed by averaging z-scores based on normative references. The cognitive screening classifiers were initially tested to see their relative performance in patients with low years of education (< = 8 years) versus patients with higher education (> 8 years) and race. Results indicated that the initial unweighted classifiers confounded lower education with cognitive compromise resulting in a 100% type I error rate for this group. Thereby, the samples were re-weighted using multiple fairness metrics to achieve sensitivity/specificity and positive/negative predictive value (PPV/NPV) balance across groups. In summary, we report the FaIRClocks model, with promise to help identify and mitigate bias against people with less than 8 years of education during preoperative cognitive screening.Keywords: AI Fairness, Semi-supervised deep learning, Relevance factor variational autoencoder, Mini-mental state examination, Attention, MemorySubject terms: Neurological disorders, Cognitive neuroscience, Attention, Short-term memory, Working memory, Alzheimer's disease, Computer science

Introduction

Dementia is a growing concern in today’s aging population. Today, more than 6 million Americans of all ages are living with Alzheimer’s disease1. Alzheimer’s is one of the leading causes of death in the US, costing more than 345 billion dollars yearly for treatment2. Therefore, early detection of dementia risk and monitoring of cognitive health in older adults is an essential area of interest. For this reason, cognitive screening is increasing in primary care settings but, is also encouraged in less traditional settings such as preoperative anesthesia clinics prior to surgery3,4.

The clock drawing test (CDT) is a widely recognized and simple screening tool used to evaluate different aspects of cognitive function, including attention, memory, and executive function5,6. The traditional CDT consists of two parts: (a) the command test condition, where the patient is required to “draw the face of a clock, put in all the numbers, and set the hands to ten after eleven,” followed by (b) the copy test condition, where the patient is required to copy a model clock6. The command condition assesses a person’s ability to understand and execute three commands requiring intact comprehension, working memory, semantic knowledge, inhibitory function, visuoconstruction, and planning abilities6,7. In contrast, the copy condition primarily assesses visuospatial skills and planning6–8. The combination of these two conditions has proven to be beneficial in diagnosing dementia and mild cognitive impairment6,8–10. Research also suggests that the differential cognitive demand of the drawing conditions provides unique insight into older adults’ postoperative risk11,12.

There is value in understanding how years of formal education contribute to screening performance. First, years of formal education are part of estimating cognitive developmental resources. Second, education is considered a proxy for ‘cognitive reserve’, as individuals with more years of education are repeatedly shown to be more resilient to neurological insult and neurodegenerative illness13–17. Third, total years of formal education is also a well-known predictor of postoperative cognitive decline and postoperative hospital visits18–20. Consequently, investigating the possible biasing impact of education when applying cognitive screening tests on low sample size populations is critical. This is particularly relevant in older patients because they generally present with fewer years of formal education relative to younger patients21,22.

Past literature indicates that cognitive test performance is affected by age, education level, race, and ethnicity23. A systematic review showed that higher levels of education positively affected clock drawing performance, while several studies suggest that illiteracy hindered optimal clock drawing construction24. Individuals with fewer years of education tend to exhibit more digit misplacement errors and increased omission of clock hands25,26. Large normative publications assessing features of clock drawing, however, are based on individuals with at least eighth grade or above27,28. It is unknown how individuals with only elementary or middle school education perform on clock drawing. This is concerning largely because older individuals with fewer than high school years are present in medical settings among those acquiring cognitive screening12. In the current research, we address the question of education and racial bias in cognitive classifiers developed on newly published deep learning (DL) representations of clock drawing centered on the US sociocultural landscape29.

In recent years, machine learning, especially deep learning, has become a burgeoning tool in cognitive health research. The deep convolutional neural network (CNN) has proved effective for scoring CDT30,31. A few studies have also used a segmentation CNN model to extract sub-features from patients’ clock drawings, including clockface, digits, and hands; another CNN model was employed to separately score the segmented outcomes32,33. However, these models require large, high-quality annotated clock drawing datasets, which are time- and labor-intensive to obtain. Additionally, the black-box nature of DL models curtails their interpretability. With the rapid advancement of computer vision, self-supervised deep generative models can be trained to create deep representations of clock drawings, that are robust, interpretable, and broadly applicable to different downstream classification tasks.

Previously, we have published a semi-supervised variational autoencoder (VAE) method to represent clock drawings using a compressed two-dimensional feature vector. This representation was encoded within an interpretable two-dimensional latent space that could classify clocks from patients with and without dementia with moderate performance34. In another study, we utilized the Relevance Factor Variational Autoencoder (RF-VAE)35 model to discover a complete set of ten clock drawing graphomotor anomalies, resulting in significantly improved performance in classifying dementia patients29.

We followed our prior work29 in this study by pretraining a RF-VAE model on a large, unlabeled, publicly available dataset from the National Health and Ageing Trends Study (NHATS); and subsequently fine-tuned the model using a dataset collected by the University of Florida and Shands Hospital as part of a federal investigation and a preoperative clinical program involving neuropsychology36–38. In this study, we created Fair and Interpretable Representations of CDT (FaIRClocks) for rarely studied cognitive outcomes such as attention and memory deficits. Specifically, we created three different classifiers for predicting the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) total score, an attention composite (ATT-C), and a memory composite (MEM-C). The ATT-C and MEM-C scores were developed by averaging standardized z-scores based on published neuropsychological age and education normative references. We then analyzed classification bias against people presenting less than 8 years formal education and Black and Hispanic groups. We mitigated the classifiers’ bias using IBM’s AI Fairness Toolkit39. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis40 was used to understand the change in the relative importance of relevant clock drawing features in these classifiers before and after bias mitigation. RF-VAE-based latent representation of the NHATS dataset

Figure 1 shows the reconstruction of the ten disentangled features in the latent space learned by RF-VAE using the NHATS dataset. The X-axis shows our definition of the ten features, i.e., size, vertical displacement of hands, rotated clockface, loss of digits, ellipse, ovate, angle between hands, side bulge, rotation of hand assembly, and square-rhomboid clockface. The Y-axis is the value of latent variables, which spans a range from − 3 to 3. Column A shows the change in the brightness of the decoded clock drawings, which reflects the size of the original clock drawings. This is because all clock drawings are resized to 64 × 64 pixels, causing the larger clocks to lose some textural information compared to the smaller clocks. Column B shows the vertical displacement of hands as the latent value increases. Column C shows the rotation of the clockface. This is an artifact of the presence of rotated clock images within the NHATS dataset. Column D indicates the loss of digits on the clock face as this latent dimension increases. Column E shows a change in the degree and the direction of the eccentricity of a clockface. Column F shows a shift in the degree and direction of the ovate/obovate (leaf-shaped) clockfaces. Column G shows a decrease in the angle between the clock hands as this latent dimension increases. Column H shows the existence of a vertical asymmetry or a side-bulge in the clockface. The degree and orientation of this bulge changes along this latent dimension. Column I show the rotation of the clock hands’ assembly. Increase of this latent dimension indicate a clockwise rotation in their hand assembly. Column J shows the existence of non-circular clocks. With higher values along this latent dimension, the clock shape tends to be rhomboid. In contrast, lower values result in a square shape. These describe the 10 latent features discovered by the RF-VAE network on the NHATS dataset.

During the training phase, the RF-VAE model derives a 10D latent representation from the clock drawings in the NHATS dataset. (a) Displayed are the reconstructed clock drawings corresponding to each latent variable. Columns denote individual latent dimensions, ranging from − 3 (top) to + 3 (bottom). (b) Interpretations for each latent space traversal are provided. The distinct constructional attributes of the clock drawings, which are most representative of the traversal of reconstructed clocks across a specific latent dimension, serve to characterize each latent dimension.

An identical RF-VAE network was previously trained on a dataset comprising 23,521 clock drawings collected through the UF and the UF Health Shands hospital system. This RF-VAE described in Bandyopadhyay et.al.29 had converged upon a similar group of features, namely: size, obovate, prolate-oblate (i.e., flattened or elongated clockfaces), vertical displacement of hands, eccentricity of ellipsoidal clockfaces, angle between hands, square-rhomboid clockfaces, ellipsoidal clockfaces in a reversed direction, side bulge and rotation of the hand assembly. In our current study, the only additional feature we found is the dimension encoding a loss of digits on the clock face. However, this feature was encoded in a combined manner with the vertical displacement of hands in the previously published study29. In Supplementary Table 1, we have provided an analysis of performance comparisons between classifiers constructed using the previously published RF-VAE encoder and the one developed in this study.

Fairness of performance analysis

To measure the classification bias in model prediction on different demographic sub-groups, we evaluated the performance of the best model for each task on each of the subgroups, as shown in Table 2 and Table 3. We considered different demographic subgroups such as race: White/Black, ethnicity: Hispanic/Non-Hispanic, and individuals with low education (less than or equal to 8 years of education) compared to those with high education (more than 8 years of education). We detected classification bias for patients with lower education in the task of MMSE and ATT-C classification. The “best” model predicted all clocks drawn by individuals with lower education to be characteristic of cognitive compromise as defined by the MMSE total score or ATT-C standardized cut-score score. This was the case in both command and copy condition CDTs. This bias, depicted in Fig. 2, represents a situation where the sensitivity value of the classifier for the low-education group is equal to 1, while its specificity value is equal to 0, thus creating a 100% type I error rate. We conducted a related samples t-test to test the difference in performance before and after reweighting the classifiers (shown in Supplementary Table 4). The p-values for both sensitivity and specificity suggest a significant difference in performance before and after bias mitigation through reweighting, observed in both the low-education and high-education groups. Bias related to education was not detected when predicting MEM-C, as shown in Fig. 2e. However, this model was unfair towards Black patients compared with White patients in classifying cognitive compromise as defined by MEM-C scores, which is shown in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S1.

In general, overall classifiers, bias mitigation reduced performances by an average of 0.03 on the area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC) for each task, shown in Table 2 and Table 3 (check-marked row), while the fairness metrics: 1-min(DI, 1/DI), and average odds different value decreased. For the low-education cohort, after bias removal, the balance between sensitivity and specificity improved significantly, as shown by the bar plots in blue color in Fig. 2. Bias removal did not significantly impact the sensitivity and specificity balance in the high-education sub-group as can be seen in the orange bar with hatch (‘//’), and is further indicated by t-test results shown in Supplementary Table 4. These results are common across MMSE and ATT-C classification. We also provided the positive predictive value (PPV) and the negative predictive value (NPV) which reflect the rate of true and false positives over the low- and high-education group. We observed NPV = 0 across MMSE and ATT-C for the patients with less than 8 years of education as shown in Fig. 3. After incorporating the reweighting function, we achieved higher NPV and PPV without hurting or even improving the performance of high-education group. Further analysis of the sensitivity of the model has been done to prove the bias is not coming from data unbalance, as shown in Supplementary Figure S2 and Figure S3. We reported sensitivity and specificity balance and PPV and NPV balance respectively. They showed that the performance wasn’t affected by the number of samples when using different cutpoints.

Discussion

In this study, we developed fair machine learning classifiers, FaIRClocks capable of predicting a global cognitive score commonly used in medical centers, and age corrected composites of attention and memory indices. Our first aim was to explore model performance for older adults with less than or equal to 8th grade years of education relative to greater than 8th grade education. A sub-aim was to explore the additional bias of race and ethnicity. A compressed deep generative representation of the CDT was developed using a large-scale publicly available dataset for this purpose.

A comparison between the previously published RF-VAE latent space29 using 23,521 clocks with the one developed in this study using 54,027 publicly available unannotated clocks reveals a broad similarity between the two latent spaces. However, the current latent space captured (a) the absence of digits and (b) the rotation of the clockface as independent features. This results from the underlying differences in clock drawings present in the two datasets. ML classifiers performed better in all 3 tasks using the NHATS embedding than the previously published embedding. This shows that the NHATS embedding, which is developed using a larger number of clocks, is decidedly better at capturing nuanced features, which were necessary for differentiating between low and high attention and memory composite Z-scores.

Clock drawing conditions differentially predicted attention and memory domain difficulties in the cohort. Copy drawings were more predictive of attention domain difficulties. Command drawings were more predictive of selective memory domain difficulties. Since MMSE is a composite attention and memory measure, the concatenated copy and command embeddings were the most predictive dataset for distinguishing between high and low MMSE scores. Prior literature supports our findings of differences between command and copy conditions relative to attention and memory functions5,41.

The most significant finding of this study is the bias correction for patients with less than eight years of education in the MMSE and attention composite prediction. While being tested separately, it was revealed that specificity = 0, sensitivity = 1, and NPV = 0 in the uncorrected ML classifier for lower education patients. This finding shows that regardless of their cognitive condition, the original, uncorrected classifier consistently classified people with low education as cognitively impaired using a standardized cut-score. Previous studies have reported the convergence between lack of education and cognitive impairment. Still, no studies have tested the sensitivity/specificity or PPV/NPV balance of commonly used CDT scoring mechanisms for education < = 8 years. Reweighting methods available in the IBM AIF360 toolbox allowed us to correct these classifiers such that they could achieve comparable sensitivity/specificity and PPV/NPV balance between low- and high-education patients, albeit with a marginal decrease in performance. This is the first study to evaluate the difficulty in measuring attention deficits through the CDT for older adults with less than or equal to eight years of education and to suggest it is feasible to mitigate the resulting bias using computational means. An additional consideration for race and ethnicity with the model was unfair towards patients identified as Black compared with White patients in classifying compromise defined by MEM-C scores.

Our study has several unique strengths, making it a pioneering work in bi-directional AI using cognitive science. Firstly, we developed the largest DCT model to date using clock drawings test images from NHATS and UF datasets for self-supervised pre-training of a generative model to represent the CDT and fine-tuning. Secondly, the generalized and interpretable latent space of the RF-VAE combined with retrospective SHAP analysis enabled us to investigate the relative importance of each latent dimension in predicting attention versus memory deficits in the unprivileged group versus the privileged group, thereby improving the interpretability and trustworthiness of our method. Thirdly, the ability of the RF-VAE latent space to compress a clock drawing into a ten-dimensional latent vector allowed us to use relatively smaller fine-tuning datasets to train lightweight task-specific ML classifiers downstream. Finally, the finding of high false-positive rates in the attention composite for individuals with lower education should stimulate research evaluating the psychometrics of cognitive assessment for this underrepresented patient population. Furthermore, it should also stimulate discussion in the domain of neuropsychology towards developing a clock drawing test better suited for this unique population.

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, all clock drawings had to be resized to 64X64, which invariably caused obfuscation of some salient features (e.g., digit shape, arrowheads, ticks) used in traditional clock scoring methodologies. This explains the absence of these variables from the RF-VAE latent space. Secondly, the moderate performance of the classifiers shows that a lower-dimensional CDT representation alone is moderately predictive of attention/memory deficits. The addition of total clock scoring time, or total number of strokes improved the performance (Supplementary Table 3) which proves that additional features available from the dCDT contain non-redundant information over and above what might be available solely from the final output clock image. However, due to the absence of the total clock drawing time for the copy condition, in this current study, we were not able to incorporate such features. The extremely low proportion of low-education patients in our dataset (36/840, i.e., 4%) and the total number of samples also restricts the generalizability of the findings. However, this further proves that collecting granular datasets of CDT and other cognitive measures for this vulnerable patient population is critical for moving the field forward. Finally, the outcome variables were not associated with any gold standard diagnosis of cognitive impairment from neuropsychologists. Rather, the outcomes were based on a published cut-score of 25 for MMSE and a 1 standard deviation cutpoint from standardized norms for ATT-C and MEM-C measures. Notably, the MMSE used to generate our outcome variable was proven to be biased in education level42–44, and the neuropsychological measures have inherent limitations relative to age, education, and race45,46. Although the current study suggests the detection and mitigation of the classification bias, we recognize that an ideal outcome measure would have been professional consensus identification of cognitive impairment after considering individual demographics coupled with large normative references for age, education, and race. Hence, the study’s clinical application is currently limited. Future interdisciplinary research efforts are needed to address cognitive profiles with screening measures in diverse patient samples.

In the future, we will continue to build more robust foundational generative models, such as the RF-VAE to represent the CDT and other visual cognitive assessments that may have less educational bias. These foundational models will be iteratively updated using publicly available datasets whenever available. Using late-stage fusion, we will also incorporate relevant dCDT metrics in downstream classification tasks. Finally, this study will provide impetus for collecting datasets to evaluate cognition in highly disadvantaged educational groups and to specifically investigate the relevance of applying CDT in this population.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted at The University of Florida (UF) and UF Health. Data were acquired by a federally funded investigation by The National Institutes of Health. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki standards and UF’s Institutional Review Board-01 (IRB) approved this investigation, and the requirements for written informed consent were waived by the IRB. Electronic Health Record data were acquired through an IRB-approved process which involved the deidentification of data provided through an honest data broker. This study is an extension of UF Health’s cognitive screener12 and is based on the implementation/creation of the Perioperative Cognitive Anesthesia Network (PeCANSM) Program established in August of 2017 as part of UF Health’s Presurgical Center47. The PeCAN program was created for older adults, aged 65 and older where patients are evaluated by preoperative licensed and board-certified neuropsychologists to assist with preoperative planning to reduce risk for delirium and other postoperative cognitive complications.

Data collection and processing

Data for older adults seen at UF Health’s Presurgical Clinic and PeCANSM program were retrospectively acquired using IRB-approved methods and an honest data broker during the period from January 3, 2018 through December 30, 2019. Using the clock drawing test and a three-word free recall memory test as described in Amini et al.12, anesthesiologists and surgeons refer to the PeCANSM program if a patient was concerned about memory, missed one of the three words on recall, or produced two or more errors using a previously described analog clock drawing scoring algorithm6. A board-certified neuropsychologist then completes an interview examining demographics, delirium, and neurodegenerative risk factors, a 30-min protocol (see37,38), followed by feedback and recommendations to the patient and care team36. For the current investigation, data were extracted from the scored protocol and included the Mini-Mental State Exam48, and standardized neuropsychology measures representing verbal36 episodic memory (MEM-C: Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised: list learning total immediate, delay, discrimination49), and attention and working memory.

(ATT-C: Letter F fluency46, Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-Third Edition Digit Span Forwards, Backwards50). Raw scores were standardized using published normative references and then averaged into a composite score.

The development and test split ratio was set as 7:3. For the MMSE, a raw score lower than 25 (categorization groups 1, 2 and 3) was used to define “impairment” at the level of mild or greater51. For the standardized composite of the domains ATT-C and MEM-C, mild or greater “impairment” was defined as a composite < =− 1.0 relative to the normative mean52,53. Patients in the training set and test set maintained the same in the three tasks. A fivefold cross-validation was further applied to the development set to obtain training and validation sets. Another cohort of clock drawings used in this work is from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) dataset, which conducts annual interviews with over 8,000 older adults in 11 rounds54.

Clock drawings

Clock drawing images used to train the RF-VAE encoder were collected from the publicly available dataset provided by the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), which spanned 11 rounds of data collection in total from 2011 to 2022 and involved 8,000 elderly patients from the United States.

Relevance factor VAE encoder

We leveraged the RF-VAE, a self-supervised deep generative model to generate a 10-feature representation of CDT, which has been demonstrated to be effective in dementia screening as shown in our prior work29. The overall workflow is shown in Fig. 5. Instead of training the model on the private dataset, we pre-trained the RF-VAE encoder on the NHATS dataset which contained 54,027 individual, unlabeled clock drawings. Subsequently, the encoder was applied to the clock drawing images collected from PeCANSM program to generate a 10 dimensions latent space vector, while the generator was not included in the classification stage. We defined each of the 10 features as the following: Size, Vertical displacement of hands, Rotated clock, Digit Placement, Clockface Ellipse, Clock face Obovate, Angle between the clock hands, Side Bulge, Rotation of hand assembly, and Square-Rhomboid.

The overall conceptual flowchart of FaIRClocks is shown. The RF-VAE was pretrained on NHATS dataset. The trained RF-VAE encoder was used in the downstream classification pipeline, while the generator was not involved. The pretrained weights from the RF-VAE encoder were applied on the classification datasets within the FaIRClocks framework to generate 10-D latent representation for the command and copy conditions of the clock drawing test separately. These 10-D latent representations were combined with demographics information and fed to the classifier after reweighing using fairness functions from the AIF360 toolkit. Logistic regression produced the best classifier in predicting the MMSE score, while support vector machines created the best classifiers for predicting ATT-C and MEM-C scores.

Machine learning classifiers

Multiple machine learning classifiers are tested, including Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machines (SVM), XGBoost, and a three-layer fully connected neural network, aiming to find the best model based on the three different measures. A binary threshold of -1 is set for both attention and memory z-scores, where values below -1 are classified as impaired, while values greater than or equal to -1 are classified as unimpaired. For MMSE total score, we follow the categorization approach proposed by Perneczky et al.55, group class 1 (mild), class 2 (moderate), and class 3 (severe dementia) into a single category. Class 0 (healthy, total score higher than 2551) is set as another class, which together with the grouped class resulted in a binary classification task to detect whether the patient was cognitively compromised. The classifiers leverage the 10 dimensions of the RF-VAE features combined with basic demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity, years of education and area deprivation index; ADI) as input, to generate binary outputs Y for each of the three tasks. In an additional follow-up analysis, we tested the improvement in classifier performance by including other clock variables such as total time to completion, and total number of strokes which were collected using smart paper and digital pen from Anoto corporation. Hyperparameters for all classifiers used a randomized grid search within a fivefold cross-validation setting. Student’s T-Test with False Discovery Rate (FDR) = 0.01 was used to calculate p-value inferring the significance.
