An interpretable contrast-enhanced CT radiomics–based pipeline incorporating automatic segmentation for predicting ISUP grade group in ccRCC
Authors: Yue Ren, Fei Yang, Shuchao Kang, Yongsheng Zhang, Yuanping Tao, Hongyan Chao, Min Xu, Wanjun Zheng, Feng Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesNon-invasive assessment of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) International Society of Urological Pathology grade group (ISUP GG) remains a clinical challenge. This study aims to develop an interpretable radiomics-based pipeline incorporating automatic segmentation for predicting the ISUP GG in ccRCC.MethodsThis retrospective study included 276 pathologically confirmed ccRCC patients who underwent preoperative contrast-enhanced CT (193 train, 83 test), and were stratified by ISUP GG (1–2 vs. 3–4). Radiomics features were extracted from the auto‑segmented tumor regions on corticomedullary-phase images. Feature selection was performed using Recursive Feature Elimination Cross-Validation across 5 classifiers. Along with clinical information, we ultimately constructed radiomics, clinical, and combined models. Models comprehensively evaluated via multiple metrics, with partial results visualized through Receiver Operating Characteristic and calibration curves, and decision curve analysis, and further interpretability analysis conducted.ResultsThe combined model significantly outperformed the clinical and radiomics models, achieving an AUC of 0.907 (95% CI 0.868–0.947) vs. 0.802 (95% CI 0.735–0.869) and 0.769 (95% CI 0.702–0.836), both p < 0.001. In the test, the combined model significantly outperformed the clinical model, achieving an AUC of 0.834 (95% CI 0.737–0.931) vs. 0.698 (95% CI 0.575–0.821), p = 0.001, but showed no significant difference compared with the radiomics model (AUC 0.834 [95% CI 0.737–0.931] vs. 0.796 [95% CI 0.695–0.897], p = 0.328). Calibration curves and DCA indicated that the combined model demonstrated good calibration and better clinical net benefit.ConclusionThe interpretable pipeline may have potential as a non-invasive tool for predicting ISUP GG in ccRCC.Keywords: ccRCC, ISUP grade group, Radiomics, Interpretability, SHAP

Introduction

The global incidence of renal cell carcinoma continues to rise [1]. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most prevalent subtype, accounts for over 75% of all RCC cases. ccRCC is often associated with poor prognosis and high mortality rates [2–4]. International Society of Urological Pathology grade group (ISUP GG) is widely recognized as an independent and highly relevant prognostic factor for ccRCC [5]. In this system, ISUP GG 1–2 tumors are classified as low-grade, while 3–4 tumors are categorized as high-grade. Generally, higher nuclear grade is associated with shorter patient survival [6]. Moreover, the choice of treatment is closely associated with ISUP GG of ccRCC [7]. Clinically, patients with low- ISUP GG may undergo relatively conservative treatments, such as radiofrequency ablation, nephron-sparing surgery, or active surveillance; while high- ISUP GG ccRCC patients typically require radical surgical resection [5]. Furthermore, higher ISUP GG is associated with an increased risk of postoperative recurrence [8, 9] Currently, the ISUP GG of ccRCC primarily rely on renal biopsy. However, this invasive procedure may lead to a range of potential complications, such as metastasis and hemorrhage [10].

There is an urgent need for accurate non-invasive imaging techniques to predict the ISUP GG of ccRCC in clinical practice. Traditional imaging methods include ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography (CT). However, the accuracy of US is significantly influenced by the experience of the operator [11], due to its limitations in specificity and accuracy, CT or MRI is required for further confirmation of suspicious findings [2]. MRI provides the benefit of being radiation-free, offering high soft tissue resolution, and enabling multi-sequence and multi-parametric imaging [12]. However, MRI has a relatively limited scanning range, and its high cost, as well as contraindications in patients with metal implants, hinder its broader application. Kidney contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) can clearly and comprehensively stage tumors, assess vascular invasion and vascular abnormalities, and detect potential abdominal metastases; therefore, it is widely used for preoperative evaluation of ccRCC [13].

Machine learning (ML) has advanced rapidly and promises to revolutionize clinical decision-making and diagnosis through the processing of medical data [14, 15]. Radiomics is a method that involves selecting volume of Interest (VOI) from medical images and extracting numerous high-dimensional quantitative features [16]. Radiomics is well-aligned with ML, and several studies have now explored non-invasive assessment of ISUP GG in ccRCC by combining ML with radiomics [13]. However, these studies often rely on labor-intensive and time-consuming manual segmentation, resulting in complex workflows that limit their clinical applicability. Furthermore, these studies generally exhibit poor reproducibility [13, 17–19]. To our knowledge, currently, there are few radiomics studies based on automated segmentation, and most lack interpretability analysis. Therefore, the aim of this study is to develop a pipeline based on CECT-radiomics model (integrating tumor auto-segmentation) for predicting ISUP GG of ccRCC, with interpretability analysis.

Materials and methods

Study population

The ethics committee of the participating institution approved this retrospective study (Approval No. 2025LL013), and the need for informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

We conducted a retrospective review of the medical records of 276 consecutive patients with histopathologically confirmed ccRCC who underwent CECT scans between June 2016 and December 2024 at a large medical center. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) histopathological diagnosis of ccRCC with clear documentation of ISUP GG; (b) CECT performed within 2 weeks prior to surgery. The exclusion criteria were: (a) poor CECT image quality or inability to scan in the supine position; (b) any neoadjuvant or prior treatment for ccRCC before surgery; (c) presence of any other malignant neoplasm; (d) history of kidney transplantation.

We collected CECT imaging and clinical data, including age, gender, and information from the picture archiving and communication system (PACS), tumor size and tumor lymph node (LN) metastasis status (0 = no metastasis; 1 = metastasis defined by short-axis diameter > 1.0 cm; x = indeterminate).

Imaging protocol

CT scans were performed using a Siemens Somatom Force CT (Germany) or a Philips Brilliance iCT 128-slice CT (The Netherlands). Acquisition parameters were as follows: tube voltage 120 kVp; automatic tube current modulation; slice thickness 3 mm; slice interval 3 mm; pitch 1.0; matrix size 512 × 512; and iterative reconstruction algorithm. For contrast-enhanced scans, iohexol (350 mgI/mL) was administered via an antecubital vein using a high-pressure injector at a dose of 1.5 mL/kg and an injection rate of 2.5–3.0 mL/s. Automatic bolus-triggered scanning was applied with a threshold of 150 HU, followed by corticomedullary-phase scanning 30 seconds after triggering, nephrographic phase scanning at 90 seconds, and excretory phase scanning at 180 seconds. All scans were performed in the cranio-caudal direction.

Image tumor segmentation, feature extraction and stability control

We developed an automatic ccRCC tumor segmentation model on CECT using nnUNet (version 2.5.1) [20]. First, radiologists A and B, both blinded to the ISUP grading, collaboratively performed tumor segmentation for all 276 patients using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0), reaching a consensus on the final VOI for each case. Subsequently, the data were randomly divided into a training set (248 cases, 90%) and a test set (28 cases, 10%), for segmentation task. The segmentation model was trained on the training set and validated on the test set, with the Dice Similarity Coefficient score used as the evaluation metric.

Prior to feature extraction, all patient images underwent standardized preprocessing to ensure consistency, minimize anisotropy, and eliminate variations caused by different machine models. To rigorously prevent data leakage, the parameters for all preprocessing steps were determined exclusively from the training set. These parameters were then applied independently to the validation and test sets without any recalibration. First, all images were resampled to isotropic voxels of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 and discretized using a fixed bin width of 25. Feature extraction was performed with Pyradiomics (version 3.1.0), a tool widely used in radiomics studies [21], following the recommended CT image parameter settings: https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/v3.1.0/customization.html#parameter-file. In cases where multiple tumor VOIs were present, radiomic features were extracted exclusively from the VOI with the largest volume. A total of 1197 features were extracted, including shape features, first-order features, Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) features, Gray-Level Run-Length Matrix (GLRLM) features, Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM) features, Gray-Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM) features, Neighborhood Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM) features, and features derived from LoG and wavelet filtering.

Although nnUNet segmentation tasks typically perform well and exhibit strong robustness [20], we adopted a conservative approach to mitigate potential discrepancies between auto-segmentation and radiologists’ manual results. In the segmentation task’s test set (28 cases), radiomics features were extracted from both the DL-predicted VOIs and the radiologists’ manually delineated VOIs. An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) greater than 0.75 indicates high feature stability [22]. We calculated the ICC for each radiomics feature and only included those with an ICC > 0.75 in the subsequent analysis.

Feature selection, model construction, evaluation, and interpretability analysis

To enhance the stability and convergence speed of the algorithm, and to ensure fairness across features of different scales, all included radiomics features were standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

The feature selection process included three steps: 1. Univariate analysis was performed on radiomics features with an ICC greater than 0.75, and features with p < 0.1 were retained for subsequent analysis. 2.The minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) algorithm (mrmr-selection, version 0.2.8) was used to select 50 features with the highest information gain and minimal redundancy. 3.Recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) (from the sklearn.feature_selection module, version 1.5.2) was applied to identify the final feature subset. To ensure a robust and unbiased feature selection, we utilized five different classifiers, including Logistic Regression (LR, sklearn version 1.5.2), Random Forest (RF, sklearn version 1.5.2), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT, sklearn version 1.5.2), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, version 3.0.0), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM, version 4.6.0), as the internal estimators for the RFECV. 10-fold 5-repeated cross-validation was used at each step, with mean area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve as the evaluation metric. Following this feature selection process, the LightGBM classifier, which demonstrated superior performance during the RFECV stage, was chosen to train the final model using the optimal feature subset. The LightGBM hyperparameter search space included: bagging_fraction (0.5–0.9), bagging_freq (1–7), colsample_bytree (0.5–0.9), feature_fraction (0.5–0.9), lambda_l1 (1e-8–50), lambda_l2 (1e-8–50), max_depth (3–7), min_child_samples (20–100), min_child_weight (1e-5–1e-1), n_estimators (2000), num_leaves (20–64), and subsample (0.5–0.9). Early stopping with early_stopping_rounds = 50 was applied to prevent overfitting, and Optuna (version 3.6.1) was used for hyperparameter optimization with 100 trials and logloss as the evaluation metric.

The dataset was stratified and randomly divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) cohorts (random seed = 42). In the training cohort, univariate logistic regression was performed on all clinical variables, and those with P < 0.1 were included in the multivariate model. Based on the multivariate analysis of clinical variables and RFECV-selected radiomics features, we constructed 3 models: clinical, radiomics, and combined.

Model evaluation and interpretability analyses followed the procedure outlined below: 1. Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics—area under the ROC curve AUC, balanced accuracy (bAcc, calculated as the average of sensitivity and specificity to better handle class imbalance), overall accuracy (Acc), sensitivity (SE), specificity (SP), and F1 score—along with comparisons of ROC curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). 2. Model interpretability was evaluated using SHAP (version 0.44.1) [23], a method that quantifies the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions, including both global and local explanations.

Histopathological examination

ISUP GG were determined based on histopathological documents. Tumor specimens were obtained from various types of nephrectomy procedures. Two specialized renal pathologists, each with 5 to 8 years of diagnostic experience, independently performed pathological assessments while being blinded to the CECT findings and clinical information. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus following discussion. According to the ISUP GG, all tumors were classified into two categories: low-grade (Grade 1–2) and high-grade (Grade 3–4).

Statistical analysis

We used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for continuous data, Mann-Whitney U test for skewed data and results were described using median and interquartile range, t-test or Welch’s test for non-skewed data and results were presented using mean and standard deviation, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data.

Intra-rater reliability was assessed using the ICC test, and correlations were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficient. Between-model comparisons of AUC values were conducted using the DeLong test, and comparisons of bAcc values were performed using the McNemar test. All statistical analyses, FS algorithms, and MLclassifiers were implemented using Python (version 3.10.16). All visualizations created using Python (version 3.10.16). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model comparison on test, model interpretability analysis

On the test set, the combined model significantly outperformed the clinical model, achieving an AUC of 0.834 (95% CI 0.737–0.931) vs. 0.698 (95% CI 0.575–0.821), p = 0.001, DeLong test, statistic = −3.210, but showed no significant difference compared with the radiomics model (AUC 0.834 [95% CI 0.737–0.931] vs. 0.796 [95% CI 0.695–0.897], p = 0.328, DeLong test, statistic = −0.977). The bAcc of the combined model was 0.787, which, although higher than that of the radiomics model (0.714), showed no statistically significant difference based on the McNemar test (p = 0.180, McNemar test statistic = 2.000). However, the combined model still demonstrated a significant improvement over the clinical model (0.640, p = 0.004, McNemar test statistic = 2.000). Furthermore, the combined model demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving a sensitivity of 0.750, a specificity of 0.824, and an F1-score of 0.738. Detailed performance metrics for all models are visually summarized in Fig. 3. The ROC curve, calibration curve, and DCA curve on test all indicated that the combined model was superior to the clinical model, as shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

This study developed an interpretable pipeline with automatic segmentation for predicting the ISUP GG of ccRCC. It demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to the clinical model in both the training and test sets, highlighting its greater potential for noninvasive prediction of ISUP GG in ccRCC.

Noninvasive assessment of ccRCC ISUP GG remains a critical clinical need. Several studies have attempted to predict ccRCC ISUP GG based on medical imaging with radiomics and ML enhancing their diagnostic value. MRI-based radiomics has been explored for its potential to distinguish low-grade from high-grade ccRCC, achieving AUCs of 0.778–0.855 [26–28]. However, a meta-analysis by Mohsen Salimi et al. reported that CT-based, particularly CECT-based, radiomics models demonstrated slightly superior performance compared with MRI [19]. CECT phase may also influence diagnostic accuracy, the corticomedullary phase is particularly advantageous for highlighting tumor microvasculature and revealing features such as heterogeneity and necrosis, which are characteristic of high-grade tumors [19, 29]. Abeer J. et al. [30] retrospectively analyzed arterial phase images from 391 ccRCC cases and achieved AUCs of 0.77–0.90 for ISUP GG prediction. Wang et al. [31] performed a multicenter retrospective study involving 197 patients and developed a CECT-based radiomics LR model to distinguish ISUP GG in ccRCC, achieving an AUC of 0.81, however, the model demonstrated suboptimal performance under imbalanced sample conditions, with a sensitivity of only 0.580 in the validation cohort—substantially lower than the 0.791 observed in this study—and lacked interpretability analysis. More importantly, a recent meta-analysis [19] highlighted that the majority of radiomics studies (24/26, 92%) still relied on time-consuming and labor-intensive manual delineation of VOIs, which severely compromises reproducibility and limits clinical translation. Accordingly, this work focuses on the role of CECT-based radiomics for predicting the ISUP GG in ccRCC, and emphasizes reproducibility, interpretability, and clinical utility. We developed a tumor auto-segmentation model to efficiently extract radiomic features and along with available clinical data to construct a predictive pipeline. We then evaluated its performance and conducted an interpretability analysis. Although the predictive performance of our model (AUC 0.834, 95% CI: 0.737–0.931) was slightly lower than that of the combined models reported in the most recent meta-analysis (AUC 0.86, 95% CI: 0.83–0.89) [19], but it is noteworthy that we evaluated the study quality using the online METRICS tool: https://metricsscore.github.io/metrics/METRICS.html [19, 32], ，Likely benefiting from automatic segmentation, rigorous feature engineering, and standardized procedures, we achieved a favorable score of 82%, higher than the reported average of 66.65% (range 49.7–88.4%), indicating overall good methodological quality.

Reproducibility is critical in radiomics research and is often closely tied to the delineation of the VOI. First, we developed a robust automatic segmentation model (DICE score = 0.939 ± 0.045 on the test set), which substantially enhances the reliability and reproducibility of the research in this work. Furthermore, the ICC test is an important metric to assess feature reproducibility. We took a conservative approach by including only features with an ICC > 0.75 for analysis to mitigate potential instability, although we employed an automatic segmentation method to delineate the VOI. The ICC results show that most features exhibited high stability (1168/1197 features with ICC > 0.75), providing evidence of the high stability of our automatic segmentation model. The feature select process conducted in this study involved RFECV in combination with multiple classifiers (LR, RF, GBDT, LightGBM, XGBoost). In each step, stratified 10-Fold cross-validation was employed, enhancing control over reproducibility. Interpretability also plays a crucial role in radiomics research. We performed an interpretability analysis on the best-performing combined LightGBM model to provide insight into its decision-making process, which is essential for clinical practice. We then developed a web application that can predict ccRCC ISUPGG in real-time and provide interpretability analysis results. By integrating the automatic segmentation model for efficient VOI delineation and radiomics feature extraction, along with the real-time accessible web application, we significantly enhanced the clinical applicability of this pipeline.

Taking a step forward, this study focuses on the role of CECT-based radiomics and integrates tumor auto-segmentation to construct an interpretable pipeline with automatic segmentation for the non-invasive assessment of ccRCC ISUPGG. The results demonstrate that the combined model significantly outperformed the clinical and radiomics models, achieving an AUC of 0.907 (95% CI 0.868–0.947) vs. 0.802 (95% CI 0.735–0.869) and 0.769 (95% CI 0.702–0.836), both p < 0.001, DeLong test, statistics = −4.974 and −4.80. In the test set, the combined model significantly outperformed the clinical model, achieving an AUC of 0.834 (95% CI 0.737–0.931) vs. 0.698 (95% CI 0.575–0.821), p = 0.001, DeLong test, statistic = −3.210, but showed no significant difference compared with the radiomics model (AUC 0.834 [95% CI 0.737–0.931] vs. 0.796 [95% CI 0.695–0.897], p = 0.328, DeLong test, statistic = −0.977). ROC curve, calibration curve, and DCA indicated high discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit with this pipeline. These findings underscore the potential reliability of the pipeline in predicting ccRCC ISUP GG and provide evidence to support clinical decision-making, such as surgical planning, pre-biopsy risk stratification, resolving diagnostic uncertainty, and guiding follow-up or surveillance strategies. In addition, the interpretable CECT radiomics-based pipeline is relatively easy to implement, and its non-invasive and reproducible nature makes it more suitable for routine clinical use and broader adoption in medical practice. Nevertheless, several practical challenges remain, including the need for rigorous multicenter external and prospective validation, the establishment of standardized imaging and analysis protocols, and the integration of the predictive model into existing clinical workflows.

This study has several limitations: (a) It is a retrospective, single-center study with a small sample size, and most importantly, it lacks external validation, which may significantly limit the generalizability and robustness of the proposed model. Future studies should incorporate large, multi-center external validation cohorts to confirm the reproducibility and clinical applicability of our findings. (b) Our research focused solely on the tumor VOI and did not investigate potential information from the surrounding areas, which could be an avenue for future research. (c) The study did not incorporate multimodal data such as MRI, which may restrict the model’s overall performance. Future research should focus on designing prospective cohorts and exploring the integration of multi-omics and multimodal data to enhance model performance and assess its applicability in diverse clinical settings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed a pipeline based on CECT radiomics and clinical factors, demonstrating potential in non-invasively predicting ISUP GG in ccRCC.
