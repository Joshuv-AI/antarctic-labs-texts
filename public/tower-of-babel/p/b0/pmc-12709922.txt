Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling of drug-drug interactions between suraxavir marboxil and CYP3A4 inhibitors: Quantitative prediction of pharmacokinetic effects on active metabolite GP1707D07
Authors: Lan Yang, Fan Yang, Chao-Zhuang Shen, Yan-Xin Wang, Xiao-Lin Wang, Lang lv, Yue-E Wu, Pan-Pan Ye, Bo-Hao Tang, Guo-Xiang Hao, Shou-Sheng Yan, Wei Zhao, Yi Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Suraxavir marboxil (GP681) is a promising novel prodrug influenza polymerase acidic (PA) inhibitor whose active metabolite, suraxavir (GP1707D07), is primarily metabolized by cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), raising concerns about drug-drug interactions (DDI) with CYP3A4 inhibitors. Traditional DDI assessment methods are limited for evaluating all potential combinations. This study aimed to develop a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model to predict the DDI risk between GP681 and CYP3A4 inhibitors of varying potency. The model was developed based on physicochemical and in vitro parameters, as well as clinical data, including a phase I single ascending dose study of GP681 tablets and a single-center phase I study evaluating the DDI between GP681 and strong CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole. The model was successfully verified against clinical data, with predicted-to-observed ratios for GP1707D07 exposure under itraconazole co-administration of AUC and Cmax of 1.042 and 1.357, respectively. Simulations using the validated model predicted a substantial increase in GP1707D07 exposure when co-administered with moderate inhibitors fluconazole (AUC ratio 2.820; Cmax ratio 1.509) and verapamil (AUC ratio 2.347; Cmax ratio 1.645), comparable to the effect of itraconazole. Weak inhibitors showed negligible effects. Consequently, clinical monitoring and potential dose adjustment of GP681 are recommended when co-administered with strong inhibitors and the moderate inhibitors. The study demonstrates the utility of PBPK modeling for efficient and predictive DDI assessment of complex prodrug systems, guiding the safe clinical use of GP681.

Introduction

Influenza viruses are responsible for both seasonal outbreaks and global pandemics, representing a significant risk to public health [1,2]. Recent increases in influenza-like illness following the COVID-19 pandemic have reinforced the need for effective antiviral strategies and preparedness. Resistance to older classes of influenza antivirals (e.g., M2 ion channel inhibitors) is now widespread, and emerging resistance to neuraminidase inhibitors such as oseltamivir further limits therapeutic options. Consequently, novel agents that target different viral functions are an important component of current antiviral development [[3], [4], [5]].

Antiviral agents targeting RNA polymerase subunits, such as the polymerase acidic protein (PA) and polymerase basic protein 1 and 2 (PB2), have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates [6,7]. Baloxavir marboxil, a selective PA inhibitor, has shown clinical benefit in reducing symptom duration but rapid resistance development has highlighted challenges for long-term utility, particularly in pediatric populations [8]. Suraxavir marboxil (GP681) is an orally available PA-targeting prodrug whose active metabolite GP1707D07 inhibits the viral cap-dependent endonuclease and has demonstrated potent antiviral activity in preclinical studies, including against strains resistant to currently used agents. These properties, together with a projected prolonged half-life, make GP681 a promising candidate for influenza therapy [9,10].

A critical aspect of GP681 ​s clinical development is its biotransformation and elimination. GP681 is converted to the active metabolite GP1707D07 via prodrug hydrolysis, and subsequent clearance of GP1707D07 involves CYP3A4-mediated metabolism. Because CYP3A4 is a major drug-metabolizing enzyme and the widespread clinical use of CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., macrolide antibiotics, azole antifungals, and calcium channel blockers), potential DDIs between GP681 and these agents may significantly compromise its therapeutic efficacy and safety [11]. For instance, preliminary clinical investigations (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05789342) demonstrated that coadministration with the strong CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole resulted in approximately 2.5-fold and 1.4-fold increases in the AUC and peak plasma concentration Cmax of GP1707D07, respectively, compared to monotherapy [12]. Given the observed effect with a strong inhibitor, moderate or weak CYP3A4 inhibitors also produce varying degrees of influence on GP1707D07 exposure, thereby impacting its therapeutic window. Consequently, Systematic evaluation of GP681–CYP3A4 DDIs is essential for rational dosing.

Traditional methods for DDI assessment, such as in vitro assays and clinical trials, are either physiologically incomplete or resource-prohibitive for evaluating all possible interactions [[13], [14], [15]]. In recent years, PBPK modeling has gained prominence as a key tool endorsed by regulatory agencies for predicting DDIs, owing to its ability to integrate physiological parameters, in vitro data, and clinical observations into a unified framework [14,16]. PBPK models simulate the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion processes of drugs across organs and tissues, allowing for quantitative prediction of both the inhibitory effects of inhibitors on metabolic enzyme activity and their consequent impact on substrate pharmacokinetics. This study aims to systematically predict the impact of CYP3A4 inhibitors of varying potency on the exposure of GP1707D07 by developing a dual PBPK model for GP681 and its active metabolite GP1707D07, incorporating their physiologically based pharmacokinetic profiles. The developed model was applied to simulate various clinical scenarios, aiming to provide quantitative guidance for dose adjustment when GP681 is co-administered with CYP3A4 inhibitors, thereby supporting the safe and effective clinical use of this drug combination.

Methods

Software and statistical analysis

Building of the whole-body PBPK model, sensitivity analysis, and parameter optimization were performed using PK-Sim version 11.3 (Bayer AG, Leverkusen, Germany) software. Visually presented plasma concentration-time profiles in previous publications were converted to numerical data using Graph Grabber version 2.0 (Quintessa, Quintessa.org).

After population simulations, pharmacokinetic metrics (for example, AUC and Cmax) is expressed as arithmetic means. DDI effects are reported as arithmetic mean ratios of PK parameters, calculated as the arithmetic mean under co-administration divided by the arithmetic mean under substrate alone.

Clinical pharmacokinetic data

The study utilized data from two clinical trials of GP681 tablets to develop and verify the model. The modeling population for GP681 monotherapy was established using demographic data from subjects across all dose groups in the single-dose tolerability clinical trial of GP681 tablets (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04729764). Pharmacokinetic (PK) data from the 20 ​mg single-dose cohort were utilized to develop the GP681 monotherapy PBPK model, while the remaining single-dose cohorts (40 ​mg, 60 ​mg, 80 ​mg) were employed for model verification. A single-center Phase I clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05789342) evaluating the DDI between GP681 and the strong CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole in healthy volunteers was utilized to develop and validate PBPK models for GP681 monotherapy and its coadministration with itraconazole. Additionally, demographic information and clinical pharmacokinetic data from published studies were incorporated to construct PBPK models for other CYP3A4 inhibitors of varying potency including itraconazole.

Model development

PBPK models of GP681 and its active metabolite GP1707D07 were built based on a “middle-out” approach. The overall schematic for the PBPK modeling workflow is presented in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Overall workflow for the PBPK modelling.Fig. 1

Overall workflow for the PBPK modelling.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated that following oral administration, GP681, the prodrug is scarcely detected in plasma but converted to an active form GP1707D07. The enzymatic hydrolysis of GP681 in human liver S9 was investigated using chemical inhibition studies. The results demonstrated that GP681 is metabolized to GP1707D07 primarily through AADAC-mediated pathways, with CES1 and CES2 contributing minimally to this metabolic process. Therefore, in the current modeling study, the hydrolysis metabolism of GP681 to GP1707D07 was simplified to be represented solely by AADAC-mediated pathways. The intrinsic clearance (CLint) value for the AADAC-mediated generation of active metabolites from GP681 was not experimentally determined. This parameter was subsequently optimized based on clinical observations from the 20 ​mg dose group (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04729764). GP1707D07 demonstrates extensive plasma protein binding and exhibits broad tissue distribution, without significant accumulation at excessive concentrations or persistent retention in specific tissues. In the phenotypic study identifying cytochrome P450 enzymes responsible for GP1707D07 metabolism, GP1707D07 was predominantly metabolized by human recombinant CYP3A4, demonstrating an in vitro CLint of 0.213 ​μL/min/pmol. In contrast, no significant metabolism was observed in systems expressing CYP1A2, CYP2B6, or CYP2C8, with in vitro CLint values less than 0.01 ​μL/min/pmol (unpublished data). The primary elimination pathway for GP681 and GP1707D07 is fecal excretion, with minimal excretion occurring through urine and bile. The mass balance investigation demonstrated that GP1707D07 constitutes the principal radioactive component, representing approximately 95.4% of serum radioactivity. Following oral administration of GP681, a cumulative radioactivity recovery of 90.1% was achieved within 336 ​h (84.1% via fecal elimination and 6.0% through urinary excretion) [12,17]. To characterize renal elimination in the model, the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) fractions for both GP681 and GP1707D07 were assigned a value of 1 as a mechanistic assumption. The physicochemical parameters of GP681 and GP1707D07, including LogP (partition coefficient), fraction unbound (fu), and solubility, were primarily determined through experimental measurements. The solubility of GP1707D07 at pH 7 was assigned the same value as GP681, as this parameter was identified as non-sensitive in prior sensitivity analyses. The fu of GP681 and its formulation parameters (modeled using the Weibull function to characterize release kinetics) were subsequently optimized through population pharmacokinetic analysis of clinical data from the 20 ​mg dose group (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04729764). Tissue-to-plasma partition coefficient was estimated by Poulin and Theil [18,19] both in GP681 model and GP1707D07 model. Cellular permeabilities in the GP681and GP1707D07 models were estimated by PK-Sim® standard methods. The Monte Carlo algorithm was used to optimize model parameters in the parameter identification tool. Sensitivity analysis

This study conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess the influence of critical drug parameters for GP681 tablets and its active metabolite GP1707D07 on model-predicted PK parameters, including the AUC, Cmax, and time to Cmax (Tmax), to validate parameter reliability. The analysis was performed using PK-Sim® by systematically perturbing individual input parameters (e.g., ±10% variation) while maintaining others constant, followed by recalculation of PK metrics. The sensitivity was calculated as follows (Equation (1)).(1)S=ΔPKPK0÷ΔPP0

The sensitivity coefficient (S) is defined as the ratio of the relative change in a PK parameter (ΔPK/PK0) to the relative variation in the input parameter (ΔP/P0), where PK0 and P0 represent baseline values of the PK metric and examined parameter, respectively. A sensitivity value of +1.0 denotes complete proportionality, implying that a 10% elevation in the input parameter (ΔP/P0 ​= ​0.1) induces an equivalent 10% increase in the PK parameter outcome (ΔPK/PK0 ​= ​0.1). A coefficient magnitude of 1 indicates proportional sensitivity (e.g., +10% parameter change induces +10% PK metric alteration), with positive/negative values denoting direct/inverse relationships.

Model evaluation

The PBPK model underwent evaluation through complementary visual diagnostics (e.g., goodness-of-fit plots) and quantitative metrics (e.g., fold error), ensuring comprehensive characterization of its predictive performance. Compare the clinical observation values with the simulated drug-time curves and plot them to observe whether the trends of the drug-time curves are consistent. The acceptability criteria for model performance were defined by a twofold variation tolerance in both AUC and Cmax. Drug-drug interactions

PBPK platform qualification requires verification against maximal observed DDIs (strong perpetrator) before extrapolation to moderate and weak perpetrators [14]. Therefore, the PBPK model for GP681 was first verified under monotherapy conditions, then extended to simulate DDI with itraconazole (a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor) using phase I clinical coadministration trial data (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05789342) [20]. The itraconazole PBPK model was refined with minor modifications based on a previously published framework [20] and subsequently validated against clinical pharmacokinetic data before being implemented to construct the DDI model. The verified DDI model for the coadministration of GP681 and itraconazole was subsequently applied to DDI models of GP681 with moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors (fluconazole, verapamil) as well as weak CYP3A4 inhibitors (cimetidine, fluvoxamine) [[21], [22], [23]]. We adapted and applied the publicly available fluconazole, verapamil, and fluvoxamine models from the GitHub repository, implementing minor optimizations on these established frameworks. Additionally, drug-specific modeling parameters for cimetidine were collected. All four single-agent inhibitor models were subsequently validated against published clinical data from the literature. The parameters for the monotherapy models of CYP3A4 inhibitors of varying potencies are provided in the Supplementary Tables S1–5. Population DDI simulations were generated in PK-Sim using the default “East Asian” physiological population templates (see Supplementary Table S6). The impact of the DDI was assessed by comparing the plasma concentration-time profiles of GP1707D07 following administration of GP681 alone versus its co-administration with CYP3A4 inhibitors, and by calculating the DDI ratios for AUC (Equation (3)) and Cmax (Equation (4)).(3)DDIAUCratio=AUCofthevictimdrugduringcoadministrationAUCofthevictimdrug(4)DDICmaxratio=CmaxofthevictimdrugduringcoadministrationCmaxofthevictimdrug

Sensitivity analysis

This analysis aimed to identify the key physicochemical and physiological parameters with the greatest influence on the exposure of the active metabolite GP1707D07, thereby evaluating the robustness and reliability of the model. A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of key pharmacokinetic parameters of GP681 tablets and their active metabolite on the primary exposure metrics (AUC, Cmax, Tmax) of the major active metabolite GP1707D07 following a single 20 ​mg oral dose. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the lipophilicity of both GP681 and GP1707D07, the dissolution time and dissolution shape of GP681, and the intrinsic clearance of GP681 via AADAC metabolism and GP1707D07 via CYP3A4 metabolism were identified as significant determinants of GP1707D07 Cmax (Fig. 3A)). For GP1707D07 AUC, critical parameters included the lipophilicity of GP681, intrinsic clearance of GP1707D07 via CYP3A4 metabolism, unbound fraction of GP1707D07, intestinal permeability of GP681, intrinsic clearance of GP681 via AADAC metabolism, and unbound fraction of GP681 (Fig. 3B)). The dissolution shape, dissolution time, and lipophilicity of GP681 were key factors influencing GP1707D07 Tmax (Fig. 3C)).Fig. 3Results of sensitivity analysis for GP1707D07, measured as the change of the predicted Cmax, AUC, and Tmax following a single 20 ​mg dose of GP681 tablet.Fig. 3

Results of sensitivity analysis for GP1707D07, measured as the change of the predicted Cmax, AUC, and Tmax following a single 20 ​mg dose of GP681 tablet.

The sources for these parameters were rigorously established: The lipophilicity values for both GP681 and GP1707D07, the fraction unbound of GP1707D07, the intrinsic clearance of GP1707D07 via CYP3A4, and the intestinal permeability parameter of GP681 were derived from in vitro experiments, ensuring physiologically relevant foundations. Dissolution time and dissolution shape parameters were calibrated against clinical observations. The fraction unbound of GP681 and its intrinsic clearance via AADAC metabolism were estimated through parameter optimization. Subsequent verification across multiple clinical dose groups of GP681 tablets demonstrated strong concordance between simulated profiles and observed clinical data, confirming the robustness of the optimization approach and the reliability of the model parameterization. In summary, the sensitivity analysis confirmed that the model outputs are most dependent on well-defined parameters sourced from in vitro experiments or carefully optimized against clinical data. This provides high confidence in the model's mechanistic structure and its foundation in physiological reality.

Drug-drug interactions

DDI analysis was to evaluate and predict the effect of CYP3A4 inhibitors of varying potency on the pharmacokinetics of GP1707D07 using the verified PBPK platform. The analysis was conducted in two parts: first, the model was validated against a clinical DDI study with the strong inhibitor itraconazole; second, the validated model was applied to simulate untested interactions with moderate and weak inhibitors.

Exposure changes of GP1707D07 with and without the CYP3A4 strong inhibitor (itraconazole)

A modified itraconazole model was developed and verified (Fig. S1A)-D), Table S8). The parameters for the itraconazole monotherapy model are summarized in Table S1, with demographic information and dosage regimens listed in Table S7. The optimized itraconazole model was then used to simulate the impact of itraconazole on the PK of GP1707D07, with the DDI dosage regimen for GP681 and itraconazole provided in Table S8.

We assessed the ability of the model to describe concentration profiles of 20-mg GP681 co-administered with itraconazole in the clinical DDI study (Fig. 4). The ratios of predicted to observed values for the AUC and Cmax of GP1707D07 following administration of GP681 alone were 1.481 and 1.593, respectively. When co-administered with itraconazole, the corresponding ratios for GP1707D07 AUC and Cmax were 1.543 and 2.161. Except for the GP1707D07 Cmax ratio under co-administration (2.161), which represented a slight overprediction, all other PK ratios fell within the acceptable range of 0.5–2.0. Furthermore, the model demonstrated close agreement with clinical observations for the ratios (coadministration/monotherapy) of AUC and Cmax. The model-predicted Cmax and AUC ratios were 1.671- and 2.101-fold respectively, which were consistent with observed values in the clinical DDI study [19]. The results demonstrated significant exposure increases of GP1707D07 when co-administered with moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors. Fluconazole co-administration elevated GP1707D07 Cmax and AUC to 1.509-fold and 2.820-fold of monotherapy values, respectively, while verapamil co-administration resulted in increases to 1.645-fold (Cmax) and 2.347-fold (AUC). These exposure enhancements were comparable to those observed with the strong inhibitor itraconazole. In contrast, weak CYP3A4 inhibitors exhibited minimal pharmacokinetic impact: cimetidine co-administration increased GP1707D07 Cmax and AUC to 1.031-fold and 1.041-fold, respectively, and fluvoxamine co-administration produced increases to 1.075-fold (Cmax) and 1.050-fold (AUC).

In summary, the PBPK model demonstrated strong predictive performance for CYP3A4-mediated drug interactions, successfully validating against clinical DDI data and providing reliable predictions for untested scenarios. The results underscore the need for clinical monitoring and potential dose adjustments when GP681 is used concomitantly with CYP3A4 inhibitors, particularly those of strong and moderate potency.

Discussion

This study successfully developed and validated a comprehensive parent-metabolite PBPK model for the novel influenza PA inhibitor prodrug GP681 and its active metabolite GP1707D07. Leveraging this model, we systematically predicted the DDI potential between GP681 and a spectrum of CYP3A4 inhibitors, ranging from strong to weak. The findings provide crucial quantitative insights into the exposure risks associated with concomitant use, directly addressing a critical gap in the clinical development pathway of GP681 and offering a scientifically robust framework for guiding its safe and effective clinical deployment. For the first time, this research integrates multi-dimensional parameters, including prodrug conversion kinetics, metabolic enzyme inhibition effects, and virtual population variability. This approach not only addresses the existing gap in clinical DDI data for GP681 but also establishes a methodological framework for studying drug interactions involving complex metabolic pathways. By validating the concordance between model predictions and clinical observations, this work underscores the utility of PBPK modeling in guiding antiviral combination therapies and lays the foundation for future personalized dose optimization and risk stratification strategies.

The primary strength of this research lies in the establishment of a robust dual PBPK model capable of simultaneously capturing the pharmacokinetics of the prodrug GP681 and its active metabolite GP1707D07, along with their intricate metabolic interplay. In addition to the parameters determined experimentally, other data were optimized using the built-in Parameter Identification Tool within PK-Sim. The AADAC CLint was optimized to 15 ​μL/min/pmol to characterize the rapid in vivo conversion of the prodrug GP681 to its active metabolite GP1707D07, resulting in prodrug concentrations consistently below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ). Furthermore, the GFR fractions for both GP681 and GP1707D07 were set to 1 to characterize their renal excretion as a physiological process. Concurrently, the optimized fu value for GP681 was determined to be 13%, while the experimentally measured fu value for GP1707D07 was 2.7%. These values indicate a high degree of plasma protein binding for both the parent drug and its active metabolite. Notably, GP1707D07, the primary species undergoing CYP3A4-mediated metabolism and consequently the entity central to the DDI process, exhibits particularly high protein binding, thereby increasing the potential for DDIs when co-administered with potent inhibitors.

Sensitivity analysis further reinforced model reliability by identifying key parameters driving exposure metrics (e.g., GP681 lipophilicity and dissolution, AADAC CLint for conversion, CYP3A4 CLint and fu for GP1707D07 clearance), the sources of which were grounded in experimental data or rigorous optimization against clinical observations. The successful prediction of the clinically observed DDI with the strong CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole, particularly the close agreement in the increase of GP1707D07 exposure (AUC ratio ∼2.1-fold predicted vs 2.5-fold observed, Cmax ratio ∼1.67-fold predicted vs 1.22-fold observed), serves as the cornerstone for model qualification according to regulatory guidance [14]. While the absolute Cmax under co-administration was slightly overpredicted (fold error 2.161), the accurate prediction of the relative change (DDI ratio) is paramount for DDI risk assessment and supports the model's utility for extrapolation to co-administration with moderate and weak inhibitors.

The model confirmed the DDI results observed in clinical trials when GP681 was co-administrated with CYP3A4 strong inhibitor itraconazole. When the DDI model was applied to evaluate concomitant administration of GP681 with inhibitors of varying potency, comparable effects to those observed with the strong inhibitor itraconazole were observed for coadministration with the moderate inhibitor fluconazole and verapamil. In contrast, coadministration with the weak inhibitors cimetidine and fluvoxamine revealed no significant DDI impact. The magnitude of DDI effects is primarily governed by three key determinants [[24], [25], [26]]: (1) the inhibitor's inhibition constant (Ki), where lower Ki values correlate with greater inhibitory potency; (2) the systemic concentration of the inhibitor, with elevated concentrations driving deeper metabolic inhibition; and (3) the underlying inhibition mechanism, wherein time-dependent inhibition, recognized as mechanism-based inactivation, typically exerts substantially stronger effects than reversible competitive or non-competitive inhibition due to its irreversible nature. Consequently, although the moderate inhibitor fluconazole exhibits a significantly higher Ki value than the strong inhibitor itraconazole, its steady-state plasma concentrations in vivo substantially exceed those of itraconazole (Fig. 4B–. 5A). This elevated concentration compensates for its lower intrinsic potency, enabling its DDI effect to reach a level comparable to itraconazole. In contrast, the moderate inhibitor verapamil, compared to itraconazole, acts as a time-dependent inhibitor of CYP3A4. This mechanism-based inactivation contributes to its ability to achieve a DDI impact magnitude similar to that of the strong inhibitor. Similar outcomes have been observed in the study by Ref. [22]. The model was calibrated to 20 ​mg data and CYP3A4 clearance was described using CLint (linear first-order clearance). Because CYP3A4 is a finite-capacity enzyme, higher hepatic substrate concentrations at 40–80 ​mg may approach Km and induce partial saturation, causing clearance to fall below the linear prediction. This mechanism would explain the observed tendency of the model to underpredict AUC at higher doses. Additionally, the apparent systematic overestimation of the predicted GP1707D07 concentration-time profile in Fig. 4A is primarily stems from inter-trial variability. The single-agent model was built using clinical data reporting a Cmax of 23.63 ​ng/mL, while the observed control data from the DDI trial showed a lower Cmax of 19.28 ​ng/mL. This difference in baseline exposure likely reflects inherent variations between the two distinct study populations. Consequently, the apparent overprediction is largely attributable to this difference in baseline values rather than a structural flaw in the model, which accurately captures the relative extent of the drug-drug interaction.

The FDA guidance [14] recommends a default no-effect boundary of 80%–125%, a change in systemic exposure measure within which is considered not clinically significant. However, such a no-effect boundary is generally considered very conservative for drugs that have wide safety margins. Based on the findings from the coadministration of GP681 with itraconazole, a modest increase in Cmax of the active metabolite GP1707D07 was observed. This outcome indicates that CYP3A4 inhibition primarily attenuates the elimination of GP1707D07 rather than causing sharp concentration spikes. Furthermore, GP681 demonstrates a wide therapeutic window and a favorable safety profile. Critically, the referenced clinical study revealed no additional safety concerns attributable to concomitant administration with itraconazole [12]. Therefore, clinical monitoring for adverse reactions is warranted when GP681 tablets are co-administered with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (such as itraconazole) and certain moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors (such as fluconazole and verapamil). Dose reduction should be considered if necessary.

The established PBPK model offers a robust and extensible platform for predicting a wider range of clinically relevant scenarios. Beyond assessing CYP3A4 inhibition, this framework can be directly applied to simulate interactions with CYP3A4 inducers (e.g., rifampin), forecast the pharmacokinetic outcomes of combination therapy with other antivirals, and predict exposure changes in special populations—such as pediatric patients, the elderly, or individuals with hepatic impairment—by incorporating specific physiological parameters. These potential applications, now discussed in the manuscript, underscore the model's significant translational value in guiding future clinical use and rational dose optimization of GP681 across diverse therapeutic contexts.

Limitation

While the model demonstrated strong predictive capability, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the simplification of GP681 hydrolysis to an AADAC-mediated process may limit extrapolation in populations or scenarios where CES2 or CES1 contributions are more pronounced; this assumption should be revisited in future model refinements. Secondly, the intrinsic clearance for CLint, AADAC was optimized rather than directly measured; although validated across doses, absolute confidence in this parameter relies on the accuracy of the underlying clinical data. Thirdly, hepatic/renal impairment, genetic polymorphisms in CYP3A4 or AADAC were not explicitly explored in this DDI context but represent important areas for future investigation using the established model framework.

Conclusion

This study underscores the power of mechanistic PBPK modeling as a critical tool in modern drug development. It efficiently addressed a significant clinical development gap for GP681 by providing quantitative DDI risk assessment across inhibitor classes, circumventing the need for numerous resource-intensive clinical trials. The established dual PBPK model framework offers a valuable methodology for evaluating complex drug interactions involving prodrugs and active metabolites, ultimately contributing to the safer and more effective clinical deployment of GP681 and similar therapeutics. Future work should focus on exploring the impact of patient factors (e.g., organ impairment, pharmacogenetics) and applying the model for personalized dose optimization strategies.
