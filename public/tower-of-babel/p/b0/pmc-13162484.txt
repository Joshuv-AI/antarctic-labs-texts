Adverse childhood experiences, bullying victimization and risk of suicidal ideation among adolescents: evidence from a longitudinal cohort
Authors: Liurong Zhang, Cui Li, Anna Jin, Yuanzhi Ren, Xinyue Wang, Chen Jie, Manlin Zhai, Liang Sun, Qirong Qin, Mingchun Chen, Yehuan Sun, Guangbo Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and bullying victimization are risk factors for adolescent suicidal ideation, yet the interaction between ACEs and bullying victimization in affecting suicidal ideation remains inadequately explored.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted among adolescents. ACEs, bullying victimization and Suicidal ideation were assessed through questionnaire survey. Logistic regression analyses were used to examine the relationship between ACEs, bullying victimization, and suicidal ideation. Interaction analyses were performed to explore the interaction of ACEs and bullying victimization on suicidal ideation.ResultsOne thousand one hundred nine adolescents were included and 7.2% of adolescents reported suicidal ideation. A significant dose–response relationship was observed between the severity of ACEs or bullying victimization and the odds of suicidal ideation. Stratified analysis revealed that the association between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation was significant among adolescents with ACEs exposure, but not among those without ACEs. Notably, the odds of suicidal ideation were highest among adolescents with joint exposure to both ACEs and bullying victimization, compared to those experiencing either factor alone.ConclusionsThis study supports that both ACEs and bullying victimization are associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation in adolescents. In addition, co-exposure to ACEs and bullying victimization was associated with even higher odds of suicidal ideation. These findings emphasize the necessity of early screening and intervention for ACEs and bullying victimization among adolescents, as well as early prevention of bullying victimization among adolescents with ACE exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27120-x.Keywords: Adverse childhood experience, Bullying victimization, Suicidal ideation, Adolescent

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) conceptualizes suicide as a profound behavioral continuum, encompassing suicidal ideation, plans, attempts, and completed suicide [54]. Suicidal ideation—thoughts and cognitive processes involving the desire to end one's life—represents a critical warning sign for subsequent suicide and is a primary target for prevention efforts [25, 46]. The global prevalence of suicidal ideation among adolescents is as high as 17.7% [43], with rates among Chinese adolescents ranging from 18 to 32% [27, 40, 47]. Adolescent suicidal ideation is influenced by multiple risk factors spanning family, community, and school environments [51]. These findings underscore the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors for adolescent suicidal ideation to inform effective prevention strategies [6].

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), encompassing a range of traumatic or stressful events (ie., abuse, neglect, and family dysfunction) occurring before the age of 18, have been identified as a significant predictor for suicidal ideation [7]. Because ACEs typically co-occur in distinct patterns rather than in isolation [2], identifying these specific clusters is essential to understanding their differential impacts on adolescent mental health. Determining how exposure to varying ACE patterns leads to different levels of suicidal ideation risk could therefore facilitate more targeted and effective interventions [59]. Although previous studies explored the association between different types or cumulative number of ACEs and suicidal ideation [7, 16, 53], a critical limitation remains in neglecting the differential impacts of specific ACE exposure patterns on suicidal ideation among adolescents. Latent Class Analysis (LCA) presents a more precise technique for pinpointing subgroups susceptible to ACEs, offering a more accurate depiction of exposure patterns, which offers a viable pathway to address these gaps [59].

Bullying victimization is a widespread and significant stressor in adolescence, with global estimates indicating that one in three young people aged 2–17 experience bullying—affecting nearly one billion individuals worldwide [5]. Furthermore, multiple studies have established a clear association between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation in adolescents [33, 34]. It is also important to note that children exposed to ACEs are at higher risk of being bullied [55]. Grounded in stress sensitivity theory, which posits that early trauma increases sensitivity to later stressors [42], ACEs may exacerbate the negative mental health consequences of bullying. Therefore, clarifying the interaction between ACEs and bullying victimization is critical for designing effective early interventions. Despite a previous longitudinal investigation into their combined effect on internalizing and externalizing problems [49], the specific impact of this interaction on adolescent suicidal ideation remains unexplored.

Therefore, we propose the hypothesis that both ACEs exposure and bullying victimization are associated with higher risk of suicidal ideation and there is a synergistic interaction. In other words, we posit that pre-existing ACEs exacerbate the association between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation, leading to a dose–response relationship wherein combined exposure confers the greatest risk. This study utilized data from the Anhui Child & Adolescent Healthy Growth Cohort (ACAHGC) to examine both the independent effects of ACEs and bullying victimization, and their cumulative effects and interaction, and to provide evidence for early screening and intervention.

Methods

Participants

This study is based on the Anhui Child & Adolescent Healthy Growth Cohort (ACAHGC), a prospective longitudinal cohort designed to track the influence of early-life stressors on child and adolescent development in Anhui Province, China. The cohort's baseline data collection occurred between October and December 2020.

To ensure representativeness, the baseline sample was recruited through a multi-stage random sampling strategy. The process began with the selection of three counties (Funan, Changfeng, Yushan) spanning the geographic diversity of Anhui Province. Within these counties, one or two schools were randomly selected. Subsequently, a baseline survey was conducted from October to December 2020 among all 4th and 7th-grade students (n = 1426) in the sampled schools. These two grades were selected to ensure that participants possessed sufficient comprehension of self-report questionnaires and to maximize the stability of longitudinal follow-up, thereby minimizing the risk of attrition.

Data collection included a baseline wave and four follow-ups over approximately three years. The questionnaire used in this study has previously been published elsewhere [30, 38, 39]. The current study analyzed data derived from the baseline survey and the third wave of follow-up (conducted between May and June 2022). Specifically, ACEs and bullying victimization were assessed at baseline, whereas suicidal ideation was evaluated during the third follow-up wave, 1.5 years after the baseline assessment. Despite the challenges posed by fluctuating school management policies, the final enrollment met the predefined sample size requirements through close collaboration with local education authorities and an extension of the recruitment period. To assess potential selection bias, we compared the demographic characteristics of participants and non-participants. The results revealed no significant systematic differences across the primary available variables, thereby supporting the relative stability of the study cohort (Table S1). An important caveat is that the study could not exclude individuals with suicidal ideation present at baseline. Therefore, while we investigate associations with subsequent ideation, the findings are interpreted as associational rather than strictly causal.

After excluding participants with missing data on key variables, the final analytical sample comprised 1,109 students. The flow of participants through the study is detailed in Fig. 1. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University, and we adhered to the STROBE guidelines for reporting.

Measurements

Assessment of ACEs exposure

Baseline ACEs exposure was measured by Childhood Traumatic Questionnaire (CTQ) [8] and six items of family dysfunction [18]. Time frame for ACEs exposure was defined as since the birth of children. Children were requested to complete questionnaires systematically recording all recalled traumatic exposures from birth through the baseline survey. Experiences of neglect (physical, emotional) and abuse (physical, emotional and sexual) were assessed by CTQ which has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese students [56]. This scale contained 28 items which are scored on a 5-point scale. Experiencing neglect or abuse was defined based on the cut-off scores of five dimensions: physical abuse (≥ 10), emotional abuse (≥ 13), sexual abuse (≥ 8), physical neglect (≥ 10), and emotional neglect (≥ 15) [39]. Participants scoring at or above the corresponding threshold were classified as having experienced that specific type of maltreatment. Each case of neglect or abuse was counted as one ACE score. In addition, household dysfunction was measured by six separate questions as recommended by previous ACEs study [39]: (1) Is there anyone you live with who is addicted to gambling, drugs or alcohol? (2) Have your parents ever separated or divorced? (3) Has your father ever beat your mother? (4) Is there a mental disorder in your family members? (5) Has anyone in your family ever been in prison for crimes? (6) Is your family economy very hard? Each question represented one type of household dysfunction. Participants were considered to have experienced a given type of household dysfunction if they answered “yes” to the corresponding item, and each “yes” response contributed one point to the ACE score. Therefore, in our study, the ACE scores ranged from 0 to 11 and reflected the cumulative level of ACEs exposure.

Assessment of bullying victimization

Baseline bullying victimization was assessed by the Delaware Bullying Victimization Scale-Student (DBVS-S). The DBVS-S is a standardized instrument that assesses four dimensions of bullying victimization over the past six months: verbal bullying, physical bullying, social/relational bullying, and cyberbullying [57]. Given that cyberbullying is relatively less correlated with the school environment, we opted to include only the three dimensions of verbal bullying, physical bullying, and social/relationship bullying [3]. Furthermore, various researchers have expressed concerns regarding whether cyberbullying aligns with the same structure as the other dimensions of bullying [32]. The DBVS-S is comprised of 14 items on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 6 (every day), with higher scores indicating greater levels of bullying victimization. The Chinese version of this scale has been validated with commendable internal consistency [57]. In this study, the Cronbach's α of the scale was 0.921.

Assessment of suicidal ideation

In the third-wave follow-up survey, adolescents’ suicidal ideation was assessed using the Self-Rating Idea of Suicide Scale (SIOSS). This scale is a 26 item checklist that captures thoughts of optimism, despair and suicide in the past year, each item is answered with “yes” or “no”. A higher score on the SIOSS indicates stronger suicidal ideation, the cutoff score of 12 was used to identify presence of suicidal ideation [29]. The SIOSS showed good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents [57]. The Cronbach'α coefficient was 0.808 in this study.

Covariates

Demographic information on adolescents, including age, gender, location of household, self-reported household financial situation, illness or hospitalization in the past year, the only child in the family, education level of father, education level of mother were collected as covariates.

Statistical analysis

Latent class analysis (LCA) was employed to identify distinct patterns of ACEs exposure. We estimated a series of models specifying two through six latent classes. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample size-adjusted BIC (ssaBIC), entropy, and the p-values of the Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (LMRT) and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) [31]. Significant LMRT and BLRT results indicated that a model with K classes provided a better fit than a model with K–1 classes [52]. To account for the potential influence of covariates on class assignment while maintaining classification consistency, we applied the modified three-step approach (R3STEP [50],) to examine associations between demographic covariates and latent class membership. The optimal model was selected based on high entropy and significant improvement in fit per the LMRT and BLRT.

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations of ACEs (specific types, cumulative score, and latent classes) and bullying victimization with adolescent suicidal ideation. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additive and multiplicative interactions between ACEs and bullying victimization were assessed. Additive interaction was evaluated using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), where RERI = OR₁₁ – OR₁₀ – OR₀₁ + 1, with RERI > 0 indicating a positive interaction. Multiplicative interaction was evaluated by the ratio OR₁₁/(OR₁₀ × OR₀₁), where a value greater than 1 suggests a positive interaction.

Given previously reported gender differences in suicidal ideation [58], stratified analyses were performed by gender. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, we excluded adolescents who had experienced illness or hospitalization in the past year, as these factors may influence suicidal ideation [15]. Second, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to identify potential confounders and repeated the analyses adjusting for those variables.

LCA was performed using Mplus version 8.3. Interaction measures were calculated using the “interactionR” package in R. All other analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

1,426 students initially participated in the baseline survey and 1,109 students participated in the wave 4 survey. This study included 1,109 students aged 8 to 15 years (mean age: 11.50 ± 1.59 years). There was no significant difference on baseline characteristics between students who were retained in the study and those lost to follow up (Table S1: Characteristics of participants by follow-up status).

For the exposure of ACEs, 48.3% had experienced at least one type of ACE, with the most prevalent being physical neglect (22.5%). As for the cumulative level of ACEs, 25.4% of students reported experiencing one ACE, 18.8% reported 2 ~ 3 ACEs, and 4.2% reported four or more ACEs. Latent class analysis (LCA) revealed the optimal classification of ACE clusters into three latent classes. This classification demonstrated the lowest AIC, BIC, and ssaBIC values, along with a high entropy value (0.848). Both the LMRT and BLRT tests were significant (Table S2: Results of LCA). The three latent classes were categorized as follows: the "Low ACE level" group (n = 948, 85.5%), which had a lower probability of experiencing any ACE; the "High ACE level" group (n = 66, 5.9%), which had a higher probability of experiencing ACEs; and the "High neglect" group (n = 95, 8.6%), which had an elevated risk of emotional and physical neglect (see Figure S1: Latent classes of ACEs and bullying victimization).

With regard to bullying victimization, 24.8% of students reported having been bullied, with social/relational bullying being the most common type (32.6%). 13.2% students reported with one type of bullying victimization, 6.5% with two types of bullying victimization and 5.1% with three types of bullying victimization. In addition, LCA identified two patterns of bullying victimization that 11.6% students had high exposure to bullying victimization and 88.4% of students had low exposure to bullying victimization.

80 students (7.2%) reported suicidal ideation. The prevalence of suicidal ideation was significantly higher in adolescents exposed to ACEs or bullying victimization. In addition, adolescents with higher cumulative level of ACEs or bullying victimization showed higher prevalence of suicidal ideation. The independent associations between ACEs exposure, bullying victimization and suicidal ideation

Overall, adolescents with ACEs exposure had higher odds of suicidal ideation. There was a positive association between physical abuse, emotional neglect, household financial difficulties and suicidal ideation and the ORs for suicidal ideation ranged from 2.14 to 3.27 (Table 2). There was a significant dose–response relationship between ACEs counts and the odds of suicidal ideation (Table S4: Cumulative level of ACEs and bullying victimization and risk of suicidal ideation). As for the patterns of ACEs exposure, we found that adolescents with “high neglect” (adjusted OR: 2.12, 95%CI: 1.06–4.24) or “high ACE levels” (adjusted OR: 3.37, 95%CI: 1.61–7.07) showed higher odds of suicidal ideation. The association between ACEs and suicidal ideation odds showed no statistically significant differences across gender-specific subgroups (Table S5: Results of gender subgroup analysis).

Similarly, adolescents exposed to bullying victimization also had significantly higher odds of suicidal ideation (adjusted OR: 2.38, 95%CI: 1.44–3.92). In addition, all forms of bullying victimization showed a significant positive association with suicidal ideation. Adolescents who experienced physical bullying had the highest odds of suicidal ideation (adjusted OR: 2.70, 95%CI: 1.48–4.94) (Table 2). Notably, the odds of suicidal ideation increased with cumulative level of bullying victimization. As for the exposure pattern of bullying victimization, compared with low exposure of bullying victimization, adolescents with high exposure of bullying victimization had 2.64 times the odds of suicidal ideation (adjusted OR: 2.64, 95%CI: 1.47–4.73). In gender-specific subgroup analyses, elevated odds of suicidal ideation were observed exclusively among girls exposed to bullying victimization, with no statistically significant association detected in boys (Table S5: Results of gender subgroup analysis).

Combined ACEs exposure and bullying victimization and risk of suicidal ideation

Compared with adolescents without ACEs and bullying victimization exposure, those with only ACEs exposure (adjusted OR: 2.26, 95%CI: 1.99–2.57), only bullying victimization exposure (adjusted OR: 1.58, 95%CI: 1.30–1.93), or both exposure to ACEs exposure and bullying victimization (adjusted OR: 5.39, 95%CI: 4.73–6.14) had higher odds of suicidal ideation. Stratified analyses indicated that bullying victimization was associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation only among adolescents with ACE exposure, whereas this association was not statistically significant among those without ACE exposure (Table 3 and Fig. 2). Consistently, we also observed that the dose–response relationship between level of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation was significant among adolescents with ACEs exposure, (Fig. 2). The interaction effect analysis results indicated that there was a positive additive interaction and a positive multiplicative interaction between ACEs and bullying victimization (Table S6: Interaction effect of ACEs and bullying victimizations). Subgroup analysis also revealed that the interactive effect of bullying victimization and ACEs was more pronounced in girls than boys (Table S7: results of gender subgroup analysis on interaction effect).

Sensitivity analysis

Overall, the results of the sensitivity analysis were consistent with the preliminary analysis, indicating that the main results were robust (Table S8-S13: Results of sensitivity analysis).

Discussion

This study is the first to synthesize the effects of ACEs exposure and bullying victimization on adolescent suicidal ideation. Consistent with most previous studies [11, 13, 26, 48], we also found positive associations between physical abuse, emotional neglect, household financial difficulties, cumulative ACE scores, ACEs exposure patterns and the odds of suicidal ideation among adolescents. In addition, our findings regarding bullying victimization were also in line with previous studies showing that bullying victimization was positively correlated with suicidal ideation and that the odds of suicidal ideation was elevated in parallel to the severity of bullying [12, 14, 22]. More importantly, although a large number of studies have examined the independent effects of ACEs or bullying victimization on suicidal ideation among adolescents, how bullying victimization affects suicidal ideation in the context of ACEs exposure is not yet clear. This study identified that the association between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation was significant only among adolescents with ACEs exposure, and that adolescents who experienced both ACEs and bullying victimization had higher odds of suicidal ideation than those who experienced only one of these experiences. These findings were similar with a previous study on the effect of ACEs and peer victimization, and substance use among adolescents [1]. Positive interactive effects of ACEs and bullying victimization on suicidal ideation highlighted in this study were similar to the study on the outcome of substance use [1], but inconsistent with a previous study on internalizing and externalizing problems of adolescents which did not find significant interaction between ACEs and bullying victimization [49]. In summary, these findings provide further support for cumulative risk model of adversity and stress-sensitivity theoretical model [42]. Subgroup analysis revealed that boys had higher odds of suicidal ideation than girls under comparable exposure to the same adverse experiences, which contrasted with previous studies [58, 60]. A potential explanation for this is that impulsive personality traits in boys exposed to negative experiences could increase the odds of suicidal ideation [10]. In addition, the current study found that the interaction between ACEs and bullying victimization was particularly significant among female adolescents. Specifically, following exposure to cumulative negative life events, female adolescents exhibit a higher susceptibility to internalizing symptoms, such as rumination and heightened emotional reactivity. These cognitive-emotional traits have been established as critical predictors of suicidal ideation [20, 23]. At the neurobiological level, early-life adversity may lead to the sensitization or dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and other stress-regulatory systems. This process effectively lowers the threshold for subsequent stressors to precipitate psychopathological symptoms [24]. Evidence suggests that females exhibit heightened sensitivity development [21, 45, 44]. Therefore, the elevated risk observed in girls reflects specific developmental vulnerabilities and gender-stratified stress-processing mechanisms, rather than inherent psychological weaknesses.

Based on the core findings of this study, we must be vigilant against the potential "secondary" harm from bullying victimization following ACEs exposure. This recognition carries significant implications for policy-making, clinical interventions, and public health strategies, particularly in the context of developing targeted screening and preventive measures. ACEs are predominantly encountered during early childhood or may persist throughout the entire childhood period. Bullying victimization tend to be more prevalent in later childhood and early adolescence [19]. Hence, the importance of early detection cannot be overstated, and we advocate for the implementation of school-based screening programs targeting ACEs and bullying victimization during entire childhood into early adolescence [35]. Screeners must recognize that children and adolescents are often susceptible to multiple forms of victimization or mental health issues [49]. It is crucial to devote increased attention to those who suffer from bullying victimization or additional mental health challenges subsequent to ACEs, as this demographic is more prone to suicidal ideation. For interventions in ACEs, The Centers for Disease Control (CDC advocates (1 augmenting financial support for families utilizing superior children's services,(2 endorsing nonviolent social norms; and (3 imparting children and adolescents with stress management, conflict resolution, and emotional control skills. Bullying predominantly occurs within schools, where children and adolescents dedicate the majority of their time. Consequently, schools should have the primary responsibility for dealing with bullying victimization [44]. We advocate for the implementation of school-centric anti-bullying strategies, complemented by community involvement. These strategies can be enacted through a threefold approach: (1) integrating curricular education to foster anti-bullying sentiments among students,(2) enhancing social competencies in adolescents; and (3) initiating comprehensive school interventions that involve students, peer groups, classrooms, educators, and school administrators in a collective effort to transform the school’s culture. Moreover, adolescents exposed to ACE who also experience bullying victimization warrant heightened attention. We advocate for integrated interventions targeting both ACE and bullying victimization, with comprehensive strategies encompassing family, school, and community levels to address the associated harms [37]. Notably, boys are often less willing to seek help from friends, family, or professionals [9], which can result in more serious injuries. Therefore, we should pay more attention to the risk of suicidal ideation in boys in our screening and prevention efforts. In short, more work needs to be done on how to prevent adolescent adverse experiences and bullying victimization and what protective factors may mitigate the impact of adolescent adverse experiences and bullying victimization on health risks.

Our study shows several strengths. We combined type-specific ACEs, cumulative ACE scores, and ACE exposure patterns to explore the association between ACEs and suicidal ideation, which is more comprehensive compared to previous studies [11, 26, 48]. In addition, we investigated the combined and interactive effects of ACEs and bullying victimization, elucidating their comprehensive impact on the suicidal ideation among adolescents. However, it should be noted that the present study has some limitations. First, a key limitation of our study is that we were unable to exclude participants with pre-existing suicidal ideation at baseline. Therefore, while our findings indicate a strong prospective association, the causal interpretation should be made with caution. Second, a significant limitation of this study is the absence of baseline suicidal ideation assessment. Consequently, we were unable to differentiate between incident (new-onset) and persistent suicidal ideation over the 1.5-year follow-up period. This measurement gap may have led to an overestimation of the prospective associations between ACEs, bullying victimization, and suicidal ideation, as pre-existing suicidal ideation could confound these relationships. Nevertheless, the robustness of the observed associations and the evident dose–response relationship suggest that early adversity and peer victimization remain critical risk factors for adolescent suicidality. Future research should incorporate baseline assessments of suicidal ideation prior to the follow-up period. Only by controlling for initial symptoms can we accurately determine the independent explanatory power of these stressors in the development of suicidal ideation. Third, the use of a self-report questionnaire to collect data introduces recall bias, which may affect the results [17]. Fourth, we conducted only one survey on suicidal ideation, and although the assessment of suicidal ideation was conducted after baseline, the failure to exclude adolescents with potential pre-existing suicidal ideation at baseline precludes our ability to establish a causal relationship between exposure and outcome. Future studies should implement multiple follow-ups to model group trajectories and investigate its correlation with negative experiences. Fifth, this study did not include cyberbullying within its assessment framework. Given that cyberbullying is characterized by high covertness and wide dissemination, its exclusion may lead to an underestimation of adolescents' overall level of bullying exposure. Although this research focuses on stressors within the physical space of the school, cyberbullying and traditional bullying may have synergistic effects in triggering suicidal ideation. Future research should consider employing specialized and validated cyberbullying measurement tools to incorporate both online and offline bullying into a single model. This would allow for a more holistic assessment of the comprehensive impact of the digital environment on adolescent mental health. Finally, it is imperative to consider the broader socio-environmental context during the study period, particularly the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Recent meta-analytic evidence indicates that the global prevalence of clinically significant depressive and anxiety symptoms—both established precursors to suicidal ideation—doubled during the pandemic [41]. Pandemic-specific stressors, including prolonged social isolation, school closures, and escalated familial tension, potentially intensified the deleterious effects of bullying victimization and ACEs on adolescent mental health [28, 36]. Furthermore, restricted access to peer support and professional mental health services during lockdowns may have compromised the 'buffering effect' that typically mitigates suicide risk [4]. Therefore, while our findings underscore the longitudinal associations between ACEs, bullying victimization, and suicidal ideation, the observed risk levels may have been further amplified by the unique stressors associated with the pandemic context. Furthermore, the representativeness of the sample may be constrained by the unique circumstances of the pandemic. Students experiencing severe psychological distress, lacking family support, or having limited digital resources were more difficult to reach. Consequently, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to populations in non-pandemic contexts.

Conclusion

Our findings support that both ACEs and bullying victimization are positively associated with the risk of suicidal ideation among adolescents. More importantly, it is critical to note that the co-occurrence of ACEs and bullying victimization substantially elevates the risk for suicidal ideation beyond the impact of either factor alone. There is a significant positive interaction between ACEs and bullying victimization and early exposure to ACEs may amplify the impact of bullying victimization on suicidal ideation. We call for the implementation of comprehensive intervention programs directed at individuals affected by ACEs and bullying victimization. Early-stage synchronous screening for ACEs and bullying victimization should be implemented, coupled with proactive support for vulnerable populations with prior ACE exposure to mitigate impact of future bullying victimization exposure.

Furthermore, we emphasize although both boys and girls exhibit susceptibility to suicidal ideation, the demonstrably heightened risk of suicidal ideation associated with ACEs and bullying victimization in boys necessitates prioritized intervention focus for this population to effectively facilitate their healthy development.
