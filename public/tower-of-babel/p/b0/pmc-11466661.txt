Flexural behavior of over-reinforced beam with ECC layer: Experimental and numerical simulation study
Authors: Qunwei Wu, Jieyong You, Hui Wang, Dongbo Wan, Zhenguo Hou, Yanpeng Li, Yinghao Wang, Xing Chen, Long Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo optimize the brittle failure of reinforced conerete (RC) over-reinforeed beams and enhance their flexural performance, a novel structural form is proposed. To be specific, the Engineered Cementitious Composite (ECC) layer is installed on top of the RC over-reinforced beam (ERCOB). A total of six test beams are prepared, comprising one unreinforced beam and five reinforced beams. The variables comprised the depth of the ECC, reinforcement ratio, and whether the ECC is configured at the bottom. The test findings are subsequently compared with simulation outcomes to validate the model's precision. Next, the influence of various variables on ERCOB flexural performance, such as load-deflection response, bearing capacity, etc., is deeply analyzed. The research indicates that the ECC applied to both the top and bottom of the specimen exhibits enhanced bearing capacity and ductility. In comparison to CB-1, the maximum load and deflection ductility coefficient of EB-2 increased from 45.73 kN to 2.63–48.52 kN and 3.85, representing increases of 6.1 % and 29.6 %, respectively. It reveals that ECC layer improves the defects caused by excessive reinforcement of over-reinforced beams, and optimizes the tensile capacity of the steel bars, thus improving the bending capacity and ductility of the specimens. Finally, the prediction model of ERCOB flexural capacity is proposed to further verify the effectiveness of ERCOB. This study not only verifies the effectiveness of ECC reinforcement, but also helps to delay the failure process of structures, provide reference for future engineering application design.Keywords: Over-reinforced beam, Engineered cementitious composite (ECC), Flexural behavior, Bearing capacity, Numerical simulation analysis, Prediction model

Introduction

To overcome building aging, damage and material property degradation, the structure is further subjected to greater loads. The demand for structural retrofitting and reinforcement has increased dramatically over the past few years. In recent decades, researchers have thoroughly examined reinforcement methods, including externally bonded steel sheets [1] and fiber reinforced polymers (FRP) [2]. Externally bonded steel plate reinforcement has some shortcomings (e.g., heavy steel plate handling difficulties, and corrosion). Besides, external bonding FRP laminates reinforcement technology exhibits several disadvantages. Nematzadeh et al. [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]] used FRP to replace the internal materials of RC beams to carry out a series of experimental studies, and the results demonstrated that FRP can substantially improve the bending performance of the specimen, but there are still some drawbacks. For instance, FRP composites cannot be compressed well under low cyclic load; in addition, the FRP plate and concrete cannot cooperate well, the reinforced beams will be subjected to brittle failure [8]. Engineering cementing composite (ECC) is a novel material that has superior performance in various domains. In comparison to conventional concrete, it exhibits significantly elevated tensile strain resistance. [9,10], which indicates that ECC has high ductility. Furthermore, ECC exhibits excellent toughness and energy absorption ability [[11], [12], [13]]. Moreover, ECC has good durability. In a humid environment, the material reaction in the ECC prevents erosion ions from entering the interior of the ECC, which notably reduces the corrosion degree of rebar in ECC [14,15]. As revealed by existing result, the diffusion coefficient and water permeability of chloride ions hardly vary before and after the cracking of ECC [16]. As seen in Fig. 1, ECC has been widely used in a number of projects (such as offshore platforms, nuclear engineering containment, and underwater tunnels).

Application scenario.

Many researchers have done experimental research on concrete structures with ECC, and the results show that employing ECC to enhance RC structures is a more efficacious method for improvement. Emara et al. examined the flexural performance of ECC-RC beams. The findings demonstrate that reinforced beams show considerable improvements in bearing capacity, stiffness, and deformation compared to unreinforced beams. Zhang [17] used ECC to reinforce piers and evaluate the performance of a bridge. As indicated by the results, ECC can enhance the mechanical qualities and longevity of bridge piers. Ge [18] examined the flexural performance of ECC-reinforced RC beams by experimental approaches. The main characteristics analyzed the height of the ECC. The findings indicate that substituting the concrete on the tensile side of a RC beam with ECC of equivalent height enhances the strength of the specimen and optimizes the breadth and distribution of fractures. Zhang [19] performed an extensive examination of the bending characteristics of RC specimens including ECC. When the height of the ECC attains 30 % of the beam height, the ductility of the RC beam significantly increases. Guan [20] performed a comprehensive examination of ECC-RC beams via testing, noting the effect of the bond between the ECC and the concrete interface on flexural load capacity and cracking behavior. The load-deflection curve and strain development are studied. The results suggest that the composite beams reinforced with ECC have higher flexural capacity. Singh [21] analyzed the bending performance of 12 test beams. As indicated by the results, in comparison to the unreinforced beam, the RC beam augmented with ECC exhibits an enhanced load-bearing capacity, which is 5.37–8.31 times that of the unreinforced beam. The ductility of ECC-concrete beams is 31 times greater than that of unreinforced beams. Wang et al. [22] studied the performance of RC beams using ECC layers. The ECC depth is specified to be 20 mm and 40 mm. The bearing capacity of the ECC-concrete specimens is enhanced by 89 % in comparison to the unreinforced specimens. In conclusion, ECC is effective as an innovative high-performance material for RC beam reinforcement. ECC has superior performance in bending and cracking resistance, while also enhancing the durability of RC structures and prolonging its service cycle.

In practical engineering, the brittle fracture of RC beams can result from excessive reinforcement design or over-reinforcement, which should be avoided. The results reveal that the deflection and cracks of beam are smaller when the concrete failure on the compressive side precedes the rebar yield. It can be observed that when the over-reinforced beam is damaged, the tensile steel bar still does not yield, indicating that the tensile strength of the rebar is not fully developed, such that the over-reinforced beam is not welcome in practice [23]. The strength of concrete structure will gradually decrease during use, in addition, when the poured concrete fails to meet the expected strength requirements, it may lead to the problem of excessive reinforcement of the beam body. In light of this condition, the majority of contemporary study mostly concentrates on augmenting the beam section height or reinforcing the compression side to avert the incidence of brittle compression failure. Placing steel and other reinforcement materials on top of the beam is an effective restraint method. For example, spiral rebar is often used in the compression side of the RC structures. Hadi [24] studied beams wrapped with steel bars. The findings indicate that the ductility coefficient of the specimen with spiral reinforcement exceeds that of the unconstrained beam, and the ductility index rises as the pitch decreases. Brittle failure resulting from the elevated reinforcement ratio of the tensile rebar can be mitigated by imposing limitations on the compression side of the RC specimen. Mohamed [25] performed experimental investigations on the compression area of over-reinforced beams. The findings indicate that spiral constraints enhance the ductility of RC specimens more substantially than rectangular limitations. Priastiwi [26] placed an additional restraint stirrup within the compression section of the RC structure. The bending performance of the beam is enhanced in comparison to the unreinforced beam. At present, there are few researches on ECC as compression material on top of over-reinforced beams. Accordingly, it is very necessary to verify its feasibility from the theoretical level.

In view of the fact that numerical simulation can make up for test defects, avoid wasting time, consuming a lot of manpower and material resources, and occupying a large area, some scholars have used this method in the research of RC beams. Arulanandam [27] employed a numerical model to investigate the effect of ECC depth on the bearing capacity of RC beams, comparing the model's predictions with experimental outcomes. The findings are closely aligned, after which the parameters are examined in detail based on the validated numerical model. The study suggest that when the reinforcement ratio is 1.7 % and the ECC layer height replacement ratio is 40 %, the beams reinforced with the ECC layer exhibit superior flexural capability. Singh [28] cemented ECC plates on the tension and compression sides of RC beams to strengthen the test beams, used ABAQUS to build finite element models, and conducted specific parameter studies. The study examined parameters like as the depth of the ECC layer, the epoxy resin binder, and the cement mortar, focusing on the bending capabilities of the reinforced beams, which proves its effectiveness in RC beam reinforcement. In conclusion, the numerical model serves as an excellent instrument for assessing the bending capacity and failure mechanism of RC specimens augmented with ECC.

Previous studies indicate a scarcity of research about the application of ECC layers in the compression zones of over-reinforced structures. Therefore, this paper makes innovations from three aspects: First of all, the issue of excessive reinforcing in RC structures can be mitigated by enhancing the damage mode and augmenting both flexural capacity and ductility. Secondly, the effect of pertinent design elements on the flexural properties of over-reinforced beams is examined comprehensively. Thirdly, a theoretical model is presented that may effectively forecast the flexural capacity of test beams. This research presents a novel approach for applying ECC layers over RC over-reinforced beams, predicated on the superior performance of ECC, as shown in Fig. 2. This approach enhances the fragile fracture characteristics of over-reinforced beams and increases their ductility. On this basis, the crack development and damage mode of the specimen with ECC layer were observed after loading. The load-deflection response, ductility index, and strain distribution of the experimental beam are analyzed. The numerical model is created using ABAQUS software, and its accuracy is validated by comparing the simulation outcomes with the test data. Further, the parameters of the test specimen with ECC layer are studied in detail. The parameters include depth of ECC, diameter of tensile rebar and ECC modulus of elastic. Finally, based on the existing theoretical calculation formula, a theoretical formula for the bearing capacity of over-reinforced beams with an ECC layer is given, effectively predicting the bearing capacity of the enhanced over-reinforced beams. This study not only validates the effectiveness of ECC reinforcement, delays the failure process of structure, improve the durability of the structure, and provide reference for future engineering application design.

Table 4 lists the basic information of the test specimen. CB-1 denotes the control beam, measuring 550 mm in total length and possessing a cross-sectional dimension of 100 × 100 mm. Measurement and loading

In this experiment, a microcomputer controlled electro-hydraulic servo general testing device was used for loading. This study used the three-point loading approach. The measured span of the test beam is 450 mm, with the loading point located at the mid-span and the support positioned 50 mm from the beam's end. The specimen was subjected to displacement at a rate of 1.0 mm/min. A sensor is positioned at the bottom of the mid-span of the test beam to measure the mid-span deflection value. Before the concrete is poured, a strain gauge is attached to the center of the tensile rebar, and the gauze coated with epoxy resin glue is protected to measure the strain of the rebar. Affix five 100 mm strain gauges at uniform vertical intervals along the mid-span surface of the pure bend section of the test specimen span to assess the strain distribution of the section. Experimental results and discussion

Failure mode

The crack propagation and damage state of the test beam in the process of loading are observed and then described (Fig. 8).

Crack development of the test beam: (a) CB-1; (b) EB-5; (c) EB-6; (d) EB-2; (e) EB-3; (f) EB-4.

The control beam exhibited a characteristic over-reinforcement failure; during loading, the rebar did not yield, and ultimately, the test beam broke due to the fracture of the top concrete, so it can be judged as bending failure, as shown in Fig. 8(a). During the initial loading phase, the initial vertical crack is observed near the bottom edge of the mid-span portion of CB-1, at which time the load reaches 8.19 kN. Later, as the load is risen to 37 kN, the quantity of cracks is increased, although the crack width remains rather stable. Due to the presence of rebars in the beam tension area, the load reaches a peak value of 45.73 kN, the concrete at the upper section is found to be fragmented, the longitudinal rebar under tension remains unyielding, and CB-1 exhibits the brittle damage state characteristic of an over-reinforced beam. As the strain intensifies, the quantity, width, and length of cracks proliferate, exhibiting characteristics of shear failure, whereas no obvious deformation occurs in CB-1.

Similar to the control beam, the fissures propagate from the tension area of the beam to the point of load application. Although the reinforcement degree is different, the typical bending failure occurs in the reinforced beams. Fig. 8(d)–(f) illustrate the fracture distribution and damage mechanisms of ERCOB constructed with ECC layers of varying thicknesses. Compared with CB-1, EB-2, EB-3 and EB-4 all show higher ultimate bearing capacity and deformation capacity. The cracking load and maximum load of EB-2 reach 12.5 kN and 48.52 kN, respectively. The cracking load and peak load of EB-3 are obtained as 12.06 kN and 50.16 kN, respectively. The cracking load and peak load of EB-4 are determined as 12.74 kN and 53.94 kN, respectively. In the early loading stage, EB-2, EB-3, and EB-4 have similar performance. During the initial loading phase, the first fracture of the specimen manifested at the mid-span region. During the loading operation, oblique cracks were observed in the shear pure bending section, extending towards the loading point at a 45-degree angle. Upon attaining peak load, varying degrees of debonding manifest at the interface between ERCOB's ECC and concrete, with increased depth of the ECC layer correlating to a more pronounced disbonding phenomenon.

Fig. 8(b) demonstrates that the cracking load of EB-5 is 13.56 kN, whereas the peak load is 51.86 kN. The crack growth of EB-5 before peak load is similar to that of EB-3. At peak load, the quantity of cracks in EB-5 is comparable to that in EB-3, but the width is increased significantly, mainly because EB-5 has a higher reinforcement ratio than EB-3. During unloading, the bulge can be seen in the ECC layer on the left side of EB-5, which is because the concrete is not a strict and uniform rigid body. During the test, fracture propagation intensifies with the rise in load, and the angle of the loading point relative to the first crack exacerbates the propagation on the left side.

As depicted in Fig. 8(c), the cracking and peak load of EB-6 are 13.65 kN and 50.77 kN, respectively. Upon the emergence of the initial vertical crack, the rupture of fibers within the ECC at the base becomes audible. As the stress escalates, the tensile rebar in EB-6 yields, resulting in a rise in the number of cracks, which continue to propagate higher. The interfacial stress between the ECC at the base and the concrete matrix escalates with increasing load, and the fibers at a particular section cannot bear any more loads and begin to slip out of the matrix at peak load. Therefore, the failure of EB-6 was caused by the failure of local bonding between the ECC at the bottom and the concrete matrix.

Load-deflection response

The deflection changes of each specimen were observed during the loading process as the load escalated, and the results were systematically arranged and depicted as load-deflection curves. The load-deflection response is categorized into three phases: (1) uncracked stage; (2) cracking to peak load stage; (3) failure stage. Fig. 9(a–c) illustrates the load-deflection response of the beams utilized in this study.

Load-deflection response of the beam: (a) different depths of ECC; (b) different reinforcement ratios; (c) whether the ECC layer is positioned at the base.

Fig. 9(a) illustrates the load-deflection responses of CB-1 and ERCOB with varying ECC layer thicknesses. CB-1 is identified as an over-reinforced beam, exhibiting concrete compression failure in the compression area, while the longitudinal tensile reinforcement does not yield. The ECC layer results in EB-2 exhibiting higher cracking and maximum loads compared to CB-1, but the load-deflection response of EB-2 has a more gradual fall than that of CB-1. This signifies an improvement in the compressive strength and deformation capability of the upper edge of the ERCOB section. Increasing the depth of the ECC layer from 5 mm to 15 mm results in minimal variation in the stiffness of ERCOB. Nonetheless, their maximum load escalates with the augmentation of ECC layer depth. The load-deflection response of EB-4 significantly decreases when the load attains its peak, attributed to the damage of the ECC on the compression side resulting from the separation at the bond between EB-4's ECC and the RC beam.

During the transition from cracking load to maximum load, it is evident that the rigidity of EB-5 surpasses that of EB-3, as shown in Fig. 9(b). Furthermore, the maximum load of EB-5 surpasses that of EB-3, mostly due to EB-5's superior reinforcement ratio, which enhances the flexural performance of ERCOB to a certain degree.

Fig. 9(c) contrasts the load-deflection response with and without the ECC layer positioned at the base of the ERCOB. Before cracking, the load-deflection responses of EB-3 and EB-6 coincide and the stiffness is almost the same. The stiffness of EB-6 exhibits a modest increase compared to EB-3, from cracking load to maximum load, however the variation is not significant. Due to the ultra-high ductility of ECC, EB-6 deforms significantly when the peak load is reached.

Table 5 illustrates that the bearing capacity of EB-2 exceeds that of CB-1 by 6.1 %, whereas the associated deflection is 61.5 % greater. The ECC layer positively addresses the deficiencies arising from the reinforcement of CB-1, enhances the utilization rate of reinforcement, and consequently improves the bearing capacity and ductility of specimens, which is valuable in engineering practice. Ductility

Ductility, which reflects the ability of a member to resist inelastic deformation without great loss of strength before failure, is a critical design criterion in the majority of RC structure design codes. Under the action of load, members with good ductility can produce large deformation, which provides early warning for structural failure. In addition, ductility can also reflect the energy absorption capacity of the component. Strain

Fig. 10(a–f) illustrates the strain distribution along the height of the section during the loading of the test beam. Positive numbers denote tensile strain in the loaded component, while negative values signify compressive strain in the loaded component. The concrete strain exhibits a linear distribution throughout the height of the segment under varying loads, which indicates that the assumption of a flat section is applicable. The strain distribution indicates that the strain at the bond between the ECC and the concrete matrix is continuous during the initial loading phase. Throughout the loading operation, the neutral axis progressively ascends, leading to a reduction in the height of the compression section. The tension zone of the concrete gradually ascends as the bending moment intensifies. In addition, ECC exhibits excellent strain-hardening performance with increasing load.

Fig. 11 illustrates the load-strain relationship of the rebar. Under identical strain conditions, the load supported by EB-2 is markedly greater than that supported by CB-1, indicating that the bending capacity of the RC beam can be enhanced by incorporating an ECC layer in the compression area. The load-strain relationship of rebar in EB-2, EB-3, and EB-4 demonstrates that the bearing capacity of ERCOB increases with the depth of the ECC layer. This means that the ECC layer can share some of the tension on the rebar. Numerical analysis

Numerical model

This paper employs ABAQUS simulation software to model the loading of the test beam. It can precisely demonstrate the material's nonlinear behavior. Steel ingots are positioned at support and loading locations to mitigate localized failure resulting from stress concentration.

The C3D8R element is utilized to model concrete, ECC, and pads, while the T3D2 element is used to represent rebar. A “Tie” constraint is adopted between the pad and concrete, and the rebar contacts concrete with the use of an “Embedment”. The test beam adopted simply supported constraints, i.e., constrain the displacement degrees of freedom to U1, U2, U3 = 0 at one end, and U1, U2 = 0 at the opposite end. The angular degrees of freedom at both ends of the beam are the same, UR2, UR3 = 0. The bond between the RC beam and the support is modeled using the “Tie” method, with the “surface-surface” contact mode employed between the concrete and the ECC. The numerical model has a mesh size of 10mm × 10 mm (Fig. 12).

Numerical model.

Concrete

Two primary methodologies utilized for concrete simulation in ABAQUS are the distributed crack model and the concrete plastic damage (CDP) model [33]. In contrast to the dispersed crack model, the CDP model is applicable for simulating the mechanical behavior of concrete under both static loads and dynamic actions. Using ABAQUS, Lee [34] developed a numerical model, presuming the absence of slip between the reinforcement and concrete. The tensile damage morphology derived from the numerical model closely resembles the crack distribution observed in the tests, and the strength and deformation values produced by the numerical model align well with the test values. In addition, there is no convergence problem in CDP model. The simulation results obtained by Raza [35] with the aforementioned settings are in strong agreement with the experimental results. Bonding interface

The computation of shear strength at the bonding between the ECC and concrete involves two parameters: the friction coefficient and shear-friction enhancement. ABAQUS provides two varieties of traction-separation models that simulate surface connectivity. The initial approach involves establishing cohesive units of specific depth between the ECC and the concrete to replicate their contact. Verification of numerical model

Load-deflection response

The whole loading process of EB-3 and EB-6 was simulated by the numerical model.

The experimental load-deflection response indicates that the cracking loads for EB-3 and EB-6 are 12.06 kN and 13.65 kN, respectively. The crack extends laterally, and the quantity of cracks escalates with the increase in strain. When the rebar yields, the corresponding yield loads of EB-3 and EB-6 are 38.01 kN and 39.26 kN, respectively. As the burden persists in escalating, the test beam enters the failure stage, during which the stress of the tensile rebar remains constant at the yield strength. As the load attains its maximum, the concrete above the section is compressed, whereas the tensile rebar retains its elasticity. The corresponding maximum loads of EB-3 and EB-6 are 50.16 kN and 50.77 kN, respectively.

The numerical model closely aligns with the load-deflection response seen in the test, as illustrated in Fig. 16. The breaking loads of EB-3-FE and EB-6-FE are 12.63 kN and 14.35 kN, respectively, exceeding the experimental results by 4.73 % and 5.13 %. This can be explained as follows: a. The hydration reaction of the cement in the concrete may induce little cracks during the first phase of the test; b. The boundary conditions set by the model are marginally different from the test; c. The link defined in the numerical model is considered ideal, assuming a complete bond between the rebar and concrete. During the experiment, a bond slip will occur between the rebar and concrete, resulting in a decrease in the adhesive strength between the two substances. The yield loads for EB-3-FE and EB-6-FE are 39.22 kN and 40.35 kN, respectively, representing increases of 3.18 % and 2.78 % over the experimental values, which are in close proximity to the experimental findings. The corresponding peak loads of EB-3-FE and EB-6-FE reach 52.12 kN and 52.24 kN, respectively, 3.91 % and 2.90 % higher than the experimental results, and somewhat greater than the experimental peak loads.

In brief, the load-deflection response generated using the numerical model and the test outcomes fit well, suggesting that the model can simulate the load development of the test beam well.

Failure mode

For the failure state of the specimen, the numerical model can simulate the corresponding crack and damage state. The test specimen exhibits a failure mechanism characterized by concrete crushing, predominantly associated with the yielding of the tension rebar and the cracking of the concrete in the tension area. The damage distribution produced by the aforementioned failure mode can be accurately documented by exporting DAMAGET from ABAQUS. Fig. 17 illustrates the crack pattern for the test specimens EB-3 and EB-6, together with the related numerical simulation.

The fracture propagation predicted by the numerical model aligns with the experimental observations, as illustrated in Fig. 17(a–d). In the case of EB-3 and EB-6, the initial vertical crack manifests near the bottom edge of the bending section during the early loading phase. As the load increases, the quantity, width, and length of cracks consistently escalate, with cracks observed in the shear bending region. After the rebar yield, the crack distribution turns out to be dense, suggesting that the crack has developed fully after the tensile rebar yield. Consequently, the concrete in the compression area is fractured.

Conclusions

The conclusions of this study are derived from experimentation, numerical simulation, and theoretical analysis as outlined below:

(1)Applying an ECC layer above the beam can mitigate flaws resulting from over-reinforcement and enhance the beam's bending performance. In comparison to CB-1, the peak load of EB-2 rose from 45.73 kN to 48.52 kN, reflecting a 6.1 % increase, while the deflection ductility index improved from 2.63 to 3.85, indicating a 29.6 % increase. This method may serve as a reference for enhancing the failure mechanism of over-reinforced beams in future practical applications.

In comparison to CB-1, the peak load of EB-2 rose from 45.73 kN to 48.52 kN, reflecting a 6.1 % increase, while the deflection ductility index improved from 2.63 to 3.85, indicating a 29.6 % increase. This method may serve as a reference for enhancing the failure mechanism of over-reinforced beams in future practical applications.

(2)The depth of the ECC can enhance the bending capacity of the RC beam. The bearing capacity demonstrated by ERCOB can be improved with an increase in the depth of the ECC. In comparison to EB-2, the peak loads of EB-3 and EB-4 rose from 48.52 kN to 50.16 kN and 53.94 kN, respectively, reflecting increases of 3.4 % and 7.8 %. The results may serve as a benchmark for improving the bearing capability of analogous structures in bridge engineering.
