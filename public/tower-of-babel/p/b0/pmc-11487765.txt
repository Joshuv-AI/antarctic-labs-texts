Rubella epidemiology in Lesotho after vaccine introduction: a five-year review, 2018–2022
Authors: Thabelo Makhupane, DSK Habedi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rubella virus is a major contributor to birth defects globally and is preventable by vaccination. In 2020, the world was supposed to be free of both rubella and Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS) however this goal has yet to be realized with only 93 out of 194 WHO member states confirmed rubella-free in 2020.MethodsA retrospective measles and rubella case-based surveillance data record review was conducted from 2018 to 2022 to document rubella epidemiology after the introduction of rubella vaccination in Lesotho and progress toward elimination. All samples submitted for surveillance purposes and tested for rubella were considered but only filtered according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the data.ResultsOf the 1041 samples that were tested for rubella between 2018 and 2022, 10 (1%) were confirmed measles positive and were excluded from further analysis. The median age of the respondents was 6.0 (IQR 4.0–8.0.) years. About 643 (62.4%) of respondents were in the age category of 5 - <13 years. Rubella prevalence was 1% (95% CI; 0.5 -1.8%). The non-measles, non-rubella rash illness rate of 2 per 100 000 population was obtained at the national level each year of the study period but by only 2 of the country’s 10 districts in 2021.ConclusionsThe study showed low rubella prevalence. Rubella infection was predominant in those aged 5 - < 13 years. Failure to meet surveillance targets at certain time points during the study period may have led to an underestimation of rubella cases. There is a need to improve the quality of measles and rubella surveillance in Lesotho. Supplementary immunization activities would also be useful in closing immunity gaps, limiting outbreaks, and advancing rubella and CRS elimination in Lesotho.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20081-z.Keywords: Congenital Rubella Syndrome, Lesotho, Rubella, Vaccination

Background

Rubella is an infectious airborne disease that predominantly presents in childhood and the early stages of adulthood. Although rubella infection is mild and self-limiting, its significance is in its potential to cause congenital abnormalities. These abnormalities are called Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS) [1]. By 2020, the world was supposed to be free of both rubella and CRS, but in 2020, only 93 of the 194 World Health Organization (WHO) member states were verified as rubella-free [2]. In 2019, 173 member states had initiated rubella vaccination [3]. The coverage of rubella vaccination worldwide increased from 40% in 2012 to 68% in 2022 [4]. The number of rubella cases reported to the WHO each year has declined, with 1.7 cases per million reported in 2018 compared to 13.9 cases per million in 2007 [5]. Although progress towards elimination has been made, rubella remains a public health concern, with an estimated 100,000 infants born with CRS annually worldwide, according to the WHO [6]. In 2022, 10,361 rubella cases were reported in the African region, representing an incidence rate of 9 cases per million population [4]. Between 2012 and 2016, 748 rubella cases were reported in Lesotho [7]. Nine cases of CRS were also reported during the same period [8].

Rubella is caused by the rubella virus, a togavirus that belongs to the genus of Rubi virus. The time between exposure to the rubella virus and the appearance of the first symptoms is 14–23 days. Early signs of illness occur in the second week after exposure, and consist of fever, malaise, and mild conjunctivitis. Other presentations include the presence of lymph nodes behind the ear, the occipital region, and the posterior cervical region. An erythematous, pruritic, and maculopapular rash has been described in 50–80% of affected persons. This infection, however, can also be subclinical with no reported rash in 20–50% of cases. Joint symptoms like arthritis and arthralgia occur predominantly in adult women [6].

CRS occurs when a mother contracts rubella in the 1st trimester of pregnancy leading to disease and birth defects in the new-born. This can occur in up to 90% of maternal infections [6]. CRS is associated with many abnormalities in different organ systems.

In Lesotho, the most common birth defects associated with CRS were cardiac (patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary stenosis) and ophthalmic (cataracts, glaucoma, microphthalmia) [8].

Similar findings were described in studies conducted in the Philippines and South Africa [9, 10]. Other manifestations of CRS in Lesotho that have been described include splenomegaly, thrombocytopenia, and mental retardation [8]. Late onset manifestations of CRS have also been described. These include diabetes mellitus and thyroid disorders which were found to be more prevalent in this population [11]. Progressive rubella panencephalitis is a rare condition that has also been described in adults with CRS [12]. It is characterized by spasticity, ataxia, seizures, and progressive encephalopathy [12]. Prevention of CRS through immunization remains the most important intervention.

The rubella vaccine is a live attenuated vaccine. It is available in a monovalent formulation. It can also be combined with other antigens like mumps, varicella, and measles. The efficacy of Rubella Containing Vaccines (RCV) is about 95% and immunity persists lifelong [6]. Adverse events commonly reported after vaccination include a low-grade fever, injection site pain or swelling, rash and irritability. Transient thrombocytopenia and febrile convulsions have also been reported [6].

The RCV that is in use in Lesotho is the Measles Rubella (MR) vaccine, which is given to infants of both sexes at the ages of 9 and 18 months. Lesotho started offering the MR vaccine in 2017 [13]. The vaccine was introduced through an integrated measles Supplementary Immunization Activity (SIA) that targeted all children at the ages of 9 months to 14 years followed by its incorporation into the routine immunization schedule as per WHO recommendations [13]. This is a strategy that is designed to eliminate rubella as well as CRS, and it also requires the vaccination of adults based on country-specific susceptibility profiles [6]. The SIA administrative coverage for MR vaccine was 89% [13]. A post SIA coverage survey that was conducted to validate the administrative coverage reported a MR vaccine coverage of 92% which was within 5% of the administrative coverage but fell short of the recommended target of ≥ 95% required to limit transmission and eliminate rubella [13].

The WHO defines rubella elimination as the absence of endemic rubella transmission within a country persistently for more than 12 months and the absence of CRS cases that result from endemic rubella transmission when the rubella surveillance system in that country is of good quality [3].

The feasibility of rubella elimination and its prioritization as a public health goal is supported by the lower rubella reproductive number (R0), and the longer rubella incubation period of 12–23 days that allows for an anamnestic response in persons who have been vaccinated [14]. In anamnestic immune responses, a function of immunological memory, more rapid and effective immune responses are elicited upon exposure to a previously encountered pathogen [15]. The feasibility of rubella elimination is further supported by the absence of widespread rubella outbreaks even during periods of reduced vaccination coverage [16]. The effectiveness of the rubella vaccine has also been established. This is supported by rubella and CRS being eliminated from all countries in North and South America, 41 countries in Europe, 5 countries in the Western Pacific Region and 4 countries in the Eastern Mediterranean Region [4].

In this review, the authors document for the first time the epidemiology of rubella in Lesotho over five years immediately after vaccine introduction and progress made to advance the elimination of rubella in Lesotho.

Methods

Aim

The study aimed to describe the epidemiology of rubella in Lesotho after vaccine introduction. The effectiveness of the measles rubella case-based surveillance system was also evaluated.

Study design and setting

A retrospective cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted to review measles and rubella case-based surveillance data from 2018 to 2022 to document rubella epidemiology in Lesotho and progress towards elimination. The performance of the measles rubella case-based surveillance system was also assessed. The study was conducted in Lesotho, a land-locked country in Southern Africa with an estimated population of about 2 million. The country is divided into 10 administrative districts. The National Reference Laboratory (NRL), where all blood samples are tested, is in the capital city of Maseru.

Case-based surveillance

Lesotho has been implementing integrated measles and rubella case-based surveillance according to WHO recommendations. To detect suspected cases, however, the measles case definition of a febrile maculopapular rash and one of coryza, cough, and conjunctivitis is used. Blood samples are taken from suspected measles cases and sent to the NRL with a case investigation form. The variables that are captured in the case investigation form include the date of birth, the sex, the district where respondents resided, the rubella immunization status, and the date of the last rubella vaccination, the date that the skin rash initially appeared and the travel history in the two weeks before the illness started. Two key indicators are used to evaluate the quality of this surveillance system. The non-measles, non-rubella febrile rash illness rate measures the capacity to adequately identify and investigate cases while the proportion of districts investigating suspected measles rubella cases with a blood specimen per year measures the extent to which all districts are playing a role in the detection of cases. Other indicators used to evaluate this surveillance system assess the source of transmission (endemic or imported), whether specimens are adequately collected and tested, and whether laboratory results are reported in a timely manner.

Case classification

A suspected case of measles was defined as anyone with a fever, maculopapular rash and at least one of cough, coryza or conjunctivitis or anyone in whom a clinician suspected measles. A confirmed rubella case was defined as a measles discarded case with recent rubella infection as evidenced by serology and who had not had rubella vaccination within the last 30 days.

Testing of specimens

Blood samples were tested for measles immunoglobulin M (IgM) and rubella IgM using commercial Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELIZA) kits SERION and EUROIMMUN according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Molecular testing is not routinely done in Lesotho. For quality assurance, 10% of samples are sent to the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD) in South Africa each year for repeat testing. This laboratory is the WHO regional reference laboratory for the Southern African region.

Data analysis

The data were summarized using descriptive statistics. A positive rubella IgM test in an individual who had not been vaccinated in the past 30 days before blood sample collection was used as an indicator of acute rubella disease. The data were grouped in terms of sex, rubella serostatus, age categories (0 - < 1 year), (1 year to < 5 years), (5 - <13 years) and (13 years and above), district of residence and date of rash onset. Cases vaccinated against rubella were those vaccinated on and after the 13th of February 2017 when the rubella vaccine first became available in Lesotho. The formula below was used to compute rubella prevalence, which was reported as a percentage.

total rubella IgM results.

The Positive Predictive Value (PPV) was used to determine the sensitivity of the measles case definition in detecting rubella cases. It was calculated by dividing all positive rubella IgM cases, including those that were dual positive for measles IgM, by all suspected measles cases that underwent rubella testing.

The non-measles, non-rubella febrile rash illness rate was computed using the formula: discarded cases after laboratory testing of blood samples divided by the total population of a district multiplied by 100. The proportion of districts reporting at least one case of measles with a blood sample per year was computed using the formula: number of districts reporting at least one measles case with a blood sample per year divided by total number of districts in the country that conduct measles and rubella surveillance. Data analysis was done using SPSS version 28. Frequencies or percentages were used to report categorical data, while the median and interquartile range (IQR) were used to report continuous data. The chi-square test was used to determine if the associations between the relevant variables were significant. A p-value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

Out of the 1050 suspected measles cases reported from all districts in Lesotho between January 2018 and December 2022 (Fig. 1), a total of 1042 (99.2%) blood samples were received. A total of 1042 (99.2%) samples underwent measles testing, and 1041 (99.1%) samples underwent rubella testing. Among the 1042 cases tested for measles, 10 (1%) tested positive. Notably, three of the 10 measles positive cases were also positive for rubella. These 10 cases were excluded from further analyses as the focus of this study was rubella, and 1031 rubella IgM results underwent further analysis. This process has been illustrated in Fig. 2.

Out of the 1031 rubella IgM results that were analysed, information on gender was available for 1030 of the 1031 cases. There were more males than females reported at 531 (51.6%) and 499 (48.4%) respectively.

Of the results that were analysed, 52 (5%) were < 1 year, 266 (26%) were 1- < 5 years, 643 (62.4%) were 5 - < 13 years and 68 (6.6%) were 13 years old and above. Unknown age N = 2. There were more cases reported in the 5 - <13-year age category followed by the 1 - <5-year age category. The median age of the respondents was 6.0 (IQR 4.0–8.0.) years.

Discussion

Rubella prevalence in Lesotho after vaccine introduction was estimated using surveillance data. Among the suspected measles cases reported, blood samples received for testing surpassed the target set by WHO of more than 80% [17]. The PPV of the surveillance system to predict rubella cases was low. This implies that most cases reported were not true rubella cases and underscores the need for laboratory investigation of suspected cases to confirm the diagnoses. The low PPV could be due to the use of the measles case definition to identify rubella, a disease that can present with little or no symptoms and this can result in inaccurate estimation of rubella prevalence. However, low PPVs have also been reported in settings with low disease incidence [18].

Out of the 1031 rubella IgM blood results that were analysed, rubella prevalence was 1%. A prevalence of 48% was reported in Lesotho before vaccine implementation [7]. This decline could be attributed to the coverage obtained in the SIA conducted in 2017 and the subsequent inclusion of the MR vaccine into the routine immunization schedule [13]. Similar findings were reported by Luce et al. [19]. Their review demonstrated a decline in the number of confirmed rubella cases ranging from 48 to 96% in 5 African countries recently after RCV introduction. These countries had also introduced rubella vaccination by SIAs that targeted boys and girls 9 months to 14 years old. In Ghana, reduced circulation of rubella after vaccine introduction was also demonstrated, with 596 cases in 2011 compared to 19 cases in 2017 [20].

In this study, rubella was equally distributed among males and females. This differed from observations made by Dongdem et al., in Ghana where higher proportions of rubella positive cases were confirmed among females [20]. Rubella was predominant in the 5 - <13-year age category. Similar findings were reported by Luce et al. and Dongdem et al. [19, 20].

Rubella cases were only identified in 2019 and 2022, and most of these cases were identified in 2022. Additionally, this was the year when the most suspected cases were reported and the year where all districts in Lesotho met the WHO surveillance target for the non-measles non- rubella febrile rash illness rate. In contrast, this surveillance target was only met by 2 out of 10 districts in 2021 indicating a reduced sensitivity to identify rubella cases in 2021.

The WHO surveillance target for the proportion of districts reporting not less than one case of measles with a blood sample per year was met throughout the study period demonstrating the capacity of most districts in Lesotho to notify and investigate cases.

The coverage of the first dose of RCV declined in all districts in 2020. This decline could be explained by the Covid-19 pandemic’s effects on health service provision. A decline in the uptake of routine immunizations during the Covid-19 pandemic was also observed in other settings. A review of childhood immunization across five WHO regions between 2020 and 2022 showed an overall decline in the number of vaccine doses administered that ranged from 25 to 51% across the regions [21]. A reduction in the number of vaccine doses administered globally was also observed by Shet at el that ranged from 9% in WHO Africa region to 57% in the South-East Asia region [22]. Although the low prevalence of rubella in 2020 could not be explained by the low rubella vaccination coverage during the same period as high vaccine coverage is required to interrupt disease transmission, the decline could be attributed to the non-pharmacologic interventions during the Covid-19 pandemic like mask wearing, hand washing and social distancing that led to a decline in transmitted diseases [23, 24]. An unwillingness to seek health care services during that period may also have contributed to the absence of rubella cases.

Persistently low vaccine coverage levels were reported in Quthing district in the years following the Covid-19 pandemic with Butha-Buthe and Leribe districts also failing to obtain the recommended coverage levels of ≥ 80% in 2022. The accumulation of children that are susceptible to rubella in these districts could lead to rubella outbreaks. Vaccination coverage levels of more than 100% were reported in 5 out of 10 districts in this review. This could be due to inaccurate estimates of the number of surviving infants, which are used as denominators when calculating vaccination coverage, or poor data quality, making it impossible to draw reliable conclusions based on this coverage data. Data quality issues were also identified in 1 out of 5 vaccine coverage reports submitted to WHO/UNICEF from 2000 to 2019 with higher rates of data quality issues identified in the Americas and Africa regions [25]. Suggested strategies to improve vaccine coverage data quality include improving the accuracy of immunization program targets (denominators), capacity building on vaccine data management and promoting the use of vaccine data for decision-making starting at health facility level [26].

In this study, three cases were identified that were IgM positive for measles as well as rubella and were excluded from further analysis. This could be due to recent infection from viruses like adenovirus that induce cross-reactive IgM antibodies. Rheumatoid factor is also known to interfere with IgM essays leading to false positive results. This is seen in settings with low rubella disease prevalence where the PPV of a rubella positive IgM result is also low and the possibility of the febrile rash being caused by other pathogens increases [27]. In South Africa, cases that were IgM positive for measles as well as rubella were thought to be more likely due to rubella infection because of the higher PPV of a rubella positive IgM result. Rubella is endemic in South Africa and the rubella vaccination is not yet well established. Molecular testing to conform measles or rubella diagnosis has been recommended in these cases [28].

Out of the 10 cases that tested positive for rubella, 7 had received rubella vaccination while 3 had not. Although the study was intended to look at the effects of population immunity rather than individual immunity, the causes of vaccination failure in this review warrant further investigation. In other settings, rubella vaccination failure was not implicated in disease transmission, and it is thought to be caused by the presence of pre-existing maternal rubella antibodies that neutralize the live viral strain of the rubella vaccine [16]. There is also evidence of waning immunity over time, however this does not seem to increase the risk of rubella or CRS, likely because of rubella vaccine specific cellular immunity [29, 30]. Mitigating strategies may include an evaluation of the duration of persistence of maternal rubella antibodies, and MR vaccine schedule optimization based on country specific susceptibility profiles.

Limitations

The study was limited by missing information particularly on age, gender, vaccination status and travel history of respondents. The study was also confounded by the case classification used to recruit cases, which was not sensitive enough to detect rubella cases. Rubella can also present with minor or no symptoms making identification difficult. Additionally, the surveillance data were reviewed for only a period of 5 years after vaccine introduction and may not be reflective of future trends of rubella transmission. However, the study provides important insights into the impact of vaccination on rubella epidemiology in Lesotho and can be used to inform vaccination strategies.

Conclusions

In this study, rubella serology was conducted on almost all blood samples submitted allowing for the confirmation of rubella diagnosis. The study showed low rubella prevalence in Lesotho over a period of 5 years immediately after vaccine introduction. Rubella was predominant in those aged 5 - < 13 years. The non-measles, non-rubella surveillance target was not met at certain time points during the study period, indicating poor performance and a reduced sensitivity of the surveillance system in detecting rubella cases. Efforts are needed to improve the quality of the measles rubella surveillance system in Lesotho, including the adaptation of a case definition that is more sensitive to detect rubella as this would allow for a more accurate estimation of rubella disease prevalence and assessment of the impact of vaccination on disease burden. Measles and rubella catch-up campaigns are also needed to close immunization gaps which will lower the number of children that are susceptible to rubella and limit outbreaks.
