Cost-effectiveness of using Intravenous Ferric Carboxymaltose compared to Intravenous Iron Sucrose for treatment of iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy
Authors: Beena Joshi, Tejal Varekar, Revathy R, Kiran Sangwan, Deepti Tandon, Ragini Kulkarni, Shahina Begum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron deficiency anemia (IDA) in pregnancy is a major public health concern in India, contributing significantly to adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes. Intravenous iron sucrose (IV ISC) is widely used; however, its low permissible dose necessitates multiple visits, reducing adherence and increasing out-of-pocket expenditure. Intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (IV FCM) offers an advantage of complete dose administration in a single visit, but the higher price of the drug is the major deterrent for its procurement. The study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of IV FCM versus IV ISC for managing moderate to severe IDA in pregnancy in the Indian public health system.MethodsA decision tree model was developed from an abridged societal perspective. The time horizon was defined as the period from the second trimester to delivery, including early neonatal outcomes. Effectiveness inputs were derived from both primary and secondary sources. Costs were estimated using national costing databases and procurement records. The outcome was measured in incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) per quality-adjusted-life-years (QALY) gained and incremental health gains. Uncertainty was explored using deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analysis. Budget impact analysis estimated the fiscal implication of adopting IV FCM.ResultsThe use of IV FCM compared with IV ISC for managing moderate to severe IDA in pregnant women incurred an incremental cost of INR 1,316 (USD 15.62) for an additional 0.069 QALYs. The ICER of INR 19,091.66 (USD 226.69) per QALY gained—equivalent to 0.08 times the cost-effectiveness threshold of 1x Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita per QALY—demonstrates that IV FCM is cost-effective. IV FCM also provided measurable clinical benefits, including improved anemia correction and reductions in adverse drug events, adverse neonatal outcomes, and maternal mortality. Sensitivity analyses showed > 98% probability of cost-effectiveness. While the budget impact indicates a higher financial outlay for IV FCM, this increased expenditure is associated with substantial incremental health gains.ConclusionsIV FCM is likely to be a cost-effective alternative to IV ISC for managing moderate to severe IDA in pregnancy, improving anemia recovery, maternal and neonatal outcomes while reducing out-of-pocket expenditure, thereby strengthening Anemia Mukta Bharat and Universal Health Coverage in India.Keywords: Anemia, Intravenous ferric carboxymaltose, Intravenous iron sucrose, Parenteral iron therapy, Cost-effectiveness analysis, Reproductive health, Anemia Mukta Bharat

Background

Anemia is a major public health concern that has a direct association with maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. Globally, in 2019, 30% (539 million) of non-pregnant women and 37% (32 million) of pregnant women aged 15–49 years were affected by anemia [1]. Anemia during pregnancy increases the risk of adverse perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and intrauterine growth restriction [2, 3]. The risk of adverse maternal outcomes, including postpartum hemorrhage and maternal mortality, increases with the severity of anemia [4]. Severe anemia during pregnancy is associated with a twofold higher risk of maternal death compared with non-anemic women [5]. In 2023, four countries together accounted for nearly half of all maternal deaths worldwide, with India alone responsible for approximately 7.2% of these deaths [6]. The burden of anemia is very high in India. According to the recent National Family Health Survey 2019-21 (NFHS-5), in India, more than 50% of pregnant women aged 15–49 years suffer from anemia [7]. Overall, these adverse perinatal and maternal outcomes impose substantial financial burden on health system and families.

Recognising the high burden of anemia among pregnant women and the risk it poses to the maternal and child health, the Government of India has prioritized anemia control as a key public health goal. The Anemia Mukta Bharat (AMB) mission, launched in 2018, aims to accelerate the reduction of anemia across all life stages. Under this mission, pregnant women receive prophylactic oral iron supplementation during the antenatal period to replenish the increased physiological demand of iron. However, for pregnant women with moderate to severe anemia detected late in pregnancy (in the late second or third trimester), or those with poor compliance due to risk of loss to follow-up, oral iron therapy alone is insufficient [8]. In such cases, parenteral iron is recommended as the first line of treatment, as it provides a superior hematological response and can obliviate the need for blood transfusion in antenatal and postpartum period [9]. The most commonly used parenteral iron therapy for pregnant women with anemia is intravenous iron sucrose (IV ISC). This therapy is considered safe, with minimal side effects. However, the limited permissible dosage which is 200 mg per day administered on alternate days, not exceeding 600 mg per week [8–10] is a major disadvantage necessitating multiple visits to the healthcare facility [2, 11, 12] thereby increasing the overall cost and reducing adherence. Poor compliance often leads to persistent anemia, which continues to adversely affect pregnancy outcomes [9].

Intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (IV FCM) is a third-generation parenteral iron formulation with proven efficacy and safety and is non-inferior to the current standard therapy (iron sucrose complex) used for correcting iron deficiency anemia (IDA) in pregnancy [9, 13]. As complete dose administration is possible in a single visit for IV FCM, it leads to an early rise in hemoglobin levels, fewer hospital visits, and less discomfort due to needle pricks [9]. Operational guidelines and directives on the use of IV FCM in pregnancy have also been issued by the National Health Mission, Government of India [8]. However, cost is a major deterrent for most states in India that hampers procurement. The market price of IV FCM is more than 10 times higher than IV ISC [14]. Studies have compared the cost-effectiveness of oral iron therapy with IV ISC in management of IDA in pregnancy in Indian context [15, 16] however, there is lack of evidence that have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of IV FCM vs. IV ISC among pregnant women with iron deficiency anemia. The large-scale adoption of IV FCM within the public health system requires evidence on its economic value relative to existing standard of care which is IV ISC. The present study assesses the cost-effectiveness of IV FCM compared with IV ISC for the management of moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy. The study also includes the budget impact assessment to estimate the fiscal implications for implementation of IV FCM in the public health system in India. By generating robust economic evidence on the comparative value of these interventions within the Indian public health system, the study aims to support informed policy decisions.

Methods

A hypothetical decision tree model was developed depicting the Indian public health setting to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of IV FCM versus IV ISC for treating moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia in pregnant women. The population cohort consisted of pregnant women aged 15–49 years with moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia. We considered the abridged societal perspective according to the principles of the Indian Reference Case, which is the preferred perspective for conducting health technology assessments in India. The abridged societal perspective includes direct medical and non-medical costs borne by providers and patients, along with out-of-pocket expenditure [13]. The time horizon was defined as the period from the second trimester to delivery, including early neonatal outcomes, which is a period of six months, to capture all clinically relevant maternal and early neonatal events attributed to intervention.

A randomised clinical trial was conducted between 2022 and 23 to assess feasibility and acceptability of using IV FCM at first referral units by the authors of this manuscript (CTRI registered (REF/2021/07/045322). The study enrolled 280 pregnant women between 2022 and 23 weeks gestation diagnosed with moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia randomized (1:1) to receive IV FCM or IV ISC. The study assessed the hemoglobin improvement, ferritin changes, adverse events and pregnancy outcomes at 4 weeks, during delivery and postpartum. The health-related quality-of-life values were estimated using the EQ-5D-5 L (EuroQol-5 Dimension, 5 Levels) instrument administered during these time points. Some primary data generated in this study was used to populate the hypothesized model where evidence from the literature was lacking. As most clinical studies reported only the mean increase in hemoglobin or ferritin levels following treatment, the proportionate improvement in these parameters among pregnant women with anemia was derived from the present study data. The pregnancy outcomes in terms of type of delivery, still birth, preterm and low birth weight (LBW) among the two arms were estimated using this primary data. Given the absence of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) data for anemic pregnant women in India, the utility values were also estimated from the current study.

Model

The model begins with a cohort of pregnant women diagnosed with moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia (IDA). In accordance with the national treatment guidelines, these women received parenteral iron therapy – either IV FCM or IV ISC. The effects were modelled for a single dose of IV FCM and an average of five doses of IV ISC based on the trial data showing a mean iron requirement of 1000 mg (Ganzoni formula) and the Anemia Mukta Bharat guidelines recommending 200 mg per infusion [17]. Adverse events associated with each formulation were incorporated into the model based on evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis [18]. Using the primary data from the randomized controlled clinical trial, the model assumes that, four weeks after treatment [18], women may either achieve recovery from anemia—defined by an increase in hemoglobin (transitioning from mild/moderate/severe anemia to no anemia) and ferritin concentrations (from ≤ 29 µg/L to > 30 µg/L)—or remain anemic.

The post-treatment anemia status influences subsequent pregnancy outcomes, including the type of delivery (normal vaginal or cesarean section) and the risk of maternal complications such as postpartum hemorrhage and maternal mortality. Additionally, maternal anemia elevates the risk of adverse fetal outcomes, including stillbirth, preterm birth, and low birth weight, which often necessitate neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission. The model also incorporates early neonatal deaths, recognizing their higher occurrence among neonates born preterm or with low-birth-weight secondary to maternal anemia. The model was run on MS Excel to assess the cost-effectiveness as per current accepted threshold of 1x Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita [19]. Measuring health outcomes

The primary outcome was measured in terms of the Quality Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) gained among the pregnant women. The HR-QoL utility values were obtained from both primary and secondary data sources. The primary study (RCT) captured the health-related quality-of-life among pregnant women with moderate to severe anemia using the EQ-5D-5 L at baseline, 4 weeks post treatment and immediate post-partum. The Indian value sets for EQ-5D-5 L were applied to estimate utility scores and calculate the QALYs using a standard method [20]. The postpartum utility values were stratified based on the neonatal outcomes categories. Infant-specific health-related quality-of-life utility values were not incorporated, as the analysis were restricted to captures maternal QALYs. The health-related quality of life data during delivery was considered from secondary data source [21]. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio per QALY gained was estimated using the model. Additionally, projected population level impact in terms of incremental health gains due to IV FCM were estimated through the model in terms of number of women who recovered from anemia, number of women with improved iron stores (Ferritin levels), drug-related adverse events averted, and adverse neonatal outcomes averted (Still birth, Preterm, LBW, NICU admissions). Measuring costs

All the direct medical and non-medical costs incurred by both the health system and patients along with the out-of-pocket expenditure were included in the analysis. As the model considers the abridged societal perspective, only the direct medical and non-medical OOPE were included, the indirect cost (e.g. loss of wage) and intangible were excluded due to difficulty in accurately capturing and quantifying these data. The medical costs included the price of the drug (either IV FCM or IV ISC), cost of administering the injectable drugs, cost of delivery, cost of management of complications such as post-partum hemorrhage (PPH)/Hysterectomy and cost of neonatal care. The public procurement price of IV FCM and IV ISC was obtained from the state health system resource i.e. Tamil Nadu Medical Service Corporation [27]. The cost-of-service delivery for anemic pregnant women which included the cost per Antenatal Care (ANC) visit, per delivery, per NICU, etc. was obtained from the National Health System Cost Database (NHSCD) [28]. The data on the out-of-pocket expenditure was obtained from the National Sample survey report data base [29]. All cost are presented in the Indian National Rupees and were inflation adjusted to year 2025 using the Consumer Price Index [30]. All costs are presented in Indian National Rupee (INR). The INR to United States Dollars (USD) exchange rate was used (1 USD = INR 84.22) for the year 2025 [31]. The input parameters of costs are provided in Table 2.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to quantify the impact of joint parameter uncertainty on the model outcomes. One-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted by varying key input probabilities, cost and utility parameters. The influence of each parameter uncertainties on the ICER per QALY gained were examined using a one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA), and the results were illustrated using a tornado diagram. For the input probabilities derived from primary and secondary data sources, variation was informed by reported confidence intervals or ranges in the data. The variation for the utility values was applied based on the observed range from the primary data. Cost parameters derived from the secondary literature and national cost database were varied based on the reported confidence or range. In case of the cost of drugs and consumables, where the cost was obtained from public procurement database a variation of ± 5% was applied reflecting the minimal price variation within the public procurement system.

For probabilistic sensitivity analysis all cost parameters were modelled using gamma distributions to account for their right-skewed nature, while probability and utility parameters were assigned beta distributions, reflecting their bounded range between 0 and 1. The model was subjected to 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations, with each iteration simultaneously sampling from the specified parameter distributions. The resulting distribution of ICERs was used to derive 95% uncertainty intervals, corresponding to the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the simulated estimates. A PSA scatter plot was generated on the cost-effectiveness plane to illustrate the dispersion of ICERs and to evaluate the robustness of the model.

A cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) was generated using results from the probabilistic sensitivity analysis to capture decision uncertainty and to illustrate the probability of the intervention being cost-effective across a range of cost-effectiveness thresholds.

Budget impact assessment

A budget impact analysis was conducted to assess the financial implications of using IV FCM compared with the current standard of care, IV ISC. The analysis aimed to generate context-specific financial evidence to support programmatic decision-making at both the grassroots and national levels, recognizing that health is a state subject and policy implementation requires locally relevant economic inputs.

The hypothetical cohort comprised pregnant women with moderate to severe anemia. To estimate the population cohort at the district and national levels, census of India data [34] and the projected population for the years 2023 was used [35]. The reported population at national level was 1.39 billion and the average population of a district was estimated to be 1.8 million. The crude birth rate from the Sample Registration System (SRS) 2021 was used to derive the number of live births, which was considered equivalent to the number of pregnancies resulting in live births [36]. The remaining number of pregnancies with other outcomes such as still birth, abortion or miscarriage, were obtained from National Family Health Survey-5. The total number of pregnancies were calculated based on the number of pregnancies resulting live births and number of pregnancies with other outcomes. The prevalence of pregnant women with moderate to severe anemia was obtained from NFHS-5 to estimate the cohort (Table 3) [37].

Population and unit cost parameters for budget impact

The unit cost for budget impact was estimated as the additional cost required beyond the existing program budget to introduce IV FCM for the treatment of moderate to severe anemia in pregnancy within the public health system which included the cost of drugs, costs associated with the management of adverse events, and consumable costs. The unit cost was used to calculate the total health system expenditure for implementing IV FCM and IV ISC for estimated cohort of population at district and national level. The incremental budget impact was estimated as the difference in total cost between intervention and comparator. This economic evaluation was conducted and reported according to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) guidelines [38].

Results

Costs estimates from the model

The total abridged societal cost per pregnant woman treated for iron deficiency anemia (IDA) was estimated for both IV FCM and IV ISC. The mean cost per woman treated with IV FCM was INR 15,058.02 (USD 182.37), compared with INR 13,742.38 (USD 166.43) for IV ISC. Thus, IV FCM incurred an incremental cost of INR 1,315.63 (USD 15.62). The higher total cost associated with IV FCM was primarily attributable to its higher procurement price, which was partially offset by lower costs overall related to IV administration, management of adverse events, and out-of-pocket expenditure which is higher in the case of IV ISC due to the requirement of multiple visits for administration of complete dose.

Health outcomes

IV FCM was associated with an incremental gain in QALYs compared with IV ISC. The incremental QALY gain of 0.069 per pregnant women (0.45 QALY with IV FCM versus 0.38 QALY with IV ISC) in favour of IV FCM was primarily attributed to a lower anemia-related QALY loss. Furthermore, IV FCM was associated with a lower probability of cesarean delivery and adverse neonatal outcomes, which contributed to higher maternal QALYs due to the comparatively lower utility values assigned to these health states. The analysis also estimated the comparative clinical benefits associated with the two interventions. For the cohort of pregnant women with iron deficiency anemia (IDA) in India (8,411,106), the projected population level impact using model probabilities with IV FCM compared to IV ISC result in an incremental 588,777 (7%) women recovering from anemia, 723,355 (8.6%) women attaining improved iron stores, and 420,555 (5%) women not experiencing drug-related adverse events. Furthermore, the intervention is estimated to prevent an incremental 480,634 (5.7%) adverse neonatal outcomes and approximately 22 (0.0003%) maternal deaths prevented (Table 4).

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio

In the base-case scenario, the higher cost and improved quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) observed with IV FCM compared to IV ISC resulted in an ICER of INR 19,091.6 (USD 231.22) per QALY gained. Considering the cost-effectiveness threshold for India, which is 1x Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita per QALY, the ICER corresponds to 0.08x GDP per capita per QALY. As the ICER remains well below this threshold, IV FCM can be considered a cost-effective intervention at the current public procurement price of INR 2,400 (USD 29.1) for 1,000 mg administered to pregnant women with moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia in India (Table 5).

Sensitivity Analysis

One way sensitivity Analysis

Each parameter was varied within its predefined range, provided in the input parameter sheet, while all other parameters were kept constant at their base-case values. The base-case ICER of INR 19,091.66 (USD 231.22) per QALY gained demonstrated considerable sensitivity to variations in specific clinical and cost parameters. The OWSA indicated that the model outcomes were most sensitive to changes in the probabilities of preterm/LBW events, caesarean section delivery and OOPE due to IV ISC, while other clinical and cost parameters exerted comparatively minor influence. Among the cost parameters, variation in the procurement price of IV FCM produced a relatively modest effect, with increased price of IV FCM drug by 5% the ICER increased to INR 22,889 (USD 271.78). Nevertheless, across all plausible parameter ranges, the ICER remained well below India’s cost-effectiveness threshold of 1x GDP per capita per QALY, suggesting that IV FCM remained a cost-effective alternative to IV ISC when parameters were varied. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA)

The PSA scatter plot for the 1000 simulated ICER on the cost-effectiveness plane demonstrated that the majority of the simulated ICER were located below the cost-effectiveness threshold, indicating that IV FCM remained cost-effective compared to IV ISC under most plausible parameter combinations. The base-case ICER was positioned well below the cost-effectiveness threshold, further reinforcing the robustness of the model findings (Fig. 3).

This distribution pattern highlights the stability of the model outcomes and indicates that parameter uncertainty is unlikely to alter the overall conclusion of cost-effectiveness. Budget impact analysis

The cohort of moderate to severe anemic pregnant women was estimated at district and national level. The estimated average number of pregnant women in India, in a district was estimated to be 10,712 and at national level it was 8,411,106 [34–37]. The unit cost for IV FCM was estimated to be INR 2,432.6 (USD 28.9) and for IV ISC was INR 216.9 (USD 2.58).

The total health system cost of providing IV FCM to pregnant anemic women in a district is INR 26.06 million (USD 0.30 million) and for IV ISC is INR 2.32 million (USD 0.28 millions). The incremental budget impact at district level is INR 23.74 million (USD 0.28 millions). Similarly, total health system cost at the national level of providing IV FCM for the entire population of 8 million pregnant anemic women in India is INR 20.46 billion (USD 0.243 billion) and for IV ISC is INR 1.82 billion (USD 0.0216 million). The incremental budget impact cost at national level is INR 18.636 billion (USD 0.221 billion). Thus, the management of iron deficiency anemia using IV FCM compared to the IV ISC for moderate to severe anemic pregnant women was estimated to incur an incremental budget impact of INR 23.74 million (USD 0.28 millions) at district level and INR 18.636 billion (USD 0.221 billion) at national level.

Discussion

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) in pregnancy remains a major public health challenge in India, contributing to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. Incorporation of IV FCM in the public health system offers several clinical and operational advantages over conventional IV ISC therapy. In this context, a comprehensive assessment of the clinical benefits relative to associated costs is essential to inform priority setting in the management of anemia in pregnancy. We conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis to assess the relative efficiency of IV FCM over IV ISC among moderate to severe anemic pregnant women. To our knowledge, this is the first study in India to undertake a full economic evaluation assessing the cost-effectiveness of IV FCM versus IV ISC for pregnant women with anemia from an abridged societal perspective. This economic evaluation compares the incremental costs incurred by the health system for treating anemic pregnant women with IV FCM versus IV ISC against the corresponding gains in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). The analysis estimated an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of INR 19,091.6 (USD 231.22) per QALY gained, for IV FCM compared to IV ISC, which is substantially lower than the cost-effectiveness threshold of India, 1x GDP per capita per QALY. Thus, under the present assumptions, IV FCM is likely to be a cost-effective intervention for managing moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia in pregnant women, especially when initiated in the second trimester. From an economic perspective, the analysis indicates that while IV FCM entails a higher acquisition cost, it is partially offset by savings from fewer administrations, lower healthcare utilization, and reduced out-of-pocket expenditure for beneficiaries. The model also depicted the projected incremental gain in the population level health outcomes of IV FCM over IV ISC, highlighting the therapeutic advantage the treatment option offers over the current standard of care. These benefits translate into incremental gains in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and potential reductions in both maternal and neonatal complications, including preventable maternal deaths. However, these gains are modelled with the limited six-month time horizon for the current study where the aim was capturing the effect from treatment initiation during pregnancy through delivery and immediate neonatal outcomes. The outcomes beyond this, long-term postpartum maternal QALYs and child QALYs were not included as they were outside the scope and limited by data availability. Consequently, the estimated QALY gains does not reflect the potential long-term maternal and child health benefits associated with improved iron status through the intervention. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis indicated a 98% probability of cost-effectiveness under current assumptions, reflecting high result robustness. However, real-world implementation factors may influence actual outcomes. The input parameters exerting the greatest influence on the ICER were the probability of preterm/LBW, cesarean section delivery and out-of-pocket expenditure for IV ISC. However, even at the upper bounds of these parameters, the ICER remained below the cost-effectiveness threshold for India, indicating the robustness of the findings and confirming the intervention’s cost-effectiveness. The findings of the study could be utilised by the health systems to support evidence-based procurement decision-making, strengthening equitable and timely access to effective anemia treatment.

Within the health system, IV FCM allows administration of a higher single dose (up to 1000 mg), facilitating more rapid correction of hemoglobin levels and replenishment of iron stores [9, 10, 39]. Multiple clinical trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated the superior efficacy of IV FCM in improving hemoglobin and ferritin concentrations compared to IV ISC, with sustained benefits over time and a reduced requirement for blood transfusion [9, 18, 40]. Furthermore, IV FCM has been demonstrated a favourable safety profile, with consistently lower incidence of adverse events reported across several studies [13, 41–45]. The improved tolerability contributes to better adherence, reduced need for repeat visits, and enhanced patient satisfaction—all of which are critical in low-resource, high-burden settings like India. Wider implementation of this cost-effective therapy has the potential to enhance treatment adherence, reduce anemia-related complications, and ultimately improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

In the Indian context, the existing evidence is limited to either economic evaluations comparing oral iron vs. IV ISC or some that only estimated the cost differences between parenteral preparations without conducting a full economic evaluation. The economic evaluation conducted by Saha et al. [15] for IV ISC vs. oral iron estimated the ICER per QALY gained of USD 9.84 for pregnant women treated for anemia and reported higher mean Hb levels with less adverse events for parenteral iron [15]. The cost-effectiveness analysis conducted by Ray et al. [16], which also evaluated IV ISC compared to oral iron estimated the ICER INR 31,951 per safe delivery (absence of composite maternal and foetal/neonatal adverse clinical outcomes). Both studies reported that parenteral formulation can be more cost-effective in treating anemia in pregnant women [15, 16]. There are two randomised controlled trials undertaken in India that claim to estimate the cost-effectiveness of IV FCM vs. IV ISC for the treatment of pregnant women with anemia. However, these studies were confined to cost comparisons, without undertaking comprehensive economic evaluations. The RCT by Jose et al. [9] estimated the total cost of therapy for IV FCM (INR 6,872.4 ± 379.7) and IV ISC (INR 6,566.3 ± 449.8) with a higher cost in IV FCM group (difference INR 306.1 (140.93, 471.3) (p value = 0.0004). However, these costs only included the cost of the drug and the cost of consumables (IV cannula, IV drip set, and normal saline) and did not include the cost comprehensively, including the cost of human resources and other capital costs, out-of-pocket expenditure due to travel and wage loss, etc. The study did not estimate the cost-effectiveness in economic terms by deriving the ICER for IV FCM [9]. Another RCT by Maheshwari et al. [44] claimed to estimate the cost-effectiveness of oral iron and injectable ISC and FCM in anemic women in 2nd and 3rd trimesters of pregnancy. The study compared the total cost of oral iron, injection Sucrose and injection FCM derived using the cost of drugs and consumables and travel costs for attaining target Hb levels 11gm/dL. This study also did not adopt a comprehensive societal or health system cost perspective, as would be expected in a full economic evaluation, nor did it estimate an ICER based on the corresponding gains in clinical effectiveness [44]. Internationally, there is one study that has reported conducting a cost-minimisation analysis comparing the total treatment for IV FCM vs. IV ISC for pregnant women with anemia in Algeria. The study also estimated the budget impact for the entire population cohort of Algerian anemic pregnant women. The study highlighted replacing IV ISC with IV FCM resulted a 17% cost reduction per patients and an overall budget saving for the government [46]. Thus, despite the methodological limitations, these studies indicated a potential cost-saving advantage of IV FCM. This shows that the findings of our study are consistent with the existing literature while also filling a critical gap in the literature by undertaking a comprehensive economic evaluation.

From a policy standpoint, adoption of IV FCM within existing national programs—such as Anemia Mukt Bharat and the Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child, and Adolescent Health (RMNCH + A) strategy—can accelerate India’s progress in reducing anemia prevalence and improving pregnancy outcomes. Ensuring access to IV FCM in public healthcare facilities could enhance treatment efficiency, safety, and equity. Given its proven efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness, IV FCM represents a viable and impactful policy option for the management of moderate to severe iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy under India’s public health system.

Within the framework of Universal Health Coverage, anemia poses a dual challenge: it increases both disease burden and financial hardship. Frequent healthcare visits, hospitalizations, and the need for blood transfusions in severe cases escalate healthcare costs for families and the public health system. Addressing anemia through cost-effective interventions, such as IV FCM for moderate to severe cases, can significantly reduce the out-of-pocket expenditure burden and improve health system efficiency. The adoption of effective anemia management strategies aligns with the Universal Health Coverage goals of ensuring equitable access, financial protection, and quality care.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. The model outcomes were based on studies where data was from limited sample sizes, which may not fully capture all probable variations in clinical practice and population characteristics. The analysis used aggregated cost estimates and may not reflect variations in procurement prices, healthcare delivery costs, or access to services. The study also did not model the infant QALYs as the study only estimated the gains in the maternal QALYs. The model considered only a six- month time horizon from second trimester of pregnancy until pregnancy outcomes that included neonatal outcomes and did not include long term infant outcomes as the multiple confounders would only increase uncertainty in the model. Additionally, indirect costs, such as productivity losses, were not comprehensively included, potentially underestimating societal benefits. In terms of the variations for the sensitivity analysis standard variation range based on the observed data and appropriate to the population level estimates based on expert consultations were incorporated due to non-availability of the required confidence intervals from the source data. Despite these limitations, the model provides a robust and policy-relevant estimate of the comparative cost-effectiveness of IV FCM and IV ISC within the Indian public health context.

Conclusions

Addressing anemia comprehensively through improved access to effective therapies such as IV FCM can generate substantial gains in maternal and child health, productivity, and gender equity. This study bridges this critical evidence gap, demonstrating that IV FCM can be a cost-effective intervention compared to IV ISC for treating iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy considering the current model assumptions. In addition to better efficacy in improving anemia status, IV FCM offers a favourable safety profile, fewer adverse events and reduced out-of-pocket expenditure to patients. Integrating IV FCM into public health programs can accelerate anemia reduction, strengthen maternal health outcomes, and support India’s progress toward Sustainable Development Goals and Universal Health Coverage.
