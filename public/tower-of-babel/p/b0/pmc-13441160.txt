Measurement Properties of the Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire in Patients With a Metacarpal or Phalangeal Fracture
Authors: Veronique A.P. van de Lücht, Louise de Haas, Gideon S. de Wolf, Mark van Heijl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire (MHQ) is among the most frequently used patient-reported outcome measures to evaluate hand disorder–specific outcomes. However, despite its frequent use in patients with hand trauma, its measurement properties have mainly been evaluated in nontraumatic hand conditions. Consequently, although the MHQ is often described as a validated questionnaire, its measurement properties in patients with acute hand trauma remain unclear. In addition, the MHQ is frequently summarized as a single overall score derived from the 6 domains, often interpreted as representing “hand function.” However, it is unclear whether combining these domains into a single score is appropriate.Methods:Three months postinjury data of the MHQ from 442 adult patients with metacarpal and phalangeal fractures were analyzed. Structural validity was evaluated using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis. Construct validity was assessed through known-group comparisons based on a priori hypotheses. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach alpha.Results:Exploratory factor analysis identified 6 factors corresponding to the original MHQ domains. Confirmatory factor analysis showed poor fit for a unidimensional model. In contrast, the 6-factor model showed better fit. Known-group comparisons confirmed 83% of the hypotheses. Domain scores showed clearer differences between clinical subgroups than the overall score. Internal consistency ranged from 0.79 to 0.95 across domains.Conclusions:The MHQ demonstrated sufficient construct validity and internal consistency for its continued use as a 6-domain instrument in patients with metacarpal and phalangeal fractures. We recommend reporting the individual domain scores, as a summarized overall score may mask clinically relevant differences between domains.

Takeaways

Question: Does the Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire demonstrate sufficient measurement properties for continued use in patients with hand fractures, and is use the overall score supported by its dimensional structure?

Findings: In 442 patients, factor analyses identified 6 factors with better fit than a unidimensional model. Known-group comparisons confirmed 83% of the hypotheses. Domain scores discriminated subgroups better than the overall score. Internal consistency ranged from 0.79 to 0.95.

Meaning: Findings support Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire use as 6 domain instrument; domain scores recommended.

INTRODUCTION

The Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire (MHQ) is among the most frequently used patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) to evaluate “hand disorder–specific outcomes.”1–3 Studies evaluating its measurement properties have primarily focused on patients with nontraumatic or chronic hand conditions.4 Consequently, although the MHQ is frequently described as a validated questionnaire, its measurement properties in patients with acute hand trauma remain unknown. Furthermore, the MHQ is often summarized as a single overall score, derived from averaging its 6-domain scores.5–9 This overall score is frequently interpreted as representing “hand function.” The appropriateness of this interpretation is unclear.

Understanding these issues requires considering how PROMs measure health outcomes. Some health outcomes, such as pain or functional ability, cannot be measured directly and are therefore referred to as constructs.10,11 PROMs assess these constructs through questionnaire items that together produce a score representing the construct of interest. For this score to be meaningful, the items must adequately reflect the construct they intend to measure and be appropriate for the population in which the PROM is used. For example, a questionnaire item about climbing stairs may reflect the construct of physical functioning but may not adequately reflect the construct of pain and may be inappropriate in a population of wheelchair users. This is evaluated through the measurement properties of the PROM. These determine the validity, reliability, and interpretability of the PROM score in the population in which it is used, as well as its ability to provide information about the construct of interest that is not limited or misleading.12–14

These considerations are particularly relevant for the MHQ, which consists of 6 domains (hand function, activities of daily living [ADL], work performance, pain, aesthetics, and satisfaction). When these domains serve only an organizational purpose and all items collectively reflect a single underlying construct (i.e., a unidimensional structure), an overall score may be meaningfully interpreted.15 However, when the domains represent distinct constructs (i.e., a multidimensional structure), combining domain scores into a single overall score may obscure clinically relevant differences between domains and mask a good outcome in 1 domain with a less favorable outcome in another. Consequently, patients with different domain-specific profiles may obtain similar overall scores while representing different patient experiences. This may reduce the interpretability of the score and limit meaningful application of the instrument.

This study aims to evaluate the structural validity, hypothesis testing for construct validity (known-groups validity), and internal consistency of the MHQ in patients with a metacarpal or phalangeal fracture. This evaluation will inform whether the current use of the MHQ in this population is supported and clarify how its scores should be interpreted and reported. This may help ensure that conclusions drawn from these scores accurately reflect patient outcomes in both clinical practice and research settings.

METHODS

Setting and Data Collection

This study used existing data from the Snapshot study of hand fractures. The Snapshot study was a multicenter observational study conducted in 12 hospitals in the Netherlands. Patients completed the Dutch version of the MHQ 3 months after injury.16 The Snapshot study is described elsewhere.17 This study performed secondary analyses on data from adult patients with a traumatic metacarpal or phalangeal fracture, excluding dislocations.

Approvals

The medical ethical review committee and institutional review boards of all participating hospitals approved the study. A waiver of informed consent was granted, as the study concerned a dossier study. Consent to use the MHQ data for research purposes was obtained through an additional question in the questionnaire. A license for the use of the MHQ to investigate its measurement properties was obtained from the University of Michigan (5H1D9-3372).

Statistical Analysis

The evaluation of the measurement properties of the MHQ was based on the definitions and recommendations of the Consensus-Based Standards for the Selection of Health Status Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) group.10,11,18–20 Factor analysis followed established guidelines.21–28 All analyses were performed in RStudio Version 2024.12.0 + 467.

Descriptive Statistics

The sample included in this study was compared with all patients in the Snapshot sample who were not included in the analyses (not eligible, nonresponder, and opt out of data use) and with the total Snapshot sample to assess response bias and generalizability. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous data were presented as means ± SDs if normally distributed, or as medians (interquartile ranges) if not.

Structural Validity

To assess the dimensionality and explore the underlying structure of the 37 items in the MHQ, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted using the fa() function from the psych package in R, with minimum residual (minres) estimation. EFA examines how questionnaire items cluster based on response patterns, thereby identifying underlying latent dimensions (factors) that may reflect distinct constructs. These may correspond to questionnaire domains.

For these analyses, the MHQ’s response options were recoded so that all values consistently ranged from 1 (worst score) to 5 (best score). Each question from the MHQ was assigned an item label (e.g., items 1.1–6.6), reflecting its position within the original questionnaire’s 6 domains. Before conducting the factor analyses, data suitability was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy (≥0.50) and Bartlett test of sphericity (P < 0.05).

Subsequently, a series of EFA was conducted with an increasing number of factors. Dimensionality and factor retention were based on multiple criteria: the proportion of variance explained per factor and the cumulative explained variance (≥50% for unidimensionality and ≥60% as acceptable for multidimensional instruments), the scree plot, and parallel analysis.29–31 Retention of more than 1 factor indicates multidimensionality. Contingent on the questionnaire’s multidimensionality, another EFA was conducted to identify the distribution of items across the factors. This analysis was conducted with oblimin (oblique, nonorthogonal) rotation because some correlations were expected between the factors. Factor loadings and cross-loadings were assessed for factor interpretation and indicate the strength of the relationship between an item and a latent factor. Factor loadings less than 0.30 were suppressed, and a maximum 10% of the items were permitted to have factor loadings greater than 0.30 on multiple factors. Clinical interpretation was also considered, and the distribution of items across the factors was compared with that of the original versions’ domain item distribution. Model fit was evaluated using 3 criteria: the root mean square of the residuals (<0.05); the proportion of off-diagonal residual correlations (<0.05); and the overall fit based on off-diagonal values (>0.95).

To further assess structural validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using the cfa() function from the lavaan package in R. Two predefined theoretical models were tested. First, a unidimensional model (1 factor) was used to evaluate unidimensionality and the appropriateness of using an overall score. Second, a 6-factor model, corresponding to the original MHQ domains, was used to assess the structural validity of this 6-domain structure. The estimator was set to robust maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit was evaluated using 4 commonly applied indices: the comparative fit index (≥0.95), Tucker–Lewis index (≥0.95), root mean square error of approximation (≤0.06), and the standardized root mean square residual (≤0.08).

Known-group Hypothesis Testing for Construct Validity

Known-group hypothesis testing was performed to determine whether the MHQ scores varied as expected between clinically relevant subgroups. Based on the assumption that a PROM measures the intended construct, clinically different subgroups that are expected to differ in that construct should differ in their PROM scores accordingly. The focus was on the direction and magnitude of the differences between the groups rather than on statistical significance. Because no minimal important difference has been established for the MHQ in this population, it was not possible to predefine the expected magnitude of between-group differences. The following a priori hypotheses were formulated based on clinical experience for all MHQ domains:

Patients with a fracture to digits 1 or 2 would have lower scores, compared with patients with a fracture to digits 3, 4, or 5, due to fact that the thumb and index finger account for most of the hand’s functionality.32 This hypothesis reflects expected early posttraumatic burden at 3 months, when compensatory digit use may not yet be fully integrated or perceived as normal.

At least 75% of the a priori defined hypotheses had to be confirmed to demonstrate sufficient construct validity. For all subgroups, the overall MHQ score was also assessed.

Internal Consistency

Internal consistency evaluates the degree of interrelatedness among the items in a PROM and was assessed by calculating Cronbach alpha. Unidimensionality is a prerequisite for the interpretation of this statistic. The structural validity of each domain was examined separately to verify unidimensionality before calculating Cronbach alpha. Cronbach alpha values range from 0 to 1, with values between 0.70 and 0.95 indicating good reliability. Higher values may indicate redundancy among items.35,36

Missing Data

No data were missing because the online MHQ assessment did not allow participants to skip questions.

Structural Validity

In EFA, the first factor explained 45% of the total variance. Evaluation of the explained variance, scree plot, and parallel analysis consistently indicated the retention of 6 factors. These findings do not support a unidimensional structure and indicate that the questionnaire reflects multiple underlying dimensions. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the analyses used to assess dimensionality and the number of factors to retain, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F35.)

The factor loadings from EFA with 6 factors are presented in Table 2, with the prerequisite tests reported in the footnotes. The first 4 factors showed item loading patterns that corresponded to the original MHQ domains: ADL, work, hand function, and pain, respectively. Items 6.1, 6.2, 6.4, 6.5, and 6.6 had the highest loadings on the fifth factor. These items all refer to satisfaction with the hand/wrist and were interpreted as the satisfaction domain. Item 6.3, which assesses satisfaction with wrist movement, showed a low loading on this factor (0.07), reflecting a weak relationship with other satisfaction items. Items 5.2, 5.3, and 5.4 loaded highest on the sixth factor. These items all address the perceived appearance of the hand and were interpreted as the aesthetics domain. Item 5.1 showed low loadings across all factors but differs in that it uses reverse phrasing and focuses on satisfaction with hand appearance. This may explain its cross-loading on the fifth factor, representing satisfaction. Despite their lower loadings, assigning items 6.3 and 5.1 to the satisfaction and aesthetics domains is supported by their content and their low loadings on other factors. The fit based on off-diagonal values was 1.00, the root mean square of the residual was 0.03, and 95% of residual correlations were smaller than 0.05, indicating good to excellent model fit. The CFA showed poor fit for the unidimensional model, providing confirmatory evidence against the unidimensionality of the MHQ. The largest differences were observed for work, ADL, and pain in the affected-digit comparison, and for work in the immobilization comparison. Internal Consistency

All MHQ domains demonstrated unidimensionality, supporting the interpretation of Cronbach alpha. A high degree of internal consistency was observed. Cronbach alpha ranged from 0.91 to 0.95 for the hand function, ADL, work, pain, and satisfaction domains, and was 0.79 for the aesthetics domain.

DISCUSSION

This evaluation of the measurement property of the MHQ in patients with a metacarpal or phalangeal fracture provides sufficient evidence of construct validity and internal consistency to support its continued use in this population. It demonstrates that the MHQ assesses 6 distinct hand-related constructs: hand function, ADL, pain, work performance, aesthetics, and satisfaction. These correspond to the MHQ domains as defined in the original instrument. The MHQ should be interpreted as a multidimensional instrument, reflecting a broader profile of hand-related outcomes, rather than as a measure of a single construct such as hand function. This contrasts with PROMs that are designed to measure a single underlying construct or focus on different aspects of hand-related outcomes.37 Recognizing these construct differences and an instrument’s dimensionality is therefore essential when selecting the most appropriate PROM for a specific clinical or research question to ensure that conclusions drawn from these scores accurately reflect the patient outcome of interest.

Although the use of an overall score is not clinimetrically appropriate due to the MHQ’s dimensionality, a limited role may exist in specific settings. In such circumstances, the overall score does not represent a unidimensional concept such as hand function, but summarizes a multidimensional construct.38 Consequently, using the overall score means accepting that improvements in 1 domain can mask deterioration in another, so that the overall score may suggest stability or improvement while this is not the case for the underlying constructs. This trade-off is acceptable only when all domains are considered equally important and its multidimensional limitations and potential for compensatory effects are recognized.

In EFA, items 6.3 and 5.1 showed low factor loadings, suggesting limited contribution and potential construct-irrelevant variance. Although removing them could improve coherence, it would reduce comparability with the original MHQ. As the individual domains still showed acceptable unidimensionality, the overall impact is likely limited, but satisfaction and aesthetics scores should be interpreted cautiously, as small differences or changes may partly reflect measurement error rather than meaningful variation in the construct being measured. Known-group testing showed no discrimination in some domains, highlighting the need to select relevant domains per context. As no minimal important difference exists for this population and published estimates vary considerably (3–23 points), interpretation against a threshold was not possible.39–41 Internal consistency was high across all domains, indicating that the items within each domain are strongly interrelated and that each domain provides reliable scores. The aesthetics domain showed lower reliability, likely due to the reverse phrasing of item 5.1, which may have introduced respondent confusion. Rephrasing this item could improve consistency but would reduce comparability with existing MHQ data, although reliability was sufficient.

These findings extend prior studies on factor analysis that were limited to different and smaller populations.42–44 Their varying results may be attributed to methodological differences.18 This study also provides new insight into the construct validity of the MHQ, as most prior studies have focused on convergent hypothesis testing, comparing the MHQ with other instruments. To our knowledge, only the original development study by Chung et al1 evaluated known-group validity by comparing patients with rheumatoid arthritis and carpal tunnel syndrome, reporting a 33-point difference on the aesthetics domain. The larger difference observed in that study likely reflects the more contrasted subgroups regarding aesthetics. Our findings align with previous studies showing good reliability for the MHQ domains, with the aesthetics domain demonstrating slightly lower reliability.4 High alpha values may also reflect item redundancy, suggesting potential for shorter forms such as the Brief MHQ.45,46 However, item reduction can increase error and compromise content validity, and evaluating these trade-offs requires a dedicated design, which is beyond the scope of the present cross-sectional study.

The present study evaluated the Dutch version of the MHQ. When PROMs are appropriately translated and culturally adapted, they are generally expected to perform similarly across languages.4,47 Therefore, although our findings are based on the Dutch version, they are likely relevant to other language versions used in comparable patient populations.

There are some limitations to the present study. Both EFA and CFA were conducted on the same dataset; however, the CFA tested predefined models and was not derived from the EFA results, thereby avoiding model overfitting. Second, the MHQ sample may introduce nonresponse bias. However, it was largely consistent with the full sample, and this likely leads to conservative estimates through reduced between-subject variability.48 As health literacy and socioeconomic status were not assessed, some bias from underrepresentation of these groups is also possible.49 Furthermore, health literacy may influence how patients interpret PROM questions and scores, which can affect understanding and communication in clinical practice.50,51 Third, known-group comparisons were limited by the small number of hypotheses and the small subgroup of patients with multiple fractures (n = 31), so evidence for construct validity is modest but adds to the MHQ’s discriminative validity. Finally, the reuse of existing data limited the assessment of several other measurement properties. Still, this study fills a key gap by evaluating internal consistency, structural validity, and known-groups validity. Moreover, validating existing PROMs with existing data is cost-efficient and enhances clinical and research utility, though ideally modifications should remain minimal to preserve comparability without compromising validity and reliability.

The MHQ should be interpreted and applied as a 6-domain instrument in patients with traumatic metacarpal or phalangeal fractures. Reporting the individual domain scores is recommended, as a summarized overall score may mask clinically relevant differences between domains and reduce the interpretability of the results. The hand function, ADL, work, and pain domains were found to be the most valid and reliable in this evaluation, though this may vary across different populations and subgroups. Future research should clarify which MHQ domains or PROMs are most suitable in defined contexts, compare performance with other instruments, and explore shorter or simplified versions to reduce response burden, particularly in populations with lower health literacy. Existing datasets offer opportunities for such analyses.
