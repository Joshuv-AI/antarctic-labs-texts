Resolving freeze-thaw surface energy exchange in soils through phase-dependent thermal measurements
Authors: Tunay Turk, Junaidul Islam, Tugce Baser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface energy exchange regulates permafrost stability and cold region climate feedback, yet key thermal parameters governing freeze-thaw transitions remain poorly constrained. In particular, soil emissivity and conductive heat transfer are often treated as static or one-dimensional properties, despite strong phase-dependent changes during freezing and thawing. Here we experimentally resolve the coupled evolution of surface emissivity, thermal conductivity, volumetric heat capacity, and conductive heat flux in sand-, silt-, and clay-dominated soils between -15 °C and 20 °C under varying saturation, organic content, and salinity. Frozen soils exhibit systematically lower emissivity than thawed soils, with distinct nonlinear transitions across phase change. Thermal conductivity increases substantially in the frozen state due to ice-enhanced grain contacts, while latent heat effects generate pronounced peaks in volumetric heat capacity near 0 °C. By explicitly quantifying both vertical and lateral conductive components, we demonstrate that neglecting lateral heat flux can underestimate total conductive energy transfer by up to 31.65%. These results provide physically constrained, phase-resolved parameterizations for land-surface and permafrost models and reduce uncertainty in infrared surface temperature retrievals across thermodynamically sensitive temperature ranges. Our findings establish a mechanistic framework linking phase change, radiative properties, and multidimensional heat transfer in cold region soils.Keywords: Permafrost, Soil surface emissivity, Infrared thermography, Soil thermal properties, Conductive heat fluxSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Arctic warming amplifies land-atmosphere energy exchange and accelerates permafrost degradation. Accurate representation of surface energy balance during phase transition is therefore essential for predicting active-layer evolution, ground stability, and associated climate feedback. Surface emissivity governs longwave radiative exchange and directly influences satellite-derived land surface temperature retrievals, yet the temperature dependence of surface emissivity in frozen soils remains poorly constrained. Field measurements often report comparable emissivity values for frozen and thawed soils, despite theoretical expectations that ice replacement should reduce thermal infrared emissivity. This unresolved discrepancy causes uncertainty in both remote sensing products and subsurface thermal models.

Land surface temperature retrieval is dependent on precise knowledge of surface emissivity, which is influenced by soil texture, degree of saturation, organic matter, and salinity1–4. The importance of accurately representing emissivity and thermal properties to constrain the interactions between permafrost thaw and climate warming has been highlighted in global-scale assessments5. Previous laboratory and field studies have demonstrated that small differences in emissivity, even with a magnitude of 0.01–0.03, can produce surface temperature errors of 0.5–2 °C in satellite-based permafrost monitoring6–8. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that surface temperature and emissivity are significantly affected by land-use and biophysical modifications, and these changes modify the energy balance in cold regions9,10. However, uncertainties in remotely sensed land surface temperature persist and can affect permafrost modeling accuracy11. Therefore, characterization of temperature dependence emissivity of permafrost is critical for both remote sensing applications and thermal modeling. Permafrost degradation can also be modulated by non-temperature environmental drivers, such as heavy rainfall or radiation changes, and accurate representation of thermal and radiative properties is therefore required12,13. Another important parameter to control land–atmosphere energy exchange is thermal properties of the near surface soils. Thermal conductivity and volumetric heat capacity are strongly dependent on temperature, phase constituents, degree of saturation (water content), mineral composition, and organic matter14–17. Freezing has been observed to increase thermal conductivity through ice formation and enhanced particle contact, whereas thawing and decreased degree of saturation result in reduced conductivity18–22. Strong nonlinearity of volumetric heat capacity near freezing has been reported due to latent heat effects23–25. Laboratory experiments have further shown that in frozen soils microscopic soil structure exerts strong control on thermal behavior by regulating particle contacts and the spatial distribution of ice within the pore space26. Further studies indicate that coupled thermal-hydraulic responses evolve over freeze–thaw transitions, highlighting the importance of integrated measurements9,10,27–29. Recent studies also use predictive models to estimate soil thermal properties across freeze–thaw cycles, highlighting model‑data integration for understanding thermal responses30. Additional complexity is introduced in clay-rich and soils with salinity through modified freezing dynamics and unfrozen fractions. Despite these known dependencies, many predictive models rely on simplified or region-specific parameterizations that neglect lateral heat transfer, soil heterogeneity, or phase-change effects31. Field studies have established that multidimensional heat transfer—including lateral advection by groundwater—is consequential in permafrost systems, with lateral advective fluxes shown to accelerate permafrost degradation far beyond what one-dimensional conductive formulations predict32,33. Kurylyk et al.34 quantified both vertical conductive and lateral advective heat transfer in peatland permafrost, demonstrating that the lateral component drives accelerated thaw relative to one-dimensional conductive formulations,notably, their study addresses advective transport by groundwater flow, which is a distinct mechanism from the lateral conductive flux examined in the present laboratory study34. Moreover, neglecting lateral heat fluxes has been shown to increase the total calculation errors in soil temperature simulations, leading to significant underestimations of the active layer thickness35.

Comparable emissivity values have been reported for frozen and unfrozen surfaces in various field measurements6,8,36. However, the reported similarity in emissivity between frozen and unfrozen conditions remains controversial because lower emissivity is physically expected when ice replaces liquid water in the pore structure, given the lower thermal infrared emissivity of ice relative to liquid water37. This discrepancy between field observations and physical expectations has not yet been resolved, in part because controlled laboratory measurements of surface emissivity for frozen bare soils are largely absent from literature. As a result, a major gap remains in existing studies. In addition, integrated datasets that combine emissivity, thermal conductivity, and volumetric heat capacity under identical conditions are rare. Most studies provide only limited coverage of the freeze-thaw transition. Overall, these missing data results in weak physical constraints for land-surface and permafrost models during the most sensitive temperature ranges.

Prior laboratory investigations have characterized either soil emissivity or thermal conductivity independently, but no controlled study has simultaneously resolved both properties across the full freeze–thaw transition under varied saturation, organic content, and salinity. In contrast to these prior studies, the measurements in this study are conducted in an integrated manner to capture the coupled evolution of emissivity, thermal conductivity, volumetric heat capacity, and conductive heat flux—enabling direct experimental testing of the theoretically predicted, emissivity reduction in frozen soils. In this work, the first experimental quantification of lateral and vertical conductive heat flux components at the laboratory scale is proposed.

In this study, a laboratory-based dataset of soil thermal properties relevant to permafrost environments is presented to quantify the radiative and thermal behavior of soils across the freeze-thaw transition (phase change). Temperature-dependent surface emissivity, thermal conductivity, volumetric heat capacity, and conductive heat fluxes are quantified for sand-, silt-, and clay-dominated soils between − 15 and 20 °C under variable degrees of saturation, organic content, and salinity. Transient infrared thermography, surface and depth thermocouples, and steady-state probe measurements are employed to resolve both lateral and vertical conductive heat flux components under identical experimental conditions. A continuous description of surface emissivity is obtained across the full temperature range, enabling direct comparison with subsurface thermal gradients and conductive heat fluxes. The results highlight the potential for energy balance errors when multidimensional effects are neglected and provide improved parameterization for cold-region remote sensing and permafrost modeling.

Background

When a soil layer in cold regions is subjected to thermal gradients, temperature-dependent phase change generates changes in unfrozen water content and pore water pressure. These changes induce thermodynamically driven hydraulic gradients, which produce cryosuction-controlled water migration toward the freezing front and excess pore pressure release during thawing24,38,39. The penetration and stability of the freezing front are controlled by soil thermal properties, primarily thermal conductivity, volumetric heat capacity, and latent heat buffering. These properties influence the redistribution of heat fluxes and determine whether ice segregation can be maintained14–16. As ice accumulates, the evolving ice-water-soil fabric alters effective thermal conductivity and diffusivity, in which temperature gradients and promoting further water migration is reinforced. Upon thawing, segregated ice reduces suction and weakens the soil skeleton, producing transient positive pore pressures, creep deformation, and irreversible volumetric strain40,41.

The radiative boundary condition at the ground surface exerts first-order control on freezing and thawing dynamics because it modulates the thermal and hydraulic processes driven by surface energy exchange. Surface emissivity governs this boundary condition, initiating and amplifying subsurface processes by controlling longwave heat loss and nighttime cooling efficiency. Changes in ice content, degree of saturation, and surface roughness modify emissivity, which in turn calibrates the magnitude of thermal gradients driving freezing and thawing3,6,7. The system therefore is a coupled feedback loop as emissivity governs thermal loads, thermal properties regulate phase-front propagation, and phase change reorganizes soil structure and water distribution, ultimately controlling deformation and long-term stability of cold-region geomaterials1,40.

Thermal conductivity depends strongly on the degree of saturation. Higher values have been measured under frozen conditions due to replacement of air-filled pores by ice and formation of ice bridges between mineral grains20–22. Dependence on mineral composition has also been reported, with higher values observed in quartz-rich sands than in fine-grained or organic-rich soils40. Gradual changes in thermal conductivity with temperature have been observed during freezing and thawing. This behavior has been attributed to retention of liquid water in small pores at subzero temperatures caused by capillary and adsorptive forces24,38,41.

Volumetric heat capacity varies strongly near the freezing front. Apparent peaks have been attributed to the latent heat of fusion24,41. Larger peaks occur at higher degrees of saturation due to a larger latent heat reservoir. Fine-grained and saline soils exhibit broader temperature ranges of phase change14,38. Organic-rich soils show higher volumetric heat capacity because of high retention capacity and the intrinsic thermal properties of organic material40. Sensitivity of inverse estimates of thermal conductivity to surface boundary conditions has been reported17. Uncertainty in emissivity propagates into subsurface thermal property estimates.

Methods

To investigate soil thermal properties, a series of laboratory experiments are conducted using a consistent set of soil samples across two different experimental configurations. In the first setup transient temperature data at both the soil surface and internal depths are recorded. The second setup focuses on obtaining steady-state thermal properties, specifically thermal conductivity and volumetric heat capacity.

Experimental set-up

Surface emissivity

The experimental setup for the surface emissivity measurements is illustrated in Figure 1. For monitoring the real-time warming trend, a FLIR X6981 (P/N: 29267-281) high-speed mid-wavelength infrared (MWIR) camera is employed. This camera features a 3-5 μm wavelength range, an f/2.5 Indium Antimonide (InSb) detector, and a 100 mm focal length lens (P/N: 4219563). Temperature measurements are recorded using Type K insulated beaded wire thermocouples (Harold G. Schaevitz Industries—HGSI BWTC-24ST-TT-060-K-MP) connected to an Excelog 6-channel handheld data logger at a sampling rate of 1 Hz.

Thermographic surface emissivity measurement set-up: Pictures (left) and a schematic illustration (right).

The samples are placed on dry ice (solidified CO2) pellets within a cooler until fully frozen. Then the samples are placed on the measurement set-up, and the data collection is initiated when the surface reaches -20°C, consistent with the IR camera’s calibration range between − 20 and 55 °C. The camera was factory-calibrated with a high-precision blackbody source, and no further atmospheric or background corrections were necessary due to the controlled laboratory environment and short measurement distance (< 1.0 m). Samples are allowed to warm under ambient room temperature conditions, with the warming trend recorded between -20°C and +20°C. During the warming phase, the IR camera and two thermocouples were used simultaneously. The IR camera and one thermocouple monitor the specimen surface, while the other thermocouple was inserted 5 mm below the surface through a pre-drilled side hole in the container. The emissivity values obtained in this work represent an effective emissivity integrated over the MWIR spectrum (3–5 μm), derived by applying the Stefan-Boltzmann-type ratio in Eq. 11 to band-limited radiance measurements. This quantity differs from spectrally resolved emissivity and from the broadband thermal infrared emissivity commonly used in land-surface models. However, soil emissivity in the MWIR and LWIR (8–14 μm) bands is governed by the same surface properties—mineralogy, moisture content, and roughness—and both respond to ice-water phase change in a physically consistent manner. The phase-dependent trends reported here are therefore expected to be representative of behavior across the broader thermal infrared spectrum. Direct quantitative application to broadband energy balance models would require spectral conversion, but the MWIR measurements provide the experimental constraints on phase-dependent radiative behavior necessary to improve parameterizations in land-surface and permafrost models and to refine dual-channel and split-window surface temperature retrieval algorithms.

Thermal properties

The experimental setup for measuring thermal properties is illustrated in Fig. 2. To maintain soil samples at steady-state temperatures across both frozen and unfrozen states, a Peltier-Thermoelectric cold-plate cooler (TE Technology Inc., CP-061HT) is utilized. The cooler is regulated by an adjustable DC power supply (DROK 0–48 V, 0–10 A). To mitigate lateral thermal loss, samples are wrapped in 1-inch-thick ceramic fiber insulation (Lynn Manufacturing) and are secured with pipe insulation tape (Redford Supply Co.).

Picture of experimental set-up used for thermal property measurements.

Thermal conductivity and volumetric heat capacity are measured using the TEMPOS Thermal Properties Analyzer (Meter Group Inc.) equipped with a dual-needle sensor probe, in accordance with ASTM D5334. The sensor consists of two parallel stainless-steel needles (30 mm long, 1.3 mm diameter, 6 mm spacing). One needle is used as a line heat source, while the second needle is equipped with a precision thermistor for monitoring the temperature response. A controlled heat pulse is applied to the heating needle, and thermal properties are determined from the transient temperature rise recorded by the sensing needle based on the transient line heat source method.

Mathematical model

The IR thermography is employed to predict soil thermal behavior in the experiments. The IR experimental setup in Fig. 1 represents boundary conditions representing an uncontrolled warming environment similar to field conditions. While this lack of control introduces challenges in predicting thermal behavior, it allows for a more realistic simulation of actual field scenarios, and data collection includes internal temperature gradients via an embedded thermocouple (TC1) and surface temperature profiles via IR measurements. While the IR measurements captured the entire surface, two specific point locations at the center and at a set radial distance of 10 mm are selected to measure lateral heat flux in addition to vertical heat flux. These two heat fluxes are used to calculate the apparent heat flux of the samples. It is noted that Tr at 10 mm represents a single boundary measurement used under the assumption of radially symmetric conduction; the resulting qapp therefore constitutes a first-order geometric correction for lateral conduction rather than a spatially resolved multidimensional flux field and does not capture advective heat transport. Furthermore, to ensure accuracy, the IR data are calibrated using surface emissivity values derived from the surface thermocouple (TC2). Results

Surface emissivity

The time series of temperatures from the experiments are evaluated by comparing the surface thermocouple (TC2) measurements with the infrared (IR) camera recordings. The thermocouple data represents a discrete point measurement at the contact location, whereas the IR temperature reflects the spatial mean of hundreds of measurement points across the soil surface to ensure the robustness of the mean trend. Using the spatial mean helps avoid local disturbances that could affect a single pixel and ensures that the measured surface temperature is consistent with the thermocouple reading. The instrumental uncertainty for temperature measurements is estimated at ±0.5°C. The magnitude of the observed temperature and emissivity variations across phase change exceeds this uncertainty, indicating that the reported trends are physically significant rather than measurement artifacts.

A continuous temperature increase is observed in the dry soils as no water phase change occurs, whereas distinct phase change behavior is seen in the soils at degrees of saturation of 0.4 m3/m3 and 0.7 m3/m3 as shown in Figure 3. Depending on the soil type, an additional 29-45 minutes is required for the soil at a degree of saturation of 0.4 m3/m3 to reach ambient temperature relative to the dry soil samples. An additional 21–38 minutes is required for the soils at a degree of saturation 0.7 m3/m3 to reach thermal equilibrium due to the energy required for water phase change and relatively larger volumetric heat capacity.

However, an unexpected drop in IR temperatures below − 20 °C is observed for dry silt in Fig. 3b. This discrepancy is attributed to surface artifacts, such as local roughness, because the IR camera calibration is limited to the range between − 20 and 55 °C. Therefore, data points below − 20 °C are excluded from further emissivity analysis and subsequent figures are presented within the reliable range between − 15 and 20°C.

Conductive heat flux

The conductive heat flux is strongly influenced by soil composition and corresponding changes in thermal conductivity and temperature gradients. Maximum heat flux values are computed at − 15 °C for all samples, as the frozen state provides both the largest thermal gradients and highest thermal conductivity. While an increased degree of saturation enhances thermal bridging and heat flux, the organic matter acts as an insulator, reducing total energy transfer. While laboratory-scale measurements on remolded soils do not account for field-scale advection, the observed multidimensional conductive flux provides a mechanistic basis for the refinement of local thermal parameterizations in permafrost models. A notable observation is that total heat transfer is affected by lateral heat flux in addition to vertical flux, indicating that consideration of lateral heat transfer is essential. The observed lateral conductive contribution of up to 31.65% in organic-rich laboratory samples suggests that one-dimensional conductive parameterizations may underestimate total conductive flux in thermodynamically sensitive soils. Whether this effect is significant at the field scale—where geometry, heterogeneity, and advective processes interact—requires further validation beyond the scope of the present study.

Surface emissivity

The emissivity for each soil configuration is influenced by soil type, organic content, and the degree of saturation. A trend is observed where emissivity values are lower in the frozen state and increase as soils reach ambient temperatures. This behavior is caused by the presence of ice in the soil pores since the emissivity of ice is lower than that of liquid water. The degree of saturation leads to a relatively large change in emissivity during the phase transition, particularly in the sand soils. While dry soils follow a relatively linear increasing trend with temperature, samples with water content showed a sharp transition. The addition of organic matter results in relatively lower emissivity values compared to mineral soils, whereas the soils with salinity have the highest emissivity values in the frozen state. For example, organic sand with salinity at − 15 °C has an emissivity of 0.8877, which is much higher than the value of 0.7112 observed in the organic sand soil without salinity. This is explained by the salt content lowering the freezing point, which prevents the full ice formation at − 15 °C, maintaining near-thawed surface conditions. The emissivity measured in the thawed state for all soils in this study ranges between 0.9564 and 0.9878, which is in strong agreement with the bare soil literature values reported in Hipps45 and Hulley et al.6

. In contrast, field studies report emissivity values for frozen or partially frozen surfaces that are often comparable to thawed conditions, typically exceeding 0.96,36, whereas lower values are measured in this study. The observed behavior reflects the direct influence of ice–water phase change within the soil matrix, where replacement of liquid water by ice reduces surface emissivity. These findings demonstrate that soil surface emissions are not static across freeze-thaw transitions and must be parameterized as a temperature-dependent variable to avoid systematic bias in cold region surface energy models.

Thermal properties

The thermal conductivity of all soils with water content is higher in the frozen state than in the thawed state. The replacement of liquid water in the pores with ice forms ice bridges between soil grains, enhancing thermal bridging and reducing thermal resistance within the soil matrix. The degree of saturation is another critical factor, as an increase from 0.4 m3/m3 to 0.7 m3/m3 leads to higher conductivity across all mineral soils. The thermal conductivity measured for frozen Ottawa sand exceeds values reported for tundra or silty soils by Nakano and Brown21, but aligns with the trends for high-quartz soils reported by Vu et al.22. Because quartz minerals have relatively higher intrinsic conductivity, their distribution in the Ottawa sand allows efficient heat flow when combined with ice bridges. The elevated frozen thermal conductivity of 4.29 W/m K observed in Ottawa sand at a degree of saturation of 0.7 m3/m3 is attributable to the combination of high quartz content, the target compaction density of 1550 kg/m3, and near-full pore saturation with ice at -15°C. At this density, the Johansen16 and Côté and Konrad14 model yields consistent predictions of 4.26. The measured value of 4.29 W/m K aligns closely with the nearly saturated upper bound of both models. An exception to the trends observed in sand and clay occurs in the silt samples, where the organic sample with salinity has higher frozen conductivity than the sample without salinity, likely due to salt ions modifying the ice formation and microstructure during freezing. This behavior is consistent observed in Ju et al.46, where thermal conductivity in silt can increase with salt addition, whereas sand consistently shows a decrease.

The volumetric heat capacity of soils with water content exhibits pronounced peaks during the freezing process. These peaks arise from the latent heat of fusion as pore water converts to ice, which increases the energy required to raise the soil temperature. Higher degrees of saturation lead to relatively higher volumetric heat capacity due to the high specific heat of water and the latent heat involved in the phase transition. Volumetric heat capacity increases with the addition of organic content because of enhanced water retention and the thermal properties of organic particles. In samples with salinity, the peak volumetric heat capacity shifts to lower temperatures, as the freezing point depression extends the temperature range over which latent heat is released. The lower volumetric heat capacity values recorded at − 15 °C compared to 20 °C across most samples are consistent with ice having a lower specific heat than liquid water.

Conclusion

Freeze-thaw transitions reorganize soil energy exchange in ways that are not captured by conventional surface energy formulations. Through controlled phase-resolved measurements, we show that soil emissivity, thermal conductivity, and volumetric heat capacity evolve nonlinearly across freezing and thawing, governed by ice-water substitution and latent heat dynamics. Frozen soils exhibit systematically lower emissivity and enhanced conductive pathways due to ice-particle contacts, while narrow temperature intervals near 0 °C produce amplified heat-transfer sensitivity.

These results provide a physical basis for discrepancies between theoretical expectations and field-based emissivity observations by demonstrating that emissivity is intrinsically temperature- and phase-dependent rather than quasi-static. In parallel, explicit separation of vertical and lateral conductive components reveals that multidimensional heat transfer is a structural component of freeze–thaw energy exchange. Neglecting lateral transfer leads to systematic underestimation of total conductive flux, particularly in organic-rich soils, indicating that one-dimensional assumptions may be insufficient to represent conductive heat transfer in the laboratory samples tested here.

The experimental dataset provides necessary input for parameterizing thermal models in cold-region climate science and engineering. Although the tests were conducted on controlled laboratory soils, the underlying thermodynamic relationships—ice–water substitution, latent heat buffering, and phase-dependent conductivity—are governed by phase-change physics common to cold-region soils. Quantitative field-scale application, however, requires further validation incorporating natural geometry, structural heterogeneity, and advective transport.

By experimentally linking radiative properties, phase change, and multidimensional conduction, this study reframes phase-transition surface dynamics as a coupled thermophysical system. Incorporating phase-dependent emissivity and multidimensional heat transfer into land-surface representations could reduce uncertainty in cold-region energy-balance models and improve predictions of active-layer evolution and land–atmosphere feedback, provided these parameterizations are validated against field observations.
