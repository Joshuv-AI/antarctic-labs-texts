Is biological mesh a suitable option for laparoscopic intraperitoneal onlay mesh plus (IPOM plus) repair in the treatment of abdominal wall hernias?
Authors: Yiting Liu, Yingmo Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of biological mesh in laparoscopic intraperitoneal onlay mesh plus (IPOM plus) repair of abdominal wall hernias.MethodsFrom January 2021 to January 2022, the clinical data of 30 patients with various types of abdominal wall hernias were analyzed. All patients underwent laparoscopic IPOM plus repair, during which the hernia defect was closed with non-absorbable sutures prior to placement of the biological mesh. Preoperative general information, as well as complications, adverse events, and hospitalization costs, were monitored and analyzed.ResultsIn this study, the patients’ ages ranged from 28 to 65 years, and the length of their hospital stays ranged from 4 to 10 days. The surgical method employed was laparoscopic IPOM plus repair, with operation times ranging from 50 to 80 min. Follow-up evaluations were conducted at 7 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years post-operation. No patients were lost to follow-up during this period. Additionally, there were no cases of abdominal hernia recurrence, intestinal fistula, intestinal obstruction, wound infection, mesh infection, chronic pain, or foreign body sensation. One patient developed seroma, which was cured via drainage. The patients’ hospitalization costs ranged from $4,211 to $8,307 USD.ConclusionThe use of biological mesh for laparoscopic IPOM plus repair with non-absorbable suture closure of the hernia defect is both effective and safe, making it a valuable option for clinical application, especially for young patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03235-7.Keywords: Abdominal wall hernia, Biological mesh, Retrospective study

Introduction

Abdominal wall hernias can be classified as primary or incisional hernias [1]. Primary abdominal hernias refer to congenital or spontaneous hernias that are not caused by trauma or neurogenic disease [2]. Incisional hernias, on the other hand, occur at the site of a previous surgical incision [3].

Regardless of type, abdominal wall hernias can only be treated with surgery. Laparoscopic intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) repair is one of the most common methods for abdominal wall hernia repair [4, 5]. The initial open approach involves extensive dissection and poses a substantial risk of infection, leading to prolonged hospitalization. IPOM can mitigate these drawbacks [6–8].

Currently, hernia surgeons place equal emphasis on safety concerns, such as postoperative recurrence, and the enhancement of abdominal wall functionality and improvement in quality of life [9]. The size of the hernia defect and the surgeon’s level of expertise significantly impact the procedural outcome [10]. Moreover, careful consideration should be given to selecting an appropriate mesh material [11, 12]. Synthetic meshes have been utilized extensively in surgical procedures for abdominal wall hernia repair; however, more recently, biological meshes have been gradually adopted in such surgeries [13].

However, evidence regarding the clinical outcomes and safety of biological mesh in laparoscopic abdominal wall hernia repair remains limited. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to evaluate the safety and efficacy of biological mesh in laparoscopic abdominal wall hernia repair. By retrospectively analyzing the clinical data of 30 patients treated at our center with a biological mesh (COOK Medical Co., Ltd., USA), we aim to provide further evidence for the clinical application and potential broader use of biological mesh in abdominal wall hernia repair.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients included in this study were adults aged 18 to 70 years, diagnosed with abdominal wall hernia, who underwent laparoscopic IPOM-plus repair using biological mesh. Patients were excluded if they had incarcerated or multiple hernias, underwent other surgical techniques, or received synthetic or non-biological prosthetic materials.

Research methods

The clinical data of 30 patients who were diagnosed with abdominal wall hernia and underwent laparoscopic IPOM plus repair with biological mesh at our center were retrospectively analyzed. In all cases, the hernia defect was closed with non-absorbable sutures prior to mesh placement. Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital. This group was treated with biological mesh (Cook Biodesign®, USA). The safety and efficacy of this treatment were analyzed to determine the clinical significance of the biological mesh for the treatment of abdominal wall hernias.

Evaluation indices

Patients’ baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, hernia type and size, and hospitalization cost, were evaluated. The safety and efficacy indices included recurrence, chronic pain, foreign body sensation, wound infection, mesh infection, hematoma and seroma, and adverse conditions, such as intestinal fistula and intestinal obstruction.

Postoperative discomfort was characterized primarily by chronic pain, which was defined as abdominal pain persisting for more than 3 months following the surgical procedure and with a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score of ≥ 4 [14, 15]. This type of pain is often severe and debilitating and can significantly affect patients’ quality of life. The assessment of postoperative foreign body sensation primarily included patients’ subjective perceptions. The persistence of unabsorbed hematomas and seromas three months post-surgery was documented through ultrasound or abdominal CT examinations. The presence of intestinal fistula and obstruction was further confirmed on the basis of clinical symptoms and imaging examinations.

Surgical methods

Following general anesthesia, standard iodophor disinfection and sterile draping were performed. Initially, artificial pneumoperitoneum was established at a pressure of 14 mmHg. A 10-mm trocar was subsequently inserted to introduce the laparoscopic lens, and two additional 5-mm trocars were placed based on the hernia location. The intra-abdominal adhesions were meticulously dissected, the hernial contents were restored, and the hernial ring was carefully closed via non-absorbable polyester sutures (Fig. 1). A biological mesh was then applied and fixed at least 3–5 cm beyond the margin of the abdominal wall defect via absorbable tacks (Fig. 2). An intraperitoneal drainage tube was introduced based on intraoperative assessment. Finally, all surgical instruments and trocars were carefully removed to evacuate carbon dioxide gas from the abdomen before each incision was closed by suturing the subcutaneous tissue and skin.

Follow-up methods

Patients were routinely followed up at 7 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years after surgery, either through outpatient clinic visits or telephone interviews. Patients were routinely followed up in the outpatient clinic 1 month after surgery. Subsequent follow-up was conducted at designated time points either through outpatient visits or telephone interviews. During each follow-up, comprehensive assessments were performed, including evaluation for postoperative complications (such as seroma formation, wound infection, mesh infection, intestinal fistula, and intestinal obstruction), assessment of hernia recurrence, and observation of chronic pain, or foreign body sensation. When clinical symptoms or findings indicated possible complications, further imaging examinations—such as ultrasound or computed tomography (CT)—were conducted as necessary to confirm the diagnosis and guide management. No patients were lost to follow-up during the study period.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study, informed by a targeted literature review and expert consultation with two hernia surgeons and one qualitative researcher. The guide covers key domains relevant to postoperative outcomes after laparoscopic IPOM plus repair, including postoperative complications (e.g., seroma, wound and mesh infection, intestinal fistula, intestinal obstruction), hernia recurrence, pain and foreign body sensation, and healthcare utilization (e.g., readmission or re-intervention). The guide was reviewed by the study team to ensure clarity and content coverage. The finalized English version of the interview guide is provided in Supplementary File 1 and is cited at first mention in the Methods section (see Supplementary File 1).

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 26.0 software. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (± s), whereas non-normally distributed continuous data are presented as medians (ranges). Categorical data are expressed as absolute numbers.

Results

A total of 30 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Among the 30 patients, 4 were male and 26 were female. They had a median age of 38 years (range: 28–65) and a BMI of 24.21 ± 1.83 kg/m². There were 5 cases of umbilical hernia, 17 cases of epigastric hernia, and 8 cases of incisional hernia. According to the ASA classification, 24 cases were Grade I, 4 cases were Grade II, and 2 cases were Grade III. All patients underwent IPOM repair. The median diameter of the hernia defect was 6.0 cm (range: 2.0–8.0), as measured during the operation, and the median operation time was 65 min (range: 50–80). The median length of hospital stay was 7 days (range: 4–10). The median hospitalization cost was $6,030 USD (range: $4,211–$8,307 USD).

The primary outcome variables assessed were complications and adverse events. Complications included recurrence, chronic pain, foreign body sensation, wound infection, mesh infection, hematoma, and seroma formation, whereas adverse events included intestinal fistula and intestinal obstruction. All patients were followed for 24 months, with no loss to follow-up. At the final assessment, there were no instances of ventral hernia recurrence or adverse events. Additionally, all patients reported an absence of chronic pain, foreign body sensation, wound infection and mesh infection. One patient developed a postoperative seroma, and a drainage tube was reinserted 6 months after surgery. Subsequent follow-up examinations revealed no recurrence of the seroma after the drainage tube was removed. Discussion

Necessitating surgical intervention as the primary treatment modality, abdominal wall hernia is a highly prevalent condition in the field of hernia and abdominal wall surgery [16, 17]. The IPOM technique is a well-established surgical method for repairing abdominal wall hernias [18]. The postoperative outcomes of these procedures significantly influence patients’ quality of life. The choice of mesh material plays a critical role in determining these outcomes. Synthetic meshes have been used extensively in IPOM surgery for many years. In contrast, biological meshes have been utilized relatively infrequently, although their application in IPOM surgery has been increasing in recent years.

Synthetic meshes can result in the excessive formation of fibrous tissue. Excessive reactive fibrous tissue formation can lead to various complications, including fibrosis, adhesion, scarring, and decreased abdominal wall elasticity, which can impact the functionality of the abdominal wall and cause discomfort, including pain and a sensation akin to the presence of a foreign body [19–21]. The endogenous tissue regeneration process of biological mesh closely mimics the natural repair mechanism of living tissues in their physiological state. The regenerated tissue results in minimal scar formation, undergoes biodegradation within the human body post-surgery, and leaves no foreign body residue [22–24]. Additionally, it possesses excellent mechanical compliance, compatibility, and elasticity, facilitating the restoration of abdominal wall function with a lower incidence of postoperative pain and foreign body sensation [25]. No chronic pain or foreign body sensation occurred during the follow-up period in this study. The patients in this group are relatively young and possess specific requirements for their quality of life in the later stages. Therefore, our center believes that, for such young patients, utilizing a biological mesh would be a more optimal choice.

Although the possibility of mesh adhesion to the intestinal tube leading to intestinal obstruction exists when the material is exposed to the abdominal cavity, no such adverse events were observed in the patients enrolled in this study. Additionally, there is a potential risk of synthetic patch erosion on internal organs within the abdominal cavity [26, 27]. This risk can be avoided with biological mesh because it can be completely degraded after surgery. No cases of intestinal fistula occurred during the follow-up period in this study. The authors emphasize that proper flattening of meshes is crucial to minimizing the risk of erosion for both synthetic and biological meshes. Furthermore, an extended follow-up period is necessary to identify any potential long-term mesh erosion.

Biological patches are composed of autologous or allogeneic skin, pericardium, submucosal tissue from the intestine, or other collagen matrices [28, 29]. These matrices undergo processes, such as decellularization, deproteinization, and deimmunization, to preserve the extracellular matrix as a scaffold [30]. The introduction of the biological mesh promotes the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells and facilitates rapid revascularization of the matrix. Stem cells migrate toward the injured tissue, adhere to the extracellular matrix, and subsequently differentiate into specific cell types that are essential for tissue regeneration. This process ultimately generates a fresh extracellular matrix that contributes to the complete repair and reconstruction of the abdominal wall [31]. The maximum intra-abdominal pressure in humans occurs during coughing or strenuous exercise, reaching approximately 22.6 kPa. This pressure can be determined by applying the relevant formula for abdominal wall tear strength, which is estimated to be approximately 16 N/cm [32]. Notably, after hernia repair, the tensile capacity of a biological patch is sufficient to meet these requirements. Some clinicians are concerned about the recurrence of abdominal wall hernias following the use of biological mesh, but during the follow-up period in this study, no instances of hernia recurrence were observed, indicating the safety and efficacy of biological mesh in abdominal wall hernia repair. Our center emphasizes the importance of accurately closing the hernial ring during surgery, we utilized non-absorbable polyester sutures for this purpose. At the same time, postoperative management is equally crucial. Strict weight control is necessary to prevent increased intra-abdominal pressure associated with weight gain. Nevertheless, longer-term follow-up is necessary to understand the sustained effect of biological mesh. Additionally, it should be noted that all patients included in this study had small- to medium-sized abdominal wall hernias with a maximum hernia ring diameter of ≤ 8 cm. Therefore, further investigation is needed to validate the effectiveness of biological mesh for larger abdominal wall hernias.

Despite their high degree of efficacy in promoting tissue repair and regeneration, the application of biological patches might lead to postoperative seroma formation. These patches consist of living cells or tissues that can elicit an immune response in certain patients [33]. Consequently, fluid accumulation within the surgical site occurs, leading to the development of a seroma. The term “seroma” refers to fluid that remains unabsorbed for a duration exceeding 3 months. Failure to address this condition promptly can result in the fluid’s serving as a medium for bacterial proliferation and subsequent secondary infection. All patients in this group experienced postoperative effusion. In 29 patients, the effusion was completely absorbed within 3 months after surgery. However, one patient had persistent effusion after 3 months, which we defined as a seroma. We observed that this seroma was not absorbed until 6 months after surgery. A drainage tube was reinserted 6 months post-surgery and subsequently removed after 10 days, with no incidence of new seroma formation, mesh infection, or hernia recurrence. For patients with seromas, timely puncture and drainage are essential. It is important to take precautions to avoid the risk of retrograde infection during the procedure.

In recent years, there has been a trend toward the increasing utilization of biological meshes in abdominal wall hernia repair. This shift has been driven by emerging research underscoring their potential advantages over synthetic meshes, including reduced postoperative pain, decreased foreign body sensation, and a lower incidence of complications, particularly in contaminated and infected patients [34–37]. Nevertheless, the widespread adoption of biological meshes in IPOM surgery has been impeded by several challenges, including the need for precise postoperative outcome data and the considerable costs associated with their use [38–40]. The median hospitalization cost for this group of patients was 43,414 yuan, primarily due to the high cost of the meshes. However, patients may receive partial reimbursement for these costs in accordance with specific national policies. Despite the significant financial implications, patients frequently perceive the advantages as surpassing the economic burden and are typically inclined to accept these costs. Consequently, continuous development and research focused on minimizing the costs associated with biological meshes are essential. Such advancements would promote wider adoption and allow a greater patient population to benefit from these innovative therapeutic modalities.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, with only 30 patients included, including just 8 cases of incisional hernia, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Second, all patients had small- to medium-sized hernia defects (maximum diameter ≤ 8 cm), so the safety and efficacy of biological mesh in larger or more complex hernias remain uncertain. Third, the follow-up period was limited to 24 months, which may not be sufficient to capture long-term complications or hernia recurrence. Therefore, further multicenter, prospective, controlled studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings and to better elucidate the potential advantages of biological mesh in abdominal wall hernia repair.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that biological mesh is both safe and efficacious for use in laparoscopic IPOM plus repair with non-absorbable suture closure of the hernia defect, especially for young patients. Although the associated hospitalization costs are considerable, they generally are accepted by patients, justifying further clinical adoption and promotion. Notably, this study included only patients whose hernia defects were ≤ 8 cm in diameter; therefore, further investigations are needed to evaluate the efficacy of abdominal wall hernia repair for larger defects. Furthermore, owing to the limited follow-up period of this study, it is necessary to conduct a multicenter study with a large sample size for further validation.
