Artificial intelligence in clinical toxicology in Africa: Emerging applications and barriers
Authors: Mikiyas G Teferi, Biruk T Mengistie, Helina K Teklehaimanot, Chernet T Mengistie, Fitsum A Gemechu, Michael A Negussie, Tilahun J Jufara, Getaw W Hassen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) has a supplementary role in clinical toxicology in Africa, addressing key challenges such as delayed diagnoses, limited expertise, and inadequate healthcare infrastructure. This method has the potential to increase diagnostic accuracy, optimize treatment strategies, and advance research on toxic substance exposure and poisoning cases. AI-driven tools, including machine learning algorithms and decision-support systems, enhance the early detection and risk assessment of toxicities. AI-powered predictive models facilitate precision medicine by designing treatment plans for individual patient profiles. Integrating this in telemedicine expands access to toxicology expertise, particularly in resource-limited settings. Additionally, AI accelerates research by analyzing large datasets, identifying trends, and predicting toxicological risks, thus contributing to public health interventions. Despite these advancements, challenges such as data poverty, ethical issues, and restrictive policies hinder its full potential in African healthcare. These gaps can be bridged through policy reforms, capacity-building initiatives, and robust AI frameworks, which will be crucial in maximizing AI benefits for clinical toxicology. This narrative review highlights the emerging applications of AI in Africa, emphasizing the need for collaborative efforts to ensure equitable and effective implementation. However, its adoption is limited by financial constraints, scarce datasets, weak infrastructure, and ethical concerns.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Clinical toxicology, Treatment, Africa, Machine learning, Emergency medicine

African relevance

Poisoning and toxic exposures remain under-recognized yet significant public health challenges in many African countries, often due to limited diagnostic resources, underreporting, and gaps in surveillance systems.

The findings support efforts to strengthen health systems, improve toxicovigilance, and advance digital health equity across low- and middle-income settings in Africa.

Introduction

Clinical toxicology is a medical discipline concerned with the diagnosis, management, and prevention of poisoning and other adverse effects from drugs, chemicals, and environmental or biological toxins. In Africa, it is characterized by a high incidence of poisoning, including accidental and intentional exposure, which poses a substantial public health burden [1]. The World Health Organization reported that, in 2012, 84 % of all unintentional poisoning-related deaths worldwide occurred in low- and middle-income countries, with Africa being a major contributor [2]. The epidemiology varies by context: for children, the ingestion of household chemicals and kerosene is common, whereas in adults, organophosphate pesticide poisoning and envenomation (such as snakebites) are associated with high morbidity and mortality [2,3] (Table 1). For example, systematic review data from Ethiopia revealed that acute poisonings are largely caused by organophosphate insecticides and household cleaning products, with most cases occurring in individuals under the age of 30 years, and a majority are due to intentional self-harm [4].

In sub-Saharan Africa, snakebite envenomation poses severe public health challenges, with an estimated one million people bitten by snakes every year. The number of deaths per year in this area is thought to be within the range of 7000–20,000 [5]. The true case‒fatality ratio is likely much higher than reported because of substantial underreporting driven by limited healthcare access and widespread use of traditional medicine. For example, only 10 out of 58 African countries have operational poison control centers, and the regions that do have such centers lack access to essential antidotes and toxicology expertise [6]. The diagnosis and treatment of poisoning are often delayed and sub-optimally achieved. Studies in Ethiopia, for example, have revealed the low availability of essential antidotes and poor toxicology treatment knowledge among clinicians [7]. Additionally, the absence of routine laboratory toxicology screening and confirmatory testing in many hospitals leads to cases being unrecognized or unreported, which contributes to the persistent underestimation of the true incidence [2,3].

The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare

Over the past decade, the importance of artificial intelligence (AI) has increased worldwide, advancing research [11,12]. Machine learning (ML) algorithms have now approached human experts in tasks such as medical image interpretation and pattern recognition in clinical data [13] Applications of AI range from clinical decision support, where systems examine symptoms, laboratory results, and imaging to help in formulating a diagnosis, to workflow automation and patient monitoring in real time. In low-resource settings, including many African nations it holds particular promise to supplement the lack of human expertise with its computational power [14,15].

Rationale for AI in clinical toxicology in Africa

The use of AI in clinical toxicology is still in its early stages worldwide, and its role in Africa is exploratory because of the limited datasets available. The use of AI in Africa’s clinical toxicology services solves the aforementioned issues with respect to diagnostics and treatment. AI can improve diagnostic accuracy and speed in poisoning cases, which is critical when healthcare providers lack specialized training or tools for toxicological evaluation [16]. ML algorithms can analyze a patient's symptoms, vital signs, and test results to identify patterns suggesting specific toxins, allowing for earlier diagnosis of poisoning even in economically disadvantaged settings where confirmatory laboratory tests are impossible [[17], [18], [19]].

AI in the diagnosis and early detection of toxicity

AI-driven diagnostic tools

AI enhances the diagnosis of poisoning and toxic exposure by providing rapid, real-time decision support. ML algorithms can recognize clinical patterns associated with specific toxins. A pilot study demonstrated that ML models accurately identified toxic agents (e.g., acetaminophen and benzodiazepines) with ∼77–80 % accuracy using patient symptoms alone [18]. AI tools can integrate multiple diagnostic inputs, including imaging and electrocardiograms. For example, computer vision algorithms analyze radiographs or CT scans for toxin indicators such as radiopaque pills or organ damage, whereas other algorithms evaluate neurological exams for toxin-specific findings [12] (Fig. 1). AI has shown potential in early diagnosis in resource-rich settings, but in Africa, its utility will depend on simpler, nonimaging-based models owing to limited access to CT scans and radiology. In resource-limited African hospitals, AI-driven tools can aggregate vital signs, clinical presentations, and available laboratory data to differentiate between common toxicities [18]. A notable example includes an AI-based triage model for mushroom poisoning [13,20]. Although access to AI tools in resource-limited settings remains constrained, emerging low-cost, cloud-based platforms and regional collaborations offer potential pathways for implementation.

Data mining and pattern recognition

AI excels at analyzing large datasets to identify patterns indicative of specific toxins. Algorithms utilizing electronic health records and poison control databases can detect subtle symptom clusters and laboratory findings suggestive of certain poisons [21,22]. Natural language processing (NLP) techniques effectively extract diagnostic clues from unstructured clinical texts [21]. These methods highlight the ability of AI to rapidly detect overlooked toxicological details. In fragmented African healthcare settings, AI-driven data mining could aggregate data from various hospitals to identify poisoning trends. For example, researchers in Morocco have proposed AI-driven software for predicting specific poisonings using clinical signs alone, which is valuable in rural clinics lacking laboratory facilities [23]. Successful AI applications in analogous healthcare areas reinforce the potential effectiveness in low-resource toxicology contexts [[24], [25], [26]]. Given the scarcity of poison centres in many African countries, robust AI implementation would require regional or multi-country data pooling to generate sufficient datasets for reliable performance.

AI in the treatment and management of toxic exposures

Personalized treatment protocols using AI

AI is transforming toxicology by enabling personalized treatment strategies that go beyond fixed guidelines. In African settings, where toxic exposure treatments often follow rigid protocols, AI can assimilate patient-specific factors such as age, comorbidities, and exposure magnitude [27]. ML models have been used to optimize antidote dosing. This ensures precision care, improves outcomes and conserves scarce medical resources [28]. AI also aids complex decision-making in polysubstance overdoses, prioritizing treatments on the basis of patient-specific needs. It integrates toxicokinetic models with clinical data to adjust antidote infusion rates and optimize interventions such as hemodialysis [29]. As AI continuously learns from new cases, it refines treatment algorithms, offering an evolving standard of care adapted to local contexts [30].

AI in drug discovery and repurposing for toxicology

AI has accelerated antidote discovery and drug repurposing, addressing poisoning cases prevalent in Africa. AI-designed synthetic proteins have shown high efficacy in neutralizing snake venom toxins, paving the way for next-generation antivenoms that are more affordable and accessible [31]. In addition to envenomation, AI-driven repurposing is being explored for chemical poisoning, identifying drugs that counteract pesticide neurotoxicity or chelating agents for heavy metal poisoning. By predicting toxicity and efficacy in computer-based simulation studies, AI streamlines research, conserving laboratory resources and making drug discovery cost-effective [32].

AI in toxicology research and data analytics

Big data and AI for toxicology research

Toxicology research relies on increasing amounts of data and current AI technology can prove useful in analyzing complex toxicological datasets. The use of AI in toxicology for predicting patient outcomes, such as organ failure, via extensive databases from poison centers has been studied; for example, ‘ToxNet,’ an AI system built with data from over 780,000 poison control center records, has been used to assist physicians in predictive analysis of patient symptoms and simulated clinical scenarios [33]. A major challenge in leveraging AI for toxicology research in Africa is the lack of large, high-quality datasets. Data analysis and ML can detect toxic exposure patterns beyond human ability by analyzing past poisoning cases, including location, timing, and clinical features, to predict contamination risks such as cholera toxins [34].

Combining social media with other approaches has been useful in monitoring X, Facebook, and Google for trends related to infectious diseases. It is argued that this information could help in predicting outbreaks earlier [14]. Owing to the inefficiencies of official reporting in Africa, AI-powered crowd-sourced surveillance could be extremely useful for detecting toxins, for example, monitoring animal death reports (which may indicate environmental poisoning). AI algorithms especially focus on predictive risk modeling for more sensitive populations. Predictive factors related to both intentional and unintentional crude poisoning have demonstrated the ability of AI to predict at-risk populations, demonstrating the effectiveness of ML algorithms [35].

Building regional and global partnerships to advance AI in toxicology

AI model development in toxicology requires collaborative teams to solve research problems. Notably, the AI African Consortium’s partnership with the University of the Witwatersrand is advancing AI-driven toxicology research across the continent [36,37].

Barriers to AI integration and adoption in Africa

Technological and infrastructure challenges

Despite its promise, integrating AI into African healthcare, including toxicology, faces significant technological hurdles. A foremost challenge is inadequate infrastructure, such as reliable electricity, internet connectivity, and digital hardware, which is foundational for any AI system to function [38]. Many African regions have limited internet penetration (on average, approximately 39 % of the population), and even within hospitals, bandwidth may be low or intermittent [15]. AI tools that rely on cloud computing or real-time data streaming can be hampered by such connectivity issues. Similarly, frequent power outages in some countries make it difficult to deploy the continuous monitoring systems or servers needed for AI algorithms [15,38].

A survey of African healthcare settings revealed that “digital health infrastructure is inadequate”, meaning that even basic methods such as electronic medical record (EMR) systems are not widely implemented [15]. Indeed, the low uptake of EMRs across Africa results in a paucity of digitized clinical data, which limits not only AI training datasets but also the ability to integrate AI seamlessly into workflows. Thus, the groundwork of digitization is a prerequisite for AI investments in hospital IT systems, data collection processes, and networking [38].

Another barrier is the shortage of computational infrastructure and expertise. Advanced AI often requires high-performance computing (for model training) and data storage solutions. These resources may be scarce in African research institutions or health ministries. As a result, AI development can be slow or dependent on external partners [18]. Furthermore, maintaining AI systems requires IT support, which many hospitals lack. There is also a human capacity challenge. A related issue is the acceptance and adoption of technology: if clinicians are not involved early and trained, they may be reluctant to rely on AI outputs, especially if the technology is seen as a “black box.”

The cost is another infrastructural barrier. While AI software itself can be inexpensive, the supporting infrastructure (computers, stable power, internet, maintenance) entails upfront and ongoing expenses. Without clear proof of cost-effectiveness (for example, an AI toxicology system reducing hospital stays or saving antidote costs), administrators may be hesitant to invest [39].

Data and ethical challenges

In addition to hardware, there are data and ethical issues with AI adoption in toxicology that must be addressed carefully. Data scarcity and fragmentation are two of the largest obstacles. High-quality labeled data constitute the lifeblood of AI models, but in Africa, toxicology data tend to be limited. Poisoning cases may be documented inconsistently across hospitals, and there is no unified database in many countries [40]. It is only a handful of nations that have poison centers that gather standardized data and even those that might fail to exchange information at the national level. This dispersion makes it difficult for AI developers to gather enough representative cases to train stable models [4]. Moreover, existing datasets may underreport informative variables (e.g., accurate toxin dose, treatment timing) or be missing outcome data, affecting model accuracy. Harmonization of data collection, for example, the creation of a minimum dataset for poisoning cases in Africa, should be performed so that when AI begins operation, it is built with a firm, comprehensive information base [4,41].

Closely related to the above is the issue of data privacy and security. Healthcare data are sensitive, and digitalizing toxicology records or linking databases for AI analysis raises concerns about patient confidentiality. A majority of African countries do not have defined laws for safeguarding health information [42]. Public concerns about data misuse and privacy require strict adherence to ethical standards, including informed consent, deidentification, strong cybersecurity, and community trust to ensure participation and data integrity [42].

Ethical accountability is another barrier. The lack of clear guidelines or rules for this purpose of AI accountability in medical practice is a global issue, but particularly in Africa, where legal systems may not yet address this scenario [14,43]. Studies of healthcare workers in various countries find that trust improves if AI is seen to consistently improve outcomes and if users are educated about its functioning [44,45].

Opportunities for AI in African healthcare

AI-assisted telemedicine and remote healthcare

One of the most immediate opportunities to apply AI in African health care, including toxicology, is through telemedicine and remote consultation services. Given the uneven distribution of healthcare professionals, with rural and remote areas often lacking specialists, AI-powered telemedicine can bridge this gap by delivering expert guidance virtually [14]. For example, an AI-driven telemedicine platform might allow the provider to input a patient’s symptoms and history, upload a photo of a plant or snake involved, and then receive immediate decision support on what toxin is likely and what first aid or antidote to administer [14,46]. Beyond acute toxicology consults, AI enhances remote patient monitoring (RPM). Patients who have been treated for poisoning (e.g., discharged after organophosphate exposure) could be monitored at home with wearable sensors that measure vital signs or neurologic status. AI algorithms on the RPM device can analyze these real-time data and alert a central clinic if signs of deterioration occur (for instance, recurrence of cholinergic symptoms), prompting an immediate teleconsultation or evacuation [11,15].

Additionally, AI can assist remote healthcare through language translation and triage. Africa’s linguistic diversity often complicates healthcare delivery; AI-based translation could allow, for example, a French-speaking toxicologist on the Ivory Coast to consult on a case in rural Ghana by translating patient information and recommendations in real time [14]. AI chatbots trained on toxicology knowledge could also interact directly with patients or laypersons who seek advice (for example, a farmer calling a hotline after pesticide exposure could be met with an AI chatbot that gathers critical information and provides initial guidance in the local language while notifying a human provider) [14,15].

The benefits of AI-assisted telemedicine are well aligned with African healthcare needs. It enhances access to care for underserved populations and reduces the burden on infrastructure by minimizing unnecessary referrals and in-person visits. Economically, treating more cases at the local level with remote supervision can be cost-saving, thereby reducing transportation and centralized hospital costs [15,46]. Tele-toxicology networks can also serve educational purposes, as local providers learn from AI suggestions and remote specialist input, building capacity over time. However, it is important to address challenges such as ensuring connectivity and training users to trust and properly utilize AI systems [14].

AI and public health interventions in toxicology

In addition to individual patient care, AI can play a pivotal role in public health strategies to prevent and manage toxic exposures at the population level in Africa (Fig. 2). One key opportunity is the use of AI to design and deploy targeted prevention and awareness campaigns. Public health authorities can use ML to identify the highest risk groups or locations of greatest risk for certain poisonings and thus target education programs accordingly. For example, if data analysis reveals that teenage girls in a particular region have a rising incidence of suicide attempts with pesticides, an AI model could flag this trend [4]. Health officials could then rapidly mount a focused awareness campaign in that region (through school programs, radio broadcasts, and social media) about the dangers of pesticide self-poisoning and promote mental health resources [14,47]. AI can support toxicovigilance in Africa by using NLP to scan import records, online marketplaces, and forums for toxic products, whereas visual recognition tools help customs identify mislabeled hazardous chemicals. Additionally, AI-driven chatbots and ML can personalize poisoning prevention education, evaluate public health interventions, and assess policy impacts, such as pesticide bans, to guide future regulations.

Conclusion and future directions

AI has the potential to revolutionize toxicology in Africa, enhancing diagnosis, treatment, and research. AI-driven tools can rapidly identify toxic exposures, allowing timely interventions. Precision toxicology ensures optimal dosing and treatment prioritization, whereas AI-driven surveillance bridges knowledge gaps, predicts poisoning outbreaks and optimizes public health responses [14,48]. It improves toxicology expertise, provides advanced decision support to remote clinics and addresses specialist shortages. African healthcare systems can leverage AI to improve the efficiency and equity of toxicology services. Success stories demonstrate Africa’s potential to become a hub for medical AI innovation rather than just a consumer [14,27,49].

To realize AI’s potential, investments in digital infrastructure, training, and policy frameworks are essential. Expanding electrification, broadband access, and affordable computing in rural clinics is foundational. Capacity building through AI training for healthcare workers and the inclusion of AI modules in medical education will ensure practical adoption. Clinician involvement in AI tool development enhances usability and trust. Robust toxicology data registries are crucial for training AI models suited to African populations. Establishing national and regional data-sharing agreements can bolster AI-driven toxicology research. Policy development must keep pace with AI advancements, ensuring regulatory oversight, data security, and equitable use. Public engagement is key to fostering trust and ethical AI deployment. Research funding should prioritize AI toxicology initiatives, supporting locally led innovations. Cross-border collaborations can accelerate knowledge sharing and the adaptation of AI solutions to diverse contexts. Proven AI interventions should be integrated into national health protocols, ensuring scalability and sustainability through cost-effective, open-source models. Continuous monitoring and evaluation refine AI applications, maximizing their impact. By addressing these barriers, AI-driven toxicology can enhance patient outcomes, strengthen healthcare resilience, and drive innovative solutions for poisoning emergencies in Africa. With strategic investment and collaboration, the next decade could witness a transformative shift, positioning AI at the forefront of toxicological care and public health improvement across the continent. With targeted investments in infrastructure, data generation, and workforce training, AI could gradually complement clinical toxicology services in Africa over the coming decade.

Dissemination of results

The findings of this narrative review will be disseminated through multiple academic and professional channels to maximize their reach and impact. Primarily, the review is submitted for publication in the African Journal of Emergency Medicine to engage regional healthcare practitioners, policymakers, and researchers. In addition, we plan to present key insights at relevant conferences focused on toxicology, emergency medicine, digital health, and artificial intelligence in low- and middle-income countries. Executive summaries and infographics will be shared with national toxicology centers, public health agencies, and academic institutions across Africa. We also aim to engage stakeholders via digital platforms, including institutional websites and social media, to promote awareness and cross-sector dialogue. The ultimate goal is to inform and catalyze policy development, capacity building, and future research initiatives that integrate AI into clinical toxicology practice throughout the continent.
