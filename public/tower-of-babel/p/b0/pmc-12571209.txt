Miniature OLEDs Driven by Memristive Switching Electrodes for Compact Display Configuration
Authors: Nahyun Kim, Kiran A Nirmal, Ho Jin Lee, Tae Geun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMemristors have emerged as promising next‐generation electronic components with significant potential for applications in nonvolatile memory, integrated circuits, and neuromorphic computing. Recent advancements in 2D materials have further enhanced memristor performance, offering high on/off ratios, low set/reset voltages, strong data retention, excellent durability, and fast switching speeds. Despite their versatility, the integration of memristors into display technologies remains relatively unexplored. This study investigates memristors incorporating a transition metal dichalcogenide layer, focusing on their switching mechanisms and potential use as electrodes in organic light‐emitting diode (OLED) devices. By harnessing the resistive switching phenomenon, the proposed system enables compact, individually addressable OLED pixels without the need for additional switching components. This innovative approach broadens the application scope of memristors in display technology and offers valuable insights into the development of efficient, compact, and high‐performance memristor‐based display systems.Keywords: driving display, memristor, organic light‐emitting diodes, resistive switching, transition‐metal dichalcogenide

Introduction

As the demand for high‐resolution displays in emerging applications such as head‐up displays and AR/VR systems continues to rise, the importance of technologies enabling mini‐ and micro‐sized pixel implementation has grown significantly. Additionally, integrated optoelectronic components that combine display and computing functionalities are attracting increasing attention due to the diverse requirements of stakeholders in the consumer electronics market.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Organic light‐emitting diodes (OLEDs) are widely used as light sources in popular display devices, including TVs, monitors, and smartphones, owing to their high brightness, low power consumption, and ultrathin form factor.[ 4 , 5 ] Moreover, their simple fabrication process and outstanding flexibility make OLEDs strong candidates for next‐generation displays, such as high‐resolution and stretchable systems.[ 6 ] OLEDs are typically categorized as bottom‐emission OLEDs (BEOLEDs) or top‐emission OLEDs (TEOLEDs), depending on the direction of light emission. The TEOLED architecture, with its near‐100% aperture ratio and excellent stability against external stressors such as light and heat, offers significant advantages over BEOLEDs.[ 7 , 8 ] TEOLEDs are also highly compatible with nontransparent backplane technologies and support a wide range of fabrication processes.[ 9 ] As a result, extensive research has focused on various TEOLED components, particularly the top and bottom electrodes, which are critical to overall device performance.[ 10 , 11 ] Innovative electrode designs such as capping layers,[ 12 ] wrinkled‐structured electrodes,[ 13 ] oxide/metal/oxide (OMO) stacks,[ 14 ] and omnidirectional reflectors[ 15 ] have significantly improved optoelectronic characteristics, including external quantum efficiency (EQE) and viewing angle. However, most of these efforts have concentrated on enhancing optoelectrical properties such as transmittance, reflectance, and conductivity.

To expand the potential applications of TEOLEDs in future high‐density, multifunctional display technologies, it is crucial to develop electrodes that provide functionalities beyond conventional optoelectronic performance. Specifically, electrodes with switching capability, computing potential, and mechanical flexibility are required to meet next‐generation demands. A promising approach involves top‐emission organic light‐emitting transistors (TEOLETs), which vertically stack organic field‐effect transistors (OFETs) with TEOLEDs. This architecture has demonstrated significant potential for high‐resolution, compact, and monolithically integrated optoelectronic systems.[ 16 , 17 ] TEOLETs achieve compact integration by using the TEOLED's bottom electrode as the transistor's gate electrode, thereby reducing the footprint of thin‐film transistors (TFTs). However, challenges persist, including the trade‐off between carrier mobility and luminescence efficiency inherent to the organic materials used in OLEDs,[ 18 , 19 ] as well as aperture ratio limitations caused by the source and drain electrodes. In this context, light‐emitting memristors (LEMs), which combine resistive switching (RS) and light emission in a single mixed‐phase active layer, have gained attention as an alternative route toward high‐density integration.[ 20 , 21 ] These devices typically employ perovskites, quantum dots, or mixed ionic–electronic conductors, requiring precise control of ion migration,[ 22 ] charge transport,[ 23 , 24 ] and exciton recombination.[ 25 , 26 ] Despite their promise, LEMs face critical challenges arising from the strong coupling of structural and functional properties. These include poor reproducibility, nonuniform spatial emission, limited operational stability, and difficulties in large‐area fabrication. Thus, while conceptually appealing, their practical implementation remains hindered by intrinsic material instabilities and complex processing requirements.

In our previous work, we implemented yttrium‐iron‐garnet (YIG)‐based RS electrodes in micro‐LED arrays and demonstrated a crosstalk‐free, passive‐matrix architecture.[ 27 ] The RS electrodes were connected in series with micro‐LEDs, enabling control based on their resistance state. While these electrodes showed excellent switching performance, including high on/off ratios, strong retention, and good endurance, they required a 150°C annealing process and a platinum adhesion layer, limiting their flexibility and scalability for cost‐effective applications. Therefore, a key challenge remains: the development of multifunctional electrodes that are not only easy to fabricate and flexible but also capable of switching while maintaining their optoelectronic properties. To overcome these challenges, in this study, we present a novel molybdenum disulfide (MoS2)‐based memristive RS (MemRS) reflective electrode, specifically Ag/MoS2/Ag that simultaneously functions as a switching device, reflective electrode, and synaptic element. The MemRS electrode is fabricated using a radiofrequency (RF) sputtering method without any annealing step, offering a simple and cost‐effective manufacturing process. When employed as a switching device in TEOLEDs, the MemRS electrode provides distinct high‐resistance states (HRS) and low‐resistance states (LRS), effectively controlling current flow into the OLEDs. The device demonstrates excellent switching characteristics, including a high on/off ratio (≈10⁴), low operation voltage (3.7 V), strong data retention (≈10⁵ s), and endurance (≈10⁴ cycles). Additionally, the high optical reflectance (92.5%–93.8%) of the MemRS electrode enhances optoelectronic performance by improving light extraction and color purity. Its high electrical conductivity also contributes to reduced driving voltage and lower power consumption, thereby extending device lifetime. Furthermore, the MemRS electrode exhibits reliable memristive switching behavior suitable for emulating biological synaptic functions, expanding its application potential beyond conventional display use. We believe this innovative integration of switching, reflective, and synaptic functionalities into a single device marks a significant step forward in the design of multifunctional electrodes for next‐generation high‐resolution, compact, and energy‐efficient display technologies.

Results and Discussion

The key performance metrics of the memristor, including its RS characteristics, synaptic plasticity, and optoelectronic performance (Figure 1a), are highly dependent on the choice of resistive materials and the device architecture. In this configuration, the memristor is connected in series with the OLED and functions as the anode electrode. While the overall structure resembles that of conventional OLED devices, the integration of the memristor enables selective control of the OLED's on/off state through its RS behavior. To ensure its effectiveness as an OLED electrode, the memristor's performance was stabilized, allowing for precise control of the current flowing into the OLED. The switching functionality of the memristor arises from the formation and dissolution of conductive filaments (CFs), which is a key characteristic of its RS mechanism. The proposed OLED demonstrates significant potential for integration into driving circuits. Due to its inherent memory characteristics, the CFs formed within the memristor remain stable unless deliberately reset. This nonvolatile behavior suggests that memristor‐based electrodes could potentially replace capacitors in certain applications.[ 27 ] While this study primarily focused on the individual on/off switching of the OLED device, the unique RS and memory properties of the memristor electrode indicate its suitability for active‐matrix (AM) display structures. In conventional AM architectures, each driving pixel unit typically comprises two thin‐film transistors (TFTs) and one capacitor (2T‐1C), as illustrated inFigure S2 (Supporting Information). In contrast, the proposed memristor‐based OLED employs the memristor as the anode, eliminating the need for a dedicated capacitor and simplifying the pixel circuit to a single driving transistor (d‐TFT). This innovative configuration not only reduces the complexity of the driving circuitry but also enables higher integration density in AM driving systems. The simplified design offers enhanced potential for developing compact, power‐efficient, and scalable display technologies, highlighting the transformative role of memristor‐based electrodes in the next generation of display systems.

Figure 2a illustrates the structure of the MoS2‐based memristive electrode (MME) in a crossbar array configuration composed of Ag/MoS2/Ag, along with Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X‐ray (EDX) mapping results for each layer. Both the MoS2 and Ag layers were successfully deposited onto a glass substrate using a straightforward RF sputtering process. The MoS2 film was deposited using RF magnetron sputtering with a 99.79% pure MoS2 target. The entire MME was fabricated without the need for additional complex processing steps. Figure 2b presents the Raman spectra of the MoS2 thin films, showing characteristic peaks corresponding to the E1 2g and A1g active vibrational modes at 381.6 and 408.2 cm−1, respectively. These values are in close agreement with those reported in previous studies.[ 28 , 29 ] The E1 2g ​ mode originates from in‐plane vibrations of Mo and S atoms, while the A1g​ mode corresponds to out‐of‐plane vibrations of S atoms.[ 30 ] Raman spectroscopy serves as an effective tool for identifying the number of MoS2 layers.[ 31 , 32 ] Compared to the E1 2g peak and A1g peaks of the single‐layer MoS2 material,[ 33 ] the E1 2g peak shifted to a higher frequency, while the A1g peak shifted to a lower frequency. The observed frequency difference (D = 26.6) aligned with the predictions for the multilayer MoS2 structure, confirming that the prepared MoS2 films were multilayered. The multilayer structure provides significantly enhanced electron mobility and higher current‐carrying capacity, which in turn enables efficient charge injection into the OLED.[ 34 ] Additionally, the increased thickness contributes to improved switching reliability and endurance. Multilayer MoS2 also allows for tunable resistance states and better structural tolerance during the formation and rupture of CFs, key factors for achieving stable memristive switching.[ 35 ] These advantages collectively support the suitability of multilayer MoS2 as a robust, scalable active material for high‐efficiency, nonvolatile memristor‐driven OLED arrays.

a) Cross‐section image and TEM‐EDS mapping of MoS2‐based memristor fabricated using RF sputtering, highlighting the layer structure and elemental distribution. b) Raman spectrum of the thin‐layer MoS2 (left) highlighting the E1 2g and A1g bands. XPS spectrum of c) Mo 3d and d) S 2p signals.

To further investigate the chemical states and composition of Mo and S in MoS2, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed. Figure 2c,d presents the high‐resolution XPS spectra for the Mo 3d and S 2p regions, respectively. The binding energy peaks at 232.55 and 229.17 eV correspond to the Mo 3d3/2 and Mo 3d5/2 states of Mo4+ in MoS2, while a peak at 226.68 eV is assigned to the S 2s core level, consistent with previous reports.[ 36 ] Additional peaks associated with Mo4+ and possible MoxSγ species indicate partial oxidation or reduction processes. In the S 2p region, peaks at 162.23 and 163.33 eV are attributed to the S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2 states of divalent sulfide ions (S2−). The Mo‐to‐S atomic ratio was determined to be 1:1.13, revealing the presence of sulfur vacancies (SVs). These SVs are known to enhance the switching performance of memristive devices by enabling multilevel resistive states through interactions with mobile metal ions.[ 37 , 38 ] Accordingly, the RS behavior in our device is most plausibly governed by the migration of metal ions from the active electrode, assisted by SV‐mediated transport and trapping. Crucially, SVs do not act merely as passive structural defects; they serve as active sites that facilitate metal‐ion injection, migration, and anchoring, thereby directly contributing to the formation and rupture of CFs.[ 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 ] To validate this mechanism, we examined the interplay between Ag ion migration and SV dynamics. The RS behavior is attributed to the reversible formation and disruption of Ag‐based CFs, mediated by SVs, as schematically illustrated in Figure S3a (Supporting Information). Conductive atomic force microscopy (C‐AFM) further supports this mechanism: well‐defined conductive regions appear in the LRS, whereas negligible current is detected in the HRS, confirming a filamentary switching model based on localized conduction pathways associated with SVs (Figure S3b, Supporting Information). The role of SVs in the RS process was further examined by XPS analysis of the S 2p core level before and after electrical biasing. The as‐deposited MoS2 films initially exhibited a Mo:S atomic ratio of ≈1:1.13, indicating intrinsic sulfur deficiencies. After electroforming and repeated switching cycles, the S 2p3/2 (≈162.2 eV) and S 2p1/2 (≈163.4 eV) peaks showed a marked decrease in intensity (Figure S3c, Supporting Information), consistent with a reduction in sulfur content. This change likely arises from electric‐field‐induced sulfur migration or Joule heating, which generates or enhances SVs during device operation. Furthermore, the evolution of the S 2p spectra suggests dynamic changes in the local sulfur environment, possibly due to preferential localization and partial reduction of Ag+ ions at SV sites. The charge imbalance and unsaturated Mo coordination at these sites stabilize Ag+ ions and promote the nucleation of Ag or Ag–S filaments. Overall, these findings confirm that SVs act as chemically active sites in the formation and rupture of CFs during RS. They are thus active participants, rather than passive defects, in the SET and RESET processes, functioning as nucleation and dissolution points for conductive filaments.

In the MME, excellent RS performance is typically characterized by a low switching voltage, fast operation speed, and long retention times. RS behavior is usually evaluated by applying a bias to the silver (Ag) electrode. As illustrated in Figure 3a, the metal–insulator–metal (MIM) structure exhibits both unipolar and bipolar RS behavior, wherein the MoS2 layer transitions between the LRS and HRS under either positive or negative bias.[ 45 , 46 ] In this study, the unipolar switching mode was applied to drive the OLED, as shown in Figure 3b. The electrical properties of the MME, measured under a constant current (CC) of 1 mA, demonstrated stable RS characteristics. Additional data showing consistent RS behavior across a range of CC values (0.01 to 1 mA) are provided inFigure S4a,b (Supporting Information). The multilevel resistance states of the memristor present a significant advantage for controlling the driving current in AM OLEDs. Applying different CC values during the set process allows for the stable formation of conductive filaments, enabling precise current injection.

The ability to modulate OLED brightness through precise control of the driving current, enabled by multiple well‐defined resistance states (LRS 1 to LRS 3) in the memristor, is crucial for applications such as analog grayscale control and advanced pixel‐driving schemes in high‐resolution display systems. As shown in Figure S5a (Supporting Information), the memristor exhibits stable multilevel low‐resistance states (LRS 1, LRS 2, and LRS 3) when programmed under increasing compliance currents of 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mA, respectively. These discrete resistance levels allow accurate and reproducible analog modulation of the driving current injected into the OLED. At a fixed driving voltage of 8 V, the OLED current (mA), luminance (cd m−2), and power efficiency (lm W−1) were measured for each resistance state. The results show a monotonic increase in luminance with decreasing resistance, confirming a direct and controllable correlation between the memristor state and OLED brightness. These findings validate the feasibility of analog grayscale modulation using multilevel memristor states. The power efficiency, ranging from 0.4 to 0.6 lm W−1, is reasonable considering the small emission area (800 µm × 800 µm) and demonstrates that brightness control is achieved without significant energy loss. Furthermore, the EL spectra (Figure S5b, Supporting Information) exhibit a consistent peak emission at ≈463 nm across all LRS states, indicating that brightness modulation is purely current‐driven and does not cause spectral shifts. The gradual increase in EL intensity from LRS 1 to LRS 3 further supports this conclusion. A corresponding photoemission image (Figure S5c, Supporting Information) visually confirms the luminance variation across resistance states, with no emission observed in the HRS and progressively brighter emission from LRS 1 to LRS 3. Collectively, these results provide direct experimental evidence of analog multi‐bit control through memristor‐driven current modulation, an essential functionality for neuromorphic vision systems and next‐generation grayscale display applications.

The I–V curves in Figure 3b exhibit threshold‐type volatile RS behavior. Specifically, the MME switches abruptly to the LRS at a threshold voltage of 1.83 V, with the current rising sharply from ≈10 pA to the CC limit—indicating a transition from HRS (OFF) to LRS (ON). When the voltage is reduced, the device reverts to the off state near 0.6 V. This low threshold voltage is attributed to the enhanced migration of Ag ions, which is facilitated by abundant defect and trap states within the MoS2 layer. These localized states lower the energy barrier for filament formation, enabling RS at reduced operating voltages. As a result, the MME achieves high selectivity (> 10⁴) and a low off‐current (< 10−⁷ A), effectively minimizing leakage current in the cross‐point architecture. The device shows average threshold voltages of 0.57 and 1.9 V, with respective fluctuations (ΔV) of 0.26 and 0.33 V (Figure 3c). To further evaluate the transient response characteristics essential for display applications, voltage‐pulse switching measurements were performed. The MME exhibited a fast SET/RESET response, with a rise and fall time of 200 ns (Figure S6, Supporting Information), meeting the temporal resolution requirements for displays operating at refresh rates above 60 Hz. These results confirm the suitability of the MME for video‐rate driving conditions.

Device reliability was further assessed through retention and endurance tests (Figure 3d,e). The MME demonstrated outstanding endurance over ≈10⁴ switching cycles and data retention exceeding 10⁵ s, maintaining an on/off ratio of ≈10⁴. The cumulative probability distributions of set and reset voltages and currents (Figure S7a,b, Supporting Information) indicate consistent switching behavior with minimal operational variability. The HRS and LRS states exhibited excellent uniformity (Figure S7a, Supporting Information), while the cumulative threshold voltage plot confirmed the repeatability of both VSET ​ and VRESET (Figure S7b, Supporting Information). To mitigate potential risks of filament hardening or incomplete rupture, which are inherent in unipolar switching, a compliance‐limited SET and pulsed RESET scheme was employed. This approach ensured stable ON/OFF operation and effectively suppressed parasitic leakage, even under prolonged cycling stress (Figure S8, Supporting Information). In addition to assessing electrical reliability, we investigated the thermal stability of the MME during switching operations. Temperature‐dependent measurements (25 to 100 °C) revealed that, although elevated temperatures induced slight variations in switching characteristics and electroluminescence performance, these changes remained within acceptable limits. Thus, the integrated system maintains robust operation up to 100 °C without significant degradation in OLED emission properties. Detailed experimental procedures and additional results are provided in Figure S9 (Supporting Information).

Compared with previously reported MoS2‐based RRAM devices (Table S1, Supporting Information), the MME structure demonstrates significantly enhanced performance. These improvements are attributed to the optimized Ag/MoS2/Ag architecture and the high defect density in the MoS2 layer, which facilitates the stable and repeatable formation and dissolution of conductive filaments. Furthermore, the top Ag electrode in the MME serving as the anode induces a microcavity effect by optimizing the optical cavity length between the semi‐transparent Ag cathodes. Reflectance spectra (Figure 3f) show that the MME anode enhances luminance and contributes to a higher EQE. Notably, the MME also exhibits stable synaptic behavior characteristic of memristors, with additional details provided in Figures S10 and S11 (Supporting Information). Collectively, these results confirm that the MME successfully integrates three critical characteristics of memristive devices: reliable RS functionality, stable synaptic plasticity, and enhanced optical performance. These multifunctional properties position the MME as a promising component for next‐generation optoelectronic applications. To validate the stable operation and performance of the MME in display applications, an OLED device was fabricated with the MME serving as a key component. The single MoS2‐based OLEDs exhibited a turn‐on voltage of 3.5 V (at 1 cd m−2), and the luminance increased linearly with applied voltage, reaching ≥ 1200 cd m−2 at 10 V, confirming stable electroluminescent behavior. The performance of the OLED pixel array was further compared with that of single‐pixel devices using both MME and Ag electrodes (Figure S13, Supporting Information). The MME‐based single device achieved a maximum EQE of 12.45%, compared to 11.02% for the Ag‐based reference, corresponding to a relative enhancement of ≈12.9%. In addition, the MME‐based OLED demonstrated higher current efficiency (11.64 cd A−1) and power efficiency (10.5 lm W−1) than the Ag‐based device (9.93 cd A−1 and 8.72 lm W−1, respectively) under identical driving conditions. Similar performance improvements were consistently observed in pixel‐array operation, confirming the robustness and scalability of the MME structure. The MME‐based array OLED exhibited a maximum current efficiency of 10.44 cd A−1 and a power efficiency of 7.91 lm W−1, while the Ag‐based reference array showed slightly lower values of 9.56 cd A−1 and 7.80 lm W−1, respectively. These results collectively demonstrate the enhanced charge injection and light‐extraction capabilities of the MME architecture. A summary of the performance comparison between single‐pixel and array devices is provided in Table S2 (Supporting Information). Table S3 (Supporting Information) also shows a comparison of key performance parameters of the proposed MME with previously reported electrodes (e.g., Ag) used in blue top‐emitting TADF‐OLEDs.

Minor variations in the performance of the OLED pixel array compared to single devices were attributed to leakage currents and differences in the effective light‐emitting area due to wiring. Notably, gradual increases in the Vdd (4–9 V) during the set state resulted in distinguishable changes in emission intensity (Figure S14, Supporting Information). At Vdd = 9 V, the maximum luminance reached 936.95 cd m− 2, sufficient for practical display applications, demonstrating the MME's capability to stably drive OLED emission. Importantly, the RS characteristics of the MME enabled self‐controlled on/off switching of the OLED without requiring additional transistors, ensuring stable current injections. These features underscore the potential of MME‐driven OLEDs for integration into crossbar matrix structures, including structured lighting panels and AM displays. To evaluate the operational stability, we investigated the impact of RS cycling on OLED emission characteristics (Figure 4g–h). As summarized in Table S4 (Supporting Information), key optoelectronic parameters, including maximum current efficiency, power efficiency, EQE, and luminance, exhibited negligible degradation with increasing switching cycles. Notably, the maximum luminance and EQE decreased only slightly, by ≈3.1% and 2.2%, respectively, demonstrating excellent operational stability under prolonged switching. These variations fall well within the acceptable range for practical applications. EL spectral measurements further confirmed device stability, showing minimal variation, with peak emission wavelength shifts of less than 1 nm and FWHM changes within 2%. This indicates that neither the emissive layer nor the interfacial regions experienced significant structural or electronic degradation during extended cycling. To further probe device integrity, cross‐sectional TEM and AFM analyses were performed before and after endurance testing. As shown in Figure S15 (Supporting Information), no apparent interfacial damage or layer interdiffusion was observed between the Ag‐based multilayer metal electrode and the organic emissive stack (MoO3/mCP≈). AFM measurements also confirmed stable surface morphology, with Ra variations remaining within ± 0.02 nm. Moreover, cross‐sectional TEM–EDS mapping conducted before and after repeated RS operation revealed no detectable Ag signal within the organic emissive layers, including MoO3, mCP, and the emissive region. This strongly suggests that metal ion diffusion and interfacial intermixing did not occur during cycling. These results highlight the critical role of the MoO3 and mCP interfacial layers in mitigating both chemical and physical degradation pathways while maintaining robust charge injection. Consequently, this interface engineering strategy effectively preserves OLED optoelectronic performance during prolonged RS operation. To systematically assess the mechanical robustness of the proposed device architecture, comprehensive static and cyclic bending tests were conducted on flexible polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) substrates. Detailed methodologies and quantitative results are presented in Figures S16 and S17 (Supporting Information). These evaluations confirm that the Ag/MoS2/Ag/OLED integrated structure maintains excellent electrical and electroluminescent stability under repeated mechanical deformation, thereby demonstrating strong potential for flexible electronic applications.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the electrical, synaptic, and RS characteristics of a memristor and utilized the fabricated memristor as the anode electrode, referred to as the MME, in an OLED device. This approach represents a structural innovation for developing multifunctional OLEDs capable of self‐driven on/off operation. We proposed a novel concept for memristor‐integrated OLEDs, demonstrating an array configuration in which individual OLED pixels can be selectively controlled without the need for additional switching components. This design culminates in a highly stable and reliable OLED display architecture.

The MME exhibited nonpolar RS characteristics, and the OLED's on/off functionality was achieved through the MME's unipolar switching behavior within the array. This novel integration of a memristor as the OLED anode presents a promising strategy for enhancing device reliability while increasing the integration density of driving circuits. The demonstrated MME not only enabled independent on/off control at the pixel level but also showed potential as a replacement for conventional d‐TFTs and capacitors, thereby paving the way for AM driving displays. This work addresses the longstanding challenge of circuit complexity in OLED display systems and introduces a simple, cost‐effective, and practical structural solution. The proposed approach offers a compelling route toward the fabrication of multifunctional OLEDs, marking a significant advancement in driving circuit design for next‐generation display technologies.

Experimental Section

Fabrication of MoS2‐Based Memristive Electrode (MME)

The memristive electrode was fabricated using a straightforward photolithography process. Glass substrates (2.5 × 2.5 cm2) were sequentially cleaned ultrasonically for 10 min each in acetone, chloroform, 2‐propanol, and deionized water, followed by nitrogen gas drying. A positive photoresist (AZ GXR‐601) was spin‐coated onto the sanitized substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s to define a photolithographic cross‐point pattern with a line width of 15 µm.

A 100 nm silver (Ag) layer and a 50 nm MoS2 layer were then deposited onto the patterned glass substrate using RF magnetron sputtering (KVS‐2000L, Korea Vacuum Tech) at deposition rates of 0.84 and 0.03 nm s−1, respectively. The base pressure was maintained below 2.0 × 10−⁶ Torr, while the working pressure during deposition was set to 5 mTorr for Ag and 2 mTorr for MoS2, with a 20 sccm argon (Ar) gas flow. The sputtering power was configured at 100 W for the Ag layer and 20 W for the MoS2 layer. After deposition, the photoresist was removed using acetone, resulting in a mesh‐structured electrode. The switching layer and top electrode (TE) were subsequently aligned orthogonally at a 90° angle to form the final cross‐point array structure.

OLED Fabrication

Prior to OLED fabrication, the patterned MME and Ag‐coated glass substrates were cleaned using the same ultrasonic procedure described previously. Material deposition was carried out via thermal evaporation in a high‐vacuum chamber (base pressure: 10−⁷–10−⁸ mbar; Daedong High Tech, Republic of Korea). The hole injection layer (HIL), molybdenum oxide (MoO3) HIL, 1,3–bis(N–carbazolyl) benzene (mCP) HTL, 10,10–(4,4–sulfonylbis (4,1–phenylene))bis(9,9–dimethyl–9,10–dihydroacridine) (DMAC–DPS) emission layer, and diphenyl–bis[4–(pyridin–3–yl)phenyl]silane (DPPS) ETL were evaporated. The electron‐injection layers LiF and Al were deposited at a rate of 0.5 nm s−1. A 20 nm thick Ag layer was thermally evaporated through a cathode shadow mask at a rate of 1 nm s−1. The OLED emission area was defined as 0.36 mm2 (600 µm × 600 µm) by the overlap between the MME anode and the Ag cathode. OLED characteristics—including current density–voltage–luminance (J–V–L) behavior, electroluminescent (EL) intensity, EQE, current efficiency, and power efficiency—were measured using a source meter (Keithley SMU 2400) and a spectroradiometer (CS‐2000, Konica Minolta) within an OLED I–V–L Test System (M6100, McScience).

Instruments and Measurements

All electrical and optical measurements—including current–voltage (I–V) characteristics—were performed using a linear sweep method on a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keithley 4200 A). For electrical tests, either positive or negative voltage was applied to the TE while the bottom electrode was grounded. The elemental composition of the MoS2 layer was analyzed by XPS using a PHI 5000 VersaProbe (ULVAC‐PHI). Morphological analyses were conducted using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS). Via‐hole structures in the MME and OLED devices were fabricated using focused ion beam (FIB, QUAMTA 200 3D). Cross‐sectional images of the multilayer structures were acquired with a focused ion beam–transmission electron microscope (FIB–TEM; Tecnai G2 F30), operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.
