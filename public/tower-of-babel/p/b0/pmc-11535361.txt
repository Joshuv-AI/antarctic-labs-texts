Case report: Novel approach to distal radius non-union treatment with free fibula flap reconstruction
Authors: Maria Albuquerque, Luís Mata Ribeiro, Miguel Botton
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDistal radius fractures are common among adults. Despite being a rare complication, occurring in only 0.2 % of the cases, non-union poses significant treatment challenges.Presentation of caseIn this article, we report the case of a 43-year-old male with a history of distal radius fracture resulting from a motorcycle accident. His initial treatment consisted of external fixation followed by a distraction plate and internal fixation with volar plate and screws. Due to plate breakage, he underwent refixation with a volar plate and autologous bone graft six months after the accident. However, five months after surgery, CT-scans showed a radius non-union. This led to a multidisciplinary approach involving orthopaedic and plastic surgery teams, where the patient underwent distal radius reconstruction using a free fibula flap. Postoperative recovery led to favourable outcomes with evidence of bony consolidation at six-month follow-up.DiscussionConventional techniques for distal radius non-union treatment may fall short in achieving bony continuity in a non-suitable soft tissue environment. Free vascularised fibular flap has emerged as a primary option for long bone reconstruction, offering advantages such as anatomical compatibility and immediate structural support.ConclusionThis case highlights the efficacy of free fibular flap in addressing complex distal radius non-unions, providing an effective solution when simpler techniques have failed.Keywords: Case report, Free fibula flap, Radius non-union, Upper limb bone defect

Introduction

Distal radius fractures account for approximately 20 % of all the fractures in adults and are the most common upper extremity fracture. Common complications include median nerve neuropathy, extensor pollicis longus or flexor pollicis longus rupture, infection, and malunion [1]. Non-union occurs in an estimated 0.2 % of cases. Risk factors for non-union include low-energy fractures, impaction, metaphyseal fractures, concomitant fractures of the distal ulna shaft, and associated distal radioulnar joint lesions [2].

The treatment of distal radius non-union remains controversial. In more complex cases, where poorly vascularised soft tissue and significant bone defects are present, an osteocutaneous free flap can be a viable option [3]. We present a case of distal radius fracture non-union that persisted despite multiple ineffective interventions and was successfully reconstructed using a free osteocutaneous fibula flap. This flap is a practical reconstructive option due to its bone strength, pedicle length, and capacity for remodelling in long bones [4]. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Case report

A 43-year-old male with a medical history of hypertension and obesity, presented to the Orthopaedics Department with a limited range of motion in his right wrist and fingers. Five months earlier, he had sustained an open distal radius fracture following a motorcycle accident. Initial treatment at another facility involved external fixation, which was converted to an internal distraction plate one month later. The radioulnar wire was removed three weeks post-surgery, and follow-up X-rays revealed satisfactory consolidation with signs of disuse osteopenia. Consequently, four months post-accident, the internal fixator was removed.

Five days after removal, the patient returned for follow-up and exhibited full wrist pronation but lacked flexion, extension, and supination. Finger flexion and extension were also impaired. CT imaging of the wrist and forearm revealed a comminuted, non-aligned, articular fracture of the distal radius with loss of radial height. MRI scans confirmed the integrity of the flexor and extensor tendons. One month later, the patient underwent debridement of the non-union, rigid fixation with a volar plate, bone grafting from the iliac crest, supplementation with synthetic bone filler containing gentamicin, and tenolysis of the flexor and extensor tendons. The immediate postoperative period was uneventful. However, five months post-surgery, CT scans revealed failure of the osteosynthesis material and persistent non-union of the bone (Fig. 1). Due to forearm instability and the goal of achieving definitive reconstruction with anatomical stabilisation and good functional outcomes, the patient was offered bone reconstruction using a free fibula flap. CT angiography of the upper and ipsilateral lower limb showed no significant abnormalities.

Preoperative forearm x-ray. Anteroposterior view (A); lateral view (B).

The procedure was performed under general anaesthesia, with antibiotic prophylaxis and carried out by two surgical teams. The orthopaedic team prepared the forearm recipient site by removing the metalware and performing a wide debridement of the non-viable radius, resulting in a 6 cm bone defect while preserving the radio-carpal joint. The plastic surgery team harvested the free osteocutaneous fibula flap from the right leg (Fig. 2). End-to-end anastomosis was performed to the radial artery, and two venous anastomoses were completed: one to a vena comitans of the radial artery and the other to the cephalic vein (Fig. 3).

Free osteocutaneous fibula flap.

Inset of the flap.

The orthopaedic team then performed osteosynthesis of the radius using a 2.5 mm reconstruction locking plate and screws. Finally, the skin paddle was sutured to the volar aspect of the forearm. Suction drains were placed in the forearm and donor site, and the leg was immobilized with a posterior cast. While no immediate major postoperative complications occurred, the skin paddle experienced epidermolysis, which was managed with serial dressings and healed within six weeks. At six-month, postoperative X-ray and CT scans showed evidence of distal consolidation (Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Discussion

The treatment of distal radius non-union presents significant challenges, often requiring approaches beyond conventional techniques, such as bone transport, external fixation, and plate compression with or without bone graft [6]. These methods may not be sufficient in cases of soft tissue compromise, poor vascularisation, or previous surgical failures [3]. In this case, the patient had undergone multiple procedures, including internal fixation and bone grafting, which failed to achieve union - an outcome commonly reported in the literature [[7], [8], [9]]. This case illustrates the importance of considering alternative, more robust reconstructive options when conventional methods fail.

In this context, the decision to use a free osteocutaneous fibula flap was pivotal due to its distinct advantages. The vascularised fibula flap closely matches the shape and size of forearm bones, facilitating internal fixation, while the donor site morbidity is low, as the fibula is not essential for load bearing or ambulation [10]. Notably, the initial failure in our patient was partly due to the choice of implant; the previously used bone plate was inadequate and led to fatigue fracture. Consequently, a stronger plate was used in the later surgical stage, which contributed to the successful outcome. The flap inherent vascularisation promotes bone hypertrophy, infection resistance, and reduces reliance on surrounding soft tissues for flap incorporation and healing, providing a tailored solution to the defect [2]. Furthermore, it enables tension-free closure in scarred environments that would otherwise be unsuitable for direct closure, while also protecting the osteosynthesis material. This approach has been well-documented in reconstructive surgeries, with high union rates [11]. Our case is consistent with these outcomes, with radiographic evidence of consolidation within six month postoperatively.

Compared to existing literature, this case presents several notable elements. Although significant, the bone defect was on the lower threshold for using a fibular flap, which is typically indicated for defects greater than 6 cm [9]. Despite this, the decision to proceed was based on the poor condition of the surrounding soft tissues and the likelihood of failure with conventional grafting techniques. This case demonstrates that fibula flaps can be considered even for smaller defects when the surrounding biological environment is compromised. While the literature on distal radius non-union often focuses on larger defects, this case contributes by extending the application of fibula flaps to smaller but complex non-unions.

Previous studies have described alternative techniques for non-union treatment, such as the Masquelet technique and corticocancellous bone grafts [9]. Both of these methods were considered here but ultimately rejected. The Masquelet technique was considered less favourable due to the compromised vascularity and integrity of the soft tissue from prior surgeries. Similarly, corticocancellous grafts, which had already been attempted, failed to achieve union, reinforcing the need for a vascularised approach. Free fibular flap provided a reliable solution, even in a poorly vascularised area, and allowed for soft tissue reconstruction following the excision of scarred tissue [9]. This case highlights a strategic shift towards a more reliable, biologically active reconstruction method in high-risk patients.

Other osteocutaneous flap options, such as the iliac crest flap and the scapula flap, were also considered. However, the iliac crest presents challenges due to its curved shape, making it difficult to fit the radius. Its dissection is also more complex, with risk of severe complications such as inguinal hernias or anterior iliac spine fractures [12,13]. The osteocutaneous scapula free flap, while reliable, can impair the range of motion in a healthy upper limb, which would be problematic for a patient with contralateral limb mobility issues. Given these factors, the osteocutaneous fibula free flap emerged as the most suitable option for this complex case, where both soft tissue and bone reconstruction was required.

Patient-specific factors, such as obesity, added further complexity. In free flap procedures, obesity is a recognized risk factor for complications, including infection and flap loss [14]. While these require heightened caution during surgery and close postoperative monitoring, they are not a contraindication. With careful preoperative planning and meticulous surgical technique, free flap reconstruction remains a viable option, emphasising the need for individualised decision-making to optimise outcomes for patients with such comorbidities.

This case provides valuable insights into the decision-making process in complex reconstructions. Its success suggests that free fibula flaps should be considered earlier in the treatment algorithm, particularly in patients with compromised soft tissues or after failed conventional treatments. While corticocancellous bone grafts or the Masquelet technique may suffice for isolated bone defects, cases involving extensive soft tissue compromise may benefit from more aggressive reconstruction with a vascularised fibula flap. Additionally, this case expands the indication for free fibula flaps to defects smaller than 6 cm, challenging the conventional size criteria for non-union cases.

Future research should focus on comparing long-term outcomes of different vascularised flaps for distal radius non-union, as well as exploring patient-reported outcomes related to functional recovery and quality of life. This would contribute to refining the treatment approach for non-unions and further optimise surgical outcomes in patients with complex comorbidities.

Conclusion

The novelty of this case lies in documenting an effective approach to a rare complication when traditional methods had proven inadequate in a patient with specific comorbidities. The success is not only evidenced by the restoration of normal forearm anatomy, but also by the recovery of the function and mobility of the limb.
