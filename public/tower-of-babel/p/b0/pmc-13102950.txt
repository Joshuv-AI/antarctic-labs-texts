Identification of intracellular target of antimicrobial peptide EWAMP-R in bacteria
Authors: Yizhao Wu, Hao Tang, Puguo Hao, Yunxiang Xu, Yifan Zhang, Muyuan Wang, Rui Jiang, Jibao Jiang, Qi Zhao, Yinsheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial peptides (AMPs) exhibit diverse antibacterial mechanisms. To elucidate the intracellular targets of the novel AMP, EWAMP-R, we generate EWAMP-R-resistant strains of Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. In E. coli, a nonsynonymous mutation in the clpX gene (1108 C > T) is identified, resulting in an amino acid change in ClpX (R370C). This mutation confers enhanced tolerance to EWAMP-R and induces a “hyperflagellate phenotype” in EWAMP-R-resistant E. coli, as verified by CRISPR/Cas9-generated clpX (1108 C > T) mutants. Further studies reveal that EWAMP-R can bind to the Walker A motif and inhibit the conformational changes of ClpX, suppressing its ATPase activity and the degradation of its substrates, such as LexA 1-84. The accumulation of LexA 1-84 downregulates the SOS response, inducing apoptosis-like death in E. coli. In contrast, EWAMP-R-resistant S. aureus exhibits only a metabolic slowdown. These findings establish ClpX as a novel target for AMPs and highlight EWAMP-R as a promising antibacterial agent.Subject terms: Antimicrobials, Proteases

Introduction

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), ancient effector molecules of innate immunity, can serve as a first line of defense in eukaryotes against pathogenic invasion1. Beyond their role in multicellular hosts, prokaryotes themselves can also produce AMPs to competitively inhibit neighboring microbes and secure ecological niches2. The therapeutic potential of AMPs lies in their structural diversity, rapid bactericidal activity, and multifaceted mechanisms of action3. While membrane damage via amphiphilic nature and positive charges remains a hallmark of AMP activity4, emerging evidence highlights their capacity to penetrate bacterial cells through energy-independent permeation or energy-dependent transporters5,6. Once intracellular, AMPs can interfere with critical macromolecules by binding DNA to block replication, sequestering ribosomes to halt translation, or inactivating enzymes to disrupt metabolic pathways7. Notably, certain AMPs can exhibit non-lytic bactericidal effects, relying solely on intracellular target engagement rather than membrane damage8. This dual-action paradigm—combining membrane destabilization with intracellular sabotage—could impose high metabolic costs on bacteria, as developing resistance would require simultaneous adaptations to counter both mechanisms6,9. Thus, AMPs that target both the membrane and intracellular components can represent a promising strategy to circumvent antibiotic resistance.

A hallmark of AMP-bacteria interactions is the induction of apoptosis-like death (ALD), a programmed stress response characterized by reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction, DNA fragmentation, and membrane depolarization10,11. ALD activation may occur either coincidentally with early membrane damage12 or through direct AMP engagement with intracellular targets such as RecA-LexA regulators or genomic DNA10,13,14. The RecA-LexA axis, central to the bacterial SOS response, can coordinate DNA repair, mutagenesis, horizontal gene transfer, induction of antibiotics tolerance and resistance, and the maintenance of the genetic variability in adaptive resistance to stressful conditions15. Excessive SOS induction can drive ALD by overwhelming redox homeostasis, positioning this pathway as a high-value therapeutic target15–17. Understanding AMP-mediated SOS dysregulation could thus unlock novel strategies to counter resistance evolution.

The ATP-dependent Clp protease ATP-binding subunit ClpX, an AAA+ molecular machine, is deeply involved in bacterial homeostasis through forming the ClpXP protease with ATP-dependent Clp protease proteolytic subunit ClpP18. In addition to its housekeeping role in protein quality control, ClpXP could also regulate virulence by processing key stress response effectors18,19. Specifically, ClpXP can degrade LexA 1-84, a truncated SOS repressor generated during LexA auto-cleavage20. The retained DNA-binding capacity of LexA 1-84 could allow it to suppress SOS genes, creating a feedback loop where ClpXP dysfunction would stabilize LexA 1-84 and attenuate SOS activation20. Despite ClpX’s centrality in bacterial adaptation, current inhibitors primarily act on ClpP21–23 or disrupt assembled ClpXP24, with only a few pharmacological agents specifically targeting this protease25–27.

In our previous study, we discovered a novel AMP EWAMP-R (the schematic diagram and amino acid sequence of EWAMP-R are presented in Fig. 1) that could damage bacterial membrane and activate the ALD pathway28. Strikingly, sub-inhibitory concentrations of EWAMP-R killed E. coli predominantly through non-lytic mechanisms, suggesting intracellular target engagement. Building on these findings, this study aims to: (1) uncover the intracellular target mediating EWAMP-R’s secondary bactericidal effects; (2) characterize the interaction between EWAMP-R and its target; and (3) elucidate the mechanism linking EWAMP-R to ALD pathway activation. By clarifying the intracellular mechanism of action of EWAMP-R, this study will advance the rational design of AMPs targeting bacterial biomacromolecules and inform strategies to exploit the extreme SOS response.

Results

EWAMP-R combined with ClpX protein in E. coli

EWAMP-R exhibited potent antibacterial activity, with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against drug-resistant bacteria that are equal to or lower than those of ampicillin (Supplementary Table 1). The gel-retardation DNA binding assay (Supplementary Fig. 1) showed no complete retardation at low EWAMP-R concentrations (0–40 μg/mL for E. coli DNA and 0–10 μg/mL for S. aureus DNA), suggesting that the direct interaction between bacterial DNA and EWAMP-R might not be the primary cause of ALD. To further investigate the mode of action of EWAMP-R beyond its membrane activity, we screened spontaneous mutants that survived at 64 μg/ml EWAMP-R, which is 8-fold and 4-fold higher than the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for wild-type E. coli (8 μg/mL) and S. aureus (16 μg/mL), respectively28. The whole genome sequencing revealed mutations in the clpX gene of E. coli and the clpP and CoA-disulfide reductase genes of S. aureus, confirmed by optimized PCR and genetic sequencing analysis.

In E. coli, the clpX mutation resulted in the substitution of the 370th residue (R370 to C370) (Fig. 1a). The sensor-II Arg motif (368-370) of ClpX was reported to be involved in ATP binding and hydrolysis19. Therefore, this mutation might be a specific response to EWAMP-R rather than a random event due to its important function. To confirm the interaction, wild-type and mutant ClpX were expressed and purified. The results of SPR indicated that EWAMP-R could bind to ClpX (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1). Hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions were observed between the chains of ClpX in molecular docking (Fig. 1c, d). For instance, hydrogen bonds formed between R12 (EWAMP-R) and S123 (chain A of ClpX), W13 (EWAMP-R) and G122 (chain A of ClpX), and W13 (EWAMP-R) and K125 (chain A of ClpX). The Walker A motif (119–128 residues) is crucial for ATP binding and hydrolysis, and these interactions could interfere with its function19. Specifically, the side chain of R12 of EWAMP-R occupied the space around V78 and I79, which are part of the ATP binding site (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 2)29. This suggests that the interaction between EWAMP-R and ClpX may reduce the nucleotide affinity of ClpX.

Furthermore, the clpX mutation conferred increased tolerance to EWAMP-R. A site-directed mutagenesis was performed using CRISPR/Cas9, introducing a mutation (1108 C > T) in the clpX gene. The MIC of the clpX mutant E. coli against EWAMP-R was 32 μg/mL, compared to 8 μg/mL for the wild-type strain and 64 μg/mL for the resistant strain (Supplementary Table 1), further supporting ClpX as a potential target of EWAMP-R. Additionally, EWAMP-R can interact with ClpX from other bacterial species like Neisseria meningitidis, indicating its potential as a disruptor of bacterial ClpX (Supplementary Fig. 4).

It should be noted that AlphaFold 3 (AF3) was also employed to model the molecular docking of EWAMP-R with ClpX. However, the best result from all simulations yielded an ipTM of 0.53 and a pTM of 0.58, with the ipTM value significantly below the 0.8 threshold, indicating limited reliability in AF3’s conformational prediction for this system (Supplementary Fig. 5)30. Abramson et al.30 have explicitly highlighted several limitations of AF3, including issues with stereochemistry, hallucination effects in disordered regions, challenges in simulating dynamic behavior, and reduced accuracy for certain target proteins. Given that ClpX contains disordered regions29, the diffusion-based generative model of AF3 may generate “spurious structured conformations” in these regions30, potentially explaining the discrepancy between the AF3 predictions and the docking results of EWAMP-R with the E. coli ClpX structure (PDB: 4i34, RCSB)29. Nevertheless, the analysis still suggests that the binding site of EWAMP-R is located near the Walker A motif of ClpX (Supplementary Fig. 5), offering insights into its potential binding preference for this region.

EWAMP-R induced ClpX mutation in E. coli leading to flagellum overgrowth

Given the evidence of ClpX binding to EWAMP-R, we explored the potential effects of EWAMP-R on E. coli by comparing the transcriptomes of wild-type and EWAMP-R-resistant bacteria. A total of 502 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was identified including 257 upregulated and 245 downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 6a and Supplementary Data 1). Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR confirmed these findings (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) cluster analysis revealed significant upregulation of pathways related to flagellar formation, chemotaxis, two-component systems, and galactose metabolism, while pathways involved in one-carbon pool by folate, oxidative phosphorylation, and purine metabolism were downregulated (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 6c and 6d).

TEM observations confirmed the overgrowth of flagella in resistant E. coli. While wild-type E. coli (ATCC 25922) rarely possesses flagella (Fig. 2b), the length of flagella in the clpX mutant E. coli (clpX 1108 C > T) was significantly increased (Fig. 2c). The “hyperflagellate phenotype” was evident in EWAMP-R-resistant E. coli (Fig. 2d), similar to other bacteria with ClpXP deletions31. Thus, the flagellum overgrowth could be one noticeable characteristic of EWAMP-R induced ClpX mutant E. coli. To understand the mechanism of flagellum overgrowth and its relationship with clpX mutation, the relative expression levels of flhC, flhD, fliC, and fliA were measured (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Data 1). After the mutation of clpX, the expression levels of fliC and fliA increased 3.6 and 1.8 times respectively while the relative expression of flhC and flhD did not change significantly. In contrast, in the EWAMP-R-resistant E. coli, flhC, flhD, fliC, and fliA were upregulated by 3.0, 1.8, 7.7, and 2.4 times respectively, compared to the wild type E. coli. In addition, the expression levels of flhC and fliC were significantly higher in the resistant strain than in the clpX mutant, indicating the synonymous mutations may also promote the transcription of flagella related genes. These results indicate that the clpX mutation induced by EWAMP-R leads to increased flagella production, supporting the notion that ClpX could be a target of EWAMP-R in E. coli.

EWAMP-R inhibited the conformational change of ClpX

To elucidate the direct impact of EWAMP-R on ClpX, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the docking result of ClpX structure (4i34, RCSB)29. Despite similar binding affinities (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2), the binding position of mutant ClpX with EWAMP-R differed from that of the wild type due to the residue substitution at the 370th site. Hence, in order to compare the effects of EWAMP-R binding on wild-type and mutant ClpX in the same position, we mutated the complex of wild-type ClpX and EWAMP-R directly using CHARMM-GUI and simulated the both complexes for 100 ns, with three independent replicates.

The systems reached equilibrium between 80 and 100 ns, as indicated by root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) values (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 1). Structural differences were observed between the ClpX and the complex (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 2). The relative binding free energy (ΔGbind) showed that EWAMP-R formed a more stable binding with wild-type ClpX (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 1). However, SPR analysis revealed that the binding affinities of EWAMP-R to both wild-type and mutant ClpX were comparable (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that EWAMP-R may interact with the mutant ClpX through an alternative binding site.

The free energy decomposition revealed significant interactions in wild-type chain A, involving residues T121, G122, S123, G124, K125, and R370 (ΔGbind < −10.47 kJ/mol), indicating a stable binding configuration, particularly within the Walker A motif (residues 119–128) (Fig. 3c). In contrast, the binding of EWAMP-R to wild-type chain B appears more complex, with several residues exhibiting ΔGbind > 10.47 kJ/mol (Fig. 3c). For the interaction between EWAMP-R and the mutant-type ClpX, only two residues in chain A (G122 and G124) showed significant binding contributions (ΔGbind < −10.47 kJ/mol), suggesting a weaker interaction compared to the wild type. This reduced binding affinity may be attributed to the R370 mutation, which might alter the binding interface and disrupts key interactions.

To further explore the effects of EWAMP-R on ClpX, we analyzed the secondary structure and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) between 80 and 100 ns. The secondary structures and SASA were not significantly altered (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b and Supplementary Data 1). The radius of gyration (Rg) was also measured to assess the tertiary structure changes of ClpX. The overall Rg values showed only minor fluctuations between ClpX and the complex. However, the Rg of chain A in wild-type ClpX was significantly lower than that in the complex (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Data 1). It can be inferred that EWAMP-R binding could reduce the compactness and inhibit the tertiary structure changes of wild-type chain A, potentially decreasing its thermal stability. Since conformational changes are essential for ClpX function29, the binding of EWAMP-R may lead to dysfunction. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the mutant-type group (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 8c–g), indicating that the mutation may alter the residues interacting with EWAMP-R, resulting in an unstable binding and less disruption of ClpX conformational changes, though the mutant residue did not directly participate in the interaction with EWAMP-R.

To further investigate the functional impact of EWAMP-R on wild-type and mutant ClpX, it is necessary to assess their ATPase activities under EWAMP-R treatment and to evaluate how impaired ClpX function affects bacterial viability.

EWAMP-R interfered with the ATPase activity of ClpX and inhibited LexA 1-84 degradation by ClpXP

ClpX is a widespread ATPase in bacteria and forms AAA+ proteases with ClpP, degrading intracellular proteins involved in various biological functions18,32. Therefore, inhibition of ClpX by EWAMP-R could disrupt the metabolism of E. coli. To assess the functional changes in ClpX under EWAMP-R stress, ATPase activity was measured. Increasing concentrations of EWAMP-R interfered with the ATPase activity of ClpX, with a more severe effect on the wild type (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 1). For example, at 16 μg/mL EWAMP-R, the ATPase activity of wild-type ClpX decreased by 88.3% compared to the 0 μg/mL treatments, while it was reduced by 69.0% in the mutant ClpX treatments. The IC50 (median inhibition concentration) of EWAMP-R for wild-type ClpX was determined to be 1.15 μM, significantly lower than that for the mutant (3.79 μM), indicating a stronger inhibitory effect on the wild-type enzyme (Fig. 4a).

As a result of treatment with EWAMP-R, LexA 1-84, a ClpXP substrate, was accumulated in enterohemorrhagic E. coli O157:H7 ATCC 35150 (EHEC) (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Data 1), which has an MIC of 8 μg/mL for EWAMP-R. LexA 1-84 and 85-202 are the auto-cleavage fragments of LexA. LexA 1-84 may still retain some repressor activity to the transcription of genes involved in SOS response, so that its accumulation could result in the inhibition of SOS response and activation of the ALD pathway20. Thus, the relative expression levels of recA, lexA, dinB and umuC were detected after EWAMP-R treatment, which are a part of the SOS response33. With increasing EWAMP-R concentrations, the expression levels increased initially and then decreased (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Data 1). Taking all the results into consideration, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the combination of EWAMP-R with ClpX may be a motivation inducing ALD in EHEC.

Biosynthesis and metabolism processes of amino acids in EWAMP-R-resistant S. aureus were inhibited

In S. aureus, mutations in the clpP and CoA-disulfide reductase genes did not affect the protein domains and were not observed in other strains. Molecular docking revealed no significant differences in binding sites with or without mutations, suggesting these mutations may be random events following DNA damage in S. aureus.

Comparative transcriptome analysis identified 166 upregulated and 360 downregulated DEGs, which were verified (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b and Supplementary Data 1). The DEGs were classified according to GO and KEGG cluster analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). Significantly downregulated pathways included the biosynthesis and metabolism of amino acids and other cellular substances, while most genes involved in pyrimidine metabolism were upregulated (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). As a consequence, though no significant morphological changes were observed under TEM, the growth rate was significantly decelerated (Fig. 5b–d). Notably, 15 genes in the S. aureus infection pathway were downregulated in the resistant strain, while only one gene spa was upregulated despite the pathway not being significantly enriched (Supplementary Fig. 11). Overall, the growth rate and toxicity of EWAMP-R-resistant S. aureus were limited compared to the wild type.

Discussion

EWAMP-R could disrupt ClpX-mediated proteolysis and trigger SOS response dysregulation in enterohemorrhagic E. coli

The protein hydrolysis mediated by the ClpXP protease is a critical cellular process essential for bacterial homeostasis and pathogenic mechanisms19. Numerous studies have highlighted the importance of ClpX in various bacteria34–36 and its role in antibiotic resistance37,38, making it as an attractive target. Consequently, several small-molecule inhibitors have been identified. For instance, dihydrothiazepine derivatives can disrupt the ClpX hexamer in S. aureus25 and suppress ClpX ATPase activity of Chlamydia trachomatis26. Additionally, PFK-158 derivatives have been shown to bind to ClpX from S. aureus27. The bovine-derived AMP F1 also has been reported to upregulate ClpX level in E. coli, leading to inhibited proliferation and disruption of cellular structures39. However, this observation only suggests a regulatory effect of F1 on ClpX, without providing evidence that ClpX is the direct target of F1.

To our knowledge, EWAMP-R represents the first AMP demonstrated to inhibit the activity of E. coli ClpX. However, due to the current lack of a well-characterized, specific ClpX-targeting drug, the efficacy of EWAMP-R remains difficult to evaluate in a comparative context. Available data indicate that EWAMP-R exhibits high affinity for E. coli ClpX (KD = 1.75 μM), comparable to the affinities of PFK-158 derivatives and S. aureus ClpX (KD = 3.6–4.1 μM)27. Upon application to E. coli, EWAMP-R induces membrane damage, which triggers the SOS response, leading to upregulation of DNA repair genes such as rexA, lexA, dinB and umuC to repair ROS-induced DNA damage (Fig. 4c)28. However, EWAMP-R’s binding to ClpX could inhibit its ATPase activity, preventing the degradation of LexA 1-84 (Fig. 4a, b). The RecA-LexA axis, a critical regulator of the SOS response, plays a pivotal role in the evolution of antibiotic resistance and has been recognized as a valuable therapeutic target17. However, only a few inhibitors targeting LexA have been identified15. LexA 1-84, a product of LexA auto-cleavage, can also act as a transcriptional repressor of SOS-related genes20. Therefore, though LexA is not the direct target of EWAMP-R, the accumulation of LexA 1-84 induced by EWAMP-R could also downregulate the expression of these genes, impair DNA repair and ultimately convert transient membrane damage into irreversible genomic damage (Fig. 4c).

Inhibiting the degradation of ClpX substrates could be a key strategy for EWAMP-R to exert its antibacterial activity. However, it is noteworthy that EWAMP-R appears to exert membrane activity primarily against S. aureus, as no mutations in the clpX gene were detected in resistant S. aureus strains induced under EWAMP-R selection, and transcriptomic analysis revealed only a general downregulation of metabolic and biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 5). This may be attributed to the higher negative charge density on the membrane of S. aureus, which facilitates stable binding of EWAMP-R and limits its internalization, thereby preventing interaction with intracellular macromolecules28.

This binding mechanism presents three optimization strategies: (1) Residue engineering to enhance ClpX affinity. For example, modifying the side chain of R12 to form hydrogen bonds with V78 and I79. Such modifications might further reduce the ATPase activity of ClpX. Additionally, sequence optimization based on the structural features of ClpX in different pathogenic bacteria could enable the design of peptides with specificity toward particular bacterial targets. (2) Membrane penetration enhancement through hydrophobic residue substitution. Amino acid substitutions may improve the ability of EWAMP-R to penetrate the membranes of Gram-positive bacteria, enabling more effective intracellular interactions with bacterial macromolecules. (3) Stability enhancement through non-natural amino acids. Replacing natural amino acids with non-natural analogs may improve the peptide’s proteolytic resistance and overall stability. These engineered derivatives may synergistically overcome resistance while minimizing the evolutionary pressure that drives adaptive mutations.

EWAMP-R resistance might attenuate the bacterial infectivity

Intriguingly, EWAMP-R-resistant E. coli exhibited a “hyperflagellate phenotype” linked to the clpX mutation (1108 C > T) (Fig. 2c, d). While flagella are classically associated with bacterial adhesion and invasion40, their constitutive overexpression in resistant strains may paradoxically impair the pathogenicity. Prior observation found that excessive flagellin production could disrupt bacterial adhesion to host cells41, likely due to steric hindrance or dysregulated motility. Furthermore, hyper-flagellation may enhance immune recognition, as flagellin is a potent activator of immune responses40. Thus, EWAMP-R resistance in E. coli, particularly the clpX mutation, would inhibit its infection due to hyper-flagellation—a unique “self-harm” phenotype in resistant strains. However, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation. For instance, comparing the bacterial adhesion capacity and host immune responses infected with EWAMP-R-induced hyperflagellate strains versus wild-type pathogenic strains would help clarify the impact of this phenotype on virulence.

Nevertheless, this study is limited in this regard. The original strain used—Escherichia coli ATCC 25922—is a non-pathogenic standard reference strain, primarily employed in this study for fundamental research on antimicrobial peptide targets, and lacks significant infectivity, making it unsuitable for infection modeling. The pathogenic strain E. coli O157:H7 ATCC 35150 was used to assess the accumulation of LexA 1–84 and bacterial oxidative stress under EWAMP-R treatment, but no EWAMP-R-resistant variants were generated from this strain. As a result, the present study cannot evaluate the effects of the hyperflagellate phenotype on infection or immune activation. Future studies can aim to generate EWAMP-R-resistant pathogenic strains exhibiting this phenotype to investigate its functional consequences in vivo.

Notably, EWAMP-R-resistant S. aureus exhibited reduced growth rates and downregulation of partial infection-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting diminished pathogenicity, though the upregulation of pyrimidine metabolism might increase bacterial tolerance to ROS, thereby increasing resistance to antibiotics42.

EWAMP-R could serve as a model for antibacterial agents’ development due to its dual mechanism of action against bacteria

AMPs can have a broad range of applications as antibacterial ingredients43. Among various types of AMPs, numerous studies have focused on the membrane damage effects of those rich in alkaline amino acids and tryptophan residues44–47. The cationic charge of alkaline amino acids can enable these AMPs like EWAMP-R to initially adsorb onto bacterial membranes through electrostatic interactions, followed by the formation of hydrogen bonds with anionic components on bacterial surfaces, such as lipopolysaccharides, phospholipid headgroups, and teichoic acids44,48. Concurrently, the indole side chains of tryptophan residues may enhance the interaction between AMPs and bacterial membranes, facilitating the efficient insertion of these peptides into the lipid bilayer44,47,49. This mechanism of action could underpin the membrane damage activity of such AMPs, making them effective against a wide range of bacterial pathogens.

Moreover, the discovery of EWAMP-R as a novel AMP targeting ClpX could represent a paradigm shift in our understanding of the mechanisms of action of tryptophan- and alkaline amino acid-rich AMPs. While such AMPs are traditionally viewed as membrane disruptors, this study demonstrates that they can also exert antibacterial effects through precise biomolecular interactions independent of membrane lysis. The interactions highlighted the critical roles of arginine and tryptophan residues in EWAMP-R’s ability to target and inhibit ClpX. This dual mechanism of action—combining membrane damage with intracellular targeting—could impose high metabolic costs on bacteria when combating EWAMP-R6,9. While microorganisms have gradually acquired resistance to the traditional antibiotics, the structural and functional insight of EWAMP-R can provide a valuable foundation for optimizing the design of new antibacterial agents.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to identify an AMP that could specifically target the ClpX protein, a critical ATP-dependent protease in bacteria. We demonstrated that induced E. coli showed resistance to EWAMP-R, accompanied by a nonsynonymous mutation in the clpX gene (1108 C > T) and a hyperflagellate phenotype linked to the upregulated genes involved in flagellar assembly. Introducing this point mutation into wild-type E. coli via CRISPR/Cas9 significantly enhanced resistance to EWAMP-R, confirming ClpX as a direct target of the peptide. Molecular docking and functional studies revealed that EWAMP-R could bind to the Walker A motif of ClpX. Specifically, the residues R12 and W13 of EWAMP-R could form hydrogen bonds with multiple residues in the motif. Additionally, the side chain of R12 may occupy the ATP binding site, inhibiting ClpX’s ATPase activity and interfering with substrate degradation. This inhibition could lead to the accumulation of LexA 1-84, a key substrate of ClpX. Elevated LexA 1-84 levels can suppress the SOS response by binding to the SOS box, disrupting DNA repair mechanisms and ultimately triggering ALD. In contrast, EWAMP-R resistance in S. aureus appeared to arise from metabolic slowdown rather than a specific intracellular target, suggesting a distinct resistance mechanism. Collectively, these findings establish ClpX as a novel target for AMPs and highlight EWAMP-R as a promising template for developing ClpX disruptors. This discovery opens new avenues for designing AMP-based therapeutics to combat antimicrobial resistance.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of EWAMP-R and bacteria used in this study

The AMP EWAMP-R was synthesized via solid-phase peptide synthesis by GL Biochem Ltd. (Shanghai, China), with a purity of ≥95%, as verified by HPLC. The bacterial strains used in this study, including E. coli ATCC 25922 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 (designated as wild-type controls), are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. To identify potential intracellular targets of EWAMP-R, spontaneous resistant mutants were generated through iterative exposure to EWAMP-R. Wild-type bacteria in logarithmic growth phase (adjusted to 10−5–10−6 CFU/mL) were exposed to EWAMP-R at 0.5× MIC and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Surviving cells were re-adjusted to 105–106 CFU/mL and subjected to escalating concentrations of EWAMP-R (x, x + 4, and x + 8 μg/mL), where x represented the highest concentration permitting bacterial growth in the preceding cycle. This selective pressure was maintained until the MIC plateaued for 7 days, after which monoclonal isolates were purified and designated as EWAMP-R-resistant E. coli and S. aureus.

Additionally, to investigate the impact of non-synonymous mutation found in the EWAMP-R-resistant E. coli, a clpX (1108 C > T) mutant E. coli was generated via CRISPR/Cas9 point mutation by Forhigh Biotech (Hangzhou, China). E. coli O157:H7 ATCC 35150 (EHEC) was used to estimate the influence of ClpX inhibition. The monoclonal bacteria were cultured in Luria-Bertani broth and agar medium (Sangon Biotech Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 37 °C.

Whole genome sequencing of resistant bacteria

The monoclonal resistant bacteria were cultured, and their whole genomes were sequenced by Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The sequencing was performed using the shotgun approach with Illumina NovaSeq and de novo sequencing with PacBio Sequel. The results were compared to the reference genomes ASM1297480v1 (E. coli ATCC 25922) and ASM2283277v1 (S. aureus ATCC 29213) from NCBI to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). To verify the identified SNP regions, re-sequencing was performed by Beijing Liuhe BGI Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Transcriptome sequencing

The transcriptome sequencing of wild-type and EWAMP-R-resistant bacteria were conducted by Personalbio (Shanghai, China). Total RNA was extracted by TRIZOL (Ambion, Texas, USA), and ribosomal RNA was removed. The RNA was fragmented to 100-300 bp, and cDNA was synthesized and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Differential RNA expression levels between wild-type and mutant bacteria were analyzed to elucidate the impact of DNA mutations.

CRISPR/Cas9 point mutation and morphological observation

The clpX point mutation (1108 C > T) in wild-type E. coli was introduced following the method described by Jiang et al.50 by Forhigh Biotech (Hangzhou, China). Primers were designed based on the whole genome sequencing results and the reference genome ASM1297480v1 (E. coli ATCC 25922) (Supplementary Table 2). Donor DNA with the clpX mutation (1108 C > T) was constructed using pUC19 and DH5α competent cells. The competent cells were transformed with the Cas9 expression plasmid, the sgRNA expression plasmid and the donor DNA via electroporation. The positive clones were selected on Kan/Spc plating medium. Genomic DNA of the positive clone, wild-type, and EWAMP-R-resistant E. coli was extracted and sequenced to compare the clpX gene sequence. The strains were cultured overnight in LB medium, then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, followed by three washes with PBS. Morphological observations were performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Molecular docking and dynamics simulation

The ClpX structure of E. coli (4i34, RCSB)29 was applied for molecular docking with the structure of EWAMP-R that we previously established28 through AutoDock Vina51. Among 60 generated docking conformations, the pose with the lowest relative binding energy was selected. The interaction analysis and visualization were performed using PyMOL52 and LigPlot+53. Additionally, the interactions between EWAMP-R and ClpX were predicted using AlphaFold3 (AF3)30, based on five independent simulations.

The docking results of EWAMP-R and the ClpX structure of E. coli (PDB ID: 4i34, RCSB)29 were used for molecular simulations. The complex of EWAMP-R with mutant ClpX was generated via direct mutagenesis using the Solution Builder module in CHARMM-GUI54–56, to analyze the differences in binding of EWAMP-R to wild and mutant type ClpX at the same binding site. The molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2021.357 (compiled with GCC 11.2.0, OpenMPI 4.1.1, and CUDA acceleration) following standard protocols, with system construction and parameterization supported by CHARMM-GUI (Supplementary Table 3)54–56: redundant components (e.g., crystallization additives, non-target chains) were removed from the initial structure; missing residues and hydrogen atoms were completed; and protonation states of titratable residues were assigned at physiological pH (7.0). Force field parameters were assigned using the CHARMM36m force field58. The complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box (rectangular, cubic crystal type) extending 10 Å from the complex surface. The system was neutralized with K⁺/Cl⁻ counterions and adjusted to a physiological ionic strength of 0.15 M KCl.

Simulations proceeded in three sequential stages:(1) Energy Minimization: Steepest-descent minimization (5000 steps) was performed with positional restraints (backbone: 400.0 kJ mol−1 nm⁻²; sidechains: 40.0 kJ mol−1 nm⁻²) to relieve steric clashes, with a convergence criterion of maximum force <1000.0 kJ mol−1 nm−1. Nonbonded interactions were handled via the Verlet cutoff scheme (electrostatics: PME, cutoff = 1.2 nm; van der Waals: force-switch modifier, cutoff = 1.2 nm, switch distance = 1.0 nm). (2) NVT Equilibration: A 125 ps NVT ensemble simulation stabilized the system at 310.15 K using a Nose-Hoover thermostat (coupling time constant = 1.0 ps). Initial velocities were randomly generated at 310.15 K, and positional restraints were retained to avoid large conformational perturbations. (3) NPT Production Simulation: An unrestrained 100 ns NPT ensemble simulation was run with a 2 fs time step. Temperature was maintained at 310.15 K (Nose-Hoover thermostat), and pressure was regulated at 1 atm using a Parrinello-Rahman barostat (coupling time constant = 5.0 ps, compressibility = 4.5e-5 bar−1). Hydrogen bonds were constrained via the LINCS algorithm. Trajectory frames were saved every 50 ps, which served as the sole input for post-simulation analysis. Numerous studies have shown that using all-atom models, the CHARMM36m force field, and explicit solvent (TIP3P) enables highly accurate molecular simulations of protein-ligand interactions, meeting the analytical requirements for investigating the interaction between EWAMP-R and the ClpX protein in this study 58–63.

RMSD, RSMF, SASA, Rg were calculated using GROMACS. The average secondary structure content of ClpX during 80–100 ns was calculated using DSSP as implemented in MDTraj, with results averaged across time. Additionally, a subset of 100 frames was uniformly extracted from the 80–100 ns segment of the production trajectory for chain A, chain B, and EWAMP-R to calculate the binding free energy (ΔGbind). The calculations were performed using the multiple-trajectory molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) method with the decomposition scheme implemented in gmx_MMPBSA 1.5.264. The events under investigation occurred within the timescale of the 100 ns MD simulation, so enhanced sampling methods were not employed.

Surface plasmon resonance

The expression systems of wild-type and mutant ClpX proteins were completed by Junji Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Suzhou, China). The wild and mutant type clpX genes were synthesized, bound to the pET-28a(+) vector and transferred to the BL21(DE3) strain, respectively. The total proteins were extracted from the collected monoclonal expression bacteria by Bacterial Protein Extraction Kit (Sangon, China). The two types of ClpX were purified using HisTrap FF Crude (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK) with Binding buffer (20 mM NaH₂PO₄, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 7.4), Elution buffer (20 mM NaH₂PO₄, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, pH 7.4) and NGC Quest 10 Plus Chromatography System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) according to the manufacturers’ guidelines. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments were conducted at the Instrumental Analysis Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University. After immobilizing ClpX onto an anti-HIS biosensor chip, EWAMP-R in HBS-EP buffer was flowed over the chip. Refractive index changes were detected and analyzed through BiacoreTM SPR Systems (Biacore 8 K, Cytiva, UK).

Measurement of ATPase activity of ClpX, Western blot and RT-qPCR

ATPase activity of wild-type and mutant ClpX was measured following Fetzer et al.24. The ATPase buffer for wild-type and mutant ClpX was prepared separately (100 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 200 mM KCl, 20 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM NADH, 2 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, 50 U/mL lactate dehydrogenase, 50 U/mL pyruvate kinase, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 4 µM ClpX). A 54 µL aliquot of the ClpX ATPase buffer was mixed with 0.6 µL of EWAMP-R solution at varying concentrations, using 0.6 µL PBS as the positive control and ATPase buffer without ClpX as the negative control. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by the addition of 6 µL of 200 mM ATP solution. The change in absorbance at 340 nm was measured to monitor the variation in NADH/H+ levels. The average relative ATPase activity was normalized to the average activity of the 0 µM group, and the inhibition rate for each concentration was calculated as 100% minus the normalized relative activity. Subsequently, the IC50 was determined using Probit regression and Logit models in SPSS 22.0.

For bacterial samples, 1 mL of overnight culture was washed three times with PBS and treated with different concentrations of EWAMP-R. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, total bacterial protein and RNA were extracted using the Bacterial Protein Extraction Kit (Sangon, China) and the Bacteria Total RNA Isolation Kit (Sangon, China), respectively. Western blot and RT-qPCR were conducted according to our previous research28,65. The following antibodies were used to detect protein expression levels: His-Tag Mouse Monoclonal Antibody (Yifan Biotech, Shanghai, China), Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) HRP (Yifan, China), Anti-LexA DNA Binding Region antibody (Merck, Germany), HRP Conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Boster, Wuhan, China), and Anti-GAPDH Antibody (Boster, China). The primers were exhibited in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistics and reproducibility

All results are expressed as mean values ± standard deviation based on triplicate measurements. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using Levene’s test. Different letters above the bars in the charts indicate significant differences among groups (P < 0.05) as determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s post-hoc test using SPSS 22.0. Values sharing the same letter are not significantly different, while distinct letters represent statistically significant discrepancies. The results were ranked in descending order of magnitude. Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 9.5.
