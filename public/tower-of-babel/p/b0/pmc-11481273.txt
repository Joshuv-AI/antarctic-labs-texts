Occupational differences in SARS-CoV-2 infection: the Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey (JACSIS) study in 2022
Authors: Ayane Sato, Hirokazu Tanaka, Takahiro Tabuchi, Kota Katanoda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, essential workers (e.g. medical workers) may be at a high prevalence of contracting COVID-19; however, the trend remains unclear in Japan. This study aimed to investigate occupational differences in the prevalence of COVID-19.MethodsData were retrieved from the 2022 Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey. We analyzed the self-reported SARS-CoV-2 infection as of October 2022. We included 21,875 participants aged 20–64 years. Participants were classified into five occupation groups (“medical workers,” “off-site medical workers,” “care workers,” “other essential workers”, and “non-essential workers”), as well as those unemployed. The prevalence ratio of COVID-19 was computed using Poisson regression. We included sex, age, SARS-CoV-2 vaccination history, number of households, education level, and residential area as covariates. Additionally, we performed similar analyses for three time periods: (1) August–September 2022, (2) October 2021 to July 2022, and (3) before September 2021.ResultsThe overall cumulative prevalence of COVID-19, as of October 2022, was 16.5%. Regarding occupation, medical workers had a higher prevalence than non-essential workers (23.5% vs 16.2% (difference: 7.2% point [95% confidence interval (CI): 4.2–10.2])). Similar results were observed among care workers compared with non-essential workers (22.1% vs 16.2% (difference: 5.9% point [95% CI: 2.5%–9.3%])). Other essential workers had a similar prevalence to non-essential workers (17.6% vs 16.2% (difference: 1.4% point [95% CI: -0.1%–2.8%]). In relative terms, medical workers and care workers were associated with an increased prevalence of COVID-19 (prevalence ratio: 1.44 [95% CI: 1.20–1.73] and 1.36 [95% CI: 1.11–1.66], respectively). In addition, we found that younger age, living in endemic region (e.g., Tokyo and Osaka), and living with more than four people contributed to the higher prevalence. According to the time period, the prevalence was significantly higher in medical and care workers than in non-essential workers only during August–September 2022, which corresponded to the severest outbreak period in Japan.ConclusionsIn Japan, medical and care workers had a higher prevalence of contracting COVID-19 compared to non-essential workers. However, this trend emerged during the peak of the COVID-19 spreading in the summer of 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20355-6.

Background

The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic began in 2020, with 704 million cases and 6.7 million deaths reported worldwide at the end of 2022 [1, 2]. The control of COVID-19 remains a global public health challenge [3, 4]; however, SARS-CoV-2 vaccines have spread to 13.2 billion doses administered worldwide until the end of 2022 [5], and this widespread distribution and development of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines have contributed toward the normalization of economies and healthcare systems. The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted preexisting health inequalities in society, and susceptibility to infection differs based on socioeconomic factors, such as race, ethnicity, sex, education, and occupation [6]. Several studies have indicated that essential workers, including medical workers, had a higher incidence of COVID-19 and mortality than other workers, given the high prevalence of infection in the workplace [7–9]. Specifically, medical and care workers potentially had a high prevalence of contracting COVID-19, despite being prioritized for SARS-CoV-2 vaccination during spring 2021 to prevent a decrease in the medical workforce due to mass infection [10].

The International Labor Organization originally defined essential workers as people who engaged in occupations whose strike would affect the life, personal safety, or health of others [11]. These occupations often involve tasks that cannot be readily performed remotely. During the lockdown period of the COVID-19 pandemic, various measures were implemented to allow essential workers to continue working. For example, children of essential workers were allowed to attend school during the closure of primary and secondary schools in the UK in 2020. To identify eligible workers, the UK government provided an illustrative definition of essential workers [12, 13]. Moreover, essential workers (especially medical workers) have been able to receive COVID-19 priority vaccination since spring 2021.

In Japan, the number of COVID-19 cases and deaths in 2020 and 2021 was relatively low, but the COVID-19 outbreak peaked in August 2022 (the number of monthly cases: 6.2 million) [1]. By October 2022 (the end of a nationwide all-case survey), the cumulative number of COVID-19 cases in Japan was 21.0 million, which represented approximately 16% of the Japanese population, with 44,266 deaths reported [1, 2]. The SARS-CoV-2 vaccine became available in February 2021, and its use was started only for medical workers but gradually expanded to other people, regardless of their occupation. The vaccination rate among medical and care workers was slightly higher than that of non-priority groups (excluding medical workers, older people, and those with primary illnesses) [14]. By the beginning of August 2022, the percentage of SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients in the Japanese population was 78% (the percentage of completed second dose) and remained relatively stable until October 2022 [5]. The Japanese government urged people to work from home whenever possible since the first state of emergency was declared in early April 2020 [15]. Additionally, people were required to refrain from travel, with restrictions on overseas travelers continuing until May 2023. Medical workers were required to provide face-to-face care for those infected, while other essential workers often had to engage closely with coworkers [16], largely because their roles involved working with people from different ethnic backgrounds for low wages [13]. Accordingly, essential workers in Japan may have been exposed to a high risk for SARS-CoV-2 infection since April 2020, as well as high-income countries. Although COVID-19 surveillance has been conducted through comprehensive nationwide surveys in Japan, it is unclear if there are socioeconomic differences, especially from an occupational perspective, in the prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection due to the lack of a national monitoring system.

This study aimed to analyze occupational differences in SARS-CoV-2 infection in Japan as of October 2022, with particular attention paid to essential workers.

Methods

Study design

We used data from the Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey (JACSIS), conducted in October 2022. This survey was based on a self-report questionnaire that collected data regarding daily life, health, social activities, and economic activities in Japan during the COVID-19 pandemic. Responses were obtained from 32,000 participants through a Japanese Internet survey company (Rakuten Insight, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) from September 12, 2022, to October 19, 2022. This company manages the largest single survey panel covering all prefectures in Japan, with a membership of 2.2 million in September 2022. We calculated the population weight score regarding the Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions conducted in 2019 to approximate sociodemographic distributions within the Japanese population [16–20]. The weight score was calculated as the ratio (inverse probability weighting) representing the number of the whole population divided by the number of the study participants by sociodemographic characteristics.

Exposure variables

Among the 32,000 participants, we excluded 3,369 invalid answers. Among the rest 28,631 participants, 21,875 participants aged 20–64 years (considering the Japanese working-age population) were included. Appendix Figure 1 shows the flow diagram of the study population.

Table 1 provides a detailed definition of the essential workers for our analysis. In Japan, there is no established definition for the classification of essential workers [21]. However, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) indicates that businesses required to continue operations during a state of emergency include medical and care workers, police officers, and teachers [22]. Therefore, we considered “medical workers,” “care workers,” “teachers,” “public servants,” “security workers,” “retail,” “transportation,” “electricity,” and “heat supply industry” as essential workers. Participants were classified into five occupation groups (i.e., “medical workers (working at hospitals and clinics),” “off-site medical workers (working in places other than hospitals and clinics),” “care workers,” “other essential workers”, and “non-essential workers”) or unemployed. Outcome measure and covariates

To assess the prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection, we assessed the self-reported cumulative prevalence of COVID-19 (cumulative cases of COVID-19 per study participant) as the primary outcome [19, 20]. The JACSIS study questionnaire included an item regarding SARS-CoV-2 infection (“I have been diagnosed with COVID-19 during the period”: yes or no) for each of the following periods: “within 2 months before the survey” (from August to September 2022), “1 year to 2 months before the survey” (between October 2021 and July 2022), and “ > 1 year before the survey” (before September 2021). These responses were used to determine the cumulative prevalence of COVID-19 between January 2020 and October 2022.

Based on a previous study [8], we considered six covariates, including sex, age (categorized in 5-year age intervals from 20 to 64 years), SARS-CoV-2 vaccination history (“third dose and more” or “second dose or less”), household size (living alone, two persons, three persons, more than four persons), education level (“junior high school graduates,” “high school graduates,” “junior/technical college graduates,” and “vocational/university graduates and above”), and place of residence (endemic region [Tokyo, Aichi, Osaka, Hyogo, Kyoto, or Fukuoka prefecture] or nonendemic region [other prefectures]). The endemic regions comprise prefectures where a state of emergency was declared four times between 2020 and 2022. All covariates were determined based on survey data in October 2022.

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of COVID-19 as of October 2022 was calculated according to demographic characteristics and socioeconomic status using the population weight score. We included the five previously mentioned occupational groups and unemployment as the main exposures. Prevalence differences (differences in the prevalence) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using multiple adjustments with reference to non-essential workers. The prevalence ratio and 95% CIs were computed through multiple-adjusted Poisson regression to assess occupational differences in non-essential workers. We included sex, 5-year age group, SARS-CoV-2 vaccination history, household size, level of education, and area of residence (endemic region or not) as covariates, with their references being “men,” “40–44 years,” “living alone,” “high school graduates,” and “nonendemic regions,” respectively. We selected the “40–44 years” category as the reference due to its typically high employment rate, unlike the younger or older age groups, which may include a higher proportion of students and unemployed individuals. Similarly, we selected high school graduates as the reference group because they represent the largest segment of Japanese workers compared with other educational backgrounds. Additionally, we analyzed the prevalence of COVID-19 according to the three time periods mentioned above: (1) August to September 2022 (the highest prevalence SARS-CoV-2 epidemic periods), (2) October 2021 to July 2022 (the dominant period of the Omicron variant), and (3) before September 2021 (the first phase of the pandemic).

Prevalence of COVID-19

The number of cumulative COVID-19 cases was 3,796 (16.5%, adjusted and population-weighted; Table 2), with an average age of 37.2 years (SD: ± 11.0 years). The weighted prevalence of COVID-19 according to occupation was as follows: medical workers (23.5%), care workers (22.1%), other essential workers (17.6%), off-site medical workers (18.1%), non-essential workers (16.2%), and unemployed (13.1%). Regarding other sociodemographic factors and vaccination history, the highest weighted prevalence was observed among male (17.2%; compared with female [15.8%]), participants aged 20–24 years (23.2%), participants who received a second dose or less (19.5%; compared with a third dose or more [14.9%]), participants who lived in endemic regions (19.7%; compared with those living in nonendemic regions [15.1%]), those with junior high school graduates (18.1%), and participants who lived with more than four people (21.7%).

Prevalence difference and prevalence ratio by occupations

Table 2 also shows the multiple-adjusted prevalence difference, and prevalence ratio of COVID-19 as of October 2022. Essential workers (combined) had a higher prevalence than non-essential workers (19.0% vs 16.2% (difference: 2.9% point [95% CI: 1.6%–4.2%]). Medical workers had a higher prevalence than non-essential workers (23.5% vs 16.2% (difference: 7.2% point [95% CI: 4.2%–10.2%])). Similar results were observed in care workers compered to non-essential workers (22.1% vs 16.2% (difference: 5.9% point [95% CI: 2.5%–9.3%])). Other essential workers had a similar prevalence to non-essential workers (17.6% vs 16.2% (difference: 1.4% point [95% CI: -0.1%–2.8%]). Additionally, unemployed participants had a lower prevalence than non-essential workers (13.1% vs 16.2% (difference: -3.1% point [95% CI: -4.4–-1.8]). In relative terms, essential workers (combined) had a higher prevalence ratio (1.17 [95% CI: 1.05–1.30]) than non-essential workers. Compared to non-essential workers, medical workers (1.44 [95% CI: 1.20–1.73]) and care workers (1.36 [95% CI: 1.11–1.66]) had higher prevalence ratios. Conversely, unemployed participants had a lower prevalence ratio (0.81 [95% CI: 0.70–0.94]) than non-essential workers.

Younger individuals had higher prevalence ratios than older individuals. Regarding other demographic characteristics, individuals who received the third vaccination dose or more had a lower prevalence ratio (0.77 [95% CI: 0.69–0.85]) than those who received the second vaccination or less. Furthermore, individuals living in endemic regions and with more than four people had higher prevalence ratios (1.30 [95% CI: 1.19–1.43] and 1.51 [95% CI: 1.31–1.73], respectively) than those living in nonendemic regions and alone. Education level and sex were not associated with the prevalence of COVID-19.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that the prevalence ratio of COVID-19 was 1.17 times higher in overall essential workers (combined group) than in non-essential workers as of October 2022 in Japan. In absolute terms, medical and care workers had + 7.2% and + 5.9% higher prevalence of COVID-19, respectively, than non-essential workers as of October 2022. Although medical and care workers did not experience a high prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection before spring 2022, this prevalence was high in summer 2022 (August and September 2022). Other essential workers, excluding medical and care workers, did not show a significantly increased prevalence for SARS-CoV-2 infection.

The highest COVID-19 prevalence among medical and care workers in Japan during the summer of 2022 may be due to the background rate of SARS-CoV-2 infections in Japanese society. However, the difference in the prevalence of COVID-19 according to occupation did not clearly appear until the spring of 2022, which could be attributed to the low background prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infections in Japanese society. Specifically, movement restrictions and strict border control could successfully maintain COVID-19 deaths at a relatively low number in 2020 compared to those in other high-income countries, which also contributed to an increase in the average life expectancy between 2019 and 2020. Moreover, we recognized that medical and care workers were subjected to stricter restrictions (e.g., prohibitions on eating out and traveling) imposed by their workplaces than other workers in 2020. Although there was an increased background level of SARS-CoV-2 infection in 2021 compared with that in 2020, the launch of the prioritized SARS-CoV-2 vaccination program for medical workers in March 2021 may have contributed to the decreased prevalence in 2021. By the end of 2021, > 75% of the Japanese population received two or more SARS-CoV-2 vaccine doses [23].

Accordingly, there was widespread SARS-CoV-2 vaccination across occupations around early 2022. However, the emergence of variant strains, such as the Omicron (observed first in December 2021) variant, increased the overall infection risk. Despite the progression of the vaccination program to administer the third and subsequent vaccine doses, medical and care workers had an increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection, in addition to an increased risk of cluster infections among patients or older individuals at their workplaces. During this period in 2022, sustained changes in health care operations and increased workload due to COVID-19 measures resulted in staff shortages in hospitals and clinics. The Japanese government eased regulations during the quarantine period for health care workers exposed to high-risk situations; this highlighted the so-called “medical collapse” as a social issue in Japanese society. Therefore, the spread of SARS-CoV-2 infection and the high pressure on health care systems may have increased the COVID-19 prevalence among medical and care workers during the summer of 2022 in Japan. This increase appeared to result in a negative spiral; specifically, staff shortages forced health care workers to return to their work without an adequate quarantine period. It is essential to adhere to standard infection prevention measures, such as handwashing, wearing masks, and social distancing, to prevent “medical collapse” due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Moreover, governments should ensure the proper distribution of personal protective equipment (PPE) to avoid shortages: medical workers experienced severe shortages of PPE [24], as subsequently mentioned. From a public health perspective, it is vital to acquire herd immunity by promoting vaccinations. Additionally, the government needs to cooperate with local communities to strengthen support for individuals at a high risk of infection and convey accurate information regarding COVID-19.

We observed small difference between essential workers and other workers regarding SARS-CoV-2 infection until July 2022 in Japan. However, medical and care workers had a high prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection in August and September 2022, which is inconsistent with the results of previous European studies. Some European countries have shown a higher incidence of COVID-19 among medical and care workers than among non-essential workers in 2020 (the early stage of the COVID-19 pandemic) [8, 9]. During this period, many medical workers worked in COVID-19-endemic areas without sufficient access to PPE [25]. In contrast, these studies reported similar results (high incidence of COVID-19 among medical and care workers) to ours regarding outcomes after 2022 in Japan. It is crucial to analyze the SARS-CoV-2 infection among essential workers (excluding medical and care workers) further to identify other occupational groups with a high prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection in Japan. For example, workers in the education sector have a higher prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection than non-essential workers in the UK [8].

A strength of this study is the high generalizability of its findings, since the prevalence of COVID-19 (16.5%) among the participants was similar to that reported by the MHLW (approximately 16%) as of October 2022 [1, 2]. This is the first study to investigate the SARS-CoV-2 infection among essential workers in Japan. Moreover, the definitions of essential workers applied in the present study can inform other epidemiological studies in Japan. Another strength was that we could assess the association between socioeconomic factors and the prevalence of COVID-19 with participants in JACSIS study, given the lack of mechanisms for links with resident registration or population databases. Furthermore, we assessed the differences in the prevalence of COVID-19 according to the time period across occupations.

This study had some limitations. First, JACSIS study was based on a self-report survey. Accordingly, some participants may have been incorrectly classified as positive or negative for SARS-CoV-2 based on the low-accuracy tests. Although the linked data between national population census data and reports of COVID-19 cases and mortality data are available in some European countries [7, 8], there remains no established framework for linking them, which has impeded the monitoring of COVID-19 incidence regarding socioeconomic factors in Japan. The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications is responsible for conducting the Japanese national population census, while local governments and MHLW share reports of COVID-19 cases and mortality data. However, our result of prevalence of COVID-19 (16.5%) was very close to that of the overall prevalence of COVID-19 among Japanese population, as discussed above [1, 2]. Second, our results may have been biased because medical and care workers were more likely to undergo SARS-CoV-2 tests than workers in other occupations, leading to more opportunities for COVID-19 diagnosis. Many health care workers interact with individuals with poor immune systems, including patients and older people. Accordingly, they underwent tests frequently when they experienced mild sickness during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic although regular SARS-CoV-2 tests for medical workers were not common in hospitals and clinics in Japan. At that time, other workers may have been reluctant to undergo these tests because of fear of SARS-CoV-2 exposure. This trend was further exacerbated by the fact that remote work mitigated the spread of COVID-19. Third, a relatively small number of the sample size among off-site medical workers (n = 366: same in each of the three periods) and care workers (n = 792) should be interpreted with caution; however, the sample size did not distort the time period analysis. Although most point estimates of prevalence ratios were more than 1 for essential workers (Fig. 1), the prevalence differences were absolutely small until July 2022, regardless of sample size, among medical workers (e.g. + 2.0% before September 2021), off-site medical workers (e.g. + 1.0% before September 2021) and care workers (e.g. + 0.6% before September 2021) compared with non-essential workers (as shown in Appendix Tables 2 and 3). Therefore, we concluded that there was small difference between essential workers and other workers until July 2022.

Conclusion

In Japan, medical and care workers are at a higher SARS-CoV-2 prevalence than non-essential workers. However, this increased SARS-CoV-2 infection risk was only observed during the peak of the COVID-19 outbreak in the summer of 2022. By contrast, essential workers, excluding medical and care workers, did not show an increased prevalence of COVID-19. Further analysis and a national monitoring framework are required to identify the association between emerging infectious diseases and socioeconomic status in Japan.
