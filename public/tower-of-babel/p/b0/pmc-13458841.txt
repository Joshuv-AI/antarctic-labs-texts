Native rhizobia nodulating soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) performs better than commercial strain across locations in South Ethiopia Region
Authors: Desalegn Dalge Dalo, Addisu Fekadu Andeta, Bereket Getachew Mamo, Ashenafi Hailu Gunnabo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is one of the important legume crops, rich in protein, vegetable oil, maintain soil fertility and used as human food and livestock feed. Despite its importance, adoption and productivity of soybean in South Ethiopia is limited by poor agricultural practices and poor access to inputs like biofertilizers. This work aimed at trapping and screening elite rhizobia strains to use as inoculants in farmers’ fields for improved soil health and crop productivity. Forty-four rhizobia strains isolated from different locations in South Ethiopia region were screened along with a commercial inoculant Bradyrhizobium japonicum strain (obtained from Menagesha Biotech Industry, Addis Ababa) in greenhouse using modified Leonard jars (MLJ). Three top performing rhizobia strains SB19, SB22, and SB24 based on the MLJ experiment and the commercial inoculant were further evaluated in farmers’ fields at different locations with varying eco-physiological conditions because environmental factors differ from place to place, affecting how living things grow, survive and function. MLJ experiment revealed that the new isolates SB19, SB22, and SB24 had significantly higher relative symbiotic effectiveness (SE%) (p < 0.05) than un-inoculated and N-fertilized control plants. Except SB19, the top performing strains did not differ from the commercial strains regarding SE%. Strain SB19 produced 34 to 61 number of nodules, while the commercial strain induced 22 to 49 nodules. In the first field experiment (2023) inoculation of soybean with SB19 resulted in average grain yields of 3.1 and 2.92 tons ha⁻1 at Arba Minch University (AMU) demo farm and Abaya campus experimental sites to be consistent with, respectively. In experiment 2 (2024), SB19 strain resulted in the average grain yields of 2.39 and 2.45 tons ha-1 at Abaya and AMU demo sites, respectively. Across all locations, the commercial strain produced an average yield of 2.25 to 2.40 tons ha⁻1, which was significantly lower (p < 0.05) than that of the native strains, but higher than the control plants, which yielded 1.87 to 2.02 tons ha⁻1. Among the evaluated strains, SB19 consistently exhibited the most promising performance across all fields and locations compared to the commercial one and others. This finding highlights the presence of highly effective, locally adapted rhizobial strains capable of nodulating soybean in South Ethiopian soils, and demonstrate their promise for selection and improvement into superior inoculant strains to enhance soybean productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55131-6.

Introduction

Food security remains a critical challenge across many developing countries1–3 with Ethiopia being among the most severely affected. Ethiopia, the second-most populous nation in Africa, is consistently ranked as one of the world’s most food-insecure states4. The country’s economy is structurally dependent on agriculture5,6, a sector that contributes about 34.6% of the nation’s gross domestic product (GDP), generating around 90% of export earnings, and employing about 80% of population7. Within this sector, smallholder farms constitute the backbone of agricultural production, cultivating about 95% of arable land and generating 90% of total output8. This is largely attributable to declining soil fertility, inadequate farm management practices, widespread soil erosion, land degradation, and limited access to external nutrient inputs4,9,10. In addition, susceptibility of the country to drought and climate change effects are becoming major challenges for food production and food security11.

To address this issue, food legumes such as soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) play a critical role, as soybean is one of the world’s most important food plants and is widely grown as a versatile industrial crop12,13. It accounts for nearly 50% of the global area under legume cultivation14. Soybean is also the most heavily traded legume worldwide15, representing more than 84.5% of internationally traded grain legumes16. This high demand is due to its nutritional value, particularly as a major source of vegetable oil and protein for both human consumption and livestock feed17–20. In addition, soybean is a multifunctional crop that contributes to soil fertility improvement21, enhances food security, and provides significant economic benefits to farmers22,23. In 2024, soybean production at the global level accounted for 420.8 million tons, of which Brazil contributed 40%, USA 28% and Argentina 12%24.

In Ethiopia, soybean cultivation commenced in the 1950s, while research on it began in the 1970s25. Its production is essential in Ethiopia for tackling malnutrition, especially among smallholder farmers who cannot afford animal-based proteins26. Soybean is a crop cultivated across various agro-ecological zones in Ethiopia17, particularly Oromia, Benshangul Gumuz, Amhara, and Tigray, and to a lesser extent in the former Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (recently Sidama zone). Soybean can be grown at altitudes ranging from 1,250 to 2,200 m above sea level (m.a.s.l.). However, it performs best between 1,300 and 1,700 m.a.s.l. It can also be grown in areas receiving 450 to 1,500 mm of annual rainfall27. But, for optimal growth and yields, soybean requires a minimum of 500 mm of annual rainfall, with ideal temperatures ranging from 23 °C to 25 °C28. Thus, it thrives in warm climates at low to medium altitudes. At present, soybean cultivation in Ethiopia covers a total of 54,543 ha, yielding a total of 125,623 tons, with a national average productivity of 2.30 tons ha−1, which falls short of the global average productivity of 2.79 tons ha-129.

The cultivation of soybean has grown in recent times, as they are a tradable commodity that produces revenue30. Additionally, the nitrogen (N2) fixing soybean crop is of significant importance for more sustainable agriculture, especially in organic farming systems31. In light of this, biological nitrogen fixation plays a crucial role in sustainable and eco-friendly food production as well as in the long-term productivity of soybean32. However, the application of rhizobium inoculants to enhance N-fixation and productivity of grain legumes remains in the developmental phase in many areas of Sub-Saharan Africa33, including Ethiopia. In Ethiopia, soybean is cultivated in smallholder farms where fertilizer use is limited and the inoculation of soybean with rhizobial inoculants is rarely practiced34. The application of an effective strain could offer a solution to enhance soybean grain yields in soils with low nitrogen35. Thus, rhizobial inoculation enhances the symbiotic nitrogen fixation in legumes, leading to greater amounts of fixed nitrogen transferred to the soil compared to non-inoculated plants36. Nitrogen fixation can consequently lessen the need for expensive inorganic fertilizers and enhance grain legume yield in the fields of resource-limited farmers. This is particularly accurate in tropical regions where nitrogen is the most limited soil nutrient, and where smallholder farmers are unable to bear the expenses of fertilizers32.

The success of soybean farming is strongly connected to use of rhizobia inoculation13 that improves soil fertility and boosts soybean production37. While, earlier studies indicated that local rhizobia strains differ in symbiotic effectiveness based on environmental factors and soybean types38,39. This emphasizes the necessity of choosing appropriate rhizobia for particular agro-ecological regions13. The lowland areas of the Gamo and Gardula Zones in South Ethiopia offer considerable promise for soybean production due to favorable climate and agricultural conditions. However, few studies have examined how well rhizobial strains adapt to these local environments, emphasizing the need for field-based evaluations to identify effective strains that can enhance soybean yields. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate indigenous rhizobial strains for enhancing soybean growth and yield in lowland areas of South Ethiopia.

The principal crops cultivated in the study area include maize (Zea mays), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L.), tef (Eragrostis tef), cowpea (Vigna unguiculata), common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris) and rarely soybean (Glycine max), integrated with moringa (Moringa stenopetala) trees in an agro-silviculture system. The soybean variety Nyala has been observed to thrive in South Ethiopia, especially in Konso, where it is rotated with cereal crops like teff or maize, and grown as a relay crop alongside maize. In the region, soybean cultivation area coverage, production status, and rhizobia inoculation practices are not known.

The lowland areas of the Gamo and Gardula Zones in South Ethiopia offer considerable promise for soybean production due to favorable climate and agricultural conditions. However, no studies have examined soybean plant responses to inoculant strains to these local environments, emphasizing the need for field-based evaluations to identify effective strains that can enhance soybean yields. Therefore, this study was aimed to evaluate indigenous rhizobial strains for enhancing soybean growth and yield in lowland areas of South Ethiopia.

Soil sample collection and analysis

Soil samples were collected from a depth of 0–30 cm from various points in farmers’ fields as well as in experimental sites (Table 1). The soil samples from each site or farm were mixed to form composite samples for the assessment of physicochemical properties at AMU, Abaya campus soil analysis laboratory. The soils were air-dried for two weeks at room temperature, grounded, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh. Soil acidity or alkalinity were determined using a pH meter in a 1:2.5 soil-to-distilled-water suspension, while soil salinity was measured using electrical conductivity meter, following the procedure described by41.

A mixture of 10 g of soil and 50 mL of distilled water in a 100 mL beaker was shaken by hand every half hour for 30 s. The pH readings were taken with a pH meter. Soil texture classes were determined using the Bouyoucos hydrometer method42. Organic carbon and total nitrogen were measured using the Walkley–Black method43 and the Kjeldahl method44, respectively. Plant available phosphorus was quantified using the Olsen method45. The cation exchange capacity (CEC) was determined with the ammonium acetate method, while calcium (Ca), potassium (K), and magnesium (Mg) were assessed using the Mehlich-3 method46.

Isolation of rhizobia from soil

Rhizobia were trapped from the soil following the method described by Howieson and Dilworth (2016)47 and using the soybean variety Nyala. Soil samples from each site were placed into plastic pots that had been surface-sterilized with 95% ethanol. Soybean seeds were disinfected by treating them with 95% ethanol for 10 s followed by 3% sodium hypochlorite for 3 min and then rinsed six times with sterile distilled water to remove any traces of the sterilizing agents. The sterilized seeds were germinated on 1% water agar, and three germinated seedlings were aseptically transferred into each pot containing the soil. Plants were grown under natural light with a 12-h photoperiod. After 45 days, they were harvested to assess nodulation and collect root nodules. The nodules were placed in vials containing silica gel and cotton, and stored at 4 °C until further processing47. Large, healthy nodules were chosen for rhizobial isolation48. 81 root nodule bacteria were isolated from the soil and authenticated using modified Leonard Jars (MLJ) as previously adopted by Gunnabo et al. (2021)49. Of the 81 bacterial isolates, 44 strains were verified as soybean-nodulating rhizobia by re-infection test of the host plant. The rhizobia strains were subsequently screened for symbiotic effectiveness in the greenhouse.

Nodulation and symbiotic efficiency test in the greenhouse

Based on preliminary nodulation and symbiotic performance, soybean-nodulating rhizobial strains were selected for further evaluation of their symbiotic effectiveness in soil culture under greenhouse conditions, with the aim of identifying the most promising strains for field experiments. The study was conducted in the greenhouse facility at the Abaya campus of AMU, South Ethiopia. Soybean was planted in plastic pots (30 cm diameter × 35 cm depth) that had been surface-sterilized by soaking in 4% sodium hypochlorite overnight48. The pots were filled with experimental soil collected from the Gamo and Gardula Zones and moistened to field capacity for one week prior to planting. These pots were used for growing soybean and screening the rhizobial strains based on the growth performance of inoculated plants. The rhizobial strains were grown in YEM broth following the procedure described in previous research47,50, the cultures were adjusted to approximately 10⁹ CFU/mL using a spectrophotometer (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.8–1.0). On the day of planting, 100 mL of the standardized broth culture was mixed with 300 g of pre-sterilized, locally sourced coal used as a carrier material. Soybean seeds were surface-sterilized and coated with the rhizobial inoculant using a sugar solution as an adhesive47,48. The inoculated seeds were then sown three seeds per pot and grown for 45 days under greenhouse conditions before harvesting. Alongside the test strains, a commercial rhizobial strain (Bradyrhizobium japonicum) from Menagesha Biotech Industry (MBI) was included as a positive control, while un-inoculated plants served as the negative control. All treatments were arranged in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications and managed uniformly with respect to watering and weeding. After 45 days, the plants were carefully uprooted and evaluated for nodulation, plant growth, and symbiotic effectiveness47. Field experiments

Inoculant preparation

The selected rhizobia strains were cultivated to the exponential growth phase (109 cells/ mL) on Yeast extract Mannitol Broth (YMB)48. The broth culture was gently mixed with fine powdered, pre-sterilized coal in ratio of a 100 ml: 30 g (v/w) based on moisture holding capacity of the coal35. Packaging was done in sterile plastic bag and covered by opaque pollen bag and maintained in a clean dry area or refrigerator until used. Five to ten grams of coal based rhizobial inoculants have been used for a kg of soybean seed coating54 with a 10% sugar solution32. The inoculant coated seeds were shade-dried for 20 to 30 min before sowing to prevent clumping and maintain viability48.

Experimental treatments and design

From the greenhouse study, three top performing rhizobia strains (SB19, SB22, and SB24), along with the commercial strain (SBcom), were chosen for field trials at AMU demo farm and Abaya campus farm in 2023. The second field experiment was carried out at Dambile and Arguba in 2024 growing season. In the field evaluation, maize served as a benchmark crop for estimating N-derived from the atmosphere (Ndfa) because it has a similar N-use efficiency to numerous legumes55,56. A total of 15 experimental plots were set up in three blocks for the field trial, after suitable land preparation for soybean. Every plot measured 4 m x 3 m (12m2)57, with seeds planted at 10 cm apart within the rows and 40 cm between the rows38,58. All the treatments were replicated three times and organized in a randomized complete block design (RCBD)32,49 and conducted in actual locations across cropping seasons. Coal-based inoculants were coated onto the seeds as stated earlier and were applied in the field at the rate mentioned above. To prevent cross-contamination, plots of non-inoculated seeds were sown initially, followed by the inoculated ones. Agronomic practices like crop weeding and management were consistent across all treatments during the cultivation period52,58. Nodulation assessment was conducted at the early flowering stage, when approximately 10% of plants had their first flowers, by carefully uprooting five plants randomly chosen from the central rows of each plot38. The plants were evaluated for nodulation, plant growth (in dry matter (g)), and nitrogen obtained from the atmosphere (Ndfa) from each plot. Ndfa was determined using the N difference approach, which assesses the total shoot N of the inoculated legumes against that of a non-legume reference plant52,59. Plant sampling and harvesting

In the greenhouse experiment, plants were harvested at 60 days. Shoots were dried at 70 °C for 48 h, and nodule numbers were counted for each plant. The dried shoots were then weighed to determine shoot dry mass. For the field experiments, five soybean plants were randomly sampled from the designated sampling rows at the stage when about 10% of plants had begun flowering, to assess nodule numbers. Similarly, at harvest maturity, the number of pods per plant was determined using five randomly selected plants within the net plot area, and the average pod count per plant was calculated. The mean number of seeds per pod was obtained by dividing the total number of seeds by the total number of pods. All plants within the net plot area were harvested and sun-dried until reaching a constant weight, after which aboveground biomass was measured. The harvested samples were then threshed and manually cleaned, and the seeds were air-dried to a uniform moisture content of 11%, which was verified using a moisture meter61. Hundred-seed weight (g) was determined by weighing 100 randomly selected seeds on an electronic balance. Grain yield per plot was recorded simultaneously, converted to a per-hectare basis, and expressed in tons per hectare38,58.

Statistical analysis

The greenhouse and field trial experiments data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the R software package 4.5.2. Averages for all the treatments were computed, and the differences were assessed and considered significant when p ≤ 0.05. The treatments were differentiated using the least significant difference (LSD) test.

Results

Strain source and soil physiochemical properties of the study area

A total of 81 soil bacteria were obtained from Arba Minch, Arguba, Kucha, Dambile Ossa, Dale, and Boricha sites. Out of the 81 isolates only 44 were identified as rhizobia strains based on hot plant re-infection test (authentication) experiment using MLJs (Table S1). Majority of the isolates were trapped from Boricha and Dale sites, probably because these two locations had significantly higher amount of available P, total N, organic carbon and active potassium (Table S1). The other locations had relatively small population of indigenous rhizobia due to poor adoption of the soybean plant in the region.

Screening effective rhizobia strains in a greenhouse

A greenhouse screening experiment revealed that all the 44 new rhizobia isolate induced nodulation on the soybean variety Nyala. The highest nodule number (90) was induced by the rhizobia isolate SB45 while the lowest (7) was produced by the strain SB47. The nodules had pink to red internal color when cut open, showing their strong nitrogen fixation activity and on average (µ) had 40.93 number of active nodules per plant. The commercial strains formed a significantly (p < 0.05) lower nodule number than the newly isolated strains SB24, SB23, SB22 and SB19 but had better nodulation than other isolates (Fig. 1a), showing the presence of competitive strains in the soil. However, the largest nodule dry matter (a relatively better indicator of symbiotic effectiveness) was induced by the commercial strain (Fig. 1b), maintaining its potency. Effects of inoculation varies by location and season

From the 2023 field experiment, the top performing strain SB19 and the commercial strain B. japonicum were further evaluated at AMU demo farm, Abaya experimental field, Arguba and Dambile sites during 2024 growing season. As shown in Fig. 3a and b, the highest nodulation (NN) and N2-fixation (Ndfa) were induced by SB19 across all locations. Surprisingly, nodulation was not observed in control plants, showing that the sites had no ingenious rhizobia compatible with the soybean. On the other hand, rhizobia inoculation induced a significantly highest (p < 0.05) nodule number in Dambile (35.22) and Abaya (34.78) than it elicited at Arguba (29.44) or AMU demo (18.78) sites (Table S2). In all cases, the commercial strain produced a significantly lower nodule number than the SB19 (Fig. 3a). In terms of N2-fixation, still SB19 outperformed the commercial strain in all locations. Nodulation and N2-fixation are often accompanied by gain in shoot dry matter or plant biomass. Accordingly, SB19 still revealed the highest shoot dry weight accumulation in all locations compared to the commercial inoculant and the controls (Fig. 3c). The lowest plant biomass values were observed at Dambile, while the highest was recorded from AMU demo site. Grain yields followed the same trend, with SB19 producing the highest yield (2.45 tons ha-1) at AMU demo farm (Table S2), followed by the commercial inoculant and the control in 2024 growing season. Regarding seasonal variation, the Ndfa induced by the rhizobia inoculation was significantly higher (p < 0.05) in 2023 (39.51%) than the amount fixed in 2024 (34.65%). But the number of nodules formed was not statistically different seasonally. The fixed nitrogen consequently accumulated higher shoot dry weight in 2023 than it was in 2024 (Table S2). Unlike the shoot dry weights, the average grain yield did not differ significantly from season to season, probably indicating the consistent performance of the strains across seasons. In general, this result clearly demonstrates the superior performance of strain SB19 in enhancing nodulation, plant growth, N2-fixation, and yield under different environmental conditions.

Discussion

In the current study, we investigated the symbiotic performance of the locally isolated rhizobia strains nodulating soybean to enhance their nodulation, N2-fixation, plant growth and yield in lowlands of South Ethiopia Region. Initially, we isolated 81 root nodule bacteria from different locations and identified 44 of the isolates as authentic rhizobial strains using MLJs. Further evaluation of the 44 strains gave top performing rhizobia strains (SB19, SB22 and SB24) that were considered as elite. These strains and the commercial inoculant strain B. japonicum were again evaluated under field conditions in different locations and seasons. Among the isolates SB19 was found to be the top effective strain in terms of nodulation, N2-fixation, plant growth and yield improvement. This strain was consistently performing over the commercial inoculant and controls across locations and seasons, becoming a promising candidate for local inoculant development. Below we discussed the role of this strain and its potential for developing as an inoculant to be used in the region or beyond.

Ethiopia has been considered as a hot spot for rhizobia diversity and symbiotic effectiveness62 that would enable exploring symbiotically effective strains for local use or beyond. Since the response of legumes to rhizobia inoculation and the benefit from the use of inoculants partly depends on the strains, agronomic performances and environment63,64, targeting of the indigenous rhizobia strains for location and/or host specific applications has been recommended65. Accordingly, in the current analysis, we explored for rhizobia strains nodulating soybean in South Ethiopia region and recovered about 44 compatible strains. We noted that recovery of rhizobia strains from soil can be influenced by location since majority (61.36%) of isolates were trapped from Boricha and Dale from Sidam Region. These locations were known to contain a relatively higher amount of soil available P, total nitrogen, organic carbon and active potassium. Besides, they were also known by better adoption of soybean cultivation in the region25. In other terms, the adoption of legumes in a given area enhances the likelihood of recovering diverse indigenous rhizobial populations from the soil. In fact, isolation protocols adopted to different laboratories were also contributing to the possible variation in rhizobia population recovery from the soil.

It has been a common trend and a golden standard to screen strains for symbiotic effectiveness after confirming their nodule eliciting potential on the host plant. Nodulation, N2-fixation and plant biomass are the key parameters that are often measured during examining the symbiotic effectiveness of the strains. According to67, the commonly used parameter to estimate the symbiotic effectiveness of the strains is the amount of plant shoot dry matter accumulated. Hence, in the greenhouse experiment, all the 44 strains were evaluated in terms of plant nodulation, shoot dry matter accumulation and symbiotic effectiveness. In this analysis, locally isolated strains were found symbiotically more effective than the commercial inoculant and the control plants. This supports that local strains could be more effective than the introduced commercial strains due to competition from naturalized populations and co-adaptation with the host plants68,69. However, this was not always the case as65, reported that the reference and commercial strain Rhizobium tropici CIAT 899 was still the best performing inoculant across genetically varying common bean varieties. The variability in terms of the research outcomes reported is not narrowed down since the results are dependent on dynamic environmental conditions. Thus, the top performing new rhizobia isolates and the commercial strain were further evaluated under field conditions.

The current field trial sites were characterized by marginally acidic soils, a pH range regarded as suitable for agricultural yield according to Murphy (1968)66, so the performance of the strains under those conditions were not expected to differ due to the pH variability. The key soil fertility defining nutrients such as the total nitrogen, available phosphorus, active potassium and organic carbon varied from location to location and found low in terms of their requirement for the plant growth and yield that also supports previous reports70,71. On the other hand, it has been well established that proper range of cation exchange capacity and good amount of organic carbon in the soil is important to supply essential plant nutrients (N, P, K) to the field standing crops and maintain the soil health70,71. Nutrient variation and poor soil fertility status in the trial sites could suffice crop performance differences that limits productivity of soybean and other crops72, calling for the use of bio-fertilizer to enhance the crop yield and maintain the soil health.

Soybean was introduced to some parts of Ethiopia in 1950s50, and serve as a host for various symbiotic bacteria that enhance soil fertility in the country73–75. The strains that we trapped using the soybean from some parts of the South Ethiopia and described above also dictates the presence of soybean compatible and naturalized rhizobia in soil. However, in locations where soybeans did not yet adopt needs inoculation of the plant with rhizobia. It is clear that inoculating soybean with high-performing N2-fixing rhizobia in areas where symbiotic bacteria are lacking have been known to increase crop yield and contributes to minimizing the environmental effects associated with synthetic N-fertilizers58. The current study demonstrated a successful establishment of an effective N2-fixing symbiosis between the soybean variety Nyala and the new rhizobia isolates, supporting earlier reports claiming availability of soybean nodulating rhizobia in Ethiopian soils38,76. In all the field trials, soybean inoculation contributed to enhanced nodulation, N2-fixing, plant growth and yield, supporting previous reports31,74,78. In both 2023 and 2024, soybean response to treatments showed a consistent pattern across all locations and seasons, with SB19 outperforming the commercial inoculant and the control in terms of nodulation, N2-fixation, shoot dry weight, and grain yield. In 2023, all parameters recorded relatively higher values, indicating more favorable growing conditions; SB19 produced the highest average grain yield at all sites, reaching its peak at AMU demo (3.1 tones ha-1), followed by Abaya (2.92 tones ha-1). Similarly, the commercial strain B. japonicum significantly improved all traits when compared to the control (un-inoculated plots) but remained inferior to SB19. In 2024, SB19 strain resulted in average grain yields of 2.39 and 2.45 tons ha-1 at Abaya and AMU demo sites, respectively. Although there was a decline in all measured parameters across locations and seasons, with SB19 still leading. The average grain yield of the commercial inoculant ranged from 2.31 to 2.40 tons ha⁻1 in 2023 and from 2.25 to 2.38 tons ha⁻1 in 2024. However, the uninoculated control plots produced lower yields, ranging from 1.89 to 2.02 tons ha⁻1 in 2023 and 1.87 to 1.91 tons ha⁻1 in 2024. Therefore, inoculation with indigenous rhizobia strains led to higher grain yields than the controls across all field locations and seasons.

The new native rhizobia isolate SB19 was better than the commercial inoculant, backing the report by Wilson et al. (2021)79, who showed that native rhizobia strains improved groundnut seed yield than the commercial inoculants in Northern Ghana. This may be likely due to the better adaptation and competitiveness of native rhizobia in local soils, enabling effective nodulation and N2-fixation. Others claimed that the commercial strains performed poorly due to limited adaptation, climatic stress, and competition from indigenous microorganisms80. The current analysis corroborated with a report by Temesgen and Assefa (2020)38, who indicated that inoculated soybean plants exhibited enhanced soybean nodulation, growth and yield in Ethiopia. Moreover, this result corresponds with the observation made by Assefa et al. (2017)81, who found that rhizobia inoculation led to increase in seeds per pod, pods per plant, plant height, and hundred-seed weight in beans. Similarly,82,83 provided success stories highlighting how rhizobia inoculation significantly improved symbiotic performance as well as the yield and yield components of beans. The maximum gain yield in the current analysis was 3.1 tons ha-1, which was higher than that reported by Belete et al. (2019)84 (2.56 tons ha-1) and Abera et al. (2015)85 (2.52 tons ha-1). A much higher yield (3.98 tons ha−1) than the current result was reported by38 from both Bako and Debrezeit Agricultural Research Centers in Ethiopia, showing the presence of huge yield gap from report to report. Higher grain yields (up to 3.70 tons ha-1) were also documented from other countries such as United States and Argentina86.

Considering specific cases of the current analysis, a slight reduction in grain yield of plants inoculated with SB19 as well as commercial strain in 2024 might be due to variations in temperature, rainfall distribution and soil fertility in Arguba and Dambile. This finding was consistent with the results of87, which indicated that rainfall variability and temperature variations are the most serious climatic factors influencing crop production, growth, and yield. Similarly, Tumbure et al. (2025)88 and Mirriam et al.(2023)89 reported that low crop yields were associated with poor soil fertility. However, Merkeb et al. (2016)90 and Mirriam et al.(2023)89 reported lower yields were due to limited P availability. Based on the findings of this study, soybean yields varied by experimental locations, which may be attributed to the differences in how well strains adapt to varying local conditions. Across all experimental sites, soybean plants showed statistically significant increases in nodule formation, shoot biomass, hundred-seed weight, plant height, and grain yield. These improvements are likely due to the effectiveness of the strains as the indigenous rhizobia inoculated plots outperformed both the commercial inoculant and the uninoculated control plots, regardless of locations.

Conclusions

In this study 44 rhizobial strains were initially screened under greenhouse conditions, from which three strains exhibited outstanding performance across all evaluated parameters and were subsequently selected for field evaluation. The results from the field trials confirmed that indigenous rhizobial strains can be effectively exploited for inoculant development, particularly when they outperform the currently used commercial inoculant. Among the evaluated strains, SB19 consistently demonstrated superior performance across all field sites, markedly enhancing soybean growth, shoot dry weight, biological nitrogen fixation, and grain yield. These findings underscore the strong potential of native soybean-associated rhizobia to substantially improve grain productivity and nitrogen inputs through biological fixation. Such an approach represents a sustainable farming strategy that can reduce reliance on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers. Importantly, this strategy offers clear economic and environmental benefits, especially for resource-constrained smallholder farmers with limited access to commercial agricultural inputs. Therefore, further research is warranted to narrow existing yield gaps through the development of commercially viable and ecologically efficient inoculants derived from these strains, alongside multi-season and multi-location trials to validate their consistency and adaptability across diverse soybean-growing areas in South Ethiopia and beyond.
