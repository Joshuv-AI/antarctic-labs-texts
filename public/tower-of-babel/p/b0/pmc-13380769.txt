Domain-general and language-specific brain networks are differentially associated with verbal intelligence during development
Authors: Zihang Zhou, Xiaohong Yang, Ping Ju, Mengjie Rong, Elizabeth Jefferies, Xi-Nian Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnderstanding how verbal intelligence develops requires clarifying the roles of domain-general and language-selective brain systems. Here, we examined this question in an accelerated longitudinal cohort of children and adolescents from Beijing (N = 170) by relating gray matter volume measures to the verbal comprehension index (VCI) of the Wechsler intelligence scale, a widely used measure of verbal intelligence. We observed that individual differences in VCI were more strongly associated with structural maturation of domain-general networks, including the default mode (DM) and multiple-demand (MD) systems, than with the language-selective network, and that these effects varied with age. Independent validation in a Chongqing cohort (N = 150) confirmed significant contributions of domain-general networks during adolescence (13–15 years). These findings highlight a previously underappreciated role of domain-general cortical systems in the development of verbal intelligence during adolescence and provide new insight into the neural basis of abstract verbal reasoning across development.

Introduction

Language competence is a hallmark of human cognition; yet, the neural systems that support it during development remain a matter of ongoing discussion. Prior work describes two broad perspectives: (1) a domain-specific view,1,2 which proposes that language relies on a dedicated cognitive domain that is independent of intelligence and (2) a domain-general view,3,4 which argues that the application of language in complex contexts recruits domain-general systems such as the default mode (DM) and multiple-demand (MD) networks. Collectively, these perspectives suggest that multiple large-scale networks may support different aspects of language competence, particularly in complex processing tasks.

Evidence from previous work indicates that language processing primarily engages a domain-specific, left-lateralized language-selective network, which consistently demonstrates a high degree of functional specificity with minimal neural overlap with other cognitive processes.2,5,6 Yet, recent studies also recognize that language competence extends beyond basic language comprehension and production—instead, language serves as a tool for achieving goals and building relationships in real-world situations. Such demands have been associated with increased involvement of domain-general systems, particularly in the MD network, which supports cognitive control and effortful processing,3,7 and the DM network, which contributes to discourse comprehension8 and goal-related semantic information.9,10,11

Although prior research has identified contributions from both language-selective and domain-general systems during language processing, findings vary in the extent to which domain-general networks are involved during linguistic operations. Some evidence supports a limited role of general-purpose cognitive systems like the MD and the DM network in language, while others highlight their importance under conditions requiring higher-order integration. This apparent variability in findings may stem from how language processing is defined and measured: tasks focusing on basic linguistic operations may proceed independently of domain-general systems during routine linguistic tasks, while verbal abilities grounded in more generalized intelligence may place heavier demands on domain-general cognitive resources.7

To better capture these higher-level demands, the present study employed the Wechsler verbal comprehension index (VCI, see Figure 1), a standardized measure of verbal intelligence that assesses expressive vocabulary, verbal reasoning, and acquired knowledge.12,13,14 Unlike tasks using artificially constrained stimuli or passive comprehension paradigms, these tests allowed us to examine how domain-general systems support broader linguistic development beyond narrow processing definitions, providing insight into the neural bases underlying verbal intellectual development.Figure 1The VCI and its subtest measurements

Compared with adults, children and adolescents have relatively lower language proficiency,15,16 yet the predominant focus on adults in prior research limits our understanding of whether children and adolescents may recruit additional resources from domain-general networks to compensate for these immaturities in verbal abilities17 or primarily rely on domain-specific language activation.18 In this study, we aim to map the developmental variation in cooperation between domain-general networks and the language network from childhood through adolescence.

We analyzed two accelerated longitudinal datasets: the devCCNP-PEK Sample from Beijing (n = 170) in our primary analysis and the devCCNP-CKG Sample from Chongqing (n = 150) for the validation of key findings (Table 1). We measured gray matter volumes (GMVs) within the language, MD, and DM networks (Figure 2) and related these structural measures to performance on the VCI (along with the scores of its subtests). Next, we modeled developmental trajectories of both network volumes and the VCI, testing their age-related dynamic associations. Given that VCI assesses higher-order verbal abilities integrating language knowledge with reasoning and executive functions,12,13,14 and considering the established roles of the MD and DM networks in executive control19,20 and semantic representation,9,10 we hypothesized that individual differences in VCI would be positively associated with structural maturation in these domain-general networks. We remained agnostic, however, about the relative strength of these associations compared to those with the domain-specific language network across different developmental periods, treating this as a key open question for our longitudinal data to address.

For network-level GMVs, AIC indicated that the best-fitting model was the one including the sex term (see Table 2). GAMM-based estimates revealed a significant non-linear decreasing trend for the development of GMV across three large-scale networks (language: p < 0.001; MD: p < 0.001; DM: p < 0.001, see Figure 3; Table 2). This non-linear trend indicates a complex maturation process, characterized by an initial increase in volume followed by a pronounced reduction, reflecting the brain’s dynamic restructuring during critical developmental phases. We also used Fisher’s z test in a post-hoc step to reveal differences in age-related development among the three networks. The MD network demonstrated a more rapid decline in GMV (r = −0.240) across childhood and adolescence than the other two networks (language network: Z = 4.02, p = 0.000; DM network: Z = 7.207, p = 0.000). Post-hoc volume-wise GAMMs further indicated that both the left and right hemispheric volumes obtained separately from the three networks showed similar nonlinear developmental patterns (see Figure S13).

GMVs in both the domain-specific language network and domain-general MD network and DM network showed significant positive associations with the VCI in younger children (the language network: p < 0.05; the MD network: p < 0.05; the DM network: p < 0.05, Table 3). Notably, this attenuation may reflect shared variance between verbal and non-verbal abilities or potential over-control, rather than indicating a lack of verbal relevance.

Between ages 13 and 15, GMVs in domain-general networks showed robust positive associations with the VCI (the MD network: p < 0.05; the DM network: p < 0.05) For GAM estimates, see Table 3; detailed information regarding hemispheric results is provided in Figures S14 and S15; Tables S7 and S8. These associations remained significant after controlling for non-verbal intelligence (the MD network: p < 0.01; the DM network: p < 0.05). This may suggest that, in older adolescents, the brain-verbal ability associations are less attributable to shared variance with non-verbal cognitive abilities.

Analyses of the PEK data indicate that the results for adolescence are the most robust, remaining significant even after controlling for non-verbal intelligence. To further validate the robustness of these findings, we examined the same age window using the CKG dataset. GAMM analyses demonstrated significant associations between the VCI and GMVs in all three networks during adolescence (the language network: p = 0.04; the MD network: p = 0.04; the DM network: p = 0.04), as shown in Figure 5 and Table 3. After controlling for non-verbal intelligence, the associations between the VCI and GMVs in the MD network (p = 0.05) and the DM network (p = 0.05) remained significant, while the association with the language network was not significant (p = 0.07). This implies that, during early adolescence, further enhancement in the ability to use language for reasoning and expression is more closely related to GMVs in domain-general networks. However, the form of this relationship differed: while the PEK data showed a linear positive association, the CKG cohort exhibited a non-linear (inverted U-shaped) relationship, with the highest verbal intelligence scores associated with intermediate levels of GMV. This suggests that the association between domain-general networks and verbal intelligence may not scale linearly with volume across all individuals or populations, potentially following the non-linear developmental trajectory of verbal intelligence observed in the CKG sample (see Figure S2 for details).

To assess the robustness of these findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses controlling for individual differences in image quality by including the weighted average quality score as a covariate (see Tables S11 and S12). The results remained significant after accounting for image quality. To further examine potential global brain size confounds, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses using different correction approaches. In the PEK sample, the effects were attenuated after controlling for total intracranial volume (TIV; ps > 0.43, see Table S13) or total GMV alone (ps > 0.19, see Table S14), but remained significant when using the ratio-based measure total GMV/TIV (children aged between 6 and 8 years: the language network: p < 0.05; the MD network: p < 0.01; the DM network: p < 0.01; adolescents aged between 13 and 15 years: the MD network: p = 0.01; the DM network: p = 0.01, see Table S15), supporting network-specific associations with verbal intelligence. In the CKG sample, these associations remained significant across all correction approaches (ps ≤ 0.05).

Results for exploratory analyses

Results for the EvLab MD system

Parallel analyses were performed using GMV extracted from the EvLab MD system. The results largely replicated our main findings regarding the dynamic associations between MD-system GMV and verbal intelligence during two developmental periods, namely early childhood aged six to eight years and adolescence aged 12 to 15 years (see Figure S8). The EvLab MD system showed significant associations with individual differences in verbal intelligence within the corresponding developmental windows (see Tables S3). Relative weight analyses using the EvLab MD system further showed broadly consistent patterns, with the EvLab MD system accounting for a notable proportion of the explained variance in the VCI in both developmental periods (see Figure S9; Table S4). Overall, these findings support the robustness of the main MD-network results across alternative network definitions.

Results with the limbic network as a control system

TVEM analysis with the limbic system as a control network revealed relatively weak correlations between GMV and the VCI (see Figure S11). GAM estimates revealed no significant associations between limbic-system GMV and verbal intelligence in early school-age children aged 6 to 7 years (the VCI: p = 0.178) or in adolescents aged 13 to 15 years (the vocabulary subtest: p = 0.324; see Table S5). These results highlight the specificity of our main results.

Results for the audiovisual integration task

GAM estimates revealed marginally significant correlations between performance in the audiovisual integration task and both the language network and the MD network during childhood, with the language network exhibiting substantially larger relative weights (relative weights: 0.02, importance: 65%) compared to the MD network (relative weights: 0.01, importance: 35%). During adolescence, the language network emerged as the only network showing a significant association with audiovisual integration before correction. Detailed statistical results are provided in Table S6. These results demonstrate that the language network is associated with specific language functions. The strong associations of the MD network and the DM network reported above are likely attributable to our use of the VCI as the measure of verbal intelligence linked to higher-order thought.

Discussion

This study characterizes how structural changes within cognitive networks track with individual differences in verbal intelligence over development. Rather than adjudicating the long-standing discussion about domain-specific versus domain-general mechanisms, our goal was to delineate the developmental timing and structural correlates of these associations. Using two large-scale longitudinal cohorts, we found that verbal intelligence develops in association with structural maturation within both domain-specific and domain-general networks, with the strength of these associations varying across age. Notably, domain-general systems exhibit stronger associations during key windows of adolescence, suggesting that developmental timing playing a critical role in shaping brain-behavior relationships. These findings highlight that the linkage between cortical maturation and verbal intelligence is not static, but dynamically unfolds across development, providing insight into the temporal and structural characteristics of how higher-order cognitive systems relate to verbal intelligence.

Our exploratory findings from TVEM show that GMV was associated with verbal intelligence only during specific developmental windows, rather than consistently across the full span of childhood and adolescence. These findings are consistent with prior research indicating major changes in intelligence21,22,23 and language functions24,25 that originate in childhood and extend well into adolescence. Importantly, the nature of this association differed across cohorts. In the PEK cohort, GMV in the MD and the DM networks showed positive linear associations with performance on the VCI. In contrast, the CKG cohort exhibited a nonlinear inverted U-shaped pattern. This pattern likely arises in part from variations in the developmental trajectory of verbal intelligence itself. The VCI followed a relatively linear developmental trajectory in the PEK cohort, whereas it exhibited a more nonlinear pattern in the CKG cohort (see Figure S2). Such pattern can lead to coexistence of positive and negative associations within the same age window, thereby producing an inverted U-shaped pattern. However, other factors, including regional differences in educational context and individual variability in cortical maturation, may also contribute to this asynchrony. Together, these findings highlight that the relationship between brain structure and verbal intelligence is dynamic and can vary across populations. Importantly, despite differences in functional form, the key associations with domain-general networks during adolescence (13–15 years) remained consistent across both cohorts. We note, however, that the sources of this cross-cohort variation warrant further investigation in future studies.

The strong association between domain-general networks and verbal intelligence across both cohorts likely reflects two key factors: the nature of the verbal tasks and the developmental stage of the participants in our study. The VCI includes subtests that go beyond basic linguistic decoding to tap verbal reasoning, conceptual knowledge, and semantic understanding.14,26 Verbal concept information and verbal reasoning relate to the way that language is deployed in everyday situations, consistent with the role of general-purpose resources to answer questions.3,27,28 These broader demands are consistent with the observed involvement of the MD and DM networks, which support flexible, goal-directed cognition and semantic integration. Children and adolescents might rely more heavily on these general-purpose systems for language processing than adults,29 whose neural architecture may afford more efficient or specialized language pathways. This interpretation aligns with previous evidence that language networks in youth are less functionally specialized than in adulthood, showing more diffuse activation30,31 and greater power across broad EEG bands.32 In line with these findings, a systematic review33 and developmental studies34 have highlighted ongoing interactions between language development and non-linguistic functions including executive control, memory, and social cognition. Taken together, our finding supports the view that in earlier stages of development, verbal intelligence depends not only on language-selective regions but also on the broader scaffolding of domain-general cognitive systems.3

Crucially, the interpretation of positive associations between GMV and verbal intelligence requires careful consideration in a developmental context. During adolescence, GMV typically follows a declining trajectory due to synaptic pruning and neuromaturational processes. Therefore, a positive brain-behavior association at a single time point does not necessarily imply that more tissue is better from a developmental perspective. Instead, such an association may reflect higher baseline volume in individuals with better intelligence,35,36 delayed maturation relative to age-based norms,37 or distinct patterns of thinning that are not captured by a single time point.38 These possibilities highlight that cross-sectional or age-varying associations between brain structure and cognition cannot be straightforwardly interpreted in terms of “more is better,” but rather should be understood within the broader framework of neurodevelopmental timing and maturation.

A related consideration concerns how we constructed the domain-general network metric. We acknowledge that aggregating the salience/ventral attention, the frontoparietal control, and the dorsal attention subnetworks into a composite measure may obscure potential functional heterogeneity across these subnetworks, despite their well-established functional synergy in supporting cognitively demanding, goal-directed cognition.39 As a result, it remains unclear whether the observed association with verbal intelligence reflects the collective contribution of all subnetworks or is disproportionately driven by specific subsystems, such as the frontoparietal control network, given its role in task adaptation and implementation40 and its distributed functional coupling with other brain networks, which positively correlates with higher intelligence scores.41 Nevertheless, the greater weight we observed for the MD system as defined in our primary results is unlikely to be influenced by this aggregation. This is because we also incorporated the MD regions defined by Fedorenko et al.42 and applied the same analysis, which yielded comparable results (see Figures S8 and S9; Tables S3 and S4). With these considerations in mind, we interpret the composite network findings as reflecting the broad association of domain-general MD systems, while acknowledging that finer-grained analyses at the subnetwork level could provide additional insights.

A separate question is whether the observed associations might simply reflect overall brain maturation. This interpretation, however, is contradicted by several lines of evidence. First, if the observed effects were entirely driven by overall brain maturation, similar patterns would be expected across all functional networks. Yet, we found dissociable developmental effects, with associations preserved in the DM and the MD networks during adolescence but absent in the language network. Second, the limbic control system showed no association with the VCI (see Table S5). Third, the three verbal comprehension subtests (see Tables S1 and S2) and an audiovisual integration task (see Table S6) revealed distinct patterns of network contributions. Finally, sensitivity analyses showed that in the PEK sample, the associations remained significant after controlling for the total GMV/TIV ratio; in the CKG sample, they remained significant across all correction approaches (see Tables S13–S15). Together, these findings support the interpretation that the observed effects reflect regionally specific neurodevelopmental processes. Nevertheless, given that the associations in the PEK sample were no longer significant when controlling for TIV or total GMV alone, a contribution of global maturation cannot be fully excluded.

Our study also highlights how linguistic and cognitive task demands impact the relative importance of the domain-specific language network and domain-general cognitive networks. Through subtest analyses, we found that the Vocabulary and Similarities subtests, which tap lexicon and word knowledge, show the language network as having the largest relative weight. In contrast, the Comprehension subtest, which assess higher-level verbal skills involving practical knowledge and judgment based on past experiences,43 shows a stronger association with domain-general factors. Moreover, in contrast to the prominence of general-purpose brain networks in our main analysis, an audiovisual integration task identified the language network as having the strongest association with performance. This aligns with previous studies showing functional specificity of the language-selective network in tasks that focus on specific linguistic domains.2,28 In contrast, the broader verbal intelligence assessed by the VCI navigates complex social situations, is better captured by the combined involvement of language-selective and domain-general networks.44 This distinction demonstrates that different facets of verbal skills draw on distinct neural architectures and, more broadly, highlights the importance of considering both specialized and distributed neural systems when investigating the neural basis of verbal intelligence.

It is important to note that the association between the VCI and these domain-general networks is consistent with prior work, given their established roles in executive reasoning and in supporting higher-order cognitive functions.45,46 As such, this association does not in itself constitute the primary novelty of the present findings. Rather, the present study provides a complementary contribution by examining this relationship from a developmental and structural perspective. Using two longitudinal datasets spanning childhood and adolescence, we show that the involvement of domain-general systems in verbal comprehension emerges or stabilizes during a distinct developmental period, revealing developmental windows during which domain-general networks show stronger links to performance, and that individual differences in this relationship are tied to specific gray matter structural properties. Together, these findings offer incremental evidence on how higher-order verbal intelligence are dynamically associated with multiple neural networks over the course of development, with the key contribution lying in delineating the developmental timing and structural correlates of these associations.

Given that the role of domain-general systems in verbal intelligence may vary across developmental stages and task demands, further research is needed to establish the extent to which our findings would generalize to tasks with different demands, and whether the involvement of the MD and the DM networks reflects the executively challenging nature of verbal intelligence during childhood and adolescence. A more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between network-level brain development and verbal intelligence will likely require integrating a broader range of measures. These includes incorporating white matter connectivity,47 connectivity gradients,48,49 and more refined examinations of hemispheric specialization.50 Although our supplementary analyses revealed largely consistent effect patterns across hemispheres, the language network is known to be left-lateralized.51,52 Future studies with larger samples and focused lateralization designs may further clarify whether left-hemisphere language regions contribute uniquely to verbal intelligence beyond domain-general systems.

Limitations of the study

A key limitation of this study concerns the nature of our behavioral measure. The Wechsler VCI captures higher-order verbal intelligence and draws on generalized reasoning and knowledge, rather than isolating basic linguistic computations. Consequently, the associations observed with domain-general networks should be interpreted as reflecting the neural support of this broader form of verbal intelligence. Our findings therefore cannot be taken as evidence for, or against, claims regarding the domain-specificity of fundamental language mechanisms. In addition, because our analyses rely on structural MRI, the observed relationships are correlational and do not establish causal pathways between cortical maturation and verbal development. Another limitation concerns the cross-cohort comparison. Although the two cohorts were analyzed independently as replications, which mitigates concerns about scanner-driven artifacts, we cannot completely rule out contributions from site-related variability. Future studies with harmonized multisite data would be valuable for further validation of our findings. Finally, sensitivity analyses using different global-volume correction approaches yielded partially inconsistent results across methods in the PEK cohort. Although associations remained detectable after ratio-based correction, future studies using larger samples and alternative normalization strategies will be important for clarifying the extent to which these findings reflect regionally specific developmental effects versus broader maturational influences. These constraints should be taken into account when interpreting the generalizability and scope of the present findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

Data from 320 participants in the Chinese Color Nest Project (CCNP, 2013–2032) were used in the present study, which was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (The Ethical Approval Number: H18017). Prior to conducting the research, written informed consent was obtained from one of the participants’ legal guardians, and written assent was obtained from the participants. Participants who became adults in the longitudinal follow-up provided written consent once becoming 18 years old. The project comprised two samples: the devCCNP-PEK Sample (n = 170), collected in the Chaoyang District of Beijing, and the devCCNP-CKG Sample (n = 150), collected in the Beibei District of Chongqing.59

The CCNP employed an accelerated longitudinal design (ALD) representing a fusion of longitudinal and cross-sectional elements. This approach allows for the measurement of within-subject changes over time while also encompassing a wide age range in a manageable time frame.60,61 Originally, 480 typically developing children aged 6–18 years (20 boys and 20 girls in each cohort, 12 age cohorts in total) were invited to participate in three assessment times of data collection, referred to as three waves of visits.60 The interval between two neighboring waves was approximately 15 months to balance the season effects. The devCCNP-PEK Sample was collected from September 2017 to December 2022 and the devCCNP-CKG Sample was collected from March 2013 to January 2017.59

Visual inspection was carried out to eliminate scans exhibiting head-motion artifacts or those with structural abnormalities, resulting in the exclusion of 97 scans from 80 participants. Participants aged 16 and 17 years (4 in the devCCNP-PEK Sample, 27 in the devCCNP-CKG Sample) were excluded because the small number in the two cohorts provided insufficient statistical power for reliable developmental modeling, restricting the study to ages 6–15 years for robust time-varying and trajectory analyses. Additionally, 49 participants without test scores were excluded. After exclusion, a total of 585 scans from 320 participants (150 females) were included in follow-up analyses (see Table 1). Detailed sample distributions for both cohorts are provided in Figure S1.

Method details

Verbal intelligence

The present study utilized the Verbal Comprehension Index from the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-IV-Chinese Version58 as indicator of verbal intelligence. Specifically, participants were presented with three core subtests. The Vocabulary subtest examines participants’ knowledge of acquired word concepts through word definition. The Similarities subtest assesses participants’ ability to identify the commonality between two given objects or concepts with a brief description. This task is designed to measure participants’ abilities in the formation of language concepts, and in abstract reasoning. The Comprehension subtest was employed to test participants’ verbal reasoning and conceptualization, and their ability to demonstrate practical knowledge and judgment according to their understanding of real-life situations and social norms.43 Detailed information regarding the contents and formats of these tests is provided in Figure 1. The subtests were measured one-on-one, and participants’ responses were manually scored (raw scores) by experimenters and then normalized (normative scores) to produce the Verbal Comprehension Index. All experimenters completed training for WISC-IV scoring. Descriptive statistics for behavioral measures for the whole group across all waves are listed in Table 1.

MRI data acquisition

T1-weighted MRI data for the PEK Sample were collected on a 3.0-T GE Discovery MR750 scanner with an eight channel array head coil at CAS Institute of Psychology. Images were collected using a 3D T1-weighted spoiled gradient recalled (SPGR) echo sequence with the following parameters: T1 = 450 ms; TR = 6.70 ms; TE = 2.90 ms; flip angle = 12°; image matrix = 256 × 256; FOV = 256 × 256 mm2; 176 sagittal slices; spatial resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Data in the CKG Sample were collected using a 3.0-T Siemens Trio MRI scanner with a 12 channel array head coil at the Center for Brain Imaging, Southwest University. The imaging protocol employed a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MP-RAGE) sequence with the following parameters: T1 = 900 ms; TR = 2600 ms; TE = 3.02 ms; flip angle = 8°; image matrix = 256 × 256; FOV = 256 × 256 mm2; 176 sagittal slices; spatial resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Each scanning site maintained identical protocols across all waves, while inter-site variations were preserved with parameter optimization ensuring comparable spatial and temporal resolutions.

MRI data processing

MRI preprocessing

Structural MRI data were preprocessed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12.8.2; https://neuro-jena.github.io/cat/) implemented in SPM 12 (Wellcome Center of Imaging Neuroscience, Institute of Neurology, UCL, London, UK; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). T1-weighted images were initially denoised using the SANLM algorithm (medium strength) and then segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) using the standard Tissue Probability Maps (TPMs). Spatial normalization was performed using the Fast Optimized Shooting registration62 to the MNI152NLin2009cAsym template, with a final voxel resolution of 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm3. Modulated GM images were generated by multiplying the segmented GM probability maps by the Jacobian determinants derived from the normalization step, thereby preserving the total amount of GM signal. The modulated images were then smoothed with an 8 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. All preprocessing steps were applied identically to images from both cohorts using the same software versions and preprocessing parameters, ensuring consistency across sites.

Image quality was assessed using the weighted average of image quality ratings (IQR) generated by CAT12, defined by spatial resolution, Brain Web Phantom (BWP) noise, and BWP bias. Each metric is expressed as a percentage with an accompanying letter grade (A–F). Scans with any metric rated below the satisfactory threshold (C; 0.75) were visually checked and manually excluded from further analysis. Descriptive statistics for the CAT12 quality metrics, including resolution, noise, bias, and weighted average (IQR), are presented by cohort and wave in Table 1. The distributions of these metrics across age and site are shown in Figure S3. A linear mixed-effects model revealed no significant difference in weighted average scores between the two scanning sites after controlling for age and sex (β = −0.0031, p = 0.08), indicating that image quality was comparable across cohorts. To account for the sensitivity of VBM to motion and quality control differences, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by including the weighted average quality score as a covariate. For details, see Tables S11 and S12.

Gray matter volume extraction

The “Estimate mean values inside ROI” function implemented in CAT12 was applied to extract gray matter volume values from the three networks for each participant and for each scanning wave. The three networks of interest were the language network, the MD network and the DM network. Volumes of these large-scale networks were determined using masks obtained from a recent study by Kong et al.63 on individual-specific 400-region parcellation from fMRI data, as shown in Figure 2.

Given the sophisticated process of intelligent thought and action associated with diverse cognitive demands, the multiple-demand activity pattern as revealed by convergent activation across multiple cognitive tasks in fMRI extends over a specific set of regions in prefrontal and posterior cortex,64,65 in particular, cortex in the ventral attention network,49 the salience network,66,67 the dorsal attention network,8 and the frontoparietal control network.68 These networks display considerable functional heterogeneity: the salience and the attention sub-networks are more biased toward bottom-up and top-down mechanisms of attention,69,70 while the control networks are involved in different types of executive control.71 Nevertheless, they collectively form an integrated system to support externally goal-directed behavior across diverse cognitive challenges.39,64 Therefore, we integrated the seven networks (Salience/VenAttn A; Salience/VenAttn B; Control A; Control B; Control C; Dorsal Attention A; Dorsal Attention B) from Kong et al.63 in our definition of the large-scale MD system. We also conducted exploratory analyses using the functionally defined MD regions provided by Fedorenko et al.42 Our main findings were successfully replicated; detailed results are presented in Tables S3 and S4. Gray matter volume in the DM network was determined by the combination of the three DM sub-networks (A, B and C).72,73 For more details about the areal-level cortical parcellation network used in the present study for network-level gray matter volume extraction, see Kong et al.63

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses

Gray matter volumes in the three large-scale networks were extracted to test for associations between brain network structure and verbal intelligence across childhood and adolescence. Data analyses and visualization were performed using SAS 9.4, and packages in R (R Core Team, 2014) respectively.

To investigate developmental associations between cortical morphology and verbal intelligence while minimizing the risk of circular inference, we employed an exploration-validation framework using two accelerated longitudinal datasets. The devCCNP-PEK Sample served as the primary cohort for modeling across childhood and adolescence (6–15 years), while the devCCNP-CKG Sample served as an independent validation cohort to test the generalizability of key findings, particularly in adolescence. The two datasets were analyzed separately to avoid site-related confounds.

Three analytical approaches were applied to address complementary aspects of the research question. First, developmental trajectory modeling characterizes the nonlinear growth patterns of network-level brain structures and verbal intelligence across development, capturing developmental dynamics such as periods of accelerated change or stabilization. Second, time-varying effect modeling examines how the strength of the brain–behavior association fluctuates continuously across age, exploring developmental periods (based on pointwise 95% confidence intervals) during which the link between cortical morphology and verbal intelligence is statistically significant. Both trajectory modeling and time-varying effect modeling were conducted solely on the PEK cohort, leveraging its larger sample to establish age-related patterns and dynamic associations for comprehensive analyses. Finally, within the age windows identified by TVEM, we performed confirmatory analyses using generalized additive mixed models (GAM/GAMM) for statistical inference and Relative Weight Analysis (RWA) to quantify the unique and relative explanatory contributions of the language, MD, and DM networks to VCI variance.

Together, these three methods form a cohesive analytical framework: trajectory modeling characterizes the nonlinear developmental patterns of verbal intelligence and gray matter volumes, time-varying effect modeling identifies when brain–behavior associations are strongest, and GAMM/RWA provides targeted statistical inference and decomposition of network importance during those critical windows.

To address potential circularity, exploratory TVEM window identification was restricted to the PEK discovery cohort; all confirmatory GAM/GAMM and RWA in these pre-specified windows were conducted in the PEK cohort, with the key adolescent findings (13–15 years) independently validated in the CKG cohort, which was not involved in any exploratory analyses or window selection. This separation of discovery and validation follows practices in developmental neuroimaging for enhancing reproducibility and generalizability.74 Validation in the 6–8 years window was not performed due to insufficient sample size in the CKG cohort and the less robust associations in the PEK cohort after controlling for non-verbal intelligence. Thus, validation efforts focused on adolescence to maximize generalizability.

Modeling developmental trajectories of verbal intelligence and GMV

To fully model differences in the growth curves of the Verbal Comprehension Index test scores and gray matter volumes on the basis of the precise estimation of curve characteristics, we employed the generalized additive (mixed) model (GAM/GAMM) in our data analyses using the mgcv55 package (version 1.9–4) in R (R Core Team, 2014), as this non-parametric regression method does not require a priori knowledge of the associations. GAMM is highly suitable for repeated measurements,75,76 accounting for both within-subject correlation over time and between-subject developmental variations in our accelerated longitudinal design. To more specifically model differences in age trajectories among network-level gray matter volumes and test scores, we employed the following set of GAMMs (see Equation 1) to detect age-related changes revealed by test scores and gray matter volumes (GMVs) separately:(Equation 1)scores,GMVs∼s(age)+(1|subject)scores,GMVs∼s(age)+sex+(1|subject)

In our models, the smoothing function s, employing a fixed degree of freedom and cubic B-splines, was set to have five knots. This number was determined to be optimal for our data, as it achieved a k-index close to one without substantial changes in model fit when adjusting the suspected k-value.55,77 This optimization was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). GAMM was fitted with different values of k ranging from three to ten, and five was determined to be optimal as it was sufficiently large to represent potential changes from best model fits, and sufficiently small to maintain computational efficiency.78 The first GAMM models gray matter volumes and test scores as smoothing functions of age. Given the evidence of significant sex differences in both intelligence structure and its underlying brain neurophysiological mechanisms,79,80 we established the second GAMM with sex as a covariate in the regression. The lowest AIC value was used to determine the best model, and the model-chosen was required to be significantly different from null. For descriptive purposes, developmental trajectories of the three subtest scores were estimated using the same GAMM framework and are presented in Figure S4.

We also performed analyses to test whether there are significant differences in developmental rates among subtest standardized scores and gray matter volumes across the three large-scale neural networks. Specifically, we used partial correlations to account for sex, and conducted a Fisher’s z-test81 using the diffcor56 package (version 0.8.4) in R (R Core Team, 2014). This approach enabled us to investigate potential differences in the pace of verbal intelligence and brain development. Results were evaluated at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Dynamic links between the development of verbal intelligence and GMV

The Time-Varying Effect Model (TVEM) macro version 3.1.153,54 in SAS 9.4 was used to simulate dynamic associations between network-level gray matter volumes and the Verbal Comprehension Index as age increases, and to locate the temporal window in which the two exhibit a significant correlation. TVEMs were estimated separately for the Verbal Comprehension Index and its three subtests (see Figure S5 for details) and for the three large-scale networks (6 hemispheric measures; see Figures S16, S18, and S20 for details). The basic functions of TVEMs were defined using the P-spline penalty for the automatic selection of smoothness and adjustment of independence.54 One time-variant variable of gray matter volume, and one non-time-varying covariate of sex (0 = Male; 1 = Female) were entered into the model simultaneously to capture temporal effect changes of covariates on the outcome of the Verbal Comprehension Index (or its subtests).

In sum, each TVEM model, run separately for networks and tests, examined the time-varying associations of network-level gray matter volume and verbal intelligence across ages 6–15 years, controlling for the time-invariant variable of sex. For each network-level gray matter volume, a figure was presented to show how its relationship with the Verbal Comprehension Index changes over time. The figure presents pointwise 95% confidence bands, with statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) between gray matter volume and the Verbal Comprehension Index indicated by the criterion (dashed line) falling out of the 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given the pointwise inference approach, these TVEM-derived age windows should be interpreted as exploratory indicators of potentially sensitive developmental periods rather than definitive inferential boundaries. To reduce the risk of overinterpretation, all age windows identified in the PEK cohort were subsequently subjected to targeted confirmatory analyses using GAM/GAMM and independent validation in the CKG cohort.

Linking verbal intelligence and its network basis at specific age groups

Based on TVEM results which determined age-specific subgroups demonstrating statistically significant correlations, we then employed the following set of GAMMs (when the specific age group involved repeated measures) or GAMs (when the specific age group did not involve repeated measures) (see Equation 2) to more specifically capture the precise estimation of curve characteristics, and to fully model association differences between network-level gray matter volumes (GMVs) and test scores:(Equation 2)scores∼s(GMVs)scores∼s(GMVs)+sexscores∼s(GMVs)+(1|subject)scores∼s(GMVs)+sex+(1|subject)

The first GAM was constructed by modeling test scores as a smoothing function of age. The third GAMM incorporated repeated measurements to account for both within-subject dependency and developmental differences among participants. We also established the second GAM and the fourth GAMM by including sex as a fixed-effect term to control for the influence of sex. Given the potential confounding effects of general cognitive abilities on verbal intelligence, we also conducted exploratory GAMM with non-verbal intelligence as covariate in the regression. Non-verbal intelligence was measured using indices of working memory, processing speed, and perceptual reasoning from the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-IV (WISC-IV), to control for the influence of non-linguistic cognitive abilities on the relationship between verbal intelligence and gray matter volumes. Additionally, to examine whether the observed effects were independent of global brain size, we performed sensitivity analyses using different correction approaches, including controlling for total intracranial volume (TIV, see Table S13), total gray matter volume (total GMV, see Table S14), and a ratio-based normalization (total GMV/TIV, see Table S15).82,83 For all of these GAM and GAMM estimations, a threshold of p < 0.05 was used to evaluate significance, and a False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to correct for multiple comparisons. The CKG sample was subjected to parallel analyses for verification.

Measuring network contributions to the development of verbal intelligence

Additionally, Relative Weight Analysis (RWA) was performed on both samples using the rwa57 package (version 0.1.1) in R (R Core Team, 2014) to quantify the unique and relative explanatory contributions of the three large-scale networks in accounting for the variance in verbal intelligence throughout childhood and adolescence. RWA transformed correlated predictors into a set of uncorrelated variables maximally relating to the original ones, thereby addressing the problem of multicollinearity.84

Exploratory analyses

To test whether our findings for the MD network generalized across definitions, we repeated the analysis using a task-defined MD map based on hard-versus-easy contrasts from prior work,42 rather than relying solely on resting-state network parcellations. This predefined set of MD regions, here referred to as the EvLab MD system (https://imaging.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/imaging/MDsystem), is shown in Figure S7. To assess the specificity of our effects for language-relevant networks, we also repeated analyses using the limbic network from Yeo et al.73 as a control (see Figure S10). Finally, to contrast our broad measure of verbal intelligence with a more narrowly defined experimental task, we compared VCI scores with performance on a lab-based audiovisual integration task (see Methods S1 and Figure S12). These exploratory analyses were limited to the PEK sample and served as supplementary tests of robustness, rather than central components of the study.
