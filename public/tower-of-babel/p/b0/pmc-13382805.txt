PolCSBD: A contextual dataset of code-mixed Bengali and Banglish for political counter-speech detection
Authors: Chowdhury Mohammad Mehedi, Ali Ajgor Emon, Masum Ahmed Mozumder, Syma Akther, Fariha Tasnim, Mohammed Borhan Uddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The detection of counter-speech on social media is heavily dependent on the conversational context, particularly in low-resource and code-mixed languages. This article presents a dataset of 10,021 contextual comment pairs extracted from political discussions on Bangladeshi YouTube channels. The dataset captures native Bengali script, fully Romanized Bengali ("Banglish"), and hybrid code-mixed sentences. Each instance consists of a parent comment and a corresponding nested reply, annotated manually by native speakers to indicate the presence or absence of counter-speech. Replies that actively dispute, correct, or challenge the parent text with a logical counter-narrative are labeled as '1′ (Counter-Speech), while replies indicating agreement, off-topic remarks, or non-argumentative insults are labeled as '0′ (Non-Counter Speech). The dataset underwent rigorous preprocessing, including the removal of URLs, user tags, and emojis, alongside the normalization of English characters. This dataset provides a foundational resource for training and evaluating natural language processing (NLP) models in understanding contextual political discourse in South Asian code-mixed environments. By preserving the direct link between a baseline statement and its response, the data allows researchers to move beyond isolated sentence classification and study actual dialogic structures. It specifically targets the acute shortage of resources for analyzing informal, phonetically typed regional dialects. Consequently, machine learning practitioners and social scientists can utilize this collection to benchmark text classifiers, test large language models (LLMs) on South Asian discourse, and explore algorithmic moderation strategies in highly polarized online spaces.

Background

The rapid expansion of social networking platforms has fundamentally altered political communication, establishing an environment where information spreads instantaneously. This unchecked dissemination has frequently normalized the propagation of hate speech, targeted harassment, and political misinformation. To combat these hostile digital environments without resorting to outright censorship or platform bans, social media users frequently employ counter-speech. Defined as direct, non-escalatory responses designed to challenge, correct, or neutralize polarizing narratives, counter-speech has become an effective grassroots mechanism for maintaining civil discourse online [1]. A recent comprehensive survey of natural language processing (NLP) applications for online safety identifies counter-speech as one of the most promising interventions against digital hate [2]. However, while initial computational sociology studies established the baseline behavioral differences between accounts that post toxicity and those that post counter-narratives [3], the sheer volume of daily online interactions makes manual moderation entirely unsustainable.

Consequently, the NLP community has actively sought to automate both the detection and generation of counter-narratives. Foundational dataset initiatives, such as the widely utilized CONAN corpus [4], introduced large-scale multilingual collections of hate speech and counter-narrative pairs written by subject-matter experts. Building upon these early repositories, recent frameworks have introduced highly complex, multi-target benchmark datasets spanning multiple countering strategies [5] and advanced generation pipelines. For example, researchers have implemented multi-stage architectures to generate, prune, and select responses based on relevance [6], while others have systematically compared the efficacy of various generative models in producing logically sound arguments [7]. Advanced systems now integrate controllable mechanisms to ensure generated responses remain polite and detoxified regardless of the original provocation [8]. As machine-driven moderation grows increasingly sophisticated, there is a concurrent demand for transparency and factual accuracy. Recent studies highlight the absolute necessity of applying Explainable AI (XAI) to hate and counter-speech classifiers to verify their decision-making logic [9], alongside the development of retrieval-augmented models focused entirely on generating fact-based counter-narratives [10].

Despite the rapid maturation of these computational techniques, the vast majority of existing datasets strictly focus on high-resource, formal languages like English, French, and Italian. This creates a severe resource disparity for low-resource linguistic environments, specifically within South Asia, which hosts hundreds of millions of highly active internet users. While recent NLP initiatives have begun exploring counter-speech generation for Indic languages like Bengali and Hindi [11], as well as abstractive text processing for Urdu [12], the practical reality of analyzing regional social media data presents immense structural difficulties. In Bangladesh, the digital landscape is heavily burdened by the rapid spread of unverified information. Recent studies have demonstrated the high social risks associated with political rumors [13] and fake news [14] circulating across Bengali platforms. Researchers attempting to build deep learning frameworks for these issues frequently encounter overlapping complications, ranging from the difficulties of fine-grained hate speech classification [15] to the mathematical complexities of interpreting deep semantic sarcasm in short, informal political text [16].

A primary barrier preventing the successful deployment of automated counter-speech detection in Bangladesh is the highly irregular spelling conventions utilized by the general public. Social media users rarely construct sentences using formal, monolingual Bengali grammar. Instead, they predominantly rely on “Banglish” an informal, code-mixed linguistic style that blends English phonetics, native Bengali vocabulary, and occasional English loanwords, often typed entirely using the Latin alphabet. Standard NLP tokenizers and pre-trained classification models fundamentally struggle to process this lexical variability. Because code-mixed text lacks standardized spelling rules, the same word can be typed in a dozen different phonetic variations, immediately causing out-of-vocabulary errors in traditional embedding spaces. While recent experiments utilizing Transformer architectures have shown promise in processing Romanized Bengali for sentiment analysis in specific e-learning domains [17], these models still require massive amounts of targeted, accurately labeled training data to function reliably on polarized political text.

Furthermore, a distinct architectural flaw exists within many traditional hate speech detection corpora: they structure social media comments as isolated, independent text strings. Counter-speech is inherently dialogic and relational; it does not exist in a vacuum. A computational model cannot accurately classify a user's reply as a “counter-narrative” without first analyzing the baseline statement it is opposing. For example, a sentence containing negative terminology might be classified by a standard model as hate speech, when in reality, the user is merely quoting or mentioning the original hateful statement in order to refute it. Recent computational frameworks emphasize the absolute necessity of discourse-informed context for generating and detecting counter-speech [18]. Training a model to understand this conversational dynamic requires data that explicitly preserves the structural link between the instigating comment and the response.

The PolCSBD dataset [19] was specifically developed to resolve these overlapping methodological gaps, providing multidimensional utility for future classification studies [20]. By extracting and carefully preserving 10,021 strictly paired contextual instances consisting of a parent comment and its direct, nested reply from highly polarized political discussions, this collection provides the exact relational structure needed to train context-aware Transformer models. Furthermore, by retaining the authentic, unedited code-mixed spelling exactly as typed by Bangladeshi users, this dataset equips machine learning practitioners and computational linguists with a rare, foundational resource to benchmark advanced NLP models against the complex realities of South Asian digital discourse.

Data Description

The PolCSBD (Political Counter Speech Bangladesh) dataset [19] is formally hosted and publicly accessible through the Mendeley Data repository. Initially we collected 10,168 comment pairs. After processing, the comprehensive corpus contains exactly 10,021 instances of preprocessed, highly contextual textual data extracted from polarized political discourse on prominent Bangladeshi YouTube channels between 2024 and 2025. To ensure a thorough portrayal of the political landscape in Bangladesh, we selected YouTube channels based on high viewership and frequency of political content. Specifically, we focused on major news outlets including Somoy TV, Jamuna TV, and News24. Unlike traditional hate speech corpora that analyze isolated comments in a vacuum, this dataset is explicitly structured to preserve the relational, dialogic nature of online arguments. Each instance is formatted as a direct two-way conversation, providing machine learning models with the necessary baseline context to accurately classify whether a subsequent reply constitutes counter-speech. The data collection was performed using the YouTube Data API by searching for specific political keywords and party names: 'Bangladesh political news', 'Bangladesh Awami League (BAL)', 'Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP)', 'Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami', and 'National Citizen Party (NCP)'. Videos featuring political talk shows and news debates related to these parties were prioritized for comment extraction. The text provided in this dataset has undergone a rigorous lexical normalization pipeline including the systematic removal of web URLs, user mentions, and emojis, alongside the lowercasing of Romanized characters to optimize the data for immediate natural language processing tasks.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The primary objective of our data collection was to obtain authentic, polarized political discussions via online platforms in Bangladesh. To do this, we gathered raw comments from popular Bangladeshi YouTube channels focused on political news, talk shows, and public debates spanning the years 2024 and 2025. We handled all the data pulling, initial cleaning, and exploratory checks in Google Colab using Python. Specifically, we used Pandas to organize the data and Regular Expressions (RegEx) to clean up the text. Data extraction and conversational pairing

Since counter-speech always happens in response to someone else, we wrote a custom Python script utilizing the official YouTube Data API v3 to scrape comments as conversational pairs. We selected a number of YouTube channels based on their popularity and regularity of political content. More specifically, we concentrated on the most reputable news channels, including Somoy TV, Jamuna TV, and News24, on the basis of their highest subscriber base, viewership, and acceptance. The data collection used the YouTube Data API to search for certain political terms and party names such as 'Bangladesh political news', 'Bangladesh Awami League (BAL)', 'Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP)', 'Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami', and 'National Citizen Party (NCP)'. Comment extraction was prioritized for videos involving political talk shows and debates. First, the script pulled top-level comments using the CommentThreads.list endpoint to serve as our base statements, or “parent_text”. Next, it took the IDs of those parent comments and used the Comments.list endpoint to grab their specific nested replies ("reply_text"). By linking the parent and reply like this, we made sure not to lose the context of the conversation. Any standalone comments that didn't have a direct reply or anchor were immediately thrown out.

Preprocessing and lexical normalization

To get the paired data ready for deep learning models later on, we moved the raw JSON data into a Pandas DataFrame and ran it through several cleaning steps. Our first priority was privacy and ethics, so we permanently removed any personal identifiers, like usernames starting with '@', as well as regular web links. After that, we took out emojis and other non-text symbols. This forces the machine learning models to focus solely on the actual words and meanings, rather than relying on visual hints or spam patterns. Because so many users write in “Banglish” (Romanized Bengali), standardizing the text was a must. We changed all English letters to lowercase so that NLP tokenizers wouldn't treat capitalized and uncapitalized versions of the same word (like “Bhai” and “bhai”) as completely different vocabulary items. At the same time, we made sure to keep all the original Bengali Unicode characters (\u0980-\u09FF) and normal punctuation so the sentences kept their natural grammar and structure.

Annotation protocol and quality assurance

We relied on independent human annotators to manually classify the conversational pairs into two categories. These annotators were native Bengali speakers with excellent English skills, and they also had a strong grasp of Bangladesh's political history, local figures, and current internet slang. Based on our guidelines, annotators first read the parent_text to understand the original point being made, and then read the reply_text. They labeled a reply as a '1′ (Counter-Speech) if it successfully challenged the parent comment by providing facts, making a logical argument, or pushing back on the narrative. On the other hand, if the reply just agreed with the original comment, went off-topic, or threw out angry insults without actually arguing the point, it was labeled a '0′ (Non-Counter Speech).

To keep our labels as accurate as possible, multiple people annotated a smaller portion of the data so we could check the Inter-Rater Reliability (IRR). Whenever the annotators disagreed, the lead researchers stepped in to make the final call. In the end, this thorough workflow gave us a nicely balanced dataset of 10,021 rows, broken down into 5596 examples of counter-speech and 4425 examples of non-counter speech.

Limitations

While this dataset provides a robust foundation for political counter-speech detection, it contains certain data-level limitations. First, the comments were extracted exclusively from YouTube. Consequently, the linguistic behaviors and conversational structures may not fully generalize to other platforms like Facebook or X, which possess different demographic profiles, character limits, and moderation policies. Second, the dataset is strictly confined to the domain of Bangladeshi political discourse. The vocabulary, historical references, and debate tactics captured may not perfectly represent code-mixed counter-speech in other domains, such as religious or sports-related discussions.

Additionally, the dataset relies entirely on textual data. Since South Asian political discourse heavily employs implicit sarcasm and irony, the absence of multimodal cues (such as the audio or video context of the original YouTube clip) occasionally restricts the full comprehension of a comment’s intent, even for human annotators. Finally, while the dataset captures the code-mixed nature of “Banglish,” the lack of standardized spelling in Romanized Bengali means that the 10,021 instances cannot exhaustively represent every possible phonetic variation of the local vocabulary. Future iterations of this research will focus on expanding the scope to Misinformation, Disinformation, and Malinformation (MDM) detection. By integrating advanced AI engines and Quantum Computing frameworks, we aim to calculate the probability of information correctness in political debates. This will allow the model to distinguish between genuine counter-speech and the spread of deceptive political narratives.

Ethics Statement

The authors confirm that the data collection complied with YouTube's Terms of Service regarding public API usage. To ensure the privacy and anonymity of the social media users, all personally identifiable information, including usernames, exact profile links, and @mentions within the text, were permanently removed during the preprocessing phase prior to publication.
