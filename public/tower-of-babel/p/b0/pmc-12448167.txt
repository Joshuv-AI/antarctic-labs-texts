Effect of Platelet-rich Plasma and Brief Electrical Stimulation Following Facial Nerve Neurorrhaphy in a Rat Model
Authors: Janelle Sloychuk, Jin Soo Song, Lin Fu Zhu, Adrian Mendez, Jillian Querney, David Côté
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:With ongoing investigations to improve facial nerve recovery after injury, brief electrical stimulation (BES) and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) application have become promising modalities. The usage of combined modalities has not yet been investigated. We aimed to evaluate differences in facial nerve regeneration between rats receiving BES (group 1) and rats receiving BES plus PRP (group 2) following transection and primary neurorrhaphy.Methods:This was a prospective randomized animal study of 2 groups of 6 rats that underwent facial nerve transection and repair at the main trunk of the nerve. Both groups received BES on postoperative day 0 for 1 hour using an implantable stimulation device, with group 2 additionally receiving concurrent PRP. The primary measure of whisker amplitude was recorded from 2 to 8 weeks postoperatively.Results:At week 2, the average whisker amplitude for groups 1 and 2 was 6.5 and 2.0 degrees, respectively. At week 4, group 1 remained unchanged at 6.1 degrees, whereas group 2 improved to 10.1 degrees. By 6 weeks, average amplitudes were 8.2 and 14.0 degrees, respectively. By week 8, measures were 9.8 and 16.7 degrees, respectively. All results were statistically significant (P < 0.05).Conclusions:Application of BES and PRP following neurorrhaphy in a rat model yields a greater effect than BES alone in expediting facial nerve recovery.

Takeaways

Question: Will the addition of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) to brief electrical stimulation (BES) improve functional outcomes after facial nerve resection and repair in a rat model?

Findings: Combined treatments of PRP and BES when applied to a repaired facial nerve immediately after transection and repair are superior to BES alone in terms of early recovery in a rat model.

Meaning: Combined adjuvant therapies (BES + PRP) show promise in influencing earlier recovery and improved functional outcomes after facial nerve injury and repair.

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral nerve injuries are common after trauma, resulting in impaired functioning, decreased quality of life, and corresponding emotional distress and social isolation.1 Following primary transection, Wallerian degeneration occurs within 24 hours, resulting in axonal necrosis.2,3 Schwann cells in the distal nerve stump undergo mitosis and dedifferentiate to guide regenerating axons, a process that declines over time to render incomplete or synkinetic regeneration and permanent deficits.4

The current gold standard treatment following primary transection of the facial nerve (FN) entails neurorrhaphy by performing a tension-free suture repair of opposing nerve ends.5,6 Even with optimal surgical intervention, full recovery is rare, with patients burdened with symptoms including muscle paralysis, synkinesis, and chronic pain.3 Animal-based models have explored various adjuncts that can be incorporated during nerve repair to improve functional recovery.

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and brief electrical stimulation (BES) have demonstrated promising results for improving functional and histological recovery in several animal-based models.5,7–9 PRP has the potential to play a role in tissue regeneration by providing growth factors, including insulin-like, platelet-derived, vascular endothelial, and epidermal growth factors, to impact inflammation, mitotic activity, and angiogenesis.10–12 This matrix of neurotrophic factors may expedite axonal recovery to mitigate muscular atrophy and fibrosis.6,13 Additionally, BES has been demonstrated to affect properties of neurons involving branching, rate and orientation of neurite growth, rapid sprouting, and axon regeneration guidance.5,14

In 2010, Hadlock et al7 designed a template for investigating FN regeneration in a rat model, outlining the measurement of functional recovery after neurorrhaphy and BES application by detecting movements of rat whisking.7,15 Preceding research at our center comparing primary transection and anastomosis of the FN in a rat model with and without BES application found significantly greater initial FN recovery in the BES treatment arm.5 This association was maintained regardless of a transection or crush mechanism of injury.14 However, there has not been any research to date investigating the potential for combined application of these promising treatments. We present the first study evaluating the combined effect of BES and PRP application in comparison to BES application alone in functional recovery of the FN in a rat model.

METHODS

Study Design

This prospective randomized control animal trial was conducted at the Surgical Medical Research Institute at the University of Alberta. A previously validated rat FN model by Hadlock et al7 was used. The study design was modified from preceding work at our center conducted by Mendez et al.14 Ethics approval was obtained from the Animal Care and Use Committee overseen by the University Animal Policy and Welfare Committee at the University of Alberta in Edmonton, Alberta (AUP00002799).

Study Subjects

Fourteen female Wistar rats (Charles River Laboratories, Canada) weighing 200–220 g were used for this study. The sample size was calculated from previous work at our center, which used a similar outcome measure, powered to detect a clinically relevant difference of 10 degrees in whisking.5 The 14 rats were block randomized into 2 groups of 7. Each animal underwent unilateral FN transection and repair at the main trunk of the nerve on the right-hand side. Each animal received BES for 1 hour following nerve repair. Group 2 received the additional application of PRP immediately after nerve repair. Functional assessment of the FN was performed at 2-week intervals from 2 to 8 weeks after nerve repair.

FN Functional Outcome Assessment

The functional outcome assessment model uses a head fixation device, body restraint, and photoelectric sensors to detect precise whisker movements as an objective measure for FN function.7 Our center previously modified this assessment model, as outlined in the methodology by Mendez et al.5 Hadlock et al7 reported an average whisking amplitude of 75.9–79.1 degrees during preoperative testing of the unmanipulated FN. Whisking measurements of the nonoperated side were not collected during the experiment and were assumed to be consistent with the previously established baseline. This baseline, as quantified from previous experiments, ranged from an average of 66.6–74.9 degrees on the unmanipulated side.5,14

Body Restraint

A custom body restraint device for the rat subjects was bioengineered (Metalworks Engineering Shop, University of Alberta, Edmonton, Alberta).5 The device consisted of a half-pipe measuring 7.6 cm in diameter and 30 cm in length. A steel bar spanning across the half-pipe provided a fixation point for the head implant and functioned to support the laser micrometers. Along the anterior portion of the half-pipe, we added a circular platform to support the weight of the rat’s head while it was placed in the apparatus (Fig. 1).

Rodent restraint and whisker model setup.

Whisker Testing

Pairs of photoelectric sensors (Rx-Laser Micrometer; Metralight, Inc., San Mateo, CA) were placed along the sides of the rat’s face to track whisker movement. Tubing (1.5-mm diameter) was placed over a midline whisker to facilitate tracking with the laser micrometer. The laser micrometers were placed at 17 degrees from the midline along each side of the lateral surface of the face and positioned 10 mm from the origin of the tracked whisker (Fig. 2). The laser micrometer consisted of an emitter, which produced a 780-nm wavelength light curtain, and a detector composed of a 28-mm linear array of 4000 charge-coupled devices (scanline). A 5-cm vertical distance separated the emitter and the detector. Movement detected within the laser curtain sent a digital signal that could then be recorded. The laser micrometers were calibrated to avoid detection of objects less than 1 mm in size to detect only the marked whisker.

Measuring whisker amplitude using the laser curtain.

Data Acquisition

The whisker movement was elicited in each subject by providing a scented stimulus (sunflower seeds). The laser micrometers themselves were connected to a 32-channel digital input/output (I/O) module (NI 9403; National Instruments, Dallas, TX), which received digital output from the laser micrometers. The I/O module was connected to a personal computer through a CompactDAQ chassis (cDAQ-9174; National Instruments). The I/O module acquired the laser micrometer signal at a sampling rate of 1 kHz. LabVIEW (LabVIEW Full Development System; National Instruments) software was used for data acquisition.

Surgical Procedure

All subjects underwent head implantation surgery followed by FN transection and repair during 2 separate surgical procedures to allow adequate recovery between procedures. Rats underwent head implantation surgery in a sequential manner. The rats were randomly assigned to groups and underwent FN surgery in a grouped manner. Group 1 received BES only, whereas group 2 received both PRP and BES treatments. Given the additional technical logistics of resource usage for preparing PRP samples, the determination was made to treat all rats in group 2 followed by those in group 1 in a block manner. During the procedures, all rats were first anesthetized with 3%–4% isoflurane and then maintained under general anesthesia using 1.5% isoflurane.

Head Implant Surgery

To ensure proper head fixation during whisker movement measurement, an implantable head fixation device was designed, composed of acrylic and long threaded screws. A small incision was made using a 15-blade scalpel, followed by blunt dissection from the anterior to the posterior margin of the cranial sagittal suture line. Using an electric drill, 4 holes were made in each quadrant of the skull approximately 15 mm apart from each other. Then, 1.6-mm screws were placed within each drill site (Fig. 3). Dry acrylic resin was liquefied and placed onto the skull, covering the placed screws. Two larger 5-mm threaded screws were then inverted with the threads directed upward before acrylic solidification for later head-mount fixation.

Acrylic helmet for head fixation.

FN Surgery

FN neurorrhaphy and repair was completed on the right side of the face of all subjects. An electric razor was used to expose the right side of the face. A small modified Blair incision was made just inferior to the right ear bony prominence. Under microscopic visualization, the parotid gland was visualized, everted, and retracted out of the surgical field. Distal branches of the FN were identified just inferior to the parotid bed and followed proximally until the main trunk of the FN was identified. The main trunk and the upper and lower bifurcations of the FN were then carefully dissected. A single transection of the main trunk of the FN was made using straight microscopic scissors; the cut nerve ends were then immediately repaired using a direct end-to-end technique. Using 9-0 nylon sutures, 4 simple interrupted sutures were made within the proximal and distal epineural nerve endings. Care was taken to ensure adequate nerve alignment.

Brief Electrical Stimulation

All animal subjects received BES. The stimulation protocol was adapted from one used by Al-Majed et al16 in the sciatic nerve rat model, further modified in preceding studies by Mendez et al.5 Two silver Teflon-coated wires were stripped of 2-3 mm of insulation (AGT0510; W-P Instruments, Inc.). Following nerve repair, the first wire was looped around the proximal stump of the FN. The second wire was embedded into muscle tissue adjacent to the FN, at a location just proximal to the first wire. The insulated wires led to an isostim stimulator (A320D, W-P Instruments, Inc.), which delivered a 1.5-mA current in pulses of 100 µs in a continuous 20-Hz train for a period of 1 hour. Stimulation was verified by the presence of a right ear flutter that persisted for the entire duration of the electrical stimulation. At the completion of stimulation, the wires were removed from the animal, and the facial incision was closed with interrupted 4-0 Vicryl sutures.

PRP Application

The animal subjects in group 2 received PRP application in addition to BES, which was conducted on all rats. PRP was prepared by centrifugation of blood from the subject rat.9 Although natural blood contains 95% red blood cells and approximately 5% platelets, a PRP solution contains approximately 95% platelets.17 Although there are variable protocols for PRP preparation described in the literature, we modified our methods from research by Yamaguchi et al.18 All solutions were stored within an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid test tube to prevent blood clotting. Approximately 10 mL of autologous blood was harvested from the saphenous vein of each rat for use on the same day. The blood was subsequently centrifuged at 700–800 revolutions per minute at 22°C for 6 minutes to separate into 3 layers: red blood cells (bottom), acellular plasma (top), and PRP layer within the middle. After centrifugation, approximately 1 cc of PRP was extracted after first using the pipette to remove the overlying acellular plasma. The PRP was subsequently activated by mixing with 0.015 mL of 10% calcium chloride solution and 100 U/mL bovine thrombin to form a gel. This solution was directly pipetted onto the FN anastomosis site immediately after primary neurorrhaphy was performed and before the application of BES.

Head Fixation and Body Restraint

Two weeks before surgery, animal subjects were handled daily for conditioning. After surgery, all subjects were placed in body restraints daily for a week. At postoperative day 14, whisker measurements were started. The researcher performing postoperative measurements was blinded to the treatment arms of the rats. Subjects were initially given low-dose isoflurane and transported to the body restraint apparatus, as described above (Fig. 1). Here, they underwent head fixation with bolts applied across the exposed screws. Whisker markers were then placed on the right side of the rat’s face. A scented stimulus was introduced, and recording started for a period of 5 minutes.

Statistical Analysis

Mean differences in whisking between the 2 groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. P values were reported as 2-tailed, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software, version 29 (IBM), was used for analyses.

RESULTS

Fourteen rats were initially partitioned equally into the 2 treatment arms. During the perioperative period, 2 rats were omitted from the study. One rat died after administration of anesthetic during the head implant fixation surgery. Another rat was euthanized before study completion as it developed an exposure keratitis after FN transection and repair, which did not resolve despite an aggressive ophthalmic medication regimen. The remainder of the rats demonstrated unremarkable cage behavior and did not demonstrate any issues with weight loss or orbital injury. Hence, 6 rats remained within each treatment arm throughout the postoperative whisker amplitude measurements.

All animals experienced complete ipsilateral loss of whisking amplitude postoperatively. At week 2, the average amplitude observed for groups 1 and 2 was 6.5 (±2.0) and 2.0 (±2.0) degrees, respectively (P = 0.0006). When comparing the 2 groups at week 4, group 2 showed an improvement to 10.1 (±16.8) degrees, whereas group 1 demonstrated largely unchanged measurement of 6.1 (±0.7) degrees (P = 0.04). On assessment at week 6, group 2 continued to demonstrate improvement to 14.0 (±4.2) degrees, whereas group 1 demonstrated a smaller degree of improvement to 8.4 (±2.3) degrees (P = 0.001). On repeated assessment at week 8, both groups demonstrated varying degrees of improved whisker amplitude at 16.8 (±15.7) and 9.9 (±2.5) degrees for groups 2 and 1, respectively (P = 0.009) (Table 1, Fig. 4). DISCUSSION

Studies to date have demonstrated the use of BES and PRP as adjuncts to neurorrhaphy in animal models to accelerate the regain of function. BES demonstrates a physiological effect involving the regulation of neurotrophic factors as well as influencing the rate and orientation of nerve regeneration.5,14 Conversely, in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that biomolecules afforded by PRPs are crucial in regulating early inflammation and macrophage polarization, as well as stem cell–like myelinating Schwann cell activation and angiogenesis to modify the nerve recovery process.8,11 Despite these promising results, well-established adjuncts including BES are still considered experimental and not frequently used in clinical practice.

The parameters and timing of our BES application were informed by existing studies. Previous work by Al-Majed et al16 evaluating BES in sciatic nerve regeneration found that stimulation for 1 hour yielded comparable efficacy in axonal regeneration to electrical stimulation for up to 2 weeks. Furthermore, Foecking et al19 found that an increased number of sessions or daily BES application was detrimental toward recovery time. The concept of a delicate balance between augmentative stimulation and excitotoxic elements from overstimulation was reflected in the publication by Hadlock et al7 showing worse FN functional recovery in rats subject to combined BES and mechanical stimulation.

As anticipated with smaller sample size–based animal models, the degree and timing of nerve regeneration following BES application is variable between studies. Although our results are limited to 8 weeks, we would anticipate a similar long-term outcome to former studies demonstrating expedited regeneration of nerve functioning without changing final functional status. Calvey et al20 confirmed the beneficial effects of single-session of BES in rat models by demonstrating significantly greater functional recovery of the sciatic nerve in their BES treatment arm. Preceding studies comparing 2 groups of 9 rats undergoing FN transection and application of BES found significantly greater whisking function in the BES intervention arm at 2 weeks but no significant differences by 4 and 6 weeks.5,14

Within a similarly designed rodent study by Hadlock et al,7 by week 11 the whisking amplitude of the BES treatment arm was equivalent to that of the control group. Similarly, a recent publication by Shapira et al4 found an initial significantly earlier recovery and improved locomotion function of the sciatic nerve after neurorrhaphy and BES, which subsequently reached levels comparable to the control group’s action potential amplitude by 12 weeks. More longitudinal assessments showed a similar trend, as a recent study by Keane et al21 found significantly greater initial functional mobility and immunohistochemical recovery after tibial nerve transection in BES-treated rats, whereas no significant differences were observed between treatment groups at the 21-week end point. They concluded that the control group was capable of axonal regeneration with a similar capacity but proceeded at a slower rate.21 Hence, although the specific timeframes of nerve regeneration in the various studies vary by interventions and methods, the literature to date suggests a common theme of accelerated nerve recovery without significant differences in outcomes relative to primary neurorrhaphy.

Ikumi et al11 investigated local PRP application with nerve grafting in a rabbit model by depicting greater regenerated axonal diameter and greater number of activated Schwann cells at 4 and 12 weeks after surgery. A randomized study of 20 patients undergoing superficial parotidectomy found that treatment with PRP had a positive effect on earlier regeneration of extratemporal FN–innervated muscles without complications.22 Additionally, a 6-month prospective evaluation of patients undergoing carpal tunnel release found that patients who received ultrasound-guided PRP injection had significant improvement in subjective questionnaire scores as well in the cross-sectional area of the median nerve.23

At the 2-week postoperative measurement, whisker testing demonstrated a significantly smaller amplitude in group 2 than in the combined treatment arm. We suspect that the initially lower outcomes in group 2 at this period were attributable to the sequence of intervention. As all rats in group 2 were treated before those in group 1, there was likely a partial learning curve in the single surgeon’s neurorrhaphy technique, which would likely improve in the second group of rats treated. This limitation may have been ameliorated through implementing a mock surgical group before the commencement of the study to help overcome the learning curve of FN neurorrhaphy in this animal model. Our results from weeks 4 through 8 demonstrate a clear trend of significantly greater FN functional recovery in group 2 with combined PRP plus BES. Although a paucity of literature exists, this is reflective of previous investigations into multimodal adjuncts to nerve regeneration. Cho et al9 evaluated FN regeneration in guinea pigs to discover significantly greater vibrissae movement on subjective evaluation and motor action potentials on electrophysiological evaluation in the PRP treatment arm at 4 and 6 weeks after primary neurorrhaphy. Moreover, when treated with PRP and neural-induced human mesenchymal stem cells, the combined group demonstrated the greatest improvement in electrophysiological evaluation.9

Limitations of our study included a smaller sample size with limited follow-up. However, as previously noted, existing literature suggests that adjunctive modalities following neurorrhaphy expedite the initial recovery of nerve function without markedly impacting final outcomes. As such, our scope of interest is focused on the initial postoperative period. The smaller sample size is reflective of the experimental nature of the study design, as part of the study objective was to outline the procedural technique of concurrent PRP and BES application. A control group, in which neither BES nor BES paired with PRP was applied, was omitted, as this research was a continuation of 2 previous experiments at our center, whereby BES was found to accelerate early functional recovery in a rat model compared with a control.5,14 BES following neurorrhaphy was considered the established baseline treatment to which additional adjuvant therapies would be compared. Future research will incorporate larger study groups to corroborate our initial findings and narrow the range in whisker movement measurements. Furthermore, we hope to incorporate different forms of nerve injury, including crush injuries, to determine the impact of the experimental adjuncts, as well as incorporate additional measurements, including motor neuron labeling and counting.

To our knowledge, this is the first animal study evaluating the combined application of both BES and PRP in nerve regeneration. Compared with the most prominent adjunct of BES outlined in the literature, a combined application including PRP demonstrates more expedited functional recovery without adverse effects. This study provided early insight into the possibility of combining multiple therapeutic techniques to expedite nerve regeneration following FN transection and primary repair.
