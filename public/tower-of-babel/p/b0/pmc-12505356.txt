Feasibility of a Shock Simulation with Ultrasound Curriculum
Authors: Sarah J. Lee, Sammar Alsunaid, Shyam Ganti, Navin Durairajan, Abdelaziz Mohamed, Aryan Shiari, Divya Venkat, Abdulghani Sankari
Published: 2025

Medical societies have recommended critical care ultrasound (CCUS) training for future intensivists, but fellowships have been slow to adapt because of a lack of universal consensus for curricular methodologies and resource shortages (1, 2). Our aim was to design an evidence-based CCUS simulation curriculum addressing shock and sepsis management.

An abstract of this work was presented at the 2019 American Thoracic Society conference (3).

Methods

This study was declared exempt by the Wayne State University Institutional Review Board. The two-week curriculum was piloted during orientation for incoming pulmonary critical care medicine and critical care medicine fellows. The training team consisted of clinical educator–track fellows and faculty intensivists with simulation development experience. For content validity, the team spent six months creating the curriculum on the basis of consensus recommendations (2, 4), practiced using the assessment tools on volunteer senior fellows, and conducted a postcurriculum survey about the learning objectives (Table 1). Equipment included a high-fidelity mannequin and Simbionix Ultrasound Mentor, with additional supplies to enhance realism. Knowledge and performance were scored using a binary system with competency checklists, with reliability of 90–95% interrater agreement. End-of-curriculum performance was reevaluated at 1 and 9 months.

Learners’ satisfaction on meeting the goal learning outcomes of the curriculum

Responses were based on a five-point Likert-type scale with 0 indicating the least satisfied and 5 indicating very satisfied.

Each learner underwent the following sequence: 1) baseline performance evaluation during an instructor-led simulation measured by competency checklists; 2) immediate postsimulation debriefing with constructive feedback and deliberate practice; 3) didactics detailing 1 hour on evidence-based septic shock management with pathophysiology and 3 hours on CCUS physics, image interpretation, and goal-directed protocols (e.g., rapid ultrasound in shock) (5); 4) a 1-hour hands-on session for knobology and practice; 5) 2-hour bedside CCUS teaching rounds in which preselected patients with shock were assessed for resuscitation and volume management; and 6) end-of-curriculum evaluation with simulation and debriefing. Knowledge before and after the curriculum was tested with 20 multiple-choice questions selected from CHEST SEEK board examination review. Simulations were performed one learner to training team, for about 20 to 30 minutes, followed by 30 minutes of debriefing and deliberate practice.

Results are reported using median, interquartile range (IQR), or proportions. Matched paired t-test analysis was conducted with learners as their own baseline controls. A repeated-measures analysis of variance with Holm-Šidák multiple-comparison tests was used to measure the effect of training sessions at 1 and 9 months with respect to baseline (SigmaStat version 3.5; Systat Software).

Results

Sixteen learners completed the curriculum between July 2018 and 2019, with high satisfaction (Table 1). The average time experienced per learner was nine hours spread over two weeks. None had previously attended a formal CCUS course, but most had some informal exposure during medical training. Comparing baseline with postcurriculum, the median score for correctly completing shock competencies improved from 12 (IQR, 2) to 24 (IQR, 3) (P < 0.001) out of a maximum score of 29. Ultrasound competencies improved from 8 (IQR, 2) to 14 (IQR, 3) (P < 0.001) out of 15 (Figure 1). The first consecutive eight learners were retested for retention after 9 months and performed similarly to the 1-month results. Retention testing for the remainder was interrupted because of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic. For shock knowledge assessments, average scores increased from 66% to 83% at 1 month and 93% at 9 months after training (P < 0.05). Ultrasound knowledge averages scores were 73% at baseline, then 93% at both 1 and 9 months (P < 0.05).

Discussion

We found that a multimodal curriculum combining CCUS and case-based simulation was feasible during a fellowship orientation and, despite comprising only 9 training hours, was effective in improving knowledge and skills with possible long-term retention. Standardized competency checklists allowed tracking of knowledge and skill acquisition. Breaking down complex procedures and disease management into discrete steps allowed educators to identify areas for improvement, which helped guide subsequent training. Other published CCUS curricula have similar formats of didactics and proctored hands-on training (6–11). However, our curriculum is unique in that critical decision making and CCUS skills are directly observed with real-time pressure in the context of an undifferentiated shock simulation, then education is enhanced via debriefing with deliberate practice and hands-on supervision on real patients with shock. This approach allows clinical integration and compressed training time without compromising skill acquisition and retention.

To the best of our knowledge, this curriculum is the first to use CCUS in the diagnosis and management of shock and sepsis in a single simulation setting. Because of the scenario’s complexity and number of competency elements, this assessment enables testing of surface and deep understanding of shock management. A multimodal approach adheres to transformative adult learning theory that mixing learning strategies tends to be more effective because learner stage is “matched” to teaching style. To enhance self-directed training, learners were allowed independent practice on the ultrasound simulator of an assortment of preloaded modules, but not the testing module.

The main strength of this curriculum is its focus on direct observation to evaluate learners’ in situ CCUS skills, critical thinking, and communication pertaining to shock and sepsis diagnosis and management. To reinforce clinical integration and bypass expensive live models (7, 8, 11), small-group sessions of two learners per supervisor scanned multiple preselected patients within an allotted time frame. To minimize educator bias and reinforce standardization, teaching scripts were used throughout the simulation. Teaching guides were annotated so that remediation would be specific, systematic, efficient, and evidence based. Debriefing provided learners opportunities to receive feedback, self-reflect, and practice specific hands-on skills, further consolidating learning (12). We have shown that our improvement gains were comparable with those obtained with longer curricula, and a multimodal approach ensures better retention than informal learning or single workshops (8–11).

Limitations on generalizability include a small sample size, single-institution scope, and access to costly simulation equipment and trained clinical instructors. The 9-month results may reflect experience gained naturally through fellowship and not necessarily curriculum retention. However, the curriculum achieved the goal of CCUS competency in diagnosis and/or management of septic and cardiogenic shock and therefore was continued in subsequent years. Moreover, this curriculum is flexible and can be adapted to address other shock states. To limit referral bias and experimenter expectancy, grading criteria were established before implementation of the curriculum and study analysis. To limit retest bias, posttest questions were rearranged compared with the pretest, and answers were not provided to learners.

Conclusions

A multimodal curriculum incorporating CCUS in a case-based simulation is an effective and efficient training in sepsis and shock diagnosis and management.
