Transverse Facial Artery Morphometry Based on Head Dimensions and Palpable Anatomical Landmarks
Authors: Necati Salman, Simel Kendir, Ayhan Cömert, Gönül Kozan, Ali Can Korkmaz, Yiğit Güngör
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe transverse facial artery (TFA) is a key vascular structure supplying the lateral region of the face. Because of its central location, the artery is vulnerable to transection during lateral facial rejuvenation and other invasive facial procedures.ObjectivesThe authors of this study aim to determine the location of the TFA origin relative to 2 palpable bony landmarks: the head of the mandible and the upper border of the zygomatic arch.MethodsThirty hemifaces from 15 embalmed cadaveric heads were examined. Two palpable landmarks—the upper border of the zygomatic arch and the tip of the mandibular head—were marked using pins. After intra-arterial injection of a mixture of liquid latex and red acrylic paint, a vertical skin incision was made anterior to the tragus to expose the TFA origin. Vertical distances from the TFA origin to each landmark were then measured.ResultsIn all specimens, the TFA originated from the superficial temporal artery. The mean vertical distance from the origin of the TFA to the upper border of the zygomatic arch was 26.6 ± 8.2 mm and to the tip of the mandibular head was 17 ± 6.87 mm.ConclusionsThe small diameter and deep anatomical location of the TFA complicate its visualization on radiologic imaging. However, its origin can be reliably approximated using palpable landmarks. Preoperative identification of these landmarks may reduce the risk of iatrogenic vascular injury and improve the safety of lateral facial interventions.

The transverse facial artery (TFA) is a key vascular structure supplying the lateral region of the face. It is located in the mid-lateral part of the face and follows a typical transverse course, which gives the artery its name. Unlike the tortuous path of the facial artery, the TFA follows a relatively straight, horizontal, or downward-concave trajectory across the lateral face.1 The TFA most commonly originates from the superficial temporal artery (STA). However, less commonly, it may arise from the external carotid artery or the maxillary artery.2 The morphometry of the TFA varies with respect to the number and length of its branches. Studies have reported variations in the number of main TFA trunks, including single, double, and triple configurations.3 In some cases, the TFA may be absent, with the facial artery compensating for the lateral facial blood supply.4,5 Because of their close anatomical relationship, the TFA is considered the primary arterial supplier to the facial nerve.6 In addition to the facial nerve, the TFA supplies the orbicularis oris, parotid gland and duct, masseter muscle, and the overlying skin through its branches.7

Compared with the facial artery, the TFA is considered to play a subordinate role, which has resulted in fewer studies focusing on it in the literature.8 Its central and extensive distribution across the lateral face makes the TFA vulnerable to iatrogenic penetrating injuries during facial rejuvenation procedures. One such procedure is the injection of botulinum toxin to treat masseter hypertrophy.1 Another common penetrating procedure is filler injection, performed using both cannulas and sharp needles.

Modern techniques and equipment can help minimize the risk of vascular injury. For example, the cannula technique is recognized as a less traumatic and lower-risk method compared with sharp-needle injection and other instrumental techniques.9 However, filler interventions have been increasingly linked to cases of blindness, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.10,11 Facelift procedures, which are invasive treatments for the lateral face, involve fat removal and the redraping of the facial skin. Hematomas caused by transection of the TFA are reported as the most common complication of this procedure.12 In addition to iatrogenic injury, the TFA is susceptible to trauma from events such as traffic accidents and explosions affecting the lateral facial region.13

Facial aesthetic procedures are frequently performed by healthcare professionals from various specialties and experience levels. These commonly performed procedures encompass both noninvasive techniques and invasive methods targeting deeper structures. This highlights the importance of practitioners having sufficient knowledge of regional anatomy to ensure safety during interventions.14 The subject of the study, the TFA, is recognized as a complex structure to visualize and analyze because of its size and intricate relationship with the parotid gland.6,15 The authors of this study aim to define the morphometry of the TFA using head morphometrics and palpable bony landmarks, including the upper edge of the zygomatic arch and the mandibular head.

METHODS

Study Design and Materials

This study was conducted on 15 embalmed cadaveric heads, including 9 males and 6 females. The cadavers had a mean age at death of 76.1 years (range, 70-91 years). A total of 30 hemifaces, from both sides of the cadaveric heads, were examined. The heads were embalmed with a 10% formaldehyde solution, and their medical histories revealed no trauma, vascular disease, or surgical procedures involving the head and neck regions.

The study was conducted in the university's Anatomy Department Laboratory over a 7-month period from June 1, 2024, to January 1, 2025. Before dissection, morphometric parameters of the cadaveric heads were measured and recorded in millimeters. Ethical approval was obtained from the University Ethics Committee. The ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki were observed throughout material selection, dissection procedures, and image presentation.

Dissection Technique

Bilateral facial dissections were performed to expose the origin of the TFA from the STA at the dissection area that extended anterior to the tragus, between the base of the mandible and the mid-temporal region. Before dissection, the external carotid arteries on both sides were cannulated with catheters, flushed with 100 mL of saline, and injected with a mixture of 50 mL liquid latex and red acrylic paint. The upper edge of the zygomatic arch and the tip of the mandibular condyle were then palpated and marked with needle pins. A vertical skin incision was first made anterior to the tragus, followed by elevation of the skin to visualize the STA and TFA, as illustrated in Figure 1A and B.

Parameters and Distances

Two sets of parameters were assessed: head morphometrics and arterial metrics. Head morphometric data included biparietal diameter, head circumference, and nasion–inion distance. The biparietal diameter, defined as the horizontal distance between the 2 eurion points (the most lateral aspects of the cranium), was measured by aligning rulers over the right and left eurion points and recording the distance with a tape measure. Head circumference was measured with a tape passing over the glabella and the inion, following the midline along the bony ridge on the posteroinferior surface of the occipital bone. The nasion–inion distance, extending from the midline point at the frontonasal suture (nasion) to the inion, was also measured using a tape measure.

The arterial metrics include the STA and TFA diameters, the origin–arch distance (the vertical distance from the TFA's origin point to the palpable upper line of the zygomatic arch), and the origin–tip distance (the vertical distance from the TFA's origin point to the palpable tip of the mandibular condyle). Data Analysis

Data were initially entered into Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) and analyzed using Jamovi (version 2.6.22.0; Sydney, Australia), an open-source statistical software. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and means ± standard deviations (mm). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in arterial diameters and distances with respect to sex and side were analyzed using independent samples t-tests. Correlations between head morphometrics and arterial parameters were evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Morphometric measurements of the cadaveric heads were first recorded to establish a reference anatomical framework for subsequent analysis. The mean values were 152.07 ± 10.78 mm for biparietal diameter, 614 ± 46.33 mm for head circumference, and 386.67 ± 45.44 mm for nasion–inion distance. No statistically significant differences were found in these morphometric parameters between male and female groups.

In all specimens, the TFAs originated from the STA, and all STAs bifurcated above the level of the zygomatic arch. The mean diameter of the STA was 2.67 ± 0.41 mm. All hemifaces exhibited a consistent TFA origin pattern, with the artery arising bilaterally below the tip of the mandibular condyle and the zygomatic arch. Regarding branching patterns, all TFAs had a single main trunk, with a mean diameter of 1.78 ± 0.34 mm. The origin points of the TFA were consistently located deeply within the adipose tissue before the over-parotid course of the artery. In this case, when evaluating the positions of the origin and supra-parotid parts of the artery in 3 dimensions, a course from deep to superficial can be identified. When comparing TFA diameters across sex and side groups, a statistically significant difference was observed only between males and females; males had larger TFA diameters (P = .02; Table 1).

Two distances were measured in the study: the origin–arch distance (vertical distance from the TFA origin to the upper palpable edge of the zygomatic arch) and the origin–tip distance (vertical distance from the TFA origin to the palpable tip of the mandibular condyle). The mean origin–arch distance was 26.6 ± 8.2 mm, and the mean origin–tip distance was 17 ± 6.87 mm. No significant differences in these distances were found between male and female groups or between right and left hemifaces.

Upon further analysis, we first examined the correlation between head morphometrics and the diameters of the STA and TFA to determine whether larger head measurements are associated with larger arterial diameters. Although the Pearson correlation analysis suggested a trend of a linear relationship between artery diameters and head measurements, these correlations were not statistically significant (r < 0).

In the second phase of the analysis, the relationship between the diameters of the STA and TFA was analyzed. This aimed to assess whether larger STA diameters correspond to larger TFA diameters. We observed strong symmetry between the right and left sides of the same artery. However, the correlation between STA and TFA diameters within the same hemiface was moderate (r = 0.49), whereas the correlations of diameters for the STA (r = 0.85) and TFA (r = 0.75) individually between sides were stronger.

DISCUSSION

Among the arteries supplying the lateral face, the TFA holds a central anatomical position between the zygomatic arch and the parotid duct. Its origin and course can be visualized using imaging modalities such as computed tomography angiography (CTA) and ultrasonography (USG), which are increasingly employed in preoperative planning for lateral face procedures.2 However, although CTA offers high-resolution vascular imaging, its routine use in all facial rejuvenation and surgical procedures is neither practical nor cost-effective. In addition, the temporal resolution of CTA may not always be sufficient to provide detailed visualization of smaller arterial branches in every case.

Koziej et al reported a 96% depiction rate, whereas Nicol et al reported 74% for right hemifaces and 62% for left hemifaces; Cuccia et al reported a 70% success rate in visualizing the TFA using imaging techniques.2,16,17 In contrast, USG is more dependent on the operator's expertise, particularly when identifying small-caliber vessels. Renshaw et al reported a 75.5% detection rate of the TFA using color Doppler USG examinations.15 Given these limitations, we propose that estimating the origin of the TFA using palpable bony landmarks can aid practitioners during the preprocedural planning of facial interventions. This approach aligns with previous studies that have also evaluated the anatomical relationships between the TFA and adjacent facial landmarks.

Whetzel and Mathes, as well as Pierrefeu et al more recently, investigated the localization of the perforating zone of the TFA in relation to the lateral canthus.18,19 In contrast to these studies, our investigation focused on identifying the proximal origin point of the artery rather than its distal segment. This distinction is clinically significant, because the origin of the TFA is at risk of being transected during preauricular skin incisions, as previously noted by Nicol et al.16 Previous studies have identified the zygomatic arch as a consistent anatomical landmark for localizing the origin of the TFA, with measurements typically taken from the artery to the lower edge of the arch. For instance, Koziej et al, Başar et al, and Trzeciak et al reported mean vertical distances of 20.9 ± 4.1, 15.49 ± 7.56, and 24.63 ± 4.1 mm, respectively.2,20,21 In contrast, in our study, we measured the distance from the TFA origin to the upper edge of the zygomatic arch, yielding a mean value of 26.33 ± 8.04 mm. We selected the upper edge of the zygomatic arch as the reference point because of its relatively flat and horizontally oriented structure, which offers a more consistent, easily identifiable, and palpable landmark during both clinical palpation and cadaveric dissections. The second anatomical landmark used in this study—the tip of the head of the mandible—was chosen for its dependable and readily palpable position on the intact lateral facial surface. This landmark also holds anatomical significance, because the TFA contributes to the vascular supply of the mandibular condyle, as previously reported in the literature.22 Damage to the vascular network associated with mandibular condyle fractures has been implicated in condylar resorption.23 Therefore, identifying and preserving the arterial origin point in relation to the head of the mandible is essential for maintaining the integrity of the local arterial supply. In a recent study, Nicol et al reported the vertical distance between the mandibular condyle and the TFA to be 1.84 ± 0.6 cm (range, 1.26-2.54 cm), a finding consistent with our results as shown in Table 1.16 However, their measurements were obtained using CT imaging, and the authors noted that identification of the TFA was sometimes not possible because of its small caliber. This limitation underscores the value of cadaveric studies in the morphometric analysis of small-caliber arteries such as the TFA, where direct visualization provides a higher degree of accuracy.

The anatomical relationship between the origin of the TFA and the parotid gland is a consistent and distinguishing feature. The TFA typically arises from the STA within or adjacent to the parotid gland.1,6,24 In dissections of this study, the origin was located at the posterior border of the parotid gland and embedded in connective tissue. This region corresponds approximately to the area anterior to the tragus, where the STA runs in proximity to the auriculotemporal nerve. The parotid branches of the STA, also observed in this region (Figure 3), had a more oblique course than the TFA. In a related study, Yang et al identified 3 TFAs and described them as superficial, middle, and inferior branches.6 However, the middle and inferior arteries described by Yang et al do not follow the characteristic transverse trajectory of the TFA. Instead, their courses are directed toward the buccal and mandibular regions of the face. Therefore, we suggest that these obliquely oriented arteries supplying the lower face should be distinguished from the TFA. Notably, current anatomical terminology does not include TFAs described as superior, middle, or inferior.25 This highlights a gap in the existing literature and nomenclature, indicating that the arterial anatomy of the lateral facial region remains insufficiently defined.

Regarding the number of branches, the TFA typically arises as a single trunk; however, previous reports have noted anatomical variations, including its absence or duplication and even triplication of the trunk.26 In dissections of this study, it was consistently observed a single TFA trunk on each side across all 15 cadavers, with each artery originating from the external carotid artery. The arterial diameter is a critical consideration in planning microvascular transfer surgeries, in addition to the number of branches. Although the TFA is generally recognized as a small-caliber artery, existing literature provides limited data on its diameter. To date, only 3 studies have reported quantitative diameter measurements: 2 based on CTA and one using USG. Koziej et al reported a mean diameter of 1.0 ± 0.4 mm, Trzeciak et al reported 2.26 ± 0.47 mm based on CTA findings, and Renshaw et al recorded 1.6 ± 0.46 mm in a USG-based study.2,15,21 In the current study, the diameter of the TFA was measured at its origin, located anterior to the tragus, with a mean value of 1.78 ± 0.34 mm. For comparison, another major arterial supply to the face, the facial artery, has been reported to range between 1.3 and 2.2 mm in diameter.27 These findings suggest that the TFA has a diameter comparable to that of the facial artery. Nevertheless, the limited morphometric data available on the arteries of the lateral face underscore the importance of detailed anatomical studies of the lateral facial arteries to enhance surgical safety and optimize operative techniques.

Cadaveric studies offer distinct advantages and limitations compared with radiological methods. The intra-arterial injection technique used in cadaveric specimens allows for accurate measurement of the outer arterial diameter, which includes the vessel wall. In a CTA study, Gündoğdu et al evaluated the STA and reported a mean diameter of 1.54 ± 0.25 mm in the anterior tragus region.28 In contrast, in this study, the STA diameter was measured at the origin of the TFA as 2.67 ± 0.41 mm. Gündoğdu et al emphasized that the difference between CTA-based and cadaveric measurements likely stems from the fact that artery wall thickness is not included in the measurements made in CTA studies.28

However, although dissection studies allow for the direct measurement of arterial diameters and wall thickness, they are typically limited by smaller sample sizes compared with radiological imaging studies. Similarly, the primary limitation of this study is the relatively small number of cadaveric specimens. Future studies with larger cadaveric samples can help further clarify the detailed anatomy of the TFA and other arteries in the lateral facial region. In terms of methodology, incorporating the definition of a “safe surgical zone” would enhance the clinical applicability of morphometric findings.

CONCLUSIONS

The TFA typically originates from the STA as a single trunk at the posterior margin of the parotid gland in the lateral facial region, following a transverse anterior course. Its mean diameter, including the arterial wall, is 1.78 ± 0.34 mm, with significantly larger diameters observed in males compared with females. Cranial measurements—such as biparietal diameter, head circumference, and nasion–inion distance—were not found to be reliable predictors of TFA diameter. The artery's small caliber and relatively deep position at its origin contribute to the difficulty of visualizing it through radiological imaging. Nevertheless, the origin of the TFA can be approximated using 2 palpable anatomical landmarks: the upper border of the zygomatic arch and the tip of the mandibular head. In this study, the mean distance from the TFA origin to the upper edge of the zygomatic arch was 26.6 ± 8.2 mm, and to the mandibular head tip was 17 ± 6.87 mm. Based on these findings, the use of these accessible landmarks may offer a practical method for locating the TFA origin, potentially reducing the risk of iatrogenic vascular injury and improving the safety and accuracy of lateral facial surgical procedures.
