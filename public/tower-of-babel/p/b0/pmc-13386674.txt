Association of IL-10 (rs1800896) and TLR3 (rs1879026) polymorphism with progression of hcv-related liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma in Egyptian patients
Authors: Ashaimaa A. Eid, Azza. H. Abbas, Ahmad M. Abbas, Khaled M. Aboshanab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis C virus (HCV) is a significant global health concern, particularly in Egypt, where its prevalence is estimated at 4.5%-6.7%. The Egyptian genotype, HCV-4, accounts for most infections and is a leading cause of cirrhosis, chronic liver disease, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). A previous study was conducted in Egypt to examine certain single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in both toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) and their association to interferon therapy. However, there is still a remaining gap to examine the correlation of both TLR3 and IL-10 gene polymorphism to the disease progression and disease prognosis among Egyptian genotype, HCV-4. This study investigates the relationship between toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) polymorphism (rs1879026) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) polymorphism (rs1800896) with liver disease progression among Egyptian HCV genotype 4 patients.MethodsA case-control study was conducted involving blood samples from Egyptian HCV patients, categorized into four groups: chronic hepatitis C, cirrhosis, HCC, and a healthy control group. Genotyping of IL-10 (rs1800896) and TLR3 (rs1879026) polymorphisms was performed using TaqMan SNP assays. Standard genomic analysis methods and international guidelines have been implemented for patient recruitment and analysis.ResultsThe IL-10 rs1800896 CC genotype frequency increased progressively with disease severity, observed in 5% of controls, 26.67% fibrosis, 46.67% cirrhosis, and 66.67% HCC patients (p-value < 0.001), indicating a significant association with progression of HCV-related liver disease. In contrast, TLR3 rs1879026 genotype distribution showed no statistically significant differences across groups (p-value > 0.05).ConclusionThe IL-10 gene polymorphism (rs1800896) may contribute to genetic susceptibility in Egyptian patients, increasing the risk of HCV-related cirrhosis and HCC progression. In contrast, TLR3 rs1879026 had no clear impact on disease progression in Egyptian patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of testing the correlation between the IL-10 gene and the TLR3 polymorphism in disease progression among Egyptian HCV patients.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13878-3.

Background

The hepatitis C virus (HCV) stands as a formidable public health challenge, affecting over 170 million individuals globally and establishing itself as a primary etiological agent in the development of chronic liver disease [1]. In Egypt, HCV is particularly prevalent, with an estimated infection rate ranging from 4.5% to 6.7% of the population, highlighting the country as home to one of the highest burdens of HCV infection worldwide. The predominant genotype in Egypt, HCV genotype 4, accounts for nearly 90% of cases and substantially contributes to the alarming rates of chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and the necessity for liver transplantation [2].

Understanding the specifics of this viral infection, particularly its association with HCC, is crucial as HCC represents a significant oncological burden within Egypt [3]. The rising incidence of HCC can be attributed to several factors, particularly the endemic prevalence of HCV infection, which is estimated to account for approximately 80% of HCC cases in the country [4, 5].

Moreover, the role of the immune system, particularly the involvement of cytokines in liver diseases, cannot be overstated. Cytokines are essential proteins that mediate immune responses and play diverse roles in the development, differentiation, and activation of immune cells [6]. The immune system’s response to inflammation, infection, and liver damage often leads to dysregulated cytokine production [7]. Specifically, Th1 and Th2 cytokines orchestrate distinct immune responses, and variations in their levels can influence the progression and severity of liver diseases. For instance, excessive secretion of Th1 cytokines can lead to chronic inflammation, while a shift to Th2 dominance often favors fibrosis and hinders liver recovery [8].

Notably, the interleukin-10 (IL-10) gene, which plays a pivotal role in modulating inflammatory responses, exhibits significant polymorphism. Variants in its promoter region, particularly the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)—rs1800896, rs1800871, and rs1800872—have been associated with altered cytokine expressions and heightened susceptibility to various diseases, including chronic hepatitis C (CHC). Understanding these genetic variants is critical for evaluating individual disease susceptibility and progression among patients [9]. However, no studies were conducted to investigate the effect of IL-10 rs1800896SNP on HCV progression in HCV-infected Egyptian patients.

Another area of interest involves Toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR3, which are crucial components of the innate immune system that recognize viral patterns and initiate inflammatory responses [10]. Research indicates that polymorphisms in the TLR3 gene can influence its ability to detect HCV and modulate the subsequent immune response. Specific genetic variations, such as TLR3 SNP (rs1879026), have been linked to increased susceptibility to HCV-related liver damage and the advancement to HCC in various populations, including Saudi Arabs and Kazakhs [10–14]. Yet [15], no studies investigated the effect of the TLR3 rs1879026 SNP on the progression of HCV infection in the Egyptian population.

IL-10 rs1800896 is a SNP located in the promoter region of the IL-10 gene on chromosome 1q31–q32. This variant can influence transcriptional activity and circulating IL-10 levels, thereby modulating anti-inflammatory responses that may suppress antiviral immunity and promote viral persistence. In contrast, TLR3 rs1879026 lies within the TLR3 gene on chromosome 4q35.1, which encodes a TLR responsible for recognizing viral double-stranded RNA and activating the TRIF-dependent signaling pathway, leading to type I interferon and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Variations at these loci may alter cytokine expression or pathogen recognition, influencing host susceptibility, chronicity, and progression of HCV-related liver disease [10–14]. Another study conducted in Egypt investigated the association of TLR3 and IL-10 gene polymorphism in response to interferon therapy, concluding certain SNPs that could be important indicators for successful interferon therapy [15]. However, there is still a remaining gap in research among Egyptian HCV patients to examine the correlation of both TLR3 and IL-10 gene polymorphism to the disease progression and disease prognosis.

Given the significant role of genetic variation in disease progression, our study aims to elucidate the relationship between TLR3 polymorphism (rs1879026), IL-10 gene polymorphism (rs1800896), and the progression of HCV disease in Egyptian patients. By examining these genetic factors, we seek to contribute to the understanding of HCV pathogenesis and the variability in disease outcomes, ultimately aiding in the development of targeted therapeutic strategies and more effective public health interventions for managing HCV in Egypt. This investigation represents an essential step in translating genetic understanding into clinical practice, potentially improving the prognosis for patients affected by this chronic viral infection.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

A retrospective case-control design with convenience-based participant recruitment was employed. This study investigated the association between IL-10 rs1800896 and toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) rs1879026 gene polymorphisms and the progression of HCV-related liver disease in Egyptian patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Pharmacy - Ain Shams University, and written informed consent was obtained from the National Committee for Control of Viral Hepatitis in Egypt. Written informed consents were obtained from patients and healthy control participants according to f the National Committee for Control of Viral Hepatitis in Egypt and the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy- Ain Shams University (approval number ACUC-FP-ASU RHIDIRB2020110301 REC #1).

Patient criteria and sample collection

A total of sixty-five peripheral blood samples were collected in 2023 from recently diagnosed, untreated HCV patients attending National Viral Hepatitis Committee clinics in distinct geographical localities across the Cairo and Beheira governorates. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 to 75 years with confirmed chronic HCV infection, defined by seropositivity for both HCV antibodies and HCV-RNA for more than six months. Exclusion criteria included co-infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), pregnancy, autoimmune hepatitis, any autoimmune disease, or a history of non-HCV-related malignancy.

Patient classification and diagnostic criteria

Participants were classified into four mutually exclusive groups using standardized clinical, laboratory, and imaging criteria: Chronic hepatitis C (CHC; fibrosis group, F1–F2): Defined as seropositivity for anti-HCV and detectable HCV-RNA for > 6 months, absence of clinical or imaging features of cirrhosis, and transient elastography (FibroScan) staging consistent with mild–moderate fibrosis. FibroScan cut-offs used for staging were: F0–F1 < 7.0 kPa, F2 ≥ 7.1 kPa and < 9.5 kPa. Patients with clinical, biochemical, or radiologic evidence of portal hypertension were excluded from this group. Liver cirrhosis (F3–F4 / compensated or decompensated): Diagnosis required either (a) FibroScan ≥ 12.5 kPa consistent with advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis or (b) characteristic imaging findings on abdominal ultrasound (nodular liver surface, coarse echotexture, splenomegaly, portal vein diameter > 13 mm) combined with clinical signs of chronic liver disease (e.g., ascites, encephalopathy) and laboratory markers (thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminemia, prolonged INR). When available, corroborative serologic indices (APRI, FIB-4) were considered adjunctive. HCC: HCC was diagnosed according to AASLD criteria: typical vascular pattern on multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI (arterial phase hyperenhancement with portal-venous/delayed washout) or histologic confirmation when imaging was inconclusive. Serum AFP and radiologic staging were recorded. Healthy controls: Controls were age- and sex-matched volunteers with no history of liver disease, negative anti-HCV and HCV-RNA tests, normal liver enzymes (AST, ALT), and normal abdominal ultrasound. Controls with prior blood transfusion within the last year, autoimmune disease, or other chronic infections were excluded. For all participants, a detailed medical history was obtained - including age, sex, history of blood transfusion, and surgical history - followed by clinical examination. Approximately 5 ml of venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent DNA extraction and laboratory analysis.

DNA extraction

Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA-containing tubes from all study groups. Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 µL of whole blood using the GeneJET Whole Blood Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and quantity were assessed by calculating the absorbance ratio OD 260 nm/280nm using a Nano Drop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Specimen handling and genotyping quality control

Peripheral venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes and stored at − 80 °C until processing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the GeneJET Whole Blood kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) per manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quantity and purity were checked (OD260/280). Genotyping of IL-10 rs1800896 and TLR3 rs1879026 was performed using TaqMan® SNP Genotyping Assays on an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) with allele-specific FAM/VIC probes (assay IDs listed in Tables 1 and 2).

Quality-control procedures included: Blind genotyping (operators unaware of clinical group), inclusion of no-template controls in each run, re-genotyping of ~ 10% of samples to assess reproducibility (expected concordance ≥ 99%), minimum accepted call rate per SNP: 95%; samples or SNP assays failing QC were excluded, and Assessment of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) in the control group; SNPs deviating from HWE (p < 0.05 in controls) would prompt re-check of genotyping and sample exclusion.

Genotyping of IL10 rs1800896

Genotyping of the IL-10 gene polymorphism (rs1800896) was performed using TaqMan® SNP Genotyping Assays (Applied Biosystems) on the ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Allele-specific FAM- and VIC-labeled TaqMan MGB probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used as indicated in Table 1. Each 20 µL reaction consisted of: 10 µL of 2X TaqMan Genotyping Master Mix, 0.5 µL of 40X TaqMan Genotyping Assay, 2 µL of genomic DNA template (20 ng), and 7.5 µL of nuclease-free water. Reaction preparation and thermal cycling conditions followed standard TaqMan® genotyping protocols [16, 17]. The detected variant was deposited in the ClinVar database in accordance with NCBI ClinVar submission guidelines to ensure reproducibility and public data accessibility.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables, including genotype and allele frequencies of IL-10 (rs1800896) and TLR3 (rs1879026), were presented as counts and percentages. Differences in genotype distributions across the four study groups (CHC, cirrhosis, HCC, and control) were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariable logistic regression models were developed to assess the independent relationships of genotypes IL-10 and TLR3 with liver disease in conjunction with clinical covariates AST, ALT, serum α-fetoprotein (AFP), total bilirubin, hemoglobin (HB), creatinine, platelet count, and prothrombin concentration (PC). The models were summarized using regression coefficients, standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values, which were calculated for each model. The models’ performance was also assessed using Chi-square statistics and Nagelkerke’s R². Furthermore, the IL-10 and TLR3 genotype associations with the disease groups were also evaluated for both main and interaction effects with analysis of deviance (ANOVA).

Annotating the two SNPs (IL-10 (rs1800896) and TLR (rs1879026) using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP)

The annotation of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to interleukin-10 (IL10) and toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) was performed using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) tool [18–20]. This analysis utilized Variant Call Format (VCF v4.2) data for the SNPs rs1800896 and rs1879026. By employing this approach, we aimed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the potential functional and clinical implications of these genetic variants in relation to the progression of hepatitis C virus (HCV) disease. Our study specifically concentrated on the SNP rs1800896 and TLR3 variant rs1879026.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

This study included 65 participants divided into four groups: CHC (n = 15), liver cirrhosis (n = 15), HCC (n = 15), and healthy controls (n = 20). The demographic and laboratory characteristics of all groups are summarized in Table 3; Fig. 1. The participants comprised 38 males and 27 females, aged between 20 and 71 years. Mean age increased across groups, with HCC patients being the oldest (mean ± standard deviation [SD]: 59.7 ± 14.1 years) and controls the youngest (37.5 ± 14.1 years). Liver enzymes (AST, ALT) were significantly elevated in disease groups compared to controls, with the highest values observed in the HCC group. Total bilirubin was also elevated in cirrhotic patients, while AFP levels were highest in the HCC group. Platelet count level was lower in cirrhosis and HCC groups, consistent with progressive liver dysfunction.

Hardy-weinberg equilibrium of polymorphisms

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium analysis was performed, and genotype frequencies of both IL-10 (rs1800896) and TLR3 (rs1879026) polymorphisms were in equilibrium (p-value > 0.05), supporting the absence of genotyping bias or selection pressure in the groups.

Frequencies and distribution of IL-10 polymorphisms among the studied groups

Genotype distribution of IL-10 (rs1800896) showed significant differences across groups (χ² = 45.47, df = 6, p < 0.001). Genotype frequencies of IL-10 (rs1800896) across the study groups are summarized in Table 4 and illustrated in Fig. 2. The CC genotype showed a progressive increase with advancing liver disease. It was observed in 5% of controls, 26.67% of fibrosis patients, 46.67% of cirrhotic patients, and 66.67% of HCC cases. In contrast, the TT genotype was most common in the control group (80%) and progressively decreased across disease groups (13.3% in the fibrosis group, 0% in the cirrhosis and 0% in the HCC group). The TC genotype was also more frequent in disease groups (60% in the fibrosis group, 53.3% in the cirrhosis group, and 33.3% in the HCC group) compared to controls (15%).

Multivariable analysis and genotype–phenotype interaction

Logistic regression models including IL-10 and TLR3 genotypes with clinical variables (AST, ALT, AFP, etc.) failed to identify significant predictors of disease status (IL-10 model: X² = 9.239, p = 0.600, R² = 0.265; TLR3 model: X² = 10.325, p = 0.587, R² = 0.291). All covariates, including creatinine, bilirubin, and AFP, were not statistically significant (p > 0.17). ANOVA showed no significant interaction effects between IL-10, TLR3, and disease (all p > 0.1). Tables S1 and S2 in the supplementary materials show logistic regression coefficients and ANOVA interaction results.

Biological interpretation of the IL-10 rs1800896 and TLR3 rs1879026 polymorphisms

The functional annotation of the two variants (rs1800896 in IL10 and rs1879026 in TLR3) was performed using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP). Overall, both variants map to non-coding regulatory regions with high potential to influence transcriptional activity of their respective immune-related genes.

IL10 rs1800896 (− 1082 T > C) – promoter/upstream functional variant

VEP annotation identified rs1800896 as an upstream_gene_variant located within the promoter region of IL10, a key anti-inflammatory cytokine. The variant is positioned at chromosome 1:206,946,897 (GRCh37), where the C allele represents the minor allele with a global MAF of ~ 0.27. Because promoter polymorphisms frequently modify transcription factor binding affinity, rs1800896 is widely recognized as a potential expression-modulating variant. Prior experimental and computational analyses suggest that allelic substitution at this locus can alter promoter activity and consequently influence IL-10 production, which is biologically relevant to inflammatory diseases and virus-induced immune dysregulation (Table S3).

The variant has been extensively reported in immune-related conditions, as reflected by more than 100 PubMed entries and a ClinVar classification of “uncertain risk allele.” Although not classified as pathogenic, its functional positioning within a regulatory hotspot supports its role as a quantitative trait variant affecting cytokine expression rather than a disease-causing mutation. In the context of HCV infection and liver disease progression, modulation of IL-10 levels may influence the balance between antiviral immunity and inflammatory liver injury, providing a strong biological rationale for its inclusion in the present study.

TLR3 rs1879026 (G > T) – intronic and regulatory region variant

The second investigated variant, rs1879026, is located within intron 1 of TLR3 at chromosome 4:186,079,167, with a minor allele frequency of ~ 0.15. VEP identified multiple overlapping consequences, including intron_variant, upstream_gene_variant, and, critically, regulatory_region_variant affecting a defined enhancer region. Intronic and enhancer-associated variants are increasingly recognized as key modulators of gene transcription, splicing efficiency, and chromatin accessibility (Table S4). Because TLR3 encodes a Toll-like receptor involved in the recognition of viral double-stranded RNA, regulatory variation in this gene has direct implications for innate antiviral immunity. The annotation links rs1879026 to multiple TLR3 transcripts, including the MANE Select canonical transcript (NM_003265.3), indicating that the variant may influence transcriptional regulation of the biologically dominant isoform. The presence of 22 PubMed citations further supports previously observed associations with immune signaling and infection susceptibility, although no pathogenic classification has been assigned in ClinVar (Table S4). Functionally, non-coding variants in TLR3 can affect receptor expression levels, ligand sensitivity, and downstream NF-κB/IRF signaling pathways. These mechanisms are particularly important in viral hepatitis, where impaired TLR3 activation may contribute to chronic infection, altered interferon responses, and liver inflammation.

Integrated biological relevance

Both SNPs represent regulatory, non-coding variants with plausible immunomodulatory effects: rs1800896 may alter anti-inflammatory IL-10 cytokine expression, influencing immune tolerance and inflammatory resolution (Table 6). The rs1879026 has the potential to modulate TLR3-mediated innate antiviral responses, thereby may affect viral clearance and inflammatory signaling. These annotations support the biological plausibility of the observed associations in the study cohort and highlight the importance of regulatory genetic variation in pathways central to liver inflammation, fibrosis progression, and host immune defense during HCV infection.

Discussion

The findings of this study elucidate the complex interplay between genetic predispositions and the progression of liver diseases, particularly in the context of chronic HCV infection. The study highlights how age was found to correlate positively with liver disease progression, with patients suffering from severe liver complications such as cirrhosis and HCC generally being older than healthy controls. This observation reinforces the established paradigm that advanced age serves as a comorbidity factor that heightens susceptibility to liver diseases. Age-associated physiological changes, such as decreased regenerative capacity of the liver and changes in immune response, may account for this increased risk [20]. By integrating genotype distributions with functional annotation using Ensembl VEP, we aimed to elucidate the biological relevance of these variants and their potential contribution to liver disease progression. The IL10 rs1800896 variant resides in the promoter region of the IL10 gene, a central regulator of anti-inflammatory immune responses. As demonstrated by functional annotation, this SNP is an upstream regulatory variant with a strong potential to influence IL-10 transcription through altered transcription factor binding. Promoter variants in IL10 have long been recognized as modulators of cytokine production, and the rs1800896 C allele specifically has been associated with lower IL-10 expression in several immune-mediated conditions [21, 22].

Our results show clear differences in genotype and allele distribution across the clinical groups, supporting the biological assumption that genetic variation in IL10 may influence inflammatory balance, hepatic injury, and disease trajectory. Given the dual role of IL-10, both protective (anti-inflammatory) and permissive (allowing viral persistence by dampening antiviral immunity), the clinical interpretation of rs1800896 must consider disease stage. In early HCV infection, insufficient IL-10 may enhance inflammation and hepatocyte damage, accelerating fibrosis [21, 22]. Conversely, excessive IL-10 favors persistent viral replication and immune evasion. Thus, the association of rs1800896 genotypes with disease severity offers mechanistic insight into how IL-10 dysregulation may contribute to the transition from chronic hepatitis to cirrhosis and ultimately HCC. The second investigated variant, TLR3 rs1879026, is an intronic and regulatory region polymorphism located within a transcriptional enhancer region upstream of the TLR3 gene. Given that TLR3 encodes a key pathogen-recognition receptor that senses viral double-stranded RNA, genetic variation affecting its expression or signaling capacity can have significant implications for antiviral immunity. Our VEP analysis confirms that rs1879026 overlaps with both intronic and enhancer regions, suggesting a likely impact on gene transcription, chromatin accessibility, and possibly mRNA processing. Although the SNP does not alter the coding sequence, growing evidence indicates that regulatory non-coding variants can exert substantial functional effects and contribute to interindividual differences in immune responsiveness. The observed distribution of the rs1879026 genotypes across the different patient groups in our cohort implies a potential relationship between this variant and HCV-related disease progression. Reduced TLR3 expression or signaling efficiency could impair interferon production and antiviral responses, allowing higher viral loads, sustained hepatic inflammation, and increased fibrosis risk [23]. Additionally, TLR3 signaling plays a role in modulating apoptosis and cellular immunity, both critical in restricting malignant transformation [23, 24]. Therefore, the presence of the rs1879026 variant in more advanced disease groups may reflect a genetic predisposition to impaired innate immune defense, ultimately influencing the risk of cirrhosis and HCC.

In the context of genetic susceptibility, this investigation reveals an intriguing change in the distribution of IL-10 (rs1800896) genotypes across the disease stages, with the homozygous C/C genotype rising progressively from controls to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC. While the logistic regression analysis did not support an independent significant correlation between disease status and IL-10 genotypes, the pattern observed, albeit not conclusive, remains interesting enough to be explored further. It aligns with existing literature that delineates the role of IL-10 in modulating immune responses during chronic viral infections. Elevated levels of IL-10 have been documented in HCV patients, suggesting that this cytokine may foster an environment conducive to viral persistence and pathogenesis. The polymorphic alterations in the IL-10 gene promoter, particularly at the rs1800896 locus, appear to further stratify the risk for disease severity, indicating that genetic screening for IL-10 variants could offer valuable prognostic insights [25, 26].

In accordance with the present findings, AL-Shihani et al. [24] showed that significant differences in IL-10 rs1800896 genotype frequencies were observed between HCV patients and controls, and the presence of a dominant or over-dominant genetic model suggests that this polymorphism may contribute to increased susceptibility to HCV infection. Also, Cao et al. reported that individuals carrying the AA genotype of IL-10 rs1800896 had a higher risk of developing liver cirrhosis compared with those carrying the GG genotype [27]. Furthermore, the TLR3 plays a crucial role in host immunity through its recognition of viral double-stranded RNA, leading to the activation of inflammatory pathways. A robust host immune response is essential for the clearance of HCV; thus, TLR3’s activation may confer a protective advantage by enhancing pro-inflammatory cytokine production. However, the TLR3 (rs1879026) genotype distribution did not differ significantly across groups, and regression analysis did not support any protective or risk association between TLR3 genotypes and liver disease. Nonetheless, the lack of replication of prior findings associating the TLR3 rs1879026 genotype with increased susceptibility to HCV in other populations suggests that these genetic associations may be population-specific and influenced by ethnic and geographic variables [24, 28].

Although previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between the TLR3 rs1879026 genotype and chronic HCV infection, particularly in the Kazakh population, where this variant was linked to increased susceptibility to disease [14]. Our findings did not support this association in the studied case-control. Additionally, while earlier research reported a markedly higher prevalence of the TLR3 + 1234TT genotype in cirrhotic patients with HCC compared to those without HCC, and a complete absence among healthy controls [23], our data did not replicate this genotype distribution pattern. These discrepancies may be attributed to ethnic and geographical genetic diversity, and differences in sample size.

The discrepancies between our findings and those of previous studies may stem from several factors, including sample size and genetic diversity among populations. Previous studies that primarily focused on Kazakh populations may not adequately reflect the genetic landscape of the Egyptian cohort [14]. Such variations underscore the necessity for larger and more diverse participant groups in future research efforts, which can allow for a more generalized understanding of genotype-phenotype relationships in relation to HCV disease progression [23]. The noted association of the IL-10 rs1800896 polymorphism with the progression of HCV disease, as indicated by differences in genotype distribution (p < 0.001), suggests that this variant may modulate the disease severity of HCV infection. However, additional studies in larger cohorts are required before it can be deemed as a trustworthy prognostic marker. The comparability to recent findings from Iraq (p-value of 0.046) further buttresses the relevance of this genetic marker across different populations [29], suggesting that IL-10 polymorphisms could indeed be incorporated into routine clinical assessments to identify high-risk individuals. By integrating genetic screening into primary care practices, healthcare providers could facilitate early intervention, thereby averting catastrophic liver complications through timely support and treatment.

The findings of this study suggest that IL-10 (rs1800896) polymorphism may serve as a potential genetic biomarker for identifying HCV patients at increased risk of progression to advanced liver disease and HCC. Although differences in IL-10 rs1800896 genotype distribution were observed across disease stages, the current findings are exploratory and do not establish clinical utility. Larger prospective studies are required to determine whether this polymorphism has independent prognostic value and whether it could contribute to future biomarker-based risk assessment strategies.

Furthermore, because several potentially important confounding variables were not available for analysis, including duration of HCV infection, viral load, treatment history, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and metabolic comorbidities, the observed genotype distributions may reflect the influence of these unmeasured factors rather than independent effects of the investigated polymorphisms. Consequently, caution is warranted when interpreting the biological significance of these associations.

While the implications of this research are promising, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations. The study’s design precludes drawing definitive causal inferences regarding the influence of genetic polymorphisms on disease progression. A longitudinal approach is advocated to serially assess genotypes and clinical outcomes in the same patient cohort over time. Such designs would provide stronger evidence of causality, enabling researchers to trace the evolutionary trajectory of liver disease in the context of individual genetic risk factors. Moreover, this study has some limitations, including the relatively small sample size. In addition, although strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied, some important clinical confounders, such as HCV viral load, duration of infection, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and detailed antiviral treatment history, were not available for all participants and therefore were not included in the analysis. These factors may influence HCV disease progression and could affect the observed genetic associations. Another limitation of the present study is the limited availability of detailed data regarding patient comorbidities. Although major confounding conditions such as HBV/HIV co-infection, autoimmune diseases, pregnancy, and non-HCV-related malignancies were excluded, other comorbid conditions that may influence liver disease progression were not systematically evaluated for all participants. Therefore, A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size (n = 65), with only 15 patients in each disease subgroup, which limits the statistical power of both the genetic association analyses and the multivariable logistic regression models. This limited power increases the risk of both type I (false-positive) and type II (false-negative) errors and may affect the robustness and stability of the estimated associations. In addition, the small subgroup sizes may have resulted in model overfitting in multivariable analyses. Therefore, the findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Independent validation in larger, well-powered cohorts is required to confirm these results and ensure their reproducibility.

Conclusion

The present study identified differences in the distribution of IL-10 (rs1800896) genotypes across stages of HCV-related liver disease among Egyptian patients, whereas no significant association was observed for TLR3 (rs1879026). However, these associations were not maintained in multivariable logistic regression analyses, indicating that neither polymorphism was independently associated with disease status after adjustment for potential confounding factors. Therefore, while IL-10 genetic variation may warrant further investigation in the context of chronic HCV infection, the current findings do not support its use as an independent prognostic marker for disease progression. Larger multicenter studies with longitudinal follow-up and comprehensive clinical characterization are needed to validate these observations and further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying HCV-related liver disease progression.
