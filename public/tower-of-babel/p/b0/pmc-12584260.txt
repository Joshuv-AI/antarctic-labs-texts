Comparison of gait parameters between post-surgery ankle fracture patients and healthy individuals: a systematic review and meta-review
Authors: Qingyan Long, Zhanyu Yang, Tingting Shao, Yuefei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious evidence indicates disparities between the recovery of motor function in surgically treated ankle fractures and the functional performance of healthy individuals. However, no systematic review has quantitatively examined these functional differences. This study aimed to summarize and evaluate differences in gait analysis outcomes, muscle strength, and plantar pressure between postoperative ankle fracture patients and healthy controls.Data sourcesA comprehensive electronic search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science up to February 2024. Keywords included Ankle Fractures, Gait Analysis, Muscle Strength, Plantar Pressure, and Rehabilitation.Study selectionInclusion criteria were based on the PICOS principle. Eligible studies included cross-sectional and non-randomized observational studies comparing gait analysis outcomes, muscle strength, and plantar pressure between postoperative ankle fracture patients and healthy controls.Data extractionExtracted gait parameters included walking speed, peak dorsiflexion angular velocity, peak plantarflexion angular velocity, step length, single support time, and cadence. Muscle strength measures included peak torque of dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, as well as the cross-sectional area (CSA) of thigh muscles. Plantar pressure outcomes included mean and peak plantar pressure and contact time. Risk of bias was assessed using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) tool and the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS).Data synthesisHeterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and the I2 statistic. Subgroup analyses based on age, fracture diagnosis, and study designs were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, Egger’s regression test, and Begg’s test. Sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out method.ResultsTwelve studies comprising 219 postoperative ankle fracture patients and 192 healthy controls were included. Compared with healthy controls, postoperative ankle fracture patients showed significant impairments across several gait parameters. They exhibited slower walking speed (WMD = − 0.13, 95% CI [− 0.45, − 0.16], P < 0.001, I2 = 90.7%), had significantly lower peak dorsiflexion angular velocity (WMD = − 7.93, 95% CI [− 13.45, − 2.41], P = 0.005), and shorter mean step length (WMD = − 0.15, 95% CI [− 0.18, − 0.12], P < 0.001). Single support time was also significantly reduced (WMD = − 16.59, 95% CI [− 19.18, − 14.00], P < 0.001), and cadence was significantly lower (WMD = − 8.44, 95% CI [− 10.87, − 6.01], P < 0.001). Although mean peak plantarflexion angular velocity was lower, this difference was not statistically significant (WMD = − 49.64, 95% CI [− 99.98, 0.71], P = 0.053). Both muscle strength and plantar pressure were notably reduced.ConclusionsDespite rehabilitation leading to improvements, patients often failed to regain pre-injury levels of walking speed, muscle strength, and normal plantar pressure distribution. Further research is warranted to clarify the pathological mechanisms underlying impaired recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-025-01463-9.

Background

Ankle fractures are among the most common fractures worldwide and represent the most frequent fractures of the lower limb. Their incidence has been increasing annually, with rates ranging from 100 to 150 cases per 100,000 person-years across various studies [1–3]. More than 50% of cases occur during physical activity in otherwise healthy, working-age individuals [4], where occupational disability imposes a substantial burden on both patients and society. These burdens arise not only from surgical treatment and subsequent inpatient rehabilitation but, more critically, from the loss of time and labor capacity during occupational disability [5–8]. To alleviate this impact, early postoperative functional recovery is essential [9].

Ankle fractures can be categorized as simple or severe. Simple fractures involve a single bone, whereas severe fractures affect two or more bones. Although several classification systems exist—such as the Lauge-Hansen and Weber classifications—surgical planning primarily considers ankle stability [10, 11]. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is widely used to stabilize the ankle in cases of joint incongruity, with reported success rates of 73% for simple fractures and only 50% for severe fractures [12]. Postoperative complications include wound hematoma and necrosis [12], with infection rates of approximately 2% and a 10% incidence of ankle joint disease during follow-up periods ranging from 2 to 38 months [13]. Prior research has demonstrated significant differences in ankle kinematics between ankle fracture patients and healthy individuals. The severity of the fracture shows weak-to-strong correlations with both postoperative ankle kinematics and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). Furthermore, follow-up studies have indicated that operative fixation of trimalleolar ankle fractures does not restore normal gait function at 6 months postoperatively [14]. Collectively, these findings suggest that ankle fracture patients may not achieve functional outcomes equivalent to those of healthy individuals following surgery.

Recovery after ankle fracture surgery involves not only bone healing but, more importantly, the restoration of gait function. Gait is a fundamental aspect of daily living, encompassing complex biomechanical processes such as joint kinematics, muscle dynamics, and neuromuscular control [15]. Ankle fractures can significantly disrupt these biomechanical parameters, resulting in gait abnormalities such as reduced stride length, slower walking speed, and restricted joint range of motion [16]. Long-term follow-up studies indicate that patients with ankle fractures have a slower walking speed, take shorter strides, and demonstrate reduced flexion and extension between the hindfoot and tibia compared with healthy individuals. Among these patients, those with trimalleolar fractures display the greatest limitations in flexibility and range of motion [17]. These abnormalities not only reduce walking efficiency but may also lead to excessive joint wear and secondary injuries, ultimately impairing long-term quality of life [16, 18]. Three-dimensional gait analysis has further revealed significant differences in ankle angle parameters in the sagittal plane, particularly a reduced maximum dorsiflexion angle during the support phase (p = 0.039) [19].

Although no comprehensive review has directly compared the postoperative recovery of ankle fracture patients with that of healthy individuals, numerous studies suggest that incomplete restoration of ankle stability after surgery contributes to differences in gait parameters [14, 20], muscle strength [21], and plantar pressure [22] between the two groups. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine whether patients with ankle fractures can achieve functional levels comparable to healthy individuals in terms of gait analysis, muscle strength, and plantar pressure following surgical treatment.

Methods

Literature search

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [23]. The literature search followed the PICOS principle. Systematic searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library up to February 2024. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and related keywords included Ankle Fractures, Gait Analysis, Muscle Strength, Plantar Pressure, and Rehabilitation. Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Literature screening and selection were conducted independently by two researchers, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by two authors, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus among all authors. Extracted data included: author name, year of publication, study design, fracture type (e.g., unimalleolar, bimalleolar, trimalleolar fractures, and classification systems such as Lauge-Hansen or Weber), sample size, mean age, sex, body mass index (BMI), type of surgical procedure, and reported outcomes. Outcomes of interest included walking speed (m/s), peak DF angular velocity (°/s), peak PF angular velocity (°/s), step length (m), single support time (%), cadence (steps/min), peak torque of DF and PF, CSA of thigh muscles, mean and peak plantar pressure, and contact time.

Quality assessment

Two quality assessment tools were applied according to study design. For the three cross-sectional studies, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) tool [24] was used to evaluate risk of bias and evidence quality. For the nine non-randomized observational studies, the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) [25] was employed. All studies were independently assessed by two researchers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion.

The AHRQ tool includes 11 items. Each item is scored as 1 for “yes” and 0 for “no”, with a maximum total score of 11. Scores of 0–3 indicate low quality, 4–7 moderate quality, and 8–11 high quality. The MINORS tool consists of 12 indicators, each scored from 0 to 2. Indicators 1–8 apply to non-controlled studies, with a maximum score of 16. For controlled studies, four additional indicators are included, yielding a maximum score of 24. A score of 0 represents “not reported”, 1 indicates “inadequate information”, and 2 indicates “adequate information”. Higher MINORS scores correspond to higher study quality and lower risk of bias.

Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis was performed on 10 studies reporting gait analysis outcomes using Stata 15.1 SE. Weighted mean differences (WMDs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. I2 values of 0–25% were considered to represent low heterogeneity, 26–50% moderate heterogeneity, and >50% high heterogeneity. A Q test P > 0.10 was also considered to indicate low heterogeneity. A random-effects model was applied when substantial heterogeneity was present, while a fixed-effects model was used for outcomes with low heterogeneity. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to variations in measurement methods and reporting for muscle strength and plantar pressure, and the limited number of eligible studies, quantitative synthesis was not feasible for these outcomes. Instead, results for muscle strength and plantar pressure were summarized qualitatively in the discussion.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the pooled results and potential confounding effects, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted. If the exclusion of a single study significantly altered the pooled estimate, that study was identified as a potential source of heterogeneity or confounding.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on fracture type and study design. Potential publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, Egger’s regression test, and Begg’s test.

Results

The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1. From the four databases searched, 2223 records were initially identified, of which 442 duplicates were removed. A further 270 studies were excluded due to ineligible study designs, 1489 were excluded for irrelevant outcomes, and 2 were excluded for including inappropriate subjects. During the full-text screening, 20 studies were assessed; 5 were excluded due to lack of full-text availability, and 3 did not report outcomes of interest. Study quality

The risk-of-bias assessments is presented in Table 2 and Table 3. All three cross-sectional studies assessed using the AHRQ tool were rated as high quality with low risk of bias; however, none reported the expected follow-up duration. The nine non-randomized observational studies, evaluated using the MINORS tool, were all rated as high quality. Four controlled studies, which scored 23 points, were insufficiently detailed in their descriptions of statistical methods. In particular, they frequently failed to specify the statistical tests used for group comparisons (e.g., t tests, Mann–Whitney U, ANOVA) or whether assumptions of normality were tested prior to selecting the method. Additionally, most did not provide information on adjustments for multiple comparisons, handling of confounders, or the rationale for selecting correlation coefficients (e.g., Pearson versus Spearman). These omissions limit the reproducibility and interpretability of their findings. Walking speed

Walking speed data were available for 165 postoperative ankle fracture patients and 183 healthy controls across nine studies. Due to significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. Postoperative ankle fracture patients exhibited significantly slower walking speeds than healthy controls (WMD = − 0.13, 95% CI [− 0.45, − 0.16], P < 0.001, I2 = 90.7%) (Fig. 2a).Fig. 2Forest plot of gait analysis results

Forest plot of gait analysis results

Peak dorsiflexion angular velocity

Two studies reported peak DF angular velocity, involving 35 postoperative ankle fracture patients and 22 healthy controls. A fixed-effects model was used given the low heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.625). Healthy controls demonstrated significantly higher peak DF angular velocity compared with postoperative patients (WMD = − 7.93, 95% CI [− 13.45, − 2.41], P = 0.005) (Fig. 2b).

Peak plantarflexion angular velocity

Peak plantarflexion (PF) angular velocity was analyzed in two studies involving 35 postoperative ankle fracture patients and 22 healthy controls. Although postoperative patients exhibited lower mean PF angular velocity, the difference did not reach statistical significance (WMD = − 49.64, 95% CI [− 99.98, 0.71], P = 0.053) (Fig. 2c).

Step length

Step length was reported in four studies including 66 postoperative ankle fracture patients and 115 healthy controls. The mean step length of postoperative patients was significantly shorter compared with healthy controls (WMD = − 0.15, 95% CI [− 0.18, − 0.12], P < 0.001) (Fig. 2d). A fixed-effects model was applied given the absence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%).

Single support time

Two studies, comprising 26 postoperative ankle fracture patients and 84 healthy controls, assessed single support time. Results indicated that postoperative patients had significantly shorter single support times compared with controls (WMD = − 16.59, 95% CI [− 19.18, − 14.00], P < 0.001) (Fig. 2e). Due to low heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%), a fixed-effects model was adopted.

Cadence

Cadence was evaluated in three studies involving 62 postoperative ankle fracture patients and 42 healthy controls. Postoperative patients demonstrated significantly lower cadence compared with controls (WMD = − 8.44, 95% CI [− 10.87, − 6.01], P < 0.001). A fixed-effects model was used due to moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39.1%) (Fig. 2f).

Subgroup analysis

Considering potential heterogeneity in postoperative ankle fracture and healthy control data sources, subgroup analysis divided the included studies into different age (≤ 50 years old or > 50 years old), fracture diagnosis (trimalleolar fractures, bimalleolar fractures, or general ankle fractures), and study design (cross-sectional, prospective, or retrospective). Due to the limited number of studies, subgroup analyses were performed only for walking speed, step length, and cadence.

Age subgroup analysis

The included studies divided participants into two groups based on age: ≤ 50 years and > 50 years.

Step length

≤ 50 years old: WMD = − 0.16, 95% CI [− 0.20,− 0.11], P = 0.712, I2 = 0.0%.

> 50 years old: WMD = − 0.15, 95% CI [− 0.18,− 0.11].

Step length analysis showed that heterogeneity decreased significantly after excluding one study with participants older than 50 years (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Cadence

≤ 50 years old: WMD = − 8.52, 95% CI [− 12.74, − 4.30], P = 0.070, I2 = 69.5%.

> 50 years old: WMD = − 8.40, 95% CI [− 11.38, − 5.42].

No subgroup differences were found in the step frequency analysis (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Walking speed

≤ 50 years old: WMD = − 0.43, 95% CI [− 0.64, − 0.21], P = 0.000, I2 = 89.9%.

> 50 years old: WMD = − 0.17, 95% CI [− 0.35, 0.01], P = 0.000, I2 = 89.6%.

In the > 50 years group, there was no significant difference in walking speed between postoperative patients and healthy controls. Also, no significant reduction in heterogeneity was observed across the two outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Fracture diagnosis subgroup analysis

Studies were stratified into three diagnostic categories: trimalleolar fractures, bimalleolar fractures, and general ankle fractures. No significant subgroup differences were detected for step length or cadence, and walking speed analysis could not be performed for certain subgroups. Pooled results for step length and cadence remained consistent, with postoperative patients showing reduced values compared with healthy controls:

Step length

Trimalleolar fractures: WMD = − 0.16, 95% CI [− 0.22, − 0.11], P < 0.001, I2 = 0.0%.

General ankle fractures: WMD = − 0.14, 95% CI [− 0.18, − 0.11], P < 0.001, I2 = 0.0% (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Cadence

General ankle fractures: WMD = − 8.93, 95% CI [− 11.85, − 6.02], P < 0.001, I2 = 65.7%.

Bimalleolar fractures: WMD = 7.30, 95% CI [− 11.72, − 2.88], P = 0.001.

For walking speed, subgroup analysis indicated that trimalleolar fractures did not show significant mean differences compared with healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Walking speed

Trimalleolar fractures: WMD = − 0.32, 95% CI [− 0.69, 0.05], P = 0.092, I2 = 96.2%.

General ankle fractures: WMD = − 0.29, 95% CI [− 0.37, − 0.22], P < 0.001, I2 = 36.6%.

Bimalleolar fractures: WMD = − 0.17, 95% CI [− 0.30, − 0.04], P = 0.008.

No significant reduction in heterogeneity was observed across these three outcomes, suggesting that fracture type was not a major source of heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Study design subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis by study design showed consistent results for walking speed in both prospective and retrospective studies, whereas cross-sectional studies demonstrated greater heterogeneity and non-significant differences.

Walking speed

Cross-sectional studies: WMD = − 0.42, 95% CI [− 0.87, 0.03], P = 0.007, I2 = 97.3%.

Prospective studies: WMD = − 0.32, 95% CI [− 0.47, − 0.18], P < 0.001, I2 = 56.8%.

Retrospective studies: WMD = − 0.18, 95% CI [− 0.32, − 0.05], P = 0.009, I2 = 69.8%.

For step length, all three study types demonstrated consistent significant differences between postoperative patients and healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis identified Zhu 2022 [32], Hsu 2019 [29], Segal 2014 [30] and Hoekstra 2023 [28] as sources of heterogeneity for walking speed. Zhu 2022 [32] was the primary source of heterogeneity in step length, while Hsu 2019 [29] contributed to heterogeneity in cadence (Supplementary Figs. 1–6).

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger’s regression test, Begg’s test, and the trim-and-fill method. Funnel plot asymmetry, together with the results of Egger’s and Begg’s tests, suggested potential publication bias in the step length analysis (Supplementary Figs. 16–21). After two iterations, one potentially missing study was identified. Secondary pooled analysis indicated that postoperative patients had significantly shorter step length than healthy controls (WMD = − 0.151, 95% CI [− 0.176, − 0.125], P < 0.001).

Qualitative synthesis

Although the qualitative synthesis provides insights into muscle strength and plantar pressure following ankle fracture, these conclusions are based on a limited number of available studies. This small evidence base restricts the generalizability of the findings and highlights the need for further high-quality research. The implications of the current synthesis may be influenced by limited sample sizes and methodological differences among the included studies.

Muscle strength

No controlled studies have reported changes in muscle strength after ankle fracture; however, two prospective, uncontrolled observational studies were included in this review.

One study evaluated 24 patients with stable, isolated lateral malleolar fractures treated conservatively with a circular cast [27]. Isokinetic testing using a CYBEX dynamometer measured peak torque (PT) and total work done (TWD) for both dorsiflexion and plantarflexion at multiple time points. After cast removal, the injured limb showed a 25.6% reduction in dorsiflexor PT and a 44.7% reduction in plantarflexor PT compared with the uninjured side. Endurance, measured as TWD, was also markedly decreased (37.8% deficit in dorsiflexion and 54.1% in plantarflexion). After three months of supervised rehabilitation, these deficits improved substantially (to 10.3% and 3.6% for strength, and 6.2% and 13.6% for endurance, respectively). By six months, both PT and endurance for dorsiflexion and plantarflexion were comparable with the uninjured side.

Another study used MRI to assess cross-sectional area (CSA) changes in specific thigh muscles during immobilization and rehabilitation after ankle or foot fracture [31]. Significant muscle atrophy was observed in the vastus lateralis (VL, − 10.9%), vastus intermedius (VI, − 8.4%), and vastus medialis (VM, − 11.2%) during the non-weight-bearing period. CSA loss was most pronounced at the mid-thigh and adjacent 5% intervals, and most atrophy was reversed after rehabilitation. In contrast, only the semitendinosus (ST) muscle showed persistent atrophy after recovery, particularly at the proximal thigh.

Plantar pressure

Only one study provided a detailed assessment of plantar pressure [34]. The analysis included mean and peak plantar pressure, contact area, and contact time, measured using a pressure platform at 6 and 12 months post-surgery. Compared with healthy controls, postoperative patients demonstrated significantly lower mean and peak plantar pressures and shorter contact times, with large weighted mean differences (0.63–0.97). Moreover, moderate negative correlations were observed between plantar pressure (mean and peak) and both ankle and calf circumferences (r = − 0.435 to − 0.674), suggesting that greater muscle atrophy and swelling were associated with lower plantar pressures. Despite gradual improvements over one year, plantar pressure and functional recovery remained incomplete [22].

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis comprehensively evaluated differences in gait, muscle strength, and plantar pressure between patients following ankle fracture surgery and healthy controls. Pooled results from 10 studies assessing gait parameters were included in the quantitative meta-analysis, which showed that postoperative patients exhibited significantly lower walking speed, DF angular velocity, step length, single support time, and cadence compared with healthy controls. Only the mean difference in peak plantarflexion (PF) angular velocity failed to reach statistical significance. This lack of significance may be explained by compensatory strategies and altered muscle recruitment after surgery, as patients often rely on extrinsic foot muscles and midfoot joints to assist in push-off, thereby masking deficits in ankle peak PF angular velocity. Variations in rehabilitation protocols and assessment timing may also play a role. Thus, although ankle and foot kinetics were generally reduced after fracture, specific parameters such as peak PF angular velocity may not differ significantly due to these adaptive mechanisms [28, 33]. In contrast, two studies examining muscle strength and plantar pressure were synthesized qualitatively. These studies highlighted reductions in dorsiflexor and plantar flexor strength, decreased cross-sectional area of key thigh muscles (including the vastus lateralis, vastus intermedius, and vastus medialis), and incomplete muscle recovery even after rehabilitation. Postoperative patients also demonstrated reduced plantar pressure and shorter contact times compared with healthy controls. Although both functional and plantar pressure outcomes improved over time, recovery remained incomplete even after one year.

Our analysis revealed that postoperative gait parameters following ORIF seldom return to levels observed in healthy individuals, consistent with previous studies. The greatest heterogeneity was observed in walking speed assessments. Prior research has reported a positive linear relationship between walking speed and peak plantar pressure (PPP) in healthy individuals [35]. However, in patients with ankle fractures, walking speed showed a negative correlation with ankle dorsiflexion range of motion (ADF ROM) [22]. This counterintuitive finding may be explained by several factors. Patients with greater dorsiflexion after surgery may also experience joint laxity, swelling, or discomfort, leading to slower walking as a protective gait strategy. Conversely, patients with more restricted dorsiflexion may have benefited from more effective rehabilitation, enabling greater confidence and faster walking despite limited ROM. Thus, pain, stiffness, and psychological factors likely contribute to this observation [22]. Walking speed and rhythm were identified as predictors for 76% of contact time outcomes, with variability attributed to concerns about ankle injury, pain, or instability [30, 32]. Heterogeneity in postoperative walking speed among patients with ankle fractures may also be influenced by fracture type (e.g., bimalleolar versus unimalleolar) or differences in gait analysis protocols. Regardless of numerical variability, studies consistently demonstrated impaired walking speed after surgery. Additionally, unilateral lower limb immobilization can induce weakness and muscle loss in both the injured and uninjured limbs. Therefore, rehabilitation should include bilateral assessment and training. Protocols should incorporate resistance, endurance, range of motion, and proprioceptive exercises for both limbs to prevent disuse atrophy and promote optimal recovery [27]. A study evaluating postoperative plantar pressure identified moderate negative correlations (− 0.435 ≤ r ≤ − 0.674) between both mean and peak plantar pressure and ankle and calf circumference in patients with ankle fractures [32, 36]. This suggests that larger limb circumference—whether due to postoperative swelling or residual muscle bulk—is associated with lower plantar pressure, likely reflecting impaired limb function or altered load distribution after surgery. The non-significant results observed in the trimalleolar fracture subgroup are likely attributable to the relatively small sample size and the resulting wide confidence intervals, which reduced statistical power. In contrast, the general ankle fracture group included a larger sample and a broader fracture spectrum, resulting in more stable and statistically significant estimates. Furthermore, adhesions and soft tissue complications following injury and surgery can increase stiffness, reduce ankle range of motion, and contribute to calf muscle atrophy. These alterations reasonably impact plantar pressure, step length, single-limb support time, and stride frequency. We assert that the causes of postoperative gait changes are multifactorial and non-specific. Previous studies have suggested that changes in plantar pressure distribution after surgery may result either from external biomechanical alterations of the joint or from adaptive strategies to relieve pain; however, it remains impossible to determine the precise contribution of each factor.

The Zhu et al. 2022 [32] study was identified as a major source of heterogeneity in walking speed and step length analyses. Several factors may explain its outlier effect. First, this study exclusively included patients with trimalleolar fractures, representing a more severe injury with greater functional limitations, as reflected by lower OMAS scores. Second, gait was assessed at a relatively early postoperative stage (mean 4.5 months), when functional recovery is often incomplete, whereas most other studies evaluated patients at later stages [37]. Third, the study employed a highly sensitive Vicon motion capture system for gait analysis, potentially detecting gait abnormalities more effectively than other methods [38]. Finally, the small sample size and the presence of additional injuries, such as syndesmotic disruption or ankle dislocation, may have introduced further variability. These methodological and clinical differences likely contributed to the pronounced heterogeneity observed when including this study in the meta-analysis.

Common rehabilitation elements across studies included progressive weight-bearing (initiated between 4 and 12 weeks postoperatively) [16, 22, 27, 32], active and passive ankle range of motion (ROM) exercises [16, 22, 27, 29], and muscle strengthening, particularly targeting the tibialis anterior, peroneals, and calf muscles [22, 26]. However, critical variations in protocols likely influenced the observed outcomes. The timing of rehabilitation initiation differed substantially—for example, Suciu et al. began exercises upon weight-bearing permission [16], whereas Böpple et al. initiated rehabilitation at 9 weeks [27]. Exercise intensity, frequency, and duration were rarely quantified. Ekinci et al., for instance, implemented a structured 6-month isokinetic strengthening program (3 × /week), resulting in near-complete strength recovery [27], whereas other studies describing “standard” or “exercise-based” rehabilitation provided limited details [28, 30, 31]. Supervised versus home-based programs and the inclusion of balance, proprioceptive, or functional task training also varied [16, 22]. These differences may partially explain divergent functional recovery timelines and residual deficits. Studies employing early, structured, and intensive rehabilitation protocols (e.g., Suciu et al.—12 weeks [16]; Ekinci et al.—6 months [27]) demonstrated greater improvements in OMAS/AOFAS scores [16] and gait symmetry. Conversely, studies lacking protocol details or using less intensive programs observed persistent biomechanical alterations (e.g., Fernández-Gorgojo et al. [22], van Hoeve et al. [17], Zhu et al. [32]). Fracture severity, particularly trimalleolar fractures, was associated with greater ROM deficits [17, 26, 32], suggesting that rehabilitation intensity should be tailored based on fracture type, as implied by van Hoeve et al. and Segal et al. The lack of detailed reporting in some studies [28, 30, 31] limits definitive conclusions regarding optimal rehabilitation strategies.

Overall, the findings of this review emphasize the importance of early, structured, and individualized rehabilitation programs following ankle fracture surgery. Clinicians should consider evidence-based protocols that incorporate bilateral strengthening, endurance, and proprioceptive training to address deficits in both the injured and uninjured limbs. Furthermore, the persistent gait and strength impairments observed—even one year postoperatively—highlight the need for ongoing functional assessment using objective measures, such as gait analysis and plantar pressure testing. Tailoring rehabilitation intensity and duration according to fracture severity and patient-specific factors may optimize outcomes. In addition, integrating patient education and psychosocial support can help address pain, fear of re-injury, and adherence to rehabilitation, ultimately enhancing recovery and quality of life.

In our meta-analysis, substantial heterogeneity was observed across several outcome measures, notably walking speed, step length, and cadence. This reflects the complex interplay of patient characteristics, fracture patterns, surgical and rehabilitation protocols, as well as methodological differences among studies. Variations in factors such as age, sex, BMI, fracture type and severity, surgical approach, rehabilitation timing and intensity, study design, sample size, and outcome measurement likely all contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Furthermore, unmeasured variables including pre-injury activity levels, adherence to rehabilitation, and socioeconomic status may have further influenced the results.

Additionally, while we systematically assessed the risk of bias and potential publication bias using multiple approaches, limitations in study quality may still impact the overall conclusions of our meta-analysis. Most included studies were observational, carrying inherent risks such as selection bias, lack of randomization, and inconsistent blinding procedures. Incomplete reporting of key methodological aspects such as allocation concealment, loss to follow-up, and outcome measurement protocols could introduce additional bias or confounding factors. These limitations may result in either overestimation or underestimation of the true effects, particularly in analyses where publication bias is suggested, such as for step length. Moreover, variability in study design and quality may have further contributed to heterogeneity in the pooled estimates.

Limitations

While the findings of this review are consistent with previous studies, several limitations in study design and methodology must be acknowledged. Conducting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in this field presents significant practical and ethical challenges. These include concerns about withholding standard treatments, difficulties in standardizing surgical and rehabilitation protocols across centers, and challenges in recruiting sufficient patient numbers due to the low incidence and heterogeneity of fracture types. Patient preferences, urgency of surgery, and injury variations further complicate randomization. Future research could address these barriers through multicenter collaborations, standardized protocols, pragmatic trial designs, and the use of large registries combined with advanced statistical methods to mitigate bias in observational studies. As most current evidence is derived from observational studies, which are susceptible to confounding and selection bias, the generalizability of the results is limited. Prospective RCTs are needed to provide more robust evidence. Notably, although only two single-arm studies were included, both utilized matched controls or normative data to allow meaningful postoperative comparisons.

Second, the meta-analysis was constrained by the availability of raw data, limiting a comprehensive evaluation of key parameters, such as ankle range of motion. This limitation also restricted the ability to conduct subgroup analyses for covariates that could influence heterogeneity in the pooled results, including critical factors such as time since fracture and time to surgery. Furthermore, certain outcomes—including peak dorsiflexion and plantarflexion angular velocity, step length, and cadence—were reported in only a small number of studies, warranting caution when interpreting these results.

Finally, while differences in gait parameters between healthy individuals and postoperative ankle fracture patients were evident, these disparities were primarily observed in basic spatiotemporal measures (e.g., walking speed, step length, and cadence). Although highlighting these differences underscores the limitations of current surgical and rehabilitation interventions, further research is needed to elucidate the underlying pathological mechanisms—such as neuromuscular deficits, proprioceptive impairment, muscle weakness, and altered joint kinematics. Investigating these factors could provide valuable insights and identify targets for future interventions. Additionally, despite a comprehensive and systematic literature search, no controlled studies specifically addressing changes in muscle strength following ankle fracture were identified, representing a notable gap in the current evidence base. High-quality controlled studies are therefore needed to clarify muscle strength recovery in this patient population.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that while gait, muscle strength, and plantar pressure improve after ORIF for ankle fractures, full functional recovery may not be achieved in all patients, even following rehabilitation. Clinicians are encouraged to implement early, structured, and bilateral rehabilitation programs focusing on strength, joint mobility, and proprioception to optimize outcomes. Future research should compare specific rehabilitation protocols to identify the most effective strategies for restoring function after ankle fracture surgery.
