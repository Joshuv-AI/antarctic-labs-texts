Demographic Change in Prevalent Type 1 Diabetes Adult Population, and Association of Risk Factors With Comorbidities and Mortality—Results From the Finnish Diabetes Control Study FinDiCon
Authors: Hannu Vessari, Tuire Prami, Essi J Peltonen, Elina Pimiä, Ilona Iso‐Mustajärvi, Marita Kalaoja, Hanne Luoto, Ilkka Vauhkonen, Tero Saukkonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe objective was to characterise Finnish adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and to explore associations between known risk factors, comorbidities and mortality.Materials and MethodsIndividuals with T1D in 2014–2021 were identified from national health care registers, and data on medication and comorbidities were combined with laboratory measurements from hospital data lakes. Comorbidity and risk‐factor associations were examined using Cox regression.ResultsWe identified 35 574 adults with T1D. The proportion of individuals aged over 70 years increased during the study period from 7.1% to 12.4% and the proportion of insulin pump users from 10% to 17%. Mean haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) decreased by 1.7 mmol/mol (0.2%) and mean low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐c) by 0.2 mmol/L (7.7 mg/dL) from 2014 to 2021. The proportion of individuals with HbA1c > 75 mmol/mol (> 9.0%) reduced slightly (4%‐points); this improvement was more prominent in insulin pump users (9%‐points). Ketoacidosis was most common in young adults. Prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) increased from 140 (95% CI 136–145) to 168 (95% CI 163–172) per 1000 persons during the study period (p‐trend < 0.001). CKD and vascular disease resulted in a multifold increase in the mortality risk, whereas lipid‐lowering and antihypertensive medications reduced this risk (p < 0.001 for all).ConclusionsThe proportion of elderly with T1D is increasing, with no concomitant increase in vascular events. Insulin pump therapy has become more common without a concomitant increase in ketoacidosis incidence. HbA1c and LDL‐c levels improved during the study. Finnish data indicate a shift in T1D demographics, characterised by improved survival, while rates of diabetes‐specific comorbidities remain stable.Keywords: cohort study, insulin pump therapy, insulin therapy, type 1 diabetes

Introduction

The prevalence of type 1 diabetes (T1D) is increasing globally [1]. As the disease is associated with an increased risk of long‐term comorbidities and increased mortality [2], this rising trend challenges healthcare systems, highlighting the need for improved public health strategies and better management approaches [3].

Effective treatment is essential in preventing complications and reducing long‐term comorbidities in T1D [4]. Insulin replacement therapy is vital for managing T1D [5], and good glycaemic control has been shown to prevent microvascular and macrovascular events, as demonstrated in the DCCT/EDIC study [6, 7]. Achieving tight glycaemic control is often challenging. In addition to glycaemic control, preventing complications in T1D requires the management of other cardiovascular risk factors. Management of hypertension and dyslipidemia with angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers and statins has been shown to improve cardiovascular outcomes in T1D [8, 9, 10, 11].

Despite comprehensive risk factor management, T1D is still associated with excess morbidity and mortality. However, recent evidence from large cohort studies has shown improvements in cardiovascular outcomes and life expectancy in T1D [12, 13, 14], and a recent global study reported increased life expectancy among older people with T1D since 1990 along with a significant decrease in diabetes‐associated mortality [15].

Finland has one of the highest prevalences of T1D globally [16]. A recent study reported improved survival among Finnish individuals with T1D during 1972–2017. However, life expectancy in this group was still much lower than in the general Finnish population [13]. Another Finnish study recently reported substantial excess mortality during 2018–2022 [17], although mortality rates had decreased compared to an earlier study reporting mortality during 1991–2014 [13].

Over the past decade, the use of advanced diabetes technology, for example, automated insulin delivery (AID) pumps and glucose sensors, has become more common among individuals with T1D in Finland. Furthermore, updated treatment guidelines have been published, recommending, for example, lower targets of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐c). We therefore aimed to examine recent trends in glycaemic control, LDL‐c, and other clinical factors as well as diabetes‐related comorbidities among Finnish adults with T1D. Because of increasing evidence of longer life expectancy in this population, we were particularly interested in examining demographic shifts over time. To address these questions, we analysed nationwide registry data covering all Finnish adults with T1D from 2014 to 2021.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources

Finnish adults with T1D were identified in a nationwide, retrospective register study: the Finnish Diabetes Control Study FinDiCon. A study permit was granted and data linkage was performed by the Finnish Social and Health Data Permit Authority Findata. The national administrative registers used for cohort identification (2014–2021) were the patient registers of the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare and the prescription registers of the Social Insurance Institution of Finland. For history and follow‐up, study data were obtained from several administrative registers of the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (1987–2021; these registers covered visits, diagnoses, laboratory measurements, and procedures in primary, secondary and tertiary healthcare), of the Social Insurance Institution of Finland (data on medication purchases, prescriptions, and reimbursements for medication), of Statistics Finland (time‐of‐death data), and from various local hospital data lakes (2014–2021; laboratory measurements). Finnish nationwide administrative registers cover data on all residents of Finland (approximately 5.5 million people). All dispensed prescriptions are recorded in the prescription registers used in the study. Data lakes are local and cover data on all individuals with healthcare contacts in the relevant region. The data lakes used in this study cover approximately 90% of the Finnish population.

Study Cohort

Individuals aged ≥ 18 years old with T1D were identified from national health care registers between January 01, 2014, and December 31, 2021 (cohort entry period) on the basis of (1) having at least two T1D specific health care visits during this period AND (2) having continuous insulin treatment with at least two annual insulin purchases annually. Patients were required to have a higher number of health care visits specific to type 1 diabetes (ICD‐10 E10 and/or ICPC‐2 T89) than type 2 diabetes (ICD‐10 E11 and/or ICPC‐2 T90) on separate dates. After the identification of T1D, an individual diagnosis date was defined for each person. This date was the first date of either a visit with a diagnosis code for diabetes, an insulin purchase, or an entitlement for reimbursement status for diabetes. Individuals with T1D were followed up from the diagnosis date onwards until the end of the study period or death, whichever came first (follow‐up period). The outline of the cohort entry period and individual follow‐up period are presented in Figure S1.

Laboratory Values and Comorbidities

When evaluating the annual distribution of laboratory values of glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), LDL‐c, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin‐creatinine ratio (uACR), the mean value of each calendar year was used for each individual. The chronic kidney disease equation (CKD‐EPI) formula was used to calculate eGFR values, and urine albuminuria values were converted to the uACR scale. Laboratory tests were taken in a standardised, accredited manner in a routine clinical setting to obtain plasma and urine samples. The HbA1c and LDL‐c distributions were classified according to the cut‐off values presented in the Finnish Current Care Guideline [18], and eGFR and uACR distributions were calculated based on the International Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification [19]. Information on the numbers of different laboratory measurements taken per individual per year, as well as summary statistics of any repeated measures during the year, was calculated as supplemental information. The mean of each individual's annual measurements was calculated before calculating the overall descriptive statistics.

Definitions for comorbidities and deaths are presented in Table S1. The incidence dates of comorbidities were defined as the first occurrence of the diagnosis or procedure code presented in the definition (ischaemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, vascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), renal replacement therapy, and eye complications), or as subsequent occurrences of the codes for (coronary artery procedures, ketoacidosis, and ankle, foot or toe amputations). Due to the serial nature of eye procedures, only one procedure from each procedure code group was included for each individual each year. These comorbidities and procedures were selected since they are clinically relevant and they can be identified from the registries with good reliability.

Statistical Analyses

Absolute and relative frequencies were used to characterise the study cohort. Incidence rates for different comorbidities and deaths were assessed annually and reported per 1000 person‐years. Similarly, two‐year prevalences were calculated and reported per 1000 persons based on the average size of the T1D cohort. To assess the effect of aging on the occurrence of comorbidities, age‐standardised incidence and prevalence were calculated, using the person‐year distribution by age groups at the end of study period as the standard population. Unstandardized incidence and prevalence were additionally calculated for subgroups based on sex, age group (18–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, and 70 years) and duration of T1D (0–9, 10–19, 20–29, 30–39, and 40‐years).

Cox proportional hazards model was utilised to explore the associations between pre‐specified risk factors, and various T1D related comorbidities and deaths, separately for each. The risk factors included were duration of T1D (continuous variable, years), age at study entry (continuous, years), sex, CKD stage (eGFR > 90, 60–< 90, 30–< 60, 15–< 30, 0–< 15 mL/min/1.73m2), albuminuria category (uACR < 3, 3–30, > 30 mg/mmol), HbA1c (continuous, mmol/mol), LDL‐c (continuous, mmol/L), triglycerides to high‐density lipoprotein (TG/HDL‐c) ratio (continuous), prior vascular disease (no, yes), use of lipid‐lowering medication (no, yes), and antihypertensive medication (number of medication groups 0, 1–2, 3 or more). The means of an individual's serial HbA1c, TG/HDL‐c, and LDL‐c values were used to represent continuous laboratory measurements in the models. Missing laboratory data values were not imputed, but for categorised laboratory measurements, missing values were coded as a separate category. The use of lipid‐lowering and antihypertensive medication were treated as time‐dependent variables in the models. Time‐dependent variables were updated at the beginning of every year during the follow‐up period based on drug purchases during that year. As a sensitivity analysis, models with categorised TG/HDL‐c and LDL‐c values and a separate category for missing values were fitted to inspect the effect of a large proportion of missing data in these variables. Categories for LDL‐c were formed based on Finnish Current Care Guidelines (< 1.4, 1.4–< 1.8, 1.8–< 2.6, ≥ 2.6 mmol/L; < 54.1, 54.1–< 69.6, 69.6–< 100.5, ≥ 100.5 mg/dL) [18] and those for TG/HDL‐c as quartiles in the whole study population (< 0.43, 0.43–< 0.65, 0.65–< 1.07, ≥ 1.07 mmol/L; < 16.6, 16.6–< 25.1, 25.1–< 41.4, ≥ 41.4 mg/dL).

Comorbidities, which were defined as the first occurrence of a diagnosis or a procedure code, were included in the survival analysis as endpoints. Analysis populations were formed separately for the analysis of each endpoint, and they included individuals with no prior history of the comorbidity in question and with at least two HbA1c measurements after the T1D diagnosis. The start of follow‐up for survival analyses was the date of the first HbA1c measurement after the T1D diagnosis. Individuals who died or who were not diagnosed with the comorbidity, were right‐censored at the end of study period or at the date of death. The nominal p value threshold was Bonferroni‐corrected to account for multiple testing by the number of risk factors in the survival models (0.05/11 = 0.0045). Trends over time for categorical and laboratory variables were examined using generalised estimation equations to account for correlation between repeated observations involving the same individual. Trends in incidence and prevalence were evaluated using Poisson regression with person‐years or population size as offset term. Years were used as a continuous variable to obtain p values for trends across the study years. The data were analysed using the statistical software R Studio version 4.2.2 for Windows.

Ethics

A study permit was granted by the Finnish Social and Health Data Permit Authority Findata with the record numbers THL/995/14.02.00/2022 and THL/267/14.06.00/2023. The study protocol was subjected to ethical consideration during the permit process. Pseudonymized data were stored and analysed in a validated secure data environment provided by Statistics Finland. Results with frequencies of < 5 persons were not reported.

Data and Resource Availability

The data of this study are available from Finnish national health care registers, but access requires approval from Findata and Statistics Finland.

Results

A total of 35 574 adults with T1D were identified in Finland in 2021 of whom 57.3% were male (Table 1). The mean age of the population in 2021 was 47.8 [standard deviation (SD) 17.3] years, and the mean duration of T1D was 25.0 (SD 14.5) years. On average 530 new cases of T1D were identified each year, with no evident change during covid‐19 pandemia. Even though the number of new cases reduced between 2014 and 2021, the absolute number of individuals increased by more than 3700 individuals. The number of individuals aged ≥ 70 years increased during the study period from 2254 (7.1%) to 4438 (12.4%) (p‐trend < 0.001). The number of individuals using insulin pump therapy increased annually from 3180 (10.0%) in 2014 to 5952 (16.7%) in 2021 (p‐trend < 0.001). During the study period, the number of individuals using regular antihypertensive medication increased from 12 721 (39.9%) to 16 856 (47.4%) (p‐trend < 0.001), and the number of individuals using regular lipid‐lowering medication increased from 8675 (27.2%) to 16 074 (45.2%) (p‐trend < 0.001).

Laboratory Values

The distributions of categorised HbA1c, LDL, eGFR and uACR values are presented in Figure 1. Mean HbA1c was 67.3 (SD 15.8) mmol/mol [8.3 (SD 3.6) %] in 2014 and 65.4 (SD 15.2) mmol/mol [8.1 (SD 3.5) %] in 2021. The proportion of individuals with HbA1c > 75 mmol/mol (> 9.0%) reduced from 27% to 23% during the study period; among patients using an insulin pump, the corresponding proportion reduced from 28% to 17% showing greater improvement (p‐trend < 0.001 for both) (Figure 1). Mean LDL‐c was 2.6 (SD 0.8) mmol/L (100.5 (SD 30.9) mg/dL) in 2014 and 2.4 (SD 0.8) mmol/L [92.8 (SD 30.9) mg/dL] in 2021. The proportion of individuals with LDL‐c ≥ 2.6 mmol/L (100.5 mg/dL) decreased from 48% to 36.5% while the proportion of individuals with LDL‐c below 1.8 mmol/L (69.6 mg/dL) increased from 12.8% to 22.8% during the study period (Figure 1). A linear decreasing trend was detected for LDL‐c across the study years (p‐trend < 0.001). Levels of eGFR and uACR remained somewhat static; the mean eGFR was 93.9 (SD 27.7) mL/min/1.73 m2 in 2014 and 92.9 (SD 25.9) mL/min/1.73 m2 in 2021 and mean uACR 9.4 (SD 46.7) mg/mmol and 9.3 (SD 40.5) mg/mmol, respectively. More detailed data on laboratory measurements are shown in Table S2.

Comorbidities

Age‐standardised incidence rates of pre‐selected comorbidities of special interest are shown in Figure 2A (for definitions of comorbidities, see Table S1). Eye procedures were the most common comorbidity and their incidence increased from 56 (95% confidence interval [CI] 53–59) to 68 (95% CI 66–71) per 1000 person‐years during the study period (p‐trend < 0.001). Ketoacidosis was the second most common comorbidity, and its incidence varied during the study period, being the lowest in the latest study year. Incidence of ketoacidosis was higher among insulin pump‐users compared to non‐users, but the overall trend followed a similar pattern in both groups (Figure S2). Death rates showed an increasing trend (p‐trend < 0.001), rising during the initial study years and stabilising thereafter, with minor variation in the latest study years.

Age‐standardised incidence (A) and prevalence (B) of diabetes‐related comorbidities and deaths. *Increasing trend across study years was detected for the comorbidity (p < 0.0045).

A decrease in eGFR, an increase in albuminuria and a history of vascular disease resulted in a multifold increase in the mortality risk (Table 2). Regular lipid‐lowering medication was the most effective factor in lowering the mortality risk (hazard ratio (HR) 0.18, 95% CI 0.16–0.20, p < 0.001). Other factors associated with a decreased mortality risk were female sex, lower LDL‐c and the use of a greater number of antihypertensive medication groups (Table 2). Incidence of ankle, foot, or toe amputation increased slightly during the study period and an increasing trend was detected for subsequent ankle, foot or toe amputations (p‐trend < 0.001) (Figure 2A). The duration of T1D affected the risk of amputations in all age groups except for the youngest (Figure S3). End‐stage CKD increased the risk of first amputation (HR 4.1, 95% CI 2.7–6.1, p < 0.001), as did prior vascular disease (HR 5.5, 95% CI 4.2–7.1, p < 0.001). End‐stage CKD increased also the risk of ischaemic heart disease (HR 3.2, 95% CI 2.3–4.4, p < 0.001) and cerebrovascular disorder (HR 2.2, 95% CI 1.4–3.6, p = 0.001). HbA1c had a risk‐increasing association with all comorbidities except renal replacement therapy, with the largest effect on ankle, foot or toe amputations (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.05–1.07, p < 0.001) (Tables 2 and 3). LDL‐c had the largest effect on ischaemic heart disease (HR 1.7, 95% CI 1.6–1.8, p < 0.001) and a risk‐increasing association with all comorbidities except mortality. In the sensitivity analysis the HRs remained similar compared to the main analysis for all other variables except LDL‐c and TG/HDL‐c ratio, which were modelled as categorical variables in the sensitivity analysis (Tables S3A and S3B).

Prevalence of eye complications increased during the study period (p‐trend < 0.001), reaching 659 (95% CI 650–667) per 1000 persons at the end of study period (Figure 2B). Unlike other comorbidities, eye complications were more prevalent in women than in men during the study years. This comorbidity was also less prevalent in the age group of 18–39 years compared to other age groups (Figure S3). Prevalence of CKD increased during the study period from 140 (95% CI 136–145) to 168 (95% CI 163–172) per 1000 persons (p‐trend < 0.001), while the prevalence of peripheral vascular disease remained more stable. Female sex was associated with lower risk of peripheral vascular disease (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.87, p < 0.001) (Table 3). A smaller, although statistically significant (p < 0.001), increasing trend was also detected for vascular disease. There was no change in the prevalence of renal replacement therapies.

Discussion

In this Finnish nationwide register study, the number and proportion of individuals aged over 70 years with T1D nearly doubled, and the number and proportion of those over 80 years of age doubled during the eight‐year study period. Although the prevalence of T1D increased among the elderly, no similar increase was seen in macrovascular complications, except for CKD. This finding was confirmed by age‐adjusted analyses. Modest improvements seen in glycaemic control and LDL‐c likely contributed, at least in part, to the stable rates of macrovascular complications. In addition, the proportion of patients receiving antihypertensive and lipid‐lowering medication increased, consistent with intensified cardiovascular risk factor management. The improvement in glycaemic control was seen especially in insulin pump users.

The excess morbidity and mortality in T1D is primarily due to disturbed glucose metabolism, mainly driven by chronic hyperglycaemia [20]. Intensive management of glucose levels has been shown to be vital in preventing micro‐ and macrovascular complications, as evidenced by the DCCT/EDIC study [6, 7]. In the present study, each 1 mmol/mol increase in HbA1c was associated with a 2% higher risk of ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease and all‐cause mortality. Thus, a difference of 11 mmol/mol (e.g., HbA1c 64 mmol/mol [8.0%] vs. 53 mmol/mol [7.0%]) corresponds to a relative increase of approximately 24% in these outcomes in our study population. Strikingly, half of the individuals in the present study had HbA1c > 64 mmol/mol (8%), whereas only approximately 20% had well‐controlled diabetes with HbA1c < 53 mmol/mol (7%). There was, however, a trend for improvement in glycaemic control, mainly explained by increased use of insulin pumps and by the improved glycaemic control in insulin pump users during the study period.

Maintaining long‐term glycaemic control with multiple daily injections (MDI) in T1D is challenging, and often compromised by hypoglycaemia prevention or fear of hypoglycaemia. Advanced technology, especially AID pumps, have been shown to improve glycaemic control as compared to MDI treatment combined with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) [21]. Although insulin pump therapy increased over time in our study, only 17% of adults with T1D were using it at the end of the study period. Notably, insulin pumps have been widely used among children and adolescents in Finland for many years, and, to our knowledge, their use has also continued to increase among adults with T1D after the end of our study period [22]. Recently published Finnish Current Care Guideline update for Insulin‐deficient Diabetes recommends wide adaptation of AID pumps to treat T1D [23]. The use of diabetes technology is expected to lead to better glycaemic control, which is likely to reduce the risk of long‐term complications. There is also increasing evidence that AID pumps can be used among the elderly with T1D [24].

The life expectancy of elderly people with T1D has increased over the past three decades [15]. Impaired physical and cognitive function together with diabetes‐related complications pose considerable challenges and a considerable treatment burden in elderly people with T1D [25]. Our study showed that the number and proportion of elderly Finnish people with T1D have nearly doubled over a relatively short time. There was a decreasing trend in the 50–59‐ and 60–69‐year‐old age cohorts, concomitantly with an increase in the proportion of individuals with a T1D duration exceeding 50 years. Together, these findings indicate improved survival among Finnish individuals with T1D. Furthermore, there was a strong positive association between the prevalence of vascular disease and both increasing age and longer duration of diabetes, with no signs of decrease during the study period. As cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death among people with T1D [13], the secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease appears to have become more successful.

The multifactorial nature of diabetic complications underscores the need for comprehensive management strategies aimed at both controlling blood glucose and addressing other risk factors, such as hypertension and dyslipidemia [26]. In our study, the proportion of people taking antihypertensive medication increased from 39.9% to 47.4%, and there was an even more notable increase in lipid‐lowering medication use, which increased from 27.2% to 45.2%. Interestingly, use of lipid‐lowering medication started to increase more rapidly in 2018 when the updated Finnish Current Care Guideline for Dyslipidaemia was published. That guideline set LDL‐c target of < 1.8 mmol/L for individuals with very high risk for cardiovascular events [27]. During the study period, the proportion of individuals reaching LDL‐c < 1.8 mmol/L almost doubled and a decreasing trend for LDL‐c was observed across the study years. Despite the encouraging development, treatment targets are still not met for many individuals with T1D, which is also seen in other countries [28]. In our study, the regular use of antihypertensive and lipid‐lowering medications was strongly associated with lower mortality. Nevertheless, they were also associated with an increased incidence of macrovascular events. These findings together suggest that lipid‐lowering and antihypertensive medications may reduce the risk of fatal outcomes, despite the occurrence of macrovascular events.

Retinopathy is considered the complication most specific to diabetes with chronic hyperglycaemia as the main risk factor [29]. Also in our study, retinopathy was the most common complication, and it was clearly associated with higher HbA1c and longer duration of diabetes. We also saw an increasing trend in eye procedures in all age groups. This suggests that retinal fundus photography screening, which is widely available in Finland, may have led to earlier detection of retinopathy complications.

Ketoacidosis was the second most common complication in our study. Ketoacidosis was most frequently observed in young adults who had recently been diagnosed with T1D. In the most recently diagnosed individuals, the incidence of this condition increased throughout the study period, although a pooled analysis showed that the incidence varied in time and was lowest at the end of the study in 2021. Several studies have reported an increased risk of ketoacidosis associated with insulin pump therapy [26]. In our study, the incidence of ketoacidosis was higher among insulin pump users compared to those using MDI; however, the incidence showed a decreasing trend in both groups throughout the study period.

Albuminuria and reduced eGFR are both strong risk factors for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in individuals with T1D [30]. In our study, CKD was the only comorbidity with an increase in prevalence during the study period. Both albuminuria and reduced eGFR were, as anticipated, strongly associated with macrovascular comorbidities, lower limb amputations, and increased mortality. Annual screening for albuminuria as well as calculating eGFR are key in early diagnosis of CKD [31]. Strikingly, albuminuria screening and eGFR measurement were done annually in only about 30% of our study population or around half of those with annual HbA1c measurements. Furthermore, the coverage of these tests was even poorer during the COVID pandemic. This suggests that the prevalence of CKD is probably underestimated, and the diagnosis is likely delayed in people with T1D. Timely diagnosis of CKD is essential since early initiation of reno‐protective medication [32] and intensification of insulin treatment [33] have been shown to slow CKD progression.

The strength of this study is that we were able to link data from several nationwide registers for approximately 5.5 million citizens. This allowed us to study a large population with extensive data and conduct diverse analyses. There are, however, several limitations in this study. These registries lack structured data on several cardiovascular risk factors, such as smoking, blood pressure, body mass index, hypoglycaemic events, and family history for cardiovascular diseases. Psychiatric comorbidities related to drug use and alcohol abuse, which are known risk factors for mortality, were not included in this study. During the study period, the proportion of AID pump users was still relatively low, so results on pump users should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the use of CGM and the monitoring data were not integrated into these registries, so glycaemic variability, a significant predictor for both microvascular and macrovascular complications risks [34], could not be addressed. Also, comprehensive data on autoantibody determination was not available from the used registers so it is possible that people with other types of diabetes, other than type 1, are included in the study cohort.

This Finnish data set indicates that the demographic profile of the people with T1D is changing rapidly, as individuals live longer while the prevalence of diabetes‐specific comorbidities remains rather stable. The increasing number of elderly individuals with T1D may pose a significant challenge for the health care system in the future. The trends towards better outcomes seen during this study could be explained by intensified risk factor management and slight improvement in glycaemic control. The increasing use of AID pumps may improve glycaemic control in the future and hopefully further reduce excess mortality and morbidity among people with T1D.
