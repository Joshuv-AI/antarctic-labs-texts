Disaster nursing competencies and psychological resilience among registered nurses in post-pandemic Saudi arabia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Fathia Ahmed Mersal, Bander Saad Albagawi, Rasmia Abd El Sattar Ali, Zakaria Ahmed Mani, Radhi Krim Alshammari, Jaber Ali Nami, Atallah Alenezi, Salman Hamdan Alsaqri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisaster nursing refers to the knowledge, skills, and professional judgement required to prevent, prepare for, respond to, and recover from disaster events. Psychological resilience refers to the capacity to adapt to adversity while maintaining professional functioning. In Saudi Arabia, post-pandemic evaluation of these capacities is particularly relevant because nurses practise in a setting shaped by mass gatherings, emerging infectious disease threats, and ongoing emergency preparedness demands. However, evidence remains limited regarding the level of disaster nursing competency among registered nurses in Saudi Arabia and its relationship with psychological resilience in the post-pandemic period. This study assessed disaster nursing competencies and psychological resilience among registered nurses in Saudi Arabia and identified factors associated with disaster nursing competency.MethodsA cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted among registered nurses working in four regions of Saudi Arabia: Arar, Riyadh, Hail, and Jizan. Data were collected electronically over nine weeks from May to July 2025 using a demographic and professional characteristics questionnaire, the Disaster Nursing Ability Assessment Scale, and the Arabic version of the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale. Of 628 invited nurses, 512 returned the questionnaire; 22 incomplete responses were excluded, yielding a final analytical sample of 490 (78.0% response rate). Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression were used to analyse the data.ResultsParticipants reported a high overall level of disaster nursing competency (mean 4.01, standard deviation [SD] 0.66) and a moderate-to-high level of psychological resilience (mean 2.97, SD 0.78). Among competency domains, Disaster Reduction/Prevention had the highest mean score (4.06, SD 0.66) and Disaster Preparedness had the lowest (3.96, SD 0.71). Multiple linear regression identified three significant predictors of disaster nursing competency: higher educational level (β = 0.311, p < 0.001), urban residence (β = 0.195, p < 0.001), and prior formal disaster training (β = 0.185, p < 0.001).ConclusionsRegistered nurses in this sample demonstrated generally favourable disaster nursing competency and moderate-to-high psychological resilience, with disaster preparedness identified as the lowest-scoring domain. The positive association between competency and resilience, together with the modifiable predictors identified, suggests that targeted educational and training strategies may inform future nursing education, disaster preparedness training, and workforce development initiatives in Saudi Arabia.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Disasters, infectious disease outbreaks, and other large-scale emergencies place sustained pressure on health systems and require nurses to function effectively across prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery phases. Disaster nursing is defined as the knowledge, skills, professional judgement, and behaviours required by nurses to deliver safe and coordinated care before, during, and after disaster events [1, 2]. Psychological resilience is defined as the capacity to adapt to adversity, maintain professional functioning under stress, and recover from challenging experiences [3]. Both constructs are increasingly recognised as essential for nursing workforce preparedness in complex emergency settings [4–6].

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted weaknesses in healthcare preparedness worldwide and reinforced the central role of nurses in emergency response. During and after the pandemic, nurses faced high clinical demands, rapid workflow changes, emotional strain, and persistent uncertainty, all of which increased the importance of both technical preparedness and adaptive coping capacity [4, 5, 7, 8]. Evidence from intervention studies has shown that structured disaster training and resilience-focused interventions can improve nurses’ emergency readiness and psychological adaptation [4, 9–11]. Nevertheless, preparedness remains uneven, and competency gaps continue to be reported across different settings [12–16].

In Saudi Arabia, disaster preparedness in nursing has particular relevance. The healthcare system must remain ready for a wide range of emergency conditions, including infectious disease threats and the service demands associated with major mass gatherings such as the Hajj [1, 17–21]. These contextual factors increase the need for a nursing workforce that is both clinically competent in disaster management and psychologically prepared to sustain effective performance during high-pressure events. Previous Saudi and regional research have drawn attention to ongoing educational needs, variability in disaster preparedness, and the importance of strengthening disaster-related nursing capacity through structured training and curriculum development [13, 22–25].

Psychological resilience is also highly relevant to nursing performance in disaster contexts. Resilient nurses are generally better able to manage stress, maintain decision-making capacity, and continue to provide care during prolonged crises [26–29]. Resilience-building interventions among nursing professionals in Saudi Arabia have shown promising value for improving professional adaptation and well-being [8, 9]. Because disaster response requires both technical capability and emotional endurance, examining these two constructs together offers a more complete understanding of nursing preparedness than assessing either one alone [28, 30, 31].

Although earlier studies have examined disaster preparedness, educational needs, or resilience separately, evidence remains limited regarding the relationship between disaster nursing competencies and psychological resilience among registered nurses in Saudi Arabia, particularly in the post-pandemic period and across multiple regions [6]. This gap is important because understanding that relationship may help identify modifiable factors relevant to workforce development, continuing education, and preparedness planning at the institutional and national levels.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess disaster nursing competencies and psychological resilience among registered nurses in four regions of Saudi Arabia, examine the association between these constructs, and identify demographic and professional factors associated with disaster nursing competency.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional quantitative design. Data were collected from registered nurses working in four regions of Saudi Arabia selected to reflect geographic and service diversity: Arar (Northern), Riyadh (Central), Hail (North-Central), and Jizan (Southern). These regions differ in population distribution, healthcare infrastructure, and exposure to disaster-related demands, making them appropriate for a multiregional assessment of nursing preparedness [1, 17, 19].

Conceptual framework

The study was conceptually grounded in the International Council of Nurses (ICN) Core Competencies in Disaster Nursing, which outline nursing roles across the phases of prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery [2], as well as in Connor and Davidson’s (2003) framework of psychological resilience [32]. These frameworks informed the choice of study variables and measurement tools and are briefly revisited in the Discussion to support the interpretation of findings; however, they did not shape the sampling strategy or the analytical methods employed.

Sampling and participants

A multistage sampling approach was used. First, the four regions were purposively selected to ensure geographic diversity. Second, healthcare facilities within each region were stratified by ownership type (Ministry of Health, other governmental, private), level of care (primary, secondary, tertiary), and bed capacity (small < 100 beds; medium 100–300 beds; large > 300 beds). Within each stratum, facilities were selected using a computer-generated random sequence, yielding 24 participating facilities: 7 in Arar, 8 in Riyadh, 6 in Hail, and 3 in Jizan. Third, eligible nurses within the participating facilities were invited to participate through institutional communication channels, and respondents were enrolled by convenience as they returned the survey. Recruitment within facilities was therefore by invitation rather than random selection of individual nurses.

Participants were eligible if they were registered nurses licensed to practise in Saudi Arabia, had completed at least one year of clinical service at their current facility, and were directly involved in patient care. Nurses in purely administrative roles, those on extended leave during data collection, student nurses, and nursing interns were excluded. Responses with less than 80% questionnaire completion were excluded from analysis. A total of 628 nurses from participating facilities were invited to take part in the study. Of these, 512 returned completed questionnaires, of which 22 were excluded due to substantial missing data, resulting in 490 responses included in the final analysis (response rate: 78.0%). The final sample size exceeded the minimum requirement of 385, as determined using Cochran’s formula with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error.

Data collection

Data were collected electronically over nine weeks from May to July 2025 using Google Forms. Each participant received a study information sheet outlining the purpose, voluntary nature, confidentiality safeguards, and data protection procedures; electronic informed consent was obtained before questionnaire completion. A pilot test with 30 Saudi nurses was conducted before the main study to confirm the clarity and contextual relevance of the survey; pilot responses were not included in the final analysis.

Measures

Demographic and professional characteristics

A structured 10-item questionnaire collected age, gender, marital status, nationality, educational level, years of clinical experience, department, hospital type, previous formal disaster training, and participation in disaster drills.

Disaster nursing ability assessment scale

Disaster nursing competency was measured using the Disaster Nursing Ability Assessment Scale, a validated 55-item instrument with four domains: Disaster Reduction/Prevention, Disaster Preparedness, Disaster Response, and Recovery/Reconstruction [4]. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree); higher scores indicate greater perceived competency. Forward–backward translation by bilingual healthcare professionals, expert review of content validity, and pilot testing ensured contextual appropriateness for use in Saudi Arabia. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α was 0.92 for the total scale (subscale α 0.89–0.94), and confirmatory factor analysis supported the four-factor structure (CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.06). The Disaster Nursing Ability Assessment Scale is the only term used for this instrument throughout the manuscript.

Connor–davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC)

Psychological resilience was measured using the Arabic version of the 25-item Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale [32], which assesses three dimensions: Strength, Optimism, and Tenacity Control. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = not true at all to 4 = true nearly all the time); higher scores indicate greater resilience. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α was 0.91 for the total scale, and confirmatory factor analysis supported the three-factor structure (CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.06) [8].

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 and R version 4.3.1. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests with visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots, and homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics and study variables. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α. Pearson correlation coefficients examined associations between disaster nursing competency and psychological resilience, and multiple linear regression identified demographic and professional predictors of disaster nursing competency. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Hail (27 May 2025; Approval No. H-2025-837). Participation was voluntary, electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection, and no personally identifying information was retained in the analytical dataset. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participant characteristics

Most participants were female (411, 83.9%), aged 25–34 years (253, 51.6%), and held a bachelor’s degree (363, 73.9%). The majority were staff nurses (414, 84.5%), and 291 (59.4%) had more than five years of clinical experience. Regional representation was Riyadh (178, 36.3%), Arar (130, 26.5%), Hail (108, 22.0%), and Jizan (74, 15.1%). Prior occupational safety or disaster-related training was reported by 350 (71.4%) participants (Table 1).

Disaster nursing competency

Descriptive statistics for the four disaster nursing competency domains are presented in Table 2 and visualised in Fig. 1. The mean overall disaster nursing competency score was 4.01 (SD 0.66; 95% confidence interval [CI] 3.95–4.06). Disaster Reduction/Prevention had the highest mean score (4.06, SD 0.66; 95% CI 4.00–4.12), followed by Disaster Response (4.01, SD 0.71; 95% CI 3.95–4.07) and Recovery/Reconstruction (3.98, SD 0.70; 95% CI 3.92–4.04). Disaster Preparedness had the lowest mean score (3.96, SD 0.71; 95% CI 3.89–4.02) (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Predictors of disaster nursing competency

Multiple linear regression analysis identified demographic and professional predictors of disaster nursing competency (Table 4). The model was statistically significant and explained 16.7% of the variance in disaster nursing competency scores (adjusted R² = 0.167; F[9, 480] = 11.93; p < 0.001). Higher educational level was the strongest predictor (B = 0.396, β = 0.311, p < 0.001), followed by urban residence (B = 0.085, β = 0.195, p < 0.001) and prior formal disaster training (B = 0.267, β = 0.185, p < 0.001). Years of experience showed marginal significance (B = 0.095, β = 0.103, p = 0.040). Exploratory model comparisons (Figs. 3 and 4) revealed modest predictive performance for resilience and are presented as supplementary analyses (Table 4; Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study examined disaster nursing competencies and psychological resilience among registered nurses in four regions of Saudi Arabia during the post-pandemic period. Participants reported a high overall level of disaster nursing competency and a moderate-to-high level of psychological resilience. Disaster nursing competency was positively associated with psychological resilience, and higher educational level, urban residence, and prior formal disaster training were significant predictors of disaster nursing competency. These findings support an interpretation in which disaster readiness in nursing is shaped by both professional preparation and adaptive psychological capacity [2, 4–6, 32].

The relatively high overall competency score is consistent with previous research showing that nurses’ preparedness can be strengthened through exposure to disaster education, emergency experience, and institutional preparedness initiatives [1, 12, 13, 16, 17, 19, 21, 33]. In the Saudi context, this pattern may reflect continued attention to emergency readiness following COVID-19 and the system’s ongoing preparedness needs for infectious disease threats and mass-gathering events [1, 17, 18, 20, 25]. As the competency measure reflects self-reported ability rather than observed performance, the result indicates favourable perceived preparedness rather than confirmed performance under real disaster conditions [6, 16, 33].

Although overall competency was favourable, Disaster Preparedness was the lowest-scoring domain and Disaster Reduction/Prevention the highest, indicating an uneven competency profile. This pattern is consistent with regional and Saudi literature suggesting that preparedness-related activities (planning, coordination, readiness processes) often remain less developed than other phases, particularly when training opportunities, institutional support, and regular drills are inconsistent [10, 13, 16, 20–22, 24, 34, 35]. The pattern also supports the ICN framework’s emphasis on balanced competency development across prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery rather than concentration on a single phase [2, 33, 34].

The mean resilience score indicates that nurses had a moderate-to-high level of psychological resilience, an important finding for a post-pandemic workforce. Previous research has consistently linked resilience with sustained work functioning, psychological adaptation, and the ability to continue providing care under prolonged stress [7, 26–30]. The positive correlation between total disaster nursing competency and total resilience further suggests that nurses who reported greater adaptive capacity also perceived themselves as more competent in disaster-related practice. This finding is consistent with the conceptual basis of the study, which draws on the ICN disaster competency framework and Connor and Davidson’s resilience theory [2, 32]. Because the design was cross-sectional, the relationship should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Among resilience subscales, Tenacity Control showed a notable association with competency, which may reflect the relevance of persistence, self-regulation, and perceived control to disaster-related performance. The resilience literature has similarly emphasized that adaptive coping and sustained functioning under uncertainty are important features of resilience in healthcare professionals [5, 8, 27–29].

The regression findings clarified which factors were most strongly associated with disaster nursing competency. Educational level was the strongest predictor, followed by urban residence and prior formal disaster training. The effect of educational level is consistent with the literature, suggesting that advanced academic preparation may improve nurses’ capacity to integrate clinical reasoning, evidence-based decision-making, and system-level understanding in emergencies [11, 12, 22, 23, 25, 36]. The role of prior formal disaster training is consistent with studies showing that structured education, simulation, and competency-based training strengthen disaster readiness more effectively than general experience alone [4, 10, 33–36]. Because education and formal training are modifiable, both are practical targets for curricular reform and continuing professional development [1, 11, 22, 23].

Urban residence was also significantly associated with higher competency. This finding may reflect greater access in urban settings to tertiary services, simulation resources, structured emergency protocols, and continuing education opportunities. Previous Saudi and international literature have noted that disaster preparedness is influenced not only by individual training but also by organisational capacity, policy implementation, and the availability of structured emergency management support [17, 18, 20, 21, 24, 25]. Accordingly, this finding is best interpreted as an indicator of contextual variation in preparedness opportunities rather than a stable individual attribute.

The unexplained variance in the regression model suggests that additional factors may influence disaster nursing competency. The literature points to organisational culture, leadership, team climate, workload, prior disaster exposure, and institutional preparedness infrastructure as relevant influences [6, 15, 20, 24, 31]. These factors warrant attention in future research to provide a more complete account of disaster preparedness among nurses.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; the observed a24ween disaster nursing competency and psychological resilience should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Second, although the sample included nurses from four geographically diverse regions, recruitment within facilities relied on convenience sampling, which may limit representativeness despite stratified randomisation of facilities. Third, the use of self-reported instruments may have introduced recall and social desirability bias, particularly for competency assessment [6, 16, 33]. Fourth, the findings may not be fully generalisable to all nurses in Saudi Arabia or to other healthcare systems, given differences in institutional preparedness and training infrastructure [18, 20, 21, 24, 25, 37]. Finally, unmeasured organisational and contextual variables may have contributed to the unexplained variance in competency and should be addressed in future research.

Conclusions

Registered nurses in the sampled regions of Saudi Arabia reported generally high disaster nursing competency and moderate-to-high psychological resilience. Disaster Preparedness was the lowest-scoring competency domain, indicating an area for further improvement despite favourable overall scores. Disaster nursing competency was positively associated with psychological resilience, and higher educational level, urban residence, and prior formal disaster training were significant predictors of competency. These findings may inform future nursing education, disaster preparedness training, and workforce development initiatives, with the caution that the cross-sectional design and self-reported measures limit causal interpretation.

Implications for practice, education, and research

The findings suggest that disaster preparedness training should give greater emphasis to planning, coordination, and readiness activities, given that Disaster Preparedness was the lowest-scoring competency domain [2, 19–21, 24, 34]. Because formal disaster training was a significant predictor of competency, healthcare institutions and nursing schools may strengthen structured disaster education, including drills, scenario-based learning, and simulation-based training [4, 10, 11, 22, 34–37]. The positive association between competency and resilience suggests that preparedness programs may also benefit from integrating resilience-supportive elements such as stress management, adaptive coping, and psychologically informed workforce support [5, 7–9, 28, 29].

From a policy and workforce development perspective, the association between urban residence and higher competency points to the need for equitable access to disaster education and preparedness resources across healthcare settings, including non-urban institutions [17, 18, 21, 23–25]. Standardised competency frameworks, regular preparedness exercises, and contextually relevant continuing education may help reduce disparities across regions and facility types [1, 2, 16, 20, 33]. Future research should adopt longitudinal and mixed-methods designs, broader national sampling, objective or performance-based competency measures, and assessment of institutional variables such as leadership, organisational culture, and preparedness infrastructure [6, 15, 20, 24, 29, 31].
