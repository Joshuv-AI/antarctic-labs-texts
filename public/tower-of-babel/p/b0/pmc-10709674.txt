Data considerations in measuring stillbirths in India
Authors: Siddhesh Zadey, Sweta Dubey, Vidhi Wadhwani
Published: 2023

Improving the accuracy of the stillbirth and neonatal mortality measurement is important for pediatric global health epidemiology with direct implications for international agencies and national governments. With about 23% of all stillbirths and 25% of neonatal deaths worldwide, India is the topmost contributor to stillbirths and neonatal mortality.1 However, India also faces data reporting and accuracy challenges. Recently, Ali & colleagues proposed the following indicators for identifying and correcting underreporting of stillbirths and early neonatal mortality in demographic and health surveys (i.e., representative household surveys conducted across 90 countries)—age heaping at day 7 after birth, ratio of stillbirths to deaths on days 0–1 (D0–1), ratio of stillbirths to deaths in the first week (D0–6), and ratio of deaths on days 0–1 to deaths on days 2–6 (D0–1 to D2–6).2

India faces some important challenges for uptake of such correction methods. First, the availability of data quality indicators is critical for the uptake and translation of adjustment metrics to data sources. Currently, the Indian health management information system (HMIS),3 civil registration system (CRS),4 or the sample registration system (SRS)5 does not provide data necessary to calculate the age heaping index at day 7, stillbirths to D0–1 ratio, and D0–1 to D2–6 ratio. Hence, while useful, quality indicators used in the adjustment analysis cannot be estimated for checking and correcting underreporting and misreporting in these data sources—commonly used for stillbirth measurement and guiding policymaking.

Second, variations across data sources have a major influence on uptake on quality indicators. For instance, the only proposed indicator that is calculable using SRS data for India is the ratio of stillbirths to deaths in the first week (days 0–6). Based on SRS data, this ratio for India is 0.19 falling in the ‘improbable’ category—pointing to the presence of under-reporting of stillbirths or misclassification. This value is about three-fold below the ratios mentioned for India across DHSs (0.56–0.69). Variations are also visible in the estimates produced by Ali and colleagues for stillbirth rate (SBR) per 1000 births, and those based on HMIS, CRS, and SRS data. Across four models, SBR estimates for India for 2019/21 range from 11.6 to 25.7 (Appendix 8, Table A7 in Ali and colleagues’ report).2 While SBR values from HMIS, CRS, and SRS for India for 2019 are 12.25, 6.69, and 3, respectively (Fig. 1a). The CRS and SRS values for SBR are several-fold less than the modeled values or HMIS which also questions whether complete vital registration is a feasible solution for low- and lower-middle income countries. Third, for large countries like India, geographic variations also play a role, and assuming an aggregate value for the country holds limited value. For instance, different data sources reveal geographic variations in SBR across Indian states (Fig. 1a). Similarly, the ratio of stillbirths to deaths in the first week varies from 0 for Telangana to 0.75 for Kerala across states (Fig. 1b). Awareness of data availability and variations across sources and geographic regions are crucial for addressing this important measurement problem. Accurate data can aid local policymaking and allocation of infrastructural, human, and financial resources for prioritization of certain states and districts in the India Newborn Action Plan.
