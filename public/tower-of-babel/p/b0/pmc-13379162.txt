USMSIT/NMUSIT Fundamental Guide on Diagnostic and Interventional Hip Ultrasonography
Authors: Ting-Yu Lin, Ke-Vin Chang, Wei-Ting Wu, Yuan-Yuan Tsai, Shao-Yu Chi, Kamal Mezian, Vincenzo Ricci, Mathieu Boudier-Revéret, Levent Özçakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hip pain arises from a spectrum of intra-articular, periarticular, and neurogenic conditions. Ultrasound offers real-time, radiation-free visualization of the hip joint, adjacent soft tissues, and peripheral nerves – making it an essential tool in contemporary musculoskeletal evaluation. Guided by the EURO-MUSCULUS/USPRM framework, this guideline proposes a standardized, anatomy-based protocol for both diagnostic and interventional hip ultrasonography. Developed under the educational mission of the Ultrasound and Musculoskeletal/Neuromuscular Sonography Initiative in Taiwan – through its USMSIT and NMUSIT programs – the protocol combines structured instruction, live demonstrations, and supervised hands-on training to promote consistency and clinical relevance. The article outlines a comprehensive scanning strategy detailing key anatomical landmarks, recommended patient positioning, and optimal transducer orientation. Representative sonographic findings of common hip disorders – including joint effusion, gluteal tendinopathy, hamstring injuries, piriformis-related pathology, and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve entrapment – are provided to support interpretation. Techniques for ultrasound-guided interventions, such as intra-articular, greater trochanteric, and piriformis injections, are also described. Standardizing image acquisition and interpretation may enhance diagnostic accuracy and support safer as well as more effective management of hip pain.

INTRODUCTION

Hip pain is a prevalent clinical complaint, affecting approximately 10% of the general population, with the prevalence of symptomatic hip osteoarthritis increasing to nearly 25% by the age of 85 years.[1] Intra-articular etiologies include labral tears, cartilage defects, and synovitis or joint effusion. Tendinous and muscular injuries – such as adductor strains, hamstring injuries, and gluteus medius/minimus tendinopathy – are especially common among athletes.[234] Furthermore, greater trochanteric pain syndrome accounts for 10%–20% of hip pain cases.[5] Bursal disorders, including iliopsoas and trochanteric bursitis, contribute to anterior or lateral hip pain. Likewise, nerve entrapment syndromes – such as lateral femoral cutaneous nerve entrapment – may present with symptoms that mimic juxta-articular or spinal pathologies.[6]

A structured approach is essential for performing reproducible and clinically meaningful ultrasound examination of the hip. Transducer selection should be individualized according to body habitus. A high-frequency linear probe generally provides optimal resolution, whereas a curvilinear probe may be necessary in larger patients to ensure adequate depth of penetration. Drawing on the European Musculoskeletal Ultrasound Study Group in Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine/Ultrasound Study Group of the International Society of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine (EURO-MUSCULUS/USPRM) framework[7], this guideline introduces a standardized, anatomy-oriented protocol for both diagnostic evaluation and ultrasound-guided interventions of the hip. Developed under the educational mission of the Ultrasound and Musculoskeletal/Neuromuscular Sonography Initiative in Taiwan – through the Ultrasound Musculoskeletal Workshop in Taiwan (USMSIT) and Neuromuscular Ultrasound Workshop in Taiwan (NMUSIT) programs – the protocol integrates structured teaching, live demonstrations, and supervised hands-on practice to enhance consistency and clinical utility.[8] It provides detailed instructions for patient positioning and transducer orientation across all anatomical compartments of the hip, ensuring a systematic and comprehensive assessment. Dynamic maneuvers, including specific hip rotations, are incorporated to improve visualization of muscle and tendon architecture. Each scanning step is supported by matched cadaveric illustrations and corresponding ultrasound images to reinforce anatomical accuracy. Comparative examples obtained from high-end and handheld ultrasound systems are included to reflect practical variability encountered in different clinical settings. In addition, the guideline presents representative sonographic findings of common hip pathologies along with practical recommendations for image interpretation and ultrasound-guided procedures. Thereby, diagnostic uniformity is promoted and seamless clinical applications are facilitated.

SCANNING STEPS

Anterior hip: Joint recess, iliopsoas bursa, rectus femoris, and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve

With the patient lying supine, the transducer is placed in an oblique sagittal orientation along the long axis of the femoral neck. This position allows clear delineation of the anterior synovial recess as well as other key anatomical landmarks [Figure 1]. The anterior joint capsule extends from the acetabulum and labrum to the intertrochanteric line of the femur. Both its anterior and posterior layers are usually 2–4 mm thick.[9] The normal anterior hip recess measures under 7 mm in thickness and is concave or flat, though it may become convex during internal hip rotation.[10] The anterior acetabular labrum of the acetabulum, located cranially to the anterior recess, appears as a uniformly hyperechoic triangular structure. The iliopsoas (iliopectineal) bursa, situated between the iliopsoas tendon and the anterior hip capsule, is normally collapsed and therefore not visible on ultrasound. The femoral neurovascular bundle is located between the iliacus and pectineus muscles, with the femoral nerve, femoral artery, and femoral vein arranged from lateral to medial, respectively [Figure 2].

In the sagittal plane, assessment of the rectus femoris begins at the anterior inferior iliac spine. The fibrillar architecture of the direct head is visualized as it inserts onto the anterior inferior iliac spine. In the same imaging plane – or with a slight lateral adjustment of the transducer – the indirect head can be identified coursing deeply toward its acetabular origin. This component typically appears more hypoechoic and demonstrates a characteristic refractive shadow caused by anisotropy [Figure 3].[11]

To find the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, the linear transducer is positioned medial to the anterior superior iliac spine.[1213] The nerve initially travels along the surface of the iliacus muscle before passing distally beneath the inguinal ligament and entering the fat-filled interval between the sartorius and tensor fasciae latae muscles. Deep to the fat-filled tunnel is the rectus femoris muscle [Figure 4].

Medial hip: Pubis and adductors

For the medial hip, the patient’s thigh is abducted and externally rotated with the knee bent. The linear transducer follows the long-axis course of the adductor muscles originating from the pubic symphysis, permitting visualization of the three layers of adductor longus, brevis, and magnus [Figure 5].[14] On imaging, the proximal attachments of the adductor longus, adductor brevis, or gracilis appear to merge into a conjoint tendon. Nevertheless, the conjoint tendon is composed predominantly of the adductor longus tendon.[15]

Lateral hip: Greater trochanter and abductors

To evaluate the lateral aspect of the hip, the patient is positioned on the contralateral side. With the linear probe placed in the axial plane over the greater trochanter, this bony prominence serves as a key anterior landmark. The anterior facet and lateral facet form an angle of approximately 120°. In this view, the anterior band of the gluteus medius tendon inserts onto the lateral facet, whereas the gluteus minimus tendon attaches to the anterior facet [Figure 6]. Superficial to the gluteus minimus tendon, the gluteus medius muscle is visible, with the iliotibial tract lying more superficially. The gluteus maximus overlies the posterior facet but does not insert onto it. On the contrary, the posterior band of the gluteus medius muscle attaches to the superoposterior facet of the greater trochanter.

By rotating the transducer toward the iliac crest in the coronal plane, the gluteus medius tendon (anterior band) can be depicted in its long axis [Figure 7]. The posterior band of the gluteus medius can be visualized in the long axis by first locating the superoposterior facet in the axial plane and then rotating the transducer 90° to align with the posterior two-thirds of the iliac crest. Similarly, the gluteus minimus is identified in the long axis by first locating the anterior facet in the axial plane and then rotating the transducer 90° to align with the anterior one-third of the iliac crest. Detailed descriptions of ultrasound imaging in multiple planes for visualizing the gluteus minimus and the two bands of the gluteus medius as they attach to the greater trochanter have been presented elsewhere.[16]

Posterior hip: Gluteal muscles, piriformis, and proximal hamstring complex

Posterior hip evaluation is performed with the patient lying prone and the legs extended. With the curvilinear transducer placed horizontally at the level of the posterior superior iliac spine, the posterior ilium appears as a hyperechoic oblique surface. At this level, the gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, and gluteus minimus are arranged superficial to deep.

To scan the piriformis muscle, the transducer is moved distally along the posterior ilium until the greater sciatic notch becomes evident as a prominent bony cleft. This notch marks the exit of the piriformis, which can be aligned with the imaging plane by angling the lateral edge of the transducer toward the greater trochanter. On ultrasound, the piriformis is triangular, with its belly located medially and its tendon extending laterally to the greater trochanter [Figure 8]. Passive hip rotation helps confirm identification by demonstrating synchronous motion of the muscle and tendon. The sciatic nerve can also be observed emerging beneath the piriformis and coursing between the gluteus maximus and the short external rotators of the hip, i.e., the superior gemellus, obturator internus, inferior gemellus, and quadratus femoris.[16]

In the transverse plane, the origins of the hamstring muscles can be traced to the ischial tuberosity. At this location, the conjoint tendon of the semitendinosus and long head of the biceps femoris sits just lateral to the ischial tuberosity, while the semimembranosus tendon lies deeper and more laterally [Figure 9]. Viewing in the sagittal plane, the conjoint and semimembranosus tendons are simultaneously visualized only in a short region just caudal to the ischial tuberosity [Figure 10]. The probe is returned to the axial plane and advanced distally toward the gluteal fold. At this level, the hyperechoic conjoint tendon separates the semitendinosus medially from the long head of the biceps femoris laterally. The sciatic nerve is identified in the fascial plane between the biceps femoris and adductor magnus muscles [Figure 11].

ULTRASOUND IMAGING FOR HIP PATHOLOGIES

Hip joint disorders and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve entrapment

A hip joint effusion is visualized as distension of the anterior hip recess surrounding the femoral neck. Simple effusions are anechoic [Figure 12a], while complex effusions could appear hypoechoic, raising suspicion for synovial hypertrophy [Figure 12b]. In osteoarthritic hips, osteophyte formation and cortical irregularity may also be present [Figure 12b]. Differentiation between synovial hypertrophy and effusion can be aided by Doppler ultrasound whereby reduced compressibility and the presence of internal vascular flow support the diagnosis of synovial hypertrophy. It should be noted that ultrasound cannot reliably exclude infection, and aspiration may be required when clinically indicated. Osteophytes, representing bony outgrowths at joint margins, are characteristic features of osteoarthritis. Other cortical abnormalities may also be detected. For instance, in a case of hip avascular necrosis, loss of smooth cortical contour and fragmentation of the femoral head can be evident [Figure 12c]. When the iliopsoas bursa is inflamed, it may appear distended with fluid, debris, or calcification [Figure 12d], along with hyperemia and thickening of the bursal wall.

In a case of hip avascular necrosis (c), loss of cortical continuity and fragmentation of the femoral head (white arrows) are observed. An amorphous calcific deposit (black arrowheads) within the iliopsoas bursa anterior to the hip joint is visualized (d). A: acetabulum, FH: Femoral head, FN: Femoral neck, ILA: Iliacus

Greater trochanteric pain syndrome

Greater trochanteric pain syndrome is most often due to gluteus minimus or medius tendon pathology, with bursal inflammation being a less common cause. On ultrasound, findings may include gluteal tendinopathy (thickened, heterogeneous tendons with hypoechogenicity), tendon tears (partial- or full-thickness anechoic defects), and calcifications within the tendons [Figure 13c]. The trochanteric (subgluteus maximus) bursa is located between the gluteus maximus muscle and the posterior facet of the greater trochanter, or between the iliotibial tract and the gluteus medius tendon. When inflamed, it appears thickened and/or distended with effusion [Figure 13d]. Smaller adjacent bursae include the subgluteus minimus bursa, located between the gluteus minimus tendon and the anterior facet of the greater trochanter; the subgluteus medius bursa, situated between the gluteus medius tendon and the lateral or superoposterior facets; and the ischial (ischiogluteal) bursa, which lies superficial to the ischial tuberosity.

Piriformis syndrome

Piriformis syndrome results from sciatic nerve entrapment by a swollen piriformis, leading to buttock pain and numbness radiating to the ipsilateral thigh and leg.[17] On ultrasound, the affected piriformis shows increased thickness, larger cross-sectional area, and altered echotexture (affected side, Figure 14a; normal side, Figure 14b). Dynamic evaluation may demonstrate sciatic nerve compression and can reproduce symptoms during hip internal and external rotation.

Hamstring injuries

Acute hamstring muscle strain appears as altered echogenicity or disruption of muscle fibers, typically accompanied by edema or intermuscular hematoma. Hamstring tendon tears are characterized by a focal gap in the fibrillar pattern, hematoma at the musculotendinous junction or within the tendon, and, in cases of complete rupture, tendon retraction. Chronic hamstring tendon injury is characterized by tendinosis, occasional partial-thickness tearing, and intratendinous calcification at the ischial origin (short-axis view, Figure 14c; long-axis view, Figure 14d). The pathology may involve either the semimembranosus tendon origin at the lateral deep portion of the ischial tuberosity or the conjoint biceps femoris–semitendinosus tendon along the superficial surface of the ischium.

COMMON ULTRASOUND-GUIDED PROCEDURES FOR HIP PAIN

Hip joint injection

The patient is positioned supine with the lower limbs extended and slightly externally rotated. The transducer is placed in an oblique sagittal plane to clearly visualize the femoral head and neck. Using an in-plane technique, the needle is advanced from distal to proximal toward the anterior joint recess at the junction of the femoral neck and head [Figure 15]. This trajectory avoids directing the needle straight into the articular gap between the acetabulum and femoral head, thereby minimizing the risk of labral or femoral cartilage injury. Care should be taken to recognize and avoid the ascending branch of the lateral femoral circumflex artery, which may cross the intended needle path. As an alternative, the probe may be positioned in the short-axis view of the femoral neck, with the needle inserted in-plane from lateral to medial.[1819]

Greater trochanteric bursa injection

For this injection, the transducer is placed horizontally over the greater trochanter, allowing recognition of the anterior, lateral, and posterior facets. Using an in-plane, posterior-to-anterior technique, the needle is guided toward the space between the gluteus medius tendon and the iliotibial tract, targeting the greater trochanteric bursa [Figure 16].

Piriformis muscle injection

For piriformis injection, the transducer is aligned with the long axis of the piriformis muscle. Careful evaluation of the sciatic nerve is essential, as it may pass through or above the muscle. Power Doppler should be used to avoid the ascending branches of the inferior gluteal artery, located beneath the gluteus maximus. Using an in-plane, lateral-to-medial approach, the needle is advanced toward the painful point, most often at the musculotendinous junction, where the piriformis is compressed between the gluteus maximus and the ischium [Figure 17].

CONCLUSION

Ultrasound provides a dynamic and accessible method for evaluating the hip and examining pathologies across anterior, medial, lateral, and posterior compartments. By applying a standardized scanning protocol and understanding the key anatomic landmarks, clinicians can identify intra-articular disease, tendon and muscle pathology, bursitis, and nerve entrapment syndromes. Coupled with ultrasound-guided interventions, this systematic approach supports more consistent evaluation and targeted treatment in patients with hip pain.
