A lipid-tuned bile acid exporter: structural insight into intestinal bile acid efflux
Authors: Hak Min Kim, Sang Won Nam, Jung Ki Jo
Published: 2026

In two recent Nature studies, Yang et al. and Wang et al. independently revealed that human organic solute transporter alpha-beta (OSTαβ) is a lipid-tuned bile acid exporter with a previously unrecognized fold and a lateral-access transport pathway. These findings provide a structural framework for understanding basolateral bile acid efflux in the enterohepatic circulation and its relevance to metabolic signaling, cholestatic disease, and therapeutic targeting.1,2

Bile acids support lipid absorption and act as potent signaling molecules that regulate metabolic and immune pathways, and have emerged as key metabolic regulators in multiple organs. Their enterohepatic circulation depends on coordinated transport steps in the intestine and liver, but the basolateral efflux of bile acids from enterocytes into the portal circulation has long remained structurally unresolved. This efflux is mediated by the organic solute transporter alpha-beta, which also transports steroid conjugates and xenobiotics and whose dysfunction causes cholestatic and metabolic disease.3,4

OSTαβ is highly expressed in ileal enterocytes, hepatocytes, brain and adrenal tissue, where it mediates basolateral export of conjugated bile acids and sulfated steroids. Earlier genetic work in mice showed that Ostα–Ostβ is essential for intestinal basolateral bile acid export and for maintaining the bile acid pool size, firmly positioning this heteromeric carrier as the key basolateral component of the enterohepatic circulation. Loss-of-function variants in OSTα or OSTβ have been linked to neonatal cholestasis, chronic diarrhea, and systemic bile acid imbalance,3,4 indicating that this transporter is non-redundant in the enterohepatic circuit. Unlike the sodium-dependent bile acid transporters NTCP and ASBT, which share an NhaA-like fold and use ion-coupled alternating access, OSTαβ functions without sodium coupling and is built from a seven-transmembrane (TM) subunit (OSTα) and a single-pass partner (OSTβ) with no clear homology to known transporter families. Structural and functional studies of NTCP have defined a classical alternating access mechanism based on an NhaA-like fold, providing a useful reference point for understanding how OSTα–OSTβ adopts a mechanistically distinct solution for bile acid transport.1

Single-particle cryo-EM analysis by Yang et al. reveals an apo OSTαβ structure and two substrate-bound complexes with taurolithocholic acid (TLCA) and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), all captured in an inward-facing conformation. Across states, OSTαβ forms a symmetric tetramer organized as a dimer of OSTαβ heterodimers with overall C2 symmetry, each heterodimer comprising a seven TM OSTα core and a single TM helix from OSTβ, topped by an extracellular cap and supported by an intracellular amphipathic helix and a cysteine-rich region. Structural similarity searches indicate that the OSTα core adopts a previously unrecognized fold and support an evolutionary link to TMEM184 proteins, suggesting that this architecture extends to a broader transporter family.

A central theme emerging from these structures is the role of lipids in tuning transporter architecture and activity. On the cytoplasmic side, a loop between TM3 and TM4 forms an intracellular helix followed by a cysteine-rich motif with seven palmitoylated cysteines that wraps around TM5 and TM6. Palmitoylation of this loop, including a specific interaction between palmitoylated Cys161 and Trp234, stabilizes a lateral substrate-binding groove, and disrupting the motif by mutagenesis or palmitoylation inhibition reduces taurocholic acid uptake and causes intracellular retention of OSTαβ.1 Additional phosphatidylethanolamine-like lipids and cholesterol molecules bridge neighboring heterodimers, neutralizing charged residues and forming a hydrophobic core that stabilizes the tetramer; many lipid-coordinating residues are conserved across vertebrate OSTα and TMEM184 proteins, implying a conserved lipid-mediated assembly strategy.

Within the TM domain, Yang et al. identify a lateral, intracellularly accessible binding groove as the main high-affinity substrate site. In TLCA and DHEAS-bound structures, substrate density occupies a pocket formed primarily by TM5 and TM6 behind the palmitoylated loop, near the intracellular membrane interface; negatively charged sulfate or sulfonate groups of TLCA and DHEAS contact Arg241 and Arg244, and TLCA additionally engages Gln260. In the apo state, an endogenous cholesterol occupies the same groove, with its sterol core stabilized by hydrophobic residues from TM5 and TM6. Alanine substitutions at Arg241, Arg244, or Gln260 markedly reduce radiolabeled taurocholic acid uptake, indicating that this chemically specialized pocket favors amphipathic, negatively charged substrates over neutral sterols.

Beyond this lateral binding site, OSTαβ contains a solvent-accessible tunnel on the extracellular side that likely forms part of the translocation pathway. A narrow, positively charged tunnel between a “lateral lobe” (TM4–TM5) and a “main lobe” (TM0–TM3, TM6, and TM7), shaped by ECL1 and ECL2 and gated by Pro209 and Glu116, connects the inner groove to the extracellular space. Additional substrate-like densities in the upper tunnel region, together with field-accelerated molecular dynamics simulations, supported upward DHEAS translocation from the lateral groove toward the tunnel. These analyses also suggested transient widening of the Pro209–Glu116 constriction and a long-lived electrostatic interaction between the DHEAS sulfate group and Lys191, supporting a lateral-access model in which substrates enter from the inner leaflet, bind in the intracellular groove and then move through a hydrophilic tunnel toward the extracellular space. This lateral access pathway, in which amphipathic substrates enter a positively charged inner leaflet groove and exit through a hydrophilic tunnel, contrasts sharply with the deeply buried binding pockets of NTCP and ASBT and suggests that OSTα–OSTβ is tuned for efficient basolateral efflux rather than sodium-coupled uptake.1

Electrophysiological experiments further support a gradient-driven, bidirectional transport mode. Whole-cell patch clamp recordings in OSTαβ expressing cells show that extracellular DHEAS induces voltage-dependent currents whose polarity reverses with membrane potential and whose amplitude scales with DHEAS concentration, consistent with facilitated diffusion in which membrane potential and substrate gradients together determine transport direction.1

From a signal transduction and targeted therapy perspective, the structural blueprint of OSTαβ has several implications.1 Mapping residues around the lateral groove, cysteine-rich motif, and oligomerization interfaces provides a framework to interpret pathogenic OSTα/OSTβ variants observed in cholestatic liver disease and congenital diarrhea,3,4 complementing earlier knockout studies in mice. The two-site lateral access pathway and its electrostatic gates offer defined pockets for pharmacological modulation of bile acid and steroid efflux, which could be exploited to tune bile acid-responsive signaling pathways in metabolic and inflammatory disease. Because basolateral efflux from enterocytes returns bile acids to the liver, OSTα–OSTβ is positioned to influence enterohepatic bile acid signaling and bile acid-driven control of glucose and lipid metabolism. This places the transporter within the core circuitry of metabolic and endocrine homeostasis.
