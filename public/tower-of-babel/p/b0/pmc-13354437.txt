The effect of Indian costus (Saussurea lappa) extract on the healing of chemically induced oral ulcer in rats: in vitro and in vivo study with histological evaluation
Authors: Hoda A. Fansa, Abd El Rahman M. Sharfeldeen, Razan Abed A. Baloush, Mohammed Ali A. Majrashi, Kamal Rady M. Elbaz, Mohamed Arafat M. Mansour, Mohamed K Kandeel, Ashraf Elsayed Ebaid, Mohammed E.A. Elhussiny, Hassan Ali Mohamed, Zaid Alezzi, Zeinab Abulwafa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Saussurea lappa (S. lappa) is a plant known for anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-ulcer properties, yet its potential in oral-ulcer healing remains underexplored. This study evaluates the effects of S. lappa extract on oral ulcers using in vitro and in vivo models.Methods:Rat tongue epithelial cells received hydroethanolic (70% v/v) S. lappa extract (0–100 µg/mL) for 24, 48, or 72 h; viability (MTT) yielded EC50 at each timepoint. The extract was high-performance liquid chromatography-fingerprinted (costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone). A lipopolysaccharide (LPS) model (1 µg/mL, 48 h) was then treated with EC50 doses; dexamethasone 2.5 µM served as drug-positive control (PC). Interleukin-6 (IL-6; pg/mL) was measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. A scratch assay assessed 72-h closure. In vivo, 27 male rats were allocated to naïve (n = 3), PC (n = 12), and S. lappa (n = 12) groups, with Days 7 and 14 endpoints (n = 6 each). Histology evaluated re-epithelialization and collagen area.Results:S. lappa showed no cytotoxicity across the tested range. It significantly reduced IL-6 versus LPS-only at all timepoints (analysis of variance/Tukey, P < 0.01); at EC50 (72 h), IL-6 did not differ from dexamethasone 2.5 µM (P > 0.05). The extract enhanced in vitro wound closure (higher relative wound density; lower gap width/area). In vivo, S. lappa-treated ulcers showed greater collagen deposition, improved re-epithelialization, and lower ulcer scores than controls at Days 7 and 14.Conclusion:S. lappa shows wound-healing and anti-inflammatory activity; future studies incorporating functional and molecular endpoints are warranted to establish effect magnitude and mechanisms.

Introduction

Oral ulcers are common lesions of the oral mucosa with multifactorial etiologies, characterized by epithelial defects with variable involvement of underlying connective tissue[1]. Management is mainly symptomatic and centers on topical corticosteroids, with adjunct rinses such as chlorhexidine and benzydamine; short courses of systemic corticosteroids are reserved for severe flares. Prolonged topical-steroid use can predispose to opportunistic oral candidiasis, underscoring the need for steroid-sparing approaches when feasible[2]. However, topical steroid therapy was not associated with increased oral candidiasis, according to multivariable and propensity score–matched analyses[3].

The human body has a natural healing response to skin and mucosal wounds, which is a complex and tightly regulated process[4]. Wound healing is mediated by various cellular factors and extracellular matrix components[4,5].

Acute wound repair proceeds through overlapping phases of hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation (granulation, re-epithelialization, and angiogenesis), and remodeling (matrix maturation and contraction). During inflammation, cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) coordinate leukocyte recruitment and later influence angiogenesis and matrix synthesis; dysregulation can delay closure. In the proliferative phase, keratinocyte migration drives re-epithelialization, while fibroblasts deposit provisional matrix. Remodeling is marked by quantitative and qualitative changes in collagen, including the transition from early type III–rich matrix toward type I–dominant scar, which impacts tissue strength[6]. These processes motivate our readouts, IL-6, epithelial coverage, and collagen area/composition, as indicators of treatment effect.

Medicinal plants have long played a crucial role in healthcare, with growing interest in their mechanisms of action in wound healing. These natural remedies are often considered safer due to their bioactive compounds and lower incidence of adverse effects[7]. Saussurea lappa (Indian costus), an aromatic plant belonging to the Asteraceae family, grows on the exposed slopes of India and Kashmir. Its root, known as costus, has been widely used in traditional medicine[8]. Phytochemical analysis has identified key bioactive constituents, including alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, terpenoids, and sesquiterpene lactones (STLs) such as costunolide (COS) and dehydrocostus lactone (DEH)[9].

Several studies have validated the pharmacological properties of S. lappa, demonstrating its anti-inflammatory[10], antimicrobial[11], antioxidant[12], and anti-ulcer effects[13].

While the healing processes of oral mucosal and cutaneous wounds share similarities, the oral cavity presents unique challenges, including bacterial exposure and mechanical stress from chewing. Effective wound management is essential for timely healing without complications. Although several medicinal plants have been explored for oral ulcer treatment[12,13], studies on the effects of S. lappa on oral tissues remain limited. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the therapeutic potential of S. lappa extract in a chemically induced oral ulcer model in rats.

Material and methods

In vitro study

Primary culture of tongue epithelial cells

Primary cells were isolated from healthy male Wistar rats (8–10 weeks; 200–250 g), obtained from the institutional animal facility. A tissue sample from a rat tongue was rinsed three times with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then sterilized with 70% ethanol. The tongue was transported to the laboratory in ice-cold PBS with added antibiotics. After removing the outer layer, the top layer of tissue was carefully cut into small pieces. These pieces were treated with an enzyme solution containing collagenase I and dispersed for 45 min at 37°C. After two rounds of centrifugation, the resulting pellet was collected and combined with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics. The cells were incubated inside culture flasks at 37°C with 5% CO2. The trypan blue dye exclusion method was used to determine cell density, and cells were transferred to T75 flasks at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Cell growth was monitored every 48 h using an inverted phase-contrast microscope, and the medium was changed regularly. The proliferation of the cells was checked on the first, third, and fifth days using a microscope and recorded with micrographs.

Purifying epithelial cells in primary culture

The primary culture often contained a mix of fibroblasts and epithelial cells, with fibroblasts being the dominant group. To obtain pure populations of each cell type, a protocol was developed by incorporating techniques from various studies. Once the cell layer reached 70% confluence, the cells were rinsed with Ca2⁺–Mg2⁺-free PBS and detached using the standard trypsin technique (0.25%). Following the addition of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (0.02%), the cells were incubated at 37°C for 3 to 5 min. Calculation of EC50 of S. lappa on tongue epithelial cells

Rat tongue epithelial cells were cultured in 96 well culture plates using DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 per well in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 10 mg streptomycin, 1% penicillin G sodium (10 000 U/mL), and 25 μg amphotericin B (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h to allow for attachment.

Following media replacement, adherent cells were treated with sequential concentrations of S. lappa root extract: 0, 1.56, 3.12, 6.25, 12.5, 25.0, 50.0, and 100 µg/mL. Dose–response curves (normalized to vehicle control) were fitted with a four-parameter logistic model in GraphPad Prism (v9), and EC50 values were derived for 24-, 48-, and 72-h exposure.

Assessment of cell viability by cell proliferation assay (MTT) at three time intervals

The Vybrant® MTT Cell Proliferation Assay Kit, cat no: M6494 (Thermo Fisher, Germany) was used to conduct the cell proliferation assay. After completion of the incubation period, 100 µL of media were withdrawn and replaced with fresh media. Add each well with 20 µL of 4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution (1 mg/mL) (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The plates were incubated for 4 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. Then finally MTT solution was eliminated and 100 μL of sodium dodecyl sulfate with hydrochloric acid (SDS-HCL) was introduced into each well. The optical density at 570 nm on a spectrophotometer was used to measure the viability of the cells (ELx 800; Bio-Tek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA).

In vitro assessment of the anti-inflammatory effect of S. lappa in tongue epithelial cells

The in vitro inflammatory model was induced in tongue epithelial cells by treating the cells with 1 µg/mL of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany) for 48 h. The LPS stock of 1 mg/mL was diluted in RPMI-1640 medium. The cells were planted in a six-well culture plate 1 day before the experiment. Approximately 1 × 105 tongue epithelial cells were planted in 200 µL of mammalian cell culture medium (RPMI-1640) (Gibco, Thermo Scientific, Germany) supplemented with 10% of FBS (Gibco, Thermoscientific, Germany) and 1% of penicillin G sodium (10 000 U/ml), streptomycin (10 mg), and amphotericin B (25 μg) (PSA) (Gibco, Thermoscientific, Germany). To achieve 70% confluence, culture plates were incubated for 24 h at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. On the following day, instead of EC50-derived doses, the cells were treated with three non-cytotoxic concentrations selected from the MTT assay (viability ≥85–90%) at the corresponding exposure times (24, 48, and 72 h), with the highest dose capped at ≤1.04 µg/mL. Furthermore, control cells were subjected to the carrier solvent (0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide [DMSO]), the potent anti-inflammatory dexamethasone was used as drug-positive control (PC), and the dexamethasone dose was adjusted to 2.5 µM. Treated tongue epithelial cells were incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2, after which IL-6 levels were measured to evaluate the anti-inflammatory effect. After every incubation, the conditioned media was gathered and used for testing inflammatory mediator release including IL-6 using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Using the rat interleukin 6 (IL-6) ELISA Kit Cat No. MBS261259 (My BioSource, San Diego, USA), the amount of IL-6 was determined.

In vitro assessment of the proliferative effect of S. lappa in tongue epithelial cells (in vitro wound healing “scratch assay”)

To evaluate the proliferative impact of S. lappa on the inflamed model of tongue epithelial cells, an in vitro wound healing “scratch assay” was conducted. As a primary step, a confluent cell monolayer was created with cultured cells. The monolayer at this stage represents the tissue’s “intact epithelial cells” before the injury. Mechanical scratching (scratch wound) was used to create a cell-free zone in the monolayer once the cells have confluence. The cells were treated with the calculated EC50 at 72 h “1.04 µg/mL.” For the control cells, the carrier solvent (0.1% DMSO) was utilized, and the dexamethasone “potent anti-inflammatory” was used as a drug-PC, the dexamethasone dose was determined to be 2.5 µM. Treated tongue epithelial cells were incubated for 72 h at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. Then the healing effect was evaluated by an optical microscope. At the end of incubation time, the relative wound density (RWD) “ratio of the occupied area in the gap to the total area in the initial gap”, wound area (µm2) “cell-free area,” and the gap width (µm) “average distance between the edges of the gap” were measured over time and data were expressed as a percentage. The proportion of the filled area in the gap to the overall area in the tested group was normalized to the initial gap. The analysis was performed using the live-cell imaging software (Labomed, USA).

In vivo experimental study

The experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Assiut University (Ref. 04-2023-300088). Twenty-seven male albino rats (150–250 g) were used and allocated to three groups: naïve (n = 3; no acetic acid), PC (n = 12; acetic acid, no treatment), and S. lappa (n = 12; acetic acid + extract). The PC and S. lappa groups were each subdivided to Day 7 (n = 6) and Day 14 (n = 6) endpoints. Rats in the S. lappa group received 100 mg/kg/day hydroethanolic root extract by oral gavage in 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC; 10 mL/kg) once daily for 7 or 14 days.

Rats were kept under controlled environmental conditions at the Animal Center of Ain Shams Medical Research Center, with food and water ad libitum. To induce oral ulcers, rats were anesthetized with a ketamine–xylazine mixture, the oral cavity was cleaned with chlorhexidine, and 50% acetic acid was applied to the dorsal tongue using a 3-mm diameter applicator for a standardized contact time to produce ~3-mm ulcer of ~1.5-mm depth; the overlying epithelium was gently removed with a scalpel. Post-procedure care included buprenorphine 0.05 mg/kg s.c. every 12 for 48 h, provision of softened/moistened chow for 48–72 h, and daily monitoring of body weight, intake, hydration, and grooming to minimize activity-related mechanical irritation. Animals were euthanized under deep isoflurane anesthesia (5% in oxygen) followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary method at Days 7 and 14 for the respective subgroups; naïve animals were euthanized at the end of the study. Days 7 and 14 were chosen to capture the late inflammatory/early proliferative phase and the late proliferative/early remodeling phase of mucosal healing, respectively. Topical acetic acid was selected because it produces a standardized mucosal ulcer of consistent diameter and depth at a fixed concentration, volume, and contact time, yielding low inter-animal variability and a pronounced inflammatory phase with delayed re-epithelialization, which enables sensitive detection of treatment effects on epithelial closure and collagen remodeling across prespecified timepoints (Days 7 and 14).

Plant material and extract preparation

Dried roots of S. lappa (syn. S. costus) were procured as whole roots from Harraz herbal market (Cairo, Egypt); identity was independently authenticated (macro-/micromorphology and comparative thin-layer chromatography), and a voucher specimen was deposited at the Faculty of Pharmacy.Extraction. Milled roots were extracted with 70% ethanol (v/v) at a 1:10 w/v solvent-to-plant ratio for 24 h at 25°C with agitation, then filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure <40°C. The dried extract was stored at −20°C in amber vials; all experiments used material from the same lot.

Standardization [high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) fingerprinting]: The extract was HPLC-fingerprinted against authenticated standards of the STLs such as COS and DEH (coincident retention times and characteristic UV spectra).

Histological examination

The area of the ulcer on the tongue was examined visually and then processed for analysis. The tissue was dehydrated using a series of alcohols, made transparent using xylene, and then embedded in paraffin at 65°C. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on 4–μm-thick slides produced from a semi-automatic microtome. The obtained histology slides underwent a scoring system in addition to qualitative analysis. The scoring system ranked the tissue on a scale of 0–4, with 0 indicating no ulcer and complete healing of the connective tissue, 1 indicating no ulcer and mild fibrosis in the connective tissue with persistent inflammation, 2 indicating the presence of an ulcer along with moderate fibrosis in the connective tissue accompanied by chronic inflammation, 3 indicating the presence of both an ulcer and granulation tissue that resulted from the chronic inflamed connective tissue, and 4 indicating the presence of an ulcer associated with acute inflammatory process manifested by the presence of an inflammatory infiltrate mixed with neutrophils and dilated vessels.

Masson’s trichrome staining

In addition to H&E, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed on selected sections to evaluate collagen fiber deposition and organization, which reflects the extent of tissue remodeling and fibrosis during the healing process. This stain differentiates collagen fibers (blue or green) from muscle and epithelial tissues (red), allowing better visualization of fibrotic changes in the connective tissue.

Statistical analysis

Data were revised, coded, tabulated, and analyzed using GraphPad Prism Software 8.4.2 (San Diego, USA). For parametric numerical data, statistical measures included the mean, range, and standard deviation (±SD), while non-numerical data were analyzed using frequency and percentage through cross-tabulation. The analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used to assess statistical significance among more than two research groups. Since ANOVA identifies differences between group means but does not specify which pairs are significantly different, post hoc tests were performed to determine specific group differences while controlling for experiment-wise error. Statistical significance was defined by the P-value, where P ≤ 0.05 indicated significance, P > 0.05 was non-significant, and P < 0.01 denoted a highly significant difference. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE criteria and TITAN criteria[14,15].

Histopathological changes in the PC group (n = 12)

Seven days after ulcer induction, the H&E-stained sections of the inferior surface of the tongue showed loss of the covering epithelium. The ulcer area was filled with granulation tissue showing dense inflammatory cell infiltration and many dilated blood vessels. The thin epithelial edge showed mitotic and apoptotic activity, and dilated blood vessels in the lamina propria (Fig. 1b). Fourteen days after ulceration, the ulcer area was covered by apparently moderate thickness of keratinized epithelium compared to the unwounded epithelium and showed shallow rete pegs. The epithelium showed mitotic and apoptotic cells. The lamina propria showed some inflammatory cells, collagen fibers, fibroblasts, and numerous blood vessels engorged with red blood cells (RBCs) (Fig. 1e).

Histopathological changes in the S. lappa-treated group (n = 12)

By Day 7 after ulcer induction, the surface epithelium was largely absent, and the defect was occupied by granulation tissue with a dense inflammatory infiltrate. The marginal epithelium appeared attenuated and contained both apoptotic bodies and mitotic figures. In the lamina propria, granulation tissue with inflammatory cells, newly formed vessels, and compact collagen bundles with fibroblasts were evident (Fig. 1c). By Day 14, healing had advanced, with the ulcer nearly re-covered by keratinized epithelium. The connective tissue exhibited dilated vessels, some congested with red blood cells (RBCs) and increased collagen. The regenerated keratinized stratified epithelium was of moderate thickness with scattered apoptosis, and the stroma contained fibroblasts and collagen fibers with only minimal residual inflammation (Fig. 1f).

Comparative analysis of IL-6 after S. lappa treatment at three time intervals

We assessed the anti-inflammatory effect of S. lappa in LPS-stimulated tongue epithelial cells by measuring IL-6 (pg/mL) after treatment with EC50 doses determined at 24, 48, and 72 h. One-way ANOVA showed a significant reduction in IL-6 with S. lappa treatment compared with the LPS-only PC (P < 0.01). Tukey’s post hoc tests showed that IL-6 (pg/mL) at all non-cytotoxic doses and exposure times was significantly lower than LPS-only (P < 0.01), and that the highest non-cytotoxic dose at 72 h (≤1.04 µg/mL) did not differ from dexamethasone 2.5 µM (P > 0.05). Discussion

Interest in identifying effective and non-toxic natural extracts for oral ulcer management has increased. Natural products have gained significant attention, constituting 16% of the drugs approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2018[16]. Several studies have validated the pharmacological properties of S. lappa root[8–12]. However, to our knowledge, no prior study has evaluated the healing potential of S. lappa extract in an oral ulcer model.

In our region, S. lappa roots are readily obtainable through Cairo and Assiut herbal markets; however, market surveys show variable quality and frequent adulteration of powdered preparations. For research and translational use, we recommend sourcing whole roots, expert authentication, voucher deposition, and chemical standardization of marker lactones to ensure reproducibility.

The pathophysiology of oral ulcers involves multiple processes, including vasodilation, cytokine production, cell death, tissue remodeling, and repair[17]. At the molecular level, the overexpression of BAX and VEGF, along with significant collagen fiber deposition, plays a crucial role in the repair process[18,19]. Additionally, both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines contribute to the healing response[18–21]. Numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated the potent antioxidant properties of various herbal extracts, which promote wound healing and protect tissues from oxidative damage. Herbal extracts also enhance fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix synthesis. Their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties further accelerate the wound-healing process[22].

In the present study, all tested concentrations of S. lappa showed no cytotoxicity in rat tongue epithelial cells at the three assessed intervals (Fig. 1). Epithelial cell proliferation was notably elevated at higher concentrations, a favorable aspect of wound healing. Moreover, S. lappa-treated tongue epithelial cells in the inflammatory model exhibited a significant reduction in IL-6 levels (pg/mL) across all intervals compared to untreated cells (P < 0.01). This anti-inflammatory effect is plausibly mediated by pathway-level modulation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling by S. lappa STLs (e.g., COS and DEH), leading to reduced transcription of IL-6 and other pro-inflammatory cytokines, with potential downstream effects on STAT3 activity. This mechanism directly aligns with our observed decrease in IL-6 (pg/mL) and provides a testable framework for future work (e.g., NF-κB reporter assays, p65 nuclear translocation, phospho-ERK/p38 profiling, and rescue experiments with pathway agonists[23]. Our findings align with those of Cho et al[24], who suggested that cynaropicrin, an STL from S. lappa, may modulate inflammation by inhibiting lymphocyte proliferation and the production of inflammatory mediators through conjugation with target protein sulfhydryl groups. The observed anti-inflammatory effects in this study further support the potential immunomodulatory properties of S. lappa. Indeed, COS, another major component of costus root oil, has been shown to significantly downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β[25].

The present study also evaluated the wound-healing capacity of S. lappa in an LPS-induced inflammatory model of tongue epithelial cells. The results revealed a significant increase in RWD in cells treated with 1.04 µg/mL of S. lappa for 72 h (P < 0.01) compared to untreated cells. Additionally, a significant reduction in gap width (µm) and wound area (µm2) was observed in S. lappa-treated cells (P < 0.01). A previous study reported that oral administration of S. lappa root extract at 500 mg/kg significantly enhanced wound healing in excision, incision, and dead space wound models in rats[26]. The anti-ulcer properties of S. lappa root may be attributed to its cytoprotective effects[27].

Time-dependent potency shift: The progressive decline in EC50 (µg/mL) between 24, 48, and 72 h indicates greater apparent potency with longer exposure – an established phenomenon in cell-based assays where IC50/EC50 values decrease as incubation time increases, reflecting kinetics of target engagement and cellular adaptation. Examples across drug classes show lower IC50s at longer exposure due to cumulative effects and delayed mechanisms rather than changes in nominal dose[28]. In our context, STLs in S. lappa (e.g., COS and DEH) modulate NF-κB/MAPK signaling and related cytokine programs, which often require hours to manifest transcriptionally, providing a mechanistic basis for time-dependent shifts[29]. Future work will distinguish exposure time from concentration by (1) matching area under the curve in wash-in/wash-out designs, (2) measuring cellular uptake/stability (LC–MS/MS), and (3) performing time-resolved dose–response for IL-6 and viability.

Collagen, a key extracellular matrix protein synthesized by fibroblasts, plays an essential role in connective tissue repair[30]. During the final phases of wound healing, remodeling occurs, leading to wound or ulcer closure. The histological findings of this study revealed a significantly higher collagen (blue–green) area by Masson’s trichrome in the S. lappa-treated group (P < 0.001). Additionally, collagen area increased between Days 7 and 14 in all groups (P < 0.001) (Table 2, Fig. 6). Microscopic examinations further demonstrated a nearly intact epithelium with enhanced collagen deposition and reduced inflammation, consistent with previous studies on the wound-healing potential of S. lappa in rat models[26,27].

Strengths and limitations

This study integrates complementary models – primary rat tongue epithelial cells and an acetic-acid–induced oral-ulcer rat model – allowing evaluation of anti-inflammatory effects (IL-6) and tissue repair (ulcer score, re-epithelialization, and collagen area) at predefined endpoints with blinded histology. Several limitations warrant caution. First, the extract was HPLC-fingerprinted (COS and DEH) but not quantified to marker content; future work should include chemical profiling with batch standardization. Second, all extract concentrations are now reported as mass/volume (µg/mL or mg/mL) rather than molar units; cross-assay comparability would benefit from dose–response curves tailored to each endpoint. Third, only a single in vivo oral dose (100 mg/kg/day, 0.5% CMC) was used, selected a priori as a mid-range anti-inflammatory dose; a formal dose–response with pharmacokinetic support is needed. Fourth, histological outcomes can be observer-dependent; slides were blinded with predefined criteria, and Masson’s trichrome was interpreted as collagen blue–green (no maturity inference by color). Fifth, cytokine measurements require species-appropriate reagents; a rat IL-6 ELISA was specified, and multiplex validation is recommended. Finally, sample sizes were modest; we now clarify n per subgroup and recommend prospective power calculations and balanced allocation. These considerations frame the findings as supportive rather than definitive and outline clear directions for replication and extension.

Conclusion

S. lappa demonstrated wound-healing and anti-inflammatory activity in oral-ulcer models, evidenced by reduced IL-6 in vitro and improved re-epithelialization and collagen deposition in acetic-acid tongue ulcers in vivo. These findings support S. lappa as a promising candidate for oral-ulcer management; however, the current evidence is primarily histological and based on a single in vivo dose. Future work should include dose–response studies, standardized extract quantification, and functional/mechanistic endpoints, e.g., VEGF, TGF-β1, collagen I/III ratio or hydroxyproline, tensile strength, and epithelialization rate – to define effect size and confirm translational relevance, alongside well-designed clinical trials.
