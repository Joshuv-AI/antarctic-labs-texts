Bleeding Downhill Esophageal Varices Secondary to Superior Vena Cava Stenosis From a Long-Term Indwelling Chemotherapy Port: A Case Report
Authors: Nivedita Singh, Prateek Jain, Divya Makkar, Gaurav Bector
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Downhill esophageal varices are a rare cause of upper gastrointestinal bleeding and differ from distal esophageal varices associated with portal hypertension. They usually occur when obstruction of the superior vena cava (SVC) redirects venous flow through collateral pathways involving the esophageal venous plexus. We report the case of a 71-year-old man with a history of colon cancer in remission, status post partial colectomy with ostomy, who presented with recurrent hematemesis and dark stools. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showed large proximal esophageal varices consistent with downhill varices, and endoscopic band ligation was performed for bleeding control. Further evaluation with contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest demonstrated severe focal SVC stenosis in the setting of a long-standing indwelling chemotherapy port catheter, with catheter-associated thrombus and prominent collateral vessels. Interventional radiology removed the chemotherapy port catheter and performed balloon angioplasty/serial balloon dilation of the SVC stenosis up to 12 mm without stent placement during the index intervention, with improved venographic flow. This case highlights the importance of considering SVC obstruction when proximal esophageal varices are identified, especially in patients with a history of long-term central venous catheters or chemotherapy ports. Although endoscopic therapy can control acute bleeding, definitive management should address the underlying venous obstruction to reduce the risk of recurrence.

Introduction

Esophageal varices are usually discussed in the context of portal hypertension, where collateral venous flow develops through the distal esophagus. Proximal esophageal varices, also known as downhill esophageal varices, are rare and have a different mechanism from the more common distal varices [1,2]. Downhill varices most often develop when venous return through the superior vena cava (SVC) is obstructed, redirecting blood through collateral pathways involving the azygos system and the esophageal venous plexus [3-5]. Malignancy remains an important cause of SVC syndrome; however, benign etiologies have become increasingly relevant with the growing use of intravascular devices [6,7]. Bleeding from downhill varices is uncommon, but reported cases show that benign SVC obstruction can present with clinically significant upper gastrointestinal bleeding [8]. Bleeding from downhill varices is thought to be less frequent than bleeding from distal portal-hypertensive varices because proximal varices are often deeper within the submucosa, have less exposure to gastric acid, and are usually not associated with cirrhosis-related coagulopathy [4,8]. We report a case of bleeding downhill esophageal varices caused by SVC obstruction related to a prolonged indwelling chemotherapy port catheter that was no longer in use.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old man presented to the emergency department with recurrent hematemesis for two days. He reported three episodes of nausea and vomiting mixed with red and maroon blood clots, along with recent-onset dark stools. He denied dizziness, chest pain, shortness of breath, or abdominal pain. His medical history was significant for colon cancer in remission, status post partial colectomy with ostomy, tobacco use disorder, peripheral vascular disease, depression, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and hypertension. He reported drinking approximately six cans of beer daily and had not followed up regularly with a physician.

On examination, the abdomen was non-distended, and several superficial venous collaterals were noted. The colostomy appeared pink and well perfused, with dark maroon stool present in the ostomy bag. His vital signs were within normal limits. Relevant laboratory results are summarized in Table 1. The patient was anemic, with hemoglobin of 9.0 g/dL on presentation and a nadir hemoglobin of 7.7 g/dL before transfusion. Blood urea nitrogen was elevated at 28 mg/dL. Liver-associated laboratory values were within reference ranges, and hepatitis testing was negative.

Given the patient’s significant alcohol use, portal hypertension was considered in the differential diagnosis. However, the proximal location of the varices, available laboratory findings, negative hepatitis testing, and CT/venographic evidence of severe focal SVC stenosis favored downhill esophageal varices. Dedicated abdominal or portal venous imaging was not available at the time of manuscript preparation, which is a limitation.

The patient was transfused with one unit of packed red blood cells after his hemoglobin decreased from 9 g/dL to 7.7 g/dL. He was started on an octreotide infusion and was given pantoprazole, ondansetron, and intravenous fluids during evaluation and management of upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy demonstrated large (>5 mm) esophageal varices in the proximal esophagus, consistent with downhill esophageal varices. The available endoscopic documentation did not describe distal esophageal or gastric varices. Red wale signs were present. A total of seven bands were placed to control the bleeding. Given the proximal location of the varices, computed tomography (CT) venography was recommended to evaluate for SVC obstruction. A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest revealed severe focal stenosis of the SVC in the presence of a central venous catheter. Evidence of catheter-associated thrombus along the course of the left subclavian vein was also noted, along with multiple prominent collateral vessels in the upper chest (Figure 1A).

The patient had a chemotherapy port catheter in place for more than 15 years, and the device was no longer in use. The prolonged indwelling catheter was considered the likely cause of SVC obstruction, leading to the development of downhill esophageal varices and upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Interventional radiology removed the catheter and performed serial balloon dilation/angioplasty of the SVC stenosis up to 12 mm. Venography confirmed severe SVC stenosis before intervention and demonstrated improved SVC luminal caliber and flow after angioplasty (Figure 1B, 1C). No SVC stent was placed during the index intervention because post-angioplasty venography demonstrated improved SVC luminal caliber and flow. Additional CT and fluoroscopic images further demonstrate focal SVC stenosis, pre-intervention SVC narrowing, and balloon dilation/venoplasty of the stenotic segment (Figure 2). At discharge, the patient was advised to follow up with gastroenterology for monitoring of the esophageal varices and with interventional radiology to evaluate for recurrence of SVC stenosis and the need for additional intervention.

Discussion

This case illustrates the importance of distinguishing downhill esophageal varices from the more common distal esophageal varices associated with portal hypertension. In downhill varices, obstruction of venous return through the superior systemic circulation redirects blood through collateral pathways, including the azygos and hemiazygos systems and the esophageal venous plexus. The resulting venous hypertension can lead to dilation of proximal esophageal veins and, rarely, variceal bleeding [1-5].

SVC obstruction can result from either malignant or benign causes. Malignancies such as lung cancer and lymphoma remain common etiologies, but benign causes related to intravascular devices are increasingly recognized [6,7]. A review of bleeding downhill varices associated with benign SVC obstruction emphasized that management should address both the bleeding varices and the underlying obstruction [8].

Catheter-associated central venous obstruction has been described in patients receiving hemodialysis, including cases of downhill varices caused by dialysis catheter-induced SVC occlusion [9,10]. Although this patient did not have a dialysis catheter, the same mechanism applies to long-term indwelling venous devices. Chemotherapy ports can be complicated by thrombosis, stenosis, and SVC syndrome, and imaging reviews of port systems describe both early and delayed complications of central venous access devices [11,12]. Catheter-associated thrombosis is a recognized cause of SVC syndrome and can be evaluated with Doppler ultrasonography or contrast-enhanced CT, depending on the clinical scenario [13]. In device-related benign SVC obstruction, chronic endothelial injury from indwelling catheters may promote catheter-associated thrombosis, fibrin sheath formation, intimal hyperplasia, and fibrosis, leading to progressive central venous stenosis [12,13].

Management of bleeding downhill varices requires control of acute hemorrhage and treatment of the underlying venous obstruction. Endoscopic band ligation may be used for hemostasis, as in this case, but definitive management should focus on restoring venous drainage when feasible. Endovascular approaches, including balloon angioplasty with or without stenting, are established options for benign SVC syndrome and SVC obstruction, with prior clinical studies and a systematic review supporting the role of endovascular therapy in selected patients [14,15]. In this patient, endoscopic band ligation controlled the acute bleeding, while removal of the inactive chemotherapy port catheter and balloon angioplasty/serial dilation of the SVC stenosis up to 12 mm addressed the underlying venous obstruction. No stent was placed during the index procedure; post-angioplasty venography showed improved SVC luminal caliber and flow, and interventional radiology follow-up was recommended to monitor for recurrent stenosis and determine whether further intervention, including stenting, would be needed. Follow-up imaging or repeat endoscopy was not available at the time of manuscript submission.

Conclusions

Downhill esophageal varices are a rare but clinically important cause of upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Unlike distal esophageal varices, which are commonly associated with portal hypertension, downhill varices usually result from obstruction of venous return through the SVC. This case highlights a rare benign cause of SVC obstruction due to a prolonged indwelling chemotherapy port catheter that had remained in place for more than 15 years despite no longer being used.

Recognition of proximal esophageal varices on endoscopy should prompt evaluation for SVC obstruction, particularly in patients with a history of central venous catheters, chemotherapy ports, pacemakers, defibrillators, or hemodialysis access. Although endoscopic band ligation can control acute bleeding, definitive management requires treatment of the underlying obstruction to reduce recurrence risk. Based on this case and prior literature on device-related SVC obstruction, timely reassessment and removal of unnecessary long-term indwelling venous devices may help reduce the risk of complications such as SVC stenosis, thrombosis, and bleeding downhill esophageal varices.
