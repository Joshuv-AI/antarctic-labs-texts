Association between physical activity of different intensities and balance ability in preschool children: gender and age differences
Authors: Kang Zhu, Ruqiang Liu, Geng Li, Yangyang Shen, Zhenwen Xie, Zhenghan Wang, Ying Long, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBalance is crucial for motor skills and physical development in preschool children. Regular physical activity (PA) improves balance, but the relationship between PA of different intensities and balance in preschool children remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between PA of different intensities and balance in preschool children, further analyzing whether this association differs by gender and age.MethodsA total of 409 preschool children were included in this study, comprising 215 boys and 194 girls. Static balance was measured using the one-leg stance test, dynamic balance was measured using the balance beam test, and PA was measured with an accelerometer. Multiple linear regression was employed to examine the association between PA of different intensities and preschool children’s balance abilities, while also considering gender and age differences in these associations.ResultsLight PA (LPA) (β = 0.157, p = 0.013) and Vigorous PA (VPA) (β = 0.163, p = 0.034) were positively associated with static balance in preschool children, while Moderate PA (MPA) (β = -0.226, p = 0.013) was negatively associated. A negative association was observed between VPA (β = -0.327, p < 0.001) and dynamic balance. Regarding gender, VPA was negatively associated with dynamic balance in both boys (β = -0.361, p < 0.001) and girls (β = -0.250, p = 0.021). LPA was positively associated with static balance in girls (β = 0.196, p = 0.038) and with dynamic balance in boys (β = 0.163, p = 0.047). Regarding age, at age five, LPA (β = 0.261, p = 0.014) was positively associated with static balance, whereas VPA (β = -0.382, p = 0.003) was negatively associated with dynamic balance. At age six, LPA (β = 0.307, p = 0.012) was positively associated with dynamic balance.ConclusionsPA of different intensities is associated with balance ability in preschool children, and this association exhibits gender and age differences. Based on these findings, schools should fully consider activity intensity, as well as gender and age differences among preschool children, when developing PA intervention strategies related to balance abilities, and adopt more targeted approaches.

Introduction

Balance serves as the foundation for preschool children to learn complex motor skills and plays a crucial role in their motor development and overall health [1]. Balance is defined as the ability to maintain posture or to adjust it automatically under static or dynamic conditions and is commonly classified as static or dynamic balance [2]. Preschool children maintain postural balance primarily through visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive systems. Information is transmitted through afferent pathways to the central nervous system, where it is processed to generate appropriate muscular responses for postural adjustment, thereby maintaining bodily equilibrium [3]. Previous research indicates that balance develops progressively with age, and ages 3–6 years constitute a critical period for balance development in children [4]. During this period, children begin to appropriately utilize and integrate three distinct sensory information sources (e.g., visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive) to maintain balance. Notably, insufficient balance development in early childhood may hinder the acquisition of fundamental motor skills and lead to related physical deficiencies, thereby limiting the improvement of motor competence and increasing the risk of injuries during physical activity (PA) [5, 6].

Previous studies have demonstrated that regular PA enhances muscle strength, thereby improving human balance and coordination [7]. Huang’s research found that sufficient PA correlates with better dynamic balance in college students [8]. Concurrently, increasing moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) also enhances static balance in young adults [7]. Furthermore, PA promotes both static and dynamic balance in older adults [9–11]. Although previous studies have shown that PA can improve balance in adults and older adults, the relationship between PA and balance in preschool children remains unclear. Existing research on balance in preschool children has primarily focused on developmental trajectories of balance, the relationship between static and dynamic balance, the effects of relevant interventions on balance, and gender and age differences in balance [2, 12–14]. In contrast, there is a relative lack of research on the relationship between PA and balance ability in preschool children, particularly studies that simultaneously examine the relationship between PA of different intensities and both static and dynamic balance abilities. Given that preschool children are in a critical period of balance development, it is necessary to use objective measurement tools to assess their PA levels and balance abilities, and to further explore the associations between PA of different intensities and their static and dynamic balance abilities. Such research will help clarify the relationship between PA and balance abilities in preschool children and provide a basis for designing interventions to promote balance development.

Gender and age are significant factors influencing balance ability and PA in preschool children. Balance ability gradually improves with age, and older preschool children perform better than younger children across all balance tests [15]. Additionally, girls demonstrate higher postural stability across various balance assessments [14]. Regarding PA, boys are typically more active than girls and engage in vigorous activities more frequently [16]. PA levels among preschool children decline with age [17]. Although previous studies have confirmed gender and age differences in balance ability and PA among preschool children, it remains unclear whether the relationship between PA of different intensities and balance ability is similarly influenced by gender and age. Clarifying this relationship will provide a basis for developing targeted intervention strategies to promote balance development in preschool children and offer evidence-based guidance for the design of school physical education curricula. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between PA of different intensities and balance ability in preschool children, and further examine whether this relationship exhibits gender and age differences.

Methods

Power calculations

Before participant recruitment, we utilized the “pwr” package in R to calculate the required sample size for the study. With the following assumptions: effect size (f2) = 0.15; power = 0.8; α = 0.05; three predictor variables; and a multivariate linear regression analysis, accounting for a 20% attrition rate, the results indicated a minimum sample size of 385 participants.

Participants

Participants for this study were recruited in May 2022 from kindergartens in Suzhou, Luoyang, and Zhengzhou, China. The research team contacted kindergartens in these three cities that were willing to participate and met the basic testing requirements. Ultimately, six kindergartens were included, and 525 preschool children were selected from these institutions. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants were as follows. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 3 and 6 years; (2) normal vision and the ability to complete the test without assistance; (3) good health and the ability to participate in PA without restrictions; and (4) provision of written informed consent by a parent or legal guardian. The exclusion criteria were: (1) visual impairment; (2) cognitive impairment that prevented understanding of or compliance with the test instructions; and (3) a history of falls or muscle injuries within the previous six months. Before participation, the research team thoroughly explained the study objectives to the participants and their parents. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty if they experienced any physical discomfort during the process. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (Ref: 102772019RT034) before participant recruitment, and all research procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Physical activity measurement

Participants’ PA levels were measured using a triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph GT3X+), a device widely used to objectively assess PA in preschool children. Participants were instructed to wear the accelerometer on the upper right iliac crest for 7 consecutive days before balance testing. Given the young age of the participants, parents assisted with attaching and removing the device. Parents were informed that the accelerometer should be worn continuously except during activities likely to damage it, such as bathing or swimming.

Data was processed using ActiLife 6.13.4 software. Zero-value sequences lasting 20 min or longer were deleted and classified as “non-wear time”. Participants were included in the final statistical analysis only if they provided at least 3 days of valid data (including 2 weekdays and 1 weekend day) during the 7-day measurement period, with a daily wear time of more than 480 min. PA intensity categories were defined according to Pate’s research [18], as follows: Sedentary Behavior (SED) (≤ 100 counts), Light PA (LPA) (101–1679 counts), Moderate PA (MPA) (1680–3367 counts), and Vigorous PA (VPA) (≥ 3368 counts).

Balance measurement

Static balance

Static balance was measured using the one-leg stance test. Previous research has shown that this test has good reliability and validity in preschool children [19]. Participants stood on a flat surface with their feet shoulder-width apart and their eyes open. At the “Start” command, participants raised both arms laterally to shoulder height and lifted one foot off the ground. The lifted foot could be raised to any height but was not allowed to touch the supporting leg. During the trial, arm movement was permitted to help maintain balance, but participants were not allowed to grasp the leg. Timing began when the lifted foot left the ground and was stopped immediately if any of the following occurred: (1) the supporting foot moved or hopped; (2) the lifted foot touched the ground; (3) the lifted foot contacted the supporting leg. Each participant completed two trials using the dominant leg; the best performance was recorded in seconds to two decimal places.

Dynamic balance

Dynamic balance was measured using the balance beam test. Previous studies have confirmed that this test demonstrates good reliability and validity in preschool children [20]. Participants walked along a wooden balance beam measuring 3 m in length, 10 cm in width, and 30 cm in height. At the start of each trial, participants stood on the platform behind the starting line, facing the beam with both arms extended laterally at shoulder height. At the “Go” command, participants proceeded toward the finish line using alternating steps. The tester stood slightly in front of and to the side of the participant, started timing when the participant began to move, and walked alongside the participant to the finish line while closely monitoring movement to ensure safety. Timing was stopped immediately when the toe of either foot crossed the finish line. Each participant completed two trials, and the best performance was recorded in seconds to two decimal places.

Anthropometry

Height (m) was measured using a portable stadiometer (GMCS-IV). Participants stood barefoot on the stadiometer base plate with the torso upright, arms relaxed at the sides, legs straight, heels together, and toes positioned at approximately 60° of external rotation. The heels, sacrum, and both scapulae were in contact with the vertical backboard. The head was positioned in the Frankfort plane, with the participant looking straight ahead and the superior margin of the tragus horizontally aligned with the inferior margin of the orbit. Height was recorded in centimeters (cm) to one decimal place. Weight (kg) was measured using a portable scale (GMCS-IV). Before measurement, participants removed outer garments and any heavy items from their pockets. During measurement, participants stood barefoot in the center of the scale platform in a natural posture; weight was read once a stable stance was achieved. Weight was recorded in kilograms (kg) to one decimal place. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared.

Quality control

First, to ensure the accuracy and reliability of accelerometer measurements, this study provided standardized training to researchers before formal data collection. The training emphasized the correct positioning and wearing methods of the accelerometers, along with relevant precautions. During the measurement period, the research team reminded parents and teachers daily to check device wear status and promptly record any abnormalities or discomfort. In cases of loose attachment or device malfunction, researchers promptly replaced or adjusted the accelerometers to maintain the continuity and integrity of data collection. Second, before the formal balance test, the tester provided participants with a detailed explanation and demonstration of the testing procedures. Each participant was allowed one practice session before the formal test to ensure full comprehension of the requirements. If participants demonstrated misunderstandings during practice, test administrators provided additional explanations and demonstrations. All balance tests were conducted in the same venue, and each item was measured twice. Testers recorded results on score sheets immediately after each test. Finally, before conducting anthropometric measurements, testers calibrated the height gauge and weight scale and explained testing requirements and precautions to participants to ensure standardized measurement procedures.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27. Before analysis, data were explored, and normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were calculated by group and gender and reported as mean and standard deviation. Independent samples t-tests were used to examine gender differences in Age, BMI, PA, and balance variables. Multivariate linear regression was employed to assess the associations between PA of different intensities and balance ability in preschool children. Concurrently, multivariate linear regression analyses stratified by gender and age explored gender and age-related differences in the association between PA of different intensities and balance ability in preschool children.

Among 525 participants, 116 were excluded for the following reasons: (1) lack of valid accelerometer readings (n = 45); (2) incomplete balance test data (n = 40); (3) outliers (n = 6); (4) presence of missing values (n = 25). Ultimately, the valid sample included in the study comprised 409 participants (Boys = 215, Girls = 194), with a mean age of 4.84 ± 0.95 years. Associations between physical activity of different intensities and static and dynamic balance in preschool children, overall and by gender

The associations between PA of different intensities and static and dynamic balance in preschool children, overall and by gender, are shown in Fig. 1. The results indicate that PA of different intensities is associated with static balance in preschool children. Specifically, LPA (β = 0.157, p = 0.013) and VPA (β = 0.163, p = 0.034) showed positive associations with static balance, while MPA (β = -0.226, p = 0.013) exhibited a negative association with static balance. Regarding dynamic balance, only VPA (β = -0.327, p < 0.001) was found to be negatively associated with dynamic balance in preschool children. Among boys, no significant association was found between static balance and LPA (β = 0.092, p = 0.288), MPA (β = -0.207, p = 0.091), and VPA (β = 0.182, p = 0.084). Regarding dynamic balance in boys, a positive association was observed between LPA (β = 0.163, p = 0.047) and dynamic balance, while a negative association was found between VPA (β = -0.361, p < 0.001) and dynamic balance. Among girls, LPA (β = 0.196, p = 0.038) was positively associated with static balance. Regarding dynamic balance in girls, VPA (β = -0.250, p = 0.021) was negatively associated with dynamic balance.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the association between PA of different intensities and balance abilities in preschool children, and further analyzing whether this association differs by gender and age. The results indicate that PA of different intensities is associated with both static and dynamic balance abilities in preschool children. Notably, the association between PA intensity and balance abilities shows gender and age differences. Among boys, PA was associated only with dynamic balance, whereas among girls, it was associated with both static and dynamic balance. Regarding age, no associations were found between PA and balance abilities at ages three and four. At age five, PA was associated with both static and dynamic balance abilities. At age six, PA was associated only with dynamic balance ability.

PA of different intensities was associated with balance ability in preschool children. Regarding static balance, studies have found that LPA and VPA are positively associated with static balance ability in preschool children. Previous research indicates that LPA performed in daily life (such as walking and standing) requires moderate muscular activity to stabilize the pelvis and regulate balance control [21, 22]. Experience in maintaining balance across multiple postures may contribute to the development of static balance abilities in preschool children. Concurrently, participation in VPA promotes adaptive changes in muscle cellular metabolism, enhances lower-limb muscle strength, and thereby supports the development of static balance in preschool children [23, 24]. This study found a negative association between MPA and static balance ability in preschool children. This finding should be interpreted with caution. One possible explanation is that the result may reflect differences in the neural control and postural regulation demands of dynamic and static balance [12, 25]. MPA primarily consists of dynamic activities, such as running and jumping, which typically involve frequent shifts in the center of gravity and changes in direction. Because balance ability is highly task-specific, higher levels of MPA may preferentially promote adaptations related to dynamic postural control while providing relatively limited stimulation for the capacities required to maintain a static posture. Consequently, this may manifest as a negative association with the development of static balance ability [26, 27]. However, the above explanation remains a plausible interpretation based on current research findings and requires further validation through future longitudinal and intervention studies. Regarding dynamic balance, only VPA was found to be positively associated with dynamic balance ability in preschool children. Existing research indicates that VPA promotes increased concentration of the sodium-potassium pump (Na+, K+-ATPase) in the human body, thereby enhancing neuromuscular excitability and improving muscle contraction function [28, 29]. This may provide a physiological basis for the development of dynamic balance ability in preschool children.

The associations between PA of different intensities and balance ability among preschool children showed gender differences. Regarding static balance, only LPA was positively associated with static balance ability in girls. This association may be related to girls’ greater preference for activities like dance, swings, and balance beams during school hours. Although these activities are relatively low in intensity, they may contribute to the development of balance and may therefore be associated with better static balance performance [30, 31]. Among boys, no association was found between PA and static balance. This discrepancy may stem from the immature central nervous system and insufficient sensory integration in boys aged 3–6 years, limiting the positive impact of PA on their static balance [32]. Regarding dynamic balance, LPA was negatively associated with dynamic balance in boys, whereas VPA was positively associated with dynamic balance in both boys and girls. VPA may enhance neural reaction speed, proprioception, and motor coordination, thereby supporting the development of dynamic balance ability in preschool children [33, 34].

The associations between PA of different intensities and balance ability among preschool children differed across age groups. At ages three and four, no association was found between PA and either static or dynamic balance abilities. Previous studies have indicated that in preschool children aged three to four years, the nervous and proprioceptive systems are not yet fully developed, and balance control relies primarily on visual information for postural adjustment [35]. However, existing research indicates that the visual-enhancing effects associated with PA are relatively limited, which may further weaken the association between PA and balance abilities in three- and four-year-old children [36]. At age five, studies found that LPA was positively associated with static balance ability in preschool children, whereas VPA showed a positive association with dynamic balance ability. Research indicates that after age four, children enter a period of rapid development as the central nervous system matures, a system responsible for integrating sensory input [37]. During this stage, VPA may support the development of dynamic balance ability by enhancing neural response speed and proprioception [37, 38]. At age six, this study found that LPA was negatively associated with dynamic balance ability. Previous research has indicated that dynamic balance depends on rapid adjustments to the center of gravity during movement, a process closely associated with neuromuscular control and muscle strength [39, 40]. A high volume of LPA may not provide sufficient functional stimulation for the development of dynamic balance; furthermore, prolonged LPA may lead to muscle fatigue, which could, to some extent, impair neuromuscular responses and consequently reduce dynamic balance performance [41–43]. It should be noted that this explanation remains a plausible interpretation based on research findings and cannot yet be regarded as a definitive mechanism; further research is needed to verify it.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The primary strength of this study lies in the use of objective measurement tools to assess preschool children’s PA levels and balance abilities, thereby enhancing the accuracy and reliability of the findings. Furthermore, to our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the associations between PA of different intensities and balance abilities in preschool children and to further analyze whether these associations differ by gender and age. This study provides valuable information and theoretical foundations for intervention research aimed at promoting balance development in preschool children. Despite the systematic analyses conducted in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study employed a cross-sectional design and therefore cannot establish a causal relationship between PA and balance ability in preschool children. Second, the sample was drawn only from Suzhou, Luoyang, and Zhengzhou, which limits the generalizability of findings. Additionally, this study did not account for the influence of other potential covariates on the results. Future research should employ longitudinal or interventional studies to explore the causal relationship between PA of different intensities and balance ability in greater depth. Expanding the sample size would also improve the external validity of the findings.

Conclusions

This study found that PA of different intensities is associated with balance ability in preschool children, and that this association differs by gender and age. Regarding gender, VPA was positively associated with dynamic balance ability in both boys and girls. LPA was positively associated with static balance ability in girls, but negatively associated with dynamic balance ability in boys. Regarding age, no association was found between PA and balance ability at ages three and four. At age five, LPA was positively associated with static balance, while VPA was negatively associated with dynamic balance. By age six, only LPA was found to be negatively associated with dynamic balance. Based on these findings, schools should fully consider activity intensity, as well as gender and age differences among preschool children, when developing PA intervention strategies related to balance abilities, and adopt more targeted approaches.
