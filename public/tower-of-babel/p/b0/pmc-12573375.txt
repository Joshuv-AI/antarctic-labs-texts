Serum neuronal pentraxin 2 levels are associated with shorter survival in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Soha Alali, Jonas Dubin, Frederik Hobin, Shreyasee Das, Charlotte Lambrechts, Erik Stoops, Eugen Vanmechelen, Philip van Damme, Koen Poesen
Published: 2025

Introduction

Synapse loss is well-documented in many neurodegenerative diseases, with recently strong associations observed in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Emerging evidence demonstrates thattransactive response DNA binding protein of 43 kDa (TDP-43) pathology, the most prominent hallmark in ALS, is linked to synapse loss,1 2 as evidenced by the inclusion of a cryptic exon in the Unc 13 Homolog A gene— which plays a key role in synaptic transmission—resulting in synaptic dysfunction.1 A recent study revealed that TDP-43 regulates the expression of neuronal pentraxin 2 (NPTX2) RNA, encoding a synaptic protein involved in synaptic plasticity. Disruption of this regulation leads to NPTX2 accumulation, highlighting another link between TDP-43 dysregulation and synaptic dysfunction.2 Moreover, a recent proteomics study has identified NPTX2 as a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker in ALS patients, revealing decreased levels compared with healthy controls (HC).3 Alterations in another synaptic protein, synaptosomal-associated protein of 25 kDa (SNAP-25), have been recently identified in Alzheimer’s disease and other neurodegenerative disorders, reflecting synaptic integrity.4 Here, we measured serum levels of NPTX2 and SNAP25 to assess ALS clinical correlates while benchmarking included synaptic biomarkers against the most well-established blood biomarker in ALS, neurofilament light chain (NfL).

Methods

NPTX2 and SNAP-25 serum levels were quantified using the LUMIPULSE and single molecule array platforms, respectively, with homebrew prototype assays developed by ADx NeuroSciences (Gent, Belgium). NfL serum levels were quantified using a commercially available kit (Fujirebio, Gent, Belgium). Measurements were performed on serum samples of 43 ALS patients, 54 disease controls (DC) and 16 HC. More detailed methodological information is provided in the online supplementary methods.

Results

The characteristics of the study cohort are shown in online supplemental tables 1 and 2. No significant differences were observed across the serum levels of synaptic biomarkers among ALS, DC and HC (online supplemental figure 1A–B). SNAP-25 levels correlated with age (rSpearman=0.36, pSpearman=0.0002), while NPTX2 did not. No sex-related associations were observed. Neither synaptic biomarker was associated with the other or with NfL. Both synaptic biomarkers effectively distinguished ALS from HC (AUCNPTX2=0.987, AUCSNAP-25=0.993) but failed to differentiate ALS from DC.

None of the biomarkers were associated with the disease progression rate or forced vital capacity (FVC). Serum levels of NPTX2 and NfL were associated with ALS Functional Rating Scale – Revised (ALSFRS-R) slope (NPTX2: (β=0.20, p=0.030); NfL (β=0.27, p=0.005), online supplemental figure 2A–B).

The association of serum synaptic biomarkers with overall survival in ALS was evaluated by dichotomising patients into low- and high-biomarker subcohorts based on the median levels of the respective biomarker. Overall survival was significantly shorter in patients with elevated serum NPTX2 (figure 1A; median survival=17.7 months vs 37.5 months, plog-rank=0.009) or elevated NfL (figure 1B; median survival=19.4 months vs 42.5 months, plog-rank=0.0008). Classifying ALS patients into three groups—(i) both elevated NPTX2 and NfL, (ii) either elevated NPTX2 or NfL, and (iii) both low NPTX2 and NfL—revealed three significantly different survival profiles (figure 1C; median survival=17.1 vs 28.7 vs 76.6 months, plog-rank<0.0001).

(B) Kaplan-Meier plot showing the overall survival probability of ALS patients (n=42) stratified by median serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels. The p-value of the log-rank test is reported. In single biomarker Cox models (online supplemental table 3), high NPTX2 (HR 3.66 (95% CI 1.41 to 9.50), p=0.008) and high NfL (HR 3.16 (95% CI 1.21 to 8.25), p=0.019) were significant survival predictors. Interestingly, combining NPTX2 with NfL improved the predictive performance compared with either marker alone (p=0.03), yielding HRs of 3.39 (95% CI 1.29 to 8.93) for elevated NPTX2 and 2.78 (95% CI 1.08 to 7.12) for elevated NfL levels (online supplemental table 3). Online supplemental table 4 presents the HRs for the earlier mentioned ALS subgroup classification ((i), (ii), (iii)), showing an HR of 9.90 (95% CI 2.56 to 38.24) for patients with both high NfL and high NPTX2 serum levels.

Discussion

This study identifies serum NPTX2 as a promising synaptic biomarker in ALS, offering additional prognostic information alongside NfL. Elevated serum NPTX2 levels were associated with shorter overall survival, and integrating NPTX2 into a survival prediction model with serum NfL improved the predictive performance compared with single biomarker models. This combination also refined patient stratification into distinct survival groups (short, intermediate and long survivors). Our findings suggest that using multibiomarker models that reflect distinct disease mechanisms enhances survival-based patient profiling, consistent with our earlier CSF findings in ALS.5 Hence, our study now demonstrates that a novel serum synaptic biomarker improves survival prediction alongside serum NfL in ALS.

Previously, Oh et al reported reduced CSF levels of NPTX2 in ALS patients compared with HC. However, we did not replicate this diagnostic finding, at least in serum. Potential explanations include differences between measurement techniques, the distinct biological properties of CSF versus blood, small sample size and disease heterogeneity unaccounted for. While NPTX2 is widely expressed in the nervous system, it is highly enriched in peripheral tissues, particularly endocrine organs, as indicated by the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org). Further research is needed to explore endocrine involvement in ALS and its influence on circulating peripheral NPTX2 levels in ALS. While Oh et al reported no association between CSF NPTX2 levels and clinical disease status in ALS, we observed an association between serum NPTX2 levels and the ALSFRS-R slope, suggesting that serum NPTX2 could serve as a potential biomarker for tracking disease severity.

The association of NPTX2 levels with overall survival in ALS aligns with previous findings of dysregulated NPTX2 expression in ALS, underscoring the link between TDP-43 pathology and synaptic dysfunction/loss.13 Importantly, the ability of a synaptic biomarker in blood to improve survival prediction beyond an established biomarker highlights synaptic dysregulation as a measurable key disease mechanism in ALS. Despite its relevance, research on synaptic biomarkers in ALS biofluids remains limited, with most studies focusing on Alzheimer’s disease. Our findings emphasise the need to prioritise synaptic biomarkers research in ALS.

Given the modest cohort size, future studies are needed to validate our findings in larger cohorts, assess longitudinal serum NPTX2 changes for disease monitoring and determine whether synaptic blood biomarkers further improve survival prediction models alongside established prognostic factors.5

Conclusion

This study identifies serum NPTX2 as a promising synaptic biomarker in ALS, providing complementary prognostic information alongside NfL. Combined with biomarkers of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, it could enable more precise approaches for ALS prognosis, disease monitoring and patient outcome evaluation. Future studies are required to validate these findings in larger cohorts, explore subtype correlations and evaluate their potential for therapeutic targeting.
