Acupuncture vs. sham acupuncture for cancer-related fatigue in patients with breast cancer: a double-center, randomized, single-blind pilot study
Authors: Yu Huang, Jianying Chang, Xianming Wu, Guishuang He, Qin Guo, Caihong Lan, Shuo Yang, Qian Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCancer-related fatigue is highly prevalent among cancer patients. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of acupuncture on fatigue levels and quality of life in breast cancer patients with cancer-related fatigue.MethodsThis pilot study employed a double-center, randomized, single-blind, placebo-controlled design. A total of 68 breast cancer patients with cancer-related fatigue who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were recruited and randomly allocated into either an acupuncture group (n = 34) or a sham acupuncture group (n = 34). Both groups selected the same acupoints: CV12, CV4, CV6, LI4 (bilaterally), KI3 (bilaterally), ST36 (bilaterally), and SP6 (bilaterally). The acupuncture group employed traditional acupuncture techniques to insert needles into the acupoints to elicit the deqi sensation. By contrast, the sham acupuncture group utilized blunt needles that did not penetrate the skin. Both groups underwent a 6-week treatment regimen consisting of 20 sessions (4 sessions per week during weeks 1–3 (approximately every other day) and a total of 8 sessions during weeks 4–6 (2–3 sessions per week, every 2–3 days), each lasting 30 min). Changes in scores on the brief fatigue inventory (BFI), fatigue scale-14 (FS-14), and hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS) were assessed at week 0, 6, 8, and 10. Additionally, compliance with and safety of acupuncture were documented. Research variables between the two groups were compared using a significance level of p < 0.05.ResultsThe study revealed a time-dependent therapeutic advantage of acupuncture over sham acupuncture. While both groups showed similar short-term reductions in fatigue (BFI: acupuncture 6.69 ± 1.66 vs. sham acupuncture 6.19 ± 1.60; between-group p = 0.223) and psychological distress at week 6 after treatment, acupuncture demonstrated progressively amplified benefits. During the 10-week follow-up period, acupuncture achieved significantly greater reductions in fatigue severity (BFI: Δ = -1.28, p = 0.011; FS-14: Δ = -1.81, p = 0.003) and psychological symptoms (HADS-A: Δ = -2.06, p = 0.006; HADS-D: Δ = -1.81, p = 0.003), with intergroup differences widening over time (week 8 vs. week 10). Both groups exhibited full compliance, and acupuncture-related adverse events were limited to mild ecchymosis (9.4%). These findings suggest acupuncture induces time-dependent modulation beyond placebo effects.ConclusionAcupuncture can sustainably and effectively alleviate fatigue symptoms in cancer patients, improve their anxiety and depressive symptoms, and demonstrate high safety and compliance.Trial registrationThis clinical trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300069361; 14/03/2023) (https://www.chictr.org.cn/).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05341-2.

Background

Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is defined as a distressing and persistent experience associated with cancer or its treatment, characterized by subjective sensations of fatigue or exhaustion that may involve physical, emotional, and/or cognitive domains [1, 2]. This type of fatigue is typically disproportionate to the recent activity level and can substantially impair patients’ daily functioning and quality of life [3]. The pathogenesis of CRF is complex, and its exact mechanism remains unclear. Research indicates that immune responses, endocrine factors, medications, nutritional/ psychological factors, and personal behaviors may all contribute to the development of CRF [4]. Potential biological mechanisms include abnormal cytokine regulation [5], autonomic nervous system (ANS) dysfunction [6], hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation [6, 7], and serotonin neurotransmitter imbalance [8], among others.

Studies have demonstrated that up to 90% of breast cancer patients experience fatigue [7]. During radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy, 30% to 60% of patients report moderate-to-severe fatigue [9]. Notably, approximately one-third of breast cancer patients develop fatigue symptoms within one year after completing chemotherapy, with significant CRF persisting for up to six years post-treatment [10]. Furthermore, 91.4% of patients reported severe functional impairments due to CRF, which substantially diminished their overall quality of life [10], exacerbated their physical and psychological burden [11], and hindered their ability to fulfill work, family, and social responsibilities [10, 12].

Modern medicine frequently utilizes pharmacological treatments (central stimulants, antidepressants, and glucocorticoids) and non-pharmacological interventions (psychological education, cognitive behavioral therapy, rehabilitation training, dietary regulation, and sleep management) to alleviate CRF [13–16]. However, pharmacological treatments remain controversial due to safety concerns and population suitability [17]. The effectiveness of non-pharmacological strategies in improving CRF remains uncertain owing to the heterogeneity of research findings [17, 18]. In recent years, traditional acupuncture therapy has increasingly emerged as a primary alternative treatment for managing cancer-related complications (e.g., nausea, vomiting, and fatigue induced by radiotherapy or chemotherapy) owing to its high practicability, minimal side effects, and significant therapeutic efficacy [19–22]. Compared with pharmacological therapies, acupuncture offers a safe and economical option with fewer adverse effects, making it particularly suitable for cancer patients with compromised immune function.

Mechanistically, acupuncture has been shown to address the underlying pathology of CRF. Previous studies demonstrate that specific acupoints can regulate immune function and neuroendocrine pathways. For instance, acupuncture at Guanyuan (CV4) can downregulate pro-inflammatory factors such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), thereby alleviating fatigue symptoms associated with chemotherapy [23]. Taixi (KI3), the source point of the Kidney meridian, is reported to improve mitochondrial energy metabolism and endocrine regulation, contributing to the relief of chronic fatigue and mood disorders [24]. Furthermore, Zhongwan (CV12), Qihai (CV6), and Zusanli (ST36) are frequently used to manage chemotherapy-related gastrointestinal reactions and regulate immunity; combinations centered on CV12 and ST36 have been shown to relieve nausea and vomiting while improving quality of life [25, 26]. Hegu (LI4) is also frequently combined with these points to reduce the overall severity of CRF by modulating inflammatory mediators and neurotransmitters [19, 27, 28]. Based on these evidence-based mechanisms and the principles of Traditional Chinese Medicine, our research group formulated a standardized acupoint prescription to enhance intervention specificity [25]. The final prescription selected for this study included: CV12, CV4, CV6, bilateral LI4, KI3, ST36, and SP6.

Although numerous randomized controlled trials have investigated the clinical efficacy of acupuncture in treating CRF in patients with breast cancer in recent years, their methodological designs still present several limitations. Most studies have compared acupuncture plus conventional treatment (basic care, psychological support, exercise guidance, and nutritional counselling) with conventional treatment alone [29–31], or have used non-acupoint, non-meridian superficial needling as a sham control [12, 32, 33]. In addition, acupoint prescriptions have often been heterogeneous and not specifically derived from the Traditional Chinese Medicine pathophysiology of CRF, and outcome assessments have usually ended immediately after the treatment course, leaving the cumulative and sustained effects of acupuncture unclear. As a result, previous trials have not systematically evaluated the difference between true acupuncture and sham acupuncture delivered at corresponding acupoints, making it difficult to distinguish specific acupoint effects from non-specific procedural effects and to build a robust framework for clinical efficacy evaluation. To address these gaps, we designed a double-center, randomized, single-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial in breast cancer patients with CRF, using a standardized, guideline-based acupoint prescription and sham needling with blunt needles at the same acupoints to better control placebo-related factors. This study evaluates the effects of acupuncture on fatigue severity, quality of life and safety over time, with the aim of providing an evidence-based basis for optimizing non-pharmacological management strategies for CRF.

Methods

Design

This pilot study employed a double-center, randomized, single-blind, placebo-controlled design. Participants with CRF were recruited from the Department of Oncology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Chinese Medicine (from March 15, 2023, to December 15, 2024) and the inpatient and outpatient departments of the Breast Tumor Center at the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (from July 25, 2023, to December 15, 2024).

Diagnostic criteria of CRF

The definition of CRF referenced the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), fatigue symptoms occurred repeatedly and lasted for more than 2 weeks, accompanied by five or more of the following symptoms: (1) Weakness in the body and heaviness in the limbs; (2) Difficulty concentrating as before; (3) Low mood, decreased interest and lack of enthusiasm; (4) Obvious decline in sleep quality compared to normal; (5) After rest, one’s physical strength cannot be restored to the previous level; (6) General daily activities feel more difficult than before; (7) Increase in negative emotions in daily life; (8) Feeling insufficient energy to complete normal life activities as before; (9) Not remembering recent events as clearly as before; (10) Fatigue lasts for hours and does not relieve after rest.

Sample size calculation and randomization

This pilot study evaluated the feasibility and scientific validity of the proposed study design. The sample size was determined based on efficacy estimates derived from a small-scale pilot study. A two-sided hypothesis test was conducted using PASS 15.0 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA), with a significance level (α) set at 0.05 and statistical power (1-β) at 0.80. According to initial data, the estimated efficacy rate for the acupuncture group was 89%, compared to 60% for the sham acupuncture group. The calculated minimum sample size per group was 31. To account for potential participant attrition, an additional 10% over-recruitment was planned, resulting in a target of 34 participants per group, for a total sample size of 68 participants (Fig.1). Participants were randomized into either the acupuncture or sham acupuncture group using a computerized randomization system with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Randomization numbers were sequentially placed in sealed, opaque envelopes, and participants were allocated according to their enrollment sequence. Clinicians opened the envelopes in order and administered the assigned treatment accordingly.

Blinded design and assessment

Given the unique nature of acupuncture procedures, complete blinding of group assignments during actual treatment was unfeasible. Therefore, this study employed a single-blind design. A strict separation of roles was maintained throughout the process: practitioner, efficacy evaluator, and statistician. A third-party investigator, unaware of group assignments, was responsible for efficacy assessment. Participants, outcome assessors, and statisticians were all blinded to specific group assignments. To assess blinding success, at the end of the 6-week treatment period, participants were asked to guess their group assignment by answering the question: “Which treatment do you think you received?” with three options: “Real acupuncture,” “Sham acupuncture,” or “Unsure.”

Interventions

Basic treatment

Both groups maintained their oncologist-prescribed cancer treatments (radiotherapy/chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, etc.). To isolate the effect of the study intervention, new drugs or non-pharmacological interventions for cancer-related fatigue were not permitted during the trial.

Standardization of acupuncturists and procedures

At both centers, the interventions were performed by licensed acupuncturists with at least 3 years of clinical experience in acupuncture. Before recruitment began, all acupuncturists attended a joint training workshop led by the principal investigator. During this workshop, the study protocol, acupoint locations (CV12, CV4, CV6, LI4, KI3, ST36, SP6), needling depth, manipulation techniques (including the criteria for achieving deqi and the number and strength of lifting-thrusting and rotating movements), and the procedures for sham acupuncture using blunt needles were reviewed and practiced. A written treatment manual with step-by-step instructions and photographs was provided, and all practitioners were required to follow this manual for every session. A senior investigator observed the first few treatments delivered by each acupuncturist to confirm adherence to the protocol, and any inconsistencies were discussed in regular monitoring meetings. No significant deviations from the standardized procedure were observed during the trial.

Acupuncture group

Huatuo 0.30 × 50 mm disposable acupuncture needles (Suzhou Pharmaceutical Products Co., Ltd.; Registration No. sxzz 20162200970; License No. sxyjx 20010020) were selected. With the patient supine and relaxed, acupoints CV12, CV4, CV6, LI4 (bilaterally), KI3 (bilaterally), ST36 (bilaterally), and SP6 (bilaterally) were selected according to Acupuncture and Moxibustion Science (detailed locations are provided in Table 1), disinfected, and affixed with fixation pads. Needles were inserted vertically through the pads to a depth of 30–40 mm (Fig.2a, b, c). After deqi, all acupoints were manipulated with gentle lifting-thrusting and twisting-rotating techniques. Needles were retained for 30 min, with manipulation repeated every 10 min for three sessions. Treatment consisted of 20 sessions over 6 weeks: four sessions/week (approximately every other day) for the first 3 weeks, then a total of eight sessions during the final 3 weeks (2–3 sessions/week, every 2–3 days).

Sham acupuncture group

A 0.30 × 25 mm blunt-head needle (Suzhou Medical Supplies Co., Ltd., Registration No. sxzz 20162200970; License No. sxyjx production license No. 20010020) was used with a disposable sponge base (Suzhou Tianxie Acupuncture Equipment Co., Ltd.) made of 5 mm thick EPE pearl cotton and 0.15 mm thick double-sided adhesive (3M9889), 11 mm in diameter. Acupoints identical to the acupuncture group were selected. Patients were positioned supine, and the selected acupoints were routinely disinfected. Fixation pads were then adhered to the acupoints, and 1-inch blunt needles were inserted through the pad to the skin surface, without penetrating it (Fig. 2d, e, f). This sham acupuncture procedure mimicked the tactile sensation of real acupuncture, providing patients with a perception of needle contact against the skin surface. Needles were retained for 30 min without manipulation. Treatment consisted of 20 sessions over 6 weeks: four sessions/week (approximately every other day) for the first 3 weeks, then a total of eight sessions during the final 3 weeks (2–3 sessions/week, every 2–3 days).

Assessment

Fatigue was the primary clinical outcome of interest. The Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI) total score was prespecified as the primary outcome, with repeated assessments at baseline and at weeks 6, 8, and 10 to track changes over time. The BFI assessed fatigue severity and functional interference across nine items on a 0–10 numerical rating scale (0 = no fatigue, 10 = worst possible fatigue); the total score therefore ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe fatigue. In clinical practice, BFI scores ≥ 4 were commonly considered indicative of clinically relevant fatigue [34].

Secondary outcomes were the Fatigue Scale-14 (FS-14) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). The FS-14 distinguished between physical (8 items) and mental fatigue (6 items) using dichotomous scoring, yielding a total score of 0 to 14, with higher scores indicating more severe fatigue. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were evaluated by licensed psychiatrists (≥ 2 years’ experience) using the HADS anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) subscales, each ranging from 0 to 21 (0–7: normal, 8–10: possible anxiety/depression, ≥ 11: probable anxiety/depression). All three instruments had been validated in oncology populations.

Safety evaluation and adverse reaction monitoring

Vital signs (temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure) were monitored closely. Any acupuncture-related adverse events, such as vascular injury, needle retention, bending or breakage, fainting, nerve damage, or needle site infection, were documented to ensure participant safety. At each visit, these events were recorded and their severity was graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI CTCAE) Version 5.0. ‘Mild’ adverse events were defined as Grade 1, which included asymptomatic or mild symptoms that resolved on their own or with minimal non-medical management, such as a warm compress.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Efficacy analyses were conducted on a per-protocol basis, including the 64 participants who received the allocated intervention and completed the main follow-up assessments; four randomized patients (two in the acupuncture group and two in the sham acupuncture group) who did not receive any treatment were excluded from the analysis. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). Before analysis, continuous data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed data with equal variances were analyzed using independent t-tests. When the assumptions for parametric tests were not met, nonparametric tests (e.g., the Mann-Whitney U test) were used. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. The main hypothesis testing focused on between-group differences in changes in BFI scores over time. Analyses of FS-14 and HADS-A/HADS-D were treated as secondary and interpreted in an exploratory manner, without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons. Missing questionnaire data were not imputed; analyses at each time point were based on available cases. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported where relevant.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Chinese Medicine (No. PY2023001; March 1, 2023) and from the Ethics Review Committee of the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (No. FZ2023-07-242; July 20, 2023). Patient recruitment began on March 15, 2023, at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Chinese Medicine, and on July 25, 2023, at the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Guizhou Medical University, both after ethics approval had been obtained at each center. The trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300069361; March 14, 2023), before enrolment of the first participant. The trial protocol and predefined outcomes were not changed after recruitment began. All participants provided written informed consent, including consent for publication of their clinical images in this article, and their data were kept confidential. Both ethics committees approved the use of patient images in Fig. 2. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials). The completed CONSORT checklist is provided as an additional file [Additional File 1] with this submission.

Results

Respondents’ characteristics

A total of 74 patients with breast cancer-related fatigue were recruited for this study. Following eligibility screening, 68 patients were ultimately included and randomized. During the study, 2 participants in the acupuncture group and 2 participants in the sham acupuncture group did not receive the assigned intervention due to personal scheduling constraints or relocation and therefore withdrew before treatment initiation. These reasons for withdrawal were recorded and were not related to group allocation or treatment tolerability. Consequently, the remaining 64 participants completed all scheduled treatment sessions and follow-up assessments and were included in the final per-protocol analysis (Fig. 1).

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences observed (Table 2). Both groups of participants were female. The analysis showed that the average duration of illness in the acupuncture group was 13.84 ± 4.87 months, with the mean age being 47.47 ± 9.98 years. The sham acupuncture group had an average duration of disease of 14.03 ± 3.21 months, with a mean age of 45.75 ± 10.48 years. There was no significant difference in the duration of illness and age in both groups (SMD = 0.17 and SMD = -0.05). The study showed that there was no significant difference between the acupuncture group and the sham acupuncture group in terms of BFI (SMD = 0.08), FS-14 (SMD = -0.17), HADS-A (SMD = -0.02), and HADS-D (SMD = 0.01).

Longitudinal changes in BFI scores in both groups

The longitudinal analysis of BFI scores showed no significant between-group differences at baseline or at week 6 (Table 3). From week 8 onwards, however, patients in the acupuncture group reported lower fatigue than those in the sham acupuncture group: at week 8 the mean BFI score was 4.38 ± 2.01 vs. 5.56 ± 1.98 (mean difference − 1.18, 95% CI -2.18 to -0.18, p = 0.021), and at week 10 it was 5.22 ± 1.68 vs. 6.50 ± 2.21 (mean difference − 1.28, 95% CI -2.26 to -0.30, p = 0.011). Confidence intervals that did not cross zero from week 8 onward indicate a sustained, clinically relevant reduction in fatigue in the acupuncture group compared with sham acupuncture (Table 3; Fig. 3 A).

Comparison of compliance and adverse events in both groups

The results demonstrated that both acupuncture and sham acupuncture exhibited good treatment compliance in managing cancer-related fatigue among breast cancer patients. These reactions were managed with heat compresses as recommended, and the ecchymosis resolved spontaneously within a few days. Treatment was continued during this period by avoiding the affected areas. Discussion

Principal findings

This pilot randomized clinical trial evaluated the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of acupuncture for CRF in patients with breast cancer. Acupuncture produced greater and more sustained improvements in fatigue, anxiety, and depressive symptoms than sham acupuncture. Short-term changes at week 6 were modest and broadly similar across groups, but from weeks 8 to 10, the acupuncture group showed a clear, clinically relevant advantage on BFI, FS-14, and HADS scores. In contrast, improvements in the sham group were more modest and less durable. This pattern suggests a delayed, possibly cumulative, effect of repeated acupoint stimulation beyond the non-specific effects captured by sham needling. Treatment adherence was excellent in both groups, and acupuncture was well tolerated, with only mild, self-limiting ecchymosis reported in a small proportion of patients.

CRF is thought to arise from complex interactions among tumour biology, treatment-related toxicities, and host responses, including endocrine dysregulation and chronic low-grade inflammation [35, 36]. Current guidelines emphasize multimodal, non-pharmacological management, such as exercise and psychosocial interventions [37, 38], and acupuncture has been reported to relieve pain, nausea, fatigue, and mood symptoms in cancer populations [39]. Consistent with these data, previous trials in breast cancer have shown that acupuncture can alleviate CRF and related emotional distress [12, 32, 33]. Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that, compared with a rigorous sham acupuncture control, an acupuncture protocol based on guideline-recommended acupoints can yield sustained benefits beyond the treatment period.

Given the pilot nature of this study and its modest sample size, the findings should be regarded as preliminary. The primary purpose is to demonstrate feasibility and inform the design of larger trials. Notably, the sustained and progressively amplified benefits observed in the acupuncture group may result from the specificity of the acupoint prescription. In contrast to non-specific needling, the protocol was designed to address the core Traditional Chinese Medicine pathogenesis of CRF, specifically the deficiency of Qi and Blood. ST36 and CV12 were selected to strengthen the Spleen and Stomach, considered the source of Qi and Blood, while CV4 and KI3 were chosen to tonify Kidney Qi and enhance endogenous energy. This targeted stimulation, together with LI4 and SP6 to regulate emotional distress, likely induced cumulative physiological effects, such as modulation of the HPA axis and cytokines [40, 41], which persisted beyond the immediate treatment period. These mechanisms may account for the specific therapeutic advantage observed over the non-specific effects of sham acupuncture.

Implications of the study

This trial used a true-acupoint sham-acupuncture design. Blunt needles with foam pads were applied at the same acupoints in the sham group to mimic the appearance and ritual of acupuncture and to preserve expectancy, while avoiding skin penetration. In contrast, patients in the acupuncture group received subcutaneous needling at a depth of approximately 25–30 mm, with three repetitions of uniform lifting, insertion, and rotation after deqi. Thus, the acupuncture group experienced substantially greater stimulation intensity than the sham group. Although some studies have suggested that superficial or non-deqi needling may still have therapeutic effects [35], multicenter randomized trials indicate that obtaining deqi is associated with better outcomes [42, 43]. In our study, the combination of proper acupoints, deqi, and repeated needle manipulation, compared with non-penetrating sham at the same points, supports the contribution of specific acupoint stimulation beyond non-specific procedural effects.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that acupuncture can be considered as a non-pharmacological option for managing CRF in breast cancer patients, especially for those with persistent fatigue despite standard supportive care or for patients who prefer to limit additional medication use. Given the pilot nature of this trial, the present results should be interpreted as preliminary and mainly used to inform the design of a larger definitive study. In particular, a future trial should include a larger sample size, compare real acupuncture with both sham acupuncture and no-acupuncture/usual care, and incorporate objective biomarkers (such as inflammatory markers, HPA axis indices, and heart rate variability) alongside patient-reported outcomes. It would also be valuable to test different intervention timings during chemotherapy and survivorship to determine the most appropriate window for treatment. Such a study would allow more accurate estimation of effect sizes, more precise separation of specific and placebo effects, and a better understanding of how applicable the findings are across different settings and patient groups.

Limitations and future directions

This trial has several limitations. Cancer-related fatigue is a subjective symptom, and our primary endpoints were patient-reported questionnaire scores. Such measures are vulnerable to reporting and expectation bias, especially in settings where placebo responses may occur. Future studies should recruit larger samples and pair these scales with objective indicators, such as inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), HPA axis measures (e.g., salivary cortisol), and autonomic indices (e.g., heart rate variability), to provide a more complete picture of treatment effects. Second, because acupuncturists cannot realistically be blinded to group allocation, the study adopted a single-blind design. Participants, outcome assessors, and data analysts were blinded, and a blinding check suggested that masking was generally maintained, but some performance or expectation bias cannot be ruled out. Third, this pilot trial did not include a no-acupuncture or usual-care-only control arm, so we could not directly compare sham acupuncture with the natural course of cancer-related fatigue. Larger confirmatory trials should consider a three-arm design (real acupuncture, sham acupuncture, and no acupuncture/usual care) to better separate placebo effects from specific treatment effects. Fourth, acupuncture was initiated at a fixed point in the treatment course, which prevented us from examining whether efficacy differs across phases of chemotherapy or survivorship. Future studies may vary the timing of intervention (e.g., starting at different treatment or follow-up stages) to clarify the optimal window for acupuncture. Finally, although primary conditions that could independently cause fatigue, including clinically significant thyroid dysfunction, were excluded through eligibility criteria and clinical judgement, milder comorbidities and non-cancer concomitant medications were not systematically documented. This may have left some residual confounding and should be addressed in future work by more detailed recording of comorbid conditions and concomitant drug use.

Conclusion

This pilot randomized clinical trial suggests that, among patients with CRF due to breast cancer, acupuncture can produce greater and more sustained improvements in fatigue, anxiety, and depression than sham acupuncture, with good tolerability and high adherence. These preliminary findings support further evaluation of acupuncture in larger, rigorously designed trials and suggest that it may have a role in comprehensive CRF management in oncology care.
