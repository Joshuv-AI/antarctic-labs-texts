Targeting the outer membrane porin OprF restores carbapenem efficacy during experimental sepsis caused by NDM-1-producing Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: S. Herrera-Espejo, J. Findlay, J. M. Cisneros, María Eugenia Pachón-Ibáñez, P. Nordmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbapenem resistance in Pseudomonas aeruginosa may depend on both carbapenemase production and outer membrane porin composition. Disruption of the major porin OprF altered motility and secretion system gene expression without reducing virulence, and restored imipenem efficacy in an NDM-1–producing strain in a murine sepsis model. To the best of our knowledge, a dual role of inhibition of pathogenicity and overcoming antibiotic resistance by disrupting a membrane protein, is novel.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa a leading cause of nosocomial infections including bloodstream infections (BSI) and sepsis. It is not only highly intrinsic resistant to many antibiotics but also the source of acquired resistance to broad-spectrum antibiotics. Carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa arises from a combination of mechanisms, including reduced outer membrane permeability, overexpression of efflux pumps, and production of carbapenem-hydrolyzing enzymes.1 In particular, metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), such as New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase-1 (NDM-1), confer high-level resistance to most β-lactams, including carbapenems, and are not inhibited by currently available β-lactamase inhibitors.2,3.

Outer membrane porins play a central role in both antimicrobial susceptibility and bacterial fitness and virulence in P. aeruginosa.4 OprD constitutes the main uptake pathway for basic amino acids and imipenem; its downregulation or loss is a well-established mechanism of imipenem resistance.5 In contrast, OprF is the major outer membrane porin and one of the most abundant proteins in the P. aeruginosa envelope.6 Beyond its structural role, OprF has been implicated in maintaining membrane integrity, regulating permeability, modulating virulence, and contributing to bacterial survival under stress conditions7. Recent in vitro evidence from our group has shown that deletion of the oprF gene leads to a compensatory increase in OprD expression, resulting in enhanced imipenem uptake despite the presence of carbapenemases.8 This interaction suggests that porin remodelling may critically influence carbapenem efficacy in metallo-β-lactamase–producing strains. Therefore, by combining in vitro phenotypic and an in vivo efficacy assay, this study sought to evaluate the impact of oprF disruption on imipenem therapy in a murine sepsis model.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

The Pseudomonas aeruginosa reference strains PAO1 and PA14 were used as the parental strains in this study. Isogenic strains, P. aeruginosa PAO1 (ATCC 15692) and PAO1 TnoprF were obtained from the “PAO1 transposon mutant library”,9 with gene oprF being disrupted by a transposon insertion. The TnoprF disruption was confirmed by PCR using primers Check_F (5’- AAAGTTTTCAGATGCGACCG − 3’) and Check_R (5’- GTGGACGGGTACTGCTTCAT − 3’). PA14 and the oprF deleted strain, PA14∆oprF, were previously constructed.10 The previously constructed recombinant plasmid, pUCP24-NDM-1,11 was introduced into PAO1 and PAO1 TnoprF by electroporation, and were included to assess the impact of OprF disruption on bacterial phenotype and antimicrobial efficacy.

All strains were routinely cultured on Luria–Bertani (LB, Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain) agar plates or in Müller–Hinton broth (MHB, Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain) at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. Overnight cultures were prepared from single colonies and adjusted to the required bacterial concentrations for each experiment using fresh culture medium. For in vivo experiments, bacterial suspensions were prepared immediately before inoculation to ensure viability and reproducibility.

In vitro assays

Susceptibility assays

Imipenem and tetracycline were purchased as standard powders (Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of both antimicrobials were determined by the standard microdilution method were determined following European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines,12 with a starting inoculum of 5 × 105 CFU/mL. 96-well plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C.

Motility assays

Surface motility assays were performed to evaluate the effect of oprF disruption. Overnight cultures of each strain were adjusted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Lonza, Verviers, Belgium) to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.6. Three microliters of each bacterial suspension were inoculated onto LB agar plates containing 0.3% agarose to assess surface-associated motility. Plates were incubated at 37 °C under humid conditions, and the diameter of surface expansion was measured after incubation. All experiments were performed in triplicate on at least three independent occasions.

RNA Extractions and RNA Seq analyses

Overnight cultures of strains PA14 and PA14∆oprF were inoculated into fresh LB broth and grown, shaking at 37 °C until the cultures had reached exponential growth phase (OD600 of 0.5–0.6). RNA was extracted using the NZY Total RNA Isolation kit (NZY Tech, Portugal) and RNA was quantified by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA samples were sent to GeneWiz (Azenta Life Sciences, Germany) for rRNA depletion, followed by cDNA library preparation and standard RNA sequencing. Analyses were performed on six biological isolates of each strain. Differential expression was determined with DESeq2 and a cut-off of Log2 fold change was used to determine biologically significant changes in expression.

Adherence and invasion to human cells

Adherence and invasion assays were conducted using human lung carcinoma epithelial cells (A549; ATCC® CCL-185™) to assess whether disruption of oprF altered the interactions between host and pathogen. A549 cells were seeded at a density of 10⁵ cells per well in 24-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Bacterial strains were grown overnight in MHB, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Biowest, Barcelona, Spain) without antibiotics. Monolayers were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 and incubated for 2 h to allow bacterial adherence. Briefly, for adherence assays, cells were washed thoroughly to remove non-adherent bacteria and then lysed to quantify cell-associated bacteria by serial dilution and plating. For invasion assays, after the initial infection period, extracellular bacteria were eliminated by incubating the monolayers with gentamicin. Cells were then washed, lysed, and intracellular bacteria were quantified.13 Results were expressed as percentages relative to bacterial growth controls performed in DMEM without cells. All assays were conducted in triplicate and repeated independently at least three times.

In vitro growth curves and competition indices

The competitive growth of the non-NDM-producing PAO1 P. aeruginosa strains and between the two PAO1 NDM-producers P. aeruginosa strains was assessed in LB, mixing by pairs 5 × 105 CFU/mL each. At different time-points, aliquots from the cultures were seeded on LB agar containing or not tetracycline to differentiate the growth of each strain. A CI = 1 indicates that the fitness is equivalent; If the CI > 1, it indicates that the tetracycline-resistant strain is able to proliferate more than the susceptible strain; if CI < 1, the tetracycline-resistant strain is weaker than the susceptible one.14.

In vivo assays

Protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation of Hospital Universitario Virgen del Rocío (Exp. Animal_1703-N-23) and by the Consejería de Agricultura, Pesca, Agua y Desarrollo Rural (27/11/2024/166), Junta de Andalucía, Spain. All animal procedures complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals15 and ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org/). CD-1 female C57BL/6J (7–9 weeks old, 25–30 g) were obtained from Production and Experimentation Animal Centre, Janvier Labs (Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with water and food ad libitum. Mice were sacrificed using an intraperitoneal (ip) lethal dose of sodium thiopental (B. Braun Medical S.A., Barcelona, Spain).

Determination of the Minimum Lethal Dose (MLD)

A murine peritoneal sepsis model was used to determine the minimum lethal dose (MLD)16 for each P. aeruginosa strain. Female CD-1 mice were inoculated intraperitoneally with escalating bacterial concentrations prepared in sterile saline, starting from an inoculum of 6 log10 CFU/mL and increasing until a dose resulting in 100% mortality was identified for each strain. Animals were monitored closely during the first 12 h post-inoculation and twice a day thereafter.

Efficacy of imipenem monotherapy in the murine sepsis model

To evaluate the impact of oprF disruption on imipenem efficacy, mice were inoculated intraperitoneally with the strain-specific MLD, causing a severe experimental peritoneal sepsis model followed by a secondary bloodstream infection (BSI)17. Treatment was initiated 2 h post-infection and administered for 24 h. Animals were randomly assigned to one of the following groups: (i) infected, untreated controls or (ii) infected mice treated with imipenem at a suboptimal dose of 30 mg/kg/q4h administered intraperitoneally.

Survival was monitored over 24 h. At sacrifice or upon death, blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture for quantitative and qualitative cultures. Spleens were aseptically removed, weighed, homogenized, and plated on blood agar to determine bacterial concentration, expressed as log₁₀ CFU/g of spleen or mL of blood. BSI was defined as the presence of viable bacteria in blood cultures.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as means ± standard deviation. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, including survival and BSI rates. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

In vitro assays

Motility assays

Disruption and deletion of the oprF gene resulted in reduced surface-associated motility compared with the PAO1 reference strains, respectively, resulting in a swarming-deficient phenotype. However, carbapenemase production alone does not impair this phenotype (Fig. 1A-B). Identical results were obtained with PA14 reference strain (data not shown).

RNA Seq analyses

Deletion of oprF was associated with significant upregulation of 20 genes encoding components of the type VI secretion system (T6SS) and concomitant downregulation of 37 genes encoding type III secretion system (T3SS) proteins. Consistently, multiple flagellar genes (e.g., fliC, flgA or flgB) were downregulated, providing a transcriptional basis for the motility defect observed in oprF-deficient strains.

Adherence and invasion to human cells

The ability of the different P. aeruginosa strains to adhere to and invade human lung carcinoma epithelial cells (A549) was assessed with PAO1 strain as the reference control (Fig. 1C-D). Carbapenemase production was associated with reduced epithelial adherence, independently of oprF status. In contrast to adherence, invasion of epithelial cells was largely preserved for all strains, although a decreased trend (p = 0.057) was observed when comparing PAO1 with PAO1 TnoprF + blaNDM−1.

In vitro growth curves and competition indices

In vitro growth and competition assays revealed a reduced growth capacity of the oprF-disrupted strain compared with PAO1 and PAO1 (+ blaNDM−1). Consistently, competition assays demonstrated a reduced competitive index of 1.4 × 10− 4 and 2.3 × 10− 4 for PAO1 + PAO1 TnoprF and PAO1 (+ blaNDM−1) + PAO1 TnoprF (+ blaNDM−1), respectively after 24 h of incubation (Table 1).

In vivo assays

Regarding the determination of the minimum lethal dose (MLD100) yielded comparable concentrations among strains, with values of 8.0, 8.4, 8.2, and 8.0 log10 CFU/mL for PAO1, PAO1 (+ blaNDM−1), PAO1 TnoprF, and PAO1 TnoprF (+ blaNDM−1), respectively.

In mice infected with the imipenem-susceptible PAO1 reference strain, imipenem treatment resulted in a marked reduction in bacterial burden in both spleen and blood compared with untreated controls (− 5.46 log₁₀ CFU/g in spleen and − 6.92 log₁₀ CFU/mL in blood). This effect was accompanied by a significant increase of survival rate (0% mortality vs. 100% in controls) and, a decrease in BSI rates (30% vs. 100%) too (Fig. 2). In contrast, imipenem efficacy was substantially reduced in mice infected with the NDM-1-producing PAO1 strain. Although imipenem treatment significantly decreased bacterial loads compared to untreated mice group (− 2.10 log₁₀ CFU/g in spleen and − 4.58 log₁₀ CFU/mL in blood), BSI and survival rates remained unchanged, indicating therapeutic failure. The disruption of oprF in the isogenic NDM-1 producer strain restored imipenem efficacy to levels comparable to those observed for the PAO1 reference strain. In mice infected with the PAO1 TnoprF + blaNDM−1 strain, imipenem treatment resulted in a pronounced reduction in bacterial concentration in spleen and blood (− 4.91 log₁₀ CFU/g and − 6.51 log₁₀ CFU/mL, respectively), together with a significant decrease in BSI and mortality rates. Under identical treatment conditions, the TnoprF (+ blaNDM−1) strain reached a high bacterial clearance, absence of BSI, and survival outcomes that closely mirrored those observed for the imipenem-treated PAO1 reference strain.

Discussion

Disruption and deletion of oprF resulted in reduced surface-associated motility compared with the PAO1 and PA14 reference strain, while this phenotype is consistent with the essential role of OprF in swarming motility, as previously reported by Bouffartigues et al.,19 who correlated the lower motility in the absence of major porin OprF with a decreased expression of rhlA and rhlB without any alteration on the expression of flagellar genes measured by RT-qPCR. By contrast, RNA sequencing carried out in the present study showed a consistent trend in the downregulation of multiple genes within the flagellar (fli) family, providing a different transcriptional basis for the motility impairment. This defect is likely rooted in the broader role of OprF in maintaining the cell envelope integrity regulation.20 and its established influence on P. aeruginosa pathogenicity.21 However, carbapenemase production alone has no impact in motility, confirming that the loss of oprF, and not the metabolic burden of blaNDM−1, is the primary driver of the motility defect. Additionally, deletion of oprF was associated with significant upregulation T6SS-related genes and the downregulation of T3SS-related genes. This reciprocal regulation of secretion systems is characteristic of a transition from acute to chronic infection states and has previously been linked to repression of flagellar gene expression and motility.7,22.

Regarding the ability of the different P. aeruginosa strains to adhere to and invade A549, carbapenemase production was associated with reduced epithelial adherence, regardless the oprF status, suggesting that carbapenemase expression imposes a metabolic or regulatory burden that downmodulates host-interaction traits.23,24 The absence of statistically significant differences in invasion rates supports the role of OprF as a modulatory adhesin rather than an essential virulence determinant.7.

The reduced growth capacity observed for the oprF-disrupted strain does not translate into attenuated virulence in the murine sepsis model. Thus, the fitness cost revealed by motility, growth curves and competition index did not predict in vivo pathogenic potential.25 This discrepancy between reduced in vitro growth and preserved in vivo virulence is consistent with the conceptual framework proposed by Andersson and Hughes,26 who demonstrated that the fitness cost of mutations is highly dependent on the environmental context in which it is measured.

Regarding the in-vivo efficacy of imipenem, treatment showed robust activity against the PAO1 reference strain, but was ineffective against the NDM-1-producing PAO1, resulting in therapeutic failure. However, oprF disruption in the NDM-1-producing strain restored imipenem efficacy to levels mirroring the susceptible PAO1, leading to significant a decrease in the spleen and blood bacterial concentration and increased survival rates. This phenotypic reversion strongly supports the notion that outer membrane architecture critically shapes the functional expression of carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa.27 Enhanced antibiotic influx through OprD may offset, at least in part, the hydrolytic activity of NDM-1, allowing imipenem to reach bactericidal intracellular concentrations.4 Importantly, although OprF has been linked to increased biofilm formation and immune evasion,7,18 its absence here created a window of therapeutic vulnerability rather than exacerbating treatment failure (Fig. 3).

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that carbapenemase production alone is insufficient to predict therapeutic failure and identify porin balance as a decisive determinant of carbapenem efficacy in vivo. By functionally disconnecting enzymatic resistance from treatment outcome, our results reveal outer membrane remodelling as a previously unrecognized mechanism for restoring antibiotic activity. Whereas OprF has primarily been explored as antivirulence or vaccine target,28,29 our data establish its modulation as a potential strategy to overcome antibiotic resistance in Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
