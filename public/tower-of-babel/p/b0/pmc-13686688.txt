Student Perceptions of 360o (3D) Imaged‐Models Used for Fixed Prosthodontics Simulation Education: Enhancing Visualisation, Understanding and Self‐Assessment
Authors: Manal Ablal, Barry Quinn, Ethan Hearty, Michael Aspden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThis study evaluated and compared the effectiveness of a novel 3D imaged models (3D IMs) and physical reference models in supporting student learning of indirect tooth preparations.Materials and MethodsFourth‐year dental students from 2021 (N = 69) and 2022 (N = 54) received access to 3D IMs and real‐sized plastic models featuring inlays, veneers and other restorations. A 5‐point Likert scale questionnaire assessed the perceived impact of both resources on students' learning. Open‐ended responses were thematically analysed to identify common themes in student perceptions.ResultsMann–Whitney U test indicated clear differences between the two cohorts. The 2022 students rated the 3D IMs more positively for visualisation, understanding and self‐assessment skills (p < 0.001). While both cohorts agreed that physical models aided hands‐on learning, the 2022 cohort gave consistently higher ratings to 3D IMs. Spearman's rank correlations showed meaningful associations between Likert items. Thematic analysis showed mixed perceptions and access challenges in 2021, along with suggestions for improving physical models. In contrast, the 2022 cohort expressed more positive views and recommended enhancements such as annotations, measurement indicators and broader integration of 3D IMs into the curriculum.ConclusionThis study suggests that 3D IMs can support students' visualisation, understanding and self‐assessment of tooth preparations, while physical models remain valuable for hands‐on learners. Adapting teaching tools to accommodate diverse learning preferences and expanding access across the curriculum may further enhance learning outcomes. Further research with larger cohorts and longitudinal follow‐up is needed to confirm these preliminary findings and assess their long‐term educational impact.

Introduction

In the wake of the global pandemic and the rapid transition to online learning, the field of education has undergone a transformative shift [1, 2, 3, 4]. This shift has challenged educators to explore innovative approaches for pedagogical enhancement, particularly in fields requiring hands‐on training, such as dentistry. Within dental education, the communication of complex anatomical and procedural concepts has long posed a challenge, necessitating the adoption of innovative teaching methodologies. Among these, the integration of digital technologies, such as 3D scanning and printing, has emerged as a promising avenue for enhancing student learning [5, 6, 7, 8].

While previous research in dental education has explored the use of 3D‐printed models to aid in teaching fixed prosthodontics [9, 10, 11], these approaches focused on physical replicas rather than interactive digital models. In contrast, this study developed and evaluated the use of novel 3D imaged models (3D IMs); virtual, annotated and fully rotatable resources specifically designed to enhance student understanding, visualisation and self‐assessment of indirect tooth preparations. To our knowledge, few studies have systematically investigated the educational impact of interactive 3D IMs within pre‐clinical fixed prosthodontics training.

One of the fundamental challenges encountered by dental students revolved around the creation of tooth preparations (TPs) that adhere to some gold standard criteria [12]. This often led students to cope with self‐assessment as they struggled to identify subtle errors that could compromise the longevity and fit of indirect restorations [13]. Traditional teaching methods often rely on physical models and faculty feedback, but these approaches may not fully address the diverse learning needs of students. As a result, many students struggle with self‐assessment and identifying subtle errors in their TPs, which can compromise their overall quality [14].

This study aimed to address the gap in understanding the potential of novel 3D imaged models (3D IMs) in pre‐clinical fixed prosthodontics (FPs) teaching and learning. By evaluating student perceptions on 3D IMs, as compared to their experiences with physical models, we explored how these digital models could enhance understanding of tooth preparations and visualisation of specific TP details. Drawing inspiration from observations made during the delivery of the fixed prosthodontics course, where students often struggled with visualising and comprehending TPs, these novel 3D IMs provided valuable insights into the benefits and limitations of integrating digital technologies in FPs, ultimately highlighting the need for more effective teaching resources like 3D IMs. Among their key advantages, 3D IMs can be freely rotated 360 degrees, zoomed in and out and feature clearly highlighted components of the TPs, allowing easy differentiation between prepared and unprepared surfaces. These models are also annotated to explain the different structural elements and the required amount of tooth reduction, adding further educational value. An additional benefit is their accessibility; students can interact with them anytime and anywhere via mobile devices, promoting flexible, self‐directed learning.

Research Question

Do 3D imaged models improve students' perceived visualisation, understanding and self‐assessment of tooth preparations compared with physical models in pre‐clinical fixed prosthodontics training?

Null Hypothesis

There's no difference in students' perceived visualisation, understanding and self‐assessment of tooth preparations compared with physical models in pre‐clinical fixed prosthodontics training.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study used a within‐subjects design to assess dental students' perceptions of 3D IMs compared to physical models in a pre‐clinical FPs course. Both were provided simultaneously, allowing direct comparison of student perceptions within the same learning context. This concurrent approach minimised the potential bias of learning effects associated with sequential exposure. Conducted over two academic years (2021 and 2022) with fourth‐year dental students (N = 123), the study primarily focused on student perceptions while also documenting course outcomes to contextualise the findings.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical approval was granted by the related Ethics Committee (study ID: 10644). Data were collected anonymously to ensure that no participant could be identified. Informed consent was obtained, and participants were provided with Participant Information prior to commencing the study.

Study Participants

Participants were 4th‐year dental students enrolled in the bridge course during the 2021 (N = 69) and 2022 (N = 54) academic years. This pre‐clinical course focuses on teaching students how to prepare teeth for various indirect restoration designs in a simulated setting.

The 2021 cohort started their course following the end of the pandemic in March 2021. These students transitioned back to face‐to‐face teaching and learning, whereas the 2022 cohort entered the program in a post‐pandemic environment.

Selection of Tooth Preparation Designs

Four tooth preparation designs were selected for the study: anterior ceramic veneer, ceramic/composite inlays, ceramic onlay and anterior minimal preparation bridge preparations. These were identified based on students' performance during the practical sessions in the simulation suite. During those sessions, student preparations were assessed by tutors and scored using Liftupp, an online assessment and student development tool. The designs most frequently associated with lower performance scores and greater student difficulty were chosen for inclusion, as they represented areas where additional support was most needed.

Preparation of 3D Imaged Models

Large‐scale stone models of maxillary central incisors, canines, premolars and first molars were prepared to represent the selected tooth preparation designs. These models were scanned using a Smart Optics Vinyl Hi‐Resolution 3D Scanner (accuracy: 5 μm), producing greyscale .stl files. These uncoloured digital models were then processed in Meshmixer (v 3.5, Autodesk Inc.), where colour coding was applied to highlight different preparation features. The final 3D IMs were exported as .obj files to create 360° rotatable images, embedded into PDF files and then uploaded to the student learning management system (Canvas) alongside a colour key and guidance for easy access and interaction (Figure 1).

Creating the Physical Reference Models of the Selected Tooth Preparations

All students were provided with a life‐sized physical model of the selected tooth preparations to be used as a physical reference for learning. The physical models represented traditional teaching methods and served as a baseline for comparison with the 3D IMs.

To achieve this, life‐size plastic teeth (Frasaco) were manually prepared to match the selected tooth preparation designs as chosen for the 3D IMs. The prepared teeth were mounted on Frasaco models and then duplicated using an acrylic material to produce mounted study models. This ensured each student had access to a physical model while practising (Figure 2).

Integration of 3D IMs Into the Course

Students received access to the 3D IMs 1 week before each pre‐clinical session through the school's Canvas platform. A short step‐by‐step guide was provided. During sessions, students could access the 3D IMs on their dental unit screens while practicing the tooth preparations. Physical models were also available for side‐by‐side comparison.

The two cohorts did not experience the same version of the intervention. The 2021 cohort accessed the 3D IMs during their early‐stage rollout under pandemic restrictions, whereas the 2022 cohort used the fully implemented version in a more stable environment. These contextual differences were considered during data analysis and interpretation.

Data Collection

Quantitative Data

A 5‐point Likert scale questionnaire was used to assess students' perceptions of the 3D IMs compared to the physical models. It included questions on the perceived impact of 3D IMs on learning, visualisation and understanding of tooth preparation designs, as well as students' preferences between the two types of models. Open‐ended questions were also included to allow students to provide additional feedback on both resources.

Qualitative Data

Responses to open‐ended questions were analysed using Braun and Clarke's six‐step thematic analysis approach [14]. This included: (1) familiarisation with the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes and (6) producing the final report. Representative quotes were selected to illustrate each key theme.

Data Analysis

Quantitative Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (Version 28). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations and frequency counts, were used to summarise responses to the Likert‐scale questions. As the data were ordinal and not assumed to be normally distributed, non‐parametric tests were applied. A p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To compare responses between the 2021 and 2022 cohorts (independent groups), the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For within‐cohort comparisons of students' perceptions of 3D IMs versus physical models, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was applied. Spearman's rank‐order correlation assessed relationships between Likert‐scale items.

To evaluate the internal consistency of the questionnaire, Cronbach's alpha was calculated, yielding a value of 0.585, indicating moderate reliability. Additionally, Cohen's ds was calculated to assess the effect size of cohort differences for each question. Cohen's ds, which accounts for differences in group sizes, was interpreted using standard thresholds: ds = 0.2 (small), ds = 0.5 (medium) and ds = 0.8 (large).

Qualitative Analysis

Open‐ended responses were analysed using Braun and Clarke six‐step approach thematic analysis [15]. This involved familiarisation with the data, generating initial codes, theme identification, theme review, defining and naming themes and final reporting. Representative quotes were selected to illustrate key themes.

Validation and Reliability

The questionnaire was pilot tested with a small group of students (N = 10) to ensure clarity and relevance. Feedback from this group was used to refine the wording of the questions. Cronbach's alpha (α = 0.585) indicated moderate internal consistency of the Likert‐scale items, which is acceptable given the exploratory nature of the study and the diversity of constructs being measured.

Summary

Overall, students' perceptions of 3D IMs became more positive in the 2022 cohort, reflecting improvements in access, usability and relevance. While earlier responses highlighted usability issues and preference for physical models, the later cohort showed greater appreciation of the digital resources and offered constructive suggestions for ongoing enhancement and curricular integration.

Discussion

This study evaluated students' perceptions of 3D IMs compared to physical models in a pre‐clinical dental setting. The quantitative results revealed that students generally found the 3D IMs beneficial, particularly in visualising preparation designs and enhancing self‐assessment. Notably, perceptions improved in the 2022 cohort following refinements in the models and greater curricular integration. Non‐parametric tests were used for the analysis due to the ordinal nature of Likert scale data, which are not assumed to follow a normal distribution. Therefore, data normalisation was not performed, as Likert responses are best analysed using statistical methods appropriate for ordinal data [15, 16].

Key Findings

Students reported that the 3D IMs supported their visualisation of tooth preparations, particularly among the 2022 cohort [6, 7]. However, statistical testing (Mann‐ Whitney) was only applied to compare responses between the two cohorts; no overall significance testing was performed for visualisation scores. These findings therefore represent observed patterns in the data rather than statistically confirmed improvements. This pattern is consistent with broader research on the educational impact of 3D and digital learning resources in health and science education [17, 18, 19]. Their ability to present intricate details of preparation designs allowed for a more comprehensive understanding of both tooth morphology and procedural requirements. This visual depth appeared to support students in recognising fine anatomical and technical details, strengthening their ability to mentally visualise ideal preparations [20].

Descriptive statistics from the questionnaire responses indicated that students found the 3D IMs moderately to highly effective for visualisation, understanding preparation designs and supporting self‐assessment [21, 22, 23, 24]. While the overall preference leaned towards 3D IMs, the tactile feedback offered by physical models remained valuable to some students. Both year groups agreed that the 3D IMs promoted a deeper understanding of the intricacies involved in tooth preparation, contributing to improved confidence and procedural accuracy [25, 26]. Additionally, the ability to compare personal work against ideal digital models promoted self‐reflection and encouraged independent learning: a key component of developing clinical competence [6, 7].

It is worth noting that a minority of students expressed a preference for physical models, potentially due to the hands‐on learning experience they provide. This finding aligns with prior studies suggesting that individual learning styles and sensory preferences can influence resource effectiveness [27].

Variations Between Cohorts

Because the 2021 and 2022 experienced different contexts and versions of the 3D IMs, they were analysed separately wherever possible and any combined or cross‐cohort comparisons are presented descriptively and interpreted as exploratory rather than inferential tests of effectiveness. The observed differences between the 2021 and 2022 cohorts may be attributed in part to the differing educational contexts shaped by the COVID‐19 pandemic. The 2022 cohort, entering their clinical training after the peak of the pandemic, had more consistent access to in‐person instruction and greater familiarity with digital learning tools. For instance, 90% of the 2022 students reported finding the 3D IMs highly beneficial for visualisation, compared to just 50% of the 2021 cohort. Similarly, 80% of the 2022 students indicated that the 3D IMs positively supported their self‐assessment abilities, compared to 55% in the earlier cohort.

In contrast, the 2021 students began their training during a period of abrupt shifts to remote learning, limited face‐to‐face teaching and broader uncertainty. These disruptions likely affected their ability to fully engage with and benefit from digital resources such as the 3D IMs. It is also possible that lower engagement by the 2021 cohort reflected the early stage of the 3D IMs rollout rather than an inherent limitation of the resource itself. At that time, the models had not yet undergone full testing and refinement, and some students reported technical or access issues. These factors, combined with the sudden shift to remote learning, may have limited their ability to benefit fully from the digital resources. This interpretation is supported by studies showing that students learning during the early stages of the pandemic reported greater difficulty adapting to online formats and experienced reduced engagement and satisfaction compared to later cohorts [28, 29]. This may help explain the stronger preference for physical models among 2021 students, with 19% indicating that physical models enhanced their understanding, compared to just 7% of the 2022 students. Because the two cohorts experienced different teaching contexts and versions of the 3D IMs, any combined or averaged results across cohorts should be regarded as descriptive only. Such aggregation may obscure cohort‐specific differences and is not intended as a definitive measure of the intervention's effectiveness. As this analysis combined responses from two cohorts who experienced different contexts and versions of the interventions, the finding should be viewed as exploratory and may have limited generalisability beyond the study setting.

These findings underscore the importance of designing flexible and inclusive dental education strategies that accommodate diverse learning preferences and account for contextual challenges. While 3D IMs offer clear advantages for visualisation and conceptual development, physical models remain essential; especially for learners who benefit from hands‐on, tactile engagement. Future research should explore integrated approaches that maximise the strengths of both modalities.

Recurring Themes

Thematic analysis of the open‐ended responses revealed several consistent themes across cohorts. Students in the 2021 cohort expressed a range of perspectives on the usefulness of 3D IMs. While some appreciated the visual detail these tools offered, others suggested enhancements, such as improved interactivity (e.g., full rotation or zooming capabilities). Although the 3D IMs were designed to be fully rotatable and zoomable, during the initial 2021 rollout these functions, while available, were sometimes sluggish or less intuitive on certain devices. By the 2022 cohort, the models had been migrated to Sketchfab, providing smoother rotation and zoom, which may explain the reduced number of such comments. A portion of this cohort also voiced a continued preference for physical models, emphasising the value of tactile learning experiences. These observations reflect findings from educational psychology literature that advocate for teaching strategies tailored to diverse cognitive and sensory learning styles [30, 31].

In contrast, the 2022 cohort generally responded more positively to the 3D IMs, citing their ease of access and visual clarity. Students in this group suggested further expansion of the model library; for example, the inclusion of lower molar or more complex preparation types. These responses support the view that digital 3D tools can play a significant role in fostering spatial reasoning and deeper conceptual understanding in clinical education [32].

Implications for Dental Education

The findings of this study emphasise the importance of flexibility and inclusivity in curriculum design. The mixed responses from the 2021 cohort suggest that a one‐size‐fits‐all approach may not effectively support all learners. For some, physical models remain an essential component of their learning process. As such, providing both digital and physical learning modalities is critical to accommodating varying student preferences and abilities [33].

However, the 2022 cohort demonstrated greater receptiveness to the 3D IMs highlights their potential as a core component of preclinical dental training. By offering detailed, accessible, and visually rich representations of ideal preparations, 3D IMs can support independent learning, enhance spatial understanding and promote reflective practice. These findings align with existing research demonstrating the pedagogical value of digital 3D resources in improving both cognitive and technical skills in dental education [34].

Actions Taken to Enhance the Educational Experience

Following students' feedback, several actions have already been implemented to improve the teaching and learning experience. The 3D IMs were migrated to Sketchfab, an online platform that enables interactive viewing of 3D models directly within a web browser. Sketchfab was selected for its user‐friendly interface, compatibility across devices and ability to add detailed annotations to different coloured sections of the models to support visual learning (Figure 5). This allowed students to intuitively rotate, zoom and explore the models anytime and anywhere. Interactive annotations were embedded within each model to highlight and explain clinically relevant preparation features and to link visual cues to clinical decision making (Figure 5). In addition, both the physical and 3D IMs libraries were expanded to include a broader range of bridge designs and tooth preparations. This was done to better represent the diversity of clinical cases and to give students more comprehensive exposure to different types of fixed prosthodontic procedures.

Limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the integration of 3D IMs in pre‐clinical fixed prosthodontics teaching; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. The modest number of open‐ended responses provided helpful insights but highlighted the need for a larger sample size to gather a broader range of student perspectives. The survey showed moderate internal consistency (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.585), underscoring the importance of refining the survey tool for future studies. Additionally, thematic analysis was conducted by a single investigator, which may introduce subjectivity; emphasising the need for multiple reviewers in future research. Although Cohen's d was used to assess differences between cohorts, effect sizes should be interpreted in the context of the exploratory nature of the study. Finally, two of the open‐ended questions were similar in scope and all were positively framed, which may have encouraged constructive suggestions over criticisms and limited the expression of negative views. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Future Plans

To address these limitations and further enhance students' learning experience, the following steps are planned. The survey tool will be reviewed and piloted to improve internal consistency and ensure alignment with the constructs being measured, thereby enhancing the reliability of future surveys. Qualitative data collection will be expanded to include a broader range of student perspectives, enriching the analysis and future thematic analysis will involve multiple examiners to reduce bias and increase credibility. Effect sizes will be more clearly contextualised in future studies to emphasise their exploratory nature and the practical significance of the findings in dental education, including fixed prosthodontics. Finally, the open‐ended questions will be redesigned to include neutral prompts as well as opportunities to share criticism, reducing positive framing bias and ensuring that both favourable and unfavourable student perceptions are recorded more fully.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the students' perception of 3D IMs alongside physical models in pre‐clinical fixed prosthodontics. The findings show that, when fully implemented and supported within the curriculum, 3D IMs can assist students in visualising tooth preparation designs, understanding key features and developing self‐assessment skills, while physical models continue to provide valuable tactile learning experiences. Differences between the 2021 and 2022 cohorts reflect both pandemic‐related disruption and the stages rollout on the 3D IMs; therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of a definitive effect.

Taken together, the study highlights the value of providing students with complementary digital and physical learning resources tailored to diverse preferences and learning contexts. Future work should assess the long‐term impact of 3D IMs on knowledge retention, confidence, and clinical performance and explore optimal ways to integrate these tools across different stages of dental education.

By combining innovative resources with student feedback, dental schools can build more inclusive, flexible and effective pre‐clinical training environments that better prepare learners for the complexity of dental practice.
