HAND1 controls the lineage bifurcation of trophoblast and amnion from human pluripotent stem cells
Authors: Changmiao Pang, Qifeng Yang, Yulong Zhong, Jinhao Ye, Yufang Lv, Shufei Xie, Yanqing Tang, Xianhua Ye, Feifan Zhang, Chao Li, Jingyi Zhang, Liangzhong Sun, Shanshan Ai, Xuefei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrophoblast and amniotic lineages, representing key extra-embryonic tissues, can be differentiated from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) under chemically defined conditions. However, the regulatory mechanisms coordinating the fate decision between these lineages during PSC differentiation remain incompletely understood. Leveraging CRISPR/Cas9-mediated loss-of-function screening in lineage-reporter PSCs, we identified the transcription factor HAND1 as a critical determinant controlling the bifurcation of trophoblast and amniotic lineages. Genetic ablation of HAND1 effectively abrogated the amniotic differentiation capacity of PSCs while concomitantly enhancing their trophoblast differentiation potential. Conversely, ectopic HAND1 overexpression impaired trophoblast differentiation. Notably, forced HAND1 expression in human trophoblast stem cells (TSCs) induced transcriptional reprogramming toward an amniotic fate, indicating its lineage-instructive capability. Mechanistic analyses demonstrated that HAND1 interacts with the TCFs and Wnt signaling effectors β-catenin to form a transcriptional complex that antagonistically modulates the balance between trophoblast- and amnion-associated gene regulatory networks. Collectively, our findings establish HAND1 as a master regulator orchestrating the amniotic versus trophoblast lineage choice during human PSC differentiation, thereby illuminating fundamental regulatory mechanism underlying extra-embryonic lineage specification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06120-5.Keywords: Lineage specification, Reporter cell lines, CRISPR screening, Trophoblast development, Transcriptional complex

Introduction

Successful embryonic development and pregnancy maintenance rely critically on the coordinated functions of extraembryonic tissues, such as the placenta and amnion. These tissues deliver indispensable structural support, nutrient transport, and immunomodulatory protection throughout gestation [1, 2]. Trophoblasts, the principal placental cell type, originate from the trophectoderm of the early blastocyst, while amniotic epithelium arises via epiblast disc delamination at later blastocyst stages [3, 4]. Post-implantation, trophectoderm undergoes differentiation into mononuclear cytotrophoblasts (CTs), which give rise to multinucleated syncytiotrophoblasts (STBs) and invasive extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs). STBs form the maternal–fetal interface essential for gas/nutrient exchange and endocrine signaling, alongside invasive EVTs that remodel uterine spiral arteries and anchor the placenta through decidual stroma infiltration [5–7]. The amnion, a thin membrane enveloping the fetus and placental interior, facilitates water/solute transport, secretes bioactive factors, and shields the embryo from external stressors [8]. Despite their pivotal roles, the molecular mechanisms controlling human amniotic niche formation and trophoblast specification remain poorly understood, hindered by tissue scarcity and ethical constraints.

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), derived from pre-implantation embryos or somatic cell reprogramming, offer a platform to bypass these limitations. By modeling extraembryonic differentiation in vitro, hPSCs enable scalable, ethical investigation of lineage specification, gene regulatory networks, and inter-lineage crosstalk [9]. Recent advances in hPSC-derived embryo-like structures and directed differentiation protocols to certain lineages further highlight their utility in deciphering human embryogenesis [10, 11].

Although existing protocols induce trophoblast- and amnion-like cells from distinct hPSC types, overlapping signaling pathways—notably BMP4, TGFβ, and Wnt—are commonly manipulated, possible the reason of yielding cells co-expressing markers of both lineages [12–15]. For instance, the trophoblast-like cells derived from BAP (BMP4/A83-01/PD173074)-treated PSCs exhibit transcriptional profile closely resemble amniotic cells, with elevated expression of amniotic markers (e.g., HEY1, VIM) [5]. In addition, our prior work demonstrated that expanded-potential PSCs (EPSCs) cultured under defined conditions are able to generate trophoblast-like cells [16]. Subsequent analysis showed that these trophoblast-like cells also expressed the amnion genes [17]. This phenotypic overlap underscores unresolved questions regarding lineage divergence from the differentiating PSCs. Given the central role of transcription factors (TFs) in cell fate determination and the shared expression of regulators like GATA3 and CDX2 in trophoblast and amnion [13], we propose that key TFs controls the divergence of the trophoblast and amnion lineages from PSCs.

To address this, we engineered lineage-specific reporter hPSCs and performed a targeted CRISPR/Cas9 screen, integrated with functional genetics and multi-omics analyses. This approach identified HAND1 as a master regulator of lineage bifurcation from PSCs, unveiling mechanistic insights into its role in orchestrating trophoblast versus amnion commitment from PSCs.

Results

HAND1 diverges amnion and trophoblast lineage from hEPSCs

We previously established human EPSCs (hEPSC) and demonstrated their capacity to generate trophoblast-like cells upon directed differentiation [16]. Independent study indicates that those trophoblast-like cells concomitantly express amnion-associated genes [17]. To elucidate the lineage identities of these differentiated cells, we performed transcriptomic profiling (RNA-seq) on 8-day differentiated hEPSCs in trophoblast stem cell (TSC) medium (Fig. 1 A and B). Our analysis revealed co-expression of canonical markers for both trophoblast and amnion lineages, such as the shared regulator GATA3, the trophoblast-specific factor GATA2, and the amnion-specific marker GABRP (Fig. 1B and C). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the presence of key trophoblast markers, including TFAP2C, SPINT1, and GATA2, at the protein level (Fig. 1D). These findings imply that hEPSCs possess bipotent differentiation capacity, enabling commitment to both amnion and trophoblast lineages. Although these extra-embryonic lineages originate from distinct embryonic precursors, trophoblast and amnion share the expression of numerous transcription factors. Our analysis based on the published data [18] revealed that 83.7% (1,280/1,529) of these factors were highly expressed in both lineages, compared to 5.8% (90/1,529) specific to trophoblast and 10.3% (159/1,529) to amnion (Fig. 1E). Given the pivotal role of TFs in lineage specification [19–21], we aimed to identify key TFs that functionally segregate these lineages during PSC differentiation through transcriptional regulation.

To monitor lineage-specific transcriptional programs during hEPSC differentiation, we engineered a GABRPGFP reporter hEPSC line expressing nuclear-localized GFP driven by the amnion-specific marker GABRP locus [13, 22] (Fig. S1A-S1C). The 3HA-IRES-H2B-GFP element was knocked in the reading frame of GABRP and the nucleus-localized H2B-GFP was linked to the same transcript by internal ribosome entry sites (IRES) sequence (Fig. S1A-S1C). The comparable expression levels of pluripotency genes between the reporter EPSCs and parental line (Fig. S1D-S1F) suggests the genetic engineering did not alter the pluripotency of the EPSCs. Therefore, GABRPGFP reporter hEPSCs were differentiated in TSC medium. At day 8 post-induction, 94.0% of cells exhibited activation of the GFP fluorescence, correlating with significant upregulation of endogenous GABRP transcripts (Fig. 1F, G and S1G). The differentiated cells were passaged as single cells and maintained in TSC medium to establish stable TSC lines (Fig. 1H). Notably, the stable TSC lines derived from these reporter hEPSCs lacked GABRP-GFP signals and amnion gene expression (Fig. 1I and 1 J), confirming absence of GABRP expression and reporter fidelity to trace lineage-specific gene activation. To determine the cell fate of 8-day differentiated GABRPGFP+ cells, we sorted out the cells and cultured them for TSC establishment test. We found that the GABRPGFP+ cells given rise to stable TSC colonies, whereas the GABRPGFP− population did not (Fig. S1H and S1I). It is possible that the GABRPGFP+ cells which co-express marker genes for both trophoblast and amnion, represent a bipotent and plastic state. The downregulation of GABRP and other amnion genes during TSC establishment likely reflects a progressive commitment to the trophoblast lineage under the selective pressure of the TSC culture media. To identify TFs driving lineage divergence from hEPSCs, we reanalyzed transcriptomes from human amnion membranes and placental trophoblasts [14, 18, 23]. Application of a dual-threshold selection strategy (top 40% amnion-high expression; bottom 40% trophoblast-low expression) identified 20 candidate TFs for further investigation (Fig. 1K). A reverse analysis (bottom 40% amnion-low expression; top 40% trophoblast-high expression) identified known trophoblast-specific TFs, including GATA2, GCM1, and SP6 (Fig. S1J), validating the reliance of our screening method.

Thus, we performed a targeted CRISPR/Cas9-mediated loss-of-function screen. Two distinct gRNAs per candidate TF were designed and co-transfected with a Cas9-expressing plasmid into GABRPGFP reporter EPSCs. Following transfection, cells were selected with puromycin and blasticidin without single-colony isolation, then differentiated in TSC media for fluorescence monitoring (Fig. 1L). Remarkably, we found that targeting HAND1 via gRNA/Cas9 profoundly reduced the GABRPGFP+ population. The residual GABRPGFP+ population (Fig. 1M) likely arises from cells that escaped complete knockout due to non-functional editing events, such as monoallelic indels or in-frame mutations, which allow the retention of functional HAND1 protein. This phenotype correlated with significant downregulation of amnion-specification markers (GABRP, IGFBP7) and concomitant upregulation of trophoblast-lineage genes (GATA2, SP6) (Fig. 1N). In contrast, targeting other TFs showed no or negligible phenotypic effects (Fig. S1K and S1L). These results establish HAND1 as a potential master regulator that orchestrates amniotic lineage commitment while simultaneously repressing trophoblast differentiation during hEPSC differentiation.

HAND1 traces amniotic transcription program during hPSC differentiation

HAND1, a TF critical for murine placental development, is highly expressed in mouse TSCs and is required for their commitment to the trophoblast giant cell lineage [24]. HAND1 is also expressed in trophectoderm of human pre-implantation embryos [25]. Nevertheless, this TF is not expressed in human placenta [6]. Consistently, we observed no detectable HAND1 expression in first-trimester human placental trophoblast subtypes, including SPINT1+ cytotrophoblasts (CTs), HLA-G+ extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs), and SDC1+ syncytiotrophoblasts (STBs) (Fig. 2A). To further validate this observation, we reanalyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets of first-trimester placental tissues [26] and confirmed the absence of HAND1 transcripts in trophoblast clusters corresponding to CTs, EVTs, and STBs (Fig. S2A). This contrasts with certain studies proposing a functional role for HAND1 in human trophoblast [27, 28]. Of note, HAND1 was instead robustly expressed in the human amnion membrane (Fig. 2A), which is consistent to the high expression levels revealed by RNA-Seq reanalyzing (Fig. 1E) and aligning with its beneficial role in amnion lineage genes activation during EPSC differentiation observed above. Collectively, these data suggest that HAND1 is an amnion-specific TF within human extraembryonic tissues. To investigate the dynamics of HAND1 expression during TSC media-induced EPSCs differentiation, we generated GATA3mCherry.HAND1GFP dual-reporter EPSC line. This was achieved via knocking the 3HA-IRES-H2B-GFP cassette into HAND1 locus of GATA3mCherry hEPSCs [29] (Fig. S2B-S2G). When differentiated under TSC culture conditions (Fig. 2B), the dual-reporter cell population showed an increase in HAND1GFP+.GATA3mCherry+ cells from 14.2% at day 4 to 93.6% at day 10 (Fig. 2C and D). Interestingly, HAND1 expression was progressively silenced during subsequent passages and became undetectable once the TSCs are established (Fig. 2C-2F and S2H). The TSCs derived from the sorted 10-day HAND1GFP⁺ cells were also GFP negative and expressed key trophoblast markers (GATA2, SP6, MSX2), but lacked expression of amniotic markers (GABRP, VTCN1, VIM) (Fig. S2I and S2J). Consistently, placenta-derived TSCs and their STB/EVT derivatives also lacked detectable HAND1 expression (Fig. 2G). These results demonstrate that HAND1 is restricted to the human amniotic lineage and is transcriptionally inactive in human trophoblasts. To elucidate the expression profile of HAND1 during early human embryogenesis, particularly at the onset of trophoblast and amnion development, we reanalyzed a published single-cell RNA-sequencing dataset from a human PSC-derived embryoid model [30, 31]. Our analysis shows that HAND1 is expressed throughout the predicted amnion cluster (GABRP+, VTCN1+) (Fig. S2K and S2L). Notably, it is also expressed in a subset of the trophoblast cluster (GATA2+, MSX2+) (Fig. S2K and S2L), indicating that PSC-derived extraembryonic lineages are not fully distinct at this early stage of differentiation.

HAND1 loss promotes trophoblast differentiation but inhibits amnion genes expression

To investigate the role of HAND1 in the differentiation of EPSCs, we generated a HAND1-deficient EPSC line (HAND1 KO) by CRISPR/Cas9 in GABRPGFP reporter EPSCs (Fig. 3A and S3A- S3E). HAND1 KO and control EPSCs were then cultured in TSC media for induction. After 8 days of differentiation, only 0.09% of cells were GABRPGFP+ in HAND1 KO cell lines, compared with 98.2% in control cells (Fig. 3B and C). qRT-PCR revealed a remarkable downregulation of amnion-associated markers (GABRP, IGFBP7, HEY1) in HAND1 KO cells (Fig. 3D). Conversely, trophoblast lineage markers were significantly upregulated, including CT markers (GATA2, SP6, TFAP2C), STB markers (GCM1, CGA), and EVT markers (ITGA1, HLA-G) (Fig. 3E and F), indicating trophoblast-prone differentiation of HAND1 KO cells. Consistently, HAND1-depleted cells formed significantly more typical TSC colonies than controls (Fig. 3G and H). Similar phenotypes were observed in an independent EPSC line (Fig. S3F). Given that human PSCs cultured in primed and naïve conditions are also capable of differentiating into extraembryonic lineages under certain differentiation methods [5, 11, 12, 17, 32–39], we examined whether the function of HNAD1 is applied to the PSCs cultured under different conditions. Our data showed that HAND1 KO cells cultured in primed or naïve conditions exhibited similar downregulations in GABRP expression (Fig. S3G-S3J). The data suggest that the role HAND1 plays in amnion-toward differentiation is less dependent of PSC cell line or culture condition variation. To determine whether the observed phenotypes were specifically due to HAND1 loss, we engineered a rescue cell line in which HAND1 expression could be conditionally restored. A doxycycline (Dox)-inducible HAND1-V5 expression cassette was targeted to the ROSA26 locus [29] in HAND1 KO PSCs, generating the HAND1 KOiDox−HAND1 line (Fig. S3K-S3M). Upon doxycycline-induced HAND1 expression during differentiation, the expression of lineage markers, including GABRP, IGFBP3, GATA2, and SP6, was restored (Fig. S3N). These findings indicate that HAND1 deletion biases hPSC differentiation toward trophoblast lineages and facilitates TSC establishment.

Global transcriptomic alterations following HAND1 deletion were assessed via RNA-seq. Comparative analysis between control and HAND1 KO cells revealed 1,619 significantly upregulated and 1,707 significantly downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 3I). Notably, the upregulated genes included multiple placenta-specific genes (SIGLEC6, PTGES) and STB markers (CGA, CYP19A1) (Fig. 3I and J). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated that upregulated DEGs were associated with placental development (Fig. 3K), indicating a transcriptional repressor role for HAND1 in trophoblast gene regulation. Furthermore, enrichment also included hypoxia response, calcium ion transmembrane transport, hormone level regulation, and type I interferon response, which related to core trophoblast functions [40–43]. These data suggest HAND1 ablation promotes mature trophoblast differentiation. Conversely, amnion development-associated genes (GABRP, VTCN1) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 3I and J). Downregulated DEGs were enriched for collagen-dominated extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, required for amnion integrity [8], and for regulation of BMP signaling pathways, which are critical for amnion specification [13, 39] (Fig. 3L). These findings imply HAND1 as a positive regulator of amnion development. Of note, we identified a number of downregulated genes in HAND1 KO cells associated with cardiac developmental processes, including MSX1, HEY2 and SNAI2 (Fig. 3J). The expression of these cardiac development-related genes align with previous reports that HAND1 is a master regulator of cardiac development [44, 45].

Collectively, these results demonstrate the dual regulatory function of HAND1 during hPSC differentiation: it represses trophoblast differentiation while promoting transcriptional programs essential for amnion development.

Overexpressing of HAND1 blocks trophoblast generation from PSCs

As depletion of HAND1 leads to depression of amnion genes and upregulation of trophoblast genes, we reasoned that forced expression of HAND1 would favorite activation of amniotic spectrum-related genes while block trophoblast cell differentiation. To test this speculation, we targeted a Dox-inducible HAND1-V5 expression cassette to the ROSA26 locus in wild-type EPSCs, thereby generating a HAND1-inducible line (HAND1iDox) (Fig. 4A and S4A). Immunofluorescence staining and western blot showed that Dox treatment resulted in HAND1 expression in almost every cell (Fig. 4B and S4B). Thus, these cells were subjected to differentiation in TSC media with or without Dox induction. After a 10-day differentiation, we observed the Dox-treated cells exhibited the substantial attenuations in trophoblast genes expression (Fig. 4C) and ability to form TSC colony (Fig. S4C and S4D) compared to control cells, accompanied with the dramatic increase in GABRP and other amniotic membrane genes expressions (Fig. 4C). To investigate whether HAND1 blocked TSC differentiation is reversible, HAND1iDox cells were differentiated for 8 days in the presence of Dox to induce HAND1 and allowed to further culture for another 6 days without Dox (Fig. S4E). Following Dox withdrawal, trophoblast markers were significantly upregulated (Fig. S4E and S4F). This indicates that the repressive effect of transient HAND1 expression in differentiating EPSCs is largely reversible.

We further investigated the effects of HAND1 overexpression in TSC that isolated from first-trimester human placenta. Interestingly, PiggyBac-based ectopic HAND1 expression altered TSC morphology, shifting from typical colonies with clear boundaries to an elongated, spindle-shaped appearance (Fig. 4D and S4G). This was accompanied by significant downregulation of TSC markers (e.g., GATA2, SP6, TFAP2C) and upregulation of amnion markers (e.g., GABRP, IGFBP3) (Fig. 4E-4G). To test whether the effect of HAND1 on placental TSCs is also reversible, we introduced a PiggyBac-based, Dox-inducible HAND1 cassette into placental TSCs (Fig. S4H). We induced HAND1 expression with 8 days of Dox treatment, followed by 6 days of Dox withdrawal (Fig. S4H). qPCR analysis revealed that TSC marker expression in the withdrawal group remained unchanged (Fig. S4H and S4I), indicating that HAND1-induced reprogramming in mature TSCs is irreversible. In addition, TSCs overexpressing HAND1 exhibited reduced trophoblast organoid formation efficiency and diminished organoid diameter (Fig. 4H and 4I), indicating the loss in self-renewal capacity. We used antibody against KI67, a proliferation marker, to stain these TSCs. Consistent with the reduced proliferation, HAND1-overexpressing TSCs exhibited the remarkable reduction in KI67 presentation (Fig. 4 J and K). Furthermore, TSCs with ectopic HAND1 expression cells reduced their differentiation potential towards STBs and EVTs, as evident by the significant reduction in both STB- and EVT-specific genes expression compared to the control cells (Fig. 4L-O). These findings demonstrate that ectopic HAND1 expression impairs core TSC properties by compromising both proliferative capacity and differentiation potential, and concurrently activates an amnion lineage-specific transcriptional program in committed TSC populations.

HAND1 directly binds amnion- and trophoblast-related genes

The previous results showed that HAND1 was able to repress the expression of trophoblast-related genes and activate the expression of amniotic spectrum-related genes. To identify which DEGs resulting from HAND1 manipulation are direct targets of HAND1, we performed ChIP-seq in differentiated EPSCs using an antibody against the HA tag fused to the C-terminus of the endogenous HAND1 protein. We uncovered 96,727 HAND1 binding sites, of which nearly 24.72% were located in promoter, 33.72% in intronic regions, and 31.83% in distal intergenic areas (Fig. 5A-B). When compared to a random genomic background, HAND1 binding in promoter regions was significantly enriched (Fig. S5A), suggesting the direct regulating role of HAND1 in gene expression. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that genes with high-affinity HAND1 binding were significantly enriched among those downregulated in HAND1 KO cells (NES = −1.334), indicating HAND1 directly promotes their transcription (Fig. 5C). This finding is consistent with the substantial overlap between HAND1 binding sites and DEGs, where 72.5% of downregulated and 62.3% of upregulated genes were associated with HAND1 binding events within 2 kb around transcription start sites (TSSs) (Fig. 5D). These DEGs include placenta and amnion markers GATA2, MSX2, GABRP and VTCN1 (Fig. 5E).

To define the direct transcriptional targets of HAND1, we employed HAND1 KOiDox−HAND1 cells described above (Fig. S3K). This design was critical to eliminate the potential confounding influence of endogenous HAND1, which is expressed during early differentiation stage (Fig. S5B). Thus, the differentiated cells were collected for RNA-seq analysis at 24-, 48- and 72-h following administration of Dox in medium (Fig. 5F). Early transcriptional changes (24 h) included the upregulation of amnion-associated markers GABRP, IGFBP7, and the genes implicated in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization COL5A2 and COL6A6 (Fig. S5C-S5D). This underscores HAND1's pivotal role in initiating the collagen-rich ECM foundation characteristic of amnion. Enrichment analysis at the 48-h time point indicated the onset of TGFβ and BMP signaling pathways (Fig. S5E), which is related to amnion differentiation. By 72 h, pathways involved in transcriptional activation were significantly enriched, along with the persistent TGFβ and BMP signaling (Fig. 5G and 5H). Meanwhile, HAND1 induction triggered significant and sustained downregulation of ion transport that essential for trophoblast function (Fig. 5H, I, S5F and S5G). This comprehensive suppression targeted trophoblast physiological processes, including nutrient exchange, fluid homeostasis, and syncytiotrophoblast secretion [46] (Fig. 5H-5I, S5F-S5G). In addition, the set of genes repressed at 48 h are also associated with "response to retinoic acid" (Fig. S5G), a regulator of trophoblast differentiation [4, 47, 48], indicating an inhibition of the trophoblast differentiation. By 72 h, downregulation of "response to hormone" further signify the attenuated trophoblast endocrine potential (Fig. 5H and I).

Collectively, the temporal GO enrichment profiles demonstrate that HAND1 orchestrates the amnion-trophoblast lineage bifurcation from PSCs by activating amnion-specific programs while repressing the transcriptional network essential for trophoblast differentiation and function.

HAND1 regulates gene expression through TCF-β-catenin complex

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which HAND1 regulates the target genes, we subjected the 8-day differentiated cells to proteomic analysis using immunoprecipitation combined with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. This analysis revealed specific interactions between HAND1 and the transcription factors TCF3, TCF4, and TCF12 (Fig. 6A), which was further validated by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays (Fig. 6B and Fig. S6A). Protein–protein docking shows that direct binding between HAND1 and TCF family members (TCF3, TCF4, TCF12) is structurally feasible (Fig. S6B). Consistently, Homer motif analysis of HAND1 ChIP-seq data revealed enriched motifs for TCFs and key trophoblast regulators, including TFAP2C, GATA2, and TEAD4, suggesting HAND1 collaborates with these factors in a coordinated regulatory network (Fig. 6C).

These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that HAND1 regulates gene expression through cooperative interactions with TCFs[25]. To functionally assess whether HAND1 cooperates with TCFs in transcriptional regulation of amnion and trophoblast genes, we engineered luciferase reporter constructs driven by the GABRP promoter and MSX2 enhancer (Fig. 6D).

Luciferase assays showed that HAND1 cooperated synergistically with all three TCFs to enhance GABRP promoter activity (Fig. 6E). In contrast, HAND1 synergistically suppressed MSX2 enhancer activity only in conjunction with TCF3 (Fig. 6F). Of note, HAND1 acts synergistically with all TCFs to activate the GABRP promoter, whereas its cooperative repression of the MSX2 enhancer with TCF3 is weaker. No other TCFs showed synergistic repression with HAND1 at this enhancer. These findings indicate that HAND1 utilizes distinct TCFs as partners to bidirectionally regulate lineage-specific genes. This differential dependence on TCF partners for activation versus repression may be due to additional factors, such as unknown co-factors, the local epigenetic landscape, or locus-specific DNA binding affinity. The cooperative regulation by HAND1 and TCFs was further assessed on two additional lineage-specific genes, ZNF564 (amnion) and GATA2 (trophoblast) (Fig. 1K and S1J), both of which are directly bound by HAND1 at their promoters (Fig. 5E, S6C and S6D).

The effector of canonical Wnt signaling β-catenin regulates lineage genes expression during lineage development via interacting with TFs [49–51]. Co-IP experiments revealed the physical interaction between HAND1 and β-catenin (Fig. 6G). Structural modeling supports the feasibility of direct binding among HAND1, TCFs and β-catenin (Fig. S6B and S6E). Therefore, we speculated that HAND1 might exerts its regulatory effects through the β-catenin transcriptional machinery. To test this, we treated cells with ICG-001, a pharmacological inhibitor of β-catenin-dependent transcription (Fig. 6H-I). ICG-001 significantly attenuated the HAND1-TCF3-mediated upregulation of GABRP and ZNF564, and the downregulation of MSX2 and GATA2 in luciferase reporter assays (Fig. 6J and K). To determine if disrupting the ability of β-catenin in gene expression regulation affects amnion gene expression, we treated the differentiating EPSCs with either ICG-001 or DMSO control for 8 days. qRT-PCR analysis showed a reduction in the expression of key amnion genes upon ICG-001 treatment (Fig. S6F and S6G). This result suggests that inhibiting the β-catenin functionally impairs the amnion gene expressions in differentiation. These findings demonstrate that HAND1 bidirectionally coordinates lineage-specific gene regulation by modulating the transcriptional output of the β-catenin.

Discussion

Placental trophoblasts and amniotic cells, originating from distinct blastocyst lineages, are indispensable for sustaining human pregnancy. However, ethical constraints and limited access to early-gestation tissues hinder direct investigation of their developmental mechanisms. Therefore, directed differentiation of hPSC serves as a pivotal strategy to generate trophoblast- and amnion-like cells for studying lineage specification and dysfunction. Despite shared transcriptional regulators, the molecular drivers of lineage bifurcation during hPSC differentiation remain unresolved. Here, through a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated screen and multiomics profiling, we identify HAND1 as a transcription factor governing the bifurcation of trophoblast and amniotic lineages from hPSCs.

HAND1, a class B bHLH protein, heterodimerizes with partners like E-proteins to modulate target gene expression via E-box motifs [25]. Murine studies underscore its critical role in placental development, particularly trophoblast giant cell differentiation [24]. In humans, HAND1 is expressed in trophectoderm and choriocarcinoma cells (e.g., BeWo, JEG3) [25, 52] but absent in normal placental trophoblasts including CTBs, STBs and EVTs [6]. Therefore, utilizing choriocarcinoma cell line as a model to study regulatory network of placental trophoblast may have limitations, though there are studies showing that manipulation of HAND altered the trophoblast genes expression or cholesterol metabolism in human choriocarcinoma cell line [27, 28]. It is well-known that there are profound structural and signaling differences between human and mouse placentae [53, 54]. One of the key player in tissue morphogenesis and signal transduction is "extracellular matrix" (ECM) [55–57]. Indeed, our analysis reveals that the "collagen-containing extracellular matrix" term is significantly downregulated upon HAND1 deletion (Fig. 3L), while both "collagen-containing extracellular matrix" and "extracellular matrix" terms are upregulated following ectopic HAND1 expression (Fig. 5G, S5D and S5E). These results suggest ECM-related cellular events are regulated by HAND1. Thus, HAND1 may be a key regulator controlling species-specific placental development, in part through its influence on ECM. Of note, a recent study proposes HAND1 as the essential regulator for human extra-embryonic mesenchyme specification from PSCs, but the molecular mechanism is uncovered [58]. In this study, we demonstrate that HAND1 orchestrates amniotic and trophoblast lineage commitment possibly by forming complexes with TCFs and β-catenin at lineage-specific regulatory elements. As a core effector of Wnt signaling, β-catenin may integrate inputs from the TGFβ and BMP4 pathways, both of which are key targets in differentiation protocols for amnion and trophoblast lineages [13, 59]. Notably, the chemically defined system used in this study, modulating Wnt and TGFβ pathways, reveals HAND1 as a possible nexus for these signals during lineage specification from PSCs. This mechanistic insight suggests that HAND1 synergizes with extrinsic cues to activate divergent transcriptional programs, offering a roadmap for refining differentiation protocols and dissecting regulatory crosstalk.

In summary, this work establishes HAND1 as a decisive regulator of trophoblast-amnion fate determination in hPSCs. By recruiting TCFs and β-catenin to lineage-specific regulatory elements, HAND1 directs transcriptional landscapes that underpin lineage identity. These findings provide a molecular framework for understanding of human extraembryonic development and advance our knowledge in PSCs application in lineage differentiation.

Material and method

Human samples

All clinical tissues samples (placentas and amnion) were obtained with written informed consent from all cases. Utilization of samples and experimental procedures were approved by the ethical committee of Southern Medical University. Our research complied with all of the relevant ethical regulations and guidelines.

Cell lines and cell culture

The M1 and H1 hEPSCs [16] were propagated on SNL76/7 STO feeders in hEPSCs medium with slight modification. Briefly, human EPSC media is N2B27-based media and supplemented with 5 μM XAV939 (Sigma, X3004), 1 μM CHIR99021 (TOCRIS, 4423), 0.1 μM A-419259 (TOCRIS, 3914), 50 μg/ml vitamin C (Sigma, 49752-100G) and 10 ng/ml LIF (SCI). To make the 500 ml N2B27 basal media, the following component were included in 394 ml DMEM/F12 (Gibco, C11330500BT) and 78.8 ml DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT): 2.5 ml N2 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17502048), 5 ml N27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17504044), 5 ml NEAA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10378–016), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 1% glutamine (Gibco, 25030081), 1% KnockOut Serum Replacement (Gibco, 10828–028), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, M6250). Medium was change daily. SNL76/7 STO feeders were prepared according to established protocols [16].

All cell lines used in this study were maintained at 37 ℃ under 5% CO2. To generated TSCs from EPSCs, the differentiated EPSCs in 2D conditions were trypsinized with 0.05% trypsin/EDTA (Gibco, 25500–054) and 7 × 104 single-dissociated hEPSCs were seeded on 1% Geltrex-coated 6-well plates in hEPSC medium with 10 μM ROCK inhibitor Y-27632(APExBIO, A3008). After 24 h, medium was changed with TS medium [14] with minor modification [16] [DMEM/F12 (Gibco, 21331–020) supplemented with 0.5% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 1% glutamine (Gibco, 25030081), 0.3% BSA (Solarbio, H1130), 0.2% FBS, 1% ITS-X supplement (Gibco, 51500056), 50 μg/ml L-ascorbic acid (Sigma, 49752-100G), 2 μM CHIR99021 (TOCRIS, 4423), 1 μM SB431542 (Sigma, 616461), 0.5 μM A83-01(TOCRIS, 2939), 10 μM valproic acid sodium salt (APExBIO, Valproic acid), 50 ng/ml EGF (R&D, 236-EG-200), 5 μM Y-27632 (APExBIO, A3008), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, M6250)]. TSC-like colonies were picked out at passage 6 and passaged by dissociation with TrypLE. The cells could be maintained for a long-time in TS medium. TSCs lines derived from placenta and M1-EPSCs were cultured as described previously [14, 60]. The cells were cultured on a Geltrex-coated well dish and passaged every 4–5 days by dissociation with TrypLE (Gibco, 12605036) at a 1:3 split ratio. The culture medium was replaced every two days. For EVT and STB differentiation, 1 × 105 TSCs were cultured and treated according to published protocol [14], and procedures for STB and EVT differentiation remained unchanged.
