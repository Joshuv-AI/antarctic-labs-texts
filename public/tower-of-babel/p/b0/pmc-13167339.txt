Perioperative hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and venous thromboembolism risk: a scoping review of available evidence and guidance
Authors: Omar Helmy, Celine Iswarya Partha Sarathi, Diwakar Sarma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectiveHormone replacement therapy (HRT) is used to manage menopausal symptoms, but its perioperative use raises concerns about increased venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk. Clinical guidelines vary, with some advising temporary discontinuation before surgery. This scoping review aims to synthesise evidence and guidance on whether perioperative cessation of HRT influences postoperative VTE risk.DesignA systematic search of PubMed and the Cochrane Library identified studies evaluating pre-operative HRT and VTE outcomes. Search terms included Hormone Replacement Therapy, HRT, Venous Thromboembolism, Venous Thrombosis, Pulmonary Embolism, Perioperative Care and Surgical Procedures, Operative.ParticipantsStudies included adult women on HRT undergoing surgery and assessed discontinuation and VTE risk. Data were synthesised narratively.ResultsThe search identified 246 records, four of which met the inclusion criteria: three primary studies and one systematic review. Only one study compared perioperative continuation versus cessation of HRT and found no significant increase in postoperative VTE with continued use. Other studies examined HRT among multiple VTE risk factors, with inconsistent findings. Clinical guidelines recognise oral oestrogen as a VTE risk factor and distinguish between higher-risk oral and lower-risk transdermal formulations; however, few provide explicit perioperative recommendations.ConclusionEvidence is limited and insufficient to support routine perioperative HRT cessation. The only comparative study found no significant increase in VTE with continuation, though limitations restrict interpretation. Overall, the literature reflects uncertainty. Given limited and heterogeneous data, perioperative HRT management should be individualised. Further prospective research is needed to determine whether temporary withdrawal, particularly of oral oestrogen, reduces postoperative VTE risk.Keywords: Hormone replacement therapy, venous thromboembolism, surgery, thrombosis, perioperative care, oestrogen, HRT discontinuation

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing both deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, represents a significant cause of perioperative morbidity and mortality. In the UK an estimated 25,000 patients die from preventable hospital-related VTEs per year. Surgical patients are particularly susceptible with a 30% increased risk. 1 Identification and modification of risk factors for VTE are therefore essential components of preoperative assessment. One such factor that may contribute to VTE risk, yet is often overlooked or inconsistently managed, is the use of hormone replacement therapy (HRT).

HRT replenishes women with ovarian hormones diminished during the natural menopausal transition. It is prescribed to alleviate associated symptoms, most notably vasomotor symptoms such as hot flushes, night sweats, and mood disturbances. Traditional systemic HRT typically consists of oestrogen alone or in combination with a progestogen, administered via oral or transdermal routes. 2 While transdermal oestrogen is generally considered to have a more favourable thrombotic profile due to avoidance of hepatic first-pass metabolism, oral oestrogen preparations are known to increase the risk of VTE in non-surgical populations, primarily through pro-coagulant changes in the clotting cascade.3–5 However, the implications of continuing or discontinuing HRT in the perioperative setting remain unclear.

Current guidelines offer limited and often inconsistent advice regarding the management of HRT in patients undergoing surgery. In the absence of robust evidence, clinicians must frequently rely on extrapolated data or anecdotal experience when advising patients about the perioperative use of HRT. Discontinuing HRT may theoretically reduce thrombotic risk, but doing so can also precipitate the return of distressing menopausal symptoms, 6 potentially impacting perioperative recovery and quality of life.

With an increasing number of women undergoing elective and emergency surgical procedures while on HRT, there is a clear need to establish whether temporary discontinuation offers any measurable benefit in reducing VTE incidence and whether this benefit justifies the associated symptomatic burden. To address this question, we conducted a scoping review of the current literature to map available evidence and guidance on whether perioperative cessation of HRT influences postoperative VTE risk.

Our aim is to provide a synthesis of the available evidence, identify key gaps in knowledge, and inform future research and clinical decision-making in this area.

Methods

This review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). 7

Aim

The aim of this scoping review was to map available evidence and clinical guidance on whether discontinuation of HRT prior to surgery affects the risk of postoperative VTE.

PubMed and the Cochrane database were searched using database specific terms related to HRT discontinuation and thromboembolism risk (Table 1). Searches were limited to English language publications. No date restrictions were applied. Full search terms included in Appendix.

Study selection and data extraction

Following de-duplication, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (OH and CIPS) using Rayyan QCRI systematic review software (https://www.rayyan.ai/). Full texts of potentially relevant studies were reviewed independently by the same reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Data were extracted using a standardised form tailored separately for primary studies and for systematic reviews. Findings were synthesised qualitatively and presented in tabular and narrative format. Risk of bias for primary studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. 8 The AMSTAR-2 scale 9 was used to assess risk of bias in systematic reviews (Figure 1).

Evidence from primary studies

A retrospective case-control study [Hurbanek et al. (2004) 10 ] investigated the association between perioperative HRT use and postoperative VTE in women undergoing hip or knee arthroplasty between 1997 and 2002 in a US clinic. The study included 108 cases with radiologically confirmed postoperative deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism and 210 matched controls without thrombosis. HRT was defined as use of oestrogen-only preparations (oral or transdermal), combined oestrogen-progesterone therapy, or selective oestrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) continued until surgery or stopped within two weeks prior.

Among 67 HRT users, 18 (16.7%) were in the VTE group compared to 49 (23.3%) in the control group. The unadjusted odds ratio for HRT use and postoperative thrombosis was 0.66 (95% CI 0.35–1.18; p = 0.17), suggesting no significant association. After adjustment for factors associated with both HRT use and VTE (age, coronary disease, and prior VTE), the association remained non-significant (aOR 0.75; 95% CI 0.39–1.38; p = 0.36), and further multivariate adjustment for comorbidities yielded similar findings (aOR 0.68; 95% CI 0.35–1.28; p = 0.19). Importantly, over 80% of patients received pharmacological thromboprophylaxis, primarily with low molecular weight heparin (enoxaparin 30 mg twice daily), and most of the remaining patients were treated with warfarin.

The authors concluded that, in the context of appropriate thromboprophylaxis, perioperative HRT use did not significantly increase postoperative VTE risk. They speculated that the prothrombotic effect of major orthopaedic surgery likely outweighs any modest risk posed by continued HRT.

However, the study's findings are limited by its observational design and lack of randomisation. Additionally, since nearly all participants received pharmacological VTE prophylaxis, the study could not assess HRT-related thrombosis risk in unprotected patients. Confounding by indication may also have influenced results, as prior VTE was more common in cases but less common in HRT users.

Richey et al. (2019) 11 conducted a retrospective matched case-control study investigating risk factors for postoperative VTE following foot and ankle surgery in a large integrated health system (Kaiser Permanente, Northern California). The analysis included 90 adult patients who developed VTE within 6 months of surgery between January 2008 and May 2011, and 90 matched controls without VTE, matched by age, gender, and surgical site.

Among several potential predictors, the use of HRT or oral contraception was significantly associated with VTE. In the multivariable conditional regression model, hormone therapy use conferred an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 8.9 (95% CI 1.3–62.9; p = .03), indicating a markedly increased risk of postoperative VTE compared to non-users. Other independent risk factors included obesity (BMI >30; aOR 6.1), history of VTE (aOR 15.7), and postoperative non weightbearing immobilisation for more than 2 weeks (aOR 9.0). Of note, only 35 patients (24 cases and 11 controls) received postoperative chemical thromboprophylaxis.

The study highlights hormone therapy as a strong independent risk factor for VTE in this surgical cohort. However, key limitations include the lack of differentiation between HRT and oral contraceptives, and no further stratification by HRT type (e.g. oral vs. transdermal, or oestrogen-only vs. combined preparations).

A large prospective cohort study by Sforza et al. (2021) 12 evaluated the incidence of VTE among 1078 female patients undergoing abdominoplasty at a private UK hospital. All participants discontinued H 4 weeks prior to surgery and resumed 2 weeks postoperatively. HRT cessation formed part of a comprehensive eight-point VTE prophylaxis protocol, which also included mechanical and pharmacologic measures (compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression devices, clexane dosing according to BMI), smoking cessation, and a BMI threshold of <40 kg/m2.

No cases of DVT were identified over a 1-year follow-up period. The authors acknowledged that the absence of events limited statistical inference and addressed this by modelling the probability of observing zero DVT cases under various assumed true incidence rates. They found their data were consistent with a true DVT risk of up to ∼0.28% at p < 0.05. When compared to historical cohorts using normality tests, their DVT risk appeared significantly lower than studies reporting higher incidence, and similar to those with low reported rates.

While the authors concluded that their protocol, including HRT cessation, may reduce VTE risk in abdominoplasty, the absence of an internal control group introduces a significant risk of confounding bias and prevents isolation of the specific effect of HRT cessation from other elements of the prophylaxis strategy.

Clinical practice guidelines review

A recent systematic review 13 examined clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) from North American and European medical societies on the perioperative management of HRT in the context of elective lower limb arthroplasty. Seven English-language CPGs published between January 2000 and January 2023 were included.

The review identified substantial variation across guidelines (see Table 3). Several major societies, namely the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP), and the European Journal of Anaesthesiologists, acknowledged that HRT confers some degree of VTE risk but did not provide specific recommendations on withholding HRT perioperatively. Notably, these guidelines did not distinguish between HRT formulations (e.g. oral vs. transdermal) or hormone types.

In contrast, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) was the only organisation to explicitly recommend discontinuing oestrogen-containing contraceptives and HRT four weeks before elective surgery. However, the supporting evidence for this recommendation was inconsistent, drawing on reviews by Edmunds and Kemmeren, 14 which presented conflicting conclusions regarding the association between HRT and VTE risk. NICE also cites guidance from the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), which classifies HRT as only a minor risk factor for VTE.

The Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) provided the most detailed guidance, distinguishing between oral and transdermal HRT formulations. SIGN noted that oral oestrogen is associated with a two- to five-fold increased risk of VTE, while transdermal preparations do not appear to carry the same risk. This recommendation was primarily based on a single systematic review and meta-analysis, which found that all but one of the included observational studies consistently demonstrated a link between oral oestrogen use and elevated VTE risk. 15 On this basis, SIGN suggested that discontinuing oral oestrogen prior to elective surgery may help reduce VTE risk.

Other organisations, including the Endocrine Society, and the International Menopause Society (IMS), acknowledged that oral oestrogen HRT increases VTE risk but did not offer perioperative management recommendations.

The authors concluded that current clinical guidelines lack consistency and specificity regarding perioperative HRT management. No included guideline recommended withholding transdermal or progesterone-only HRT formulations. The review highlighted a clear need for further high-quality evidence to justify withholding oestrogen containing oral forms.

Discussion

This scoping review examined the current evidence on whether discontinuing HRT prior to surgery impacts the risk of postoperative VTE. Across three primary studies and one systematic review of clinical practice guidelines, findings remain heterogeneous and inconclusive, with variation in study design, surgical context, HRT formulation, and use of thromboprophylaxis.

Two primary studies offered conflicting evidence. Hurbanek et al. (2004) 10 found no significant association between perioperative HRT use and postoperative VTE in women undergoing hip or knee arthroplasty, provided that appropriate pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was used. This suggests that the prothrombotic burden of major orthopaedic surgery may outweigh any incremental risk conferred by continued HRT. In contrast, Richey et al. (2019) 11 identified hormonal therapy, including HRT and oral contraceptives, as an independent risk factor for VTE following foot and ankle surgery, with a markedly elevated adjusted odds ratio. However, this study did not differentiate between hormone types or routes of administration, limiting its applicability to the specific context of HRT use. Notably, only 35 of 180 participants received chemical thromboprophylaxis, which may partially explain the discrepancy in findings.

A third study, by Sforza et al. (2021), 12 implemented a comprehensive VTE prophylaxis protocol that included preoperative HRT discontinuation. No DVT events were observed in this cohort of 1078 women undergoing abdominoplasty, though the absence of a control group and the multifaceted nature of the intervention preclude firm conclusions about the role of HRT cessation in isolation. These findings suggest potential benefits but highlight the difficulty in disentangling the effect of HRT withdrawal from other prophylactic measures.

Clinical practice guidelines

Beyond primary research evidence, we examined clinical practice guidelines. It is important to note that guideline recommendations represent expert consensus rather than direct evidence from perioperative studies, and may be based on extrapolation from non-surgical populations or theoretical considerations of thrombotic risk.

A systematic review of clinical guidelines by Abouharb et al. (2024) 13 revealed inconsistent recommendations across professional societies. While most acknowledged the increased VTE risk associated with oral oestrogen, only the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) explicitly recommended stopping oestrogen-containing HRT 4 weeks before surgery. The Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) provided the most nuanced guidance, suggesting discontinuation of oral HRT based on observational data linking it to increased thrombotic risk, while distinguishing it from transdermal formulations.

Taken together, the limited number and the nature of included studies severely constrain the conclusions that can be drawn. Only Hurbanek et al. directly compared perioperative HRT continuation versus cessation, and even this remains an observational study with broad exposure definitions. Richey et al. combined HRT with oral contraceptive use without stratification by formulation or route, limiting its relevance to menopausal HRT specifically. Sforza et al. embedded HRT cessation within a comprehensive prophylaxis bundle, making it impossible to isolate any independent effect of stopping HRT. Furthermore, studies rarely stratified findings by HRT route (oral vs. transdermal), despite the clinically important differences in thrombotic risk profiles between these formulations. These fundamental methodological limitations mean that the review demonstrates uncertainty rather than evidence of benefit or harm from perioperative HRT cessation.

Beyond these specific limitations, the evidence base reveals broader knowledge gaps. No randomised controlled trials have evaluated perioperative HRT discontinuation. Most studies were conducted in specific surgical contexts with varying baseline VTE risks, making generalisability challenging.

There is also variation in thromboprophylaxis practice, which significantly modifies VTE risk and may mask any potential effect of HRT. Regional guidelines based on experiential medicine advocate prescribing extended thromboprophylaxis for surgical patients on HRT. As seen in Hurbanek et al., the routine use of anticoagulation may mitigate the thrombotic risk associated with continued HRT, potentially reducing the need for preoperative withdrawal in low- to moderate-risk cases.

Strengths and limitations

This review synthesised evidence from both primary clinical studies and guideline-level recommendations, providing a broad overview of current practice. Nevertheless, it is constrained by the small number of eligible studies and marked heterogeneity in design, patient populations, surgical procedures and definitions of HRT. The AMSTAR-2 9 appraisal of the included systematic review of guidelines yielded a ‘critically low’ overall rating, driven by the absence of a registered protocol, limited search strategy, lack of a comprehensive list of excluded records and no formal quality appraisal of the included guidelines. These methodological weaknesses limit confidence in that review's conclusions. Likewise, the observational nature of the primary studies precludes causal inference, and publication bias may also be present, with studies reporting null associations potentially under-represented. Additionally, the search was restricted to PubMed and the Cochrane Library without inclusion of other databases such as Embase or regional databases. This may have resulted in missing relevant perioperative or European observational studies and represents a source of potentially incomplete evidence mapping. Given the limited number of studies, heterogeneity in design and populations, high risk of bias in observational studies, and the inability to isolate the effect of HRT cessation, the overall certainty of evidence must be rated as very low across all outcomes.

Implications for practice and research

Given the limited and methodologically constrained evidence base, clinical decisions about perioperative HRT management must be highly individualised, balancing potential thrombotic risk against consequences of abrupt HRT withdrawal, such as vasomotor symptoms, mood disturbance, and reduced bone protection. The current evidence does not permit confident recommendations for or against routine HRT cessation. The limited quality and quantity of existing research underscores the urgent need for prospective studies that can meaningfully address whether stopping oral oestrogen therapies before surgery reduces postoperative thrombotic risk.

Furthermore, the distinction between oral and transdermal oestrogen is clinically critical and must be considered in perioperative decision-making and in the design of future studies. Transdermal preparations bypass hepatic first-pass metabolism and carry substantially lower VTE risk in non-surgical populations. Future research should prioritise prospective or randomised studies evaluating perioperative VTE risk in users of different HRT formulations, ideally stratified by route of administration (oral vs. transdermal), surgical risk, baseline VTE risk, and thromboprophylaxis regimen. Standardised reporting of outcomes and HRT exposures, including type, route, duration, and timing of cessation, will be crucial to develop evidence-based guidelines.
