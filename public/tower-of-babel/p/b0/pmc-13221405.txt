Target-aware molecule SMILES generation using a large language model with retrieval-augmented generation, multi-turn memory, and a predictive model
Authors: Piotr Karabowicz, Radosław Charkiewicz, Alicja Charkiewicz, Jacek Nikliński
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given the vastness of chemical space and the cost and time requirements of high-throughput screening, resource-efficient computational strategies are needed to prioritize candidate drug molecules. Here, we evaluated whether an open-weight large language model (LLM) augmented with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), multi-turn memory, and a pretrained drug–target interaction predictor can generate target-aware molecules without task-specific retraining. Protein–ligand–pKi examples retrieved from BindingDB, Davis, and KIBA were used as contextual guidance, while DeepPurpose provided the optimization signal during iterative SMILES refinement. Our approach produced a statistically significant increase in predicted pKi across successive multi-turn memory iterations, indicating that retrieval- and memory-guided refinement may improve target-conditioned molecular generation. The proposed framework also showed favorable molecular generation characteristics, with novelty reaching 100%, diversity up to 0.882, and uniqueness up to 1.0, suggesting that retrieval did not reduce the process to simple reproduction of known ligands but instead supported exploration of new regions of chemical space. In addition, the approach yielded supportive docking results consistent with the generation of chemically relevant candidate ligands. However, the increase in predicted affinity was accompanied by trade-offs in molecular quality, including reduced validity and drug-likeness in later iterations. Taken together, these findings suggest that this framework provides a flexible and comparatively resource-efficient strategy for target-aware de novo molecular design, while further multi-objective optimization and independent validation remain necessary.Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10822-026-00834-1.

Introduction

Drug discovery is a long and expensive process. Traditional approaches, such as high-throughput screening and structure-based drug design, require extensive experimental validation and rely heavily on prior structural knowledge of ligands and protein targets. The chemical space is extraordinarily vast, with estimates exceeding 10⁶⁰ possible molecules, meaning that only a small fraction has been explored to date [1]. Machine learning (ML) is an effective tool for analyzing large amounts of data. Large language models (LLMs) represent an advancement in modern ML, that extends the capabilities of deep learning (DL). These models are pretrained on massive text corpora using objectives such as next-token prediction or masked-token reconstruction, enabling them to learn complex contextual relationships between tokens, whether natural language words, Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry Specification (SMILES) symbols, or amino acid residues [2, 3]. Through this pretraining, LLMs acquire strong generative and reasoning abilities that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks, including scientific applications that require precise sequencing modeling. Such versatility has made LLMs increasingly attractive tools for chemistry and discovery, wherein sequence-based generative modeling plays a central role. LLM can accelerate drug development processes by enabling the generation and optimization of novel molecular structures with desirable pharmacological properties. DL-based generative models have demonstrated their ability to produce valid and diverse molecules, thereby reducing development costs and enhancing the efficiency of early stage drug discovery [4].

The application of generative deep learning for de novo molecular generation has gained increasing attention in recent years. By representing molecules as SMILES strings, that is, linear character sequences encoding molecular graphs, researchers can apply language models, particularly transformer-based architectures, to chemical design [5]. Although adequately trained models can reliably generate syntactically valid SMILES sequences, the principal challenge remains the conditional generation of truly novel molecules that are functionally relevant to a specific drug target [6]. As highlighted by recent surveys (Song et al. [7], Ma et al. [8]), models built on diverse general-purpose backbones—including the GPT series, BERT, LLaMA, Galactica, T5, and BART, as well as more recent architectures like Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, or Qwen—serve as the foundation for a new generation of chemical AI. Their domain-adapted variants and specialized frameworks (e.g., DrugGPT, BioGPT, ChemBERTa, DrugLLM, DrugAgent, MolRAG) can perform de novo molecular design, multi-objective optimization (balancing affinity, QED, SA, ADMET), and DTI reasoning in few-shot or instruction-tuned regimes. These models, supplemented by task-oriented systems such as 3DSMILES-GPT, SynLlama, and Tx-LLM, demonstrate remarkable generalization potential, often matching or surpassing task-specific deep learning baselines on standard benchmarks such as MoleculeNet, TDC, or GuacaMol.

Identifying a candidate molecule and determining its drug–target interaction (DTI) are important stages in the drug discovery pipeline. Drug-target interactions describe the binding relationship between a small molecule and a protein target, and their accurate characterization is meaningful for understanding the mechanism of action and predicting therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, DTI prediction plays a significant role in rational drug design. Deep learning–based approaches have substantially advanced computational DTI modeling by capturing complex nonlinear patterns in chemical and biological representations, enabling more accurate predictions [9].

Current approaches predominantly rely on training models from scratch or fine-tuning existing ones, however, both strategies incur substantial computational, temporal, and financial costs [10]. Therefore, there is a growing interest in computationally less expensive methods. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a paradigm designed to address key limitations of LLM, including hallucinations, outdated knowledge, and limited transparency in reasoning. RAG operates by performing a similarity-based search over an external knowledge repository to identify the most relevant entries, which are then incorporated into the model input as contextual augmentation to condition and guide the generation process of the language model. By integrating external databases into the generation process, RAG enables models to ground their outputs in up-to-date, verifiable information, thereby improving the accuracy and reliability of knowledge-intensive tasks [11]. This approach combines the contextual reasoning abilities of LLMs with dynamically retrieved evidence, allowing continuous knowledge updates and seamless incorporation of domain-specific data. Consequently, RAG provides a more accurate and interpretable alternative to purely parametric models, supporting scientific and technical applications that require factual precision. As demonstrated by Zhang et al. [12], the integration of RAG with specialized pharmacological knowledge bases is essential to mitigate "knowledge hallucinations" and random errors inherent in general-purpose models. Their evaluation of a RAG-based framework, showed that providing specific scientific context significantly enhances the model's ability to propose actionable lead compound optimization strategies, such as metabolic stability or solubility improvements, which general LLMs often fail to detail accurately. Simultaneously, new architectures such as Rag2Mol have significantly expanded the capabilities of LLMs in chemistry. This system uses RAG to design ligands tailored to specific protein binding pockets based on data from structural databases.

The second aspect of improving LLMs generation is multi-turn memory, which enables a model to retain and leverage information from earlier stages of a multi-step interaction. Instead of treating each query independently, models with multi-turn memory maintain contextual state across successive prompts, enabling more coherent reasoning, improved long-range dependency modeling, and better alignment with user intent [13]. In scientific or generative workflows, such memory facilitates iterative refinement, consistent sequences, and handling of complex tasks that require recalling previously generated content or constraints. Despite the rapid progress in AI-driven drug discovery, the development of scalable and resource-efficient algorithms for target-specific molecular generation remains a significant challenge. Unlike previous retrieval-based methods that focus primarily on structural alignment, our approach introduces a unique integration of multi-turn memory with an external pKi predictive model, allowing for an iterative optimization of binding affinity that is absent in existing frameworks. By utilizing a pre-trained, open-source LLM enhanced by RAG, we demonstrate that high-quality, target-aware molecules can be designed without the need for specialized retraining or deep architectural expertise. This pipeline not only lowers the technical barrier to entry but also promotes the democratization of drug discovery, providing an accessible and accurate alternative for researchers to perform sophisticated molecular design without extensive computational backgrounds.

Material and methods

Generative language model

We utilized GPT-OSS-20b (GPT Open Source 20 billion parameters), an open-weight reasoning model developed with an efficient Mixture of Experts (MoE) transformer architecture. The model was trained using large-scale distillation and reinforcement learning methods to optimize its reasoning capabilities and inference efficiency [14]. We selected a mid-sized 20-billion-parameter model that offers a favorable balance between computational efficiency and generative capability. To assess the impact of the model on molecule generation, a comparative model, LLaMA 3.1:8B (8 billion parameters) [15], was employed.

RAG and data

Entries containing the target amino acid sequence and corresponding ligand SMILES were retrieved from BindingDB [16] and from the Davis and KIBA benchmark datasets [9] to construct the RAG context. For the BindingDB-based setting, Ki-derived pKi values were additionally retained as standardized affinity annotations. In contrast, the Davis and KIBA datasets were used only as sources of retrieval examples and their native affinity annotations were not used as predictive targets in the DeepPurpose optimization step.

We implemented an algorithm that identifies the most similar protein sequences to a given query sequence. It evaluates the similarity between the query and all proteins in the dataset, assigns a similarity score to each entry, and selects the top ten records with the highest scores. In this way, the method retrieves the closest protein–ligand–pKi examples for retrieval-augmented generation.

One hundred protein sequences were extracted from BindingDB to create the test set. These sequences were distinct from those included in the RAG dataset.

RAG datasets diversity and similarity

SMILES were parsed and canonicalized using RDKit, and invalid entries were removed. Duplicate ligands were identified based on canonical SMILES and redundancy was quantified as the ratio of total to unique molecules. Chemical scaffolds were extracted using the Bemis–Murcko definition. Molecular similarity was computed using Morgan fingerprints (radius = 2, 2048 bits) and the Tanimoto coefficient. To ensure computational efficiency, nearest-neighbor similarity was estimated by sampling up to 100 candidate neighbors per molecule. Dataset diversity was summarized as mean nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity and the fraction of ligands with similarity > 0.85.

Protein sequences were deduplicated and redundancy was quantified analogously. Sequence length statistics were calculated to characterize variability. Sequence identity was estimated from up to 10,000 randomly sampled pairs using global alignment implemented in Biopython. Mean, median identity and the fraction of pairs with identity > 80% were reported. Assessment of internal diversity and similarity of generated SMILES to retrieved examples

To assess whether retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) constrained the chemical space explored by the model, we quantified both the internal diversity of generated molecules and their similarity to the retrieved RAG examples. For each test case, the six molecules generated were compared against the corresponding top-10 retrieved molecules. Molecular similarity was calculated using Tanimoto similarity on Morgan fingerprints (radius = 2, 2048 bits). Internal diversity was defined as one minus the mean pairwise Tanimoto similarity among the generated molecules. Similarity to RAG was quantified as the mean of the maximum Tanimoto similarity between each generated molecule and the set of ten retrieved RAG molecules. This analysis was performed separately for each generation setting to determine whether higher predicted affinity was associated with reduced diversity or increased structural proximity to retrieved examples.

Multi-turn memory, predictive DTI model, and prompting

The approach incorporates a multi-turn memory strategy, in which a generative model proposes successive ligand structures while retaining a record of earlier designs and their predicted activities. At each stage, the model receives the target protein sequence together with the previous SMILES outputs and corresponding pKi prediction, enabling context-dependent refinement rather than isolated, single-step generation. This conversational memory operates as an adaptive search process that directs structural modifications based on prior performance. Candidate molecules are assessed using a pretrained drug–target interaction convolutional model that predicts binding affinity. In all optimization experiments, the predictive signal used to rank and refine candidate molecules was the DeepPurpose-predicted pKi value; therefore, pKi served as the single scalar objective throughout the multi-turn iterative procedure, regardless of the retrieval database used to provide contextual examples.

In this study, DeepPurpose [17] was used in its pre-trained configuration based on a convolutional neural network (CNN) encoder for both ligand and protein representations (CNN–CNN architecture). SMILES strings were encoded using a drug CNN module, whereas protein sequences were embedded using an analogous CNN encoder designed to capture local residue-level patterns relevant for binding.

Two prompting stages were used in this pipeline. First, an instruction prompt constrained the model to produce only a single SMILES string for a given protein sequence (“Generate a SMILES string for the given protein sequence. Write ONLY the SMILES string, with no explanations.”). In the optimization stage, a system prompt defined the model as an expert computational chemist tasked with iteratively improving molecules to maximize the predicted pKi while preserving chemical validity (“You are an expert computational chemist. Your goal is to iteratively improve molecules to maximize predicted pKi while ensuring validity.”), followed by user prompts that provided (i) RAG-derived examples of protein–ligand–pKi triplets and (ii) a multi-turn history of previously generated SMILES with their predicted pKi, instructing the model to “return ONLY the improved SMILES” at each iteration.

Docking, QED, novelty, uniqueness, diversity, and statistical evaluation

All molecules were docked using AutoDock Vina [18]. For each case, the receptor structure was retrieved from AlphaFold [19] using the UniProt identifier and converted to PDBQT format. Ligands were prepared from SMILES by generating a 3D geometry and written to PDBQT (Meeko). The docking search space (grid box) was determined automatically from the protein atom coordinates (center as the coordinate mean; box size as the min–max range along each axis with an additional 12 Å margin, capped at 25 Å per axis). We report the raw Vina affinity (score raw, kcal/mol) and, to reduce ligand-size dependence, we also compute ligand efficiency score le = -score raw / N heavy, where N heavy is the number of heavy atoms. If a reference ligand was provided for a given pair (ref smiles), the reference was docked under identical conditions (same receptor and grid box), and we report score ref as well as delta score = score raw − score ref. The resulting docking score corresponds to the predicted affinity, with more negative values indicating stronger interaction. Additionally, compound-level metrics such as QED, novelty, and uniqueness were calculated to assess drug-likeness, structural originality, and diversity of the generated molecules. To ensure a transparent and rigorous evaluation, all generated SMILES were first canonicalized using RDKit before calculating structure-based metrics. Uniqueness was defined as the fraction of non-redundant canonical SMILES among valid generated molecules. Validity was assessed using RDKit molecule parsing; only outputs successfully converted into molecular objects were considered valid, while malformed strings, chemically invalid SMILES, and missing outputs were treated as invalid. Diversity was calculated for valid molecules only as one minus the mean pairwise Tanimoto similarity of Morgan fingerprints. Novelty was calculated against the canonicalized SMILES in the corresponding RAG dataset.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to assess the normality of distribution. Statistical differences between groups were examined using Student's t-test for independent samples, using Statistica 13.3.

Pipeline overview

The pipeline starts by generating initial ligand candidates (SMILES) based on protein sequences (Fig. 1), which are further contextualized using similar examples retrieved from a reference database. These ligands enter a multi-turn optimisation loop, where each iteration produces refined molecules informed by prior proposals and predicted activities. Valid structures were evaluated using drug–target interaction models. The resulting information is fed back to guide subsequent refinements, creating a continuous data flow from the protein input to the optimized ligand designs (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Model pipeline overview. Iteration-level analysis of mode collapse, repetition, and memory leakage

To evaluate how the behavior of the model changes over successive refinement iterations, we examined three generation-related effects at each iteration. First, mode collapse was assessed as a decrease in the proportion of unique molecules generated within a given iteration, indicating reduced diversity of the output. Second, repetition was defined as the proportion of duplicated SMILES produced within the same iteration, reflecting the tendency of the model to generate the same structures multiple times. Third, memory leakage was defined as the proportion of molecules generated in a given iteration that had already been produced in earlier iterations, indicating the extent to which later outputs repeated previously generated compounds rather than introducing new ones.

pKi values across iteration and ablation study

The ablation study suggests that the observed behavior arises from the interaction of retrieval context, iterative memory, predictive feedback, and backbone model choice rather than from any single component alone. In the full configuration, RAG + gptoss + MTM + BindingDB + pkiprediction, the mean predicted pKi increased from 5.67 in the first iteration to 6.41 in the sixth iteration, while the best molecule identified across all iterations reached a predicted pKi of 6.63 (Table 3). Statistical analysis showed significant improvement relative to the first iteration (Table S1), consistent with the effectiveness of the iterative optimization procedure under this scoring framework. Removing retrieval augmentation while retaining MTM and predictive scoring (gptoss + MTM + pkiprediction) yielded a lower final best-SMILES value of 6.51, which is consistent with a beneficial contribution of retrieved target-related examples. However, this non-RAG variant still exhibited a clear iteration-dependent increase, indicating that iterative memory-based refinement itself contributes substantially to the observed gains. The role of explicit pKi feedback was more nuanced: the RAG + gptoss + MTM + BindingDB variant without predictive feedback reached a best-SMILES value of 6.71, suggesting that the predictive module changes the optimization trajectory rather than uniformly maximizing the final best-case score. The retrieval source also affected model behavior, with the Davis-based variant reaching a best predicted pKi of 6.40 and the KIBA-based variant 6.56, indicating that the composition of the retrieval database influences the region of chemical space explored by the model. Replacing GPT-OSS with LLaMa (RAG + llama + MTM + BindingDB + pkiprediction) produced high initial pKi values but no clear iterative gain, suggesting that not all LLM model type exploit retrieval and memory equally effectively. The distinct behavior of the LLaMA model configuration suggests a model-specific failure mode that extends beyond simple performance differences. Unlike GPT-OSS, LLaMA did not exhibit consistent iterative improvement and showed substantially lower validity together with increased memory leakage. Several factors may contribute to this effect. First, GPT-OSS-20B has a larger parameter count and employs a Mixture-of-Experts architecture, which increases its effective capacity and may facilitate integration of heterogeneous signals such as retrieval context, multi-turn history, and external predictive feedback. In contrast, the smaller dense LLaMA 3.1:8B model may have limited capacity to jointly process these inputs, resulting in weaker adaptation across iterations. Second, the increased memory leakage observed for LLaMA suggests reduced stability in multi-turn self-conditioning, leading to progressive reuse of previously generated molecules rather than productive exploration of chemical space. Third, the higher similarity to retrieved examples combined with reduced internal diversity indicates that LLaMA may over-rely on retrieval context without effectively generalizing beyond it.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the effectiveness of retrieval-augmented, multi-turn optimization pipelines is strongly dependent on the underlying model architecture and training regime. In this context, models optimized for reasoning and instruction-following may be better suited for iterative molecular design workflows than smaller general-purpose dense models. Overall, these findings support the view that the final performance emerges from the combined action of retrieval, memory, predictive scoring, and model selection.

To avoid overemphasizing isolated best-case molecules, we examined molecular quality metrics both across all iterations (Table 5) and for the final best SMILES selected for each target in each pipeline (Table 6). In the full RAG + gptoss + MTM + BindingDB + pkiprediction setting, mean predicted pKi increased over successive rounds, but this improvement was accompanied at the iteration level by a decline in validity from 82% in the first iteration to 60% in the sixth iteration, a decrease in mean QED from 0.617 to 0.464, and an increase in mean logP from 3.020 to 3.618, whereas diversity remained relatively stable. Validity includes failed outputs (e.g., missing SMILES or refusals) as invalid; thus, the lower validity in later iterations reflects more such failures. The endpoint analysis of best SMILES confirms the same general pattern: although the full BindingDB-based RAG pipeline retained high validity (93%), high novelty (97.849%), and high diversity (0.862), its mean QED (0.517) was lower and mean logP (3.829) was higher than in the non-RAG GPT-OSS baseline (gptoss + MTM + pkiprediction; QED 0.624, logP 3.010), indicating that affinity-oriented optimization did not uniformly improve overall molecular quality. Similar trends were observed for the Davis- and KIBA-based RAG variants, whereas the llama-based configuration combined very poor endpoint validity (23%) with high diversity, underscoring that high predicted affinity alone is insufficient to characterize model performance. Taken together, the iteration-wise and endpoint-level analyses show that best-case molecules should be interpreted together with broader quality metrics, as optimization toward the pKi predictor is associated with measurable trade-offs in validity and drug-likeness. Based on these observations, we set the number of multi-turn optimization iterations to six, as additional iterations increased predicted pKi but simultaneously reduced molecular validity and QED, indicating a trade-off between affinity optimization and overall molecular quality. Iteration-wise analysis showed that generative stability was generally maintained across refinement steps (Table S2). For most GPT-OSS-based configurations, both mode collapse and within-iteration repetition remained very low across all six iterations, typically ranging from 0.00 to 0.04, indicating that iterative optimization did not substantially reduce output diversity within each round. The main exception among the GPT-OSS settings was gptoss + MTM + BindingDB, which showed higher values in the first iteration but stabilized thereafter. Memory leakage was also low for most GPT-OSS-based variants, usually remaining below 0.07. In contrast, RAG + llama + MTM + BindingDB + pkiprediction showed a pronounced increase in memory leakage over successive iterations, reaching 0.39 in iteration 6, which suggests progressively stronger reuse of previously generated molecules. Overall, these results indicate that the iterative refinement procedure was generally robust, with limited evidence of degeneration except for increased historical reuse in the llama-based variant.

In comparative analyses against previously reported target-aware molecular generation models, our framework showed a distinct balance between conservative generation and structural exploration (Table 9). The GPT-OSS-based RAG-MTM variants did not maximize validity to the same extent as the most conservative methods, such as DeepDTAGen, but they consistently achieved high novelty and perfect uniqueness in the evaluated best-SMILES outputs, reaching 100.00% novelty on the Davis and KIBA databases and 97.85% on BindingDB with pKi prediction. Relative to methods such as PGMG and Synthalinker, the proposed framework therefore appears to provide a more favorable balance between novelty and uniqueness while maintaining competitive validity in the stronger GPT-OSS-based settings. This pattern suggests that the model explores a broader region of chemical space than several literature baselines that are more strongly biased toward familiar drug-like patterns. At the same time, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously and treated as approximate rather than as direct head-to-head benchmarks, because differences in datasets, preprocessing, benchmark definitions, and evaluation protocols may substantially influence the absolute values of validity, novelty, and uniqueness. The observed increase in the pKi values across successive iterations (Table 3) underscores the effectiveness of the multi-turn memory strategy as a form of iterative optimization. By maintaining the context of previous proposals and their predicted activities, the LLM functions not only as a static generator but also as an adaptive agent capable of structural refinement. This process mimics the traditional lead optimization phase in medicinal chemistry, where small modifications are made to the scaffold to enhance the binding affinity. The fact that the "best SMILES" achieved significantly higher pKi scores than the first iteration suggests that the model can successfully navigate the chemical space toward more potent candidates by "learning" from the feedback provided by the DTI predictive model within the conversation history. For the best-generated molecules, our pipeline achieved a mean predicted pKi of 6.83, which is lower than the affinities reported for the more conservative DrugGen model (7.22), but higher than those obtained with DrugGPT (5.81).

The high diversity and near-maximal novelty of the generated molecules suggest that the integration of RAG did not constrain the model to simple imitation of previously known ligands. Instead, the retrieved examples appear to have functioned as context-providing anchors that biased the model toward chemically and biologically relevant neighborhoods while still allowing the generation of structurally new candidates. This interpretation is supported by the observation that many high-scoring molecules were absent from the retrieval database and remained only moderately similar to the retrieved examples. Such a balance between data-grounded conditioning and structural exploration is desirable in de novo molecular design, although the present analysis does not yet establish whether these novel structures correspond to experimentally useful chemotypes. We note that the use of a fixed retrieval size of ten represents a practical design choice aimed at limiting prompt length while preserving relevant contextual examples. Although the RAG datasets are characterized by substantial record-level redundancy, our diversity analysis suggests that this redundancy is not equivalent to complete duplication in either chemical or sequence space. Accordingly, the retrieved examples may still provide meaningful guidance to the model despite the constrained retrieval size. The integration of RAG provides a level of interpretability that is often lacking in 'black-box' generative models. By conditioning the generation on ten explicitly retrieved protein–ligand pairs, the framework allows researchers to trace the structural influence of known inhibitors on the newly generated candidates. This transparency, combined with the model's ability to operate in a zero-shot manner without costly fine-tuning, demonstrates a scalable and resource-efficient approach to drug discovery.

Despite these encouraging findings, several limitations inherent to the use of general-purpose LLMs in chemistry must be considered. The occasional generation of non-chemical responses, such as “SMILES_NOT_AVAILABLE” or refusal messages, highlights the lack of chemistry-specific instruction tuning in the base GPT-OSS-20b model. More importantly, the optimization loop was driven by a single pre-trained DTI predictor, creating a risk that the model learned to exploit predictor-specific biases rather than to identify molecules with genuinely improved biological activity. In addition, the increase in predicted pKi was accompanied by declining validity and QED together with increasing logP, indicating that affinity-oriented optimization alone is insufficient as a sole design objective. Future work should therefore incorporate stronger validity constraints, multi-objective optimization including ADMET related properties, and external validation with orthogonal scoring methods or experimental assays. The present framework should consequently be viewed as hypothesis generating rather than as a direct route to validated drug candidates.

In summary, this study presents a target-aware SMILES generation pipeline based on GPT-OSS-20b combined with retrieval-augmented generation, multi-turn memory, and predictive feedback. Within this in silico framework, the method produced a systematic increase in model-predicted pKi across refinement rounds and generated molecules with high novelty and substantial chemical diversity, while several configurations also showed supportive docking results. At the same time, optimization was associated with declining validity and drug-likeness metrics, underscoring the need for multi-objective optimization and independent validation. The proposed approach is therefore best viewed as a flexible and comparatively resource-efficient strategy for generating and iteratively refining target-conditioned molecular hypotheses rather than as a substitute for downstream experimental confirmation.
