CVD risk assessment by ethnicity in Aotearoa New Zealand: a national observational study
Authors: Vanessa Selak, Janine Paynter, Sandra Hanchard, Karen Marie Brewer, Tua Taueetia-Su’a, Suneela Mehta, Shanthi Ameratunga, Matire Harwood, Corina Grey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) risk assessment is a key strategy for reducing the burden of, and inequities in, CVD. The aim of this study was to determine whether there are inequities in CVD risk assessment by ethnicity in Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ).MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of a de-identified national cohort of people aged 25–74 years living in NZ on 31 March 2018, recommended for CVD risk assessment according to current guidelines. The cohort was generated using both Statistics NZ’s Integrated Data Infrastructure and health service user populations. People were excluded if they had a prior history of CVD. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression modelling was used to explore associations between ethnicity (Māori, Pacific, Indian, European or Other) and evidence of CVD risk assessment. We used national laboratory claims between 1 April 2018 and 31 March 2023 to find tests related to CVD risk assessment as a proxy for completed assessments.Results1,476,747 people (40.5% women, 12.1% with diabetes) were eligible for CVD risk assessment on 31st March 2018. Overall, 67.1% of eligible men (88.9% of those with diabetes) and 65.5% of women (86.3% of those with diabetes) had tests required for a CVD risk assessment between 1 April 2018 and 31 March 2023. This proportion varied by ethnicity, from 62.3% among Māori to 65.7% among European, 69.8% Other, 69.9% Pacific and 80.3% among Indian Peoples. The odds of CVD risk assessment after adjustment for socioeconomic deprivation and residential district were lower for Māori men (adjusted OR, aOR, 0.77 (0.72–0.83) with diabetes, 0.73 (0.72–0.74) without diabetes) and women (aOR 0.93 (0.87–0.99) with diabetes, 0.89 (0.87–0.91) without diabetes) compared with Europeans. Pacific men also had significantly lower odds of CVD risk assessment compared with Europeans (aOR 0.86 (0.78–0.94) with diabetes, 0.72 (0.70–0.74) without diabetes), as did Pacific women without diabetes (aOR 0.95 (0.92–0.98)). Indian people with and without diabetes had significantly higher odds (aOR 1.2–1.7) of CVD risk assessment compared with Europeans.ConclusionsWe found Māori and Pacific Peoples experienced inequities in CVD risk assessment. There is a critical need for strategies that eliminate inequities for these underserved populations.

Background

InAotearoa New Zealand, inequities in the burden of cardiovascular disease (CVD) for Māori (the Indigenous people of Aotearoa New Zealand), Pacific and South Asian peoples are well recognised [1]. Māori, Pacific and Indian peoples are more likely to develop diabetes and CVD than Europeans [2, 3]. Compared to their European counterparts, Māori and Pacific Peoples have a much higher prevalence of CVD risk factors such as smoking and high blood pressure [2, 4–6], are more likely to develop heart failure [7], and are twice as likely to die from coronary heart disease [8, 9]. Coronary heart disease is the most important contributor to avoidable deaths in Māori and Pacific Peoples, contributing up to one year of their approximately 6–7 year reduction in life expectancy compared to non-Māori non-Pacific Peoples [10]. Indian men have the highest prevalence of coronary heart disease of all ethnic groups [2], but their mortality rate from ischaemic heart disease is intermediate to that of Europeans and Māori/Pacific Peoples [9].

The reasons for these inequities are complex. Compared to Europeans, Māori and Pacific Peoples have lower median incomes, are more likely to live in areas of high deprivation and leave school without formal qualifications [11, 12]. South Asians, on the other hand, have lower consumption of fruits and vegetables and are less physically active than their European counterparts [13] but are more likely to have a tertiary qualification [14]. Māori, Pacific and South Asian peoples are all more likely to experience racism and discrimination compared to their European counterparts [15]. These wider determinants of health impact opportunities to engage in health-promoting activities and to access health care in a timely fashion.

International CVD primary prevention guidelines [16, 17], including those in Aotearoa New Zealand [1], recommend that decisions regarding the use of cardiovascular preventive medications among people without CVD should be informed by absolute CVD risk. CVD risk assessment is generally undertaken by primary care providers in Aotearoa New Zealand, but there are known barriers to accessing primary care services, including insufficient general practitioners, especially in some rural areas, leading to long wait times [18]. Costs of primary care services are also a barrier to access for many [18].

Equity of CVD risk assessment in Aotearoa New Zealand was found to be poor in a 2016 national report [19]. CVD risk assessment coverage estimates were based on primary care enrolment [19–21], which itself is incomplete and inequitable. For example, in April 2019, it was estimated that 93% of the total population were enrolled with primary care, with enrolment at 89% among Māori [22]. That estimate used a Health Service User (HSU) cohort, comprised of individuals who had accessed a health care service and had been assigned a National Health Index, a unique HSU identifier [23]. It is now possible to construct more complete population cohorts, drawing on linked administrative sources from across health and other sectors using Statistics New Zealand’s Integrated Data Infrastructure (IDI) [23]. Health researchers using IDI, HSU and Census-based cohorts to construct total resident population denominators for 2013 (estimated at 4,414,287, 4,266,789 and 4,242,051, respectively) concluded, after comparing the cohorts by age, gender, ethnicity, area-level deprivation and District Health Board, that the IDI population was the most comprehensive [23]. In this study, we have combined IDI and HSU populations to increase coverage by either alone. As CVD risk assessment requires publicly funded laboratory testing for both lipids and glucose (as well as renal function for people with diabetes) and claims for these tests are maintained nationally, the completion of relevant tests can be used as a proxy for CVD risk assessment. The aim of this study was to determine whether there are inequities in CVD risk assessment among people without established CVD who are eligible for CVD risk assessment in Aotearoa New Zealand, in a more complete national cohort than has been used to date.

Methods

Cohort

A de-identified national cohort of the resident population at 31 March 2018 was created by identifying people in either IDI and/or HSU cohorts.

In order to be included in the national cohort for this study, individuals were required to meet at least one of the following three criteria: (1) Identified within the IDI Spine and appeared one or more times in at least one of the following data sources: tax, education, and injury claims between 1 April 2017 and 31 March 2018; OR (2) Identified as having interacted with health services based on documentation in at least one of the following health datasets between 1 April 2017 and 31 March 2018: general medical subsidy claims, community laboratory test claims, the national non-admitted patient collection, the community pharmaceutical collection, and the publicly funded hospitalisation discharge collection; OR (3) Included in Primary Health Organisation enrolment data submitted between 1 April 2017 and 31 March 2018; or included in the Primary Health Organisation enrolment data submitted subsequently (1 April 2018 to 30 September 2018) but with a last consultation date or enrolment date on or before 31 March 2018.

In addition, individuals were also required to meet all of the following criteria in order to be retained in the national cohort: (1) Present in Aotearoa New Zealand for more than 6 months between 1 April 2017 and 31 March 2018 according to migration data; AND (2) alive on 31 March 2018 (according to the national mortality database, further information about this and all other national collections available from: https://www.tewhatuora.govt.nz/for-health-professionals/data-and-statistics/nz-health-statistics/national-collections-and-surveys/collections); AND (3) aged between 25 and 74 years on 31 March 2018. People with missing sex were also excluded at this stage (n = 90).

Once the national cohort had been created, study-specific exclusion and inclusion criteria were applied. Individuals with a history of CVD were excluded. Individuals were considered to have a history of CVD if, prior to 31 March 2018, they met at least one of the following criteria: hospital admission coded with a diagnosis of coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease or haemorrhagic stroke; hospital admission coded with a procedure related to coronary heart disease or peripheral vascular disease; or dispensing of at least three anti-anginal medications in the preceding 5 years.

Then inclusion required the individual to meet criteria for CVD risk assessment according to the 2013 guidelines [24]. These guidelines recommend a CVD risk assessment from the age of 35 years for men and 45 years for women who were of Māori, Pacific or South Asian ethnicity, and from 10 years later for men and women of other ethnic groups, up to the age of 74 years. People with diabetes were required to have a CVD risk assessment from the date of diagnosis (i.e. irrespective of their age, sex or ethnicity). The recommended frequency of CVD risk assessment ranged from annually (for those with diabetes and/or high CVD risk) through to 5–10 yearly for those with low CVD risk.

CVD risk assessment

CVD risk assessment requires laboratory testing for both lipids and glucose (as well as renal function for people with diabetes). The National Laboratory Claims Collection was used to assess whether at least one claim had been submitted for each individual for each of the following tests in the 5-year period from 1 April 2018 to 31 March 2023: lipid (laboratory test code BL0 or BL4), glucose or glycosylated haemoglobin (BG2, BG3, BG5, BG0), renal function (BR1, BR8, BR9, BR0, BP7, BP8). If all required laboratory testing had been conducted at least once in the 5 years between 1 April 2018 and 31 March 2023, individuals were classified as having had CVD risk assessment for this study; otherwise, they were classified as not having had CVD risk assessment.

Variables

Ethnicity was defined for each individual using a single data source; the first available of the following: Census, Ministry of Health, Statistics New Zealand (source ranked ethnicity [25]). For those in whom more than one ethnic group (multi-ethnic) was identified (n= 266,370), a prioritisation rule was used to assign an individual to one ethnic group. This prioritisation rule was based on the national ethnicity data protocols and prioritised groups in the following order: Māori > Pacific > Indian (including Fijian Indian) > European > Other (all non-specified ethnic groups, including the 7,104 people with no ethnicity recorded) [26]. Indians are currently the only South Asian group that can be distinguished in Aotearoa New Zealand routinely collected health data; they comprise 90% of the total South Asian group, but non-Indian South Asians cannot be disaggregated at present from the Other group.

Socioeconomic status was measured using the area-based New Zealand Index of Deprivation (NZDep2018) [27]. This is generally based on small areas of 30–60 dwellings (around 60–120 residents). Regions, from north to south of the country (and their associated districts), are as follows: Northern (Northland, Waitematā, Auckland, Counties Manukau), Te Manawa Taki (Waikato, Lakes, Bay of Plenty, Tairāwhiti Gisbourne, Taranaki), Central (Whanganui, Capital Coast and Hutt Valley, Hawkes Bay, Mid Central, Wairarapa), Te Waipounamu (Nelson Marlborough, Canterbury, South Canterbury, West Coast, Southern). Individuals with missing deprivation or region were assigned a separate categorisation and are included in the modelling results.

History of diabetes was defined using the Ministry of Health algorithm for the Virtual Diabetes Register [28]. According to this algorithm, individuals were considered to have a history of diabetes if they met at least one of the following criteria up to 31 March 2018: hospital admission coded with diabetes; outpatient attendance for diabetes or diabetes retinal screening; prescriptions of specific antidiabetic therapies; laboratory orders for diabetes management.

Statistical analyses

Categorical data were summarised as frequencies and percentages. This was a snapshot of the Aotearoa New Zealand resident population eligible for cardiovascular assessment aged between 25 and 74 years on the 31 st March 2018. However, it is important to note that Māori and Pacific populations may be underrepresented in national administrative data [29]. Associations between ethnicity (with adjustment for sex, NZ deprivation quintile, district and diabetes status) and CVD risk assessment were investigated using univariable and multivariable logistic regression. The descriptive summary suggested a possible interaction between history of diabetes and ethnicity, so an interaction term (ethnicity x history of diabetes) was added to the multivariable logistic models.

Results

A total of 2,886,939 (including 402,213 [13.9%] Māori) Aotearoa New Zealand residents aged 25–74 years at 31 March 2018 were identified for potential inclusion in the study (Fig. 1). This total represented 32,310 people additional to the corresponding total identified from the Census (2,854,649 total of whom 377,610 (13.2%) were Māori). From this total, 1,476,747 people were identified as being eligible for CVD risk assessment at 31 March 2018. Overall, 4.2% were excluded due to a history of CVD (European 4.7%, Indian 3.3%, Māori 4.6%, Other 1.7%, Pacific 4.0%). Of people eligible for CVD risk assessment, a third were from the Northern region and 15.5% were Māori, 6.7% Pacific, 4.5% Indian, 8.1% Other and 65.2% European. The regional and ethnic distribution was similar for men and women. There was a slight difference in the distribution of socioeconomic deprivation by sex (Tables 1 and 2), with a higher proportion of women than men in the highest deprivation quintile (20.4% vs 19.5%). Overall, 67.1% of men (88.9% of those with diabetes) and 65.5% of eligible women (86.3% of those with diabetes) had CVD risk assessed according to laboratory testing data between 1 April 2018 and 31 March 2023. Among men, this proportion varied by ethnicity from 61.3% of Māori up to 66.9% Pacific, 67.4% European, 68.8% Other and 78.9% Indian (Table 1). While the proportion of men with diabetes assessed for CVD risk was higher than for men overall, variation by ethnicity was evident (Māori 85.1%, European 88.4%, Pacific 90.1%, Other 91.7%, Indian 94.2%). Similarly, amongst women, the proportion CVD risk assessed varied by ethnicity (European 63.2%, Māori 63.7%, Other 71.4%, Pacific 74.2%, Indian 82.5%). CVD risk assessment among women with diabetes was higher than for women overall, though variation by ethnicity was also evident (Māori 83.7%, European 84.5%, Other 88.9%, Pacific 89.7%, Indian 92.0%) (Table 2). Discussion

A total of 1,476,747 people (40.5% women, 12.1% with diabetes) were eligible for CVD risk assessment according to a national cohort of the resident population at 31 March 2018, identified in either IDI and/or HSU cohorts. Overall, 67.1% of eligible men (88.9% of those with diabetes) and 65.5% of women (86.3% of those with diabetes) subsequently had tests required for a CVD risk assessment between 1 April 2018 and 31 March 2023. This proportion varied by ethnicity, and the odds of CVD risk assessment were lower for Māori men (adjusted OR, aOR, 0.77 (0.72–0.83) with diabetes, 0.73 (0.72–0.74) without diabetes) and women (aOR 0.93 (0.87–0.99) with diabetes, 0.89 (0.87–0.91) without diabetes) compared with Europeans. Pacific men also had significantly lower odds of CVD risk assessment compared with Europeans (aOR 0.86 (0.78–0.94) with diabetes, 0.72 (0.70–0.74) without diabetes) as did Pacific women without diabetes (aOR 0.95 (0.92–0.98)). Indian people with and without diabetes had significantly higher odds (aOR 1.2–1.7) of CVD risk assessment compared with Europeans.

Across all ethnic groups, the proportion of people eligible for CVD risk assessment who were subsequently risk assessed according to laboratory testing data was greater among those with diabetes than those without. This may be due to the earlier and more rigorous implementation of policy and programme interventions for people in Aotearoa New Zealand with diabetes [19]. Disparities in CVD risk assessment by ethnicity, particularly lower rates of CVD risk assessment amongst Māori and Pacific Peoples highlighted in the 2016 report, have persisted [19]. This is consistent with other components of the CVD care continuum. After adjusting for demographic and clinical factors, Māori and Pacific Peoples presenting with acute coronary syndrome are less likely to receive angiography and revascularisation [30].

It is unclear as to why Indians had higher levels of CVD risk assessment than Europeans. One factor may be their migration status, with 76% of Indians across all ages overseas-born, compared with 17% of Europeans (Māori 2%, Pacific 34%) [14]. Migration is associated with factors that can both facilitate access to health services (e.g. an increase in the proportion of people with qualifications, including tertiary education) and act as barriers to access (e.g. reduced knowledge of the Aotearoa New Zealand health sector). The results for Indians in this study are consistent with selected 2011–2012 New Zealand Health Survey data based on self-report that indicated that South Asians were more likely than the reference category (Europeans and Others) to have cholesterol and diabetes testing, although they were no more likely to have blood pressure testing [13].

CVD risk assessment was included as a national health target in Aotearoa New Zealand between 2012 and 2016, during which time CVD risk assessment was reported to have increased from 46.0% up to 90.5% [31]. In 2016, as the target of 90% had been achieved, this measure moved from being a national health target to being a District Health Board accountability measure [31]. While no more recent national data are currently publicly available, one District Health Board (Counties Manukau Health) reported that in 2021/2022, 77% of those eligible for CVD risk assessment in the preceding 5 years had received it, ranging from 70% for Māori to 76% for Pacific and 80% for Other (mainly European) ethnic groups (data were not reported separately for Indians) [32]. Counties Manukau Health attributed the decline in CVD risk assessment in their district to the impact of COVID-19.

While it is encouraging to see the higher level of CVD risk assessment and attenuation of some ethnic inequities among people with diabetes (compared with those without diabetes), Māori continue to have reduced access to CVD risk assessment compared with Europeans. Pacific men also have substantially lower odds of receiving a CVD risk assessment (15–30%). National diabetes strategies and programmes are likely to have contributed to higher levels of CVD risk assessment overall, but these findings align with other evidence that a generic approach to health risk assessments is limited [33]. More complex interventions to address structural, social and personal causes of ethnic inequities, including improving access to primary care, are required to meet legislative requirements [34] to achieve equitable health care.

This study had a number of strengths and limitations. The large difference between the proportion of eligible individuals CVD risk assessed based on the reported national health target (90.5% in 2016) and our study (67% of men, 66% of women) is likely to primarily reflect differences in the denominator used. The health targets’ denominator was restricted to health care users (those enrolled in a primary health organisation in particular), whereas our denominator was broader, maximising capture of those living in Aotearoa New Zealand within the study period, irrespective of their use of health care services. The other differences between methods used were that for the health targets a confirmed complete CVD risk assessment was required, whereas our study used lab claims data as a proxy for having had a CVD risk assessment, which will overall tend to overestimate the level of CVD risk assessment. While the combination of tests included is likely to be consistent with a primary care clinician ordering tests contributing to CVD risk assessment, we cannot be sure that this is what was intended or if it was eventually used by the primary care clinician as a CVD risk assessment with appropriate follow up. On the other hand, while data on laboratory testing is likely to be mostly complete given the requirement to submit the data for reimbursement for these publicly funded tests, some instances of laboratory testing may have been missed (either not recorded or mis-recorded). Diabetes status was based on an administrative data-based definition, rather than a clinical diagnosis.

The much broader denominator used in the study is a key strength as it better reflects the health needs of the population that is meant to be served by the health service, as opposed to just reflecting those already accessing the health service. This broader population perspective was made possible by conducting this study in the IDI, which also enabled the use of Census data for ethnicity, known to be of higher quality than health sector ethnicity data [35]. Health system targets and indicators for population-based initiatives, such as CVD risk assessment, that are restricted to health care-user denominators should consider similarly expanding their denominators to more accurately reflect actual access and inequities in access to those initiatives.

While the use of prioritised ethnicity and a limited number of ethnic groups simplified the analysis, this meant we were unable to account for the approximately 10% of people who self-identified with more than one ethnic group, or for the heterogeneity within ethnic groups (e.g., Pacific). Further, the “Other” group includes a broad range of ethnic groups, including non-Indian South Asians who are at increased risk of CVD and would ideally have been included in a separate category with Indian individuals. The use of prioritisation will not influence the estimates by ethnicity if the null hypothesis is true, i.e. no difference in CVD assessment by ethnicity. However, the prioritisation is a form of aggregation and simplification, so it has the potential to lead to an underestimate of inequity.

Another strength of this study is the stratification of results by sex and diabetes status and adjustment for district and deprivation. However, there are other factors that are likely to have a bearing on CVD risk assessment. For example, distance to a primary care clinician, ease of getting a primary care appointment, and the ability of the primary care clinician to offer an appropriately supportive environment rather than a hostile, dictatorial or judgemental environment. Other potentially important factors include the costs of accessing care, insecure employment, experience of discrimination and racism, and impacts of colonisation and ongoing marginalisation in a largely western-centric health system [36]. Finally, while only a single claim during the 5-year follow-up period for each required laboratory test was used to classify individuals as having had a CVD risk assessment, that 5-year period (1 April 2018 to 31 March 2023) included the COVID-19 pandemic, during which access to non-pandemic health care (including CVD risk assessment) was likely to have been inequitably disrupted [37, 38], which may have been an additional contributor to our findings.

Conclusions

This study found that Māori and Pacific Peoples experienced inequities in CVD risk assessment. While health providers generally have limited ability to change the social determinants of health, it is well within our remit to ensure that people with the greatest health needs are able to access high-quality, responsive, and culturally safe health care services. Such services must also ensure that patients and their family are supported to manage their own CVD risk and/or chronic condition(s) with confidence [39]. Currently, there is still work to do to ensure CVD risk assessment is being used to its full potential by everyone.
