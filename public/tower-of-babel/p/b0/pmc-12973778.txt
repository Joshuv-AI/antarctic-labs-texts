Confirming and notifying death: blended learning acceptability among medical students, a pilot mixed-methods study
Authors: Daphné Prieur-Drevon, Maximilien Abel, Johanna Laplace, Benoît Gardelle, Laurent Calvel, Catherine Lamouille-Chevalier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYoung doctors confronted with death confirmation and death notification experience practical and emotional difficulties. The medical curriculum does not provide any specific training on these tasks. On clinical placements, exposure to death is rare and late. Death notification conditions the relatives’ grieving process. The aim of this study was to explore the perceived acceptability of specific training on these topics by students in their last year of medical school. The participants followed a specifically designed e-learning course that provided theoretical elements, then participated in a simulation session on death notification. In this mixed-methods study, acceptability was explored quantitatively using self-questionnaires before and after the training, and then qualitatively through focus groups. Fourteen students were included. The study showed a significant improvement in self-efficacy as well as a positive impact on the seven components of acceptability. The students emphasized the complementary nature of the teaching methods and the unexpected contributions to interprofessional skills. The participants were pleased with this teaching approach and the topics covered. They expressed a need for more teaching on interpersonal skills and emotion management, which is currently neglected by the medical school curriculum. Clinical experience insufficiently enriches the curriculum, which could benefit from more mentoring and interprofessional collaboration. This innovative theoretical and practical training is perceived as acceptable by students and deserves to be enhanced and made permanent. Future work should explore its impact on clinical practice.KEYWORDS: Death, determination of death, pedagogy, teaching methods, simulation training, communication, bereavement

Introduction

Most doctors are confronted with their patients’ death in their medical careers, sometimes during their studies [1,2]. In France, death certification is a medical responsibility [3]. Doctors are often in charge of notifying the death to the patient’s relatives.

Currently, all students follow mandatory palliative care theoretical training, but it does not include specific training on death confirmation or death notification, nor is it addressed in other modules in the curriculum, such as forensic medicine or intensive care. Confrontation with death comes late in medical studies. Residents feel insufficiently prepared for these tasks. Both practical and emotional difficulties are reported to be significantly correlated [1,4]. These hardships are particularly linked to a feeling of lack of exposure to dying patients, lack of preparation through theoretical courses, insufficient knowledge on administrative procedures and a lack of communication skills to notify families [1,4–6].

For families receiving the news, how the notification goes conditions the grieving process: if the information is poorly delivered, the risk of complicated grief increases [7,8]. Relatives are affected by the contents as well as the form of the message they have been delivered [8,9]. The desire to provide appropriate and respectful care for the deceased and their families calls for an improvement in teaching on these topics.

No type of teaching intervention has proven to be superior to others when teaching palliative or end-of-life care [10]. To ensure adequate teaching impact, constructive alignment must be strongly considered [10,11]. The association of different interventions, including simulation, could be beneficial to develop communication skills [12–14].

This study aims to explore the relevance of specific training during medical school. The main objective was to assess the perceived acceptability of a blended-learning program on death confirmation and death notification by students in their last year of medical school.

Materials and methods

Study design

This longitudinal monocentric study used a mixed methods research strategy incorporating both quantitative data from questionnaires and qualitative data through focus groups.

Participants

Students from the Faculty of Medicine, Midwifery and Health Sciences in Strasbourg, in their final year of medical school, just before starting the specialization course (or third cycle of French medical studies), were eligible. Other eligibility criteria were being 18 years of age or older, having taken the National Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) in May 2024 (required to become a resident), and never completed a palliative care rotation.

The research team contacted students through student representatives and via their university email. If volunteering and eligible, students were included after further information and signature of a consent form.

Study setting

Participants undertook a blended-learning competency-based teaching module. The instructional design approach is available in Appendix 1. First, they followed an asynchronous e-learning session on death confirmation. It involves legislation, clinical examination and pacemaker removal. It also provided basic elements on death notification.

A few days later, they took part in a 3.5-h session at the simulation center based within the university hospital.

Three scenarios focusing on death notification were conducted in a ward-like consultation room with a separate observation room. Each scenario involved a doctor (played by a student) notifying death to one or two simulated relatives (student or professional healthcare practitioner, depending on the role) and was supported by a facilitator nurse (professional ward nurse).

The first scenario involved notifying an expected death over the phone. The other scenarios involved delivering the news in person of an unexpected death, to shocked relatives in the second scenario and to angry relatives in the last scenario. A debriefing session followed each situation. The debriefing session started with a descriptive phase gathering the reactions and feelings of the participants in the simulation and their summary of the scenario in one sentence. Then the analysis phase enabled participants, observers and teachers to analyze the situation in detail and develop their reflective skills. Finally, the synthesis phase highlighted the positive points, the areas of improvements and key theoretical points at stake in the scenario.

From participant inclusion to the end of data collection, this study was conducted from June 6th, 2024 to July 3rd, 2024. It took place after medical students finished their final examinations and 4 months before starting residency.

Variables

The primary endpoint was the acceptability of the teaching program, explored through the seven component constructs of Sekhon’s theoretical framework [15]. Sekhon defines acceptability as ‘a multi-faceted construct that reflects the extent to which people delivering or receiving a healthcare intervention consider it to be appropriate, based on anticipated or experiential cognitive and emotional responses to the intervention’ [15].

The seven component constructs are described as: burden (the perceived amount of effort required to participate in the intervention), affective attitude (how an individual feels about the intervention), ethicality (the extent to which the intervention has good fit with an individual’s value system), intervention coherence (the extent to which the participant understands the intervention and how it works), opportunity costs (the extent to which benefits, profits or values must be given up to engage in the intervention), perceived effectiveness (the extent to which the intervention is perceived as likely to achieve its purpose), and self-efficacy (the participants’ confidence that they can perform the behaviors required to participate in the intervention). This framework considers acceptability in its entirety, without restricting it to an analysis of satisfaction alone.

The secondary outcome was the evolution of thanatophobia. As medical students are not often confronted with death during their studies, this explores whether students feel that training on these topics has an impact on caring for patients at the end of life.

Data collection methods and measurements

In this pilot project, combining both quantitative and qualitative methods enabled several assessment levels of the students’ perception: anticipated and retrospective acceptability, the evaluation of the intervention’s delivery process and the identification of possible improvements.

Anonymized questionnaires filled out on the e-learning platform at two different times measured quantitative variables. Pre-teaching questionnaires collected demographic data as well as previous exposure to death confirmation, items on anticipated acceptability and items on thanatophobia. After completing the course, the students filled questionnaires on acceptability and thanatophobia.

The post-simulation questionnaire included the same items along with items related to satisfaction and the full institutional simulation feedback questionnaire.

The acceptability questionnaire was composed of 7 Likert-scale items ranging from 0 to 10, exploring the component constructs of acceptability, inspired by Sekhon’s theory-informed questionnaire [16].

Thanatophobia was explored with the Thanatophobia Scale Instrument (TSI), a validated scale composed of 7 Likert-scale items ranging from 1 to 7, to measure healthcare professionals’ attitudes towards caring for dying patients [17].

Focus groups, guided by a constructivist/interpretivist paradigm, took place the day after the simulation, following an interview guide to gather qualitative data on acceptability component constructs, thanatophobia and improvement ideas. This method is known to generate a synergistic group effect [18]. Two of the researchers conducted the focus groups, one as the moderator, the other as an observer and note-taker. The focus groups were audio-recorded on two voice recorders. These researchers also carried out the teaching session and took part in other faculty lessons, so they may have already met some of the participants.

Both the focus group guide and the surveys are available as supplementary materials in the appendix.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis of the quantitative data was performed. A paired sample Student’s t-test was used to analyze the differences in the quantitative variables pre- and post-training (all students as one sample were compared before and after the intervention), using R software version 4.3.1. In this observational pilot study, sample size determination was not performed, but 2 groups of 8–10 students were expected to test the simulation.

Data processing included transcribing verbatim and de-identifying each focus group prior to analysis.

Two distinct analysts, using a thematic approach inspired by grounded theory, performed qualitative analysis separately to minimize researcher bias. They examined, coded and grouped data into themes using MAXQDA 2024. Divergences were discussed and resolved by consensus.

If participants left the study after filling in the questionnaires but before the focus group, the analysis included the quantitative data.

Ethical considerations

Our Institutional Review Board approved the study (Strasbourg Ethical Committee of the Faculties of Medicine, Odontology, Pharmacy, Nursing schools, Physiotherapy and Midwifery. Reference number CE-2024-52).

Reporting

This work follows the ICMJE recommendations and reporting guidelines for observational studies (STROBE checklist) [19] and qualitative studies (SRQR tool) [20].

Quantitative analysis

Impact on acceptability

Affective attitude was reported positively, with students feeling good about the intervention before the study (mean of 8.29/10 on a Lickert-scale ranging from 0: very badly to 10: very good), and after (M = 9.00/10).

Students estimated the effort required to participate at a mean of 4.79/10 before the study. After the teaching session, they evaluated the cognitive effort needed at 4.29/10 and the global burden at a mean of 2.57/10.

Participants’ self-efficacy significantly increased after the training in skills relating to both death confirmation and death notification (Figure 2). No subgroup analysis could be performed between students who had previous exposure to these tasks and those who did not, because of the small sample size.

Evolution of students’ self-efficacy before and after training.

Feedback on intervention coherence and perceived effectiveness showed results positively in agreement at more than 90% with all items in relation to these component constructs.

Evolution of thanatophobia

Before the intervention, participants reported thanatophobia at 15.2 (mean, SD = 8.86) on the TSI. After the training session, there was no significant difference in the global score of thanatophobia (M = 11.7, SD = 9.34, p = 0.99) or any of its components.

Qualitative findings

‘You don’t actually get any training in this at university. I mean, it’s very theoretical and it’s never practical’

Various factors explained the involvement in this teaching. The participants felt like they lacked preparation through their theoretical and clinical training to perform death notification and death confirmation. To improve their skills and reduce their apprehension at the prospect of carrying out these tasks when they become residents motivated them to participate.

On clinical placements, the participants reported little to none exposure to confirming or notifying death. When they manage to perform these tasks, they often have a bad experience or witness the difficulty of the resident performing the tasks. Students regretted a lack of mentoring, with the absence of a debriefing space afterward, or the lack of efficient role models.

At university, even though national examinations now include OSCEs, students noticed the persistent lack of practical training on communication skills and lack of instructions for these tasks.

​​​​​​‘I thought the e-learning was well done and interactive. It wasn’t too long’[about the simulation]: ‘Once I was in the room, I don’t think I ever thought “there’s the camera”’

This multimodal teaching was acceptable to students.

Burden was low, as the intervention occurred after participants finished their university obligations. They did not have to give up other opportunities. Although this training might represent an additional constraint in an already dense curriculum, the interest shown surpasses this constraint. Motivated students would make themselves available at any time during the year, making it necessary to maintain this intervention on a voluntary basis.

Students maintained a positive affective attitude all along. They were enthusiastic beforehand. During the simulation session, this persisted thanks to the emotional security provided by briefings and debriefings, by scenarios unfolding in a room separated from observers and by the caring atmosphere. The students felt relieved that the sequence differed from their exam preparation and that it took place some time away from the exams. They were more willing to address these profound topics and were not focused on an assessment checklist.

About ethicality, this teaching was in line with participants’ values. They noted the precaution of warning before any possibly shocking sequence in the e-learning module (pacemaker removal, for example). During the debriefings, professionals shared their experiences. Sometimes expressing diverging strategies enables students to understand various good approaches and to keep what best suited their own vision of care.

‘For two hours you feel like you’re part of a team. Really’

Regarding intervention coherence and perceived effectiveness, the participants considered the intervention as coherent and perceived it as likely to achieve its purpose, as it met their prior expectations. They highlighted the complementarity of teaching methods. There was no redundancy and e-learning provided a valuable basis for simulation exercises.

The participants emphasized the unexpected contributions of interprofessionality. Although they initially imagined notifying death alone, they understood the valuable support of being a two-person team and preparing for the talk. It was their first time having a plurality of professions among teachers (psychologists, nurses, and doctors), which was enriching and essential.

Simulation learning was relevant, regardless of the student’s role. Those playing the physician directly experienced the situation and its management. The observers could analyze and question how they would have led the conversation. Those acting as the patient’s relative felt how the intended and perceived information may differ, and how tone and posture are important. The participants deemed debriefing essential, as everybody shared their experience and feelings. It enabled them to reflect on and improve their clinical performance.

‘I think the day it's going to happen, I'll still be panicked, or it will still make me anxious but I'll say to myself: “Okay, I know I can rely on this”’

Self-efficacy increased after the intervention. It helps participants acquire automatic reflexes for clinical death confirmation. Some suggested adding simulation exercises on death confirmation to practice on mannequins. They now have basic knowledge to help them prepare and conduct death notification and develop interpersonal skills. They stated that it did not affect the emotional difficulties potentially experienced when confronted with death. They expect this moment to remain stressful, without being able to anticipate their own feelings. Being comfortable with the practical side clearly eases these apprehensions.

‘In medicine, we’re never asked how we feel about things’

Without a direct confrontation with a deceased person in this study, the students felt like it did not influence their attitude towards a patient’s death. They were not disappointed because they did not expect any change on this topic, as it was not within the course’s objectives. However, they highlighted the initiation of reflection on this subject, which was never addressed before in their curriculum. The participants requested a specific space in their studies to reflect upon death. They also called for a reflexive module on emotion management as healthcare professionals.

Discussion

Following Sekhon’s recommendation [15], the in-depth analysis of its seven component constructs reveals the perception of an acceptable training program to soon-to-be residents. Its innovative themes and varied modalities were of great interest to the students. The participants felt that they had increased competence and reduced apprehension about carrying out the addressed tasks. This is consistent with other works on death notification teaching either on the phone or in-person, finding that it improves students’ confidence and competence in these tasks [21–23].

Some of these components echo perceptions at the core of motivation: the perception of the task’s value, self-efficacy and the perception of having some control [11]. The higher the students’ motivation is, the greater their performance, their persistence and the quality of their learning [11], hence the importance of maintaining this training as optional.

Medical students remain ambivalent between examination performance and professionalization: though aware of their future work, they do not feel prepared and prioritize knowledge and know-how in an intense curriculum that neglects interpersonal skills and reflexivity. Through vicarious learning, clinical experience should be the opportunity to develop these social skills by observing other professionals [24]. Unfortunately, as shown in this study as well as in others, the lack of mentoring and exposure to proper end-of-life care does not enable this [1,5]. The development of inter-professional learning at universities with simulations and on the wards could be beneficial. Nurses provide valuable guidance for residents on the wards, particularly for end-of-life care [5]. Relatives report feeling more compassion when death is notified by a nurse rather than a doctor, who is seen as less empathetic but able to communicate more about technical information [9].

This training session did not impact thanatophobia. The participants justified it by the absence of exposure to a real or simulated deceased patient. However, before the intervention, the thanatophobia score were already quite low compared to other studies, with Merrill reporting a mean score of 22.7 (SD = 7.1) for fourth-year medical students, 23.7 (SD = 8.6) for doctors [17], or Gryschek reporting at least 21.54 for final-year medical students [25]. This might be due to the difficulty for students to assess how they would feel in a situation they had never faced before. In a curriculum where emotion and reflection upon death have no room, difficulty in end-of-life situations is not easy to accept, let alone express, as other professionals can perceive it as a sign of weakness. It is difficult to expose hardships, linked to either clinical work or personal situations. As medical professionals have a higher risk of burn-out and other psychiatric diseases, mental health remains taboo and stigmatized in this environment [26]. However, it is a major concern, as the prevalence of medical student burn-out is up to 88% in some countries [26]. In France, 66% of medical students and residents went through burn-out in their curriculum, 57% presented with anxiety, 27% with depression, and 21% contemplated suicide [27]. The students expressed a need for more talking space to reflect upon coping with death and emotions. This could be an opportunity to work on how it impacts students and the care they provide.

As observed through this work, even when the topic is death, simulation is useful for improving non-technical skills such as teamwork and verbal or non-verbal communication [28,29]. The debriefings encourage reflective practice, falling within experiential learning [11,30]. This learning model was first described by Kolb as a process in which students learn through experiencing something, reflecting on it and then understanding and transforming this experience [31].

Perspectives

In light of the relevance and perceived acceptability of this training program reported by this pilot study, its sustainability and local extension to a larger number of students is in the works. To offer this training to more students, it is necessary to assess in advance the local feasibility on a practical level. From an economic standpoint, the detachment of medical and paramedical staff from their care activities to supervise the simulation session represents a significant cost [11,32]. The cost of hosting distance learning on Moodle as well as the use of the simulation lab is important and is provided by the University. Organizational constraints include combining the schedules of healthcare professionals providing the simulation of the participating students, as well as the simulation’s lab capacity. An educational program’s feasibility also takes into account the time to grade students, which is not required, as the formative feedback is automated for e-learning through pre–post assessments online, and is included in the simulation debriefings [11,32].

An expansion to other universities as well as other healthcare professionals (nurses and midwives) is also considered.

This study explores the first level of Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model in terms of immediate reaction and satisfaction [33]. It is important to explore the long-term impact of a teaching intervention, especially levels 3 (on behavioral changes) and 4 (impact on clinical practice) [14,33]. For the third level, further studies could compare the first experience of confirming and notifying death for the first time for students who undertook the training versus those who did not. To explore Kirkpatrick’s fourth level, studies could investigate the experience of bereaved relatives regarding the notification by trained participants. Since inter-professional collaboration is key, it could provide a valuable input to assess the team’s experience, depending on whether the physician has been trained or not.

Strengths

Exploring the impact of specific training on death confirmation and notification is innovative. The relevance of using simulation in healthcare studies has been proven, and its ability to learn communication skills is growing [34]. Studies on teaching death notification are scarce in the international literature and evaluate different teaching techniques than those used in this study [22,23].

The training program was constructed following a competency-based approach and best practice recommendations for simulation training [35].

The university approved the program and was involved in its construction. This made it possible to perform a pilot study in a way that would be scalable, taking into account participants’ feedback to improve this module’s quality and increase student adherence to the program.

The choice of focus groups to assess acceptability led to more proactive students and interactive feedback. Two separate investigators carried out the thematic analysis, thus limiting subjectivity and strengthening the analysis. Using quantitative and qualitative methods to explore varied and complementary dimensions provided thorough results.

Weaknesses

The study included volunteer participants, which could constitute a recruitment bias. However, the intention of keeping this teaching on a voluntary basis can quickly overcome this reservation. Additionally, qualitative analysis seeks subjectivity, not representativeness.

With 14 participants for 2 groups, the number was slightly lower than the estimated 8–10 per group. This was due to last-minute cancellations or students being unable to attend part of the course, sometimes due to concurrent compulsory teaching. Nevertheless, it was sufficient for the simulation to run smoothly. When the between-groups difference did not follow a normal distribution, using the Wilcoxon paired test confirmed the significance of the results. It was not possible to carry out an additional statistically significant subgroup analysis, but this would only be for exploratory purposes. There is no missing data.

There could have been a Hawthorne effect, with participants adapting their answers to what they thought the investigators expected (same investigators delivering the intervention and leading the focus groups). As it was part of the improvement process, the investigators specifically asked the students what they disliked or would have preferred. The participants also had the opportunity to provide additional feedback in the anonymized post-training questionnaires, which did not reveal new information.

Conclusion

This innovative blended-learning program focusing on death confirmation and death notification is perceived as acceptable by medical students. It also initiated reflection upon death, upon the emotions students may feel when confronted with a deceased patient and how it affects clinical practice. This resulted in participants calling for more teaching on these themes as well as more mentoring and interprofessional collaboration.

Once enriched with the suggested improvements, this training deserves to be made permanent and offered to more students.

Further research is needed to assess the long-term impact of this program on clinical practice.
