Perceptions of blood volume and iron recovery following a whole‐blood or plasma donation: A cross‐sectional study with donors and non‐donors
Authors: Amanda Thijsen, Rachel Thorpe, Barbara Masser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerceptions of how blood or plasma donation affect the body and recovery experiences drive subsequent donation behavior. This paper aims to describe (1) how donors typically feel after a donation, (2) how long donors and non‐donors believe their bodies need to recover from volume and iron loss, and (3) what activities donors undertake to recover following a whole‐blood (WB) or plasma donation.Study Design and MethodsA survey on blood donation recovery was distributed to WB and plasma donors via email, and to non‐donors online. Comparative analyses (donor type, gender, and age group) were conducted.ResultsThe survey was completed by 632 WB donors, 601 plasma donors, and 294 non‐donors. Most donors (54%) reported feeling no different after their donation, with similar experiences reported by WB and plasma donors. Women and younger donors reported feeling more tired than men and older donors. Two‐thirds believed blood volume recovery occurs within 24 h, with non‐donors estimating longer recovery times. For iron, most (72%) respondents believed their bodies recover within a week, with WB donors and younger donors believing it takes longer. Regarding post‐donation advice, most donors report drinking extra water (75%) and avoiding strenuous exercise (62%), with fewer donors eating iron‐rich foods (16%) or taking iron tablets (6%).DiscussionSome donors and non‐donors over or underestimate post‐donation recovery times. These findings highlight the need for targeted education to improve donor understanding of physiological recovery and promote evidence‐based recovery strategies.Keywords: blood donation, health, iron, non‐donors, plasma, recovery, whole‐blood

INTRODUCTION

Understanding perceptions and experiences of recovery following blood or plasma donation is an important aspect of informing strategies to recruit and retain blood donors. Donor behavior is influenced not only by physical experiences but also by beliefs of the health impacts of donation. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5

Inaccurate knowledge, particularly regarding iron replenishment, may lead to unnecessary concern, changed donation frequency, negative responses to deferral, or inappropriate recovery behaviors. 6 , 7 Non‐donors may be deterred from donating due to inaccurate assumptions about recovery time or adverse effects. 8 , 9 Moreover, differences in perceptions across demographic groups, such as age and gender, can inform tailored communication strategies.

By identifying gaps in accurate knowledge and common recovery practices, blood collection agencies can better support donor health, improve education efforts, and promote evidence‐based recovery strategies such as iron supplementation where they are necessary. 10 The aim of this survey study was to examine donors' and non‐donors' perceptions of recovery after a blood or plasma donation. Specifically, we wanted to (1) determine how whole‐blood (WB) and plasma donors typically feel after a donation, (2) how long donors and non‐donors believe their bodies need to recover from volume and iron loss, and (3) what activities donors undertake to assist their recovery following a donation. We examined differences by donor type, gender, and age group.

METHODS

Study design and procedure

This paper presents a component of a larger cross‐sectional online survey study examining donor and non‐donor beliefs on the impact of blood donation on their mental and physical health (https://osf.io/gcb4e/). Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Australian Red Cross Lifeblood (Lifeblood) Human Research Ethics Committee (2022#17). Data were collected and managed using Qualtrics hosted by Lifeblood.

Eligible donors (n = 10,599) who exclusively donated either WB or plasma were invited to participate via email from November 2022 to February 2023. Donors were eligible if they had donated in the last 7–14 days and had a valid email address. Donors were excluded if they had an active deferral, a registered donor adverse event, or had donated for therapeutic or autologous reasons.

Non‐donors were recruited through the research site Prolific from December 2022 to February 2023. Those who completed the pre‐screen survey and had never donated but were eligible to do so were invited to complete the main questionnaire.

Survey instrument

Donors rated how they typically feel after they have donated blood/plasma (Likert scale: −3 = More tired, 0 = No difference, 3 = More energized). All respondents were then asked how long they think their body needs to recover from (a) the amount of blood/plasma given, and (b) the iron lost in a blood donation. Lastly, donor respondents were asked a multiple‐choice question about what activities they specifically do to help their body recover from a donation, which was developed using Lifeblood's standard post‐donation care advice (https://www.lifeblood.com.au/donors/blood‐plasma‐platelets/making‐a‐donation/prepare‐and‐aftercare#donation‐step‐3) and input from Lifeblood medical experts. All study materials are available on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/gcb4e/).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using Stata (StataCorp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). For descriptive analysis, sample characteristics and survey items were summarized by means (standard deviation) and medians (interquartile range) for continuous variables and by frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Univariate differences between donor type (non‐donor vs. WB donor vs. plasma donor), gender (woman vs. man), and age group (18–45 years vs. 46–80 years) were examined using t‐tests and chi‐square goodness‐of‐fit tests. Statistical significance was defined as p <.05.

RESULTS

A total of 1233 donors responded to our survey (11.6% response rate). The final donor sample included 632 WB donors (53% female; aged 51.9 ± 13.6 years; median 3 WB donations [IQR: 1–8 WB donations]) and 601 plasma donors (62% female; aged 47.5 ± 14.5 years; median 3 plasma donations [IQR: 1–6 plasma donations]). In addition, 294 non‐donors (52% female; aged 30.7 ± 10.4 years) completed the survey.

Experienced physical recovery

Most donors (54%) indicated that they typically felt no different after they had donated WB or plasma (Median: 0, IQR: −1‐0), with 28% reporting feeling more tired (rated as <0) and 18% reporting feeling more energized (rated as >0). Between groups, women reported feeling more tired than men (−0.30 ± 1.46 vs. 0.13 ± 1.24), t(1231) = −5.39, p <.01. Further, younger donors indicated feeling more tired than older donors (−0.32 ± 1.57 vs. −0.01 ± 1.26), t(1231) = −3.69, p <.01. No evidence was found of differences in experienced recovery between WB and plasma donors (p = .42).

Perceived recovery time of volume loss

Two‐thirds of respondents (62%) believed it takes less than 24 h for the body to recover from volume loss (see Figure 1). Non‐donors believed it takes longer to recover than WB and plasma donors, χ2(16) = 111.35, p < .01, with the mid‐point being 1–3 days for non‐donors and 12–24 h for WB and plasma donors. Further, most plasma donors (70%) thought it takes less than 24 h to recover from volume loss compared to 62% of WB donors and 49% of non‐donors. Younger donors believed it takes longer to recover than older donors, χ2(8) = 57.06, p <.01. Notably, 13% of older donors indicated no recovery time required compared with just 4.6% of younger donors. No evidence was found of differences by gender (p = .12) in perceived recovery time.

Perceived recovery time of iron loss

Perceived recovery time of iron loss was only asked of respondents who had indicated earlier in the survey that lowering of iron levels is a health impact of blood donation (n = 680; 44.5%). This health belief was significantly more endorsed by non‐donors than donors (non‐donors: 217 [73.8%] vs. WB donors: 325 [51.4%] vs. plasma donors: 138 [23.0%]), χ2(2) = 227.38, p <.01, by women more than men (405 [46.9%] vs. 268 [40.9%]), χ2(1) = 5.46, p <.02, and by younger donors aged 18–45 years more than older donors aged 46–80 years (405 [46.9%] vs. 268 [40.9%]), χ2(1) = 78.00, p <.01. Of the respondents who believed that lowering iron levels is an impact of blood donation, most (71.9%) believed it takes less than a week for the body to recover from the iron lost, with a third (32.3%) believing it takes less than 24 h. An overview of perceived recovery time of iron loss by donor type can be found in Figure 2. WB donors believed it takes longer to recover from iron loss than non‐donors and plasma donors, χ2(16) = 38.51, p <.01, with the mid‐point being 3–7 days for WB donors compared to 1–3 days for non‐donors and plasma donors. Looking at age, younger donors believed it takes longer to recover from iron loss than older donors, χ2(8) = 24.80, p <.01, with the mid‐point being 3–7 days for younger donors compared to 1–3 days for older donors. No evidence was found for gender differences in perceived recovery time (p = .40).

Significant differences were found between WB and plasma donors in recovery activities, with more WB donors indicating taking iron tablets, χ2 (1) = 9.13, p <.01, and eating iron‐rich foods, χ2 (1) = 4.20, p = .04, than plasma donors. Looking at gender, all recovery activities significantly differed between the two groups, with a greater proportion of women reporting recovery activities than men (all p's <.05). On the other hand, a larger proportion of men than women indicated that they do not undertake any recovery activities, χ2 (1) = 12.33, p <.01. Finally, looking at age group differences, more younger donors indicated engaging in all of the recovery activities than older donors (all p's <.05), except for avoiding rushing around and avoiding standing up for long periods (both p's >.05). Further, more older donors reported not undertaking any recovery activities than younger donors, χ2 (1) = 22.49, p < .01.

DISCUSSION

Our study provides new insights into perceived and experienced recovery following a donation among donors and non‐donors. We found that most donors reported feeling no different after donating WB or plasma, but more than a quarter reported feeling more tired. The rate of fatigue is at the higher end of the 3%–24% reported in research to date. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 We also observed similar gender differences, with women feeling more tired than men. 3 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Reduced blood volume and hemoglobin loss are believed to contribute to tiredness following a donation, 15 although recent evidence does not support an association with iron status. 14 Further, we found that respondents donating plasma also reported tiredness (29%), with no significant differences found between the two donation groups, suggesting other factors may be at play. More research is needed to understand what contributes to tiredness following blood or plasma donation to design strategies to minimize this following a donation.

Similar to other studies, 16 , 17 , 18 we found limited accurate knowledge of the effects of blood donation on iron health. Only 51% of our surveyed WB donors believed lowering of iron levels is an impact of donation compared to 74% of non‐donors and 23% of plasma donors. Further, we found that 72% of respondents believe it takes less than a week for their body to recover from iron lost in a donation, which is far less than the actual recovery time of approximately 6 months for WB. 19 , 20 It is important to address this belief among donors to reduce their risk of low iron and ensure they are equipped to manage their iron health appropriately. Furthermore, we found limited use of iron recovery activities, such as taking iron supplements or eating an iron‐rich diet, among our surveyed donors. The recent introduction of routine ferritin testing by Lifeblood 21 is an important step to educate donors about their iron health. However, more research is needed to identify how to deliver this information to increase donor knowledge and use of potential mitigation strategies. 10

In line with Lifeblood's post‐donation care advice, most of our surveyed donors prioritize hydration after a donation. Drinking extra water following a donation helps to restore blood volume and minimize the risk of dizziness and fatigue. 22 Other strategies to minimize the risk of dizziness, such as avoiding alcoholic drinks or avoiding rushing around, were less adhered to. More research is needed to determine whether this results from a lack of awareness of these post‐donation strategies, a gap in knowledge as to why this advice is provided, or low motivations to follow this advice.

The limitations of our study include the potential for self‐selection bias as people more concerned with health impacts of blood donation may have been more inclined to participate in this survey. We have no indication, however, of the magnitude and consequences of this bias in this study. Further, in our analysis we focused on age differences only, which may be confounded by donation experience. Future studies could further examine differences in recovery activities and beliefs by donation experience. In addition, for non‐donors, “blood donation” was not distinguished by type and was likely interpreted as WB donation, which may bias their estimated recovery times. Our study is, however, the first to our knowledge to describe perceived and experienced recovery following a WB or plasma donation.
