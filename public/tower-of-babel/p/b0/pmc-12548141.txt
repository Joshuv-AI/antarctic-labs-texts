Revolutionizing sepsis diagnosis using machine learning and deep learning models: a systematic literature review
Authors: Muhammad Zubair, Irfanud Din, Nadeem Sarwar, Botir Elov, Samariddin Makhmudov, Zouheir Trabelsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition resulting from a dysregulated immune response to infection, often leading to organ failure and death. Early detection is vital, as delays significantly worsen outcomes. In recent years, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL), has shown great promise in enhancing early sepsis detection by identifying digital biomarkers from large-scale clinical datasets. This systematic review analyzes and synthesizes existing ML/DL approaches applied to sepsis prediction, with an emphasis on intensive care unit (ICU) settings. A total of 80 studies were included, covering diverse data sources (e.g., MIMIC-III, eICU), feature selection methods, algorithm types, preprocessing techniques, and evaluation metrics. The models ranged from traditional techniques like logistic regression and decision trees to advanced architectures such as LSTM, transformers, and ensemble methods. A key contribution of this review is the inclusion of a forest plot summarizing reported AUC and sensitivity values from selected studies, offering a comparative visual of diagnostic performance. This helps highlight the relative effectiveness of different models and provides insights into their generalizability across clinical datasets. The review also discusses challenges related to model interpretability, ethical considerations, and the lack of external and temporal validation in many studies. It further identifies trends such as the use of real-time EHR data, patient-specific model development, and explainability tools like SHAP for clinician trust. By mapping out methodological strengths and limitations in current research, this work provides actionable recommendations for future studies and clinical deployment. The review contributes to the development of more robust, interpretable, and clinically relevant ML/DL models for early sepsis detection and improved patient care outcomes.

Introduction

Sepsis is a potentially lethal situation induced by the body’s reaction to infection and continues to be one of the great global challenges, most dramatically seen in developing nations [1]. Sepsis is among the leading causes of death in intensive care units (ICUs) worldwide, and its recognition, particularly in the early stages of the disease, remains a medical challenge. The advent of an abundance of available digital health data has created a setting in which ML can be used for digital biomarker discovery, with the ultimate goal to advance the early recognition of sepsis [2]. The synergism between constrained healthcare resources, disparities in the economy, and weak infrastructure increases the incidence of sepsis [3].

In developing countries, the main barriers to timely detection and effective management of sepsis include limited access to healthcare services, lack of essential medical supplies, and insufficient healthcare infrastructure. Socioeconomic factors such as poverty and malnutrition also increase susceptibility to infections, thus amplifying the risk of developing sepsis. Addressing these challenges is important not only in reducing illness and mortality due to sepsis [4, 5]. Sepsis is still a leading cause of mortality in the critically ill patient, especially in the intensive care unit and emergency department. Early detection and timely intervention improve the outcome of the patient. The application of ML models is becoming a promising tool for enhancing the prediction and diagnosis of sepsis through clinical data and advanced algorithms for identifying high-risk patients. The research collection discusses diverse ML techniques: from traditional models, such as Random Forest and Logistic Regression, to more complex approaches, like Convolutional Neural Networks and ensemble methods. It demonstrates potential for improving detection of sepsis, prediction of mortality, and clinical decision-making.

Importance of early diagnosis

Early diagnosis of sepsis is important because the condition progresses very rapidly in a medical emergency. If untreated, it leads to failure of the organs and subsequently death. It is brought about by an inappropriate immune response to an infection, with the associated massive inflammation and tissue destruction, potentially escalating into septic shock life-threatening condition, with severely low blood pressure and multi-organ failure [6]. Timely detection enables healthcare providers to administer life-saving interventions such as antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and supportive therapies, significantly improving patient outcomes.

The research interest among different stakeholders in sepsis reveals the collaborative approach to fighting this life-threatening condition. Institutions account for the highest percentage at 36%, with a focus on furthering theoretical understanding and finding new solutions. Clinical trials amount to 21% in translating research into practice, testing treatments and interventions. Pharmaceutical companies contribute 15% to developing therapies for sepsis-related complications. Laboratory studies, at 13%, deliver critical perceptions into the biological mechanisms of the disease, running embattled research. Patient advocacy groups, making up 9%, raise awareness, influence research priorities, and ensure patient-centred approaches, while policymakers, comprising 7%, shape healthcare policies, funding, and regulatory frameworks to support sepsis management. This distribution in Fig. 1 below reflects a multidimensional effort to combat sepsis, aligning with the challenges and opportunities presented earlier. The involvement of diverse stakeholders, combined with advancements such as ML and digital biomarkers, is crucial for addressing the complex nature of sepsis and improving patient outcomes.

Challenges in sepsis diagnosis

Studies indicate that each hour of delay in treatment increases mortality risk by 7–8%, highlighting the time-sensitive nature of sepsis. Unlike many other diseases, its non-specific symptoms, such as fever and rapid heart rate, make early recognition challenging, yet essential, to prevent catastrophic outcomes and save lives [7, 8]. One major challenge with sepsis is that its underlying processes are not comparable to conditions like inflammation. These conditions share indicators such as altered blood pressure and heart rate, symptoms like fever, and consistent molecular patterns, including immune system imbalance. Due to the unique systemic characteristics of sepsis, the use of biological and molecular indicators (commonly referred to as biomarkers) has been proposed as a potential approach to improve the accuracy of sepsis diagnosis and identification [9, 10]. Although significant efforts have been made to identify suitable indicators, the low sensitivity and specificity have limited the broad acceptance of any single or combined biomarker for diagnosing and treating sepsis [11, 12]. This is despite the many efforts that have been undertaken.

The development of data-driven biomarkers has seen significant progress in recent decades and holds promise for addressing current difficulties. The method aims to analyze and use health-related information (such as patient records or medical data) by using ML techniques that identify patterns and make predictions or decisions based on data. The availability of digital data at high resolution is progressively expanding, catering to those at risk of sepsis and those already suffering from sepsis [13]. The dataset encompasses a comprehensive range of information, including laboratory results, vital signs, genetic data, molecular data, clinical records, and health history. A comprehensive study of digital health markers enabled by flexible data use could replace the traditional narrow focus on evaluating only blood test indicators for health insights.

ML algorithms offer a natural ability to effectively analyze complex and diverse digital patient data to accurately predict the development of sepsis in patients [14, 15]. This is achieved by identifying and using predictive patterns in the data. This allows us to accurately predict who is likely to develop sepsis. The identification of predictive patterns is usually performed using one of two approaches: Supervised Machine Learning or Unsupervised. Supervised learning algorithms are typically used to predict unlabeled data using labelled training data (e.g., whether a patient has sepsis or not) as a source of knowledge. In contrast, unsupervised learning does not involve the process of assigning labels to data. These algorithms are responsible for searching for patterns in the data, both familiar and unfamiliar. Many studies published in the past two decades have used various computer models to effectively predict the development of sepsis at the early detection stage [16, 17]. A proposed detailed framework was introduced called the multi-task Gaussian process recurrent neural network (MGP-RNN), which could be used for end-to-end training and handling of multiple tasks.

Several alternative approaches based on a combination of multi-task Gaussian processes and recurrent neural networks for the proposed system have been discussed. These are methods that indicate very accurate predictions for the onset of sepsis. As such, for instance, antibiotics were prescribed 17 h late, and sepsis only 36 h later [18]. In their paper [19], the authors further proposed that single-task Gaussian processes can be integrated into neural networks. They also introduced the concept of a “Gaussian process adapter,” which can be effectively applied within the end-to-end learning paradigm.

In an earlier study, the substitution of the last fully connected layer with a Gaussian process adapter architecture that includes the usage of dynamic time warping along with a TCN termed MGP-TCN leads to a superior prediction of early detection of sepsis with much higher accuracy [20]. Considering such massive research in this direction along with its huge day-to-day improvement, it’s significant to time and again realize the new state-of-the-art improvements done in this area as well [21].

Recent research on sepsis prediction has increasingly focused on leveraging ML and DL models to enhance early detection and improve patient outcomes. Despite notable progress, several critical challenges persist. Many studies rely on retrospective datasets such as MIMIC-III or eICU, which often lack temporal validation or external generalizability. The heterogeneity of clinical data—ranging from structured (e.g., vitals, lab tests) to unstructured formats (e.g., clinical notes)—also presents integration and preprocessing difficulties. Additionally, the performance of ML/DL models is frequently reported without interpretability, hindering clinical adoption. Issues related to model transparency, ethical considerations, and inconsistent feature engineering further limit real-world implementation.

Research gap

Previous studies on ML/DL for sepsis detection often lack external validation, comprehensive model comparisons, and focus on interpretability. Most reviews cover limited model types and rarely address ethical concerns or include visual performance summaries like forest plots. This review fills these gaps by evaluating a wide range of algorithms, comparing diagnostic performance, discussing explainability tools, and highlighting implementation challenges to support more robust and clinically applicable sepsis prediction models.

Motivation

This study aims to systematically review and synthesize the existing body of work on ML/DL applications for early sepsis detection. Unlike prior reviews that focused narrowly on model types or outcomes, this work provides a comprehensive evaluation of data sources, algorithmic strategies, performance metrics, and clinical relevance. It also introduces a forest plot comparing reported AUC and sensitivity values across models, offering a visual summary of diagnostic performance. Through this structured analysis, the study seeks to inform the design of more robust, interpretable, and generalizable sepsis prediction models suitable for clinical settings.

Research objectives

To explore the types and performance of ML and DL models used for early detection and prediction of sepsis in clinical settings.

To examine the clinical features and variables most frequently used for training predictive models in sepsis diagnosis.

To identify and categorize the key techniques and methodological strategies employed in ML/DL-based sepsis prediction models.

To evaluate the impact of these models on clinical outcomes, including timeliness and accuracy of sepsis diagnosis.

To provide a comparative analysis of ML/DL approaches and highlight current challenges, research gaps, and directions for future model development.

Research questions

The following research questions, which are derived from the stated objectives, need to be addressed to thoroughly investigate and achieve the goals outlined in the study, providing a clear direction for the research process.

RQ1: Which machine and deep learning algorithms have been used for sepsis prediction?

RQ2: How do the algorithms differ concerning predictive accuracy, data requirements, and clinical application?

RQ3: How do various machine and deep learning algorithms compare in predicting sepsis regarding accuracy, methodology, and performance metrics across different datasets?

RQ4: What strategies have been adopted in the existing research to navigate the ethical and data privacy challenges posed by applying machine and deep learning to sepsis prediction using healthcare data?

Systematic review procedure

Search strategy

A multi-pronged strategy was adopted to ensure a comprehensive and systematic exploration of the topic. An extensive literature search was conducted across three reputable academic databases: IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and Scopus. These platforms are well-regarded for their broad coverage of biomedical, technical, and interdisciplinary research. The search yielded 310 articles from IEEE Xplore, 206 from the ACM Digital Library, and 1,011 from Scopus. The effectiveness of the literature search was further enhanced through the strategic use of Boolean operators. Operators such as “AND” and “OR” facilitated the combination of relevant keywords while efficiently excluding unrelated studies [22, 23]. For example, the query “Sepsis AND ML” was used to specifically retrieve articles that addressed the application of machine learning in sepsis detection and diagnosis, thereby filtering out broader or irrelevant content. Additionally, search filters were applied to refine the results based on predefined criteria such as article type, publication date, and language, further improving the relevance and precision of the retrieved literature [24].

To ensure the inclusion of up-to-date research, the review was limited to articles published within the last two decades. This timeframe captures recent developments and aligns with standard review methodologies that enhance relevance through defined publication windows. Study selection criteria

The process of selecting studies for a systematic literature review requires careful attention to ensure both relevance and completeness. This review relied on clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, which played a key role in choosing the articles. For the inclusion criteria, preference was given to significant research studies that explored sepsis prediction using structured and thorough methods. These studies primarily focused on human participants to provide a better understanding of the real-world effectiveness of these algorithms. For example, the paper by [25] focuses on the application of a machine-learning framework for in-hospital mortality prediction for ICU patients with sepsis, demonstrating the potential and flexibility of ML in this domain.

But still, it was important for the review to be fair and relevant. Thus, for these reasons, several sources were excluded. For language considerations, articles in non-English languages were omitted; they may be challenging to interpret and, in turn, affect their accuracy. Further, studies that did not involve ML or DL directly in the context of sepsis prediction, or studies that were not focused on human subjects, were excluded. For instance, research using an ML-based radiomics model in predicting HPV types in cervical cancer [26] provided relevant information, but it was excluded since it does not focus on the interest of the review.

Screening process

The screening process was structured in a step-by-step manner. Titles and abstracts were screened first to filter out irrelevant studies. Then, full-text articles were critically assessed for eligibility based on the criteria. To ensure that the study selection process is reliable and consistent, an inter-rater reliability approach was adopted [27]. Inter-rater reliability corresponds to the agreement among multiple reviewers independently reviewing and classifying the same data or studies. Two separate independent reviewers evaluated two independent reviewers for study inclusion eligibility using predefined criteria in the current review. To reduce bias and ensure accuracy, an agreement between reviewers regarding exclusion/inclusion was sought by taking recourse towards a third reviewer for final resolution of the discrepancies. It will minimize the chances of lowering reliability during the selection of the study. This approach ensures that the selection is both thorough and well-organized, combining structure with teamwork to maintain rigour and completeness [25, 28].

The study selection criteria, planned and guided by a structured methodology, ensured the inclusion of high-quality. The study is relevant to research on sepsis prediction using ML and DL techniques. The criteria were designed to capture well-conducted studies on the clinical application of those algorithms. Each stage, beginning with defining criteria to the final assessments, was carried out maintaining accuracy, consistency, and relevance.

This was done in two steps: the titles and abstracts were reviewed first to exclude irrelevant studies, followed by a detailed full-text assessment to confirm eligibility. This ensured that only relevant and high-quality studies were included.

Inclusion criteria

IC1: Studies using clinical trials and datasets for the validation of the predictive models in sepsis outcomes.

IC2: Studies that have applied machine and DL techniques for sepsis prediction with human subjects.

IC3: Studies that are well-structured and have clinical applicability.

IC4: Studies that were published between 2016 and 2025.

Exclusion criteria

EC1: Studies that do not use structured methodologies and are not based on clinical trials or observational data related to sepsis prediction.

EC2: Studies that are not conducted to practice or do not demonstrate in practice the effectiveness of sepsis prediction models.

EC3: Research that was not specifically conducted on the prediction of sepsis by the machine or DL.

EC4: Researchers without human subjects who were not related to sepsis prediction.

Selection process

The selection process for the SLR started with obtaining articles from three important databases: IEEE, ACM, and Scopus. In total, 310 articles were retrieved using IEEE, 206 using ACM, and 1011 using Scopus. Duplicates are identified and extracted from the dataset, hence removing 600 entries. Articles in Chinese (75) and French (70) have been excluded due to restrictions on the language. After removing duplicates and language-specific articles, there were 782 articles remaining for screening. Articles from the screening stage that were out of date or outdated in their technique were excluded at this stage. 445 articles were removed from the pool of selections at this step. Then, 237 articles were requested to retrieve full texts, and a request was made to retrieve all those articles to examine further. However, 162 could not be retrieved, and the remaining 125 articles were forwarded for eligibility. The remaining articles were assessed for eligibility at the eligibility assessment stage. Of the 125 articles that were evaluated, 45 were excluded. The reasons for exclusion include review article 27, AURAC absent in studies 8, meta-analysis 5, conference paper 3, and book Chap. 2. Following these exclusions, a total of 80 studies remained to be included in the final SLR. This detailed process ensured that only relevant and of high studies meeting the necessary criteria were included in the review.

Types of models used

A range of ML and DL models have been applied to the early detection of sepsis, each offering unique advantages in handling the complexities of clinical data. Commonly employed models include Decision Trees, Random Forests, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and ensemble-based or DL variants. These models are not only selected for their predictive capabilities but also for their adaptability to clinical datasets, such as time-stamped electronic health records (EHRs) [33], missing data structures, and feature interpretability requirements.

Decision trees and random forests

Decision Trees are simple yet effective models that split data based on feature values, functioning like flowcharts. They are well-suited for analyzing both categorical and numerical clinical data but are prone to overfitting on small datasets. Random Forests, an ensemble of multiple Decision Trees, overcome this limitation by aggregating predictions, enhancing accuracy, and capturing nonlinear patterns, making them valuable for sepsis detection from physiological and clinical indicators.

Support Vector Machines (SVMs)

Support Vector Machines are robust supervised learning models effective in high-dimensional spaces. They classify data by identifying an optimal hyperplane, and in nonlinear scenarios, kernel functions (e.g., radial basis function) are used to map data into higher dimensions. SVMs have been applied to sepsis detection by leveraging physiological and biochemical markers. However, they can be computationally intensive, especially with large datasets and when extensive hyperparameter tuning is required.

Logistic regression

Logistic Regression is a widely used statistical model for binary classification, including sepsis prediction. It estimates probabilities using a logistic function and is favoured for its simplicity and interpretability. While effective in linear scenarios, it may not capture complex variable interactions in clinical data, but it remains a reliable baseline in sepsis detection studies.

Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM)

Gradient Boosting Machines are powerful ensemble methods that build decision trees sequentially, with each new tree correcting the errors of the previous one. Variants like XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost are widely used for clinical data involving features such as lab results and vital signs. GBMs offer high accuracy and handle missing data well, but they require careful tuning to prevent overfitting and can be computationally intensive on large datasets.

Neural networks

Neural Networks are well-suited for complex, high-dimensional clinical data. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) capture spatial patterns like vital sign trends, while Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are effective for modelling sequential data in sepsis progression. Despite their power, these models require large datasets, are computationally intensive, and often lack interpretability, limiting their clinical adoption.

Ensemble methods

Ensemble methods enhance prediction accuracy by combining multiple models, using techniques like bagging, boosting, and stacking. In sepsis detection, they integrate models such as Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, and Logistic Regression to improve robustness and reduce bias. While effective and reliable in clinical settings, they can be computationally demanding and less interpretable than individual models.

Deep learning models

DL models such as Deep Belief Networks (DBNs) and Autoencoders are effective in extracting complex patterns from large-scale clinical data. DBNs use stacked restricted Boltzmann machines for unsupervised pretraining, while Autoencoders excel in dimensionality reduction and feature extraction. These models are valuable for analyzing heterogeneous sepsis-related datasets but often require high computational resources and lack interpretability, limiting their clinical adoption.

SHAP (Shapley additive Explanations)

SHAP is a model-agnostic explainability tool that attributes each feature’s contribution to a model’s prediction, addressing the interpretability gap in complex ML models. By quantifying the influence of variables like vital signs or biomarkers, SHAP enhances model transparency and clinician trust in sepsis prediction frameworks. However, it can be computationally intensive, especially with large datasets and high-dimensional feature spaces. These models, each with unique strengths and limitations, contribute significantly to advancing sepsis detection and prediction, enabling more accurate diagnoses and timely interventions to improve patient outcomes.

For instance, Decision Trees (including Random Forests) are favoured for their interpretability and ability to handle nonlinear relationships, crucial for identifying complex sepsis indicators [34, 35]. Support Vector Machines (SVMs) prove effective in high-dimensional spaces, making them beneficial for datasets with numerous parameters [36, 37]. Logistic Regression offers a statistical approach to predicting binary outcomes, making it suitable for initial sepsis prognosis [38]. Gradient Boosting Machines are capable of handling missing data and improving prediction accuracy through ensemble learning [39, 40]. Neural Networks (CNN & RNN) are employed for their ability to capture temporal patterns in patient data, vital for tracking sepsis progression [41, 42].

Some studies also showed a trend towards using Ensemble Methods, which combine multiple ML models to improve prediction accuracy and stability, essential for reliable sepsis detection in clinical settings [43]. In addition, DL (a subset of ML) has become increasingly popular, with models such as deep belief networks (DBNs) and autoencoders excelling at extracting complex representations from multifaceted sepsis-related data [31]. Sepsis detection and prediction techniques

Sepsis detection and prediction have advanced with the adoption of ML techniques, which integrate diverse data, support real-time analysis, and apply robust evaluation metrics. In this context, each technique represents a specific computational approach to address key challenges in early diagnosis and model reliability. The goal is to generate accurate, actionable insights that enhance clinical decision-making and patient outcomes. The following are ten key techniques used in sepsis prediction, along with relevant references and descriptions.

Highlighting heterogeneity in approaches

Sepsis manifests variably based on factors like age, infection type, and comorbidities. Tools like SHAP and patient-reported data help identify key predictors and support personalized risk assessment.

Comparative evaluation of models

Sepsis complexity often exceeds the capacity of single models. Ensemble approaches like Random Forests and Gradient Boosting Machines improve reliability by capturing non-linear interactions among biomarkers.

Integration of data types

Integrating structured data (e.g., vitals, labs) with unstructured data (e.g., clinical notes, imaging) enhances sepsis prediction by offering a more comprehensive view of patient status, improving clinical decision-making [51, 57].

Application to specific clinical scenarios

Personalized predictive models tailored to specific populations—such as neonates or immunocompromised patients—improve accuracy by accounting for distinct physiological parameters, enabling more targeted interventions [39, 51].

Temporal and internal validation

External and temporal validation are crucial for developing robust sepsis prediction models. Validating across disease stages and time ensures model reliability despite evolving clinical conditions [41, 54].

Performance metrics for evaluation

Key performance metrics like AUC, sensitivity, and accuracy are vital for evaluating model effectiveness. High sensitivity reduces false negatives, ensuring timely identification of sepsis cases [28, 40].

Timeliness and Real-Time applications

Tools for rapid detection and diagnosis are essential in managing sepsis. Predictive algorithms integrated into hospital systems trigger timely alerts based on patient vitals, accelerating interventions and improving survival rates [30, 57].

Experimental evidence presentation

Experimental evidence supports the potential of sepsis prediction models. Rigorous testing across diverse patient datasets enhances early detection, predictive reliability, and trust in their broader clinical applicability [25, 41].

Effective sepsis prediction models can reduce hospital costs and prevent critical complications by enabling timely interventions [57, 59]. Their adoption supports better patient outcomes, adherence to clinical protocols, and more efficient use of healthcare resources.

Evaluating ML-Driven solutions and practical advancements in sepsis prediction

This section highlights the growing impact of ML in improving sepsis detection and prediction. ML models utilize diverse clinical data such as EMRs, vitals, and demographics—to provide accurate, timely predictions. The NAVOY Sepsis algorithm, externally validated for ICU use, demonstrates strong predictive performance for early intervention. Similarly, ML models used in emergency departments outperform traditional early warning systems. Ensemble approaches combining models like LightGBM and XGBoost, especially when using hourly patient data, show superior results. These advancements underscore ML’s real-world potential to enhance early diagnosis and support clinical decision-making in sepsis care.

Feature engineering for sepsis detection

To be effective in predictive modelling, it is very important to emphasize feature engineering or a corresponding task that would include the transformation of the raw data into more useful features. For example, concerning sepsis detection, feature engineering involves defining and querying the specific clinical features from structured clinical databases such as EHR, laboratory results, and vital signs [55, 60, 61]. By targeting the best predictors even at the early stage of model building, such as placing the most effective features, it is possible to increase the efficiency of the models aimed at predicting the occurrence of sepsis. The model-building process also entails dealing with issues like data absence, overfitting of the model, and feature death to improve the model [62]. This literature review discusses the state of the art of feature engineering as applied to sepsis detection, including reviewing the most important studies on feature selection and feature extraction techniques for sepsis prediction [63]. The following are the key points discussed in this review regarding the role of feature engineering in sepsis detection:

Feature selection and extraction for sepsis detection

Feature selection and extraction play an important role in selecting the most relevant medical indicators for predicting sepsis. The studies stress selecting clinical input features that play a crucial role in determining the outcome of a patient and understanding the multifactorial causes of the syndrome. The proper extraction of these features increases the detection accuracy of sepsis [41, 64].

Importance of timely sepsis prediction

The timely intervention of sepsis can be done with early prediction. ML models Like XGBoost have proved to be accurate predictors of sepsis within the first 6 h of its onset. This suggests that ML and DL models must be implemented to ensure early detection and effective treatment [65, 66].

Handling missing data and overfitting

Missing data handling and avoidance of overfitting play important roles in enhancing the precision and generalizability of a machine-learning model. The techniques used here, for feature replacement or selection, are mean imputation and chi-square, which help maintain the robustness and reliability of the model while trying to predict sepsis [67].

Model interpretation

Interpretability tools in ML, such as SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations), explain the reasoning behind certain predictions made by a model. SHAP helps reveal why a particular feature results in a model classifying a patient as sepsis. This adds explanatory power to models and guides clinical decision-making [53].

Predictive accuracy of ML models

XGBoost has demonstrated the highest accuracy of prediction in sepsis detection. Models like LSTM and SVM have shown their potential to be good in the detection of sepsis. These models work with high accuracy based on feature selection and extraction to enhance the accuracy of the predictions [54].

Reducing feature redundancy

Feature redundancy reduction is another efficient strategy to improve the performance of a model. Methods have been presented to remove redundant features from the data and extract strong, meaningful features from large-dimensional data. This enables sepsis detection models to predict more accurately and reliably [68].

Feature engineering across models

Feature engineering plays a vital role in different models for sepsis detection using ML. Choosing the correct features, extracting relevant data, and removing redundancy are the essential steps in creating an accurate model. Integration of all these strategies helps improve the performance in the prediction of the onset of sepsis [41, 64] and [54].

Overview of previous study

ML has transformed sepsis prediction and management by enabling timely, accurate interventions. Related studies fall into five categories: early detection, mortality prediction, interpretability and feature selection, specialized clinical challenges, and model evaluation. Early detection models like Random Forest and DL anticipate ICU sepsis onset. Mortality prediction uses models such as XGBoost to assess risk and guide decisions. Tools like SHAP enhance interpretability and feature relevance. Specialized studies target unique patient groups, while evaluation frameworks assess and refine existing models, collectively shaping future research and clinical practice.

Early detection and prediction of sepsis in ICU

This team of researchers is developing the early detection of sepsis using ML models in ICU settings. All the studies focus on high accuracy and early intervention. For example, one research study [28] utilized the Random Forest model, resulting in an AUC of 0.94 to improve the prediction of sepsis mortality in patients within the ICU. Likewise, another research [50] used XAutoNet and a CNN-based Autoencoder that showed public health benefits through early detection. In addition, the ensemble approach using XGBoost, Light GBM, and Random Forest was utilized [41], which provided improvements in accuracy six hours before the diagnosis of a clinical disease. The MGP-RNN DL model was studied in a multi-centre approach [51] and therefore provided an excellent evaluation of early sepsis detection. Finally, the NAVOY Sepsis algorithm using CNNs yielded high AUROC scores in the early predictions for the European ICU environment [55].

Mortality prediction in Sepsis patients

This category focuses on estimating mortality in sepsis patients and sometimes applies biomarkers and clinical measurements to inform the selection of treatment. In this Sect. [25], created a new blending model composed of RF, XGBoost, LR, SVM, and more to learn interesting temporal correlations related to hospital mortality. Correspondingly [8], took advantage of LASSO, RF, GBM, and Logistic Regression Models to predict ICU mortality related to patient risk by using metrics such as the Brier score and AUROC. Another work [58] obtained an AUC of 0.94 and an F1 score of 0.937 using XGBoost for in-hospital mortality prediction. Finally [40], proposed a gradient-boosting model for emergency department triage, which boosted the classification accuracy to 97.67%, further optimizing resource allocation.

Improving ML interpretability and feature selection

Studies in this category involve making ML models interpretable and feature selection for improved performance. For instance [54], applied XGBoost with SHAP analysis that provided interpretable results and outperformed other models such as MGP-RNN in prognosis prediction. Another study [53] used Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and SVM, combining modified chi-square feature selection to improve both recall and precision for the detection of early sepsis. A study [39] tested ML algorithms, such as Logistic Regression, SVM, and Random Forest, on various datasets and emphasized the feature importance and generalizability of models.

Advanced ML techniques for specialized scenarios

This group deals with advanced techniques of ML for particular clinical challenges. One article [26] applied SVM and Logistic Regression to predict the HPV type in cervical cancer patients; it had a very structured approach to extracting and analyzing data. Another study [34] was on CNNs and Random Forest models to predict outcomes of sepsis in patients undergoing hematopoietic cell transplantation. Moreover [56], introduced SERA, an algorithm combining both structured and unstructured data that employs methods including Lasso, SHAP, and XGBoost to mitigate false positives with early detection of sepsis.

Evaluation frameworks and scoping reviews

These studies review and comment on existing ML models with insights and recommendations for further development. For example [49], tested the ML models, which included Linear Regression and Neural Networks, in predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes, targeting quality improvement based on patient-reported data. Another related study [30] reviewed the regression-based ML model with a systematic approach that revealed the potential of unstructured clinical text in facilitating the early detection of sepsis. Finally, the COMPOSER DL model [57] was tested for its effect on clinical outcomes and resulted in a 1.9% reduction in mortality rates due to sepsis and improved compliance with care bundles for sepsis.

Key factors affecting sepsis prediction model performance

Sepsis prediction models utilize ML to analyze clinical data and give early warnings for the development of sepsis. The following points summarize the critical factors that affect their performance and reliability:

Type of data used

The type of data used within a sepsis prediction model determines its overall accuracy and reliability. Some studies integrate both structured and unstructured data, including electronic health records, laboratory results, and vital signs, as well as unstructured data, such as clinical notes or free-text data, which may offer more comprehensive views of a patient’s condition [114]. Other models may look specifically at structured data, such as vital signs or lab results, meaning their scope of prediction may be slightly narrowed, but can be built to work much faster and possibly even more efficiently. Different data types can also blend well together to improve the outcome of the model because, especially in sepsis, multiple factors are complex.

Generalization to unseen data

In summary, sepsis prediction models need to avoid overfitting the training data. Overfitting occurs when a model performs well on the data it was trained on but fails to generalize to new, unseen data [8]. Poor real-world clinical performance can occur when, outside of training datasets, actual patient data differs. Fundamentally, such a model is robust enough to generate effectively across different populations and diverse settings. Techniques like cross-validation and the use of independent test sets can facilitate proper generation by models, avoiding heavy dependence on specific datasets but instead adapting to diverse clinical environments.

Model transparency (“black box” Nature)

Quite several advanced ML models, such as DL, present a “black box problem” because of the complexity; these models predict correctly; however, in a real clinical setting, such decision-making is not clear, rendering it difficult to have the interpretation of the same. Algorithm transparency is essential to winning over the trust of the clinicians and ensuring the clarity of the model recommendations with clear actionability [115]. Techniques such as SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations), or LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) could help shed light on what kind of input features make such differences in the model output, which is even a life-or-death issue in healthcare.

Addressing data imbalance

Sepsis is usually a rare occurrence, making datasets used for training predictive models imbalanced, having significantly fewer sepsis events than non-sepsis events. Data imbalance can then lead to skewed predictions, where the model may favour the majority class (non-sepsis) and miss out on actual cases of sepsis. Improvement can also be realized in the model’s capacity to correctly classify sepsis cases by applying various data imbalance techniques, like oversampling the minority class of cases (sepsis cases) or undersampling the majority class of cases (non-sepsis cases) [56]. Other data generation techniques, such as SMOTE - Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique, can generate synthetic data, providing an opportunity for better-balanced training that should result in more precise and accurate prediction models.

Sensitivity to sepsis events

Considering that early detection is critical, models must be sensitive to sepsis events. In other words, they should not produce false negatives or miss a patient who has sepsis. High sensitivity means that the models identify as many of the sepsis cases as possible so that appropriate interventions can be made on time and may save lives. However, sensitivity typically comes with a cost to specificity: correctly identifying that the individual does not have sepsis. And balance needs to be maintained across these measures. Performance metrics of AUC-ROC and precision-recall curves help judge how well a model is classifying cases as either sepsis or a non-sepsis event, maximizing sensitivity [116].

A forest plot was generated to compare the diagnostic performance of selected ML/DL models based on reported AUC and sensitivity values (Fig. 9). The plot highlights the variability in performance across studies, with AUC values ranging from 0.74 to 0.91 and sensitivity scores from 0.70 to 0.86, demonstrating the relative effectiveness of different algorithms in early sepsis detection.

Choice of performance metrics

The right selection of performance metrics will help to evaluate the efficacy of the sepsis prediction model. Though useful, the standard metrics include accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision. The metrics fail to cover the clinical significance of the model in detail. For example, the use of precision-recall curves is significant when the case of sepsis is infrequent, as it happens in the detection of sepsis. The area under the curve of precision-recall gives a more meaningful evaluation when dealing with imbalanced data sets [116]. How the performance metrics are in line with clinical goals of minimizing false negatives to help in early intervention and reduce delays in treatment is essential for improving the outcomes of patients.

Therefore, based on these key factors, better sepsis predictive models can be developed to be more precise, reliable, and clinically relevant enough to improve patient outcomes and reduce mortality from sepsis.

Challenges and limitations of ML techniques for sepsis detection

This paper’s review presents several challenges and limitations that need careful consideration, especially in methodology, data interpretation, and the scope of analysis. Challenges facing this paper include availability and quality of data, class imbalance, generalizability, interpretability, clinician trust, and ethical and legal issues. Ethical and legal considerations also arise. Each of these factors holds a crucial position in the reliability and applicability of ML models in healthcare, so a balanced approach is needed to overcome these limitations. Discussion of these challenges is as follows.

This review has several limitations. The included studies were heterogeneous in design, data sources, and outcome measures, which limited direct comparisons and prevented formal meta-analysis. Inconsistent reporting of performance metrics further challenged comparative synthesis. The review was restricted to English-language articles from selected databases, possibly omitting relevant work. Additionally, many models lacked external validation and real-world clinical implementation, affecting the assessment of their practical utility. Lastly, no formal evaluation of publication bias was conducted due to reporting variability.

Data availability and quality

The quality and completeness of the data are crucial to the development of reliable ML models. Clinical datasets, which often originate from EHRs, are often characterized by missing or incomplete entries, thereby limiting their utility for training and validating models. Noise and inconsistencies in data further complicate the process of building robust models. Standardizing data collection practices and addressing data gaps are critical to improving model performance [25, 28, 48] and [26].

Class imbalance and rare events

Sepsis is a rare event compared to other conditions, leading to an imbalance in datasets where septic cases are significantly outnumbered by non-septic cases. This imbalance can bias ML models toward predicting the majority class, reducing their ability to detect true positives. Techniques like oversampling and synthetic data generation have been employed, but may lead to overfitting. Balancing datasets remains an ongoing challenge [30, 41, 52, 53] and [55].

Generalizability across clinical settings

Models often fail to generalize effectively when applied to new datasets or healthcare settings due to differences in population characteristics, clinical practices, and data collection protocols. This lack of generalizability limits their usability across diverse healthcare environments. External validation using independent datasets is essential to assess robustness but is hindered by privacy concerns and regulatory barriers [34, 51, 52] and [8].

Interpretability and clinician trust

Many ML models, particularly those based on DL, are perceived as “black boxes,” making their predictions difficult for clinicians to understand. This lack of interpretability can erode trust and hinder adoption in clinical practice. Tools like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) help provide insights into feature importance but often fail to address the underlying complexity of the models. Bridging the gap between accuracy and interpretability is vital for clinical acceptance [49, 50, 54] and [39].

Ethical and legal concerns

The use of patient data in ML models raises ethical and legal challenges, particularly concerning data privacy, informed consent, and fairness. Regulations like GDPR impose strict compliance requirements for data handling and sharing, which can slow down model development. Additionally, biases in datasets may lead to unfair predictions, exacerbating healthcare disparities. Ensuring ethical integrity and compliance is a critical consideration in ML deployment [56–58] and [40].

Discussion

Early detection of sepsis plays a critical role in improving patient outcomes. Prompt recognition at the initial stages enables timely intervention, reducing the risk of multi-organ dysfunction and increasing survival rates. ML has emerged as a powerful tool in this domain due to its ability to process large volumes of clinical data and generate early warning signals. These systems support clinicians in making timely decisions, ultimately reducing ICU stays, lowering hospital costs, and enhancing overall patient care.

However, the evolving definition of sepsis poses a challenge for ML model development. Changes in diagnostic criteria influence patient cohorts, which can affect model training, validation, and performance. Ensuring that models remain robust despite these definitional shifts is essential. While advanced algorithms such as DL offer high accuracy and can model complex patterns, they often require large datasets and lack interpretability, limiting clinical trust and scalability. In contrast, simpler models like logistic regression offer transparency and are easier to implement, though they may not capture non-linear relationships as effectively.

Another key challenge is the class imbalance in medical datasets, where sepsis cases are significantly outnumbered by non-sepsis cases. This imbalance can lead to biased models that underperform in detecting critical sepsis events. Additionally, clinical data often contain noise, missing values, and inconsistencies, which can negatively impact model performance. Addressing these issues requires robust preprocessing techniques and careful feature engineering.

Lastly, the use of ML in sepsis detection raises important ethical considerations. Issues of data privacy, informed consent, and security must be addressed to ensure the responsible and trustworthy application of these technologies in clinical practice.

Sustainability and lifecycle management of AI systems

It refers to the continuous process of maintaining, updating, and ethically overseeing AI models after their deployment in healthcare settings. This ensures that the systems remain accurate, reliable, and clinically relevant over time, particularly in dynamic and high-stakes environments such as pandemics. As medical data, disease patterns, and treatment protocols evolve, AI models must undergo routine performance monitoring, retraining on updated datasets, and recalibration to address prediction drift. Lifecycle management also involves compliance with emerging global health regulations (e.g., WHO, FDA) and ongoing ethical evaluations to ensure fairness, transparency, and accountability. Without these measures, AI tools risk becoming obsolete, potentially leading to inaccurate predictions and loss of trust among healthcare providers and patients. Therefore, sustained oversight is critical for the safe, effective, and equitable use of AI in long-term public health strategies.

Stakeholder-Centric AI development

The involvement of stakeholders across the spectrum patients, caregivers, clinicians, policymakers, and regulatory bodies is fundamental to ensuring that AI applications are ethically aligned and context-sensitive in pandemic settings. Active stakeholder engagement ensures that AI tools are both socially acceptable and practically applicable, promoting trust and adoption in diverse healthcare contexts.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that ML and DL models hold significant promise in advancing early sepsis diagnosis. By leveraging large-scale clinical data—including electronic health records, vital signs, and laboratory results—these models can generate accurate and timely predictions that often surpass conventional diagnostic methods. The 80 high-quality studies included in this review offer valuable insights into diverse model architectures, data preprocessing strategies, performance metrics, and clinical applicability of ML/DL-based sepsis prediction systems.

The findings underscore that model effectiveness is closely linked to data quality, balanced representation of septic and non-septic cases, and the interpretability of algorithmic outputs. This review highlights that tree-based ensembles (e.g., Random Forest, XGBoost) and deep neural networks (e.g., LSTM, transformer-based models) are particularly effective in capturing complex temporal and non-linear patterns relevant to sepsis onset. Moreover, the incorporation of explainability tools such as SHAP enhances clinician trust by making AI-generated predictions more transparent and interpretable.

By synthesizing and visually comparing model performance through a forest plot, this review provides a comparative framework for assessing the diagnostic strength of various models. It also identifies critical gaps such as the lack of external validation, insufficient real-world testing, and limited ethical safeguards that must be addressed to ensure clinical readiness and broader adoption.

Ultimately, the insights gained from this review can guide researchers, clinicians, and policymakers in developing more robust, generalizable, and ethically sound ML/DL models for sepsis detection. A balanced approach that emphasizes interpretability, fairness, and real-world validation is essential for successful clinical integration. As global health systems prepare for future crises, AI readiness must be embraced not as a luxury but as a strategic imperative capable of saving lives, optimizing resources, and informing real-time policy decisions. Realizing the full potential of AI in sepsis care and pandemic response will require sustained interdisciplinary collaboration, rigorous validation, and a commitment to equity and transparency.

Future work

Future research in ML for sepsis identification must focus on dealing with the challenges of data integrity and class imbalance. As sepsis is a rare occurrence, strategies for addressing these problems, including advanced data augmentation methods and synthetic data processing, will be crucial to improve model performance. Additionally, the development of real-time prediction mechanisms that can constantly monitor patient records and trigger early interventions could significantly reduce the time to treatment, thus improving survival rates. Researchers should further explore using multi-modal data, including both structured and unstructured clinical information, for a more comprehensive understanding of patient conditions and improved prediction accuracy.

Another important point for future work is to improve the interpretability of advanced ML models, especially DL models, to make them clearer and more understandable to health professionals. Using tools like SHAP and LIME, researchers can gain additional insight into the importance of features, but further development will provide more actionable insights for clinical decision-making. Additionally, as healthcare settings vary widely, external validation and adaptation of these models to diverse medical environments are essential for ensuring generalizability. Future studies should also explore integrating these technologies into clinical workflow, ensuring they are all effective and easily implemented by healthcare providers. The focus must be on ensuring that such models not only enhance sepsis detection but also contribute to improving overall patient outcomes and healthcare system performance.
