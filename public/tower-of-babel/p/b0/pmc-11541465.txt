Rapid dilatation of mycotic aneurysm of dissecting aorta caused by Edwardsiella tarda
Authors: Naoto Fukunaga, Tatsuto Wakami, Akio Shimoji, Otohime Mori, Kosuke Yoshizawa, Nobushige Tamura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEdwardsiella tarda is a gram-negative bacillus that is typically found in freshwater environments and the feces of reptiles. A 48-year-old woman with Marfan's syndrome presented to our hospital with fever, nausea, and general fatigue. She had previously undergone a mechanical Bentall procedure and total aortic arch replacement for acute type A aortic dissection. E tarda was identified from the blood culture. Computed tomography scanning revealed a mycotic aneurysm of the dissecting descending aorta. Despite appropriate intravenous antibiotic therapy, the aneurysm dilated rapidly. Thus, an emergency surgery was performed to replace the descending aorta with a graft that was surrounded by a latissimus dorsi muscle flap. Her postoperative course was uneventful.Keywords: Mycotic aneurysm of aorta, Edwardsiella tarda

Edwardsiella tarda is a gram-negative bacillus that is found typically in freshwater environments and the feces of fish and reptiles, such as lizards, snakes, and alligators. Human infections owing to E tarda are rare and usually associated with gastrointestinal disease.1,2 The first human cases were published in 1969.3 Herein, we present a case of rapid progression of mycotic aneurysm of an aortic dissection caused by E tarda.

Case Report

A publication consent form was obtained from the patient.

A 48-year-old woman with Marfan's syndrome visited our hospital for fever, nausea, and general fatigue that had been present for several days. She had undergone a mechanical Bentall procedure and total aortic arch replacement with conventional elephant trunk placement 5 years ago for acute type A aortic dissection involving the descending aorta. Her C-reactive protein (CRP) level was elevated at 26.3 mg/dL, and the white blood cell count was 17,600/μL. E tarda was isolated from blood cultures obtained upon admission. Transthoracic echocardiography did not reveal any signs of infection in the mechanical aortic valve. Furthermore, the left ventricular function was preserved with a well-functioning mechanical aortic valve. Intravenous ampicillin (2 g/q4h) was administered according to the organism's sensitivity. Computed tomography (CT) angiography revealed chronic aortic dissection (aortic diameter, 53.6 mm) (Fig 1, A), which was stable in size when compared with previous images. However, the patient's fever persisted, and the CRP level continued to rise despite the appropriate antibiotic therapy. CT angiography was repeated 6 days later, and it showed a significant increase in the aortic diameter (63.8 mm) (Fig 1, B). There was no clear evidence of graft infection such as gas around the graft. Before the present infection, the annual expansion rate was approximately 5 mm. Given the rapid aortic dilatation at the level of the chronic dissection in a patient with bacteremia, the patient was diagnosed with a mycotic aneurysm of chronic aortic dissection. No other imaging studies were not obtained because the aorta had dilated quickly, necessitating immediate surgery.

(A) Computed tomography (CT) angiography depicts a 53.6-mm mycotic aneurysm of the dissecting aorta. (B) The rapid progression of mycotic aneurysm (an increase of 10.2 mm) over a 6-day interval.

Surgical repair was deemed necessary owing to the deteriorating situation. The patient was placed in the right decubitus position. The plastic surgeons harvested the latissimus dorsi muscle flap for wrapping the vascular graft. The muscle was wrapped with wet gauze until it was placed around the vascular graft. A thoracotomy was placed in the left fourth intercostal space to gain entry into the left thoracic cavity. The part of the descending aorta at the level of the seventh vertebrae was confirmed on transesophageal echocardiography. The distal aortic clamp was later placed here. The visceral arteries were not involved in the distal aortic clamp. A cardiopulmonary bypass was established via femoral cannulations, and the body temperature was decreased to 32°C in the bladder. An occlusion balloon was placed in the previous elephant trunk graft under fluoroscopic guidance. The mycotic aneurysm in the descending aorta was replaced successfully with a rifampicin-soaked 22-mm Gelweave graft (Vascutek Terumo Inc, Scotland, UK) surrounded by the latissimus dorsi muscle flap. Cardiopulmonary bypass was maintained for 252 minutes.

Histological examination of the excised aortic aneurysm specimen revealed inflammatory cell infiltration, which was suggestive of an infection. However, no organism was isolated from the aortic tissue. Intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g/q24 h) was administered for 6 weeks instead of ampicillin owing to an allergic reaction to the drug. Subsequently, life-long oral antibiotics were initiated. Postoperatively, the patient's fever resolved, and the CRP level decreased. Postoperative CT scan revealed successful replacement of the descending aorta (Fig 2). Her clinical course was uneventful. No infection recurrence was observed at the 5-month follow-up. Our subsequent imaging surveillance plan includes repeat CT scan at the 6- and 12-month follow-up visits. Thereafter, an annual CT scan is planned.

Discussion

E tarda is a gram-negative bacillus typically found in freshwater environments and the feces of fish and reptiles, such as lizards, snakes, and alligators. Human infections owing to E tarda are rare and usually associated with gastrointestinal disease.1,2 It can also cause wound infections, abscesses, and bacteremia in association with marine exposure.4

Reports of mycotic aneurysms of the native aorta owing to E tarda are rare,4 with only a few documented cases, including our current patient. These patients present with general fatigue (n = 1), cellulitis (n = 1), and back pain (n = 2). Thus, identification of the apparent source of infection may be challenging because these symptoms are unrelated to the gastrointestinal system. Because the patient did not have any risk factors, such as owning reptiles or swimming in a lake, for infection, the source of infection in our patient remains unclear. However, the history of nausea upon admission suggested a potential association with a gastrointestinal disease, a common source of human infections.1

E tarda is susceptible to numerous antibiotics, with only a few exceptions.5 In our patient, the administered antibiotics were chosen according to the sensitivity test results. However, the infection did not respond to the intravenous antibiotic therapy over a 2-week period. The patient continued to have fever spikes and an elevated CRP level. Furthermore, the chronic aortic dissection in the descending aorta exhibited rapid dilatation within 6 days. Based on the clinical course and imaging findings, we diagnosed the patient with a mycotic aneurysm of the chronic aortic dissection, which was confirmed on histopathological examination.

A notable feature of our case was the rapid progression of the mycotic aneurysm in the presence of E tarda bacteremia. In a previous report of E tarda bacteremia that caused a mycotic aneurysm of the aortic arch, such a progression was not observed, and intravenous antibiotic administration for 4 weeks produced a good outcome. In this patient, the mycotic aneurysm remained stable because the infection was controlled effectively. Subsequently, elective graft replacement of the aortic arch was performed.6 Similarly, another case of mycotic aneurysm of the descending aorta owing to E tarda infection was treated successfully with stent graft implantation and suppressive antibiotic therapy for >3 years.4 In both patients, antibiotic therapy controlled the E tarda infection effectively.

Our case highlights the fact that E tarda bacteremia can precipitate the rapid progression of an aortic aneurysm in cases of refractory infection, despite the administration of appropriate antibiotics. Thus, physicians should monitor aneurysm size diligently via repeated imaging studies to guide appropriate management strategies and optimize patient outcomes.

Conclusions

E tarda rarely causes infections in human, and is susceptible to numerous antibiotics. However, in patients with mycotic aneurysm of the native aorta that is refractory to antibiotics administered according to the susceptibility pattern, the aneurysm size should be monitored to ensure timely intervention if required.
