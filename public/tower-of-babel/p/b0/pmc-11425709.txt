Impact of dietary ingredients on radioprotection and radiosensitization: a comprehensive review
Authors: Md Monirul Islam, Nahida Sultana, Chang Liu, Aihong Mao, Takanori Katsube, Bing Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation exposure poses significant health risks, particularly in radiotherapy and nuclear accidents. Certain dietary ingredients offer potential radioprotection and radiosensitization. In this review, we explore the impact of dietary ingredients, including vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other bioactive compounds, on radiation sensitivity and their potential for radioprotection. Radiosensitizers reoxygenate hypoxic tumor cells, increase the radiolysis of water molecules, and regulate various molecular mechanisms to induce cytotoxicity and inhibit DNA repair in irradiated tumor cells. Several dietary ingredients, such as vitamins C, E, selenium, and phytochemicals, show promise in protecting against radiation by reducing radiation-induced oxidative stress, inflammation, and DNA damage. Radioprotectors, such as ascorbic acid, curcumin, resveratrol, and genistein, activate and modulate various signaling pathways, including Keap1-Nrf2, NF-κB, PI3K/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), STAT3, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), in response to radiation-induced oxidative stress, regulating inflammatory cytokine expression, and promoting DNA damage repair and cell survival. Conversely, natural dietary radiosensitizers impede these pathways by enhancing DNA damage and inducing apoptosis in irradiated tumor cells. Understanding the molecular basis of these effects may aid in the development of effective strategies for radioprotection and radiosensitization in cancer treatment. Dietary interventions have the potential to enhance the efficacy of radiation therapy and minimize the side effects associated with radiation exposure.Keywords: Dietary ingredients, radiosensitivity, radioprotector, radiosensitizer, cell survival, apoptosis

Introduction

Ionizing radiation (IR) is increasingly used in medical therapy and diagnosis. In addition, individuals are exposed to cosmic radiation during long-duration space activities. IR has numerous negative biological effects on living organisms [1], inducing pathological states, such as cancer [2] and acute radiation syndrome [3]. Cancer is the second leading cause of global mortality [4], and approximately 60% of cancer patients receive IR as part of their therapeutic treatment [5]. However, while IR is effective in eradicating cancer cells, it can also harm normal cells, leading to cellular damage and undesirable side effects. The biological effects of IR occur through both direct and indirect mechanisms. IR interacts indirectly with biological systems, producing free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS) from the molecules surrounding the DNA, which then oxidize various cellular components, resulting in DNA damage. IR also directly affects the body by breaking DNA strands and causing cell damage [6–8]. Protection against IR is crucial in various aspects of human life. Many compounds have been tested for their radioprotective effects, but their success has been limited, primarily because of toxicity-related problems. For example, amifostine was the first drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical use in human cancer treatment because of the negative effects of certain chemo-radiotherapy drugs [9]. However, amifostine has been reported to have high costs, limited organ protection, high toxicity, undesirable side effects, and complications [10]. Research to minimize these adverse effects is ongoing. Interest has been growing in dietary supplements, natural dietary compounds, and minerals as potential sources of radioprotectors, owing to the inherent toxicity of synthetic chemicals. Animal experiments have shown that antioxidants, such as vitamin E, a large number of natural products, and melatonin, are protective against the damaging effects of radiation, with less toxicity and side effects [11]. However, numerous studies have identified various natural dietary compounds that can sensitize cancer cells to radiation, such as vitamins, hormones, ferulic acid, glutamine, arginine, ubiquinone, and hydroquinone, also providing some level of radioprotection. For example, sesamol, resveratrol, β-glucagon, polysaccharide ginseng, genistein, and vitamins C and E have been shown to exhibit radioprotective and chemosensitive properties [12–18].

Many factors can modify IR-induced biological effects, including carcinogenesis, such as pre-exposure to low doses and dietary lifestyle-related factors. Food supplements and natural products are potential protective agents against radiation damage and carcinogenic chemicals [19,20]. Natural compounds can be effective in increasing the radiation sensitivity of cancer cells and reducing the side effects of radiotherapy. Numerous natural substances and synthetic compounds have been studied for the decoration of radionuclides in the body. Previous studies have identified several mechanism-based factors that can affect radiation sensitivity, such as the tumor microenvironment [21], tumor glycolysis [22], psychological stress [23], protein biomarkers [24], reactive oxygen species [25], and dietary phytochemicals [26].

Since the early 2000s, there has been an increased focus on the use of dietary interventions to decrease toxicity and improve the outcomes of cancer patients [27]. Research has demonstrated that dietary supplements containing antioxidant agents suppress space radiation-induced carcinogenesis [28]. Antioxidants act as free radical scavengers and reduce IR-induced DNA damage caused by IR [29]. Dietary nutrients and habits have a major impact on health, quality of life, and cancer. The potential beneficial effects of food supplements and beverages have attracted considerable attention. In particular, natural dietary components of foods or beverages hold promise as radiation-protective agents [30,31]. Diet and nutrition are effective strategies for cancer prevention and treatment, and dietary natural products have shown potential in this area [32,33]. Many epidemiological studies have highlighted the link between diet and cancer risk [34,35]. Several authors have reported that nutrient modulation through diet, fasting, or short-term starvation can selectively protect normal cells in mice [36,37] and, potentially, patients with chemotoxicity, without reducing the therapeutic outcome of cancer cells [38–40]. Recent studies have demonstrated that antioxidant supplements, such as vitamins A, E, and C, reduce the risk of lung cancer [13,41,42]. Zhu et al. (2017) performed a dose-response meta-analysis and found that for every additional 2 mg/day of vitamin E intake, lung cancer risk decreased by 5% [43]. There has been an increasing interest in the general public regarding the use of dietary ingredients and natural products to reduce the effects of radiation after nuclear accidents, as well as secondary radiation effects after cancer radiotherapy or space missions. The cytotoxic effect of radiotherapy is limited because of radioresistance in cancerous cells. Therefore, it is necessary to develop alternative strategies to increase the radiosensitivity and radioprotection of normal tissues by combining dietary ingredients to enhance the outcomes of radiation therapy while minimizing its side effects.

However, the impact of dietary measures on responses to radiosensitivity and radioprotection has been poorly elucidated. The findings of this review provide a foundation for further research and may contribute to the development of novel strategies to improve cancer treatment. Therefore, a review was performed to evaluate the role of dietary ingredients in radiosensitization and radioprotection. In this comprehensive review, we summarize the literature on the relationship between dietary ingredients, radiosensitivity, and radioprotection.

Materials and methods

Information sources: We searched three databases, PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science, for relevant articles.

Keywords: The search terms used in the Advanced search feature, Diet, Nutrition, Dietary Ingredients, Ionizing Radiation, Radiation therapy, Radiotherapy, Calorie Restrictions, Radioprotectors and Radiosensitizers.

Access dates: The literature search was conducted between October 2022 and October 2023.

Criteria: Using advanced search features in the mentioned databases, we screened articles based on the presence of search terms, titles, and abstracts. Initially, 532 potentially eligible articles were identified. After excluding 169 duplicates and articles not relevant to our study, we identified 128 articles that met our inclusion criteria. Furthermore, we reviewed the reference lists of the selected PubMed papers to identify any relevant articles that may not have appeared in our initial search. Overview of Radioprotectors and Radiosensitizers

Radioprotectors are substances or agents designed to shield living organisms from the detrimental effects of IR exposure [44]. IR radiation, such as X-rays, gamma rays, and specific particles, can harm cells and tissues by inducing DNA damage and other molecular disturbances. Radioprotectors function by lessening or preventing these effects, thereby effectively reducing the likelihood of radiation-related injuries. An ideal radioprotective substance should be safe for use, offer significant shielding for healthy tissues, and have minimal or no effect on protecting tumor cells from IR. These compounds primarily boost the body’s antioxidant defense mechanisms, thereby eliminating harmful free radicals [45]. Radioprotectors have applications in various fields, including nuclear submarines, radiography, military responses to nuclear attacks, radiological procedures, aviation, and manned space missions [46]. They also have a role in clinical oncology in minimizing the toxicity of radiotherapy without interfering with the anticancer effects of radiation. Radioprotectors mitigate radiation damage through mechanisms such as free radical scavenging, hydrogen transfer reactions, and mixed disulfide and endogenous non-protein sulfhydryl mechanisms. These compounds may remove free radicals, induce the production of natural radioprotectors (e.g. catalase and superoxide dismutase), reduce post-radiation inflammation, and enhance DNA repair [47]. Despite over six decades of research on the development of radioprotectors, there is currently no safe and effective nontoxic radioprotector available for human use [48].

Radiosensitizers are substances or agents that enhance the responsiveness of cancer cells to RT. By combining radiosensitizers with radiation treatment, the effectiveness of radiation in eliminating cancer cells can be enhanced. Radiosensitizers are closely linked to radiosensitivity, which refers to the inherent susceptibility of cells, tissues, and organisms to the harmful effects of IR. It varies among different cell types and organisms, with some cells being more sensitive to radiation-induced damage than others. Radiosensitivity is influenced by various factors, including linear energy transfer (LET) range [49], oxygen effect, oxygen enhancement ratio, age, sex, genetic susceptibility, and lifestyle factors [50,51]. Certain tissues and cells may be more sensitive to radiation than others. For example, erythroblasts, epidermal stem cells, and gastrointestinal stem cells are among the most sensitive cell types [52]. Lymphocytes and oocytes were highly sensitive. Nerve cells and muscle fibers are among the least sensitive cells. Genetic factors also play a role in cell vulnerability to IR [53]. For instance, human T cells exhibit lower radiosensitivity than B cells, and their overall radiosensitivity was assessed using a clonogenic assay. The absence of peroxidase activity in human leukemia/peripheral lymphocytes and lymphoma cells makes them highly vulnerable to ROS-induced oxidative stress [54].

In recent years, there has been increasing interest in exploring the effectiveness of dietary ingredient-derived radiosensitizers for enhancing the efficacy of radiation therapy. Numerous dietary ingredients have been investigated for their radiosensitizing properties and several studies have highlighted their promising effects on cancer cells [55–58]. These preliminary studies indicate that natural dietary ingredients, such as radiosensitizers and radioprotectors, offer new possibilities for improving the effectiveness of radiation therapy in cancer treatment [59]. Naturally occurring dietary antioxidants and minerals have been widely examined for their potential synergistic or antagonistic effects with radiation to provide health benefits, and a number of dietary compounds have demonstrated radioprotective and radiosensitizing activities (Figure 2). The identification of nontoxic and efficient radioprotective agents that can be easily administered prior to radiation exposure is a crucial goal for radiation biologists and oncologists.

Radioprotection and radiosensitization through diets and dietary ingredients

Diet is often considered a food regimen, and dietary habits have a major impact on health, quality of life, and cancer. It has been shown to influence cancer risk, treatment toxicity, and recurrence, but little is known about how diet interacts with cancer treatment modalities. Besides diets designed for weight management, scientists are studying several food regimens to establish healthy medical and functional diets with the correct balance of minerals, vitamins, and other nutrients to prevent and cure health problems. Previous studies in animal models have demonstrated that metabolic-based therapies such as caloric restriction (CR), ketogenic diets (KD), fasting, antioxidants, and other minerals and vitamins may decrease the occurrence of primary tumors and slow the growth of already existing primary tumors [60].

CR is a diet modification that can be defined as a net energy deficit in an individual’s consumption of food, as measured by caloric intake. CR aims to reduce total caloric intake between 20%-40% of energy lower than that of a typical diet intake without limiting essential vitamins and minerals [61,62]. Animal studies have shown that CR increases apoptosis, augments antiproliferative effects, and decreases DNA synthesis [63–65]. In fact, many novel therapeutic agents are known to also be targets of CR, such as GTP proteins RAS/RAF, Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK), IGF-1 receptor (IGF-1R), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR).

Fasting is a dieting pattern consisting of abstinence from solid foods for a specific period. It has been practiced for centuries, both as a religious observance and part of human evolution, shaping human metabolic flexibility [66]. Beyond weight loss, scientists endorse fasting diets, pointing to associated health benefits [67]. Studies have shown that fasting, low-protein diets, and temporary reductions in calorie intake have advantageous in vivo and in vitro effects on certain types of cancer in mouse models and cancer cell lines [68–71]. Preliminary studies that have been performed on animal models have shown promise in the use of intermittent fasting (IF) to enhance cancer treatment in transgenic and transplant mouse models of neuroblastoma, glioma, fibrosarcoma, breast, and ovarian cancers [72].

In contrast, a ketogenic diet (KD) typically consists of 90% fat, 2% carbohydrates, and 8% protein, forcing cells to rely on lipid oxidation and mitochondrial respiration rather than glycolysis for energy metabolism. KDs have been shown to inhibit tumor growth when combined with radiochemotherapy or targeted anticancer drugs in a variety of cancer models [73–76].

Natural dietary compounds found in food and plants, which have minimal side effects and toxicity, have garnered attention because of their antioxidant, anticancer, and radiation-protective properties [77–79]. For example, curcuma, known for its tracheal smooth muscle relaxation properties [80], has been investigated as a radiosensitizer for the treatment of human cervical tumor cells [59]. Dietary interventions such as CR, IF, KD, and the inclusion of natural dietary ingredients such as vitamins and minerals have been shown to significantly scavenge free radicals, improve immune system function, and mitigate radiation-induced damage [20,81–83]. Radioprotective and radiosensitive properties of minerals and vitamins

Various dietary ingredients, including vitamins and minerals, possess natural antioxidant properties, and play important roles as cofactors in metabolic processes. This section explored the radioprotective and radiosensitive properties of specific minerals and vitamins.

Selenium (Se)

Selenium is an essential trace mineral that functions as a cofactor for enzymes and proteins such as thioredoxin reductase, ribonucleotide reductase, and selenoproteins. These substances are involved in DNA synthesis, protection against cell damage, and defense against infections [84]. Selenium can be obtained through supplements or naturally in food. Studies in mice have demonstrated the radioprotective effects of Se and its derivatives. Intraperitoneal injection either before (within 1 h) or shortly after (within 15 min) exposure to radiation (9 Gy) enhanced 30-day survival rates [85]. A selenium derivative, 3,3 di-selenopropionic acid, also protects mice by reducing DNA damage and apoptosis [86]. Furthermore, dietary selenium (100 µg) mitigated radiation-induced nephropathy in mice [87]. A recent review concluded that selenium supplementation with sodium selenite (200-500 µg/day) improves the quality of life of patients and prevents side effects of toxicity without reducing the effectiveness of radiotherapy. However, caution is advised regarding high intake and long-term supplementation owing to potential toxicity [88]. Low selenium and lycopene levels increase radiation sensitivity in prostate cancer patients [57]. One study by Nie et al. (2023) investigated the radiosensitizing effects of selenium on cancer cells [89]. Specifically, certain selenium species induce G2/M cell cycle arrest, halting cells at the replication stage to enhance radiosensitivity. This study provides mechanistic insights into the pathways involved in selenium-mediated radiosensitization. In another study [90], selenium-l-methionine exhibited protective effects against radiation-induced acute pneumonitis and lung fibrosis in rats. Overall, Se supplements have the potential to enhance radioprotection and modulate radiosensitivity.

Ascorbic acid (vitamin C)

Vitamin C (C6H8O6) is a well-known antioxidant that protects organisms from oxidative damage caused by exposure to IR. Studies have demonstrated the ability of vitamin C and its derivatives to scavenge different free radicals and protect DNA from radiation-induced strand breaks caused by gamma radiation [91]. Pre-treatment with supplemental vitamin C (150 or 250 mg/kg/day) orally for three days before a lethal radiation dose (14 Gy) followed by bone marrow transplantation reduced gastrointestinal syndrome and effectively reduced radiation lethality in mice. Post-treatment with high doses (3 g/kg) of vitamin C immediately after exposure (7-8 Gy) promotes recovery of the hematopoietic system, reduces apoptosis in bone marrow cells, and increases survival rates [92,93]. Previous research has indicated that mice treated with vitamin C before radiation exposure showed reduced lung damage compared to the control group. The study suggested that the antioxidant properties of vitamin C may help mitigate radiation-induced injury by reducing oxidative stress and tumor necrosis factor-alpha [94]. Clinical investigations have indicated that intravenous administration of 1.5 g/kg of vitamin C three times a week is safe and nontoxic in cancer patients [95]. However, a high dose of vitamin C can also increase DNA damage by producing hydroxyl radicals, which limits its clinical application [96].

Tocopherol (vitamin E)

Vitamin E (C29H50O2), which includes tocopherols and tocotrienols, is a potent antioxidant in the human body and effectively scavenges free radicals generated by radiation exposure [97]. Tocopherol derivatives have been shown to protect DNA from radiation-induced strand breaks [98]. A series of studies on gamma-tocopherol (GT3), a member of the vitamin E family and an inhibitor of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA), demonstrated its protective effect in mice when administered at doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg BW when injected 24 hours before irradiation [99]. GT3 significantly increases granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and interleukin-6 (Il-6), reducing radiation-induced cytopenia [100,101]. Tocopherol succinate (TS) inhibited apoptosis in various cells and protected mice from lethal radiation doses when administered at 400 mg/kg 24 hours before irradiation [102]. It was previously described that tocotrienol safeguards mouse bone marrow and human CD34 cells against the harmful effects of radiation by activating survival pathways associated with extracellular signal-related kinase activation and the mammalian target of rapamycin [103]. However, high doses (75 mg/kg) of these vitamin E derivatives have shown adverse effects in non-human primates, leading to increased mortality without significant protection compared with the irradiated control [104].

In summary, minerals and vitamins such as selenium, ascorbic acid, and tocopherol possess antioxidant capabilities that make them effective in protecting against radiation. While these dietary-derived ingredients show promise in mitigating radiation-induced damage, careful consideration is necessary to determine the optimal dosages and avoid potential toxicities. Further research is required to gain a deeper understanding of their therapeutic advantages and to ensure their safe and effective application in clinical settings.

Radioprotective and radiosensitive properties of naturally occurring dietary ingredients

Numerous studies have explored the effect of various dietary compounds present in a wide range of beverages and foods on various human ailments. Among these compounds, naturally occurring phytochemicals and antioxidants have been recognized for their ability to protect the liver, heart, and brain, and inhibit tumor growth [105–110]. The radioprotective and radiosensitive properties of natural dietary compounds are discussed below.

Ellagic acid (C14H6O8)

Ellagic acid (EA) is a bioactive flavonoid and a naturally occurring antioxidant found in various fruits and vegetables, such as pecans, walnuts, blackberries, raspberries, strawberries, and grapes, primarily in the form of ellagitannin [111]. It has recently gained increased attention for the prevention and treatment of cancer [112]. As a natural radiosensitizer, EA is known to induce apoptotic radiosensitivity due to its biological activities, such as anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities [113]. It acts as a potent anticancer agent, enhancing radiation-induced oxidative stress and cytotoxicity in tumor cells [114]. EA significantly increases radiation-induced apoptotic sensitivity in irradiated breast cancer (MCF-7) cells through mechanisms involving mitochondrial deregulation, cell cycle arrest, and nuclear damage. Conversely, it aids normal cells in recovering from radiation damage [115]. Treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma cells with EA (10 µM) and γ-radiation (7.5 Gy) has been shown to increase ROS generation, decrease survival marker levels such as p-Akt, p-NF-κB, and p-STAT3, and upregulate p53 protein expression [116]. Several studies have explored the radiosensitive properties of EA. It enhances the efficacy of radiotherapy by mitigating radiation-induced damage to normal cells and increasing the susceptibility of cancer cells to radiation [117,118]. EA accomplishes this by modulating various intracellular pathways involved in the cell cycle, apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress, thereby enhancing the radiosensitivity of cancer cells [119]. According to Zhong et al. (2019), EA has the potential to significantly enhance the sensitivity of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells to doxorubicin (DOX) and cisplatin (CSP) treatment, promoting cell apoptosis mediated by DOX and CSP [120]. Furthermore, EA has shown potential as a radiosensitizer in combination with other therapeutic agents. For instance, it has been observed that EA improves the radiosensitivity of glioblastoma cells when used in combination with temozolomide, a standard chemotherapeutic agent for glioblastoma [121].

Genistein (C15H10O5)

Genistein (Genistein tinctoria L.) is a natural isoflavone derived from soybeans and other legumes [122]. Various studies have highlighted its multifaceted benefits in combating tumors. Its effects include antioxidation [123], tumor suppression by modulating endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated stress and mitochondria-dependent pathways [124], inhibition of cell proliferation [125], deactivation of the MAPK pathway [126], promotion of cancer cell apoptosis [127], deactivation of glucose transporters (GLUT1) and hexokinase 2 (HK2) to suppress aerobic glycolysis [128], and reduction of thioredoxin-1 in human liver cancer cells [129]. In in vivo studies with tumor-bearing mice, treatment with genistein (200 mg/kg) at 5-10 Gy was reported to have a protective effect against radiation-induced intestinal damage and slow tumor growth [130]. It also demonstrated the radiosensitive effects of genistein and partially mitigated radiation-induced lung damage [131]. Calveley et al. (2010) found that genistein treatment reduced the levels of many inflammatory cytokines, protected against DNA damage, and provided partial protection against fibrosis and pneumonitis [132]. It also enhanced hematopoietic cell recovery and 30-day survival in mice (89% compared to 19%) following subcutaneous administration of genistein (150 mg/kg) 24 hours prior to a lethal radiation (9.5 Gy) dose [133]. In vitro studies have shown that low concentrations of genistein (1.5 µM) could protect L-02 cells from radiation-induced injury by inhibiting apoptosis, DNA damage, chromosome aberration, impaired DNA repair, and upregulating and downregulating certain genes [134]. A study conducted by Liu et al. (2013) revealed that genistein (10 µM) significantly sensitized breast cancer cells via G2/M cell cycle arrest, increased DNA damage, and induced apoptosis [135]. Furthermore, genistein has been shown to inhibit various activities of bacterial enzymes (tyrosine kinase) and has displayed anticancer action through the involvement of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR/NF-κB, JNK, and extracellular signal-regulated protein kinases (ERK) signaling pathways in biological systems [136–138].

Curcumin (C21H20O6)

Curcumin, a phenolic compound found in the rhizomes of turmeric (Curcuma longa), has demonstrated its ability to protect against radiation-induced heart injury in rats [139] by reducing lipid peroxidation and maintaining the activity of antioxidant enzymes, especially at the highest dose (10 µg/ml) used [140,141]. When administered to patients, curcumin exhibits the potential to kill tumor cells and protect normal cells against the harmful effects of radiation. It is a potent immunomodulatory agent capable of regulating the activation of various immune cells, including B cells, macrophages, T cells, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, while enhancing antibody responses [142]. In an in vivo study conducted by Kunnumakkara et al. (2008), curcumin was shown to target NF-κB activation genes, thereby increasing the radiation sensitivity of human colon cancer [143]. In contrast to its radioprotective effects in normal cells and tissues, curcumin can radiosensitize a variety of human cervical tumor cells to IR, particularly by blocking the NF-κB signaling pathways [59,144] or by increasing ROS production and activating the MAPK pathway [145]. Similarly, tetrahydrocurcumin (THDC), a metabolically stable analog of curcumin, can radiosensitize glioma (C6) cells through G0/G1 arrest [146]. Studies have shown that curcumin targets a well-known antioxidant enzyme, thioredoxin reductase-1 (TxnRd1), to sensitize squamous carcinoma cells to radiation [147]. Additionally, curcumin can enhance the radiosensitivity of other cell types, such as breast cancer and colorectal cancer, by targeting IR-induced NF-κB activating signaling pathways [148,149]. Recent studies have demonstrated that curcumin, when conjugated with albumin-based nanoparticles, increases the survival rate of irradiated mice [150].

Sesamol (C7H6O3)

Sesamol, also known as 3,4-methylenedioxyphenol, is a natural phenolic compound found in sesame (Sesamum indicum) oil and is known for its potential therapeutic benefits [151]. Although sesame seeds are commonly consumed as nutritious food, sesamol is recognized for its properties as an antioxidant-based radioprotector with strong free radical scavenging abilities, iron chelating capabilities, and the ability to prevent lipid peroxidation [152,153]. It also exhibits chemopreventive [154] and hepatoprotective effects [155]. Earlier studies have reported that sesamol (at a dose of 100 mg/kg) reduced radiation-induced mortality and resulted in a 100% survival rate in C57BL/6 male mice (administered 30 min prior to radiation exposure at a dose of 7.5 Gy) from hematopoietic and gastrointestinal injury [156]. Furthermore, sesamol is believed to have a wide range of biological effects, including a reduction of radiation-induced cytogenic damage to the bone marrow and peripheral blood cells in mice [157]. It enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes [158], arrests cell growth at different stages of the cell cycle [159], triggers apoptosis [160], inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway [161], reduces the expression of TNF-α and IL-β [162], and modulates the expression of key regulatory proteins, such as Bax, caspase-3, p53, and Bcl2 [156,163].

Caffeine (C8H10N4O2)

Caffeine, also known as 1,3,7-trimethylpurine-2,6-­dione, is a naturally occurring purine derivative found in coffee, cacao, and teas. It has also demonstrated radioprotective activity against IR-induced damage [164]. Caffeine, along with caffeic acid, is an important constituent of coffee and possesses various properties, including anticancer [165], anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects [166,167]. It plays many beneficial roles in neuronal stimulation and enhances the antitumor immune response by blocking adenosine receptors in higher organisms [168]. However, caffeine has also demonstrated significant radiosensitizing effects in an orthotopic transplantation mouse model of LM3 hepatocellular cancer. It reduces cell survival and disrupts G2 phase arrest in irradiated LM3 cells [169]. Caffeine makes mesothelioma cells (H2052) more sensitive to pemetrexed, resulting in increased cell killing, and promotes the phosphorylation of ataxia-telangiectasia-­mutated (ATM) and Chk1, indicating its potential to enhance pemetrexed-based radiation or chemotherapy [170]. Animal studies have reported that administration of caffeine (at a dose of 80 g/kg) delays IR-induced skin damage associated with radiation therapy [171]. Several epidemiological and experimental studies have suggested that caffeine may have potential anticancer properties because of its ability to prevent UVB radiation-induced photo-carcinogenesis through the activation of the phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) [172], PI3K/Akt, mTOR, and p53 pathways [173–176]. Finally, a few studies have shown that caffeine and caffeic acid intake have various effects, including reducing radiation-induced protein in melanoma cells [177] and increasing oxidative stress and apoptosis in various cancer cell lines [178,179].

Resveratrol (C14H1203)

Resveratrol (3,4,5-trihydroxystilbene), a well-known plant-derived polyphenol belonging to the stilbene compound family, is found in various dietary sources, including grapes, soybeans, peanuts, and berries, and exhibits diverse biological and medicinal activities [180]. Resveratrol is recognized for its natural antioxidant properties and ability to modulate the effects of radiation. It acts as a radiomodulator, protecting normal cells while radiosensitizing tumor cells to radiation. This is achieved through free radical scavenging, pro-oxidants, chromosomal aberrations, and apoptotic properties [181–184]. The radioprotective effects of resveratrol are particularly prominent in non-dividing cells, which exhibit strong radiosensitizing properties during the irradiation of dividing cells in the G2-phase [185]. Its radioprotective mechanism involves potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Resveratrol reduces radiation-induced oxidative stress by scavenging ROS, enhancing the level of glutathione (GSH), and promoting the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx), GSH transferase, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) [186,187]. The activation of nuclear factor-erythroid-2 related factor 2 (Nrf2) may contribute to the stimulation of antioxidant enzyme activity by resveratrol, thereby protecting normal cells [188]. In vivo experiments have demonstrated that resveratrol, as a radioprotector, can ameliorate DNA damage and inhibit apoptosis by upregulating the expression of SIRT1 and Bcl2 while downregulating the expression of RhoA and cleaved caspase-3 [17,189]. Additionally, resveratrol exerts anti-inflammatory effects by reducing the ­levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, nuclear factor-kappa (NF-κB), and interleukin 1β (IL-1β). It also inhibits the expression of phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3K), protein kinase B (Akt), and mTOR, contributing to its radioprotective properties [190]. Moreover, resveratrol demonstrated synergistic effects when combined with IR. In head and neck carcinoma cells, the activation of suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS-1) by resveratrol results in the inhibition of STAT3, leading to apoptosis and enhanced radiosensitivity [191]. Similar observations have been reported in highly tumorigenic and radioresistant glioblastoma multiforme tumor-initiating cells (GBM-TICs), where resveratrol increased cell sensitivity to radiation by inhibiting STAT3 signaling pathways, inducing apoptosis and autophagy, disrupting DNA damage repair systems, and ultimately leading to radiosensitization [192]. Resveratrol also sensitized prostate cancer cells to IR by upregulating the activity of the cytotoxic proteins perforin and granzyme B in cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells [184]. The synergistic effect of resveratrol and IR in inhibiting the proliferation of prostate cancer cells was associated with an increased expression of antiproliferative or tumor suppressor molecules, including p15, p21, and mutant p53, as well as a decreased expression of cell division protein kinase 2 (cdk2), cyclin B, and cyclin D [193]. Furthermore, resveratrol at a concentration of 20 µM enhanced the susceptibility of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells to radiation doses up to 8 Gy. This synergistic strategy increases the production of ROS and induces DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), leading to cell aging and death [194]. Overall, resveratrol exhibits radioprotective properties by reducing oxidative stress, promoting antioxidant enzyme activity, and modulating inflammatory responses. It also sensitizes tumor cells to radiation through various mechanisms, including the inhibition of signaling pathways, induction of apoptosis and autophagy, and disruption of DNA damage repair systems. The synergistic effects observed when resveratrol was combined with IR highlight its potential as a therapeutic strategy in cancer treatment.

Molecular mechanism of radiosensitizer and radioprotector

Naturally occurring dietary ingredients have been demonstrated to be potent radiosensitizers with radioprotective and radio-regulatory activities. They protect normal cells while increasing radiation toxicity in tumor cells. The mechanism of radiation sensitivity to dietary ingredients and various dietary interventions can vary considerably. Most compounds exert and enhance their radiation sensitization to tumor cells through various mechanisms, such as reoxygenation of hypoxic tumor cells [195]; increasing radiolysis of water molecules to produce radiation-induced free radicals, ROS, and other secondary biomolecular reactive species; or regulating multiple molecular mechanisms to induce cytotoxic action, enhance DNA damage, inhibit DNA repair mechanisms, normalize tumor vasculature, and disrupt cell survival pathways [196]. They can also promote the expression of radiation-sensitive genes or inhibit the expression of radioresistance genes [197] and regulate non-coding RNAs, including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), to enhance the radiosensitivity of various tumor cell lines [77,198]. In contrast, dietary radioprotectors provide radioprotection to alleviate the harmful effects of IR on normal cells. They achieve this by enhancing antioxidants and DNA repair, inducing hypoxia [199], promoting anti-inflammation, synchronizing cells, and inhibiting cell death signaling pathways [200].

During irradiation, IR hydrolyzes the water molecules into free radicals [1]. Free radicals, including hydroxyl radicals (•OH), H•, H2, and H2O2, can directly and indirectly damage cells by targeting the DNA. Among these free radicals, hydroxyl radicals (•OH) are particularly harmful and cause extensive cellular damage [4]. Natural dietary radioprotectors activate various antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, glutathione peroxidase, peroxiredoxin reductase, and thioredoxin reductase, to catalyze free radicals and maintain cellular redox balance [201–203]. Free radical scavenging occurs through two main mechanisms: hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) and single-electron transfer (SET). In the HAT mechanism, a radioprotector transfers a hydrogen atom (H) to hydroxyl free radicals, whereas in the SET mechanism, the radioprotector donates an electron to stabilize the redox reactions in free radical molecules [204]. Additionally, radioprotectors can interact with biological targets, such as DNA to stabilize the target and prevent radiation-induced damage through the formation of mixed disulfides. Amino-thiol radioprotectors, including glutathione disulfide, guanidoethyl disulfide, and cysteamine, bind to DNA, and the effectiveness of these compounds parallels their ability to protect against radiation damage [205].

Activation of the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway by natural dietary radioprotectors provides a promising strategy to increase antioxidant enzyme activity, protect cells from oxidative stress, and potentially enhance the effectiveness of radiotherapy. Ascorbic acid, curcumin, and genistein can significantly activate the Keap1-Nrf2 pathways, dramatically increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes [206–208]. Nrf2 is considered the primary transcription factor that plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular redox balance. Under physiological conditions, Keap1 mediates proteasomal degradation of Nrf2 in the cytosol. Under oxidative stress, modified cysteine residues inhibit Keap1, thereby protecting Nrf2 from proteasomal degradation [209–211]. As a result, Nrf2 accumulates in the cytosol and is translocated to the nucleus. Translocated Nrf2 dimerizes with sMaf, binds to the promoter, and transcribes target cytoprotective antioxidant genes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione reductase (GR), and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1). This process induces antioxidation and protects the cells from the harmful effects of oxidative stress [209,212,213]. In tumor cells, radiosensitizers can increase radiosensitivity by ubiquitinating and degrading Nrf2 through the activation of keap1, leading to the upregulation of oxidative stress by inhibiting the activities of antioxidant enzymes.

Cellular oxidative stress can also activate the expression and function of another redox-sensitive protein, the NF-κB transcription factor, which is directly or indirectly involved in several cellular processes, including cell proliferation, growth, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis [214–216]. In addition to influencing cell survival and apoptosis, the NF-κB signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating different inflammatory cytokines that have radioprotective functions [217,218]. Radioprotectors activate the NF-κB signaling pathway either directly or indirectly by activating the TNF-α and ERBB pathways [219,220]. In genotoxic stress, NF-κB induces the upregulation of different cell proliferation and apoptosis regulatory genes, including Cyclin D1, a key mediator of cell division and the G1/S phase transition [221]. The transcription factor NF-κB is also known to activate antiapoptotic genes, such as c-IAP-1, c-IAP-2, and c-FLIP [222,223]. Furthermore, NF-κB blocks the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway through the transcription of Bcl-2 family genes [224]. In contrast, natural compounds, such as polyphenols and flavonoids, have the potential to inhibit NF-κB activity [225]. Natural dietary compounds, such as curcumin and tocopherols, with potential radioprotective properties have also been shown to act as radiosensitizers by inhibiting the function of NF-κB [226–228]. Consequently, radiosensitizers inhibit the inflammatory and survival actions of NF-κB to increase the radiosensitivity of cancer cells [229].

Similar to NF-κB, radioprotectors activate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR, STAT3, and MAPK pathways in response to radiation-induced oxidative stress. IR activates Akt signaling through receptor tyrosine kinase-mediated activation of PI3K [230]. For the full-phase activation of Akt, PI3K first activates phosphoinositide–dependent kinase 1(PDK1) and then PDK2 by phosphorylating Akt at threonine 308 and serine 473, respectively [231]. Akt promotes the transcription of the gene Bcl-2 through NF-κB signaling, and activates mTOR signaling to inhibit apoptosis [232,233]. Furthermore, after irradiation, Akt signaling facilitates non-homologous end-joining-mediated repair of DNA DSBs in normal cells, leading to increased cell survival [234]. Under radiation-induced oxidative stress, radioprotectors activate another cytoplasmic transcription factor, STAT3, a member of the STAT family of proteins [235]. During activation, STAT3 dimerizes, translocates to the nucleus, and transcribes genes associated with cell survival and cell proliferation to protect cells [235]. STAT3, alone or in conjugation with NF-κB signaling, has been reported to prevent radiation-induced damage [236]. IR-induced ROS activate MAPK, another important pathway involved in DNA damage (DSBs) repair and cell survival. Radioprotectors can facilitate the MAPK signaling pathway during radiation-induced stress conditions. This serine/threonine kinase pathway consists of three members: ERK, stress-activated protein kinases/c-Jun N-terminal kinases (SAPK/JNK), and p38 MAPK [237]. Activated ERK signaling regulates the expression of antiapoptotic proteins after radiation exposure [238]. Several proapoptotic proteins can also be directly inhibited by ERK signaling [239]. ERK signaling plays a crucial role in enhancing DNA repair through the upregulation of DNA repair genes [240]. Genotoxic stress activates SAPK/JNK, which is required for DNA repair and survival, whereas p38 MAPK is activated by cellular stress and promotes cell survival and proliferation [241,242]. In contrast, different natural dietary radiosensitizers impede cell survival and DNA damage repair pathways to enhance DNA damage and induce apoptosis in irradiated tumor cells.

In tumor cells, radiosensitizers increase the production of ROS, which causes DNA DSBs and triggers a series of molecular events. Sensing proteins ATM/ATR are overexpressed in response to radiation-induced DNA damage, which can activate the p53 tumor suppressor and trigger apoptotic cell death [15]. Activated p53 promotes cell cycle arrest and apoptosis by activating the p21 gene, leading to cell cycle arrest, and the Bax gene of the Bcl-2 family, which induces apoptosis. Additionally, p53 binds to and inhibits the antiapoptotic Bcl-2 [243,244]. The proapoptotic protein Bax forms pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane, allowing the release of cytochrome C [245]. Cytochrome C further activates caspase-9, which in turn activates caspase-3, leading to apoptosis [246]. In the p53-independent apoptotic pathway, radiation-induced cellular redox imbalance depolarizes the mitochondrial membrane, triggering the activation of cytochrome C and its subsequent downstream targets to promote apoptosis [246]. In contrast to radiosensitizers, radioprotectors target both the p53-dependent and p53-independent apoptotic pathways in normal cells following IR. They upregulate the expression of the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 to suppress apoptosis in normal cells. This helps protect normal cells from IR-induced apoptosis. Stress response pathways modulated by radioprotecting and radiosensitizing dietary ingredients. When exposed to IR, free radicals such as ROS are produced in the cellular system. Radiosensitizers enhance radiation-induced ROS production to break DNA double strands, induce p53-mediated apoptosis, and arrest the cell cycle in tumor cells. Radiosensitizers can also facilitate the p53-independent or oxidative stress-mediated mitochondrial cytochrome C pathway in tumor cells to promote apoptosis. Furthermore, radiosensitizers sensitize tumor cells to radiation by inhibiting the MAPK, STAT3, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, NF-κB, and Nrf2 pathways, leading to transcription of various genes involved in cell proliferation, cell survival, DNA damage repair, and antioxidation. On the other hand, radioprotective dietary ingredients activate the MAPK, STAT3, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, NF-κB, and Nrf2 pathways to minimize the detrimental effects of ROS on normal cells. Additionally, radioprotectors inhibit p53-dependent and p53-independent apoptosis in response to oxidative stress, thereby protecting normal cells from the damaging effects of radiation.

In summary, the mechanisms of action of dietary ingredients such as radiosensitizers and radioprotectors are multifaceted and involve modulation of various molecular and cellular processes. Radiosensitizers enhance the radiosensitivity of tumor cells by increasing oxidative stress, DNA damage, apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest. In contrast, radioprotectors protect normal cells by activating antioxidant enzymes, promoting DNA repair, inhibiting apoptosis, and facilitating cell-cycle progression. This intricate interplay between dietary ingredients and radiation response pathways highlights the potential of these natural compounds as adjuvants in radiotherapy to improve treatment outcomes and minimize normal tissue toxicity.

Conclusion and future perspective

In conclusion, the influence of dietary ingredients on radioprotection and radiosensitization is of growing significance, with the potential to significantly affect the outcomes of both radiation therapy and exposure in various scenarios. Research indicates that specific dietary components can modify the impact of radiation on the body, with some enhancing radiation resistance and others increasing it. Several dietary components, including antioxidants, phytochemicals, vitamins, and minerals, have been shown to reduce radiation-induced damage and bolster cellular repair mechanisms in normal cells. The molecular mechanisms underlying the actions of dietary ingredients, such as radiosensitizers and radioprotectors are intricate and multifaceted. Radiosensitizers can heighten radiation toxicity in tumor cells by augmenting ROS production, inducing DNA damage, hindering DNA repair processes, and disrupting cell survival pathways. Conversely, radioprotectors alleviate the detrimental effects of IR in normal cells by promoting antioxidant and DNA repair mechanisms, anti-inflammatory responses, and cell survival pathways.

These underlying mechanisms encompass the modulation of a variety of molecular pathways, including the Keap1-Nrf2, P13K/Akt/mTOR, STAT3, and MAPK pathways, as well as the p53-dependent and p53-independent apoptosis pathways. Dietary ingredients with radiosensitizing properties can impede these pathways in tumor cells, increasing radiosensitivity, whereas radioprotective ingredients can activate or safeguard these pathways in normal cells to minimize the adverse effects of radiation. The utilization of dietary ingredients holds promise for enhancing the effectiveness of radiation therapy in cancer patients, safeguarding healthy tissues from radiation damage, and mitigating the effects of radiation in occupational and environmental settings. Ultimately, gaining a more comprehensive understanding of the effect of dietary ingredients on radiation sensitivity and susceptibility will contribute to more effective strategies for radiation protection and improved overall health outcomes. Nonetheless, further research is imperative to fully comprehend the intricate interplay between diet and radiation sensitivity and to develop targeted dietary recommendations for specific populations.
